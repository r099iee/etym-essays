- Old English — slow and sluggish: læt means slow, tardy, negligent — a moral judgement as much as a speed.
- c. 1200 — running late: Middle English late gains the modern sense of after the proper time, and bi-lated forms appear for hindered travellers.
- c. 1600 — overtaken by night: Shakespeare fixes belated as benighted — travellers whom darkness catches on the road.
- 17th–18th c. — generalised delay: sermons and letters extend it from night-caught travellers to late repentance, late letters, and late seasons.
- 19th c. — the apology trade: Victorians make belated birthday and belated thanks the standard formulae of polite regret.
- Today — the rueful latecomer: overwhelmingly social and apologetic — belated cards, wishes, and pardons — with Tolkien's belated tea as its cosiest memorial.
