• Weakling: Similar because it shares the depreciative -ling suffix and names a stunted, pitiable creature; differs because a weakling lacks strength from any cause, while a starveling is wasted specifically by hunger.
  • Runt: Similar because both name an undersized, underfed specimen, often the smallest of a litter; differs because runt stresses smallness of breed or birth, while starveling stresses wasting by want of food.
  • Scarecrow: Similar because both evoke a gaunt, ragged, straw-thin figure of pity or mockery; differs because a scarecrow is literally stuffed straw (figuratively a ragged person), while a starveling is a living creature thinned by hunger.
  • Skeleton: Similar because both picture a body reduced to bone, the textbook image of extreme thinness; differs because skeleton is clinical or hyperbolic slang (‘a mere skeleton’), while starveling names the hunger and the sufferer together.
  • Waif: Similar because both name a neglected, homeless, hungry innocent of the streets; differs because a waif stresses abandonment and homelessness (often a child), while a starveling stresses the visible wasting of the body.
  • Pauper: Similar because both belong to the Victorian vocabulary of poverty and want; differs because a pauper is legally destitute (a parish charge), while a starveling is physically famished, whatever the parish books say.
  • Famished wretch: Similar because both describe one hollowed by hunger and deserving pity; differs because wretch is general misery of any kind, while starveling is hunger's own special word.
  • Underling: Similar because it shares the -ling diminutive of contempt and smallness; differs because an underling is low in rank or power, while a starveling is low in flesh and feeding.
  • Hireling: Similar because it shares the mercenary -ling sneer and suits the fable's hack (a horse for hire); differs because a hireling is contemptible for serving pay, while a starveling is pitiable for lacking bread.
