- 16th c. — the double-l spelling “councillor” is fixed; meaning “a member of a royal or civic advisory assembly” — an office of rank and trust, tied to courts and guilds.
- 17th–18th c. — “councillor” consolidates in civic government: borough, town, and parish councils; the word becomes bureaucratic and administrative, slightly dry.
- 19th c. — democratisation: elected town councillors appear under reforms such as the Municipal Corporations Act (1835); the office shifts from patrician appointment to public representative — perception becomes democratic and accountable.
- 20th c. — “councillor” is standard for local elected representatives in the UK and Commonwealth; also used of university, church, and corporate councils; register remains official and respectful.
- 21st c. — the British double-l spelling contrasts with American “councilor”; the word is firmly civic-administrative, associated with local government; mildly formal, occasionally abbreviated in use.
