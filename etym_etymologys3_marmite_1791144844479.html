Marmite begins, if the best theory holds, with a sound: the low mur-mur-mur of a pot coming to the boil. Everything else — the soup, the trademark, the national metaphor — is commentary on that simmer.

  • The murmur family (PIE to Old French): human languages independently invent mur-/mar-/mum- syllables for low continuous noise (Latin murmur, Greek mormurō, Sanskrit marmara, English murmur itself, borrowed back from French). Indo-Europeanists reconstruct an imitative base *mur- rather than a true lexical root: it means ‘the sound’ by sounding like it. Latin murmurāre (to murmur) and noun murmur carried the idea through the Empire; Old French diversified it into murmurer (to murmur), marmouser/marmotter (to mutter, mumble — whence English marmot? no, false friend: the rodent is separate; but marmotter, to mumble, is kin), and the expressive syllable marm- for grumbling noises of every kind, from discontented men to boiling pots.
  • The pot christened by its noise (1300s): Middle French marmite (attested 14th c.) names the covered simmering pot — on this account, ‘the murmurer,’ the vessel that talks while it cooks, fitted with the old noun-forming suffix -ite. A rival homonym complicates the record: Old French also knew marmite as a painted grotesque face on pots and, by transfer, a hypocrite or sneak (cf. marmiteux, a poor wretch with a piteous face). Whether pot-face named pot or pot named face is a genuine philological tangle; the Trésor de la langue française tradition favours the murmur etymology for the vessel while recording the doubt. What is certain is the object: squat, lidded, earthen or metal, the slow-cooking heart of the household, and by the 17th century the namesake of petite marmite, the clear bourgeois soup simmered and served from the same vessel (beef, vegetables, marrow).
  • Crossing into English (1800s): English travellers, cooks (Carême’s influence, then Escoffier’s), and historians of civilisation adopted marmite and petite marmite as menu-French and local colour — exactly the register of the source passage, where lice become a petite marmite with anthropological deadpan. Pronunciation settled near /mɑːˈmiːt/, spelling kept French.
  • 1902: the jar shaped like the pot: the Marmite Food Extract Company of Burton-on-Trent began selling yeast extract — a by-product of brewing, rich in glutamate and B vitamins — in small earthenware jars modelled on the French marmite. The name was literal packaging: ‘the stuff in the little pot.’ Through two world wars (troops’ rations, vitamin-B prophylaxis), post-war nurseries, and student toast-racks, Marmite became a British institution aggressively unlike anything else in the larder: black, sticky, intensely savoury, bifid — worshipped and loathed in near-equal measure.
  • From spread to metaphor (1990s–present): the “Love it or hate it” campaign (1996 onward) turned consumer polarisation into brand equity, and the name escaped the jar: the Marmite effect, Marmite elections, a Marmite manager. Dictionaries now record the extended sense. So a Roman murmur became a French pot, became an English soup-word, became a Staffordshire jar, became the default idiom for divisiveness — and the lice-soup of the source passage sits squarely mid-chain, when marmite still meant dinner and Durant could make it mean revenge.

Put your ear to the word and you can still hear it simmer.
