turgid:
- A strictly physical word (early 17th c., 1610s) - borrowed from Latin turgidus, 'swollen'; a turgid vein or limb is tautly engorged with fluid, stretched at the point of strain
- From body to style (18th-19th c.) - the word migrates to language and rhetoric: turgid prose is bombastic, inflated, puffed up - and the two senses touch whenever an atmosphere or utterance seems swollen too full to be spoken
- The scientific retention (19th c.) - botany keeps turgor and turgid for the internal fluid pressure of plant cells, so the physical sense never dies
- Contemporary - both senses remain equally alive: a turgid atmosphere, turgid style, a thing taut with content and near the point of bursting

contempt:
- A formal attitude (14th c.) - borrowed via Old French from Latin contemptus: the settled verdict of worthlessness, a thing cut off from consideration
- Institutionalised in law (16th c.) - 'contempt of court' gives the word its cold, formal, judicial weight; contempt becomes not only private feeling but public judgement
- Contemporary - the coldest and most judicial of the scorn words: a recorded verdict of worthlessness, from private disregard to the full severity of law
