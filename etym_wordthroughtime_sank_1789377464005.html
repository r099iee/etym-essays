- Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Germanic (c. 3500 BCE–1st c. CE) - Strong Class III ablaut verb *senkʷ- / *sinkwaną, where the past singular took the o-grade vowel *sankw- ('dropped down, receded, flowed away').
- Old English (c. 8th–11th Century) - Attested as strong verb *sincan*, with past singular *sanc* or *sonc* ('went down into water, subsided, fell down'); used for drowning men, sinking ships, and setting stars.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century) - Spelled *sanc*, *sanke*, or *sonk*; retained strong ablaut conjugation; expanded from physical water submersion to human posture (dropping to one's knees, fainting, or bowing in worship).
- Early Modern English (c. 16th–17th Century) - Standardized as *sank* (with competing variant *sunk* in past tense); established in chivalric literature, biblical translations, and drama (Shakespeare) for courteous kneeling and emotional despair ('my heart sank').
- 18th to 19th Century (Georgian & Victorian Eras) - Grammarians formalized the three-part ablaut distribution (*sink / sank / sunk*); widely utilized in romantic literature (as in Oscar Wilde's courtly scene in *The Remarkable Rocket*) for deliberate chivalric genuflection and romantic devotion.
- 20th to 21st Century - Preserved as the universal past tense of physical submersion, financial market plunges, celestial sunsets, and idiomatic emotional devastation.
