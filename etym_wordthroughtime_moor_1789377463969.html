- Old English Period (c. 7th–11th Century) - Attested as *mōr*, denoting barren marshes, fens, and dangerous wilderness lying beyond settled farmland; prominently featured in *Beowulf* as the desolate, misty marshland home of Grendel (*mōr-hopu*).
- Middle English Period (c. 12th–15th Century) - Evolved into *mor* and *moore*, widely recorded in manorial surveys, placenames, and royal hunting records to designate expansive tracts of unenclosed upland heath, peat bogs, and common pasture.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Maintained across agricultural treatises and legal enclosement disputes as unproductive commons suitable only for peat-cutting, rough sheep-grazing, and turf harvest.
- 18th to 19th Century (Romantic & Victorian Eras) - Transformed into a supreme gothic and romantic motif: an emblem of untamable sublime nature, tragic doom, and isolation (celebrated in Emily Brontë's *Wuthering Heights*, Conan Doyle's *The Hound of the Baskervilles*, and Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*).
- 20th to 21st Century (Contemporary Era) - Re-evaluated ecologically as globally vital peatland carbon sinks, Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), and protected National Parks.
