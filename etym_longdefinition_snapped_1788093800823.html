SNAPPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb snap, whose core meaning is "to break suddenly with a sharp cracking sound" ("the arrows snapped against the dragon's scales"), and which radiates into a remarkable family of related senses all built on the idea of a sudden, sharp action or sound: to bite suddenly, to click, to speak sharply, to lose self-control, to photograph, and to fasten with a click.

Primary senses

  • To break with a sharp sound: "the branch snapped under his weight", "the rope snapped". The break is sudden, complete, and audible — the word is onomatopoeic, imitating the crack itself.
  • To make a sudden sharp sound: "snap one's fingers", "the twig snapped", "the lock snapped shut". Here nothing breaks; the word names the click or crack.
  • To bite or seize suddenly: "the dog snapped at the stranger". The image is of jaws closing fast; from this comes "to snatch" ("snap up a bargain").

Secondary and figurative senses

  • To speak sharply and irritably: "she snapped at the waiter". The tongue is imagined as a jaw that bites; the sense is attested from the 19th century.
  • To lose one's composure suddenly: "after years of strain, he finally snapped". A mind, like a taut rope, breaks all at once; this sense became prominent in 20th-century psychology and journalism ("he snapped under pressure").
  • To photograph: "snap a picture" — from snapshot, originally (1808) a gunshot fired quickly without careful aim, transferred (by 1890) to a quickly taken photograph.
  • To fasten with a click: "the case snapped shut", "snap fasteners" (patented in the 19th century); hence "snap-on" tools and "snap buttons".
  • Adjectival uses: "a snap decision/judgement" (quick, impulsive), "a snap election" (British politics: called at short notice), "a snap" (colloquial: an easy task — "it was a snap").
  • Games and sport: the card game Snap (late 19th century; "Snap!" cried on matching cards); in American football, "the snap" is the quick pass from centre that starts each play.
  • Modern digital life: "snap" as the root of Snapchat (2011), where a "snap" is a photo or video message that appears briefly — the photographic sense, reborn.

Semantic bridge

  • All senses radiate from the sharp, sudden crack: breaking (the crack of the object), clicking (the crack of the fingers), biting (the crack of jaws), angry speech (the crack of a whip-tongue), breakdown (the crack of a strained mind), photography (the crack of the shutter, plus the old "quick shot" idea), and fastening (the crack of the catch).
  • Suddenness is the common thread: a snap is always quick, complete, and irreversible — which is why "snap" also means "instant" ("snap decision").
  • The word's onomatopoeic origin (a Germanic expressive base) explains its vigour: it has been re-borrowed again and again into new registers because the sound it names is unforgettable.

Grammar notes

  • Regular past: snap – snapped – snapped (the doubled p preserves the short vowel).
  • Rich phrasal system: "snap up" (buy eagerly), "snap out of it" (recover suddenly from a mood), "snap to it" (get moving), "snap back" (recover; also a sharp reply), "snap off".
  • In the context sentence ("a hail of dark arrows ... snapped and rattled on his scales"), the intransitive break-sense is active: the arrows strike the dragon's hide and shatter with sharp cracks — the perfect context for the word's onomatopoeic core.
