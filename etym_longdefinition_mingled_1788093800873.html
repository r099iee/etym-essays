Mingled is the past tense and past participle of the verb mingle, and it performs two grammatical functions: it is a finite verb form (e.g. "the two waters mingled") and, far more commonly, a participial adjective describing something that has been blended or combined with something else (e.g. "mingled feelings", "mingled yarn"). Historically it is a close kin of "mix": Old English mengan "to mix" was reshaped in Middle English with the frequentative suffix -le into minglen, so the deepest core sense of the word is simply "mixed together, blended". In the source sentence — "in your hoard is mingled much of the wealth of his halls and towns" — Bard of Esgaroth uses the participle in its passive sense: the treasure Smaug stole from Dale has been so thoroughly mixed together with Thorin's hoard that the two stocks of gold can no longer be separated, which is precisely why the claim for restitution becomes so urgent and so difficult to adjudicate.

Primary senses — physical blending and social contact.

  • Transitive physical sense: to combine two or more substances or objects so that they interpenetrate and form one mass; the participle records the achieved state — mingled blood, mingled dust, mingled waters. The key nuance separating mingle from fuse or dissolve is that the elements typically remain co-present in principle: they are jumbled together, not annihilated, and a mingled thing can still be thought of as a togetherness of its parts.
  • Intransitive sense: of things, to become united of their own accord — rivers mingling at a confluence, laughter and music mingling in the air; "mingled" states the completed union.
  • Social sense: from the late sixteenth century, "to mingle with people" means to move freely among and associate with a group — to circulate at a gathering, to merge into a crowd without surrendering one's identity (a host mingled with her guests; spies mingled with the crowd). This is the sense still alive at parties: "networking and mingling".

Secondary and tertiary senses — the mental, the moral and the idiomatic.

  • Mingled emotions: the most productive modern idiom: "with mingled hope and fear", "mingled feelings", "mingled relief and guilt". The semantic bridge is a transparent metaphor: just as physical substances interpenetrate, so do affections of the mind; the participle conveys that the emotions are co-present and can no longer be felt apart.
  • Abstract qualities: mingled motives, mingled fortune, a mingled reputation — blended attributes in which the constituents show through but cannot be cleanly separated; both the participle and the verbal noun "a mingling" are used in this shading sense.
  • Peoples, lineage and seed: "mingled seed" (Authorized Version, Leviticus 19:19) and the old discourse of "mingled blood" and "mingled peoples" — populations or stocks combined through long contact; the word here carries a neutral-to-positive charge of interpenetration.
  • Crafts and textures: "mingled yarn", mingled colours — threads or hues combined in weaving, one of the oldest applications of the participle (the same biblical text forbids a garment "mingled of linen and woollen").
  • Playful and reduplicated forms: the sixteenth-century "mingle-mangle" ("a confused mixture", probably blended with the verb mangle) shows the word's tendency to slide toward disorder; plain "mingled" keeps the milder suggestion of harmony rather than chaos.

Register and boundaries. Today mixed is the unmarked everyday word and mingled is the literary, slightly archaic one. "Mingled" survives chiefly in dignified narration — Tolkien uses it freely in The Hobbit — and in fixed phrases such as "with mingled feelings", where it adds solemnity. It is milder than fused (components lose identity), weaker than amalgamated (full merger, corporate or metallic), and warmer than combined (bare factual union). Its emotional colouring is one of gentle, intimate interpenetration rather than force or violence.
