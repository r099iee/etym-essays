lettrisme is a transparent modern French formation, lettre plus -isme, but each morpheme carries two millennia of weight. Lettre descends from Latin littera, also spelled litera, meaning a character of the alphabet in the singular and an epistle, documents or literature in the plural litterae. Latin grammarians already debated its origin: some linked it to Greek diphthera meaning prepared hide or tablet, suggesting an Etruscan mediation, others left it as obscure Italic. What is certain is its centrality: litterae meant both the material marks and the culture built from them, a duality French preserves in lettre as character and lettres as literature. Old French lettre by the 12th century meant both, as in charters and romances, and by the 17th century belles-lettres meant polite literary culture itself.

The suffix -isme travels from Greek -ismos, forming nouns of action, doctrine or imitation, through Latin -ismus and ecclesiastical medieval Latin, into Old French as -isme in words like baptisme. By the 19th century it had become the productive marker of artistic schools: romantisme, réalisme, symbolisme, cubisme, dadaïsme, surréalisme. To coin lettrisme in occupied Paris in 1942 was therefore to claim equal rank: as surrealism was built on dreams, lettrism would be built on letters.

  • littera to lettre: phonetic simplification of geminate -tt- and final -a to mute -e, with semantic split between character and literature.
  • -ismos to -isme: regular French sound shift of Greek -os to -e, preserving doctrinal force.
  • 1942 coinage: Isidore Isou, aged 17, proclaims a new poetry in Bucharest notebooks, brings manifesto to Paris 1945-1947.
  • 1947-1952 diffusion: manifestos, review La Dictature Lettriste, Cannes film scandal, schism producing Lettrist International and Situationists.

Isou deliberately chose the French form to root his universal claim in Parisian prestige, even though he wrote with a Romanian accent and Yiddish background. The anglicised doublet Lettrism appears in English histories from the 1960s, but French lettrisme retains the philosophical tincture noted in the source. After the war, when existentialism asked what meaning remained after catastrophe, Isou answered that meaning itself was exhausted and only matter remained: chew the letters, carve them, amplify their breath. Later evolution saw the term generalise: art historians now use lettrisme for any letter-image practice from Greek vases to Jasper Johns, detaching the suffix from Isou's sect and restoring it to its literal sense of letter-ness. Phonetically French /lɛtʁism/ with uvular r and final mute e contrasts with anglicised /lɛˈtriːzmə/, a shibboleth of insider knowledge.

Socio-culturally the coinage marks a shift from interwar word-magic to post-war media critique: letters as shapes anticipate television graphics, concrete poetry and code art. To trace lettrisme is thus to trace how a humble schoolroom unit, the letter, became first literature, then doctrine, then weapon against literature itself.
