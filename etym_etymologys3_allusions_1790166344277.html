The evolution of allusions begins not with reference but with play. Its deepest recoverable ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root conventionally reconstructed as *leid- / *loid-, glossed as "to play", and by Michiel de Vaan more literally as "to let go frequently". This root is not one of the famous, rock-solid PIE roots like *bher- or *deh3-; it is reconstructed comparatively from a scattered family: Middle Irish laidid "impels", Greek lind-esthai "to contend", Albanian lind "gives birth", Lithuanian leisti "to let", Latvian laist "let, publish, set in motion". The semantic thread is release, impelling, letting something go in play. From this playful releasing comes Latin ludere "to play, sport, jest, mimic" and its noun ludus "game, play, school, gladiatorial training".

In Classical Latin, ludere was extraordinarily productive. With prefixes it generated a whole moral vocabulary still present in English:

  • colludere → collude: to play together, hence to conspire secretly.
  • deludere → delude: to play down from, to mock, to deceive.
  • eludere → elude: to play out of, to evade.
  • praeludere → prelude: to play before, an introduction.
  • ludicrus → ludicrous: sportive, then absurdly laughable.

Within this set, alludere combined ad- "to, toward" + ludere "to play". In Cicero, Plautus and later authors it meant "to play with, to jest, to make fun of, to refer to mockingly". The noun of action allusio (gen. allusionis, acc. allusionem) thus meant "a playing with, a play on words, a reference". Late Latin writers, especially in rhetoric, used allusio for a witty play upon words or a veiled hint. This is the direct etymon cited by the OED: borrowing from Latin, not inherited through Germanic.

Old French did not prominently continue the word in popular speech; it survived as a learned term. Middle French allusion in the 16th century meant hint or reference, and it was from this learned French-Latin channel that English borrowed it in the middle Tudor period. Etymonline dates are precise and important for allusions:

  • 1540s: "metaphor, parable" — a sense now obsolete. Early humanist writers used allusion for allegorical comparison.
  • 1550s: "word-play, joke" — still close to Latin playfulness.
  • 1610s: "passing or casual reference" — the modern literary sense. This is the decisive semantic narrowing from explicit play to implicit reference.

Seventeenth-century English fixed the modern distinction that an allusion is never an outright explicit mention. To allude is to hint without naming; to mention is to name. John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667), Alexander Pope's Dunciad (1728) and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) — itself a tissue of Edwardian slang, quotations and allusions to numerous poets, exactly as in the source context — canonised allusion as the central intertextual device of English poetry. By the Augustan age, critics distinguished Biblical allusions, classical allusions, historical allusions and personal allusions, and handbooks warned young orators not to allude obscurely.

Phonetically almost nothing changed: Latin allusio [alˈluːsioː] → French [alyzjɔ̃] → English /əˈluːʒən/. The spelling retained double-l from ad- assimilation (ad-ludere → alludere). The plural -s is regular. The paronym illusion (from illudere "to mock, deceive") developed in parallel, explaining the persistent allusion / illusion confusion: both are plays, but allusion plays with shared knowledge, illusion plays upon perception.

Socio-culturally, allusions moved from elite humanist code to democratic meme. In the 19th century Matthew Arnold and James Joyce made allusiveness a test of cultivation. In the 20th century New Criticism, structuralism and intertextuality gave it theory: Kristeva, Genette and Bloom's anxiety of influence. Today internet culture produces allusions at speed — a film still, a line from Shakespeare, a classical tag — assuming a shared body of knowledge exactly as Britannica defines. The plural form in the prompt, "allusions to numerous poets", is typical: allusions are counted when sources are multiple and playful, precisely preserving the Latin sense of playing among texts.
