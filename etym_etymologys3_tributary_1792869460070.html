The odyssey of tributary begins with the number three and ends with every path in the world flowing into one Road: Rome's tribes, Rome's taxes, and at last the world's rivers.

Three tribes — the Roman beginning: Early Rome divided its people into three tribus, the Ramnes, Tities, and Luceres, whether of three hills, three peoples, or three ancient war-bands no antiquary now agrees. From tribus came tribuere, to assign or allot by tribes, and from that verb the noun tributum, the levy assigned tribe by tribe to fund wars, Rome's first direct tax. Citizens paid it grudgingly and celebrated its abolition after the Macedonian spoils of 167 BCE; provincials and allies paid it as the price of peace.

Tribute — the empire's price: As Rome grew, tributum widened from a citizen levy to the systematised exactions of empire, assessed by census, gathered by publicans, and resented from Judaea to Britain. The adjective tributarius marked whoever owed it: tributary persons, lands, and states. Client kings from Herod to the Brigantes bought their thrones with it, and the word carried the clink of coin and the weight of subjection together, duty shading into humiliation as republic hardened into empire.

France and England — the vassal adjective: Old French tributaire carried the political sense into feudal Europe, where chroniclers counted tributary princes on every frontier, and Middle English tributarie, from the fourteenth century, did the same duty for chroniclers of Wales, Scotland, and the Crusades. For two hundred years the word belongs to chanceries and histories, marking who pays whom and how much independence the payment buys, the account-book as destiny.

Rivers learn to pay: Late Latin writers had already, occasionally, called lesser streams tributarii of greater ones, and Renaissance geographers revived the metaphor with delight as the age of discovery mapped the world's watersheds. By the seventeenth century English travellers describe the Danube's, the Nile's, the Ganges' tributaries as a matter of course; by the eighteenth the Amazon's hundred feeder rivers make tributary a word every schoolboy knows. The money has become water, and the subjection has become service: to feed a great river is an honour no stream refuses.

Science spreads the watershed: The nineteenth century carried the river-word into every discipline that maps flow: geologists with tributary valleys and tributary glaciers, anatomists with tributary veins joining the vena cava, even students of traffic and trade with tributary streets and tributary markets. Meanwhile the political sense fossilised into history, tributary states surviving only in books about China, Rome, and the Ottomans. Its milestones read:

  • PIE three: *trei-, the numeral behind Rome's three tribes and a thousand European threes.
  • Tribal levy: tribus to tributum, citizenship organised into taxation, the citizen's grudging war-fund.
  • Imperial adjective: tributarius marking the empire's payers, from client kings to provinces, duty edged with humiliation.
  • Feudal transmission: tributaire and tributarie counting vassal princes across medieval frontiers.
  • Fluvial metaphor: lesser streams as tribute-payers, revived by Renaissance geographers mapping the newly worldwide watersheds.
  • Scientific generalisation: veins, valleys, glaciers, and streets all turning tributary, while the tax-collector retires to the footnotes.

So when every path becomes the one Road's tributary, the saying spends Rome's oldest small change: three tribes' war-tax, melted down through two thousand years, now minted fresh as geography, philosophy, and praise for every doorstep's spring.
