- (13th c., Middle English pasture via Old French) - Enters English as the manor’s word for grazing land and for the right to graze: feed for kine and oxen — the very pairing of the source line.
- (14th–16th c.) - Legal institutionalization: “common of pasture” as a property right; enclosure disputes make pasture a political word of the countryside.
- (16th–17th c.) - Spiritual colour: Psalm 23 (KJV 1611: “He maketh me to lie down in green pastures”) makes pasture a word of divine provision and peace.
- (18th–19th c.) - Agricultural improvement — pasture rotation, sown leys, the great cattle economies of the New World; pastoral lends the word a literary haze of simple rural virtue, and its irony.
- (20th c.) - The idioms: “greener pastures” (leaving for something better) and “put out to pasture” (retiring the old worker like the old horse) turn the ancient feeding-land into a metaphor of destiny. Perception: nourishing on the one hand, gently dismissive on the other.
- (today) - Utterly ordinary: farm handbooks, “pasture-raised” labels on eggs and beef, the greeting-card greener pastures. The old feeding-thought — land that feeds — still runs beneath every use.
