ROOT 1: SWEET
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*sweh₂d- / *swād-
"sweet, pleasant, delightful to the taste or smell"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*swōtuz
"sweet, fragrant"
↓
Old English (c. 700–1050)
swēte
"pleasing to the senses, sweet-tasting, fragrant, beloved"
↓
Middle English (c. 1150–1450)
swete / sweete
"sweet, fragrant, delightful"
↓
Modern English (present)
SWEET

ROOT 2: WILLIAM
PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*welh₁- (will, choose) + *ḱel- (cover, protect)
"to wish, desire" + "to hide, shield, protect"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*wiljô + *helmaz → *Willahelmaz
"will, desire" + "helmet, protection" → "one who possesses resolute will as a protective helmet"
↓
Old High German / Frankish (c. 7th–9th c.)
Willahelm / Wilhelm
"resolute protector (masculine personal name)"
↓
Old Northern French / Norman (c. 10th–11th c.)
Williame / Guillaume
"William (introduced to England by William the Conqueror in 1066)"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.)
William / Willyam
"popular masculine personal name, archetype of the rustic suitor"
↓
COMPOUND BOTANICAL FORMATION
Early Modern English (c. 1570–1597)
Sweet-william / Sweet William
"Dianthus barbatus; fragrant garden pink (attested in Gerard's Herball, 1597)"
↓
Modern English (present)
SWEET-WILLIAM
