lavished (verb, past tense and past participle of lavish) — /ˈlævɪʃt/. To have given, granted, poured out or spent in very great — often extravagant — abundance. In the source sentence — "its intelligence will be lavished on the perils of the hunt and the tricks of trade" — the word means that a nomad people’s mental energy is wholly poured out upon the urgent uncertainties of hunting and petty commerce, so that nothing remains for the arts and refinements of civilization.

I. Core sense: generous bestowal in abundance

  • Lavish something on/upon someone: to give without stint: the patrons lavished gifts on the monastery; she lavished attention and praise on her students. The typical objects are intangibles — praise, affection, care, attention, time, effort, hospitality — as well as money and gifts.
  • Lavish money on something: to spend profusely, often on luxuries: he lavished a fortune on the renovation.
  • The passive is common and is the construction used in the text: their intelligence is lavished on the hunt means their intelligence is spent, with nothing hoarded for higher uses. The losses are as important as the gains: what is lavished on one object is thereby denied to another, so the word always implies scarcity elsewhere.

II. The adjective and the noun

  • Lavish (adjective): "very generous or abundant; extravagant, luxurious": a lavish banquet, lavish praise, a lavish lifestyle. The adjective is far more frequent than the verb in modern English.
  • Lavishness (noun) and lavishly (adverb): the abstract noun and manner adverb complete the family: lavishly decorated, lavishly funded.

III. Polysemy: generous versus wasteful

The word is permanently balanced between admiration and censure:

  • The admiring side: lavish equals unstinting, bounteous, princely. Lavished praise, lavished love and lavished hospitality are tokens of generosity and devotion; nobody objects to being the receiver.
  • The censorious side: lavish equals prodigal, wasteful, extravagant beyond reason. Lavished money can be squandered money; a lavish court is a corrupt court. The Latin etymon’s storm-image (see etymologyS2) survives in the feeling that what is lavished is poured out like a flood — and floods, however bountiful, wash things away.
  • Context selects the register: the verb’s object — attention and love (admiring) versus money and resources (censorious) — decides how the abundance is judged. In the source text the balance is ironic: the intelligence is "lavished" (spent generously, even wastefully) upon the perils of the hunt — spent on what is costly and uncertain, not on what would build culture.

IV. Semantic bridge from the root

  • The historical root is "to wash": Latin lavāre gave Old French laver, and the Old French noun lavasse meant "a great rain, a downpour, a flood" — water poured out at once, in excess. From "poured-out water" the Middle English adjective lavas meant "extravagant, profuse," and the 16th-century verb lavished means precisely "poured out like a storm." Every modern use of lavished carries that buried image of water in spate — abundant, generous, and out of proportion to what is needed.

V. Grammar and collocations

  • Patterns: lavish something on/upon somebody (active), be lavished on somebody (passive), lavished with praise (a less standard variant; on/upon is the norm).
  • Fixed favourites: lavish praise (on), lavish attention (on), lavish affection (on), lavish care (on), lavish money or gifts (on), lavished with honours. In business prose, spill out and splash out are casual equivalents; lavished keeps a dignified, slightly old-fashioned air.
  • Near but not the same: to lavish is to give a great deal of what one has; to squander is to waste carelessly; to bestow is to confer formally, not necessarily in excess (see similarWords for the full contrasts).

VI. Why the word fits the sentence

Durant’s point is that the Bedouins’ intelligence is not lacking but misallocated: it is poured out entirely upon securing food — "the perils of the hunt and the tricks of trade" — with none reserved for "the laces and frills, the curtsies and amenities, the arts and comforts" of civilization. Lavished conveys both the profusion of the intelligence and the extravagance of its expenditure, exactly the double judgment the passage needs.
