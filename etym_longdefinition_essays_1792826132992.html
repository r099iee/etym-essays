Essays, the plural of essay (noun, stressed on the first syllable: ESS-ay), means in the source sentence trial attempts, experiments, first exercises: The lesser rings were only ______ in the craft — the Elven-smiths' practice pieces, rehearsals before mastery, trifles that taught the hand for the great work to come. Gandalf's phrasing reaches back past the schoolroom to the word's oldest sense: an essay is literally a try, and the Rings began as tries.

In dominant modern use the noun has specialised into the short prose composition — the school essay, the college application essay, the critical essay — a piece of writing that attempts (the verb still audible inside it) to handle a subject in limited space. Between the craftsman's trial and the student's composition lies one long unbroken meaning: a bounded attempt at something larger, offered for judgement, complete in itself yet confessedly provisional.

Core meaning in detail: As trial, an essay is any first pass: assayists essay metals, engineers essay bridges in model, lovers essay confessions in drafts. As composition, it is non-fiction's most flexible form — from a paragraph of Montaigne to a volume of Orwell — personal in voice, limited in scope, and free in structure, distinguished from the article (which informs) and the treatise (which exhausts) by its willingness to wander and wonder aloud. The verb to essay (stressed on the second syllable: ess-AY) preserves the raw trying — he essayed a smile, she essayed the pass — now literary, but once the word's whole business.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A short prose composition on a subject (primary modern sense, from 1600s): school essays, critical essays, photo essays. The bridge from trial is Montaigne's invention: his Essais (1580) were attempts at self-knowledge — I attempt myself — and the title's modesty became the genre's name, so every student since writes attempts and calls them essays.
  • A trial, attempt, or experiment (Tolkien's sense; oldest sense, from the 15th c.): essays in the craft — practice rings before the Great Rings. The bridge is the original French essai, a trying: before the word ever met a classroom it meant the craftsman's test piece, the assayer's cupel, the lover's first letter — anything put forward to see if it will do.
  • A test of metal purity (specialised, as assay, the doublet spelling): the assay office essays gold. The bridge is metallurgy's precision: the same trying, weighed in grains — essay and assay are one word split by spelling, the laboratory keeping assay while literature kept essay.
  • To attempt or try (verb, literary): to essay a difficult climb, she essayed an apology. The bridge is grammar's memory: the verb never specialised into composition, so it still means bare trying — formal, slightly antique, and exact when try feels too blunt and attempt too heavy.
  • A tentative musical, dramatic, or artistic sketch (extended sense): essays in verse, orchestral essays, a painter's essays in blue. The bridge carries the craftsman's trial into the arts: short pieces that test a style before the symphony, the novel, the canvas — modest, signed, and kept.
  • A student's examination answer (institutional sense): essay questions, essay exams — answers that attempt rather than tick boxes. The bridge runs through schooling: because compositions were called essays, the exam that demands them is an essay paper, and generations learn to attempt under the clock.

Register and connotation: essays is modest and hopeful — the word confesses limits while claiming attention. To call a thing an essay (a ring, a poem, a try) is to ask indulgence: judge it as a beginning. That humility is its charm and its armour, from Montaigne's what do I know to the schoolboy's my essay on holidays to Gandalf's grave judgement on the lesser rings — trifles to their makers, yet, the wizard warns, still dangerous for mortals. Small tries, great shadows.
