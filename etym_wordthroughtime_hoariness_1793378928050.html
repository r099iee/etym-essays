- 8th–10th c. — heroic Germanic landscape: in Old English poetry (such as Beowulf and The Wanderer), hār and hārnes denoted the gray frost of winter, the coats of wolves, ancient boundary stones (hār stān), and venerable battle-veterans (hār hilderinc).
- 12th–14th c. — patriarchal authority: in Middle English, horinesse became associated with biblical patriarchs, holy hermits, and Christian wisdom, celebrated in homilies as the crown of a righteous life.
- 16th–17th c. — renaissance poetic sublime: Edmund Spenser and John Milton used hoar and hoariness to depict primordial forests, mossy battlements, and the ancient snows of mountain peaks.
- 18th–19th c. — romantic aesthetic patina: John Ruskin and romantic aesthetic theorists celebrated hoariness as the visible, radiant patina of time that bestows sublime majesty upon architecture, landscape, and art.
- 20th c. — emergence of the pejorative idiom: while retaining its poetic and botanical prestige, informal English developed a critical pejorative use for stale jokes, trite theatrical tropes, and worn-out political formulas.
- Contemporary — elevated literary gravitas: primarily encountered in serious historical, aesthetic, and poetic contexts to evoke the sacred, enduring dignity of the ancient world.
