The story of concavity begins with a remarkable conceptual paradox in the Proto-Indo-European root *keua- (also reconstructed *ku- or *keu-), which appears to have meant both 'to swell' and 'hollow, arch, vault'. For the earliest speakers the two ideas were not opposites but two faces of a single physical fact: when a material swells outward - an earthen mound, a clay dome, a blister, a swelling belly - it inevitably creates a hollow space within its own curve. An arch, after all, is simultaneously an outward bulge and an inward vault. Some scholars therefore treat 'swell' and 'hollow' as one root; others, including Pokorny's great dictionary, separate them into distinct etyma. The debate matters for our word only in the abstract, because every branch of Indo-European that preserved the root used its 'hollow, vaulted' face to name cavities and vaults.

The fortunes of the root across the family are instructive. In the Indo-Iranian branch the 'swelling' face flourished: Vedic Sanskrit suna 'swollen, grown' shifted to mean 'empty, lacking', giving sunya 'emptiness, the void' - the word that would later serve as the mathematical zero and as a central concept of Buddhist and Hindu philosophy. In Greek the root yielded kuar 'the eye of a needle, a hole, an orifice', preserving the practical sense of a small piercing. The Germanic languages kept both faces in forms meaning 'hole, opening' and the ship's hull (Old Norse hufr, a curved hollow body), while the Celtic branch produced Old Irish cuan 'a harbor', a sheltered hollow that receives ships. But it was the Italic branch, with its engineering cast of mind, that built the precise word that concerns us: cavus.

Latin cavus meant 'hollow, excavated, concave, deeply recessed'. Roman writers used it everywhere - of caves and caverns (both from cavus), of hollow reeds, of the concave bowls of wine cups, of the vaulted interiors of domes and aqueduct arches. To emphasize a thing hollowed out completely or arched inward, Latin added the intensive prefix com-, producing concavus, 'hollowed out entirely, arched inward, concave'. This classical adjective is the direct ancestor of our word. By the Late Latin era the abstract suffix -itas was welded to it to form concavitas, 'the state of being concave', a word at home in the technical prose of the late empire.

The journey into English passed through French. Old French took the adjective as concave 'hollow, curving inward', and Middle French formed concavite 'a hollow place; the state of being deeply concave', with the -ite suffix that descends from Latin -itas. English borrowed the word in the late sixteenth century: concavity is recorded from the 1590s, in the same decades that English scholars were eagerly importing the technical vocabulary of Renaissance science.

  • - Optics and astronomy: from classical and medieval analysis of burning mirrors (concave mirrors were studied by Archimedes, Ptolemy, and Ibn al-Haytham) to the concave lenses of early telescopes and microscopes, the word became a fixed term of the new science.
  • - Anatomy and natural history: physicians and naturalists described the concavities of bones and organs in learned, Latin-flavoured English.
  • - Architecture and engineering: surveyors and engineers computed the concavity of arches, domes, and water conduits, giving the word a measurable, quantitative life.
  • - Modern usage: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, concavity and convexity are everyday, precisely defined properties in calculus, economic theory, lens design, and computer-aided design, where a digital surface is classified by the sign of its curvature.

Finally, the word escaped the laboratory entirely. Because a concavity is a space defined by what it does not contain, it became a natural image of interior emptiness - the hollow of the chest after a loss, the void of a mind eroded by grief. The literary metaphor in the supplied sentence, which pictures dark thoughts stirred in the deep concavity of a troubled mind, stands at the end of four thousand years of the word's drift from swelling earth to vaulted cave to measured curve to image of the hollowed self. It is a striking demonstration of how a term of cold geometry can be made to carry the full weight of human feeling, and how a single Latin stem could travel from the steppes, through the engineering vocabulary of Rome, into the exact sciences of the Renaissance, and finally into the inner chamber of a character's mind.
