hasty is an adjective meaning "done or acting with excessive speed, especially so as to be rash, precipitate, or insufficiently considered; hurried." In the specific literary context of Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Jane Bennet says, "I would not wish to be hasty in censuring anyone; but I always speak what I think." Her meaning is that she refuses to rush to a negative verdict on anyone's character before weighing the evidence; being "hasty in censuring" would be to condemn someone without due thought. In Regency moral vocabulary the word is a warning against exactly the intellectual and ethical flaw that Elizabeth, by contrast, is shown to commit in judging Mr. Darcy — the leap to unexamined conclusions. Austen's use therefore makes "hasty" a key term of her ethics of judgment: patience, evidence, and second thoughts are set against hasty conclusions, and Jane's temperate nature is defined by her refusal to be hurried into blame.

Primary sense. Hasty denotes speed that is excessive for the purpose at hand — movement, speech, or decision undertaken so quickly that it outruns reflection. It almost always carries a pejorative or cautionary shading: not speed as efficiency (that would be quick or swift) but speed as carelessness.

Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Rash or precipitate in judgment. "A hasty decision," "a hasty generalization," "hasty in censuring" — the dominant sense since the 17th century, applied to conclusions reached without due consideration.
  • Quick-tempered; irascible. "A hasty temper," "a hasty word spoken in anger" — speed of emotion, where anger comes upon one too quickly to be controlled. This sense preserves the word's oldest Germanic association with violent fury.
  • Merely fast in motion (now rarer and somewhat formal). "A hasty retreat," "a hasty glance," "hasty footsteps" — speed of movement without necessarily implying blame.
  • Premature; too early. "A hasty marriage," "a hasty departure" — done before the proper time, as in the proverb "Marry in haste, repent at leisure."
  • Fixed culinary sense: hasty pudding. A quick-cooked porridge or pudding of flour or cornmeal, so named because it could be made in haste. The term survives chiefly in dialect and older cookery books, and it is embedded in American folklore through the song "Yankee Doodle."
  • Technical / logical sense. In logic and critical reasoning, the "fallacy of hasty generalization" is drawing a broad conclusion from too few instances — a precise technical use of the everyday word.

Register, collocation, and grammar. Hasty is a gradable adjective (hasty, hastier, hastiest; more/most hasty). It modifies nouns of action, speech, and judgment: "hasty retreat," "hasty decision," "hasty words," "hasty conclusion," "hasty exit," "hasty lunch." It appears in proverbs and maxims ("Haste makes waste," "Marry in haste, repent at leisure") and in the idiom "to be hasty in + gerund." The register spans everyday speech, moral philosophy, legal cautionary language, and literary fiction; in Austen the word belongs to the language of character and prudence, marking the difference between the person who judges slowly and the one who condemns on impulse.
