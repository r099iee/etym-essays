The name of Tolkien's great wolves is one of the oldest words in his legendarium. It descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *wergʰ-, "to press, to oppress, to choke" — a root of violence and weight that appears in Latin urgēre, "to press, drive, urge", ancestor of English urge and urgent, and in Lithuanian var̃gas, "need, distress, misery", as well as, by a separate Germanic formation, in English worry, which once meant literally "to strangle". The student must be careful here: this root is not the same as PIE *werg- "to work", which gives English work and Greek érgon; the two are unrelated homophones of the reconstruction, and the temptation to merge them is a classic false-friend trap. The sound change that matters most in the Germanic branch is the treatment of the laryngeal and the labiovelar: the root's final consonant yields the Germanic g, and the ʰ aspiration disappears in the stem *warg-, producing a word whose hard, guttural shape — the strangler's syllable — is itself a fit vehicle for menace.

From *wergʰ- Germanic formed *wargaz: literally "strangler", hence "criminal" — the man condemned to death by hanging, the choke-man — and then the outcast, the outlaw, the accursed one expelled from human law and, in the old belief, from human name. The word is widespread in the old North. Old English wearg means "criminal, felon"; Old High German warg means the same; and in Old Norse vargr two senses fuse: "outlaw, evildoer" and, by one of the most famous euphemisms in the history of language, "wolf". The wolf was so feared that men would not speak its true name and called it instead the vargr, the strangler, the outcast of the forest — and so thoroughly did the taboo work that in modern Swedish the everyday word for wolf is still varg, the old euphemism having displaced the plain Norse úlfr almost completely. Icelandic kept the plain word, úlfur, beside the poetic vargur, but in the Scandinavian mainland the shadow-word won. This is a classic case of taboo avoidance in action: a feared predator is renamed by an epithet of danger and exile, and the epithet hardens into the ordinary name.

It was precisely this word that J. R. R. Tolkien, professor of Anglo-Saxon and lover of Old Norse, reached for in The Hobbit (1937). His Wargs are not wolves but vargar — ancient, malevolent, quasi-sentient creatures of the Edge of the Wild, allied with goblins, capable of coordinated hunting and of speech; the very naming of them, "for so the evil wolves over the Edge of the Wild were named", performs in miniature the old Norse act of definition, filing a feared species under the natural history of the wilderness. The capitalization marks them as a proper noun, a nation and an army rather than a species of beast, and the plural form — the Wargs — is the form under which they enter the story as a hostile collective, answering their grey chief in a circle about the pine-tree. Tolkien's choice was learned: he knew the Old English wearg, the Old Norse vargr, and the taboo that made the wolf's name unspeakable, and he deliberately freighted his wolf-race with the ancient outlaw's curse.

After Tolkien, the word entered the wider fantasy ecosystem. In the mid-1970s Dungeons & Dragons respelled it worg, giving the game's monster a slightly different shape and a life of its own in tabletop and video-game settings; other games and fictions kept warg, and the word became the standard generic term for the intelligent evil war-beast, distinct from the natural wolf. In the 1990s George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire converted the noun into a verb: "to warg" is to enter and control an animal's mind, and the coinage spread the word even wider through popular culture, so that a television audience now knows "warging" as a form of magical possession. Through all these transformations the ancient core has never dissolved: every use of warg, in whatever spelling or part of speech, still carries the old Germanic charge of the strangler, the outlaw, the accursed one, the wolf one dare not name.

  • Chronology: PIE *wergʰ- (reconstructed); Proto-Germanic *wargaz (reconstructed); Old Norse vargr "outlaw, wolf" (Viking Age); Old English wearg "criminal" (sibling branch); Tolkien's Warg (1937); plural WARGS; the gaming respelling worg (mid-1970s); the verb "to warg" (1990s).
  • Sound and etymology: PIE *wergʰ- (distinct from *werg- "work"); Germanic *wargaz "strangler"; taboo substitution for *wulfaz "wolf" in North Germanic — a semantic event, not an etymological one.
  • Socio-cultural factors: ancient taboo-avoidance of the wolf's name; medieval outlawry; Tolkien's medievalist revival; twentieth-century genre fiction, role-playing games, and television.
  • Literary attestations: Old Norse law and saga texts for vargr; modern Swedish varg "wolf"; Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings (1954–55); Martin, A Song of Ice and Fire (from 1996).
