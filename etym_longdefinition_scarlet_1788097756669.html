1. The core sense: a vivid warm red. Scarlet denotes a brilliant, intense red with a distinctly warm, orange-leaning cast. In the spectrum of English reds it stands between crimson, a cooler and bluer red, and vermilion, a brighter and more yellow-orange red, and in everyday speech it is the "hottest", most theatrical red of all: the red of a cardinal's choir, of a guardsman's tunic, of a ripe tomato, of a lipstick at maximum saturation, of a flag that reads "danger" or "passion" at a hundred metres. In colour science scarlet corresponds to spectral wavelengths of roughly 590 to 630 nanometres, and the common screen approximations of the colour (for example the hex value #FF2400) confirm its orangeward lean: scarlet glows and advances toward the viewer, whereas crimson retreats into violet shadow.

2. The historical core: a superlative cloth. Etymologically, scarlet was not first a colour but a fabric: the supremely expensive woollen broadcloth dyed with kermes insect pigment, and later with American cochineal, that was prized across medieval Europe above every other textile. In medieval accounts "a scarlet" was a length, a garment, or a quality-class of cloth, frequently dyed "in grain", that is, with the fastest and most costly of red dyes, and its wearing was governed by sumptuary laws that restricted it to the nobility, the higher clergy and high civic officers. Royal wardrobe accounts, guild statutes and merchants' ledgers show scarlet acting, in effect, as a branded luxury good, and the colour was at first only the incidental badge of that luxury.

  • Textile and dyeing sense: in textile history and art technology, scarlet is the specific fast red produced by kermes (Kermes vermilio, the oak-scale insect) and later by cochineal (Dactylopius coccus), fixed with tin mordants; "scarlet dyeing" was a distinct and protected urban craft in the medieval Low Countries and northern Italy, and the word survives in dye-house language to this day.
  • Ecclesiastical and ceremonial sense: cardinals are "raised to the scarlet"; the Passion narratives dress Jesus in a scarlet or purple robe in mockery of royalty (Matthew 27:28-31); and scarlet robes survive in university, legal, academic and civic ceremony around the world, where the colour signals high office rather than mere colour preference.
  • Military and livery sense: the scarlet tunics of the British Army, celebrated in the Victorian phrase "the thin red line", scarlet royal liveries, scarlet post-boys, and modern scarlet team colours such as the Manchester United "Red Devils" all mark scarlet as the colour of energy, loyalty and readiness to fight or serve.
  • Medical sense: scarlet fever, scarlatina, takes its name from the fine scarlet rash, the bright "strawberry" tongue and the flushed face of the streptococcal infection; into the nineteenth century the very word carried household terror of quarantine, deafness and child mortality, and it remains a notifiable disease today.
  • Moral and literary sense: the "scarlet woman" (Revelation 17, riding on the beast "arrayed in purple and scarlet") is the archetype of luxurious, flaunting sin, and Nathaniel Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter (1850) fixed scarlet in the modern mind as the colour of adultery and public shame, with Hester Prynne's embroidered "A" sewn in "fine red cloth" and "surrounded with an elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold-thread".
  • Natural-history sense: scarlet tanager, scarlet macaw, scarlet ibis, scarlet pimpernel (both the tiny red flower and Baroness Orczy's elusive 1905 hero of that name), scarlet runner bean, scarlet oak, scarlet fever in nature's reddest plumage: naturalists reserve the word for the most saturated, most flag-like living reds.
  • Modern jargon and popular culture: scarlet is a lipstick and cosmetics shade, a fashion and brand colour, a fashionable given name (Scarlet, Scarlett, made iconic by Gone with the Wind), the signature hue of Marvel's Scarlet Witch, and in Russia the "scarlet sails" of Alexander Grin's romance stand for unattainable beauty and hope arriving on the horizon.

3. The semantic bridge. A single thread runs through every sense: the most costly, the most saturated, the most attention-commanding red. The word shifted from cloth to colour by metonymy once the dye's famous red became the cloth's defining property; then from colour to every vividly red object, from birds and gems to rashes and team strips; and from the cloth's aristocratic associations into moral symbolism, so that scarlet sin is the sin that flaunts itself in crimson brocade, while the modern glamour of a "scarlet" lipstick or sports car is simply the old prestige rebranded as style. Even the medical sense belongs to the same family: the rash is scarlet because it is the reddest red a sick child can show.
