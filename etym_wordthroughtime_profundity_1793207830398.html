- 14th c. — sheer physical depth: seas, gulfs, and pits measured downward, often with awe at what the sounding line finds.
- 15th–16th c. — the inward turn: depth of learning and of the great doctors; the mind itself becomes something that can be deep or shallow.
- 17th c. — philosophy's praise-word: profound inquiries into causes; depth becomes a property of reasoning, and shallowness its public insult.
- 18th c. — irony discovers it: satirists mock profundity that is only dimness, and politicians like Cobbett withhold it as a backhanded compliment for schemers.
- 19th c. — reverence restored: Romantic criticism makes profundity the crown of Shakespeare, Beethoven, and scripture; German Tiefe deepens the cult.
- Today — double-edged: commencement praise for depth of character beside reviewer suspicion of bogus profundity; the oceanic origin survives only in poets.
