- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Latin to Germanic Contact) - Borrowed early from Latin *catillus* / *catīnus* ('deep dish, bowl') into West and North Germanic dialects as *katilaz*, describing a deep metal cooking basin or bowl.
- Old English (c. 8th–11th Century) - Attested as *cetil*, *cetel*, or *cytel*, denoting a large iron or bronze cauldron or pot used over an open hearth for boiling meat, water, and food.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century) - Spelled *ketel* or *chetel*; universally used in domestic inventories, wills, and manorial records to designate the primary multi-purpose open cooking pot of the household hearth.
- 17th to 18th Century (Early Modern to Georgian Era) - With the widespread introduction of tea, coffee, and chocolate into Britain, the word narrows decisively to the *tea-kettle*—a specialized metal pot with a spout, lid, and bail handle; proverbial phrases like 'pot calling the kettle black' emerge.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Ubiquitous in domestic life and literature (as in Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*); concurrent adoption in geology (*kettle hole*) to describe depressions left by melting glacial ice.
- 20th to 21st Century - Revolutionized by electric appliances (the automatic electric kettle); expands in modern urban policing as a controversial tactical verb (*kettling* crowd control).
