The compound overall has two root words, over and all, each descending from an independent Indo-European root, and each requires its own complete derivation chain before the fusion is described.

CHAIN 1 — the component “over”:
Stage 1 — PIE *upér / *upó, “up, over, above” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE): an adverbial base marking position in vertical space, “up from under”.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *uber, “over, above” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE).
Stage 3 — Old English ofer, “over, above, across, beyond” (attested from c. 7th century CE).
Stage 4 — Middle English over / ouer, “over, above, across” (12th–15th century).
Stage 5 — Modern English over (preposition/adverb/prefix) → fuses with all to form overall (see fusion below).

CHAIN 2 — the component “all”:
Stage 1 — PIE *al-, “beyond, other” (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE): a root of “beyondness” whose sense of “what lies beyond every particular” underlies totality.
Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *allaz, “whole, entire, all” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE): the semantic shift from “beyond/other” to “every, the whole”.
Stage 3 — Old English eall, “all, every, whole, entire” (attested from c. 8th century CE).
Stage 4 — Middle English al / all (12th–15th century).
Stage 5 — Modern English all → fuses with over to form overall (see fusion below).

FUSION — the compound:
Stage 1 — Old English phrase ofer eall, “over all, across the whole extent” (attested before the Norman Conquest).
Stage 2 — Middle English overal, adverb “everywhere, altogether, over the whole extent” (14th century).
Stage 3 — adjective “total, comprehensive” (14th–16th centuries: “overall length”, “overall impression”).
Stage 4 — American noun over-alls, bib-and-brace protective trousers (1759).
Stage 5 — British singular noun overall, the one-piece protective working suit covering trunk and limbs (20th century).

Note on debate/nuance: The PIE laryngeal and accent details of *upér are reconstructed and debated in detail (the alternative form *upér vs *upó), but the adverbial chain itself is secure. The noun sense is not a direct continuation of the adverb but a 18th-century English-internal reanalysis: the garment is literally the one worn “over all” the other clothes, re-materialising the abstract word of totality as cloth. The Sanskrit word for “all”, sarvá, belongs to a different PIE root (*solh₂-wó-) and is not related to English all.
