Clipping her one on the ear-hole is a dated British colloquial phrase meaning “striking her a sharp blow on the ear.” It is not a single dictionary headword but an idiom built from three ordinary pieces: the verb clip “to strike smartly,” the pronoun-cum-noun one “a blow,” and the jocular compound ear-hole “ear.” The phrase belongs to the upper-class, public-school, and servant-talk register of early twentieth-century British fiction, above all to the narration of P. G. Wodehouse’s Bertie Wooster.

Word-by-word parsing.

  • Clipping: the present participle of clip in its “strike” sense — a sharp, glancing blow, as if with a clip or cuff. It is not the “cutting” sense, nor the “fastening” sense, though all three share the same spelling.
  • her: the indirect object — the person receiving the blow.
  • one: a colloquial noun meaning “a blow, a hit,” as in “give him one.” It is the numeral used as a pronoun for an unspecified stroke.
  • on the ear-hole: a prepositional phrase specifying the target; ear-hole is a jocular or dialectal word for the ear, literally the opening of the ear.

Meaning and force. The idiom is vivid, physical, and slightly comic. It does not describe a brutal assault; it describes the sort of swift, indignant corrective cuff that a schoolmaster, nanny, or irritated relative might administer. In Wodehouse, the phrase is characteristically followed by a disclaimer: the narrator would have liked to do it, but the code of politeness forbids. The comedy lies in the gap between the impulse and the social restraint.

Register and dating. The phrase is marked as dated upper-class British slang. It belongs with clip round the ear, box someone’s ears, cuff, and smack. It is not current in ordinary speech, and it would sound deliberately archaic or facetious today. It is also associated with a period when corporal punishment of children was more socially acceptable, which gives it a historical and slightly uncomfortable edge.

Grammar and syntax. The construction is verb + indirect object + one + prepositional phrase of place: clip someone one on the ear. The one is not a numeral referring to a single ear; it is the fossilized slang noun for a blow. Variants include clip someone round the ear, clip someone on the ear, give someone a clip on the ear, and box someone’s ears. The phrase is transitive and usually appears in the progressive or infinitive (“prevented me clipping her one”).

The Wodehouse context. P. G. Wodehouse (1881–1975) made this kind of idiom a signature of Bertie Wooster’s narrative voice: public-school slang, music-hall vivacity, and an air of innocent mischief. Bertie frequently reports the urge to clip someone one or to paste them one, only to be restrained by the Wooster code. The phrase in the card’s context — “The exquisite code of politeness of the Woosters prevented me clipping her one on the ear-hole” — is a textbook example: the violence is entirely hypothetical, and the joke is the contrast between the refined code and the schoolboy impulse.

Semantic components and bridges. Clip “strike” is a specialized development of clip “to cut or shear”: a clipping blow is a sharp, cutting stroke. One “a blow” is a nominalization of the numeral, a common slang pattern (“give him one,” “have a go”). Ear-hole is a compound of ear and hole, literally the opening of the ear, used jocularly for the whole ear. The phrase therefore layers three separate semantic developments, each ordinary in itself, into a single comic image.

Modern equivalents. A contemporary speaker might say “clip her round the ear,” “give her a smack on the ear,” “cuff her,” or “box her ears.” American English prefers “box someone’s ears” or “smack someone upside the head.” The Wodehouse phrase is now a period piece, useful for pastiche and for signalling a certain vintage Britishness.

Sensitivity note. The phrase describes hitting a woman or girl and belongs to an era when such correction was treated comically. Modern readers may find the casual violence uncomfortable. In explaining the idiom, it is worth noting both its linguistic interest and its dated, gendered assumptions.
