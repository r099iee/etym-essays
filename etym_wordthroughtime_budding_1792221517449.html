- Late 14th c. (Middle English) — First recorded as a botanical verbal noun (buddynge), denoting the natural swelling, sprouting, and opening of plant buds in early springtime.
- 16th c. (Elizabethan Era) — Expanded into rich poetic and literary imagery by Renaissance dramatists (including Shakespeare and Spenser), metaphorically comparing youthful innocence, beauty, and early love to tender floral buds.
- 17th–18th c. (Enlightenment & Augustan Age) — Codified into the conventional idiom for developing human intellect and vocational talent, establishing phrases such as "budding genius," "budding author," and "budding scholar."
- 19th c. (Victorian Science & Microbiology) — Adopted as a formal scientific term in botany (propagation via grafting), microbiology (asexual blastogenesis in yeast cells), and zoology (outgrowths in hydras and polyps).
- 20th c. to Present — Ubiquitously employed across colloquial speech, cultural journalism, and business reporting to characterize promising novices, emerging economic sectors, and blossoming relationships.
