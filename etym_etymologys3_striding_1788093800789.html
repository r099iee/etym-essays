1. The prehistoric base and the split inside Germanic. The ancestor of stride is reconstructed as PIE *streidh-, glossed ‘to stride, to stretch the legs’ (Watkins, American Heritage Dictionary of Indo-European Roots; Pokorny, Indogermanisches etymologisches Wörterbuch; also followed by Etymonline), plausibly a dʰ-extension of the wider root *ster- ‘stiff, rigid, spread out’ that underlies English stretch, stark and the German strecken family. Two debates surround this stage. First, whether *streidh- is primary or a derivative of *ster- — scholars differ, and the shape of the extension is not recoverable with certainty. Second, and more seriously, the Germanic reflexes split into two meaning-branches: a walking branch (English stride, Middle Dutch striden ‘to stride, to contend’) and a contending branch (Old High German strītan ‘to fight, quarrel, contend’, Old Norse stríða ‘to strive, struggle, fight, withstand’, modern German streiten ‘to quarrel, fight’). Because English and Middle Dutch share the walking sense while the continental languages show fighting, the walking sense is generally judged older, with ‘contending’ developing on the continent from the image of straining or striding against an opponent — but some etymologists separate the two branches into different roots outright. English itself shows no trace of the fighting sense at any date.

2. Old English (before 1100). The verb is strīdan, a class-1 strong verb (strād, stridon, past participle striden), meaning ‘to stride, to walk with long steps’ and ‘to straddle, bestride, mount’ — the mounting sense is well attested of horses, since to ‘stride’ a horse is to swing a leg over it and sit astride. The present participle strīdende is the direct ancestor of the card form striding. Old English also had the related noun stride ‘a long step’ and the prefixed verb bestrīdan ‘to bestride, to stand or sit astride’ (later immortalised by Shakespeare's bestride in Macbeth: ‘bestride our down-fall'n birthdom’). Note the peculiar phonology: OE strīdan had a long vowel that remains long in Modern English stride (/straɪd/), whereas the participle kept the short root vowel (stridden), the same alternation as ride/ridden.

3. Middle English (c. 1200–1500). The verb continues as striden, and the participle/gerund as stridyng(e). Alongside the literal sense, Middle English develops the crossing sense: ‘to stride a brook’ = to clear it in one step. The 13th–14th centuries also multiplied periphrastic tenses (was stridynge), so -ing forms became far more frequent than in Old English; the noun stride ‘a step’ and, by metaphor, ‘extent, reach’ is well established. The word appears in the romances and chronicles of the age for both mortal heroes and gigantic or demonic beings, whose strides are measured as units of demonic distance.

4. Early Modern English (1500–1800). The noun stride becomes a favourite unit of heroic distance and conquest prose (‘within a stride of the city walls’); Milton and the epic tradition use strides for the gait of superhuman beings. From the 17th–18th centuries the transferred phrase to make (great) strides ‘to progress’ is current, and bestride remains a vigorous literary verb. The participle striding keeps both feet planted in the physical world — the King James Bible's translators and the great essayists alike use it of great men and great movements.

5. The nineteenth century. Two idioms crystallise: to hit one's stride (from horse-racing and pacing: reaching the point of maximum easy speed) and to take (something) in one's stride (to absorb difficulty without breaking pace). Victorian adventure fiction fixes striding as the gait of the decisive hero — striding over deserts, striding into rooms, striding past lesser men — so the -ing form carries a muscular, slightly menacing confidence into the modern era.

6. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Three new domains: computing (the array stride — the fixed spacing between successive elements in memory — documented in programming and architecture literature from the 1960s; strided access patterns are central to cache and SIMD engineering), music (stride piano, the Harlem jazz style of the 1920s in which the left hand strides between bass notes and chords), and sports science (stride length, stride frequency). In everyday speech the bare verb now survives mainly in idioms and fitness or business prose, while striding remains the literary default for unstoppable, self-possessed advance — exactly the register of the Tolkien passage that this card serves.
