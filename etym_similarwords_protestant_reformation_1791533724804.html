• The Reformation: Similar because it refers to the exact same 16th-century historical epoch and movement; differs because omitting "Protestant" is the common shortened historical convention within Western historiography, though it can theoretically cause ambiguity with the Catholic Counter-Reformation or earlier medieval reform movements.
  • Counter-Reformation: Similar because both were monumental 16th-century religious movements triggered by the crisis of the Western Church; differs because the Counter-Reformation (or Catholic Reformation) was the internal revival, doctrinal consolidation (Council of Trent), and anti-heresy campaign launched by the Catholic Church to resist and roll back Protestant gains.
  • Radical Reformation: Similar because both rejected papal authority and Catholic scholasticism during the same era; differs because the Radical Reformation (Anabaptists, Spiritualists, Anti-Trinitarians) rejected both Catholicism and the Magisterial Protestant reformers (Luther, Calvin, Zwingli), demanding complete separation of church and state, adult believer's baptism, and communal pacifism.
  • English Reformation: Similar because it was part of the broader break from Rome; differs because the English Reformation was uniquely driven by dynastic politics and statecraft under Henry VIII and Thomas Cromwell, establishing the national Church of England via the Act of Supremacy rather than primarily theological controversy.
  • Schism: Similar because both denote a profound formal division within Christian ecclesiastical communion; differs because schism is a generic canonical term for institutional separation without necessarily altering core dogma (such as the East-West Schism of 1054 or the Western Schism of 1378), whereas the Protestant Reformation fundamentally overturned core dogmatic theology.
  • Protestant Revolt: Similar because it denotes the same historical break; differs because "revolt" is a loaded historiographical label historically preferred by Catholic polemicists to characterize the movement as an unlawful political and theological rebellion rather than a legitimate moral reformation.
  • Calvinism: Similar because it is a primary branch of the Protestant Reformation; differs because Calvinism denotes a specific Reformed theological system based on John Calvin's Institutes, characterized by double predestination and covenant theology, representing only one faction within the broader Reformation.
  • Lutheranism: Similar because it was the inaugural theological movement of the Protestant Reformation; differs because Lutheranism represents the specific ecclesiastical and confessional tradition founded upon Martin Luther's teachings and the Augsburg Confession, distinct from the Reformed or Anglican traditions.
