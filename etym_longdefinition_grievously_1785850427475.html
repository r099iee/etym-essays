GRIEVOUSLY (adverb): in a manner that causes severe suffering, serious harm, great sorrow, or grave damage; to a degree that is heavy, oppressive, or deeply wounding. The word is the adverbial form of grievous, and its modern core meaning is "severely, extremely (and with harmful or sorrowful consequences)". Grievously modifies verbs of injury, loss, error, and sin: to be grievously wounded is to be hurt so badly that the injury is life-threatening; to be grievously wronged is to suffer an injustice so deep it cannot be shrugged off; to err grievously is to make a mistake with ruinous results. Unlike plain intensifiers such as very or extremely, grievously always implies a heavy consequence — physical, legal, moral, or emotional — and retains a residue of its ancient meaning "heavily, weightily". It is a word of solemn or formal register, at home in legal documents, historical narrative, religious prose, and dignified literary style. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Physical harm (the primary modern sense): grievously wounded, grievously injured, grievously burned — severe, often life-threatening bodily injury. In this sense the adverb is standard in military and medical reportage.
  • Legal usage: the noun phrase grievous bodily harm (GBH) is a statutory category in English and Commonwealth criminal law (codified in Britain by the Offences against the Person Act 1861), denoting serious physical injury inflicted unlawfully; grievously is used in legal and journalistic contexts to specify the level of harm or injury involved.
  • Moral and religious usage: to sin grievously, to offend grievously, to fall grievously — serious moral transgression, a heavy sin as opposed to a venial one; the word is traditional in confessional and homiletic prose.
  • Emotional and psychological injury: grievously wronged, grievously hurt, grievously disappointed — deep emotional damage or profound injustice, the adverbial counterpart of the verb to grieve.
  • Financial, social, or structural damage: a kingdom grievously burdened by taxes (as in the context sentence), an economy grievously damaged, a community grievously divided — heavy, oppressive, long-lasting harm to a collective body.
  • Intensifier of error or miscalculation: to misjudge, miscalculate, or underestimate grievously — to err to a disastrous degree, with consequences out of proportion to a simple mistake. This is a common modern literary use.
  • Obsolete-adjacent "heavily, weightily" sense: the oldest English sense of the adverb, "in a heavy or oppressive manner, weightily", survives only in historical and poetic echo; the modern word has redirected that weight toward suffering and harm.
HISTORICAL BRIDGE: Grievously descends through a chain of weight. Its ultimate ancestor is the Indo-European root for "heavy", which produced Latin gravis, "heavy, weighty, severe". From gravis came the Latin verb gravare, "to load, burden, oppress", and the noun gravitas, "heaviness, dignity"; Old French inherited these as grever, "to burden, oppress, injure", and grief, "heavy, burdensome, sorrowful", and from grief's stem English took the adjective grievous. The semantic development is a steady transfer from the physical to the moral and emotional: first the literal weight of a load, then the figurative weight of oppression and sorrow, then the severity of injury and harm. When someone is grievously burdened or grievously wounded, the modern word is still saying, beneath its surface, that a weight has been laid upon them — a burden of pain, loss, or injustice too heavy to bear. The adverb in -ly simply carries this loaded adjective into verbal territory, so that it modifies the action itself: to act in a heavy, harmful, sorrow-laden manner. REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS:
  • grievously + past participle of harm: grievously wounded, grievously injured, grievously burned, grievously maimed.
  • grievously + verbs of moral or legal transgression: sin grievously, offend grievously, err grievously, wrong grievously.
  • grievously + verbs of burden or loss: burdened grievously, taxed grievously, impoverished grievously, bereaved grievously.
  • The legal collocation grievous bodily harm is fixed and technical; the adverb does not normally appear in the statute itself but is used in commentary.
  • Register: formal, literary, solemn; in modern speech the word is comparatively rare, chosen for dignity or irony. Near-synonyms by register: severely (neutral, clinical), gravely (formal, medical), fatally (death-dealing), sorely (mild, older), deeply (emotional).
