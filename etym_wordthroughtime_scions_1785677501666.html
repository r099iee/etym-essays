- [14th c., entry into English] - Adopted from Old French as a purely horticultural term: a scion is a shoot or twig cut from one plant to be grafted onto another. Exclusively botanical, practical farming language with no human sense.
- [16th-17th c.] - The figurative sense emerges: a scion of a family or race is a descendant grafted onto the family stock. Applied first to princely and noble offspring, the word acquires its dynastic, aristocratic warmth and its botanical-poetic colouring.
- [Modern usage] - The horticultural sense survives in grafting and fruit-tree cultivation, while the dominant everyday sense is genealogical and lofty: a scion is the heir or offspring of a distinguished house. Register shifts from neutral botany to formal, admiring, slightly old-fashioned flourish.
