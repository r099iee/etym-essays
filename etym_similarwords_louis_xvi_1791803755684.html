• Louis XV: Similar because he is the immediately preceding king of the same name and dynasty, and his reign shaped the court Louis inherited; differs because Louis XV reigned from 1715 to 1774, was known as le Bien-Aimé, and died on the throne rather than under the guillotine.
  • Louis XVII: Similar because he is the next numeral in the sequence and Louis XVI's son; differs because he never reigned—he died in prison in 1795 at the age of ten—and his title was proclaimed by royalists rather than exercised.
  • Louis XVIII: Similar because he is Louis XVI's younger brother and the next king actually to reign; differs because he took the throne only in 1814, after the Revolution and Napoleon, and his numbering skips the dead dauphin.
  • Louis-Auguste: Similar because it is the same person's birth name, used before 1774; differs because it names the private individual and the duc de Berry, without the dynastic and constitutional weight of the regnal title.
  • Louis Capet: Similar because it denotes the same man during his trial; differs because it was a republican re-naming designed to deny his kingship, invoking Hugues Capet and reducing a king to a family surname.
  • the last king of France: Similar because it identifies Louis XVI by his historical position; differs because it is a descriptive epithet rather than a name, and it is imprecise—he was technically king of the French from 1791 and the Bourbons returned in 1814.
  • Louis le Dernier: Similar because it is another name for the same king; differs because this polemical nickname (“Louis the Last”) was coined to assert that the monarchy was finished, and it carries a republican judgement absent from the neutral numeral.
  • the martyr king: Similar because it is a title applied to Louis XVI by royalists and traditionalist Catholics; differs because it is confessional and evaluative, asserting sanctity rather than recording a reign.
  • Louis XVI style: Similar because it uses the same words; differs because the attributive phrase denotes a decorative period (neoclassicism, c. 1774–1792) and not the king, and it survives in the antiques trade as a purely aesthetic label.
