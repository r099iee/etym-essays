The name. No field of the Memnon story is certain at the root. The Greeks themselves read the name Μέμνων as a word of the men- family — the root of μέμονα, "I am minded, am resolved", and μέμνημαι, "I remember" — and so took "Memnon" to mean "the steadfast, mindful one", a name fit for a hero who stands his ground. Modern scholarship is more cautious: the -mn- cluster is rare in ordinary Greek vocabulary and smells of the pre-Greek substrate, some linguists have floated an Anatolian (Luwian) source for the name — pointing to its syllable shape and the hero's Eastern home — and Robert Beekes' etymological dictionary simply lists the name as of unexplained origin. The truth may be that the hero's very name outlives the language that borrowed him, and that the man and the word both arrived in Greece out of the East.

The epic figure. The story is fixed by the 7th century BCE in the Aethiopis, the lost epic of the Trojan cycle: after Hector's fall, when Troy is at its darkest, Memnon comes from the distant south — Ethiopia, at the rim of the world — at the head of his people. He is the son of Eos, the Dawn, and of Tithonus, the Trojan prince from whom the Dawn had begged immortality without eternal youth; the whole family belongs to the morning. At Troy, Memnon kills Antilochus, and Nestor's grief is loud in the Odyssey, which remembers the fallen "son of the fair-throned Dawn" (Book 11), a phrase the ancient commentators took for Memnon himself. Then Achilles kills Memnon in the duel that Greek vase-painters never tired of showing; Zeus grants the Aethiopian hero life after death, and his cult is bound to his tomb. Virgil, in the frieze of Carthage's temple in the Aeneid (1.489), names "the arms of black Memnon" — the oldest fixed picture of the hero's colour in Latin — and Ovid (Metamorphoses 13) turns his pyre into the Memnonides, the fighting birds that rise from his ashes year after year. The image is constant: a great, dark, noble prince of the dawn, mourned by light itself.

The statue takes the name. When Greek and Roman travellers sailed up the Nile they found at Thebes two colossal seated statues — the mortuary colossi of Pharaoh Amenhotep III, carved in quartzite in the 14th century BCE, some 18 metres tall, facing the river across the plain. Earthquake damage in antiquity shattered the upper body of the northern colossus; thereafter, each dawn, as the fractured stone warmed in the sun, it emitted a brief resonant tone. The Greeks called the singer "Memnon", extending the hero's name to the monument, and understood the sound as his cry of greeting to his mother the Dawn. The epigraphic record is a marvel and a gift to history: Strabo, visiting around 25 BCE, describes the statue; Germanicus heard it in 19 CE (Tacitus, Annals 2.61); Pausanias likened it to the breaking of a lyre-string; and more than a hundred Greek and Latin inscriptions — set up by Roman soldiers, prefects and tourists — record the miracle, morning by morning. In November 130 CE, the court poet Julia Balbilla, riding with Hadrian and Sabina, inscribed four epigrams on the colossus's leg, celebrating Memnon's song. Around 199 CE Septimius Severus had the broken colossus restored; the rubble that had sung was rebuilt, and the song was heard no more. The "Memnonium" of the travellers — the great Ramesside temple west of Thebes — completed the confusion of hero, pharaoh and god in the Roman imagination of Egypt.

The modern name. Through the Middle Ages, Memnon survived in Latin learning as a curiosity: an Ethiopian prince in Homer's company. The great Egyptomania of the 17th–19th centuries restored him to the world: travellers, savants and the Napoleonic expedition fixed the "Colossi of Memnon" on the map of Nile tourism, and the two giants of Amenhotep III still stand on the Theban plain, among the most painted and photographed monuments of Egypt. In letters, Memnon remains what the myth made him — the noble foreign champion whose dawn-song is grief and joy in one — and Wilde's version, enthroned at the Second Cataract, watching the stars and crying out at the morning star, is the Romantic last chapter: myth, statue and sunrise compressed into a god of the Nile.

  • Eras: pre-Greek or Anatolian name → epic cycle (7th c. BCE) → Homeric allusion → Greco-Roman Egypt (the vocal colossus) → medieval Latin learning → Egyptomania → Wilde's Nile god.
  • Semantic drift: a warrior-hero of Troy → the singer of a broken colossus → a god of the Nile's sunrise → a tourist landmark and classical allusion.
  • Shifts in perception: from Greek hero to Egyptian wonder to Romantic deity to modern landmark — the same name, reborn at every turning of the Nile's story.
