- Medieval — the spoken word: Old French parole means speech, talk, a formal promise given by mouth.
- 17th c. — the soldier's honour: a captured officer gives his parole not to fight or flee, and lives free on that promise.
- 18th–early 19th c. — prisoners of war at large: whole armies of captives live on parole in market towns, trusted by signature alone.
- Late 19th c. — the reform invention: Elmira and the new parole boards turn the gentleman's promise into a supervised system for common convicts.
- 1916 — Saussure borrows it: parole becomes the linguist's term for individual speech against collective langue.
- Today — hearings and hashtags: parole means boardrooms, risk scores, and licence conditions — half mercy, half monitoring — with the wry figurative sense of behaving as if watched.
