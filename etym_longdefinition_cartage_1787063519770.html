Cartage is a noun of the "-age" family (like postage, storage, drayage) meaning primarily "the conveyance of goods by cart" — the physical act of hauling merchandise overland in a wheeled vehicle — and, by a natural extension, "the charge or fee paid for such conveyance." The word belongs to the vocabulary of trade, transport, and logistics, and it has kept this narrow commercial frame for its entire modern life.

Primary sense: the act of transporting goods by cart

In its concrete sense, cartage denotes the business or operation of moving goods by cart, wagon, dray, or similar wheeled carrier. Historically this meant horse-drawn vehicles: sacks of grain, barrels of ale, bales of cloth, coal, and produce moved from farm to market, from dock to warehouse, and from warehouse to shop under the heading of cartage.

  • The activity: carting, haulage by cart; a "cartage service" or "cartage firm" performs this work.
  • The journey scale: cartage typically implies short-distance, terminal-to-terminal movement — the "last leg" between a port or railhead and a local destination — rather than long-haul transit.
  • The trade context: the word arose in an age of customs tolls, wharfage, and warehousing, when each stage of a goods journey had its own named charge.

Secondary sense: the charge or fee for carting

From the act, the word shifted by the standard "-age" logic (the suffix builds nouns of action, result, or charge — compare postage, towage, portage) to name the money paid for the service. A bill of lading or freight invoice could itemise:

  • cartage charges — the amount invoiced for moving the goods;
  • cartage agents — firms or individuals who arrange and perform such hauling;
  • inland cartage / local cartage — the technical phrases in shipping and insurance law distinguishing the road-leg from the ocean or rail leg.

Domain-specific and technical usage

  • Logistics and freight forwarding: "cartage" survives as an operational term for local pickup-and-delivery around terminals; a "cartage company" in some markets is still an ordinary business type.
  • Marine and customs law: historical and modern documents distinguish "wharfage" (use of the wharf), "portage" (carrying between waterways), and "cartage" (overland hauling); the distinctions matter in liability and insurance clauses.
  • British commercial English: in Britain, "cartage" (also "carting") retained its everyday use for goods transport longer than in North America, where it now sounds period-specific.
  • Bureaucratic and insurance register: "cost of cartage," "cartage and storage" remain standard headings in old-style accounts and in some modern insurance schedules.

Related words and semantic boundaries

  • carriage — same "-age" structure and "charge for transport" sense, but also a passenger vehicle and a way of bearing oneself; far wider.
  • haulage — the modern near-equivalent for road/truck transport and its fee; broader, machine-age, and free of the horse-and-cart image.
  • drayage — the technical successor for dock/railhead hauling by dray; narrower and specialist.
  • freight — the goods themselves (or the charge), not specifically the carting act.
  • portage, towage, lighterage — sister "-age" charges for carrying by hand, tow, or barge.

Current status and connotation

Today cartage is a specialist and somewhat dated word. It has not fallen out of use — it remains live in logistics, moving-and-storage contracts, and legal boilerplate — but it has a distinctly traditional, horse-and-cart flavour that haulage and freight do not. Its meaning has been remarkably stable since the 17th century: the same two senses, "the act of carting" and "the fee for carting," have carried through industrialisation, motorisation, and containerisation without drift, a fossil from the commercial vocabulary of the age of the waggoner.
