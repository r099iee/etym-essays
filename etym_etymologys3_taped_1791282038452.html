Taped is the past form of tape, a word that began as a humble textile and became, through adhesive chemistry and electronics, a universal metaphor for fixing and recording. Its deep origin is obscure, its modern story exceptionally clear.

Old English tæppe meant a narrow woven strip or fillet used to bind, tie, or edge garments, from a Germanic base *tapp- whose wider connections are unknown. It is not from Latin; attempts to link it to PIE *dab- or *tep- remain speculative, and major dictionaries mark it origin unknown. What matters is continuity: from Beowulf onward, tape binds. Middle English tape still means inkle, braid, or ribbon for lacing and measuring — the tapeline used by tailors gives us the word tape-measure.

  • Textile centuries: tape as woven fillet for tying, trimming, and sealing. Red tape for official documents, first as literal pinkish ribbon binding legal bundles, later as bureaucracy itself. To tape meant to bind with this ribbon or to edge with it.
  • Adhesive revolution: in the 19th century, surgeons plaster tape and electricians friction tape coat fabric with adhesive. By the 1920s-30s, cellulose Scotch tape creates transparent sticky tape. The verb to tape now means to seal, mend, or fasten with this strip: taped box, taped ankle, taped windows against blast.
  • Magnetic era: in the 1930s-50s German Magnetophon and American reel-to-reel use iron-oxide coated paper and plastic tape to store sound. To tape means to record: to tape a show, taped interview, taped out for broadcast. The noun tape spawns tape recorder, tape deck, ticker tape, and later videotape.
  • Figurative completion: to have something taped, and in this card taped out, means to have it fixed, settled, arranged so it will not come loose. The image blends sealed parcel and finished recording: nothing will shift.

Phonetically tape is stable: Old English tæppe /tæp.pe/ shortens and lengthens to Middle English tape /taːp(ə)/, then Great Vowel Shift raises it to modern /teɪp/. Inflection is regular: tape, taped /teɪpt/, taping. RP keeps diphthong /eɪ/ clear, General American identical.

Socio-culturally tape tracks technology. Red tape symbolised state power, sticky tape symbolised household repair, magnetic tape symbolised mass media. To say everything is taped out in a Wodehouse story sits at the adhesive-plus-planning intersection: everything is tied up, measured, and settled, like parcels sealed and labelled. Police tape, crime-scene tape, ticker-tape parades, and mixtapes all extend the same strip idea.

Literary and technical attestations are abundant: 19th-century surgical manuals order wounds taped, early radio guides speak of taped programmes, Watergate makes taped conversations infamous. The debated only point is the ultimate Germanic root; from Old English onward every stage — woven fillet to adhesive strip to recording medium to fixed arrangement — is documented in trade catalogues, patents, and broadcasts.
