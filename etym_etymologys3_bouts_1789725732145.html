The ancestry of BOUTS bends — literally. Its ultimate root is PIE *bheug- to bend, bow, curve, a prolific base documented by Pokorny and Beekes in Greek pheugein to flee by bending away, Latin fugere to flee, German biegen to bend, and English bow, bight, bought, bucket in older senses. Proto-Germanic *bug- continues the root as to bend or stoop, giving Old English bugan to bow, bend down, submit, with past beah and participle bogen. The noun family denotes the result of bending: a loop, coil, or ring.

Middle English bought, bowght, bight means a bend, fold, or loop in rope, a coil, and by extension the bend of a river, a bay (bight), and a circuit of the plough around a field. Agricultural use is decisive: a bout-row is one going-and-returning of the plough, a full turn. From field circuit to work circuit to fighting circuit the path is short: to go a bout is to take one’s turn, whether at ploughing, wrestling, or drinking. The spelling bout appears in the 1540s as a shortened dialectal variant of bought, with -ought simplified to -out as in parallel forms.

  • Contest sense: sixteenth- and seventeenth-century fencing and wrestling manuals use bout for a set-to, a trial of skill. Shakespeare’s contemporaries speak of a bout at cudgels. The sense stabilises in pugilism: a boxing bout is a scheduled encounter fought in rounds, each round itself a mini-circuit of advance and retreat. OED citations run continuously from 1548 onward.
  • Work and indulgence senses: a bout of work preserves the ploughing turn; a drinking bout preserves the round of cups passed around company. Both keep boundedness and return: one goes round and stops. Nineteenth-century novels use bout of industry and bout of dissipation without any fighting frame.
  • Medical transfer: Victorian medicine maps combat onto disease: the patient meets fever round by round, suffers a bout of influenza, endures bouts of rheumatism. The metaphor dignifies endurance while admitting recurrence. Early bouts of arthritis in the Baker sentence is a mature example: arthritis as lifelong opponent met first in skirmishes, later in rout.
  • Phonetics and plural: singular bout /baut/ rhymes with out, plural adds regular /s/ giving bouts /bauts/. Spelling retains -ou- of the shortened bought, distinguishing it from boot, boat, and bought itself. No irregular inflection survives.

From bent rope to bent plough-line to boxing ring to sickbed, bouts has travelled by steady analogy of the round: things that go around and come back deserve one word, whether rope, plough, fist, bottle, or pain. The arthritis sentence leans on the full journey, letting schoolboy pains sound like opening rounds of a losing fight.

Lexicographically the path is unusually clean: every OED citation from circuit of the field through trial of arms to spell of sickness shows the round-idea intact, with no competing French or Latin overlay to muddy the native Germanic line. That transparency lets modern writers pun deliberately, sending patients into bouts and fighters into bouts of flu, knowing readers will feel the shared circle underneath.
