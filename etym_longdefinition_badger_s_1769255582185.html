badger’s — In the moment of awakening in Prince Caspian, Caspian sees “the face that was looking into his own” and registers that “it was not a man’s face but a badger’s.” The possessive works here with perfect economy: it assigns the face to a species — these are the striped, blunt-snouted features of a badger — while the contrast with “a man’s face” carries the scene’s central wonder. This is Trufflehunter, a Talking Beast: the face is recognisably a badger’s, yet it is “larger and friendlier and more intelligent than the face of any badger he had seen before.” The word thus performs a double movement, first anchoring the creature firmly in the natural world and then being stretched by the extraordinary intelligence the narrator insists upon. The tone is one of dawning, half-terrified wonder — the shock of a rational mind looking out of a wild animal’s face.

Primary sense. As a possessive form, badger’s means “of the badger, belonging to or characteristic of a badger” — the possessive singular of the noun badger. A badger is a stout-bodied, short-legged, nocturnal burrowing mammal of the family Mustelidae (the weasel family), the common European badger being Meles meles: a grey, black, and white animal with a distinctive black-and-white striped face, a strong flattened body, powerful digging claws, and a long, bushy tail. The word here is an elliptical possessive: “but a badger’s [face]” — the noun being possessed is understood from context, a construction natural in English and used to avoid repeating “face.”

Grammar and usage of the possessive. The apostrophe-s genitive is one of the oldest features of English, the remnant of the Old English genitive ending -es (as in stānes, “of a stone”). With singular nouns ending in s, usage wavers: “Badger’s” (as here) is the standard written possessive for the singular animal, while the plural possessive “badgers’” marks ownership by more than one. In speech, “badger’s” and “badgers’” are indistinguishable, so the meaning is carried by context; in the Caspian sentence the singular possessive is unmistakable because the reference is to a single face.

Polysemy & secondary senses. Although “badger’s” is a single grammatical form, the noun it derives from carries a rich web of meanings that the possessive inherits:

  • The animal itself: the burrowing mammal, valued in folklore as wise, tenacious, and solitary; its distinctive striped face is the image at work in the Narnian scene.
  • The verb “to badger”: a separate word derived from the noun via the 18th-century sport of badger-baiting — to badger someone is to pester, nag, or harass them relentlessly, like dogs baiting a badger. The possessive “badger’s” can thus indirectly evoke this bothersome, persistent quality.
  • Badger-skin / fur trade: historically badger pelts, bristles, and fur were used for brushes (shaving brushes), clothing, and leather; “badger’s” brushes survive as a term in shaving culture.
  • Sports and mascots: the badger is a common emblem of tenacity; the University of Wisconsin’s sports teams are the Badgers, and “Buckey”-the-badger-style mascots appear in many regions.
  • Older senses: in Tudor and Stuart English, a “badger” was also a kind of licensed corn-dealer or hawker (from the verb to badge, “to carry”), so an Elizabethan “badger’s” could mean a dealer’s, though this sense is now obsolete and unrelated in origin to the animal.

Register and style. The possessive is entirely ordinary English, at home in any register. What gives it its special force in the Caspian passage is its combination of the ordinary and the uncanny: a perfectly normal English genitive attached to a perfectly wild referent. The brevity of “but a badger’s” — a bare possessive with its noun omitted — mimics the brevity of perception itself, the mind grasping the animal before it can finish the sentence, and then catching itself on the impossible intelligence of the face.

Historical bridge. The semantic bridge runs from a burrowing animal of the weald to a whole set of cultural meanings: the tenacity of the cornered animal (the verb), the folk-wisdom of the badger as old and canny (Trufflehunter is exactly this), and, more recently, a beloved and protected native creature of conservation campaigns and children’s literature. The possessive form carries all of these associations with it wherever it appears.
