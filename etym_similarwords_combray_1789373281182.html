• Illiers: Similar because both refer to the exact same physical town in Eure-et-Loir that forms the geographic center of Proust's childhood; differs because Illiers is the actual, historical municipal reality, whereas Combray is the fictionalized, aestheticized literary realm created in *In Search of Lost Time*.
  • Balbec: Similar because both are major fictional French locations in Proust's masterpiece that represent formative stages in the narrator's awakening; differs because Balbec is a coastal seaside resort on the English Channel embodying social ambition and sea imagery, whereas Combray is an inland agrarian village embodying childhood, family ritual, and pastoral memory.
  • Yoknapatawpha: Similar because both are legendary fictional counties or towns created by modernist novelists that synthesize regional reality into mythic universal art; differs because William Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha County embodies the violent, tragic racial history of the American South, whereas Proust's Combray embodies the intimate psychological mechanics of European bourgeois memory and aesthetic awakening.
  • Macondo: Similar because both are celebrated fictional villages created by twentieth-century literary masters to explore time, memory, and ancestry; differs because Gabriel García Márquez's Macondo in *One Hundred Years of Solitude* is defined by magical realism, political upheaval, and cyclical solitude in the Colombian tropics, whereas Combray is grounded in Belle Époque French psychological realism and sensory memory.
  • Wessex: Similar because both are semi-fictionalized literary territories based on real regional geography created by major novelists; differs because Thomas Hardy's Wessex is an entire historic region in southwest England emphasizing fatalistic agrarian struggle against modernization, whereas Combray is a tightly focused provincial village focusing on the resurrection of individual childhood memory.
