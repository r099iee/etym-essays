The biography of SUMPTUOUSNESS begins with one of the smallest and oldest verbs in the Indo-European family: the root *h₁em- (also written *em-), "to take, to distribute." The root is securely attested across the family — Latin emere "to take, to buy," Lithuanian im̃ti "to take," Old Church Slavonic jęti "to take," Old Prussian īmt — and it is sometimes compared with the Hittite ēpzi "he seizes," though that equation is debated among specialists and is best left unasserted. In Latin, emere did not primarily mean a commercial purchase; it meant to take possession of something. Buying is, etymologically, taking — a fact that matters for the whole later development of this word, because everything sumptuous is ultimately something that has been taken up and consumed.

In Latin the root was made enormously productive through prefixing. Sumere joins sub-, "up from under, into one's hand," to emere, giving "to take up, to take upon oneself, to lay out money, to spend." From the past participle sumptus came the noun "expense, cost, outlay" — literally what has been taken up and used — and this became a pillar of Roman fiscal language (sumptus publicus, "public expenditure"). The adjective sumptuōsus, "costly, expensive, lavish," then gave the Late Latin abstract noun sumptuōsitās, "costliness," which travelled into Old French as sumptuosité and sumptueux, and into English as sumptuous in the late fifteenth century. The final step is purely native: English attached the Germanic noun-forming suffix -ness, of ancient Old English descent, to the French-derived adjective, producing sumptuousness in the sixteenth century — a characteristic Renaissance hybrid in which a Latin soul wears a Germanic suffix. The word has been in continuous literary use ever since.

The semantic drift is a study in the elevation of money into beauty. At every stage the root keeps its economic core — sumptus is "expense" — and the English word has never lost the sense of great cost. What changed was the value placed on that cost. In Rome, sumptuōsus was already double-edged: the sumptuary laws (leges sumptuariae) of the Republic and Empire tried to restrain private luxury at feasts and funerals, so the adjective carried a whiff of excess even as it described magnificence. That double tone — admiration for the splendour, reproach for the spending — came down the centuries. In the Middle Ages and Renaissance, "sumptuous" described the pomp of kings and the Church; in the moralising literature of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, "sumptuousness" was the vice of the spendthrift and the pride of the ostentatious rich, so that an age of frugal virtue could both marvel at a sumptuous palace and censure the sumptuousness that built it.

Socio-culturally, the word has always been aristocratic. It belongs to courts, cathedrals, great houses, state banquets and ceremonial interiors — the settings where expense is not a practical necessity but a language of rank. To describe something as sumptuous is to place it at the top of a social as well as an economic scale: sumptuousness is the material proof of power, the wealth that can be seen and walked through. This is precisely why Hawkes chooses it in Hong lou meng. Dai-yu's tour of the Jia mansion is a journey through graduated wealth, and the word is reserved for the moment when the gradient peaks: the main inner hall whose sumptuousness exceeds everything she has seen that day. The word measures both the building and the girl — the building's rank in the house, and the girl's dawning recognition of where she now stands.

In modern English the moral reproach has faded almost to nothing and the admiring sense dominates: "sumptuous" and "sumptuousness" now name costly splendour with a note of old-fashioned grandeur, the wealth of an heirloom interior rather than the flash of modern money. The word has also widened slightly, attaching to non-material things — a sumptuous style of prose, a sumptuous spread of colours — where the root idea of expense becomes richness of effect. Yet even here the etymological memory survives: a sumptuous style is one that has spent lavishly, on words, on ornament, on elaboration, exactly as a sumptuous feast has spent on food. The word is thus a living fossil of its own history, carrying within it the Roman accountant's ledger, the Renaissance court's display, the Victorian moralist's reproach, and the modern connoisseur's admiration — all folded into a single abstract noun of dazzling costliness.
