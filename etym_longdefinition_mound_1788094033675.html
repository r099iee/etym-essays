Mound is a noun meaning a raised mass of earth, stones, sand or other material — a hillock, a heap, an artificial bank, or a natural rounded rise in the ground. In the sentence "they lifted old treasures from the mound or from [the walls]," the word names the great heaps of gold in Smaug's hoard: treasure piled up like a hill of earth, so that the dwarves dig it out and lift it from the mound as if mining a small mountain of wealth. The word spans the whole scale of raised things, from an anthill to a burial barrow to a baseball field, and its history runs from hedges to earthworks to heaps of gold.

Primary senses.

  • A raised heap of earth or stones: the general sense — "a mound of earth", "a mound of rubble", "an ant mound"; anything heaped up into a rounded pile.
  • A natural hillock or small rise: a low, rounded hill, smaller than a hill proper — "they stood on a grassy mound overlooking the river".
  • An artificial earthwork: a bank of earth raised by human hands for defence, burial, or ceremonial use — burial mounds (barrows, tumuli), defensive mounds (the motte of a motte-and-bailey castle), and the platform mounds of the Mississippian culture.
  • A heap of valuables (by extension): treasure piled up — the dragon-hoard sense of the context; also "a mound of paperwork", "a mound of data" — any large piled quantity, the heap-image transferred to objects, work, and information.

Domain senses.

  • Archaeology: the mound is the archaeologist's landmark — burial mounds, temple mounds, and the great earthworks of the "Mound Builders" of the Ohio and Mississippi valleys (a term coined in the 19th century); excavation of a mound is standard fieldwork.
  • Military engineering: a mound or rampart of earth defends a position; the motte — the earthen mound of a medieval castle — is the mound par excellence of fortification.
  • Baseball: the pitcher's mound — the raised earth (now rubber and dirt) in the centre of the diamond from which the pitcher throws; the term became standard in the early 20th century and is now the most familiar sporting use of the word.
  • Geology and gardening: glacial mounds, sand mounds, molehills; gardeners raise mounds for planting; "to mound" (the verb) means to heap up ("mound the soil around the roots").
  • Fantasy and literature: the treasure-mound of the dragon's hoard is a stock image — gold piled so high it is spoken of as a mound; Tolkien's dwarves lift treasure "from the mound", and the word carries the burial-mound chill of ancient riches.

Secondary and figurative uses.

  • A piled quantity: "a mound of dishes", "a mound of snow", "a mound of work" — the heap-image applied to any large accumulation; informal but fully standard.
  • The burial association: because so many ancient mounds are graves, the word carries a faint undertone of the barrow — hidden dead, buried treasure, ancient kings; fantasy prose exploits exactly this undertone.
  • The verb to mound: "to heap up" (18th–19th century); "mounded" — raised in a heap; also "to mound up" in gardening and earthworks.
  • Derivatives: "mound-builder" (the prehistoric earthwork peoples; also a general term for mound-raising cultures), "moundy" (rare adjective, full of mounds).

Why the word means all this. The word's earliest recorded sense (c. 1500, Scots) is "a hedge or fence" — an enclosure that protects — and its history moves through "defensive earthwork" to "any raised bank of earth" to "a hillock" to "any heaped pile". The semantic bridges are all the same shape: a raised line or heap of earth. Because earthworks protected settlements, mound gained the defensive colour; because burial mounds are the most famous ancient earthworks, it gained the grave's undertone; because treasure and earth are both heaped, the dragon's gold became a mound. Every modern sense — the pitcher's mound, the ant mound, the mound of paperwork — is that single image of raised, heaped material, still carrying the echo of hedges and ramparts from its first recorded century.
