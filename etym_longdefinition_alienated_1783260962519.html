In the opening sentence of Macaulay's History of England, alienated is the hinge on which the whole narration turns: “I shall recount the errors which, in a few months, alienated a loyal gentry and priesthood from the House of Stuart.” The word is used as a transitive verb in the simple past, and its object is a whole social class that had been, until “a few months” before, impeccably loyal. The nuance is deliberately double. Politically, alienated describes the severing of allegiance: the gentry and clergy were not merely annoyed or disappointed by James II's errors — they were made other, transferred out of the circle of loyalty, until their support for the crown dissolved into hostility or indifference. Emotionally, the word carries pathos: these were loyal people, and the rupture is presented as a loss to the Stuarts, an avoidable catastrophe brought on by bad policy. The tone is analytic and diagnostic — Macaulay writes like a physician of the body politic, isolating the exact process by which a regime loses its natural supporters — yet the word never loses its legal gravity, for in seventeenth-century usage alienation was still felt as a real act of transfer, like the conveyance of an estate.

Sense 1 — To estrange or turn away in feeling, affection, or loyalty (the transitive sense on this card). When X alienates Y from Z, X drives Y away from Z, making Y a stranger or an opponent. This is the ordinary literary and political sense: “His radical policies alienated the moderate members of his own party”; “The king's heavy taxation alienated the merchant guilds.” The word's force is that it describes an act performed by one party that transforms the relation of another — it is causative and transitive, always implying a “from.” The agent is the subject, the estranged party the object, and the result is the making of otherness between them.

Sense 2 — To transfer property to another (the legal sense, historically the earliest English sense). In law, to alienate (or to make an alienation) is to convey or transfer ownership of land or goods to another person: “he alienated the estate to his nephew.” This is the original English sense, taken from Roman and feudal law, and it is the deep root of all the modern meanings: to make something another's. The nouns alienable and inalienable belong here — what can and cannot be transferred away — and the famous phrase “inalienable rights” means rights that cannot be given away or taken away.

Sense 3 — The past participle and adjective: made foreign or estranged. Alienated also works as an adjective describing a person or group that has been rendered other — “she felt completely alienated from her colleagues” — and, in the more active psychological reading, describes the state of not belonging, of being a stranger in one's own world. The adjectival use shades into the modern psychological and sociological sense.

Sense 4 — Psychological and sociological alienation (19th–20th century, largely from Marx).

  • Marxist alienation (German Entfremdung): in the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, Marx described the worker as alienated from the product of his labour, from the act of production, from his fellow humans, and from his own species-being: the worker's labour becomes a commodity, his activity belongs to another. The German word was rendered into English as alienation, giving the term a permanent place in social theory.
  • Alienation of affection (law): in Anglo-American tort law, a claim by a spouse against a third party who “alienates the affection” of the other spouse — the legal transfer of loyalty and love, a vivid survival of the property metaphor.
  • Social psychology and psychiatry: since the mid-twentieth century, “alienation” names the sense of powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, and isolation that individuals feel in mass society; “the alienated individual” is a stock figure of modern social criticism.

Sense 5 — Related adjective forms and nouns. The family includes alien (adjective and noun: “foreign; a foreigner; an extraterrestrial,” from Latin alienus), alienation (the noun of all the above), alienator and alienee (legal: the transferor and transferee), and the adjective alienated itself. The noun alien in its 1950s science-fiction sense revived the old “foreigner” meaning with a planetary twist — the being who is other by nature.

Semantic architecture. All these senses hang from a single Latin chain: alius “other” → alienus “belonging to another, foreign” → alienāre “to make another's, to transfer” → alienātus “made other.” The verb always means to make something or someone other — whether an estate changes hands (legal), a heart changes allegiance (political), or a person loses the feeling of belonging (psychological). Macaulay's sentence, written in the 1840s, sits exactly at the hinge: old enough to feel the legal gravity of the word, modern enough to use it as a precise instrument of political analysis. Among its near-synonyms, estranged is the closest but stresses the cooling of personal affection in the private sphere; disaffected describes a passive condition of disloyalty rather than an active act; and withdrawn suggests voluntary retreat rather than something done to a person or group.
