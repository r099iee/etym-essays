The noun monkery denotes the state, system, way of life, or collective body of monks — and, by extension, a place of monastic seclusion. But the word is almost never neutral. From its earliest appearances in English it carried a distinctly pejorative, anti-clerical charge: it reduces monastic life to an institutional "business" and mocks the spiritual pretensions of cloistered communities. In a sentence such as "It must be lonely here in your monkery," said by Shi-yin to a monk, the word suggests a remote, austere, and possibly suspect place where a monk lives withdrawn from the world, and it faintly questions the value of that withdrawal.

Etymologically the word is a straightforward English formation: monk (from Old English munuc, ultimately Greek monachos "solitary one") plus the noun suffix -ery (from Old French -erie, denoting a place, a practice, or a collective). The suffix is the same one that gives us cookery, bakery, drapery, and nunnery — but where those words are neutral descriptions of a craft or place, monkery was born inside a storm. It entered English in the sixteenth century, precisely when the Protestant Reformation and the English Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–1541) were turning monasticism into a byword for superstition, idleness, and financial corruption.

Core senses:
  • The state or condition of being a monk; the monastic life taken as a whole.
  • The system or institution of monasticism — the rules, customs, and organisation of monastic orders.
  • A monastery, or more broadly any secluded place inhabited by monks.
  • A collective body of monks; a religious community considered as a group.
Exhaustive polysemy and modern senses:
  • Neutral-historical use: in older writing, monkery could simply mean "monasticism", describing the institution without judgement, especially in histories of the early Church or of religious orders.
  • Pejorative-polemical use (the dominant historical sense): from the Reformation onward, monkery named monastic life as a corrupt, mechanical, money-driven contrivance — the vocabulary of Protestant satire aimed at the "monkish business" of the monasteries, from which England's monasteries were forcibly stripped.
  • Jocular-figurative use: any life of extreme seclusion, celibacy, or withdrawal can be teased as "monkery" or "monkish"; a hermit's hut, a reclusive scholar's study, or a bachelor's silent apartment may be wryly called a monkery.
  • Register today: archaic and chiefly historical or ironic; it is not used respectfully of living religious communities, where the neutral monasticism is preferred.
Historical bridge: the word's biography is inseparable from its birth-date. Because monkery was coined in England in the very century of the Dissolution of the Monasteries, its earliest recorded uses already glint with the anti-clerical satire that the Reformation gave it. The suffix -ery had built many neutral collectives in Middle English (as in "nunnery", attested earlier); monkery joined the set but was pulled sharply pejorative by the political and religious context of the 1500s, when "monkish" itself became an insult. The mnemonic — "MONK-ery: the place of a MONK" — captures the concrete suffixal meaning (a place associated with monks) that later broadened into the abstract "system". Register and usage:
  • Chiefly historical; used today for period colour, Reformation history, or wry dismissiveness.
  • Compare the neutral monastery (place), monasticism (system), monastic (adjective or person) — all lacking the sneering edge of monkery.
  • No modern derived senses; the word has not produced jargon or technical meanings in any field.
