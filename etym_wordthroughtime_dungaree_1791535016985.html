- Early 17th c. – Coarse Indian export fabric - Recorded in British East India Company shipping manifests as cheap, sturdy calico cloth manufactured near Bombay for trade in Asia and Africa.
- Late 17th to 18th c. – Cheap cloth for poor laborers & sails - Perceived as low-status utilitarian fabric used for sail repair, bedding, and rudimentary garments for slaves, sailors, and manual laborers.
- 19th c. – Standard naval sailor & dockworker trousers - Pluralized into *dungarees*, designating coarse blue slops and work trousers issued to naval seamen and merchant crews across the British Empire.
- Early to Mid-20th c. – Industrial worker bib overalls & naval work uniform - Associated with factory labor, railroad mechanics, and civilian defense workers during World Wars I and II (the iconic uniform of "Rosie the Riveter").
- 1960s to 1970s – Anti-fashion statement & feminist workwear - Adopted by youth counterculture, civil rights activists, and feminist movements as an egalitarian rejection of tailored bourgeois dress.
- Modern Era – Casual fashion & vintage streetwear - Integrated into mainstream casual fashion as retro streetwear, children's wear, and trendy utilitarian fashion while retaining industrial heritage.
