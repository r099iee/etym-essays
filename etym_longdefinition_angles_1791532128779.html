Angles is the name of a Germanic people who, according to Bede, crossed the North Sea with the Saxons and Jutes in the fifth century and settled in Britain. Their name became Englaland, “land of the Angles,” and hence England; their speech became Old English. The word is thus a small tribal name with an unusually large afterlife: a people mentioned in a few lines of Tacitus and Bede gave its name to a nation, a language, a church and a global diaspora.

Who the Angles were

Tacitus, in Germania 40 (98 CE), lists the Anglii among the tribes of the lower Elbe region, in the company of the Reudigni, Aviones, Varini and Eudoses, who worshipped the earth goddess Nerthus. Ptolemy’s Geography in the second century places the Angloi in roughly the same north-German area. Their homeland is usually identified with Angeln (Latin Anglia), the district in eastern Schleswig between the Schlei inlet and the Flensburg fjord, in what is now northern Germany and southern Denmark.

Bede and the adventus Saxonum

The decisive account is Bede’s Historia ecclesiastica gentis Anglorum (731), I.15. Bede names three peoples — Angli, Saxones and Iutae — and says the Angles came from Angulus, “a region called Angulus, which lies between the provinces of the Jutes and the Saxons and is said to remain deserted to this day.” He dates the arrival to 449 and names Hengist and Horsa as leaders. Modern historians treat the date and the brothers as legendary, but the broad picture of migration from the North Sea coast is supported by archaeology and place-names.

From Anglia to England

Old English called the people Engle, their language Englisc and their land Englaland; the collective term Angelcynn, “the English race,” is common in Alfred’s reign. The name displaced the older Latin and British terms — Britannia, “Saxony” — because the Angles, though perhaps fewer in number than the Saxons, supplied the dominant political name. By the tenth century the kings of Wessex styled themselves rex Anglorum, king of the English, and “England” had become a territorial idea.

Anglian dialect and the English Church

Old English dialects are conventionally divided into Northumbrian, Mercian, West Saxon and Kentish; the first two are called Anglian, and they produced much of the earliest religious literature, including the Lindisfarne Gospels gloss and the works associated with Cædmon and Bede. The Latin adjective Anglicanus gives English Anglican, and Anglia remains the Latin name of England and of East Anglia. The medieval church province of Canterbury was not called “Anglican” until the sixteenth century, when the word acquired its modern ecclesiastical sense.

Onomastic pitfalls

  • Anglesey is not “island of the Angles.” It is Old Norse Ǫngullsey, “hook island,” from ǡngull “hook” and ey “island”; the Welsh name is Ynys Môn.
  • Angle in geometry comes from Latin angulus “corner,” a different but ultimately related Indo-European root; it has nothing to do with the tribe.
  • Anglo- as a prefix means English or British, not specifically the tribe: Anglo-Saxon, Anglo-Norman, Anglophone.
  • Anglo-Saxon is a later compound; the Angles did not call themselves that, and the phrase Angli Saxones appears first in continental Latin sources to distinguish the island Saxons from those in Germany.

Modern historical and genetic usage

Historians still use “the Angles, Saxons and Jutes” as a convenient shorthand, while stressing that the migrants were a mixed and probably modest population, that “Angles” may have been a political label rather than a biological people, and that British survival was substantial. Ancient-DNA studies since the 2010s have complicated the older picture of mass replacement, and archaeology emphasises acculturation as much as migration. The word “Angles” nevertheless remains the eponym at the centre of English national origins.

Semantic bridges

  • Tribal name → territorial name: the Angles’ name is attached to the land they settled, not to a surviving tribal institution.
  • People → language: Englisc means both “of the Angles” and “the English language.”
  • Language → nation and church: England, English, Anglican, Anglophone.
  • Nation → ethnic and imperial category: nineteenth-century “Anglo-Saxon” extended the tribal name to a supposed race and then to a global political community.
