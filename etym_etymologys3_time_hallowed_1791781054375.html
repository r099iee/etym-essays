Time descends from Old English tīma, a word for “a time, season, hour, occasion” that glosses Latin tempus and hora in the surviving texts. Old English also had tīd, the broader word for time and season, which has survived only in the narrowed sense of tide; it was tīma that generalized in Middle English to become the ordinary noun for duration, leaving tide to the sea.

  • Proto-Germanic *tīmô is reconstructed from Old Norse tími, Old English tīma, Old Saxon tīmi and cognates; the modern Scandinavian words for “hour” (Swedish timme, Danish time) preserve the older, bounded sense.
  • The usual derivation connects the Germanic noun with a PIE root *deh₂- “to divide, allot”, the source of Sanskrit dā́ti “cuts off” and Greek daíomai “divide, distribute”. Time on that account is “what is divided up” or “the allotted span”.
  • That link is plausible but not secure: the Germanic noun has no exact cognate outside Germanic, and some scholars treat the root connection as speculative. It should be presented as the standard hypothesis, not as demonstrated fact.

Hallowed is the past participle of hallow, from Old English hālgian “to make holy, consecrate”, itself derived from hālig “holy”. The Germanic adjective *hailagaz is built on *hailaz “whole, sound, healthy” — the ancestor of whole, heal and health — so that “holy” begins as “whole, uninjured, intact”, a notion of complete soundness that was applied to persons set apart for the gods.

  • Old English religious prose uses hālga for a saint and hālig for holy places, days and scriptures.
  • The verb hālgian is the one used in the Lord’s Prayer: sī þīn nama gehālgod, “hallowed be thy name” — the source of the modern archaism.
  • All Hallows (the feast of all saints) survives in Halloween, and hallow as a verb now appears almost only in prayer and poetry. Lincoln’s Gettysburg Address — “we cannot dedicate, we cannot consecrate, we cannot hallow this ground” — shows the word’s elevated, liturgical register in modern English.

Making the compound

English has a small family of time- compounds of this type: time-honoured (recorded from the late sixteenth century), time-worn, time-served, and time-hallowed, which is the latest and most religiously coloured of them. The model is transparent: an agent noun plus a past participle, as in sun-baked or wind-swept. The compound belongs to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when antiquarian sentiment, Gothic revival and national nostalgia made “the hallowing effect of time” a commonplace of public rhetoric.

Semantic and cultural shift

  • Religious origin: hallowing is an act of God or the Church; a hallowed place is set apart from the ordinary.
  • Romantic transfer: from the eighteenth century, age itself begins to do the hallowing; ruins, customs and constitutions acquire sanctity simply by lasting.
  • Political use: in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the compound defends inherited institutions — monarchy, procedure, privilege — by presenting duration as a kind of consecration.
  • Ironic reversal: in contemporary usage the same phrase can mock the reverence it names, as when a newspaper writes of “time-hallowed incompetence”.

Sound and spelling

Time underwent the Great Vowel Shift from Middle English /tiːm/ to modern /taɪm/, while hallowed keeps the old participle ending and is pronounced /ˈhæl.əʊd/ in British English, /ˈhæl.oʊd/ in American. In the compound the stress falls on the first element and the two parts remain hyphenated, since the phrase is attributive; unhyphenated time hallowed would be read as two separate words.

Connotation today

The word is a small act of piety in itself. To call a practice time-hallowed is to grant it the benefit of the doubt, to imply that what has lasted deserves reverence; to withhold the word is quietly to deny that claim. In the source passage the compound gives custom a sacred, almost geological authority, exactly the quality the argument needs before it can set custom against law.
