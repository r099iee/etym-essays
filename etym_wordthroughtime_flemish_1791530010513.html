- 7th-9th century - Flandris and Flandra name a marshy coastal district; the connotation is marginal wetland rather than people or language.
- 10th-12th century - the County of Flanders grows rich on wool and cloth; "Flemish" begins to suggest prosperous towns, skilled weavers and North Sea trade.
- 13th-14th century - Ghent, Bruges and Ypres dominate the cloth market and resist kings; English borrows Flemmysshe, and the word carries a mixture of admiration for craftsmanship and suspicion of turbulent burghers.
- 16th-17th century - English pamphlets stereotype the "Flemish drunkard" and Flemish mercenaries, while Flemish Protestant exiles settle in England; the adjective acquires a coarse, comic tinge alongside its commercial sense.
- 17th-18th century - Rubens and Van Dyck make "Flemish" a compliment in art and collecting; Flemish lace, horses and Picturesque gardens become marks of taste.
- 19th century - the Flemish Movement turns the word into a programme: language rights, schools, literature and history, against the French-speaking elite; art history fixes "Flemish Primitives" as a canon.
- 20th century - two world wars, the Front Movement and the Language Border give "Flemish" an institutional and electoral charge; Belgium federalises.
- 21st century - "Flemish" is an official regional label and a tourism brand; in politics it can signal either civic regionalism or ethnic nationalism, and abroad it is increasingly used for Belgian Dutch and Flemish art alike.
