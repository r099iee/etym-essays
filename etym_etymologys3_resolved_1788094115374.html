RESOLVED carries within it the whole history of the Latin verb solvere 'to loosen, untie, release', built on the PIE root *leu- 'to loosen, divide, cut apart' — the root that surfaces in Greek λύειν (lúein) 'to loosen, release' (whence analysis, paralysis, catalysis), in Sanskrit lunāti 'cuts', and in the English family of loose, lose, loss, and the Latin-derived solve, dissolve, absolute, and solution. Latin solvere is itself a compound: *se-luō 'to loosen apart', where se- marks separation (as in seditio 'a going apart', secedo 'to withdraw'). From this physical verb of untying ropes and releasing captives, classical Latin builds resolvere — re- 'back, again' + solvere — 'to loosen again, to undo, to unfasten, to open (a knot)'. Already in Latin the word begins to move from the physical to the mental: Cicero uses resolvere for dissolving doubt and for breaking an argument into parts, and the philosophical tradition uses it for analysis in the strict sense — breaking a compound into its constituents.

  • Latin → Old French (12th c.): Old French resoudre takes over the full Latin range: 'to untie, dissolve, reduce to parts, settle (a dispute)'. The 12th-century scholastic and legal milieu gives the word a technical career — resolving questions, resolving cases, resolving substances.
  • Entry into English (14th–15th c.): Middle English resolven (also resolve) is first attested c. 1380–1400 meaning 'to dissolve, to melt, to break down into parts' — a physical/alchemical sense (Chaucer's Canon's Yeoman's Tale uses the language of dissolving and 'resolving' metals). The participial form resolved initially means 'dissolved, reduced, analysed'.
  • 16th century — the semantic turn: the decisive shift happens in early modern English: 'to resolve a doubt/question' (already in Latin) becomes 'to free from doubt', and from 'freeing one's mind from doubt' the word slides to 'settling one's own mind' — i.e., deciding. By 1580–1600 'I am resolved' = 'I have made up my mind' is current, and 'resolved to + infinitive' is established. Tyndale and the Authorised Version use resolved in both senses; Shakespeare exploits the double meaning in his drama of deliberation (Hamlet's 'resolution' speech, c. 1600).
  • 17th century — institutionalisation: parliamentary and legal usage fixes 'Resolved, that...' as the formula of formal collective decision (the House 'resolves'), and the adjective resolved 'determined, firm' is established ('a resolved mind', Milton). The old analytical sense remains alive in science: Newton's 'resolution' of forces and of light into the spectrum (Opticks, 1704) is literally the Latin 'loosening into parts'.
  • 18th–19th centuries — the technical diaspora: the word family (resolve, resolution, resolute, resolvable) colonises every formal domain: law (resolution of a court), politics (UN resolutions), chemistry (resolution of a racemic mixture), music (a dissonance 'resolves' to a consonance — the tension is 'loosened'), mathematics and physics (resolving vectors, resolution of forces), and optics (resolving power of a lens — the ability to separate close objects, a direct heir of 'dividing into parts').
  • 20th–21st centuries — the digital afterlife: computing inherits the whole family: screen resolution (the number of separable pixels), DNS resolution (turning a domain name into an address), a 'resolved' bug or ticket (settled, closed). In ordinary speech, resolved has settled into two comfortable roles: the strong decision verb ('she resolved to act') and the status adjective ('the matter is resolved'), while its 600-year-old 'untied knot' metaphor remains the invisible logic that makes 'resolving a conflict' sound like untying it rather than winning it.

Phonetically, resolved shows the regular French/Latin borrowing pattern: Middle English /rɛˈzɔlv(ə)d/ → early Modern English /rɪˈzɒlvd/, with stress on the second syllable and a reduced first syllable; the -ed is always pronounced as a full syllable /ɪd/ after the final /v/ — 're-ZOL-ved' — which keeps the participial shape audible. Orthographic history: Latin resolvere → Old French resoudre → Middle English resolven → Modern English resolve/resolved. Key literary anchors: Cicero's resolvere; Chaucer, Canon's Yeoman's Tale (c. 1390s); Tyndale's New Testament (1526); Shakespeare, Hamlet (c. 1600); Milton, Paradise Lost (1667); Newton, Opticks (1704); and the modern digital uses that have made 'resolution' one of the most productive technical nouns in English.
