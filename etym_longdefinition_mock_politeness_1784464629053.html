Mock-politeness is politeness performed as imitation, sarcasm, or teasing rather than as sincere courtesy: the forms of deference (title, tone, bow of phrase) without the goodwill those forms usually signal. It is a hyphenated compound of two English words — mock, 'to deride; imitative, sham,' and politeness, 'courteous behaviour' — and the hyphen keeps the double structure visible: real polite surface, insincere or teasing intent. It is not a fixed dictionary headword of older prose but a transparent formation that English builds freely whenever social nuance needs a label, and in the modern period it has even become a technical term in linguistics. When Thorin 'turned with mock-politeness to Bilbo' after labeling him the 'burglar-expert,' the adverbial phrase marks a theatrical show of respect that is really irony — royal dwarf to reluctant hobbit, an invitation edged with skepticism. The tone is social comedy and status friction; the politeness is a mask, not a bond. The two halves of the compound
  • Mock — borrowed into Middle English from Old French mocquer 'to deride, jeer'; as a verb it means to ridicule or to imitate in derision, and as a combining element mock- marks anything counterfeit, performed, or imitative: mock-heroic, mock-trial, mock-turtle (the fake dish), mock-orange (the shrub whose flowers imitate orange blossom).
  • Politeness — the abstract noun of polite, from Latin polīre 'to polish': rough edges rubbed smooth become refined manners. Politeness is thus the polished surface of behaviour, and mock-politeness is that surface worn without the good faith behind it.
Polysemy and secondary senses
  • Everyday social sense — courteous forms used to tease, test, insult, or distance: the exaggerated bow, the ironic 'sir,' the too-civil phrase that is really a joke or a slight. This is the sense in the card's sentence.
  • Linguistics and pragmatics — in late 20th- and 21st-century impoliteness research, 'mock politeness' is a technical term for the strategic use of overly polite or conventionally courteous forms to mock, insult, or signal distance; the scholarly use formalizes the everyday compound.
  • Literature and drama — the mock-polite tone is a staple of satire, comedy of manners, and the novel, where a character's surface courtesy is punctured by context; related compounds (mock-modesty, mock-solemnity, mock-heroic) fill out the family of performed manner.
  • Related but distinct — sarcasm is broad hostile irony that need not wear courteous forms; irony may be situational rather than performed; condescension looks down while pretending kindness; mockery alone may be open ridicule without the veneer of manners. Mock-politeness insists on the double structure: real polite surface + insincere or teasing intent.
Historical bridge The compound's force comes entirely from its parts. The mock- prefix has meant 'sham, performed' since the early modern period, when English began attaching it to nouns to mark fake or imitative versions (mock-battle, mock-modesty); the politeness element carries the classical 'polish' metaphor of refinement. The hyphen welds the two: mock-politeness is polished behaviour that has been put on rather than possessed — courtesy as performance, deference as a tease. In the Bag End scene Thorin performs the courtesies of a host while testing the hobbit Gandalf has thrust upon the company, and the compound names that exact double gesture.
