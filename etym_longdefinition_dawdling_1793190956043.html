Dawdling, the present participle and gerund of the verb dawdle, names the art of going slowly on purpose or by nature: loitering on the way, lingering over trifles, letting the minutes leak away while some duty, appointment, or exasperated companion waits.

In the source sentence the word lands as a jeer: dawdling bag of bones — an insult flung at some slow gaunt creature or person, accusing it of shuffling and loitering when it should bestir itself. The comedy lies in the mismatch of sounds: the soft drawling syllables of dawdling draped over the hard rattle of a bag of bones, slowness made audible.

Core behavioral sense in detail: To dawdle is to spend time without spending it well — to walk when one should run, to fiddle when one should act, to let attention wander from the road to the hedgerow. The dawdler is rarely wicked, only unhurried: the child distracted by puddles on the school run, the traveler lingering at every stall, the committee revisiting settled points. The -ing form stresses continuation, the slow leak of the morning going on and on.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Loitering and moving slowly (primary, from 17th c.): dawdling on the way home, dawdling over breakfast. The bridge is the word's own sound, which drawls and drags as the dawdler does.
  • Wasting time idly instead of acting (from 18th c.): dawdling away the afternoon, dawdling instead of deciding. The bridge extends slow motion to slow purpose: the feet loiter first, then the will follows.
  • Lingering over work with trifling fuss (occupational): dawdling over a task, dawdling with details. The bridge runs through officious slowness: busy hands that never advance the job, polishing what needs no polish.
  • Ambling with pleasure, pottering (affectionate shade): dawdling through a market on holiday, dawdling along a river path. The bridge softens idleness into enjoyment: the same slowness condemned on a school morning is praised on a summer ramble.
  • Delaying out of reluctance or dread (psychological): dawdling before bad news, dawdling at the dentist's door. The bridge finds motive in the loitering: feet slow because the heart does not want to arrive.
  • The noun dawdling as habitual idleness (gerund use): no more of your dawdling, punished for dawdling. The bridge converts the act into a chargeable habit, the schoolmaster's and sergeant's favorite accusation.

Register and connotation: dawdling is scolding but fond — the nursery, the schoolroom, and the barracks parade ground are its natural habitats. It accuses without condemning: the dawdler is exasperating rather than evil, and the word often carries a covert affection for the very idleness it rebukes. As an insult modifier, as in the source sentence, it paints slowness as character: not merely slow now, but slow by constitution, bone-deep and incurable.
