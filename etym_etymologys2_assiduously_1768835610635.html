assiduously - PIE root *sed- "to sit; to settle down" (reconstructed; c. 5th millennium BCE) — the common ancestor of the "sit" words across the family: Sanskrit sīdati "he sits down," sadas "seat, assembly," upaniṣad "sitting down near"; Greek hézomai "I sit," hédra "seat"; Latin sedēre "to sit"; Gothic sitan; Germanic *sitjaną (English sit). - Latin sedēre "to sit" (attested, 1st c. BCE classical texts) — the direct Latin reflex of *sed-, from which the compounds of sitting were built. - Latin assidēre "to sit down at, to sit close beside" (ad-, "at, toward," + sedēre; attested in classical Latin) — the compound in which the seated posture is directed toward a task or a place; the semantic bridge to constancy: one who sits at a thing does not leave it. - Latin assiduus "sitting constantly at; persistent, unwearied" (attested classical adjective) — the man who remains seated at his post when others rise; extended to "constant, continuous" of things. - Latin assiduitās "constancy, perseverance" (attested classical noun) — the abstract noun of the same idea; a praised civic and military virtue in Rome. - English assiduous "constant in application" (borrowed c. 1620, from Latin assiduus) — the adjective; the adverb follows within decades. - English assiduously (adverb, 17th c., assiduous + -ly) — the adverbial form required by the passage: "so assiduously kept from the carvings." Note on debate/nuance: no stage in this chain is genuinely contested; *sed- is one of the most securely reconstructed PIE roots, and every form above is well attested. The only nuance worth flagging is the semantic step from "to sit down at" (a physical posture) to "constant, unremitting" (a moral quality) — a bridge already complete in classical Latin assiduus, where constancy was read directly off the posture of the man who stayed seated at his post.
