PIE (c. 4000–3000 BCE)
*syew- · *syuH-
“to sew, stitch, bind”
(cf. Latin suere, Sanskrit sīvyati)

↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE)
*siwjaną
“to sew”

↓
Proto-Germanic (same period)
*saumaz
“that which is sewn; seam, hem, edge”

↓
Old English (c. 8th–9th c.)
sēam
“a seam; a junction of edges; a suture”

↓
Old English (c. 10th–11th c.)
sēamestre
“a woman who sews”
(seam + -estre, feminine agent suffix;
glosses Latin consutrix)

↓
Middle English (14th c.)
sempster · seamster
“a sewer; now of either sex”
(the suffix -ster has lost its feminine force)

↓
Middle English (15th c.)
sempstresse
“a female sewer”
(re-feminised with French -esse)

↓
Modern English (16th–17th c.)
seamstress
“a woman who sews professionally”
(spelling re-aligned to “seam”)

↓
SEAMSTRESS

Note: the derivation is securely attested at every stage; the only nuance is morphological — two distinct suffixes layer over the noun “seam”, and the modern spelling re-anchors an older semp- form to the base word. The PIE root is reconstructed but its reflexes (Latin suere, Sanskrit sīvyati, Greek weaving terms) are well established.
