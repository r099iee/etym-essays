- 15th c. — the spiked barrier: a turnpike is a revolving frame of pikes blocking a road or breach, a soldier's gate that wounds whoever forces it.
- 1663 — the first toll act: the Great North Road is put to toll, and the gate that once stopped armies begins stopping travellers for pennies.
- 1706–1750s — the trusts multiply: local gentlemen take whole highways in hand, borrowing against future tolls to gravel, ditch, and straighten.
- 1750s–1830s — the golden gridiron: twenty thousand miles of turnpike-road bind market, port, and mill; coaches run to timetables and the tariff board rules the highway.
- 1830 — Cobbett's contempt: the reformer quits the metalled toll-road for lanes and downs, making the turnpike the emblem of official, hurrying England.
- 1840s — gate-smashing and railways: Rebecca rioters in Wales burn the toll-houses while locomotives steal the traffic, and the trusts slide toward bankruptcy.
- 1888 — the councils inherit: county councils take over the roads, tolls are extinguished, and turnpike becomes a memory of gates and keepers.
- 1940 — America revives it: the Pennsylvania Turnpike christens the motor age, and the old gate-word now means the fastest road of all.
