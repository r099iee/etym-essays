Reams, the plural of ream, means in its oldest trade sense a bulk unit of paper — classically 480 sheets (the short ream) or 500 sheets (the long or printer’s ream) — and in its now-dominant figurative sense, vast quantities of writing: reams of advice, reams of regulations, reams of commentary, almost always with a sigh at the sheer volume.

In the source sentence the figurative sense shines: Dora Baggins had written reams of good advice for more than half a century — shelf upon shelf of letters and maxims from a long-lived, well-meaning relative, the quintessential family flood of counsel. The plural is doing comic work: nobody counts Dora’s sheets, but everybody feels their weight.

Core meaning in detail: The paper trade’s ream was a wholesaler’s bundle: twenty quires of twenty-four or twenty-five sheets, wrapped and sold to printers, offices, and schools. Before digital documents, institutions literally bought paper by the ream, and the towering white stacks beside every photocopier kept the measure visible. The figurative leap treats any long text as if it were that pile: advice, evidence, and bureaucracy all arrive by the ream.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A paper quantity of 480–500 sheets (primary historical sense, from the 14th c.): the stationer’s unit — three reams of A4. The bridge is direct inheritance from Arabic through French: a wrapped bundle of paper sold wholesale.
  • Huge amounts of writing (dominant modern sense): reams of advice, of poetry, of legal argument. The bridge is the bundle made metaphor: the physical pile becomes any overwhelming mass of words, usually more than anyone wanted.
  • Cream skimmed from milk (obsolete homonym, Old English ream): an entirely separate native word for cream or froth, surviving in dialect. The bridge is none at all — a coincidence of spelling between two unrelated words, the cream sense now lost.
  • To widen or bevel a hole (verb ream, reamer): engineers ream holes with a rotary cutter. The bridge is again coincidence: this ream comes from a Germanic root for making room, unrelated to the paper bundle, though spellcheckers treat them as one.
  • Royal and printer’s variants (trade technicality): perfect reams of 516 sheets and printer’s reams of 500+ for wastage. The bridge is the trade’s honest fudge: paper spoils, so the bundle grew to guarantee the count.

Register and connotation: reams is wry and world-weary — the vocabulary of in-trays, archives, and well-meaning relatives. It flatters nothing: reams of praise is rare, while reams of paperwork is universal. The singular survives in offices (a ream of paper for the printer), but the plural belongs to rhetoric — a shorthand for abundance tipping into excess, delivered with an affectionate or exasperated smile.
