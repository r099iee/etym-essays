LOPE — linear chain for lope:
1. Proto-Germanic *hlaupaną "to leap, run, spring" (c. 500 BCE–CE 500). No secure PIE etymology is agreed upon; Baltic forms such as Lithuanian lǎpti "to climb, creep" have been set beside it, but the OED marks the deeper origin uncertain.
2. Old Norse hlaupa "to leap, run, spring" (Viking Age, c. 800–1050 CE; the North Germanic reflex of *hlaupaną).
3. Middle English lopen (c. 1450) "to run with long strides" — a loan from the Norse word into the northern dialects of Middle English.
4. English lope (TARGET) — the verb and noun on this card.
Note on the sibling split: the native Old English reflex of the same Germanic root is hlēapan, which became English leap ("the vertical, springing jump"); the Norse loan lopen specialized as lope ("the long, bounding, forward stride"). Two English words from one Germanic verb, divided by the manner of the leap.

SKELTER — linear chain for skelter:
1. Expressive base *skelt- (English dialectal, c. 16th c.) "to hurry away, run hastily" — a sound-symbolic formation; no reconstructible PIE ancestry. The sk- onset belongs to the English phonestheme for fast, messy, scattering motion (cf. scatter, scurry, scuttle).
2. English helter-skelter (a. 1590s) "in headlong, confused disorder; pell-mell" — a rhyming jingle compound built on the dialectal verb; the helter- element is probably echoic of confused bustle.
3. Back-formation: English skelter "to scurry, scamper away" (dialectal, chiefly British) (TARGET) — the verb on this card; 20th c. also noun/adjective "a helter-skelter," the fairground spiral slide.
Note: "skelter" was never a stage in the descent of an ancient root; it is a jingle-word set free from its compound. Its "origin" is therefore the expressive base plus the compound, not a PIE etymon — and this is stated explicitly rather than invented.
