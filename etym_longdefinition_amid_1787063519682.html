AMID (/əˈmɪd/) is a preposition meaning “in the middle of”, “surrounded by”, “among”, or “in the course of”. It positions something within the body, crowd, period, or atmosphere of something else. In the card’s source context (“Soon afterwards the other dwarves were brought into the town amid scenes of astonishing enthusiasm”), the word places the dwarves inside a tide of welcome — surrounded and borne along by enthusiasm on every side.

Core sense — physical and spatial middleness: Etymologically amid is “in the middle” (on middan in Old English). The basic spatial sense places a thing among other things: “a house amid the pines”, “a figure amid the crowd”.

  • Sense 1 — in the middle of, surrounded by: the spatial core; “the island amid the lake” — the thing is enclosed or flanked on all sides.
  • Sense 2 — among (a group or collection): “amid the cheering spectators” — a member or single item within a larger set, especially a crowd or mass of like elements.
  • Sense 3 — in the course of, during (a period or event): “amid the celebrations”, “amid the chaos of war” — something that happens while an activity or state is underway.
  • Sense 4 — against a background or atmosphere of: the modern journalistic favourite; “the minister resigned amid rumours of scandal” — the action takes place within an atmosphere, whether real or merely surrounding.
  • Sense 5 — in spite of / under the conditions of: a nuance of modern headlines; “the festival went ahead amid heavy rain” — the surrounding conditions are adverse but the thing persists.

Amid, amidst, among, between: Amid and amidst are exactly synonymous, differing only in the extra excrescent -st of amidst, which gives it a slightly more literary or poetic ring. Both mean “in the middle of” and take singular or mass objects (“amid the noise”). Among means “in the company of” and is used with plural, countable members of a group (“among the guests”); between marks a position separating two things, though it extends to more than two when they are paired. Amid is preferred in modern journalism for its brevity.

Register today: In headlines and news copy amid is a workhorse — short, precise, and hard to misread — which is exactly why editors prefer it to the heavier amidst. In verse and in the high style it keeps its ancient dignity, placing a person, object, or event at the very centre of a scene, borne along by what surrounds it.

Historical bridge: Old English had the phrase on middan, “in the middle”, built on the adjective midd (“middle”, from the Indo-European root for “middle”). By Middle English the phrase had fused and drifted into the preposition amid(de), with the adverb/preposition a- from on. The medieval senses — spatial middleness and being among a group — are the ancestors of today’s spatial and social uses; the “during” and “amid reports of” uses are modern extensions in which “the middle” of an event or atmosphere replaces the middle of a physical space.
