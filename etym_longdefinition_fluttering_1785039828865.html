FLUTTERING is the present participle and gerund of the verb FLUTTER: to move with a quick, light, irregular series of small motions, most typically the rapid, trembling beats of a wing, or the trembling of leaves, flags, eyelids or pulse, made without any steady or directed course. The defining qualities are lightness, rapidity, irregularity and lack of control: flutter is the opposite of the strong, even beat of sustained flight, of the firm grip of a held position, of the steady progress of a journey. A thing that flutters trembles, flickers and dances in place, unanchored and unsteady; it hovers without settling, beats without steering. The word also names, by extension, the nervous movement of the heart or of emotion — a heart that flutters, a flutter of excitement, of panic, of hope — and the visible twitch of eyelids or lashes. Because of this dual life, fluttering is both a physical and an emotional word: it describes the movement of creatures and objects in the world and the corresponding inner trembling of a person who watches or feels. In The Hobbit the word appears in the dusk that closes the travellers' approach to Rivendell (Chapter 3), as they wait in growing darkness: "There were moths fluttering about, and the light became very dim, for the moon had not risen." The sentence is built of negatives and near-negatives: the moon has not risen, the light has become very dim, and the only motion in the deepening gloom is the aimless, feathery beating of the moths. Fluttering is chosen precisely because it is directionless: the moths do not fly anywhere, they drift and dart around the travellers, and their tiny irregular wing-beats are the last visible sign of life as night takes the valley. The word's -ing form stretches the action into a continuous present, so that the flitting goes on and on as the light fails, giving the scene its hushed, restless, faintly enchanted texture. Because flutter is small and light, the passage stays gentle rather than sinister: the moths are delicate, feathery, almost tremulous, and their motion softens rather than frightens. The verb thus does double duty — it animates the scene and sets the nervous, expectant tone of a company waiting, listening, watching in the dark. Polysemy & secondary senses:
  • Physical motion: wings fluttering (moths, birds, butterflies); flags and banners fluttering in the wind; leaves fluttering to the ground or in a breeze; curtains and dresses fluttering; eyelids fluttering (trembling, as in sleep or faintness); eyelashes fluttering.
  • The heart and nerves: a heart fluttering or palpitations; to feel a flutter of fear, hope, excitement, pride, desire; in cardiology, atrial flutter is a specific, fast, regular beating of the heart's upper chambers (distinct from atrial fibrillation) — a technical medical use of the very same image.
  • Objects and light: a flame fluttering (flickering as it is blown); a light fluttering; a pulse fluttering under the skin.
  • Sporting and figurative: to have a flutter is British informal for a small gamble or bet ('a flutter on the horses'); a flutter in the market is a brief period of excitement or nervous activity; a flutter of activity or of interest; the 'flutter' of a butterfly's wings in the chaos-theory 'butterfly effect' (a small, light disturbance with large consequences).
  • Engineering and science: control-surface flutter in aerodynamics — a destructive high-frequency oscillation of an aircraft's wing or tail, a serious aeroelastic phenomenon; flutter in sound reproduction, the rapid variation of pitch or speed in a recording (as in 'wow and flutter'); Flutter, the name of Google's open-source UI framework (2017), which knowingly borrows the word's quick, light, unsteady motion.
  • Textiles and appearance: a 'flutter' in fashion or dance is a piece of fabric or a movement that flutters; 'fluttering' describes anything that moves with repeated light agitation.
Set beside its neighbours: hover is sustained suspension in one place; flit is quick, directed darting from one point to another; flap is heavy, broad, often audible beating; waver is unsteadiness of light, resolve or sound; flicker is the intermittent flash or trembling of light; quiver is a fine rapid shaking without travel. Flutter uniquely combines lightness, rapidity and aimlessness — movement that is all texture and no direction, which is exactly what makes moths at dusk and hearts in love both 'flutter.'
