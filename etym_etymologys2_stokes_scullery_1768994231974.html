STOKES (five-stage chain; PIE *steig- "to prick, stick" → Germanic → Middle Dutch → English): 1. PIE *steig- "to prick, pierce, stick" (reconstructed, ca. 4000 BCE; the root yields Latin īnstīgāre "to goad, incite" (English instigate) and stinguere, Greek stízein "to prick, tattoo" (English stigma), and Germanic *stik-, *stōk- "to prick, thrust, poke"). 2. Proto-Germanic *stikaną "to stab, prick" and the related o-grade base *stōkaną "to thrust, poke" (ca. 500 BCE–200 CE; the same family gives Old English stician "to pierce" — modern stick, stitch — and Old High German stōhhan "to thrust, poke"). 3. Middle Dutch stoken "to poke, thrust, stir (a fire), to add fuel to a fire" (attested from the 14th–15th c.; from the *stōk- grade; the same Dutch word gives Dutch stok "stick, staff" and stoker "fireman, stoker"). 4. Dutch stoken (modern) "to stoke a fire, to heat, to stir up" — English borrowed the verb in the 17th century directly from Middle/Dutch stoken. 5. English stoke "to poke and stir (a fire, furnace, or stove); to add fuel to it" (first attested 1680s, borrowed from Dutch stoken) — and English stokes, the third person singular present of the verb ("he stokes the fire") — the target form stokes. The figurative sense "to feed or intensify (an emotion, controversy)" develops in the 20th century. SCULLERY (five-stage chain; PIE *skeu- "to cover" → Latin → Old French → English): 1. PIE *skeu- (also *skeuə-) "to cover, conceal, protect" (reconstructed; the root also yields Latin scūtum "shield," English hide via Germanic, and Greek skûtos "skin, hide" — the "covering" idea throughout). 2. Latin scutra "a flat dish, platter, a shallow pan" (attested; probably from the same *skeu- "cover" base as scūtum "shield," the flat dish conceived as a "covering" surface; note this connection, while standard, is not completely beyond debate — see the note below). 3. Latin scutella "a small dish, bowl, cup" — the diminutive of scutra (attested from the 1st c. CE, in Apicius and other Imperial authors). 4. Old French escuele "a dish, bowl" (from Latin scutella with the regular Romance sound changes: sk- > esk- prothesis, -tella > -ele/-elle), and Old French escuelerie "the office or department of the dish-bearer; the room where dishes are kept and washed" (from escuele + the suffix -erie "place of, function of"; attested from the 12th c.). 5. Middle English squillerye / skuillerye / scullery "the scullery, the room for washing dishes and rough kitchen work" (borrowed via Anglo-French escuillerie, attested from the 13th–14th c.) — Modern English scullery "a small back kitchen or dish-washing room" — the target word scullery. NOTE ON A NUANCED STAGE (stated explicitly): the connection of Latin scutra "flat dish" to the PIE root *skeu- "to cover" (the same root as scūtum "shield") is the traditional etymology, but some authorities treat scutra as of uncertain origin within Latin — it may be an independent formation or a substrate word — so the link to *skeu-, while widely repeated, should be read as probable rather than certain. The chain from scutra onward (scutra → scutella → escuele → escuelerie → scullery) is fully attested and secure.
