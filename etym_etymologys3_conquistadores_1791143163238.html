Conquistadores continues a fully Latin-Romance lineage. The verb begins with PIE *kom “with, beside” (Latin com-, con-, English co-) fused to PIE *kʷeh₂s- “to seek, wheedle out, acquire” (Latin quaerere “seek, ask,” quaestio “inquiry,” English quest, question, query, inquisitive). In Classical Latin conquīrere meant “to seek out, collect, ransack,” as in levying troops; in Late and Medieval Latin, under Germanic pressure where “seek to get” easily becomes “seize,” it hardened to “conquer, acquire by arms” — hence Old French conquerre (English conquer) and Iberian conquistar. The -ist- infix is the Latin inchoative/frequentative -isc- via Vulgar Latin *conquaeritare, giving Spanish conquistar “to conquer” by the 13th century (Berceo, Poema de Fernán González).

Agent noun conquistador adds Latin -ator (“one who does”), regular in Spanish as -ador (trabajador, matador). Already in the Reconquista it titled those who “won” towns from Muslim rule; after 1492 the Catholic Monarchs and Charles V reused the capitulación system to license private conquests overseas, so conquistador became a quasi-legal status with rights to booty, offices, and encomiendas. Cortés styles himself conquistador in his Cartas de relación (1520s); Oviedo, Gómara, and Díaz del Castillo fix the plural conquistadores in chronicle prose. Phonologically Old Spanish /konkistaˈdor/ survives almost unchanged; English borrows it in the 19th century via Prescott’s History of the Reign of Ferdinand and Isabella (1837) and Conquest of Mexico (1843), keeping Spanish spelling but anglicizing stress and vowels.

  • 1200s–1491: Reconquista conquistadores: conquerors of Córdoba, Seville, Granada; honourable, crusading sense.
  • 1492–1550: American conquistadores: Columbus’s successors, Cortés (1519), Pizarro (1532); the word now means transatlantic empire-builder.
  • 1550–1800: conquistador as rank and reproach: Crown curbs them (New Laws 1542); Las Casas’s Brevísima (1552) makes conquistador a byword for cruelty in Protestant Europe (Black Legend).
  • 1800s: English loan: Prescott romanticizes; English plural wavers between conquistadors and conquistadores, the latter more colourful.
  • 1900–present: contested heritage: from Francoist glorification to decolonial removal of statues (Columbus, Valdivia); metaphorical “corporate conquistadores” spreads.

Morphologically Spanish keeps the full paradigm — conquistar, conquista, conquistador/a, conquistadores/as — while English borrows only the noun. The capital C in the source (“the Conquistadores”) marks the definite historical group in Mexico, not conquerors in general. Etymologists note no serious debate about this chain; the only nuance is the semantic leap from “seek together” to “conquer,” explained by Late Latin military usage where levying (“seeking out men”) and seizing territory merged — a shift paralleled in French conquérir and Italian conquistare.
