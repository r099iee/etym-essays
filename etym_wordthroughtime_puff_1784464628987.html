Old English Imitative Start - Puff descends from the Old English pyffan ("to blow in puffs"), an imitative word whose shape mimics a short expulsion of air through the lips — sound-symbolic from birth.
Middle English Broadening - Middle English puffen widens the sense from blowing air to panting, swelling with air, and figurative inflation, so the word covers breath, smoke, pastry, and pride.
17th-19th Century Figurative Uses - Puff develops its famous extensions: a reputation is "puffed up" with empty praise, pastry puffs with steam, and smoke puffs from a pipe — all sharing the air-and-swell image.
Contemporary Sense - Today puff is the everyday verb for short bursts of breath (panting) and air, and a noun for soft masses and smoke, with its comic, slightly comic register intact in motion senses like "puff along."
