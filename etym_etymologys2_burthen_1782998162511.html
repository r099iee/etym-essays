1. PIE *bʰer- 'to carry, bear, bring forth' (the great carry-root of Indo-European; reflexes include Sanskrit bhárati 'he carries', Latin ferō 'I carry', Greek φέρω, Gothic bairan; Pokorny *bher-). Note — homophone guard: a distinct PIE *bʰer- 'to bore, cut' gave English bore, borer; do not merge. 2. Proto-Germanic *beraną 'to carry, bear, bring forth' (the direct verbal continuation; cf. Old English beran, Old High German beran, Old Norse bera). 3. Proto-Germanic derived noun *bur-þ-injō 'that which is carried, a load' (formed on the zero grade *bʰr̥- of the verb with the abstract noun-suffix *-þ-, the ancestor of the -th-/-d- of our word; cf. the parallel formations in German Bürde and Old Norse byrðr 'burden'). 4. Old English byrþen (also byrden) 'load, weight, charge, duty' (attested from the earliest glosses; already used both physically and morally). 5. Middle English burthen, burden, birthen 'load, weight, charge, duty' (14th–15th c.; the -th- and -d- spellings coexist). 6. English burthen (the archaic -th- spelling, preserved in Biblical, poetic, and nautical use; the standard modern form is burden, with the dental /θ/ displaced by /d/ in most dialects c. 15th–16th c.). Debated nuance: the exact shape of the Proto-Germanic noun is reconstructed as *bur-þ-injō on the evidence of Old English byrþen, Old Saxon burthinnia, and Old High German burdin (modern German Bürde), but the precise suffix layering is debated among Germanic philologists; the core formation — zero grade of *bʰer- + a -þ- abstract suffix — is not in doubt. The final -n in English (burden/burthen) reflects the re-analysed weak-noun inflection of the Middle English period.
