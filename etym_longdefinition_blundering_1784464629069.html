Blundering is the present participle and gerund of the verb blunder: moving or acting blindly, clumsily, or stupidly — making noise and mistakes through lack of care, skill, or awareness. It can describe physical stumbling or figurative error, and the two senses often co-exist when large people crash through a quiet world. In the prologue of The Hobbit, the narrator explains that hobbits' magic is "the ordinary everyday sort which helps them to disappear quietly and quickly when large stupid folk like you and me come blundering along, making a noise like elephants which they can hear a mile off." The participle is kinetic and comic and self-deprecating: Big Folk announce themselves by clumsy passage while hobbits simply vanish. The tone is humorous, slightly rustic, onomatopoetically heavy — the word's thick consonants match an elephantine tread. Grammatically it is progressive motion-as-manner: not a single finished blunder but the ongoing mode of approach that hobbits can hear a mile off. Preserve the -ing form of the text. Blundering fuses two ideas that English keeps close together: clumsy movement and stupid error. The physical sense is the older: to blunder is to stumble blindly, to crash into things, to go forward without seeing where one is going. The intellectual sense is a transparent extension — a person who "blunders" in a decision acts as if blind to the facts, careless and wrong-footed. In most uses the two are not separable: a blundering person is someone whose body and mind are both out of step, noisy and mistaken at once. The word carries a strong judgement of carelessness rather than malice — blundering people are not evil, just inept, and the word's comic force comes from that mixture of harmlessness and noise.
  • Primary sense (physical motion): moving blindly or clumsily, stumbling and crashing through obstacles, especially noisily.
  • Secondary sense (cognitive error): making stupid, careless mistakes; acting in a confused or blundering way in decisions or speech ("blundering remarks," "blundering diplomacy").
  • Grammatical uses: progressive participle ("they come blundering along"), gerund ("blundering around in the dark"), and participial adjective ("a blundering fool," "blundering officials").
  • Register nuances: chiefly comic, self-deprecating, or contemptuous; heavier and noisier than stumbling, less malicious than error.
  • Collocations: blundering around, blundering into, blundering through, blundering idiot — the word commonly pairs with motion verbs and particles.
  • Related forms in the same family: blunder (noun, a clumsy mistake), blunderer (agent noun), the phrase "a blunder" in diplomacy and chess (a gross error), and the separate, folk-etymologized blunderbuss (from Dutch donderbus, "thunder gun").
Polysemy and secondary modern meanings: in management and journalism, "blundering" describes stupid administrative or governmental mistakes — a "blundering bureaucracy" — the intellectual sense applied to institutions. In military history the classic "blunders" of campaigns (as in "the blunder at Gallipoli") carry the same weight of carelessness with high cost. The historical bridge runs from the physical to the mental: the Middle English verb meant "to move blindly" and "to mix up, confuse" from its first attestation, and the "confuse" sense was already halfway to the modern intellectual one. The word's Scandinavian ancestor, Old Norse blunda, meant "to shut the eyes," and a person who acts with shut eyes both stumbles and errs — the two senses are, etymologically, one image. Register and boundaries: blundering is distinct from stumbling, which is a trip-and-near-fall; from clumsy, which is an adjectival state rather than an action; from barging, which stresses intrusive force; from lurching, which is an uneven swaying gait; and from erring, which is purely cognitive and carries no physical image. Blundering keeps both channels open at once — body and mind both wrong-footed — and that double charge is exactly what Tolkien uses when he has "large stupid folk like you and me" crash like elephants through a world that prefers quiet feet.
