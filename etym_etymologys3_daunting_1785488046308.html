A root of taming. Long before it ever frightened anyone, the root of daunting lived in the stable and the field. The Proto-Indo-European root *demh₂- described the physical act of taming and breaking in animals - the patient bodily labour of bending a wild creature's will to human service. The same root also yields the words for "house" (Latin domus, Greek domos) - the place where the wild is domesticated - and the family is rich across the whole Indo-European world. The Indo-Iranian line. In the Indo-Iranian branch the sense of taming was exceptionally rich. Sanskrit root dam (dam), "to tame, restrain", produces damayati (damayati), "he tames, subdues", damana (damana), "taming, restraint", and daman (daman), "a rope or cord" - the very strap with which the beast was bound. The idea survives into modern Hindi in दमन (daman), "suppression, repression", where the ancient image of crushing wildness into obedience still glimmers beneath the abstract meaning. The same root in Greek gives damazo (damazo), "I tame" - the verb used of taming horses and of taming a lion, from Homer onward. The consistency of the sense across three branches of the family - Sanskrit, Greek and Latin - proves that taming was one of the oldest meanings the root carried, rooted in the shared pastoral life of the earliest Indo-European speakers. The Latin line. In the Latin world, the root produced domare, "to tame, subdue, conquer", used literally of taming horses and, metaphorically, of conquering peoples or mastering one's passions; from it descend indomitable and dominate, and the Germanic adjective tame itself is a distant doublet of the same PIE root, so that tame and daunt are ultimately cousins. Latin literature is full of the metaphor: the orator tames the passions of the crowd, the general tames the rebellious province, the philosopher tames his own desires. The word family links the farmyard (domitor, "tamer") to the household (domus, "house") and to mastery itself (dominus, "lord, master"). The frequentative domitare. Latin then built a frequentative, domitare, "to keep on taming, to tame again and again" - a dogged, iterative form that captured the habitual struggle against resistance, the repeated effort of breaking a creature in. It is this frequentative, not the simple verb, that is the direct ancestor of daunt, and the iterative meaning matters: the tamer must work again and again, because the wild thing resists every time. That sense of repeated, grinding effort is quietly preserved in the modern word's suggestion of a difficulty that must be worked at. Through Old French into English. Old French inherited danter (later spelled dompter by learned scribes, who restored the Latin p), "to tame, conquer, quell", and through Anglo-Norman the word entered Middle English around 1300 as da(u)nten, "to subdue, vanquish, overawe". In the chivalric and legal French of the fourteenth century, to "daunt" was to bring under control - a castle, a country, a beast, a rebellious spirit. The word entered English at a moment of intense French influence, after the Norman Conquest, and it carried with it the whole feudal vocabulary of lordship and conquest. The semantic turn: from conquest to intimidation. The decisive semantic turn came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when daunt moved from physical conquest to psychological effect. The object of the verb changed from wild beasts to human spirits: to daunt a person came to mean to bring their courage down, to overawe, to intimidate, to frighten into hesitation. The verb in this sense appears throughout Renaissance English, meaning "to discourage, dishearten, frighten". The negative adjective dauntless, "fearless, unsubduable", grew from the same stem: a dauntless person is one whom nothing can daunt. The two words - daunt and dauntless - mark the semantic watershed: by the time dauntless was common (from the 1590s onward), the verb's centre had moved decisively from the stable to the mind. The participle daunting (c. 1656). The present participle used as an adjective, daunting, is first recorded about 1656, and it settled into its modern role: describing the thing that daunts. A daunting task, prospect, enterprise or challenge is one that deters by its size or difficulty. The tone of the modern adjective is distinctive: it is a calm, rational hesitation rather than visceral terror. A daunting task does not make you flee; it makes you pause, steel yourself, and begin - which is why success against a daunting task is a genuine achievement of skill. The word has thrived in the modern registers of work and ambition - a daunting schedule, a daunting workload, a daunting mountain to climb - always keeping the suggestion of something that must be met with preparation and courage. The present passage. In our passage the word is perfectly placed. Dr Attridge brings "a well-practised expertise" to "this daunting enterprise" - the enormous scholarly and editorial project that would daunt lesser hands. The etymological metaphor is quietly at work beneath the sentence: the enterprise is a wild thing that must be broken in, subdued and mastered, and the practised expert is the tamer. The author's praise acquires its full force precisely because the word keeps alive, under its modern meaning of intimidation, the ancient image of the tamer and the beast. Conclusion. The career of daunting is a journey from the animal to the abstract: a PIE root of taming (*demh₂-), a Sanskrit family of restraint and the binding rope, a Latin verb and frequentative of breaking-in (domare, domitare), a medieval French and Anglo-Norman word of conquest (danter), a Renaissance English verb of intimidation (daunt), and finally a modern adjective of measured, rational challenge (daunting, c. 1656). Behind the modern word for a hard task stands the oldest of human skills: the taming of the wild.
