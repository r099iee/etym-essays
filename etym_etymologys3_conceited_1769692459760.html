The history of conceited is a history of the mind's two kinds of conceiving — taking in an idea, and taking in a very high opinion of oneself. The word is a tissue of Latin grasping and Old French fancy, and its meaning traveled from the clever notion to the vain person in a few short centuries. It begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *kap-, "to grasp, seize, take, hold." This was one of the most productive roots of the family: in the Germanic branch it became *habjaną "to have" (the original sense "to grasp, hold in the hand" softening into possession), giving English have; and the same "hard to hold" idea gave heavy. In the Italic branch it produced Latin capere, "to take, seize, grasp," the great ancestor of capture, captive, capable, capacity, and cable (a rope that "takes hold").

From capere Latin formed concipere, con- "together, with" + capere "to take" — literally "to take together, to take in." The word worked on two levels at once, and this double life is the key to everything that follows. Physically, concipere meant "to conceive" in the bodily sense — to receive and hold new life in the womb. Intellectually, it meant "to take in with the mind" — to form an idea, to conceive a thought. Both senses flowed into English: conceive a child, conceive an idea, concept, conception. The past participle conceptus, "conceived, taken in," sits beneath the modern English concept — a thing grasped by the mind.

The crucial turn happened in French. Old French used the past participle of concevoir, "to conceive," as a noun: conceit (also conceyte), meaning "a thing conceived in the mind — a notion, a thought, a fancy." Anglo-Norman carried it into English, and by the 14th century Middle English conceit meant a thought, opinion, or concept. In the Elizabethan age the word acquired its most celebrated sense: a "conceit" was an elaborate, witty, far-fetched image — the "metaphysical conceit" of John Donne, who compared lovers' souls to a pair of compasses, or drew an entire argument from a flea. In this literary world the adjective conceited (formed in English from conceit + -ed) first meant "full of fancies, ingenious, clever, imaginative" — a compliment.

The slide from compliment to insult is the word's most instructive chapter. Already in the 16th century conceit could mean "a (too) high opinion," especially in the compound self-conceit — "the opinion one conceives of oneself." A person full of self-conceit had taken in an inflated notion of their own worth; and conceited followed suit, moving from "full of clever notions" to "full of a high opinion of oneself." The older admiring senses were driven out, and by the 17th century the word was firmly a term of reproach. It is a small masterpiece of semantic drift: the same mental act of "conceiving" produced both the poet's brilliant fancy and the bore's inflated self-image.

By the 18th century conceited was precisely the moral fault it remains today — the unjustified, self-admiring estimate of one's own merits. Austen uses it with characteristic sharpness in Pride and Prejudice (1813): the Bingley sisters are "very fine ladies... but proud and conceited." The pairing is exact — pride is their high self-estimation, conceit is the vain self-admiration that colors all their dealings, and both are the vices of people whose fortune and rank have taught them "to think well of themselves, and meanly of others." In the modern idiom the word has kept this force, sitting between everyday vain and clinical narcissistic, always naming the person who has conceived too high an opinion of themselves and, in conceiving it, lost sight of everyone else.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *kap- → Latin capere → concipere → conceptus → Old French conceit → Middle English conceit → English conceited [kənˈsiːtɪd]; the -ed suffix is the ordinary English adjectival ending.
  • Semantic drift: "grasp, take" → "take in (an idea or life)" → "a thing conceived, a notion" → "a clever, fanciful idea" → "a (too) high opinion of oneself" → "vain, self-important."
  • Socio-cultural factors: Renaissance wit culture prized the brilliant conceit; the moralizing 17th–18th centuries turned self-estimation into a diagnosed vice; the novel of manners satirized the conceited aristocrat.
  • Literary attestations: John Donne's metaphysical conceits (17th c.); 16th-century moralists; Austen's Pride and Prejudice (1813) — "proud and conceited"; modern everyday usage.
  • Modern situation: the literary "conceit" survives as a term of art; the adjective is wholly negative and everyday, describing vain self-importance.

Conceited is thus a word with a double life: its noun cousin still names the highest flights of poetic fancy, while the adjective names the lowest form of self-regard. The two senses — brilliant imagination and puffed-up vanity — are, etymologically, the same act of the mind that "takes in" too much.
