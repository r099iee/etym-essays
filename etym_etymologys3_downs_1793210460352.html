The story of downs begins not in England but in the hill-forts of the Celtic west. Reconstructed Proto-Celtic *dūno- meant a fort or fortified height — a place of refuge with a bank and a view — and it survives in Old Irish dún, Welsh din, and the Gaulish town-names scattered across Roman maps in -dunum, from Lugdunum to Cambodunum. When Saxon settlers spread across the chalk country of southern Britain, they took the British word from British mouths and applied it to the smooth open ridges themselves: Old English dūn is a hill, fortified or not, and the great chalk spines of the south became, simply, the hills.

Sheep make the meaning:

  • The Saxon sheep-walk: chalk will not grow corn kindly, but its thin sweet turf fattens sheep superbly; from the earliest charters the downs are valued exactly as the source sentence values them — as reliable sheep food, folded, grazed, and manured in the great open-field routine.
  • Wool wealth: medieval monasteries ran vast downland flocks, and the wool trade built churches and fortunes; the word down travelled with the bales, naming the upland pastures of the Cotswolds' edge and beyond.
  • The Southdown breed: 18th-century Sussex farmers perfected the neat dun-faced Southdown sheep on these hills, and the breed's name carried down around the farming world — to Australia, New Zealand, and America — wherever chalk-like pastures wanted the perfect sheep.

The proper noun and the playground:

  • Capital letters: by the early modern period the two great ranges were the Downs — North and South — landmarks for travellers, smugglers, and armies alike; the sheltered Kent roadstead borrowed the name for its anchorage behind the Goodwin Sands.
  • Sport on turf: the springy chalk sward proved perfect for galloping horses, and Epsom, Goodwood, and Brighton laid their courses on downland; the Derby has been run over downs turf since 1780.
  • Literature's hills: from Cobbett's Rural Rides through Kipling's Puck of Pook's Hill to W. H. Hudson's shepherds, the downs became the stage of English nostalgia — larks, dewponds, hill-figures, and sea-winds — the open hill as the nation's memory palace.

The living sward: modern science has confirmed the shepherds' judgment with new reasons: unimproved chalk grassland, grazed short for centuries, holds more species per square metre than almost any English habitat — orchids, rock-rose, bastard toadflax, Adonis blues — and survives only where grazing continues. The South Downs National Park now guards the bargain struck in the source sentence: the chalk feeds the turf, the turf feeds the sheep, and the sheep, cropping close, keep the whole flowering commons alive. Few English words bind geology, farming, sport, and poetry so tightly as these five quiet letters. And the sentence that prompted this card states the oldest clause of that compact: upon a bed of chalk, the downs always afford good sheep food.
