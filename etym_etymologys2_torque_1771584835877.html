TORQUE — two chains, both from the same Latin verb; chain (a) yields the neck-ring, chain (b) the physics term. Chain (a) — the neck ornament: • PIE *terkʷ- "to twist" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; attested in Greek átraktos "spindle," in Sanskrit tarkú- "spindle," and in Latin torquēre "to twist"; the root is one of the more securely reconstructed PIE roots of twisting). Debate: some handbooks separate the Germanic word for "distaff" (Old English *þeorel) and dispute whether Greek and Sanskrit forms are cognate, but the Latin evidence for a twisting root is secure. → Latin torquēre "to twist, turn, wind, spin; to distort" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, in Plautus and Ennius; a common verb with a large family: tortus, torques, torqueō, tortio). → Latin torques (also torquis, 3rd declension) "a necklace, collar; a twisted metal neck-ring" (attested from the 2nd–1st c. BCE; Caesar and Livy use it for the Gallic neck-ring; the formation is a deverbal noun "the twisted thing"). → Old French torque / torce "a twisted band, a collar" (inherited or borrowed from Latin torqua, attested in Old French from the 11th–12th centuries; the shape torque/torque circulates in Romance). → Middle French torque, Modern French torque (the form of the late medieval/Renaissance term for a twisted collar or band, whence the English spelling). → English torque "a neck ornament, the Celtic neck-ring of twisted metal" (borrowed from French torque in the late 16th–17th century — OED first records c. 1580s — and used especially of ancient Gallic and British collars; the spelling "torc" is a later archaeological variant). → Modern English TORQUE (neck-ring sense). Chain (b) — the physics term (same Latin root, separate modern coinage): • PIE *terkʷ- "to twist" (as above) → Latin torquēre "to twist" (attested, 3rd c. BCE) → Latin torquēre, past participle tortus, with the gerundive/verbal noun torque "a twisting" (the direct Latin source of the modern term; Latin torqueō supplies the stem). → New Latin torque / English torque "rotational force, the moment of a force about an axis" (technical coinage of the 19th century — attributed to the physicist James Clerk Maxwell in the 1880s, who proposed "torque" as the name for the turning moment, from Latin torquēre "to twist"; the term was adopted in mechanical and electrical engineering, with the unit eventually standardized as the newton-metre). → Modern English TORQUE (mechanics sense). Note: the two senses are distinct loans (the ornament via Old French in the 16th century; the physics term coined from Latin in the 19th century) converging on the same spelling; both are fully attested and uncontested from Latin onward.
