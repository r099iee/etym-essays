Wheezes is the third-person singular present form of the verb wheeze, and also the plural of the noun wheeze. In its primary physical sense it names breathing — or any air-forced sound — with a high, whistling, rasping quality produced when airways are narrowed: asthma, bronchitis, allergic constriction, colds, age, or exertion. The sound is continuous or recurrent rather than a single sharp gasp; it suggests labor, obstruction, and often frailty. The verb is used intransitively (a person wheezes) and transitively (to wheeze out a few words between breaths).

Core senses.

  • Sense 1 — respiratory (primary). To breathe with a hoarse whistling sound, or to make such a sound, because the airways are constricted. In clinical contexts a wheeze is defined as a high-pitched, musical, predominantly expiratory sound of narrowed air passages — a standard sign on auscultation in asthma, COPD, and bronchitis.
  • Sense 2 — mechanical (metaphoric extension). Machines that force air or steam through worn or narrow passages "wheeze": old engines, bellows, accordions, pumps, and — as on this card — an ancient steam radiator that "clanks and wheezes," slowly blowing warm air. The metaphor transfers the strained, squeezed-air acoustics of the human airway to mechanical devices.
  • Sense 3 — animal/labored (pathos). Animals — especially old dogs and horses — wheeze; the word carries the pathos of age, illness, and exhaustion whenever a living creature labors for breath.
  • Sense 4 — noun, British slang/theatrical (secondary). As a noun, wheeze means (i) the whistling sound itself, (ii) a clever scheme or trick, and (iii) in theatrical and comic jargon, a stock gag, old joke, or labored routine. Hence wheezes as "old jokes, tired tricks, theatrical routines" — connecting "tired breath" with "tired material."

The historical bridge between senses. The respiratory sense is primary and oldest; the mechanical extension arises naturally whenever people hear a device making the same squeezed-whistling noise as a wheezing chest. The slang sense — a clever scheme or a stock comic routine — is dated to 19th-century British show business and slang; the likely connection is the idea of something "got out with effort" (a gag laboriously delivered) or a joke that wheezes its way out, and it yields collocations like "an old wheeze" for a tired joke.

Collocations, register, domains. Collocations: wheezes heavily/loudly; chest wheezes; old engine/radiator wheezes; asthma wheezes; political/comic wheezes (noun). The respiratory sense spans neutral-medical to everyday descriptive language; the slang sense is informal, British-tinged, slightly dated or theatrical. Domains: medicine, caregiving narrative, industrial and mechanical description, fiction (atmosphere of age, cold, illness), and British theatrical journalism.

Antonyms and contrasts. Respiratory ideals: breathes easily, breathes freely, breathes silently. Against near-synonyms, a wheeze is continuous and musical (unlike a single gasp), involves obstruction noise (unlike mere rapid panting), and is high-pitched (unlike the coarse rattles or moist rales of the clinic).
