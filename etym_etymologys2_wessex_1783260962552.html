CHAIN 1 — THE WEST ELEMENT (West-): Stage 1 — PIE *wes-pero- "evening, west" (reconstructed; the west conceived as the quarter of the setting sun; the deeper segmentation of the root — *we-/*wes- "to pass the night, dwell" — is DEBATED and should not be pressed beyond what the handbooks allow). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *westraz / *westra- "west, westwards" (reconstructed; cf. Old Norse vestr "west", Old High German westar "westwards", Gothic *wistr-). Stage 3 — Old English west "west, towards the west; the west" (attested from the 8th century in glosses and verse). Stage 4 — element West- in the compound Westseaxe "the West Saxons" (attested 7th–9th century; the kingdom's self-designation, distinguishing its people from the East and South Saxons). Stage 5 — contracted compound Wessex (Middle English Westsex / Wessex from the 12th–13th century; the unstressed medial vowel of Westseaxe was lost). CHAIN 2 — THE SAXON ELEMENT (-sex, from Seaxe): Stage 1 — PIE *sek- "to cut" (reconstructed; a well-established root, cf. Latin secare "to cut", Old English sagu "saw"). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *sahsą "knife, single-edged sword" (reconstructed; the seax, the characteristic blade of the tribe). Stage 3 — Latin Saxōnēs "the Saxons" (attested from the 2nd century AD — Ptolemy's Geography, and later Ammianus Marcellinus in the 4th century; a Latin rendering of the Germanic self-name). Stage 4 — Old English Seaxe "the Saxons" (attested from the 8th century; the nativized tribal name). Stage 5 — Westseaxe "the West Saxons" (the kingdom name, attested 7th–9th century), a compound of west + Seaxe. Stage 6 — contracted Wessex (Middle English from the 12th–13th century; the modern English proper noun, revived and popularized as a historical and literary name in the 19th century). DEBATE NOTE: the "knife" derivation of the Saxon tribal name (from *sahsą "blade") is traditional and widely accepted but not absolutely certain; some scholars treat the tribal name as of obscure origin, possibly attached to the same root by folk association. The Westseaxe compound and its contraction to Wessex, by contrast, are historically secure.
