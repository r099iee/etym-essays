- [Old English (before 1100)] - hālgian, "to make holy, consecrate," from hālig "holy": used of churches, altars, ground, and persons set apart for God — a strictly religious word of formal consecration.
- [Medieval Christian use] - Fixed in the liturgy — "hallowed be thy name," hallowed ground — carrying the full weight of divine sanctity and the awe owed to what God has set apart.
- [16th–19th century: the figurative transfer] - Hallowed loosens from the altar to anything treated as sacred by tradition: hallowed customs, hallowed memories, hallowed institutions — reverence and long custom, not divinity, now do the consecrating.
- [Modern register] - Predominantly figurative and mildly archaic-solemn: hallowed names the quasi-religious reverence paid to tradition, precedent, and canon — sanctity by venerable usage rather than by God; the literal sense survives frozen in "Halloween" (All Hallows' Eve).
