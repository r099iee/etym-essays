Aleutian is not an Indo-European inheritance at all but a colonial-era ethnonym turned toponym turned adjective, tracking Russian, British, and American encounters with the Unangan people of the North Pacific. Understanding it requires Indigenous, Russian, and English layers together.

The people call themselves Unangan (singular Unangax̂), meaning coastal people or we-people, speakers of an isolated branch of the Eskimo-Aleut language family. Outsiders, however, came to call them Aleut. The precise source is debated:

  • Chukchi hypothesis (most cited): Siberian Chukchi əlʹəwət / aliat islanders was picked up by Cossack fur traders and rendered in Russian as aleut. This fits the pattern of exonyms imposed via intermediaries.
  • Unangan-source hypothesis: eastern Unangan alaxsxaq mainland, peninsula (referring to Alaska) was misheard and generalized to the people. Early wordlists show agæljan, aliat variants.
  • Russian folk-etymology: once borrowed, Russian speakers linked it to familiar phonetics, stabilizing spelling алеут by the 1750s.

Russian promyshlenniki reached the Near Islands in 1745 after Bering's 1741 voyage. Catherine II's administration classified Aleuty as tribute-paying natives for the sea-otter trade. Russian America (1799–1867) centred on Unalaska and Kodiak; Orthodox missionaries created an Aleut alphabet, and the term entered European science via Cook (1778), who wrote Aleut and Aleutian Islands into English charts. After the U.S. purchase of Alaska (1867), American ethnology fixed Aleut for the people and language and Aleutian for anything pertaining to them.

Morphologically, English added the Latinate suffix -ian meaning belonging to, as in Parisian, Bostonian:

  • Aleut (noun): a member of the people; the language (now often called Unangam Tunuu in revitalization contexts).
  • Aleutian (adjective/noun): of or relating to the Aleuts, their language, or the 1,200-mile volcanic archipelago from Attu to the Alaska Peninsula. As noun, an inhabitant; often in plural Aleutians for the island chain.

Capitalization preserves proper-name status: Aleutian women, Aleutian kayak (baidarka), Aleutian basketry, Aleutian Low (the semi-permanent North Pacific low-pressure system named for its position). Lowercase aleutian is an error in standard usage.

Cultural history deepens the word:

  • Technology admired worldwide: the Unangan iqyax̂ (kayak) and ulax̂ bentwood hat, and the year's-long woven spruce-root robe and grass baskets noted in the source sentence, became anthropological type-specimens of extreme adaptation to treeless, stormy islands.
  • Trauma and resilience: forced relocations, disease, and WWII internment decimated communities; since the 1970s Unangan activists have reclaimed Unangan over Aleut, so sensitive writers now prefer Unangan people with Aleutian as geographic adjective.
  • Natural history extension: Aleutian Canada goose, Aleutian tern, Aleutian shield fern — biologists use Aleutian for endemic Beringian taxa, and meteorologists for the Aleutian Low that steers Pacific storms.

Phonetically, Russian /ɐlʲɪˈut/ entered English as /əˈluːt/ with anglicized stress, and Aleutian is /əˈluːʃən/ by palatalization of /t/ before -ian, parallel to Venetian, Martian. The long chain from Unangan self-naming through Chukchi and Russian ears to English maps thus encodes first contact, fur-trade empire, and modern reclamation in a single adjective.
