Bourgeoisie is a textbook Germanic-Romance hybrid whose form tells urban history: a Germanic fort-word Latinized, Gallicized, then suffixed as an abstract collective and exported worldwide with Marx.

The ultimate root is PIE *bʰerǵʰ-, high, lofty, fortified elevation, source of English borough, burgh, bury (Canterbury), berg,堡 cognates and Sanskrit bṛhant (high). In Proto-Germanic this yields *burgs (feminine) — fortified hill, stronghold, later town — preserved in Gothic baurgs, Old High German burg, Old English burh (fortified town, borough). Late Latin borrows it back as burgus (Vegetius, 4th century) for frontier fort-town, a rare Germanic loan into imperial administration.

  • Old French bourg to burgeis: Gallo-Romance bourg (walled market town) produces burgeis (12th century, Chanson de Roland, charters) — free town-dweller with walls, oath and market rights, distinct from villein and knight. Anglo-Norman carries burgeis into Middle English as burgeis, burgess (town representative, parliamentary burgess).
  • Middle French bourgeois: by the 16th century spelling stabilizes as bourgeois (with ou for /u/), meaning urban householder, master-artisan, merchant; adjective bourgeois — of the town, common, un-noble. The suffix -ie (Latin -ia) forms collectives as in clergie, pairie; bourgeoisie is first attested 1707 in Saint-Simonian administrative French for the body of bourgeois.
  • Revolutionary and Marxist elevation: Sieyès' What is the Third Estate? (1789) makes bourgeoisie the nation; 19th-century industrialization splits it into grande, moyenne, petite; Marx and Engels in Manifesto (1848, German Bourgeoisie borrowed directly from French) fix it as owners of means of production. English borrows whole in early 19th century (Byron, 1820s travel writing) keeping French /ʒwɑː/ and nasal vowel anglicized.
  • Phonetics: French /buʁʒwazi/ → RP /ˌbʊəʒwɑːˈziː/ with initial secondary stress, /ʒ/ preserved, /wɑː/ for oi, final /ziː/. Spelling retains ou, eo, ie French values opaque to English.
  • Semantic pejoration: from legal-neutral to economic-analytic to cultural-insult. Flaubert's Dictionnaire des idées reçues, Baudelaire, Rimbaud and later Dada turn economic category into aesthetic accusation: to be bourgeois is to love straight lines, correct spelling, balanced accounts — exactly the alphabetic reason the present sentence says pranks attack.

Thus the passage's claim — it was more than the bourgeoisie pranks sought to pique — depends on this double sense: sociologically the middle-class audience booed at Cabaret Voltaire, philosophically the bourgeois mind identified with linear print rationality. The loanword's Frenchness itself performs distance: English writers keep it French to keep it critical.

Attestations: burgus in Vegetius 4th c.; burgeis 1080 Domesday Anglo-Norman; bourgeois 1530s Rabelais; bourgeoisie 1707 Dangeau memoirs; English 1715 Defoe bourgeois, 1830s Bulwer bourgeoisie; Marx 1848; Flaubert 1850s letters; Tzara 1918 épater la bourgeoisie.
