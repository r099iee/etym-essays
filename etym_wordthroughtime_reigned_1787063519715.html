- 13th–14th c.: reigned enters English as the past tense of reign, borrowed from Old French regner for royal rule — a solemn, courtly, chronicle word.
- 14th–16th c.: standard in royal records and chronicles for stating the length of a monarch’s tenure.
- 17th c.: figurative use flowers in Shakespeare and the King James Bible — error reigned, silence reigned, sin reigned, night reigned — extending a king’s authority to abstractions and nature.
- 18th–19th c.: “reigned from … to …” becomes the fossil formula of royal chronology; the figurative sense grows fully idiomatic in journalism.
- 20th–21st c.: reigned keeps both faces — a dignified historical verb for monarchs and a natural figurative verb for conditions that hold sway (“confusion reigned”); the reign/rein/rain homophone trio remains a classic spelling trap.
