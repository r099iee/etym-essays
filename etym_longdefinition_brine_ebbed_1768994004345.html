brine: Water saturated with salt — the intensely saline liquor of the sea and of pickling, and here the substance with which the sky's darkness is compared: "dark as the brine of desert seas." The word is chosen for its deadness and bitterness: brine is sea-water that has lost its life, reduced to a chemical residue — oxygen-poor, sterile, corrosive — in which nothing can live. The simile therefore makes the sky not merely dark but lethally saline, as if the heavens had the look of a dried ocean-bed. The nuance is of harshness, ancientness, and abandonment: brine is what an ocean becomes when it dies, and the sky shares that deathly cast. Primary and extended senses.
  • Salt water proper: brine is water strongly charged with salt — the seawater of the ocean, especially concentrated by evaporation, or any salt solution of similar strength. In this core sense it is distinguished from plain "seawater" by its saturation: brine is the state of water in which life fails.
  • The pickling bath: in cookery and food preservation, brine is the strong salt solution in which meat, fish, and vegetables are cured — a bath that preserves flesh by making it uninhabitable to the organisms of decay. To brine is to soak in such a bath; the verb and the noun share the same sharpness.
  • Industrial and scientific: in industry and chemistry, brine is the saturated salt solution of the salt-works and the chlor-alkali process, from which salt and other chemicals are recovered; "brine" is also the technical term for the dense saline fluids used in refrigeration and drilling.
  • Geological: geologically, brine is the residual water of dying seas and salt lakes — the Dead Sea's water, or the deep, dense brine pools that settle at the bottom of ocean basins where the salt has concentrated and nothing lives.
  • Poetic transfer: because brine is what remains when water dies, the word carries an elegiac, desiccated tone in literature — the sea's bitter residue, the dregs of an ocean, harsh and sterile, which is exactly the note Peake strikes.
ebbed: Past tense of ebb — to flow back or recede, of tide, and by extension of water, life, or vigour withdrawing from its former fullness. "Desert seas that have ebbed to desert pools" describes seas that have retreated from their ancient breadth and drained down to stagnant remnants. The word carries the full tidal rhythm of withdrawal — slow, inexorable, hopeless — and its tone here is geological and elegiac: whole seas have gone out and will never return, and what remains (the brine, the pools) is the dregs of their departure. Primary and extended senses.
  • Tidal: to ebb is the tide's going out, the regular fall of the sea from full to low water; ebb and flood name the two halves of the tidal cycle, and the word has named that movement from the earliest English texts.
  • Of water generally: by extension, any water that recedes or falls — rivers in drought, floods going down, seas withdrawing from a shore — ebbs.
  • Figurative withdrawal: the dominant modern sense is the withdrawal of life, strength, hope, courage, or fortune: life ebbs away, patience ebbs, enthusiasm ebbs. The metaphor keeps the tidal rhythm — something that was full and is now, slowly and inexorably, emptying.
  • The ebb of fortune: "the ebb and flow" of events, luck, and business keeps the image of the tides as a figure for rise and fall, gain and loss — the natural rhythm of everything that waxes and wanes.
Comparison of the pair. The two words work together as the sentence's engine: the seas ebbed, and their bitter residue — the brine — is what the sky is now dark with. Against their near-synonyms they are exact: seawater is merely descriptive; salt names the mineral rather than the liquid; sea implies living, moving water — only brine denotes the saturated, killing solution. For ebbed: receded is formal and neutral; retreated suggests deliberate withdrawal; subsided implies sinking down; drained implies an external agency drawing the water off — only ebbed preserves the tidal metaphor and its rhythm of regular, inevitable withdrawal.
