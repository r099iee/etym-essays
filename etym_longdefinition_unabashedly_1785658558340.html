UNABASHEDLY: In a manner entirely free of shame, embarrassment, self-consciousness, or fear of disapproval; openly, boldly, and without a flicker of hesitation, even in circumstances that would make most people withdraw or apologize. The adverb describes the manner of an action — how something is said, done, worn, or displayed — and its whole force rests on the claim that the person acting feels no compunction at all. The central sense: freedom from shame. At its core, unabashedly means "in a way that shows no shame or embarrassment." Where bashfulness is the instinct that makes us lower our eyes, blush, soften our voice, or retreat when we are exposed to scrutiny, to act unabashedly is to act as though that instinct has been switched off. It does not deny that the exposure is happening — it denies that the exposure matters to the actor. In the card's sentence — "She stepped onto the stage unabashedly, facing the skeptical crowd without a flicker of hesitation" — the word measures the gap between the audience and the performer: the crowd is skeptical, ready to judge, and yet the stage is crossed without a flicker of hesitation. Unlike bravely, which implies fear felt and then overcome, unabashedly implies no fear that touches composure at all.
  • Sense 1 — composure under exposure (primary): performing, speaking, or existing in the full glare of judgment without discomposure; "she praised her own work unabashedly," "he faced the criticism unabashedly."
  • Sense 2 — bold confidence and candor: doing or declaring something bold, personal, or unconventional with confident openness; "unabashedly honest," "unabashedly proud of her origins," "unabashedly sentimental." Here the emphasis is on authenticity worn without apology, and the tone is approving.
  • Sense 3 — shamelessness / impudence (negative charge): when the thing done is vulgar, improper, or selfish, unabashedly shades into "shamelessly" or "impudently"; "he unabashedly padded his expense account." The same absence of shame that is admirable in candor becomes offensive in insolence.
  • Sense 4 — without concealment, openly: the word also carries transparency — nothing hidden, no pretense of modesty; "the ads were unabashedly commercial," "a politician unabashedly courting the wealthy."
The historical bridge between the senses. The word is the adverb built on abashed ("put to shame, disconcerted") plus the native English negative prefix un-. A person who is abashed has had their composure broken by shame or confusion; a person who is unabashed is one on whom shame cannot get a purchase. The shift from the adjective to the adverb (c. 1840s) is a shift from state ("not disconcerted") to manner ("in a not-disconcerted way"), and it allowed the word to attach to verbs of saying, looking, declaring, and behaving. As 19th-century culture increasingly prized reserve and concealment, the adverb became the favorite of writers praising anyone who refused to be disciplined into hiding — hence its modern approving charge in phrases like "unabashedly authentic." Register and usage. Mid-formal, journalistic-to-literary, mildly approving. It is common in reviews, profiles, and essays ("an unabashedly sentimental film," "an unabashedly ambitious founder"), and rare in casual speech, where "without shame" or "not embarrassed to" usually takes its place. It works as a manner adverb modifying a verb ("she unabashedly announced...") or as a modifier of a predicate adjective ("unabashedly proud"). Related forms. Unabashed (adjective, 1670s) "not disconcerted"; abashed (adjective) "embarrassed, disconcerted"; abash (verb) "to destroy the self-possession of"; bashful (adjective) "shy, self-conscious," from the same Old French stem esbahir; unabashedness (rare noun). Note the family's shared image: to abash is to "lower" a person, to bring the proud gaze down to the floor; unabashedly is the adverb of the person whose gaze never drops.
