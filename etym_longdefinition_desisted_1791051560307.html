Desisted is the past tense and past participle of the verb desist: to have stopped doing something, ceased an action, or refrained from continuing, especially after being asked, ordered or persuaded. Perhaps they, too, were once civilized, and desisted from it as a nuisance captures the formal, slightly literary sense: they voluntarily abandoned civilization, standing off from it as one would from a bother.

Grammatically desist is intransitive and almost always takes from plus gerund or noun: desist from smoking, desist from trespassing, desist from further action. Desisted therefore frames cessation as deliberate restraint rather than mere ending. One does not desisted by accident; one desists out of prudence, obedience, fatigue or disillusion. Legal language loves it: cease-and-desist letters order recipients to desisted in future — to have stopped and to stay stopped.

Stylistically desisted is formal, official and a degree archaic in conversation. Everyday speech prefers stopped, quit, gave up, knocked it off, packed it in. To write desisted signals law, minutes, history or moralizing anthropology, as in the source sentence about peoples abandoning civilization. The perfect and pluperfect — have desisted, had desisted — stress completed forbearance with lingering effect.

  • Sense 1 — Core: stopped, ceased: They desisted from firing. The noise desisted at midnight (rare impersonal use). Bridge: Latin desistere to stand away, hence to leave off. Implies a prior ongoing activity now halted.
  • Sense 2 — Legal-prohibitive: refrained on demand: He desisted after receiving the cease-and-desist order. After warnings, the company desisted from infringement. Here desistance is compliance; failure to have desisted invites penalty.
  • Sense 3 — Moral-prudential: gave up as nuisance or error: As in the context, desisted from civilization as a nuisance. They judged the thing not worth continuing. Similar to abstained, but abstain is prospective, desist is interruptive.
  • Sense 4 — Archaic transitive glimpses: Early Modern English occasionally desisted something (desist thy suit), now obsolete; modern use requires from. The -ed form can also be adjectival: desisted efforts, though rare.
  • Aspect and collocation: Desisted is punctual-completive: it views stopping as an event. It collocates with efforts, attempts, hostilities, practices, proceedings, operations. It resists progressive (*were desisting is rare) and prefers simple past or perfect.

Connotation: restrained, dignified, non-violent. Armies desist from shelling; neighbours desist from complaints; scholars desist from speculation. It never means collapsed or failed, always chose to stop. In anthropological prose — once civilized, and desisted — it lends ironic grandeur: civilization as a hobby one can politely drop.

Legal drafting still prefers desisted for its completed, compliant nuance: to have desisted is not merely to have paused but to have obeyed and to remain stopped, a shade that stopped and quit cannot reliably convey.
