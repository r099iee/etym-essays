- [Vedic Samhitā Era (c. 1500–1000 BCE; Ṛgveda X.60.4 & Atharvaveda XIX.39.9)] - first attested in the earliest Vedic hymns as the name of a celebrated ancient monarch or tribal prince whose lineage is praised for unyielding strength and divine favor - [perception: revered as an ancient, quasi-divine royal chieftain of the early Indo-Aryan peoples]
- [Epic Itihāsa & Rāmāyaṇa Era (c. 500 BCE–200 BCE; Vālmīki)] - elevated in the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata into the supreme foundational progenitor of the Solar Dynasty (Sūryavaṃśa) and first king of Ayodhyā, whose bloodline defines ideal kingship and dharma - [perception: celebrated across the subcontinent as the glorious ancestral root of Lord Rāma]
- [Buddhist Canonical Period (c. 5th–3rd c. BCE; Pali Tipiṭaka)] - preserved in Middle Indo-Aryan as King Okkāka in texts like the Ambaṭṭha Sutta and Mahāvastu, revered as the distant royal ancestor from whose banished sons and daughters the noble Śākya clan originated - [perception: venerated by early Buddhists as the proud royal ancestor of Siddhartha Gautama, establishing the Buddha's unblemished kṣatriya lineage]
- [Classical Jain Agamas & Purāṇas (c. early 1st millennium CE)] - integrated into Jain universal history (*Śalākāpuruṣa*) as the sacred lineage initiated by Lord Ṛṣabhanātha (Ādinātha), the first Tīrthaṅkara, who taught humanity agriculture, writing, and statecraft - [perception: honored as the most sacred spiritual and royal lineage in Jain cosmology, yielding 22 of the 24 Tīrthaṅkaras]
- [Historical Epigraphy & Deccan Sovereignty (3rd–4th c. CE; Nagarjunakonda)] - adopted by the historical Andhra Ikshvaku dynasty of coastal Andhra Pradesh, who claimed descent from the epic solar king while patronizing major Buddhist monasteries and performing Vedic sacrifices - [perception: utilized by southern imperial rulers as the ultimate symbol of ancient legitimacy and prestigious solar bloodline]
- [Medieval Purāṇic & Bhakti Synthesis (c. 6th–17th c. CE)] - extensively systematized in the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, Bhāgavata Purāṇa, and Tulsīdās's Rāmacaritamānasa, cementing the phrase "House of Ikṣvāku" as the literary archetype of sacrifice, truthfulness, and royal duty - [perception: invoked in devotional poetry and temple liturgy as an eternal symbol of noble righteous conduct]
- [Modern Indology, Epigraphy & Living Tradition (19th–21st c.)] - analyzed by modern historical linguists, archaeologists, and comparative mythologists examining ancient Indian urbanization, Vedic-substrate interactions, and epic geography - [perception: universally recognized as one of the most enduring foundational patronymics in world epic literature]
