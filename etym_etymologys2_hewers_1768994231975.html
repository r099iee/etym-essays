PIE *kau- “to strike, hew, beat” (c. 4500–2500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *hawwaną “to hew, strike, chop” (c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Old English hēawan “to cut, chop, hew, strike with a blade” (attested c. 700 CE, in early verse) → Middle English hewen “to hew, cut” (c. 1150–1500) → English hew “to cut, shape, or fell with a blade” (modern) → Old English agent noun hēawa / -hēawere “one who hews” (attested c. 1000) → Middle English hewere “one who hews” (c. 1300) → English hewer (modern), whose plural hewers is fixed in the biblical collocation “hewers of wood and drawers of water” (King James Bible, 1611). Debate/nuance: the precise PIE reconstruction behind Proto-Germanic *hawwaną is debated — Kroonen and others group it under a root *kaw- “to strike, hew,” while some handbooks allow variant laryngeal-colouring reconstructions — but the Germanic form and all later stages are secure. Note also that Latin caedō “to cut down” belongs to a different root (*keh₂id-) and is excluded; the Latin cousin usually cited is cūdō “to beat, pound,” from the same *kau-, which also yielded caudex/cōdex “tree trunk, book” (hence English codex, code).
