BOA — a Latin snake-name without a secure root: boa enters modern zoology as Linnaeus's genus Boa (Systema Naturae, 10th ed., 1758), but the word is far older. Latin boa (also spelled boa, bova) appears in Pliny the Elder's Natural History (8.14) and later in Solinus and Isidore as a huge Italian serpent that suckled cows — bos-associated by folk-etymology — or infested Calabria. Etymonline, OED, and de Vaan agree the deeper origin is unknown and probably non-Indo-European or expressive. Ancient guesses linked it to bos (ox, because it was said to suck cows), to Greek boas, or to a hissing imitative bo-, but none shows regular sound correspondences. The honest statement is therefore: earliest securely attested form is Latin boa, a large snake, 1st c. CE; earlier derivation unknown. Linnaeus revived the classical word for the American snakes brought back by Dutch and Portuguese travellers, fixing it as a genus: Boa constrictor, Boa canina (emerald tree boa), Boaedon by extension.

  • Classical report: Pliny describes Calabrian boae of enormous size persecuted for killing cattle; the account mixes pythons, hearsay, and moralising. Medieval bestiaries copy it with illuminations of ox-suckling serpents, keeping the word alive in learned Latin.
  • Linnaean revival (1758): Linnaeus needed a short classical genus for the New World constrictors in the royal collections. He chose Boa for its brevity, antiquity, and vagueness — a ready label with no competing meaning. Pronunciation anglicised to /ˈbəʊə/ with two syllables.
  • Victorian menagerie fame: travelling shows, Exeter Change, and from 1828 the London Zoo displayed live boas, advertised on handbills as Huge Boa Constrictors! Alive! Children's natural histories (Goldsmith, Mudie, Wood) illustrated them coiled round deer, fixing the popular image Ruskin draws on.
  • Garment transfer (1830s): feather stoles called boas because they wind like snakes; e.g. Dickens and Thackeray dress vain ladies in boas. The zoological and fashion senses coexist for a century without confusion because context disambiguates.

CONSTRICTORS — from Latin stringere to draw tight: constrictor (plural constrictors) is a regular Latin agent noun in -tor: one who constricts. It builds on constrictus, past participle of constringere, to bind together, to draw tight — itself con- (together, intensive) + stringere (to draw tight, to strip, to graze). Latin stringere continues PIE *strenk- or *streig-, narrow, tight, taut — cognate with Old English streng (string, cord), German Strang, Greek strangos (twisted), English string, strain, strict, strait, constrict, astringent. De Vaan derives stringo from *stren-g-, to press, with nasal infix, noting the ( constrictor family (constrict, constriction, constrictive) as secure, while the exact PIE laryngeal shape is debated among specialists — a nuance to record, not to hide. Latin authors use constringere for binding faggots, freezing rivers (cold constricts), and grief constricting the heart; Late Latin medical writers use constrictor for sphincter muscles. English borrows the adjective constrict via French in the 15th c. and the agent constrictor in the 17th c. for anatomy (constrictor of the pharynx) before Linnaeus applies it to snakes.

  • Phonetic path: PIE *strenk- gives Latin stringere / strictus with regular -ng- to -nct- alternation (stringo, strinxi, strictus). Prefix con- assimilates only in spelling (constringere). English keeps Latin spelling and anglicises stress to /kənˈstrɪktə/ with reduced first vowel and /ɒ/ to /ə/ weakening.
  • Anatomical precedence: 17th–18th c. surgeons name throat and eye muscles constrictores; e.g. superior constrictor of the pharynx. The snake sense is therefore a metaphor from the body: the serpent acts like a living sphincter.
  • Herpetological fixing (1758–present): Linnaeus's binomen Boa constrictor makes constrictor a species epithet; popular English nominalises it (a constrictor, constrictors) for any squeezing snake. Modern taxonomy splits boas and pythons but keeps constrictor in trade names.
  • Plural morphology: Latin constrictores levels to English constrictors with regular /z/ plural; hyphenation with boa- follows 19th-century compound habit for natural-history names.

Recombined, boa-constrictor is first attested in English travel and natural-history prose in the late 18th century (Pennant 1781, Shaw 1802) as boa constrictor, quickly hyphenated in book and newspaper English. Its 19th-century heyday coincides with empire, menageries, and leather fire-hoses: the same public that paid to see a boa crush a rabbit watched parish hoses swell with water. The simile pipes like boa-constrictors is therefore period-exact, not fanciful — both are thick, jointed, muscular tubes that fill and writhe. Modern usage keeps the hyphen in British field guides, drops it in American pet trade (boa constrictor), and extends it metaphorically to debts, monopolies, and regulations that slowly squeeze.
