discontented (adjective) — dissatisfied, unhappy, or restlessly displeased with one's circumstances; lacking inner contentment; chronically ill at ease with one's lot.

Primary sense: chronic dissatisfaction

Discontented describes a state of mind, not a single grievance. A person who is discontented is not merely annoyed by a specific thing; the displeasure colours the whole disposition. In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Mrs. Bennet "when she was discontented, fancied herself nervous": the word captures a temperament that converts every small discomfort into self-dramatized suffering, and Austen's irony lies in the gap between the trivial causes (daughters' prospects, visiting, news) and the theatrical effect. Discontentment is a mood that feeds on itself.

Distinction: discontented vs. dissatisfied

Dissatisfied is the neutral, focused word for being displeased with a specific result — dissatisfied with the service, with the salary. Discontented is the deeper, more general word: it implies an unsettled, restless unhappiness with one's overall situation or temper, often with an edge of complaint, self-pity, or agitation. Dissatisfaction is about a thing; discontent is about a state of being.

Noun, adjective, and verb forms

  • discontent (noun): the state or feeling of dissatisfaction, especially with the existing order — "political discontent," "social discontent."
  • discontent (adjective, archaic): same meaning as discontented, now chiefly poetical.
  • discontent (verb, rare): to deprive of contentment; the participle "discontenting."
  • malcontent (noun/adjective): a discontented person, especially one actively hostile to the established order — a stronger, more political word.

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Social and political unrest: "Now is the winter of our discontent" — Shakespeare's Richard III (c. 1592–94) made the noun the emblem of political dissatisfaction with a reigning order; "the discontented" can name a class or faction resentful of authority, giving the word overtones of agitation, grumbling, and revolt.
  • Restlessness and agitation: the adjectival form implies a body and mind that cannot settle — pacing, fidgeting, complaining — rather than quiet sadness.
  • Self-pitying register: in modern use "discontented" often carries a faintly negative judgement of the person so described (as in Mrs. Bennet's case): their dissatisfaction is seen as excessive or theatrical rather than justified.

Semantic bridge

The word is built from the negative prefix dis- ("apart, un-") plus contented. Content comes from Latin contentus, the past participle of continēre ("to hold together, contain"): the contented person is one whose desires are "contained," held safely within internal boundaries like water in a vessel. To be discontented is to be "uncontained" — desires overflowing the vessel into restless, spilling complaint. The metaphor is physical: contentment is enclosure; discontentment is overflow.
