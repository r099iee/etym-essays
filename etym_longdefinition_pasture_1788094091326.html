Pasture (noun; also verb) denotes land covered with grass and low herbage on which cattle, sheep, horses, and other grazing animals feed — and, by extension, the feed itself and any situation that nourishes. In the source lines, “Seek the sunlight and the day, / Back to pasture, back to mead, / Where the kine and oxen feed!,” the word is set in deliberate parallel with mead (the old word for meadow): both name the open grass-lands of a remembered home, where kine and oxen feed by nature. The noun carries layers of technical, legal, and figurative history.

1. Primary sense — grazing land. Pasture is ground kept under grass or sown forage for livestock to graze, whether enclosed (a paddock or close) or open (common pasture, hill pasture, rough grazing). Agricultural English distinguishes permanent pasture (sown once, grazed for years) from ley or rotation pasture (reseeded in a crop cycle); “rich pasture,” “poor pasture,” and “bare pasture” grade its fertility. In ecology, pasture is a managed grazing ecosystem, sustained by herbivores and their dung cycle, often far richer in species than arable land.

2. Secondary sense — the grass itself. The word also means the forage growing on such land: “the pasture was sweet that year”; animals “eat their pasture”; “pasture is scarce in drought.” This sense is really the older French meaning — herbage, fodder — carried over from the source word for “feeding.”

3. Figurative senses — nourishment and destination. The most famous are the idioms. “Greener pastures” (also “pastures new”) means a better situation, often a better job or land elsewhere — the grass is proverbially greener on the other side, and the pasture is the very field of that proverb. “To put out to pasture” is to retire: literally of old working horses no longer able to labour, sent to graze out their days; metaphorically, with wry sympathy or condescension, of people and things considered past usefulness (“the board put the old chairman out to pasture”). A pasture can be any field of activity or sustenance: scholars speak of the “pastures of the mind.”

4. The verb. To pasture (transitive) is to put animals to graze on land (“he pastures his flock on the fell”); to pasture (intransitive) is to graze (“sheep pasture among the ruins”). The adjectives pastured, the noun pasturage (“the right of grazing; the land itself”), and the loanword pastoral complete the family.

5. Legal and historical colour. In medieval and early-modern law, pasture was a property right: rights of common pasture attached to tenements, allowing villagers to graze stock on the manor commons; “common of pasture” was one of the classic easements. The enclosures — the taking of common pasture into private fields — are among the most contested episodes of English rural history, and the word pasture is central to that account. In the New World and the Dominions, pasture and pastoral named the great grazing industries; Australia’s “pastoral leases” are vast Crown lands let out for grazing.

6. Fixed phrases and technical uses.

  • Pasture rotation / rotational grazing: the modern practice of moving stock between paddocks so grass can recover — a cornerstone of sustainable agriculture.
  • Carrying capacity: the stocking rate a pasture can support — the ecologist’s measure of a pasture.
  • Pasture-fed / grass-fed: the marketing label for livestock raised on pasture rather than concentrate, now a badge of quality in meat and dairy.
  • Pasture bloat: the veterinary hazard of bloat in livestock on legume-rich pasture.
  • Pasture land, pasture fence, pasture gate: the working vocabulary of the farm.

7. Etymology-aware notes for the learner. Pasture descends from Latin pāscere “to feed, graze” — the same verb that gives pastor (“shepherd,” literally “feeder”), pastoral (“of shepherds”), and pasturage. The ancient root *peh₂- “to protect, feed” is also the ancestor of food, feed, and fodder: the English words for eating and the English word for grazing land are one family, split between Latin and Germanic branches. Note that pastry is NOT a member — it comes from paste, from Greek pastē “barley porridge” — a classic false friend.

8. The semantic bridge. The heart of the word is feeding: pasture is the place that feeds, the act of feeding, and the food itself. From “feeding ground” the sense widened to “any nourishing situation” (greener pastures) and to “retirement” (put out to pasture, where the old worker is, like the old horse, sent to feed and rest). Every modern sense keeps the ancient equation: land that feeds.
