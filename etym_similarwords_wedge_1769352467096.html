• Chock: Similar because both are shaped blocks used to hold or secure things; differs because a chock is specifically a block wedged under a wheel to prevent rolling, while a wedge splits, lifts, or secures by its tapering edge and has a far wider range of senses.
  • Shim: Similar because both are thin pieces used to fill a space or level an object; differs because a shim is flat, thin, and used for fine adjustment or filling gaps, while a wedge is tapered and used to split, force, or hold apart.
  • Cleat: Similar because both are fastening or gripping devices of wood or metal; differs because a cleat grips or anchors (ropes, boots, structure), while a wedge works by tapering pressure to split or jam.
  • Spike: Similar because both can be driven into things for splitting or fastening; differs because a spike is pointed and penetrates, while a wedge is tapered but blunt-edged and works by lateral pressure rather than piercing.
  • Plug: Similar because both are driven into an opening to close or fill it; differs because a plug stops or seals a hole, while a wedge splits or holds apart, or holds open, rather than sealing.
  • Taper: Similar because both describe a gradual narrowing to a point or edge; differs because taper names the shape or the act of narrowing in general, while wedge is the specific thick-to-thin tool and any object of that shape.
  • Peg: Similar because both are small driven pieces used to fasten or split; differs because a peg pins or joins and is usually cylindrical, while a wedge is a tapering flat-sided prism that works by spreading force.
  • Cone: Similar because both are tapering solids; differs because a cone is round in cross-section and tapers from a circular base to a point, while a wedge is flat-sided and triangular in cross-section, and only the wedge is a working tool and simple machine.
  • Pyramid: Similar because both are tapering triangular solids; differs because a pyramid tapers to a point from a base, while a wedge tapers to a thin edge and is fundamentally an implement for splitting or jamming.
  • Insert: Similar because both involve forcing something into a space; differs because insert is the general verb for putting one thing into another, while wedge specifies the tight, forceful, tapered fit and carries the image of splitting or jamming.
  • Jam: Similar because both mean to force something tightly into a space so it cannot move; differs because jam suggests crowding and sticking, while wedge suggests the deliberate tapered insertion of the tool and its splitting power.
  • Drive a wedge between: Similar because both describe creating division; differs because "drive a wedge" is a metaphor with the full mechanical image of splitting, while the plain word divide or split lacks the image of the tapered tool forcing its way in.
