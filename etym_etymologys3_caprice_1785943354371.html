The word caprice is a traveller. It begins, so far as we can trace it, with a goat — or possibly with a hedgehog — moves from Latin into Italian, from Italian into French, and from French into English in the mid-seventeenth century, changing its meaning at almost every border it crosses. Its story is one of bodily images becoming psychological abstractions, and of an abstraction then being exported to music and painting. The Italian breeding-ground. Latin had a perfectly ordinary word for the goat: caper (he-goat), capra (she-goat), the source of the Romance capra in Italian, Spanish, and French chèvre. On this stem Italian built a cluster of words in -iccio, a suffix forming adjectives of tendency. The noun capriccio first appears in sixteenth-century Italian meaning a sudden start, a shiver, a shudder — the involuntary leap of the body in fright or cold. Why this goat-connected word meant a shudder is precisely where the etymologists divide. One school reads it as capo "head" plus riccio "hedgehog" (or "curly"), the picture of the hair standing on end like a hedgehog's spines at a moment of sudden fear. Another school reads it straight from capra: the goat is the archetypal jumper, frisky, unpredictable, taking sudden bounds for no reason, and its leap is the model of a sudden unmotivated start. Both images point the same way — a body that moves without warning, out of fright or animal high spirits — and both fed the transfer of the word from the body to the mind: a capriccio became a sudden unmotivated turn of thought or mood. The leap into French and English. In the seventeenth century French borrowed caprice from Italian and passed it on to English, where it is first recorded around the 1650s–1660s in the sense "an unaccountable or sudden change of mind." English kept the French spelling, caprice, while the Italian capriccio survived as a separate loan in music and art. The word's sound and its meaning arrived together: suddenness and motivelessness were the cargo, and English speakers at once found it useful for describing unruly temper, arbitrary rulers, fickle fortune, and the unpredictable shifts of nature — a use that runs from Restoration drama through the eighteenth-century novel and into modern journalism. The artistic export. Italian had already begun to use capriccio as a term in music and art before the word reached England. A musical capriccio was a piece of free, fantastic construction, exempt from the strict rules of the sonata and the fugue; from the Baroque keyboard capriccios of Frescobaldi and Bach to Paganini's 24 Caprices for solo violin of the 1820s, the caprice is a virtuoso's display of whim raised to technique. In painting, a capriccio (or veduta ideata) is a landscape that rearranges reality — real ruins and imaginary buildings, real cities and invented monuments set together as the artist's fancy dictates, the school of Canaletto and Guardi. Both uses preserve the root idea: a work that obeys no rule but its maker's sudden invention. English adopted capriccio for both arts, keeping the Italian form alongside the everyday noun caprice. Semantic trajectory at a glance.
  • Bodily start: the shiver or shudder, the hair standing on end, the goat's sudden bound (Italian, 16th c.).
  • Psychological turn: the sudden, unmotivated change of mood or mind (Italian capriccio; French and English caprice, 17th c.).
  • Social and political extension: the arbitrary action of rulers, the unpredictability of fortune and of the market (18th–21st c.).
  • Artistic specialization: the free-form musical composition and the imaginary painted landscape (Baroque to the present).
  • Character trait: capriciousness, chronic suddenness of mind (modern psychology and everyday usage).
The word thus travels from the barnyard and the frightened flesh to the concert hall and the gallery, always carrying with it the image of a leap that no one could have predicted — which is precisely what makes caprice, whether in a person or in a market, at once alarming and a little delightful. In contemporary usage the noun is less common than its adjective capricious, but it remains the standard, slightly literary word for an unreasoned and unannounced change of mind.
