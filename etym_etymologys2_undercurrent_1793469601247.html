Chain 2: Base Noun / Participle 'current'
PIE (reconstructed, c. 3500 BCE)
*ḱers-
‘to run’
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*korz-ō
‘to run, speed’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE–1st c. CE)
currere
‘to run, flow, move rapidly’
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. CE)
currēns (stem current-)
‘running, flowing’ (present participle)
↓
Old French (c. 1100–1200)
corant / courant
‘running, flowing, circulating’
↓
Middle English (c. 1300–1400)
curraunt
‘running, flowing water’
↓
Early Modern English (late 17th c., c. 1680)
under-current / undercurrent
‘subsurface flow of water’
↓
Modern English (current)
undercurrent
‘subsurface stream; underlying emotion or trend’

Note: As an English compound formed in the late seventeenth century, undercurrent fuses the native Germanic locative preposition under (from PIE *n̥dʰer-) with the Romance loanword current (from Latin currere, "to run", via Old French).
