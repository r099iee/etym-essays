- Latin gesta → Old French geste — 'deed, exploit, heroic tale': a word of martial glory (chansons de geste; res gestae).
- 14th-century English geste — the 'gests' of Arthur and Alexander: still the sound of knightly romance.
- 15th century — the word slides downmarket: idle tales, trifling words, mockery; moralists begin to condemn 'jestes.'
- 16th century — the split: court jesters professionalize wit; jest-books make jest a print commodity; 'in jest' marks everything said unseriously.
- 17th century — the King James Bible condemns 'jesting' (Ephesians 5:4); Puritans attack it as sin while Restoration wit reclaims it; 'jesting Pilate' (Bacon, 1625) fossilizes the philosophical shrug.
- 18th–21st century — joke takes over speech; jest retreats to books, formality and irony; today it reads as dated, bookish, wry — a word that fell from heroes to jesters and never climbed back.
