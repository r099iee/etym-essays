The word TYRANNIZES is a Greek-origin verb with no Indo-European pedigree. It descends from a single borrowed stem of uncertain, non-Greek origin: Pre-Greek / Anatolian substrate *tyrann- "lord, master" (origin debated: perhaps Lydian or Luwian; no PIE ancestry established; Beekes classes Greek týrannos as Pre-Greek) → Greek týrannos (τύραννος) "absolute ruler, usurper-king" (loanword into Greek, first attested in the 7th c. BCE in the age of the archaic tyrants) → Greek tyrannízein (τυραννίζειν) "to act as a tyrant, rule despotically" (verbal formation in -ízein, the suffix behind English -ize; attested in classical Greek) → Latin tyrannizāre "to tyrannize" (Late Latin denominative verb built on tyrannus; imperial and post-classical) → Old French tyranniser "to tyrannize" (medieval, 12th–13th c.) → Middle English tyrannisen "to tyrannize" (borrowed from Old French, 15th–16th c.) → English tyrannize "to rule despotically; to treat cruelly" (modern, from c. 1590s) → English TYRANNIZES (third-person singular present, as on this card) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The ultimate origin of Greek týrannos is unknown. It is unanimously treated as a loanword from the non-Greek world of Asia Minor rather than an inherited Indo-European word; proposed sources include a Lydian or Luwian term for a lord or ruler, but no proposal is conclusive (Beekes: Pre-Greek substrate). No PIE etymon is to be reconstructed. (2) The English noun tyrant arrives on a separate branch, via Old French tirant (vowel weakening y > i) alongside Latin tyrannus; tyrant and tyrannize share the same borrowed stem *tyrann- but are not separate roots. (3) The -ize suffix is the Greek -ízein, which marks the verb as explicitly "Greek-patterned"; this is standard historical morphology, not in dispute.
