justifie (verb, archaic spelling) — the early modern English spelling of justify (the spelling Milton printed in Paradise Lost, 1667): to show or prove (something) to be right, just, reasonable, or warranted; to defend as righteous; in theology, to declare or account (a person) righteous before God. On the card the word appears in the poem's famous theodicy: "I may assert th' Eternal Providence, / And justifie the wayes of God to men." Core senses. (1) To show to be right or reasonable: to provide adequate grounds or reasons for an action, belief, or policy ("he tried to justify his actions"); (2) to vindicate or exculpate: to clear (a person, oneself) from blame or suspicion ("he was fully justified by the evidence," "she justified herself before the committee"); (3) theological "to declare righteous": in Christian theology, especially after the Reformation, the act by which God accounts a sinner righteous, or by which a person is made acceptable to God — the doctrine of "justification by faith"; (4) typographic "to justify": in printing and word-processing, to space the words of a line so that the text aligns evenly with both margins ("justified text"). Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Theological sense and the Reformation: "justification" became the hinge of Protestant theology (Luther: justification by faith, not works); the verb "to justify" a person "in the sight of God" is the core of this doctrinal usage, and it is this register that underlies Milton's grand claim to justify God's ways to men.
  • Theodicy: from the Greek theós "god" + díkē "justice," theodicy is the attempt to justify divine providence despite the existence of evil; Milton's Paradise Lost is the supreme literary theodicy, and the verb justifie is its announced programme.
  • Typography and computing: "justified text" or "full justification" spaces words so both left and right margins are straight; the sense derives from the older meaning "to make exact, to adjust to a standard," and now applies to word processors, web layout, and print.
  • Mathematics and reasoning: "to justify" a proof or conclusion means to supply the valid grounds or demonstration for it; "justified true belief" is a standard definition of knowledge in epistemology.
  • Everyday moral defence: "to justify oneself" is to defend one's conduct; "the ends justify the means" is the classic (usually condemned) maxim about consequences licensing actions.
  • Older and technical senses: formerly "to make exact or equal," "to adjust (a legal claim) by law," and "to administer justice to"; the legal sense "to show good cause for a debt or claim" survives in older jurisprudence.
  • Derivatives and spelling: justifiable, justifiably, justification, justificatory, justifier; the -ie spelling (justifie, justifying) reflects early modern orthographic practice — Milton wrote justifie and wayes — before the standard -y spelling (justify) was fixed, so the card's form is a period spelling of the same verb.
Historical semantic bridge. The verb is a Latin compound, iūstificāre, "to make just": from iūstus "just, lawful" + -ficāre "to make" (from facere, "to do, make"). The original and oldest sense is therefore causal — "to make just" — which in Christian Latin acquired the specialized meaning "to declare righteous" (the Vulgate's translation of the Greek dikaióō). Through Old French justifier the verb entered English in the fourteenth century with the legal and moral sense "to show to be just." Over the centuries the causal "make just" weakened into "show to be just" (defence, vindication, reasoning), while two very different modern branches grew: the Reformation's theological "declare righteous," and the typographer's "make lines even," the latter from the old notion of adjusting things exactly. Milton's use sits at the theological pole, but the modern card senses — moral defence, textual alignment — all trace back to the same Latin compound.
