pastoral — the biography of a shepherd. The word's entire history is bound up with the tending of flocks, and it begins in Proto-Indo-European with *peh₂-, a root whose meaning was at once protection and feeding — the two acts a shepherd performs and the two gifts a parent gives. At this deepest level the root makes no distinction between guarding the flock and feeding the family; both are forms of tending, of keeping creatures alive by care. The daughter languages split the emphasis in instructive ways. The Indo-Iranian branch kept the protective face: Vedic pāti means “he protects,” Sanskrit pāla is the guardian, and the great compound gopāla, “cowherd,” survives into modern Hindi in pālan “care, rearing” and in the name Gopala itself. The Germanic branch kept the feeding face and made it the source of our most basic words for sustenance: Proto-Germanic *fōdijaną gave English feed, *fōdą gave food, *fōdrą gave fodder, and even fat descends from the participle of the feeding root — the “well-fed” thing. Greek and Baltic both made the same shepherd-word from the same variant grade: Greek poimḗn and Lithuanian piemuõ, “shepherd,” are almost exact twins, both meaning “the tender of the flock.”

Through Rome. The line that reaches English runs through Rome. Latin pāscere meant “to feed, to graze, to pasture” — a verb of herdsmen — and from it Latin made pāstor, the shepherd, literally “the feeder,” the man who does the feeding that the root always described. The Romance languages carried the word down in the mouth of the shepherd himself: Italian pastore, Spanish pastor, French pasteur all mean “shepherd,” and the surname of Louis Pasteur is that very word, inherited from the shepherding trades. In Late Latin the adjective pāstorālis, “of the shepherd,” was formed, and Old French passed it into English as pastoral in the fifteenth century — first in the literal sense of shepherds and rural life.

The literary revolution. What turned a shepherd's adjective into a genre is the poetry of Theocritus and Virgil. Theocritus's Idylls in third-century-BCE Sicily invented the singing shepherd, and Virgil's Eclogues gave the figure its permanent home in Arcadia, idealizing the life of flocks and pastures as a golden age of ease and song. From the Renaissance onward, “pastoral” became the name of that genre and its whole world — the shepherds of Sidney's Arcadia, Spenser's The Shepheardes Calender, the pastorals of Milton and Pope — and it spread from verse into painting and music: Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony, the pastorale, the painted landscape of sheep in a meadow. At the same time the church had been making the same metaphor: since the shepherd is the feeder and guardian of his flock, the Christian minister became the pastor, and his ministry the pastoral office — the shepherd of souls, a sense alive in Greek (poimēn) as in Latin, and still standard in “pastoral care” and “pastoral counseling” today.

The broken ideal. From the eighteenth century onward a tension grew between the ideal and the actual land. Pastoral came to describe real countrysides, often with condescension (“pastoral charm”), while the working reality of farms — flat, exhausted, indifferent — sat uneasily beside the golden dream. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the ideal is usually invoked with irony: a “pastoral landscape” can be flat working farmland stripped of Arcadia, and the word now signals a broken or exhausted dream as often as it describes a place. That is the whole evolution of the word in the passage under study: the shepherd's adjective, carrying its full history from the PIE tending root through Rome and Arcadia, applied to a land that offers a solitary migrant nothing but flatness and emptiness.

  • c. 4500–2500 BCE: PIE *peh₂- “to protect, to feed” — guarding and feeding as one act.
  • 3rd–1st c. BCE: Latin pāscere “to graze, feed”; pāstor “shepherd, the feeder”; Greek poimḗn “shepherd” — both from the same root.
  • c. 4th–5th c. CE: Late Latin pāstorālis “of a shepherd”; church usage makes the pastor the shepherd of souls.
  • c. 12th c.: Old French pastoral.
  • 15th c.: Middle English pastoral — the literal shepherd-and-rural sense.
  • 16th–18th c.: the literary revolution — Theocritus and Virgil; pastoral becomes a genre of idealized rural ease, then spreads to painting and music.
  • 19th–21st c.: the ideal meets the real land; irony and the broken dream; the religious sense stays standard in ministry.
