Wasser is one of the words that allows historical linguistics to reconstruct a society. The Proto-Indo-European noun for water was heteroclitic: it alternated between a nominative *wódr̥ and an oblique stem *wedn-, a pattern preserved in Hittite wātar, Greek hýdōr (genitive hýdatos), and in the Germanic and Slavic descendants in altered form. The root *wed- is one of the most widely distributed in the family: it underlies Sanskrit udán-, Umbrian utur, Old Church Slavonic voda, Lithuanian vanduõ and English water. Where a language has lost the noun, traces often remain in compounds and river names, which is why hydronymy is a favourite tool of prehistory.

The Germanic stage. Proto-Germanic generalised an r-stem noun, *watōr, whose descendants include Old English wæter, Old Frisian weter, Old Saxon watar, Old High German wazzar and Old Norse vatn. The English and German words are therefore cousins, not copies. Their similarity is the visible residue of a common ancestor, exactly as the card says of Milch/milk and Brot/bread.

  • Grimm's law. PIE *w remains *w in Germanic; the dental *d of the oblique stem is retained, while the syllabic r develops as ur or ar depending on the branch. The Germanic forms are thus regular developments, not irregular borrowings.
  • The High German consonant shift. West Germanic *t becomes zz (later ss) in intervocalic position in High German, giving Old High German wazzar against English water. The same shift separates German essen from English eat and German das from English that.
  • Vowel history. The Old High German a of wazzar is retained in Middle High German wazzer; the modern Wasser reflects the Early New High German lengthening and the standardisation of a before the shifted sibilant.

Old and Middle High German. Old High German wazzar is attested from the eighth century in glosses, charms and biblical translation. It denotes the element, the sacrament, the Flood and the water of springs and rivers. Middle High German wazzer broadens the reference to bodies of water — a river or lake can be called a wazzer — and courtly poetry uses flowing water as an image of instability, separation and the passing of time. The Minnesang and the Arthurian romances regularly stage encounters at fords, springs and bridges, and the word accumulates a literary atmosphere of freshness, danger and change.

Reformation and the Bible. Luther's translation made Wasser a textually fixed word. “Und der Geist Gottes schwebte auf dem Wasser” (Genesis 1:2), the waters of the Flood, the crossing of the Red Sea, the water turned to wine at Cana and the baptism of Christ in the Jordan all gave German speakers a set of canonical images. Baptismal water in particular joined the word to ideas of purification, new birth and admission to the community. The Wasser of the Bible is not merely a substance; it is a sign.

Science and industry. The chemical revolution of the late eighteenth century turned water from an element into a compound and produced the German name Wasserstoff for hydrogen, a calque on Lavoisier's hydrogène. The word's elemental simplicity made it a natural base for nomenclature: Sauerstoff, Kohlenstoff, Stickstoff follow the same pattern. In the nineteenth century, hydropathy and the spa movement made Wasser a therapeutic keyword, while urban sanitation and the engineering of Abwasser, Grundwasser and Hochwasser made it a matter of public administration. The great river systems of the German lands — Rhine, Elbe, Danube, Oder — gave the word a geopolitical dimension that persists in the vocabulary of canals, dams and shipping.

Idiom and literature. German idiom is saturated with water. Stille Wasser sind tief is proverbial; ins Wasser fallen, Wasser auf die Mühlen gießen, mit allen Wassern gewaschen and Wasser predigen und Wein trinken are all common. The Romantics made the liquid a favourite symbol of the unconscious and the infinite: the Lorelei's Rhine, the mill streams of the fairy tales, the nocturnal waters of Eichendorff and the Wasserflut of Wilhelm Müller's Winterreise, set by Schubert, where water images the unstoppable flow of grief. Wassermann, the Water Bearer, gave the word an astrological and later a pop-cultural life.

Contemporary politics. In the twenty-first century Wasser has become a keyword of environmental politics. Wasserknappheit, Wasserverschmutzung, Wasserrecht and the privatisation of Wasserversorgung are contested issues, and the old element word now carries the anxiety of a finite resource. Bottled water has turned the substance into a branded luxury, while drought and flood make the compound vocabulary of disaster everyday news. The word's long stability is precisely what makes these new uses feel urgent: when a culture's most elementary noun becomes a political problem, the problem is understood to be fundamental.
