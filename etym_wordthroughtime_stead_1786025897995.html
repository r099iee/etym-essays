- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.): stede was the primary everyday noun for a physical place, town, site, or piece of land.
- Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.): Gradually displaced in general spatial meaning by the Norman French loan place; contracted into specialized phrases like in stede of.
- 15th – 16th Century: Fused into the compound preposition instead; the phrase in one's stead became standard for substitution and vicarious governance.
- 17th – 19th Century: Solidified the idiom stand in good stead (to be of great service or advantage); standalone spatial usage survived only in toponyms (Hampstead) and compounds (homestead, bedstead).
- Modern Era: Preserved as a formal, literary, and evocative noun denoting substitution ('in his stead') and enduring utility ('stand in good stead').
