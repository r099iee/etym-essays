PIE *peuĝ- "to prick, stab, cut" (reconstructed root of Latin pungō; cf. Greek pugmḗ "fist") → Latin pungō, pungere "to prick, pierce, sting" (classical, attested from the 1st c. BCE), with the past participle punctum "prick, point" → Vulgar Latin *pīctiāre / *pinctiāre "to pinch, nip, compress with the fingers" (inferred, unattested — see note) → Old North French pinchier / Old French pincier "to pinch, squeeze with the fingers, nip" (attested from the 12th c.) → Middle English pinchen "to pinch, squeeze, grip" (attested from c. 1300) → Modern English pinch (verb and noun) → pinch (noun in the idiom "in a pinch", this card). Debated/nuanced stage: The Vulgar Latin intermediate *pinctiāre is not directly attested; it is inferred to bridge Latin pungere "to prick" and the Romance pincier / pinchar family. Some scholars instead trace the French verb to a Celtic or to an imitative source, so the connection to pungere, though widely accepted, is not certain. English also has parallel Germanic finger-words (Dutch, Low German) that resemble pinch but are not the source of the English verb, which is chiefly the Old French / Old North French loan.
