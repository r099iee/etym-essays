arch (noun) — a curved, symmetrical structure spanning an opening and supporting the weight above it, built of wedge-shaped stones (voussoirs) or of brick or concrete that press against each other so that the curve holds the gap open. In the ruins it is the 'bent doorway' of the past — a structural threshold that has survived the fall of the roof. The arch in this passage is the gate in the castle wall: 'a great arch which must once have had a gate in it but was now almost filled up with the largest of all the apple trees.' The tone is architectural and liminal. Unlike a plain doorway, which is a hole cut in a wall, an arch announces itself — it has a shape, a voussoired brow, an ancient engineering confidence. Structurally it is the survivor: the wall has broken down around it, the roof has long since fallen, but the arch still stands, still frames a passage. Symbolically it is the threshold — the children break through the apple-tree branches and 'all blinked because the daylight became suddenly much brighter,' stepping from the shadow of the wood into the walled open court of the lost garden. Primary sense. The core architectural meaning is precise: a load-bearing curved opening or span. The arch works because the voussoirs, wedge-shaped stones, transfer the weight of the wall above outward and downward into the jambs, so the opening can be very wide without a lintel. The curve is not decorative — it is the mechanism. Secondary and extended senses.
  • By shape-extension 'arch' names any bowed form: the arch of the foot, the arch of the eyebrow, the arch of the sky or of a branch — the curve without the engineering.
  • In anatomy the word is used of curved structures: the aortic arch, the dental arch, the zygomatic arch — curved bones and vessels that resemble the builder's curve.
  • In geometry and mathematics 'arc' is the sibling word: a part of the circumference of a circle, from the same Latin source; in electrical jargon an 'arc' is the luminous curved discharge between electrodes.
  • In modern technical language 'arch' appears in computing architecture (an 'arch' as a design framework), in the narrative 'arc' of a story (usually spelled arc), and in monumental history as the 'triumphal arch'.
  • In figurative prose the 'arch of history' or 'arch of time' uses the curve as an image of a sweeping span — the whole bent trajectory of events.
  • A separate and unrelated word is the adjective and prefix 'arch-' (archbishop, archangel, an arch smile): this comes from Greek arkhē 'rule, chief' and means 'chief' or 'playfully cunning' — it must never be merged with the builder's arch.
The semantic bridge. The noun descends from Latin 'arcus', the bow — the hunting weapon. Roman builders, who had learned to construct with wedge-shaped voussoirs, borrowed the image of the bent bow for the curved structure that carries weight; the weapon, the geometric curve, and the building member all share one image of something bent, storing force. An arch, like a drawn bow, is a curve under tension. Nearest companions.
  • A 'doorway' is simply an opening in a wall meant for a door; it need not be curved and carries no structural glamour.
  • A 'gateway' is an opening in a fence or wall made to be closed by a gate — the arch here is precisely a gateway whose gate is gone.
  • A 'portal' is a grand, ceremonial doorway, usually decorated, belonging to cathedrals and palaces; it is always a threshold of importance.
  • A 'vault' is not an opening but the arched ceiling or roof that spans a space — the arch built in three dimensions.
  • An 'arcade' is a series or row of arches (or arches carried on columns).
