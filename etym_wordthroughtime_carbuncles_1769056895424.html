- 13th c. — "carbuncle" enters English from Old French and Anglo-Norman as a gem-name and a medical name at once: the deep-red stone that glows like a live coal, and the burning boil that shares its fire; both senses descend from the Latin "little coal."
- 14th–17th c. — in heraldry, romances and lapidary handbooks the carbuncle becomes one of the great stones, prized for a red so deep it was said to shine in the dark and even light whole halls; the word carries a medieval, marvellous glamour.
- 18th–19th c. — the gem-sense fades before scientific mineralogy: jewellers adopt "ruby" and "garnet," and the carbuncle becomes old-fashioned, a period-flavoured word of antiquarian catalogues; meanwhile the medical sense remains in ordinary use, still burning like a coal.
- Contemporary — "carbuncles" in the gem sense survives as a deliberately archaic, heraldic word (as in the Narnian treasure list), while the boil-sense stays current in medicine; the same Latin ember lives on at a distance in the chemist's "carbon."
