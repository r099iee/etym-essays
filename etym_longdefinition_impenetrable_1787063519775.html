Impenetrable describes anything that cannot be passed through, entered, pierced, or broken into. The word is built on a spatial idea — something forms a complete barrier — and it is used with equal fluency of physical objects, intellectual puzzles, and human emotions. It is the negative form of “penetrable,” from the Latin verb penetrare, “to enter, go into the inmost part.”

Primary sense — physically impenetrable. An impenetrable surface, barrier, or material admits nothing: an impenetrable fortress cannot be breached, an impenetrable jungle cannot be crossed, impenetrable darkness cannot be seen through. In The Hobbit, Smaug boasts that “no-where” can be found his equal because he “possesses” a “waistcoat of fine diamonds” — a playful literalisation: the dragon's gem-encrusted hide is impenetrable to arrows and swords, and Tolkien lets the grand heroic word rub against the comic domestic image of a waistcoat.

Secondary sense — intellectually impenetrable. Metaphorically, whatever resists understanding is impenetrable: an impenetrable text, jargon, argument, or language is opaque, too dense or obscure to be grasped. The metaphor treats the mind like a fortress that cannot be entered.

Tertiary senses.

  • Of people and emotions: an impenetrable reserve, calm, or silence means a person's feelings or intentions cannot be read or reached; emotionally sealed off.
  • Of sound and light: impenetrable silence, impenetrable fog, impenetrable darkness — total absence of what would pass through.
  • Of arguments and logic: an impenetrable defence, a mathematically impenetrable proof (in cryptography, an impenetrable code) — secure, invulnerable to attack.
  • In physics and materials science: impenetrability is a technical property — matter cannot occupy the same space simultaneously; an impenetrable material blocks passage or radiation (impenetrable to X-rays).
  • In military and defensive language: impenetrable lines, impenetrable armour, an impenetrable position — of fortification and security.
  • Of persons under questioning: an impenetrable witness or suspect gives nothing away; their story resists all attempts to break it.

Register and nuance. “Impenetrable” is formal and emphatic; in speech it often has a slightly literary or grand flavour (as in the Tolkien line). Its near-synonyms carve the space into narrower parts: “impervious” stresses resistance to an external effect (impervious to water, to criticism); “impermeable” is technical, of materials that block liquids and gases; “impassable” is about terrain and roads that cannot be travelled; “inscrutable” and “unfathomable” are about faces and depths that cannot be understood.

Common collocations. “impenetrable darkness / jungle / fortress / armour,” “impenetrable jargon / argument,” “impenetrable reserve / calm,” “impenetrable mystery,” “impenetrable to the enemy / criticism / light.” The word frequently pairs with a following “to + noun” or appears in a genitive image (“an impenetrable wall of silence”).
