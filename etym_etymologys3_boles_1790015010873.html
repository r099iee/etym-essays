Boles as tree trunks comes from the north sea, not Rome. Its ancestor is Old Norse bolr (masculine, plural bolir), meaning stem, trunk, mast — the upright that carries sail or crown. Vikings who settled Yorkshire, Cumbria, and Scotland after 900 CE brought bolr for ship-masts and pine stems; northern Middle English borrowed it by the mid-14th century as bole, bool, bol: trunk of a tree. Southern English already had native stamme, trunke (trunk via French), but bole survived because it named the merchantable length prized by wrights and foresters.

The deeper Germanic prehistory is swelling. Proto-Germanic *bulaz — if correctly reconstructed — meant something swollen, round, thick: trunk, block, plank. It belongs to the great PIE family *bhel- (2) to blow, swell, inflate: source of English ball, balloon, bowl, bulk, bole, boll (cotton boll), bull (grown male?), belly, bellows, billow, bolster. Phonology is regular: PIE *bh- becomes Germanic *b- by Grimm's Law; vocalic *-el- with u-grade gives *bul-; Old Norse loses final -az and voices l before r to give bolr /bolr/ with rounded vowel; Middle English borrows as /boːl/ rhyming with hole. Plural adds -es → boles /bəʊlz/.

  • Medieval timber economy: 14th-15th century northern accounts list boles of oak, boles of fir sold for keels, beams, and charcoal. A bole is value: straight, thick, clear of knots. Poets notice too — Gawain-era alliterative verse leans knights against boles.
  • Early Modern literary fixing: 16th-17th century herbals and Bibles use bole, body, stock interchangeably, but bole keeps northern-archaic colour. Shakespeare prefers trunk, stock; Spenser and Drayton prefer bole for aged forest, giving it poetic-pathetic tone that persists.
  • Imperial forestry 18th-19th c.: With Baltic, Canadian, and Indian timber trade, bole becomes mensurational: bole length, bole diameter at breast height, clear bole, merchantable bole. Manuals teach to cruise boles for board-feet. The word is now technical and commercial, spoken by surveyors with tapes and calipers.

The 20th century splits bole in two directions. Conservation and fantasy — Muir on sequoias, Tolkien's Fangorn, Le Guin's forests — revive boles as cathedral pillars, beings with biography, rings as memory. To write boles thirty or forty feet through, tossed and shattered belongs here: awe at scale made poignant by wreckage, like bombed cathedrals. Meanwhile plantation forestry keeps bole as yield unit. Both share Norse practicality: a bole is what stands until it falls, and what pays or awes whether standing or shattered.

Crucially, bole red clay never touches this story. That bole comes from Greek bolos clod, lump → Latin bolus → Medieval Latin bolus armenicus → English bole pigment for gilding bole, medicine, and pottery. Identical spelling is coincidence; context — trees with boles — disambiguates instantly.
