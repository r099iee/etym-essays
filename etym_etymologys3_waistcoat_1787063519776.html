A word made of two ordinary words. Waistcoat is a transparent English compound: waist plus coat. It was coined in English rather than borrowed, and both halves have their own long histories. “Waist” descends from Old English wæst, “growth, build, stature,” itself related to the verb “wax” (to grow): the waist is, strangely, the part of the body where growth is most evident as the torso narrows. The traditional account links wæst to the Indo-European root *h₂weg-, “to grow, increase,” though the exact semantic path (why “growth” should come to mean “the middle of the body”) is not perfectly transparent and remains a point of scholarly caution. “Coat,” on the other hand, is a Romance loan: from Late Latin cotta, a coarse outer tunic, through Old French cote into Middle English cote by the 14th century. The compound “waistcoat” is attested from the late 16th century, applied to a waist-length under-garment.

The 1666 revolution in men's dress. The word's most consequential moment came in October 1666, when Charles II of England issued a proclamation establishing a new national dress for men: a long coat, a waistcoat worn under it, and breeches, in an attempt to steer English court fashion away from the dominance of French style. Chroniclers record that the king himself wore a long waistcoat at the introduction. The garment caught on and became the ancestor of the modern tailored waistcoat. Over the next century it underwent a striking evolution: the original waistcoat was long (reaching toward the knee) and had sleeves; by the middle of the 18th century it had been cut to hip length and the sleeves removed — precisely the modern shape. The word, however, stayed the same, and for a while “waistcoat” could even be pronounced “weskit,” reflecting a spoken form that had drifted far from the spelling.

The golden age of the waistcoat. In the 18th century the waistcoat was the showpiece of male fashion. Gentlemen wore waistcoats of silk and velvet, embroidered with flowers and scenes in gold and silver thread; a man's rank could be read in his waistcoat. The American and French revolutions and the industrialisation of the 19th century changed all this. Victorian dress was dark, sober, and uniform; the waistcoat was absorbed into the three-piece suit as a quiet, functional component, still worn daily by clerks, gentlemen, and servants alike. The single-breasted waistcoat with a “V” opening, worn with a watch chain across the chest, became the visual signature of the respectable 19th-century man.

The twentieth-century retreat and the famous dragon. In the 20th century the waistcoat left everyday wear: it survived in formal dress, ceremonial uniform, waiters' livery, and period costume, and the American term “vest” steadily displaced the word across the Atlantic (where “vest” had once meant something else). Its period associations made it a favourite of historical novelists — and of one of the greatest, J. R. R. Tolkien, whose dragon Smaug boasts in The Hobbit (1937) of possessing “a waistcoat of fine diamonds.” The image is deliberately incongruous: a garment so respectable, so gentlemanly, worn by a fire-breathing monster. The humour depends entirely on the word's settled associations with tailoring, propriety, and the 19th-century gentleman.

Today. Waistcoat remains a standard British noun, now covering both the formal tailoring piece and a range of sleeveless garments (quilted and knit “sleeveless jackets”). Its pronunciation continues to split between the spelling-pronunciation /weɪs.kəʊt/ and the traditional /ˈwɛs.kɪt/. The word is a small museum of English social history: a compound noun, a royal decree, a century of embroidery, a Victorian uniform, and a dragon's joke.
