- [14th–15th c.] - Middle English forms the compound *peche-tre* from Old French *pesche* and Old English *trēow*, designating the prized Mediterranean fruit tree cultivated in monastic and noble walled gardens - [perception: exotic, delicate luxury tree; valued by herbalists and royal gardeners for medicine and sweet fruit]

- [16th–17th c.] - Early Modern English naturalists, herbalists (such as John Gerard), and poets celebrate *peach-tree / peach-trees* for their extraordinarily early spring blossoms and vulnerability to late frosts - [perception: horticultural marvel and symbol of fragile vernal beauty in Elizabethan and Jacobean garden literature]

- [18th–19th c.] - Commercial orchard cultivation expands across Britain, Europe, and the American colonies, while Romantic and Aesthetic literature (notably Oscar Wilde's *The Selfish Giant*) elevates the blooming peach-tree into a supreme allegorical emblem of paradise, childhood innocence, and divine resurrection - [perception: poetic perfection, fairy-tale enchanted gardens, and rural abundance]

- [20th–21st c.] - Global industrial pomology breeds hundreds of hardy cultivars, while the image of flowering *peach-trees* remains an internationally recognized artistic motif in Asian cultural festivals and Western landscape art - [perception: vital agricultural crop and universal aesthetic herald of springtime]
