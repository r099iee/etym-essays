Desolate (adjective) describes a place or a person emptied of life, comfort or companionship. Applied to land, it means deserted, uninhabited, bleak and often ruined — a wasteland where nothing lives and no one comes. Applied to a person, it means profoundly lonely, forlorn, inconsolable — left utterly alone. The word always carries emotional weight: desolation is not mere emptiness but emptiness that has been abandoned or bereft. In the reading context it describes land 'desolate and empty', so lifeless that the travellers' stores need no guard.

Primary senses.

  • Of places — deserted and lifeless: uninhabited, bleak, showing no signs of life or cultivation — 'a desolate landscape', 'a desolate stretch of coast', 'desolate ruins'. The sense combines physical emptiness with a feeling of abandonment and sadness.
  • Of people — utterly lonely: forlorn, wretched, deprived of all comfort and companionship — 'she felt desolate after his death'. This is the earliest English sense (14th century: 'deprived of comfort or companionship') and survives strongly today.
  • Of experiences and feelings: a 'desolate existence', a 'desolate mood', 'desolate grief' — states characterized by utter solitude, barrenness of comfort, or inconsolable loss.
  • The verb (transitive): to desolate means to lay waste, devastate, or deprive of inhabitants — 'the war desolated the countryside'. Archaic or literary in modern use, it comes straight from the Latin past participle.
  • Biblical and theological register: 'desolation' is the state of ruin and abandonment that follows divine judgment — 'the abomination of desolation' (Matthew 24:15), 'your house is left unto you desolate' (Matthew 23:38). In this register desolate implies not just emptiness but the withdrawal of God.
  • Ecology and science: a 'desolate' habitat is one too harsh or degraded to support life — desert, polar waste, or land laid waste by eruption or war. Astronomy applies the word to lifeless, barren planets and moons.

Semantic bridges. The whole family of meanings radiates from the Latin verb dēsōlāre, 'to leave alone, abandon, lay waste' — literally 'to make utterly alone' (dē-, 'completely', + sōlus, 'alone'). The bridge from person to place is abandonment: a person left alone is desolate; a place left alone — deserted by its people — is desolate. The bridge from abandonment to ruin is historical: what is left alone falls into ruin and waste, so 'desolate' comes to mean both 'lonely' and 'laid waste'. The emotional and the physical senses have coexisted since the word entered English in the 14th century, and both still live today.

Register and collocations. Desolate collocates with land, place, landscape, plain, coast, island, house, room, city, ruins, and — of persons — heart, soul, feeling, widow, and mourner. It is a strong, literary word: 'bleak and desolate', 'desolate and empty' are common doublets. The noun desolation names the state or the place itself ('a scene of desolation'); the adverb desolately and noun desolateness are regular. Its register ranges from biblical gravity to romantic landscape description to quiet psychological description of grief.
