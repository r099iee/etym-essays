"Epoch" denotes a distinctive, extended period of time characterized by particular events, qualities, or developments — a long stretch that stands out as a new era or age. In the source sentence — after an epoch of oscillation between old and new — it means a prolonged interval of instability and swing before private property triumphed, stressing duration and historical weight rather than a single moment.

Everyday use treats epoch as a grander synonym for era, age, period: the Victorian epoch, the epoch of space travel. Technical uses sharpen it: geology's epochs (Holocene, Pleistocene subdivisions), astronomy's epoch (reference date for coordinates), computing's epoch (zero-date from which time is counted, e.g., Unix epoch 1970-01-01). In all cases the idea is a fixed starting point that defines what follows.

  • Historical period (core, source sense): A memorable age with unifying character: the epoch of Napoleon, an epoch of revolution, an epoch of oscillation. Here duration matters — an epoch lasts years to centuries — and so does distinctiveness: it differs qualitatively from what came before and after. The source uses it precisely thus: a whole epoch of wavering between communal and private tenure.
  • Geological epoch: A formal subdivision of a geological period (e.g., Holocene Epoch, Eocene Epoch), typically millions of years, defined by stratigraphy and fossils. The Anthropocene epoch debate — whether human impact merits a new epoch — shows the term's power to name planetary turning points.
  • Beginning point / datum: In the older technical sense, an epoch is the starting instant itself, not the whole span: the epoch of an era (e.g., birth of Christ, Hegira). Astronomy retains this: epoch J2000.0 is the reference moment for star positions. Computing's Unix epoch is midnight 1 January 1970 UTC.
  • Personal / biographical: An epoch in one's life — a phase that remakes everything after: going to university, surviving war, becoming a parent. E.g., "the war was an epoch in her life." Here scale shrinks to the individual but the structure (before/after divided by event) remains.
  • Figurative intensifier: Epoch-making, epochal events or discoveries: momentous, era-defining. An epochal novel, an epoch-making invention. The adjective epochal and phrase epoch-making carry this laudatory, headline force.
  • Connotation and register: Elevated, formal, historiographical. Longer and weightier than period, more technical than age, less calendrical than era. Mispronunciation and spelling (epoch vs. epic) are common learner pitfalls; the final -ch is /k/, not /tʃ/.

Polysemy pivots on point vs. span: Greek epokhe meant a stop or fixed point; modern English mostly means the span that follows the stop. The bridge is natural — to name the starting post is to name the race that starts there. The source sentence uses the span sense, but its oscillation between old and new preserves the original idea of a held, suspended moment before decision.
