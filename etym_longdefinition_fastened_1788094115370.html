FASTENED is the past tense and past participle of the verb fasten ("to make fast, to attach firmly, to secure"). In the Tolkien context ("bats swirled about the heads and ears of elves and men, or ___ vampire-like on the stricken"), the word describes the bats attaching themselves to the fallen — clinging, gripping, and fixing themselves onto their victims like vampires. The sense is concrete and physical: to be fastened is to be firmly attached, held, or secured so that one cannot easily come loose. The word is a core member of a large family built on the Germanic adjective fast ("firm, fixed, secure") and shares its root with fast (as in "hold fast", "make fast"), fastness, and fasten itself.

Primary senses:

  • To attach or fix firmly (transitive): "she fastened the brooch to her dress", "he fastened the rope to the post", "fasten your seatbelt" — to join two things so that they hold together securely. The means may be mechanical (buckle, clasp, hook, bolt, nail, stitch) or adhesive (glue, tape).
  • To close or secure (transitive): "to fasten a door, a window, a coat, a bag" — to shut and secure something so it will not open. This is the sense in everyday "fasten your seatbelt" and "the dress fastens at the back".
  • To fix the gaze or attention (figurative): "she fastened her eyes on him", "he fastened his attention on the problem" — to direct and hold one's perception or thought steadily upon something, as if physically attaching it.
  • To fasten on/upon (phrasal): to seize upon, to attach oneself to, to latch onto: "the bats fastened on the stricken" (the Tolkien sense), "the blame was fastened on him", "he fastened on the idea and would not let it go". The bridge is physical attachment applied to pursuit, accusation, or mental fixation.
  • To become attached (intransitive or reflexive use): "the magnet fastens to the metal", "the hook fastens easily".
  • Nautical (technical): "to make fast" — to secure a rope or ship firmly to a cleat, bollard, or anchor; fastened in this register means tied down securely against movement.

Related vocabulary. Fastened sits at the centre of a Germanic word-family: fast (adjective and adverb: firm, and separately quick — a famous semantic drift of the same root), fasten, fastener ("a device that fastens": zip, button, clasp, clip), fastening ("the act or the device"), and fastness ("a stronghold; firmness"). The adjective fast meaning "quick" is the same root with a striking reversal: "firm, steadfast" developed through "firmly, vigorously" into "quickly", so that a runner who goes "fast" goes vigorously — a development peculiar to English.

Semantic bridges. The unifying image is firmness and immovability: what is fastened does not move, come loose, or fall. From "made firm" the word extends to (1) mechanical attachment (brooch, rope, bolt), (2) closure and security (door, coat, seatbelt), (3) mental direction (eyes, attention "fixed upon" an object), and (4) predatory or accusatory seizure (bats fastening on the stricken; blame fastened on the innocent). The past participle fastened emphasizes the resulting state — a condition of secure attachment — which is why the Tolkien image is so effective: the bats are fixed, clinging, immovably attached to the fallen, like living clamps.

Register today. Fastened is neutral and everyday: "the gate was fastened", "fasten your seatbelt", "the buttons are fastened". It is slightly more formal than "stuck" or "attached" and carries a deliberate sense of security and firmness. In literary prose of Tolkien's register it retains its full physical force: to be fastened on something is to be bound to it, unable or unwilling to let go.
