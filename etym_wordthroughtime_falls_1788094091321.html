- (700s–1000s) - feall: "a falling, ruin, death" — purely existential; the season sense germinates in the phrase "fall of the leaf".
- (1500s) - "A fall of water" becomes a standing phrase for a waterfall or a spring flood; rivers are said to "fall" into the sea, and the verb's motion prepares the noun's geography.
- (1600s–1700s) - The plural crystallizes as a geographic proper-noun form: "the Falls" — explorers write of "the great Fall" and then "the Falls" of Niagara; the plural sticks because a river's descent is a series of drops; American maps fill with Falls (Great Falls, Passaic Falls).
- (1800s) - Tourism and the sublime: Niagara Falls becomes the world's most famous waterfall; painters and honeymooners re-make "the falls" as a place of awe; in wrestling "best of three falls" fixes the sport sense; dialect American "in the falls" (= in autumn) records the season word made plural.
- (1900s–today) - "Falls" remains the everyday word for waterfalls in North America and technical English; finance uses "falls" for collapsing prices ("sharp falls in shares"); the plural still names places, sports, and markets alike.
