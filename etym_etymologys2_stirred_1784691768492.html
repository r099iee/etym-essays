PIE *ster- “stiff, rigid” (reconstructed; the root of Greek stereos “solid, stiff” and Latin sterilis “barren, unproductive”) — with the extended form *steur- “to turn, disturb, stir up” (debated, see note) → Proto-Germanic *sturjaną “to disturb, agitate, turn over, set in motion” (reconstructed weak verb; 1st millennium BCE–CE) → Old English styrian “to move, set in motion, agitate, excite, impel” (attested; also the compound āstyrian “to disturb, trouble” and the noun styre “movement, emotion”) → Middle English stiren / sterien / sturien “to move, agitate, churn, rouse, incite” (12th–15th c.) → Early Modern English stir “to move, agitate, rouse” (16th c.) → Modern English stir, weak past tense stirred (stir + -ed, with consonant doubling) “moved slightly, agitated, emotionally roused” => stirred (modern English past tense / past participle) DEBATE / NUANCE: The Germanic verb *sturjaną is secure and the Old English line is fully attested. The deeper Indo-European connection is debated: many handbooks set up an extension *steur- of the root *ster- “stiff” (with the semantic bridge “stiff → twisted → turned → disturbed”), but the exact reconstruction is uncertain, and some scholars treat the Germanic family as a word of obscure or Germanic-only ancestry. The connection of stir to storm (Proto-Germanic *sturmaz) is plausible but disputed. The prison slang stir (as in “in stir”) is an unrelated homonym of debated origin, possibly from Romani stariben.
