peep is a surprisingly recent verb, and its history is the story of two separate coinages that happened to converge on the same sound and spelling. Neither strand can be traced back to a confident Indo-European root, and the whole word illustrates how onomatopoeia and informal speech leave a fuzzier record than the classical strata of the lexicon.

The 'look' strand

The visual verb appears in Middle English in the 15th century as a variant of peke / pike, the ancestor of modern peek, meaning 'to look slyly or briefly'. The origin of this little cluster of look-words — English peek and peep, with Middle Dutch kēken and Low German kiken 'to look' across the North Sea — is obscure. Most dictionaries judge them to be expressive or imitative: a short, sharp vowel followed by a stop was felt to mimic the quick snap of a glance. English clearly reshaped or reinforced its form under Dutch influence in the later medieval period, when trade and printing made the Low Countries the great channel of English borrowing.

  • mid-13th century: peep-hole is attested in the sense of an eye-slit or spy-hole, showing that the idea of looking through an aperture was already fixed.
  • 15th century: the verb pepen 'to look through a narrow opening' is recorded, at first as a variant of peek.
  • 1620s: the transferred sense 'to begin to appear, to emerge into sight gradually' appears — exactly the sense Tolkien uses of the stars peeping forth.
  • 1590s: the noun 'a sly or quick look' follows in the same family.

The 'squeak' strand

Independently, an echoic verb meaning 'to make a short, high-pitched cry' is recorded from the 1520s, imitating the squeak of a mouse or the thin call of a nestling bird. Because it sounded identical to the look-word, the two merged: by the 18th century a single lexeme peep carried both meanings in dictionaries. The idiom not a peep ('not a word, not a sound') shows the sound-sense feeding modern idiom, while peep of day shows the look-sense producing a dawn idiom.

Cultural and literary colour

In the 19th and 20th centuries the word stayed squarely in everyday, domestic language — nursery rhymes, folk verse, and children's stories — which is exactly the register in which Tolkien, writing in a consciously archaic but warmly homely style, could use it of the stars without pretension. The merged history means the word sits comfortably in both natural description and comic or sly contexts, and it has remained fundamentally unchanged in form since the 17th century, with the 'emerge' sense now arguably its most productive literary use.

The settled modern word

By the 18th century the two strands were inseparable in dictionaries, and the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries fixed the idioms that survive today: peep of day for dawn, peep-show for a viewing-box, and the common collocations of peep with out, forth, in, and through. The word's smallness is its signature — every sense, visual or auditory, names something brief, thin, or half-revealed — which is exactly the quality that lets a fantasy author put a gentle, old-fashioned verb into a high narrative without breaking its tone. In contemporary English the look-verb is casual and ubiquitous (a shy glance, a glimpse of a headline), while the archaic-looking 'emerge' sense quietly does the poetic work for which the word was originally sharpened.
