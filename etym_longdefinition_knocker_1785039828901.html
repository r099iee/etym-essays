**KNOCKER (noun) — at its plainest core** At its most basic level, a **knocker** is an agent noun built from the verb *to knock* with the suffix *-er* ("one who or that which knocks"): a person or a thing that delivers sharp, repeated blows, and, by extension, any device, heavy tool, or instrument used for striking forcefully to create noise or to pound objects. The word has been part of English since the Middle Ages, and its meanings have grown outward from that single physical action in several distinct directions — domestic, industrial, social, slangy, and figurative — which makes it a rich case of polysemy radiating from one concrete root. **Primary sense and the literary context** In the goblin chant of the Misty Mountains in Tolkien's *The Hobbit*, the line "Knocker and gongs!" uses the word in its oldest and simplest register: a crude, heavy instrument that strikes hard and loudly, keeping brutal time with the goblins' underground march. Tolkien deliberately opposes it to the polite domestic door-knocker of a respectable house; the goblin *knocker* is a thing of iron that thuds and clangs ceaselessly, at once mechanical and dehumanizing. As an agent noun it hovers between human and machine — a goblin who deals blows or a device that delivers them — and this ambiguity is the source of its ominous effect. **The concrete domestic sense: the door-knocker** From the early modern period the word is most familiar as the **door-knocker**: the hinged ring, hammer, or clapper mounted on a door, lifted and struck against a metal plate to announce a visitor. This sense is entirely concrete and functional — a small, purposeful striking-tool whose only job is to produce a sharp, summoning report. The door-knocker also carried social meaning: its size, material (brass, iron, or bronze), and ornament (lions, gargoyles, ring-handles) silently announced the householder's wealth and standing on the street. From this sense comes the British idiom "on the knocker," meaning going from door to door, as a salesman or canvasser does, each call announced by the knocker. **The industrial and occupational senses** In the nineteenth century the word extended from objects to occupations. The **knocker-up** was a paid waker who walked the streets before dawn, rapping on the windows of sleeping factory workers with a long pole or a pea-shooter, earning a few pence a week; he was an everyday figure in the mill towns of industrial England. A *knocker* could also name the mechanical striking part of a machine or pump, and in mining and pumping contexts the "pump knocker" was the worker — or the mechanism — that kept the pump going by repeated strokes. **The figurative and slang senses** From the verb's figurative sense *to knock* meaning "to disparage, criticize" (recorded from 1892), a **knocker** becomes a carping critic or detractor — a fault-finder, a naysayer, someone who belittles whatever is on offer. This is now one of the word's most common adult uses in colloquial English, especially in phrases like "the knockers and naysayers." Separately, in twentieth-century British and Australian slang, the plural **knockers** (1930s onward) came to mean a woman's breasts, a crude anatomical transfer from the idea of things that bounce or knock together. In sporting idiom the noun is tied to the boxer's knock-out blow (as in *knockout*, 1887), and the verb's compounds have proliferated: *knock-down* (1760, auctioneering; also a knockdown price or blow), *knock-off* (to stop work, to steal, to copy cheaply, or to kill, mid-20th century), *knockabout*, *knockback*, and *knockout*. **Distinctions from near-synonyms**
  • *Hammer*: similar because it is a tool for striking; differs because it is defined by its striking face for driving nails and shaping metal, while a knocker is defined by its purpose of knocking — producing a sharp, repeated signal or blow.
  • *Mallet*: similar because it is a striking implement; differs because it is a lighter, broad-headed wooden or rubber beater, softer and broader in effect.
  • *Gavel*: similar because it is a small striking device; differs because it is a ceremonial hammer for calling order, symbolic rather than functional.
  • *Club / cudgel*: similar because they are blunt striking weapons; differ because they crush and batter rather than report a sharp, repeated knock.
  • *Knocker-up*: similar because it shares the striking-root and the occupation; differs because it names specifically the paid waker who roused workers, a person-sense no tool word shares.
**Semantic reasoning / historical bridges** The chain of senses is entirely transparent, because every one of them is generated from the same source verb. The door-knocker literalizes "to knock" as a door signal; the knocker-up transfers the rapping action to waking people; the critical sense extends "to knock" metaphorically from striking an object to striking at a reputation — knocking an idea is beating on it, as one beats on a door to attack it; and the slang plural exploits the same root idea of pendulous things that knock together. Each sense preserves the original meaning "to strike with a sharp blow" while relocating it to a new object, tool, person, or abstraction. **Summary** KNOCKER is thus a textbook agent noun whose concrete sense (a thing that knocks) has stayed stable for over six centuries while sprouting a chain of well-bridged secondary meanings — domestic (the door-knocker), occupational (the knocker-up), critical (a detractor), and slang (the plural anatomical sense) — all derived transparently from the one root action of striking sharply and repeatedly.
