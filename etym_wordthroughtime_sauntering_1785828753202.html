- Middle English — sawtren-type verbs perceived as musing, doting, mental idling; not yet the modern boulevard stroll
- 17th c. — Physical sense firms: to saunter is to walk idly or leisurely; social flavor of purposeless ease among town and courtly scenes
- 18th c. — Fashionable urban leisure: sauntering as genteel killing of time; still open to moralists' mild reproach of idleness
- 19th c. Transcendental / nature writing — Thoreau and heirs recode sauntering as noble, meditative, free engagement with landscape; prestige peak
- Early–mid 20th c. — Character-description staple: cool nonchalance, hands-in-pockets entrance; less class-bound
- Late 20th–21st c. — Everyday verb for any slow confident casual walk; can be warm (holiday saunter) or ironic (sauntering in late); folk Holy-Land story still retold in popular etymology lore
