The biography of damp begins with fire, not water. Its root, Proto-Indo-European *dʰewh₂- (also written *dʰeu-), meant "to fume, smoke, raise dust or vapour," and it is one of the most far-flung roots in the family: Sanskrit धूम (dhūmá-) "smoke," Greek θυμός (thūmós) "breath, spirit," Latin fūmus "smoke," Old Church Slavonic дымъ (dymŭ), and Lithuanian dū́mai "smoke" all descend from the same "smoking, breathing" source. The word's whole later history is a strange transfer of allegiance: a root that begins as the word for hot smoke ends, three thousand years later, as the English word for cold wetness. Somewhere along the way the vapour the root named cooled, condensed, and settled. The Germanic branch and the sound shifts. The Germanic branch produced Proto-Germanic *dampaz, "vapour, steam, smoke." Scholars treat its link to *dʰewh₂- as plausible but not certain, since the pre-Germanic form is hard to reconstruct; OED glosses the word's origin as uncertain, "perhaps from the PIE root *dheu- 'to rise in a cloud.'" Phonologically, the shift from PIE *dʰ- to Germanic *d- is a textbook Grimm's Law change (Indo-European voiced aspirates become plain voiced stops), which is consistent with the connection, though the vocalism remains debated. Whatever its exact pedigree, *dampaz prospered in the North Sea and Baltic languages: Old High German damph became modern German Dampf ("steam," as in the Dampfschiff, the steamship); Middle Dutch damp is still "vapour, mist" (Dutch dampig "steamy"); and it was the Middle Low German word that English borrowed in the fourteenth century. Bad air, not wet cloth. Significantly, the first English sense was not moisture but gas: a "damp" was a noxious, suffocating exhalation — the feared air of mines and closed chambers. The word entered the technical vocabulary of mining as the miners' names for the two great underground killers: firedamp (methane, which explodes when it meets a flame) and chokedamp (carbon dioxide, which suffocates). Damp was thus originally what you died of, not what you dried. The poetic and practical memory of this sense lingers in dialect and literature, where "the damps" are the cold, sickly exhalations of pits and cellars — the "bad air" of a place where no sun has reached. The semantic cooling. The word's great story is the cooling of that hot vapour into wetness. As "vapour" shaded into "condensed, hanging moisture," the noun broadened to mean mist, fog, and then plain humidity; the adjective "damp" (sixteenth century) settled on "slightly wet" — the chill, clingy wetness of walls and cellars rather than of rain; and the verb travelled in the opposite direction, from "to choke, stifle" (to damp a fire) to the figurative "to dull, depress" (to dampen one's spirits, a damper on the party). In British building usage the noun produced the technical "rising damp" and the "damp-proof course" (twentieth century), while engineering gave us the "damper" that deadens vibration and sound — a piano damper, a chimney damper — and the modern shock-absorber. By the nineteenth century the word held the full range it shows in Lewis's sentence: a noun ("the draft and the damp") for the stale cold moisture that inhabits an underground chamber, still faintly haunted by its ancient meaning of noxious vapour, and a stock word of Gothic and adventure fiction, the atmospheric signature of vaults, caves, and ruined halls. Cultural and literary arc in summary.
  • PIE *dʰewh₂- "to fume, smoke, raise vapour" → Proto-Germanic *dampaz "vapour, steam, smoke" (link plausible but debated).
  • Middle Low German damp → Middle English damp (14th c.) "noxious vapour, fume, exhalation."
  • 16th c. — adjective "damp" = slightly wet, moist; noun broadens to mist, fog, humidity.
  • 17th–18th c. — miners' firedamp and chokedamp; verb "to damp" = to choke a fire, then to depress (dampen spirits).
  • 19th–20th c. — Gothic fiction's "the damp"; building terms "rising damp," "damp-proof course"; engineering "damper."
  • Contemporary — an everyday adjective for cool, sullen, uncomfortable wetness (not soaked, not flooded), still carrying a faint undertow of that old noxious vapour.
The damp of Cair Paravel is, etymologically, the last ghost of fire-smoke — the exhalation of a root that has been cooling for four thousand years, from the smoke of sacrifice to the wet chill of a buried treasure chamber.
