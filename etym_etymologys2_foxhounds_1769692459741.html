Component 1 — FOX:
1. Proto-Indo-European *puḱ- / *peuḱ- "thick-haired, bushy-tailed, tufted" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; cf. Sanskrit puccha "tail", Lithuanian pūkas "down, tuft"). DEBATE: the PIE etymon for "fox" is uncertain; the Germanic words are best explained as a descriptive formation meaning "the bushy-tailed one," but some scholars treat *puḱ- as speculative.
2. Proto-Germanic *fuhsaz "fox" (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE; regular Grimm's Law shift of PIE *p → PGmc *f and PIE *ḱ → PGmc *h).
3. Old English fox "fox" (attested from c. 8th c.; cf. feminine fyxen "vixen").
4. Middle English fox "fox" (c. 12th–15th c.).
5. English fox (modern) — first element of the compound.

Component 2 — HOUND:
1. Proto-Indo-European *ḱwōn- (genitive *ḱun-) "dog" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; cf. Sanskrit śvan, Greek kyōn, Latin canis).
2. Proto-Germanic *hundaz "dog, hound" (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE).
3. Old English hund "dog" — the general word for dog in Anglo-Saxon (attested from c. 8th c.).
4. Middle English hound "dog; hunting dog" (c. 12th–15th c.). SEMANTIC NARROWING: after the word dog (of obscure origin) became the general term, hound narrowed to mean specifically a hunting dog.
5. English hound "a hunting dog" (modern) — second element of the compound.

Compound assembly — FOXHOUND:
1. English fox + hound combined to name the dog that hunts foxes (term foxhound first attested in English c. 1610s–1620s, at the rise of organized fox hunting).
2. English foxhound "a hound bred to hunt foxes" (17th c. onward).
3. Plural: English FOXHOUNDS (final node).
