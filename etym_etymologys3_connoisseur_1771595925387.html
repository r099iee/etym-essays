Connoisseur — the biography of the knower. The word is the French grandson of one of the oldest and most productive of all Indo-European roots, *ǵneh₃-, "to know, to recognize," a root whose family spans the greater part of Europe and India. To follow the word from its root to the drawing room of the eighteenth century is to trace the history of European knowledge itself. The great knowing root. The PIE root *ǵneh₃- produced words for knowledge in every major branch of the family. In Greek it gave gignōskein, "to come to know," and gnōsis, "knowledge" — the source of English gnosis, gnostic, diagnosis, prognosis, and gnomic, and of the whole vocabulary of ancient science, which was called knowledge of nature. In Sanskrit it gave jñā-, "to know," with jñāna, "knowledge," the very word that opens the Bhagavad Gita and survives in modern Hindi as gyān (ज्ञान), "knowledge, spiritual insight." In the Germanic languages it produced English know, ken, can, and cunning, and German kennen and können. In the Slavic languages it produced Russian znat' and its kin. And in Latin it produced noscere, "to come to know," the ancestor of a vast English cohort — recognize, ignore, cognition, cognizance, notion, incognito, notice. No English word family is more cosmopolitan, and "connoisseur" is among its most elegant children. The Latin and Old French middle. It is the Latin line that leads to our word. Latin noscere was an inchoative verb, "to begin to know," and it was strengthened with the intensive prefix com-, giving cognoscere, "to get to know thoroughly, to recognize." In the spoken Latin of Gaul the consonant cluster gn underwent a characteristic palatalization: the sequence softened into something like the ɲ of Spanish ñ and then collapsed toward a single n. Old French conoistre, "to know," shows the results of that softening, and modern French connaître preserves the aftermath in its silent -ître, whose written circumflex still marks the lost cluster. From this verb French derived its agent noun with the productive suffix -eur, which names a person by the activity he performs: conoistre + -eur gave connaisseur, "one who knows." The birth of the taste-expert. The semantic narrowing happened inside French. In the seventeenth century, the age of the salons, the Academy, and the great royal collections, connaître took on a specialised life in the vocabulary of the arts: to know a painting, a wine, or a piece of music was to know it as good, to recognize the genuine and the fine by trained perception. The connaisseur was the man who could tell the real from the fake, the master's hand from the copy, the fine vintage from the ordinary. The word meant not merely the scholar but the man of cultivated sensibility, the person whose knowledge had become taste. By the time English borrowed the word in the early eighteenth century — first recorded around 1710, in the years of the Grand Tour and the great English collections — it arrived complete: the refined, educated judge of the fine arts. The English career. English took the word in the baggage of the Academy and the French school of art criticism that dominated European taste. It quickly became the standard term for the discriminating judge — of painting first, then of music, wine, porcelain, and the whole expanding field of taste. The eighteenth century also produced connoisseurship (1714), the discipline of attribution and evaluation that made the connoisseur a professional figure; the same century produced the connoisseur's shadow, the dilettante, and the debate — still live — about whether taste was learned knowledge or natural perception. The spelling was fixed in English as connoisseur, with the older French -oi- orthography, even after modern French reformed the word to connaisseur with -ais-; the English form is thus a small fossil of the French of the age of Louis XIV. Ruskin and the satirical turn. The nineteenth century sharpened the word's double edge. Ruskin, in his preface to the early volumes of Modern Painters, sets up the "ordinary connoisseur" as the orthodox judge before whom his own opinions "will sound heretical." The phrase is satire with a velvet glove: the connoisseur by definition claims cultivated judgment, but the qualifier "ordinary" exposes the type as formulaic and complacent, the professional appreciator whose taste is inherited rules and fashionable names rather than direct perception. Ruskin spends the whole book fighting this class of judges — men who judge a Turner by what they have been taught to admire rather than by what they see. He borrows the connoisseur's own vocabulary in order to expose its bankruptcy, and the word has carried that satirical possibility ever since: to call someone a connoisseur is to praise their taste, and to call them an ordinary connoisseur is to dismiss their taste as a set of reflexes. Today. Modern English uses connoisseur across the whole range of refined appreciation — connoisseurs of wine, of coffee, of cinema, of opera — and the word still carries the Gallic elegance of its birth. It can be entirely sincere, and it can shade into pretension, the very quality Ruskin exposed: the connoisseur's knowledge, like all knowledge born of the root *ǵneh₃-, is real, but it can harden into the performance of knowing. The word is a small monument to the history of European knowledge — a root shared with Sanskrit gyān and English know, refined through French salon culture, and given to English to name the expert judge of taste.
