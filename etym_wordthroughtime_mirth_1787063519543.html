- [8th–11th c.] - [Old English Spiritual & Aesthetic Delight] Emerged in Old English as myrgþ / mirigþ, signifying deep joy, pleasantness, sweet musical melody, and heavenly spiritual bliss.
- [12th–14th c.] - [Middle English Festive Socialization] Shifted into Middle English as myrthe / merthe, increasingly associated with courtly feasting, minstrel songs, seasonal revelry (May games, Christmas), and social amusement.
- [15th–17th c.] - [Elizabethan Comedic & Theatrical Zenith] Solidified in Renaissance drama and literature as the quintessential descriptor for comedy, joyous laughter, and the therapeutic triumph of humor over melancholy.
- [18th–21st c.] - [Poetic Refinement & Archaic Warmth] Preserved in modern English as an elevated, evocative literary noun, celebrated for conveying wholesome, timeless, and uninhibited festive happiness.
