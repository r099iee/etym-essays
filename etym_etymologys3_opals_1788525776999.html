1. Indo-Aryan origin and the Indian gem trade. The etymology of opals begins in ancient India, the historical epicentre of early gemstone mining and lapidary arts. The primary ancestral term is Sanskrit upala (उपल), meaning simply "stone, precious stone, or jewel." As Indian merchants transported spices, silk, and raw gemstones westward across overland caravan routes and Indian Ocean maritime conduits into the Hellenistic Levant and Egypt, the Sanskrit word was adapted into the Mediterranean lexicon.

2. Classical Hellenistic and Roman natural philosophy. In Hellenistic Greek, the term emerged as opallios (ὀπάλλιος), specifically denoting the extraordinary iridescent stone imported from the East and from ancient European deposits in the Červenica (Czerwenitza) mountains of modern-day Slovakia. The Romans borrowed the Greek term directly as opallus.

The foremost classical account of the opal appears in Book XXXVII of Pliny the Elder’s Naturalis Historia (c. 77 CE). Pliny celebrated the opal above nearly all other gems, famously describing its unique optical synthesis:

"There is in them a softer fire than the ruby, there is the brilliant purple of the amethyst, and the sea-green of the emerald—all shining together in an incredible union. Some by their refulgent splendour rival the colours of the painters, others the flame of burning brimstone or of fire quickened by oil."

Pliny also recorded the famous historical episode of Roman Senator Nonius, who chose exile rather than surrender his magnificent hazelnut-sized opal ring to the covetous triumvir Mark Antony.

3. Folk etymologies and medieval lapidary traditions. During the Roman era and Middle Ages, two influential folk etymologies attached themselves to opallus:

  • Ops, Goddess of Abundance: Roman writers frequently associated the name with Ops, the wife of Saturn and goddess of wealth, agriculture, and abundance, claiming the stone was her special earthly gift.
  • Ophthalmios (The Eye-Stone): In medieval Byzantine and European lapidaries, the word became conflated with Greek ophthalmios ("eye-gem"). Because the stone seemed to contain all colors, it was believed to cure all eye ailments and grant its wearer invisibility, earning the popular name patronus furum ("patron of thieves").

The word passed into Old and Middle French as opale, entering English records in the late 16th century (attested in the 1590s during the Elizabethan era, as seen in Shakespeare's Twelfth Night, where the clown Feste tells Duke Orsino: "thy mind is a very opal," referencing the Duke's shifting, inconstant emotional states).

4. The Victorian superstition and the Australian renaissance:

  • The Scott superstition: For centuries a stone of universal good fortune, the opal suffered a catastrophic blow to its popularity in 1829 with the publication of Sir Walter Scott's gothic historical romance Anne of Geierstein. In the story, the heroine's magical opal talisman loses its fire when touched by holy water, and the wearer vanishes into ash. The European public misread the fictional tale, and opal sales dropped by more than 50% across Europe within two years as the gem became branded as a bringer of misfortune.
  • The Decadent revival and Australian mines: In the late 19th century, the opening of vast, high-grade opal fields in the Australian outback (Coober Pedy, White Cliffs, Lightning Ridge) revolutionized the gem world. Queen Victoria helped dispel the lingering superstition by presenting her daughters with magnificent Australian opal jewelry. Simultaneously, Aesthetic and Decadent writers like Joris-Karl Huysmans and Oscar Wilde celebrated opals for their elusive, shifting perfection. In Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose, opals stand alongside emeralds as the gold standard of earthly wonder.

Today, opals remains a word steeped in geological wonder, ancient commerce, literary romance, and dazzling optical complexity across cultures.
