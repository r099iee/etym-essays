Structuralists are, in the primary sense, the thinkers of the mid-20th-century movement that treats languages, myths, kinship, fashion, and literature as systems whose elements mean nothing alone and everything by opposition and combination, following Saussure linguistics through Lévi-Strauss, Barthes, Greimas, and early Foucault toward a science of underlying form.

In the source sentence the word names folklorists of a particular school: Structuralists have analysed oral tradition in detail, for example Maranda and Maranda in 1971, but mostly without explicitly contrasting oral composition with written composition. Here the term is mildly critical: brilliant at internal pattern, inattentive to the media divide between mouth and page that orality theory puts first.

Core meaning in detail: The structuralist wager is that a tale, a menu, or a marriage rule works like a language: surface abundance generated by finite deep contrasts such as raw versus cooked, nature versus culture, center versus margin. Analysis therefore segments the corpus, finds binary pairs and mediating thirds, and writes transformation rules by which one myth turns into another. Its emblems are the phoneme, the kinship atom, the mytheme, the narrative function, each meaningless in isolation and eloquent in relation.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Paris-school movement members (primary historical sense, c. 1950–1970): Lévi-Strauss in anthropology, Barthes in literature and fashion, Lacan in psychoanalysis, Althusser in Marxism. The bridge is shared method: Saussure langue over parole applied far beyond words.
  • Folklorists and myth analysts (source-sentence sense): Propp morphologists, Dundes motif analysts, Maranda structural semanticians of myth. The bridge is genre fit: oral tales with variants invite exactly the paradigmatic charting structuralism loves.
  • Linguistic structuralists proper (earlier, 1920s–1950s): Prague, Copenhagen, and American descriptivists mapping phonemes and morphemes. The bridge is origin: every later structuralism borrows its prestige from phonemics.
  • Architectural and design structuralists (1960s–1970s Dutch school): van Eyck and Hertzberger building polyvalent forms. The bridge is analogy: buildings as languages whose users complete the utterance, a cousin rather than a sibling of Paris doctrine.
  • Loose journalistic sense meaning rigid systematizers (often pejorative): theory-heavy academics accused of forcing life into grids. The bridge is caricature: the method famous for binaries becomes shorthand for any cold schematic mind, fair or not.
  • Post-structuralist aftermath (late 1970s onward): former Structuralists turned critics of structure, Derrida, late Barthes, Kristeva. The bridge is succession: the plural noun now marks a generation to be transcended, so to call someone a Structuralist can date them fondly or dismissively.

Register and connotation: Structuralists is seminar-historical, capital-S optional but frequent, carrying chalk-dust, diagrams, and Parisian smoke. Used admiringly it honours rigour; used impatiently, as in the source sentence, it sighs at brilliance that missed the point, detail without the oral-written contrast. The singular structuralist doubles as adjective, structuralist poetics, while the lower-case structuralist can mean anyone with a taste for systems, from engineers to coaches.
