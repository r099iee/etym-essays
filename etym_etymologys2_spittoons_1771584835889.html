PIE *sp(y)eu- "to spit, spew, eject from the mouth" (imitative / onomatopoeic root; reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *spīwan- "to spew, spit, vomit" (reconstructed) → Old English spǣtan "to spit" (iterative verb; attested from the Old English period) — alongside Old English spīwan "to spew" (whence English spew) and Old English spātl "saliva" (dialect spattle) → Middle English spitten "to spit" (12th–15th c.) → English spit "to eject saliva from the mouth" (modern) → English spittoon "a vessel for spitting into" (spit + augmentative -oon, borrowed via French -on from Italian -one < Latin -ōnem; first recorded 1819, OED) → English SPITTOONS (plural, as on this card) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The PIE root *sp(y)eu- is imitative; the *sp- onset mimics the sound of expectoration, and the exact root shape (*spy- vs. *speu-) is reconstructed with variation across the family. (2) The suffix -oon is a learned augmentative (balloon, cartoon, buffoon, lagoon), adopted into English from French and Italian in the 16th–19th c.; its use in spittoon marks the vessel as a "big spit-thing". (3) Greek ptyein "to spit" shows the *sp- > *pt- metathesis (ptysis "a spitting", whence English hemoptysis; ptyalon "spittle", whence ptialism); Latin spuere "to spit" gave sputum and spūma "foam", and its compound conspuere produced Portuguese cuspir "to spit" and cuspideira, whence English cuspidor. These are collateral cognates, not ancestors of spittoon.
