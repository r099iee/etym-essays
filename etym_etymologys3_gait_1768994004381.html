Gait is a word with an honest gap at its root. Unlike most of English’s basic movement vocabulary, it cannot be traced back to an Indo-European verb of going; the handbooks agree that it is Germanic-internal, a word whose ultimate ancestor is simply unknown. Its story, therefore, is not a long sound-shift narrative but a story of contact, dialect, and semantic narrowing — of a Viking word for “road” that slowly turned into a medical word for how a person walks.

The Viking word for “way.” The oldest reconstructible stage is Proto-Germanic *gatwō, “way, road, street,” a word whose earliest recorded faces appear at opposite ends of the Germanic world: Gothic gatwō “street” in the fourth-century Wulfila Bible, and Old Norse gata “path, way, road” in the Viking Age. The same word survives in modern German Gasse “lane, alley” and Swedish gata “street,” both direct descendants of *gatwō. Its origin beyond Germanic is debated — some scholars have floated a connection to a PIE root of “going,” but nothing has been proven, and the OED candidly records the word as “of unknown origin.” This is one of the few words in the language where the honest beginning of the story is a question mark.

The Norse loan into English. English acquired gata through the sustained contact of the Viking settlements: Middle English took the word as gate, meaning “a way, path, or road,” and by extension “a going, a journey.” For centuries this gate lived chiefly in the northern and Scottish dialects, where it remains alive today — a Scot still speaks of “the gate to the town” or “to go one’s gate,” and the word appears in place-names across the north. It is important to distinguish this Norse gate (“way”) from the other English gate (“a hinged door or opening,” from Old English geat, a different Germanic word); the two are etymologically unrelated homographs that happen to share a spelling, and the “manner of walking” word is the Norse one.

The narrowing: from “way” to “way of going.” The decisive semantic shift came in the sixteenth century, when gate began to move from “the way” to “the way one goes” — from the road itself to the manner of moving along it. This metonymy (the route → the walking) is the single most important change in the word’s history, and it was registered orthographically: the specialised spelling gait was adopted to keep the new sense apart from the “pathway” and “doorway” words. In the same century the word entered the technical vocabulary of horsemanship, where the “gaits” of a horse — walk, trot, canter, gallop, and the ambling gaits of pace and rack — were codified by the riding masters; the term remains exact and technical in equestrian science to this day.

From stables to clinics to prose. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries completed the modern career of the word. In polite prose, gait named the dignified, stately step of the well-born, and novelists made it the standard instrument for characterising people by how they move — the swagger of the bully, the nervous shuffle of the coward, the measured tread of the hero are all “gaits” that can be read like text. Simultaneously the medical profession took the word into the clinic: the “gait” became a diagnostic object, and neurology built a rich descriptive vocabulary around it — the festinating gait of Parkinson’s disease, the ataxic gait of cerebellar damage, the antalgic gait of a painful limb. “Gait analysis” is now a laboratory discipline using force plates and motion capture, and the same scientific eye has been turned on animal locomotion and, most recently, on legged robots, which are engineered for “dynamic walking gaits.”

Modern state. Today gait runs two settled lives. In everyday prose it is the cool, observational word for a person’s manner of walking — slightly clinical, faintly literary, the narrator’s instrument for clocking and describing motion, which is exactly how the cave passage uses it (“walking at a fair gait”). In science and medicine it is a precise technical term of biomechanics and neurology. Meanwhile its older “way, path” sense survives in the dialectal and Scottish gate, a living fossil of the Norse borrowing. The word has thus done something most movement words have not: it travelled from the road to the walker, from the geography of place to the anatomy of the body, while never quite letting go of either.

  • Sound changes: The borrowing from Old Norse to Middle English shows regular loss of the Norse final vowel in some forms (gata → gate); the spelling gait (sixteenth century) is a deliberate orthographic differentiation, not a sound change — both spellings were pronounced alike.
  • Key attestations: Gothic gatwō “street” (4th c.); Old Norse gata (Viking Age, recorded in the sagas); Middle English gate “way, road” (12th–14th c.); the walking sense in the 16th c.; the equestrian sense in 16th–17th c. riding manuals; “gait analysis” in 20th-c. biomechanics.
  • Debated points: The ultimate origin of *gatwō is unknown; a PIE “going” root has been proposed but is not accepted, and the word must be treated as Germanic-internal. The modern “manner of walking” is a sixteenth-century narrowing, not an ancient meaning.
