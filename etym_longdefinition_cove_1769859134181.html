cove: (1) a small, sheltered bay, inlet, or recess in a coastline, usually enclosed by steep or rocky ground; (2) a recess, hollow, or sheltered corner in a surface; (3) dated British slang for a fellow, man, or chap; (4) regional (especially Appalachian American) use for a small sheltered valley or hollow between hills. Sense in this literary context. In the passage "...the sea blue with silver foam in a little cove under the grey cliffs, there was a queer feeling there..." the word names a small, sheltered, horseshoe-shaped inlet in the shoreline, tucked under grey cliffs. The writer (Eleanor Farjeon in Jim and the Pirates / the Rover story) uses the word to create a specific geography: a protected pocket of water where the sea runs calm and silver-foamed, closed in by high rock, and — crucially — a place with a "queer feeling," later crowded with mermen and sea-goblins. A cove, being enclosed and secret, is the perfect setting for uncanny coastal encounters: it is a pocket of the wild sea held within the land, private, isolated, and faintly mysterious. Primary sense — a small sheltered bay. In standard modern geography a cove is a small coastal inlet or bay, typically semicircular or crescent-shaped, bounded by cliffs, headlands, or rocky points that shelter its waters from wind and open sea. It differs from a full bay mainly in size and enclosure: a bay is large and open, a cove is small, intimate, and protected. Coves are formed by erosion where softer rock has been worn back into a cliff line, by river mouths, or by drowned valleys, and they are prized by sailors as anchorages and by swimmers as calm, sheltered spots. Secondary sense — a recess or hollow. By extension cove can name any recess, hollow, or sheltered niche — a hollow in the ground, a corner of a room, or a sheltered nook. This is the older, more general sense, surviving from the word's Germanic past when it meant simply "chamber" or "den." Tertiary sense — dated slang for a person. From the 16th century, thieves' cant and later general British slang used cove to mean "fellow, man, chap" — as in "a rum cove" (a strange fellow) or "a knowing cove." This sense is now largely dated or archaic, but it appears in older novels, sea stories, and Cockney-tinged writing, and it is a striking example of a word's two lives: a geography term and a slang term that share a spelling but almost certainly do not share an ancestry. Regional sense — an Appalachian hollow. In the mountains of the southeastern United States, especially Appalachia, a cove is a small valley or sheltered hollow between ridges, often a flat-bottomed clearing settled by farmers. The famous Cades Cove in the Great Smoky Mountains is the best-known example. This sense extends the "sheltered recess" idea from coast to mountains and shows how a maritime word was carried inland by English-speaking settlers. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • A small, sheltered bay or inlet of the sea — primary modern geographic sense.
  • A recess, hollow, or sheltered niche in any surface or terrain — general extension.
  • A small sheltered valley or hollow between hills (Appalachian American, from the 18th–19th c.) — regional.
  • Slang "fellow, man, chap" (British, dated; thieves' cant from 1567) — separate lexeme, unrelated origin.
  • Historical "an inner chamber, den, or snug room" — the Old English sense, now obsolete except in poetic or archaic use.
  • In sailing/nautical usage, a small creek or boat anchorage sheltered by the land — technical register.
The semantic bridge. The geographic word descends from Old English cofa "a chamber, den, or recess" — an interior, sheltering space. The leap to geography is easy to trace: a sheltered room became a sheltered pocket of coast, a hollow in the hills, a niche in a cliff. The underlying meaning in every modern sense is "a sheltered, enclosed space," whether cut into rock, hillside, or coastline. The slang sense "fellow," by contrast, is a separate English word of obscure origin that merely happens to share the spelling, so it is not part of the same bridge. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "A quiet cove / a hidden cove / cove of the bay" — geographic, neutral-to-picturesque.
  • "Cove" vs. "bay": a bay is larger and more open; a cove is smaller, more enclosed, more sheltered.
  • "Cove" vs. "inlet": an inlet is any narrow arm of the sea into the land; a cove adds the idea of enclosure and shelter.
  • Confusables: cave (a hollow in rock, from Latin cava), cove as slang "fellow," covet (to desire, unrelated).
In the test sentence cove is used in its primary geographic sense, chosen precisely because the word's "sheltered, enclosed" core makes the little sea-pocket under the grey cliffs feel secret and strange — the ideal scene for mermen and sea-goblins.
