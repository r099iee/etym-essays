Sluggard is a sturdy old term of reproach for a habitually lazy person — a slow-moving idler who lingers in bed, dodges labour, and lets the world's work slide past: the human equivalent of the garden slug, all slime and no speed.

In the source sentence it is barked as a reveille: Wake that sluggard Fatty! — the sleeper sprawled and snoring while others are already astir, roused with the contempt reserved for one whose sloth holds up the whole party. The exclamation mark does half the work: this is a word meant to be shouted across a dormitory.

Core meaning in detail: A sluggard is not merely tired or resting but constitutionally averse to effort — slow to rise, slow to move, slow to finish, ingenious only in excuses. The word's spelling tells its anatomy: slug, the slow creature or slow person, plus -ard, the old pejorative suffix also heard in drunkard, coward, laggard, and dullard — a suffix that turns a flaw into a character, a habit into an identity. To call someone a sluggard is therefore to pass a moral sentence, not to describe a mood; it belongs with the seven-deadly-sins vocabulary of sloth rather than with the medical vocabulary of fatigue.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A habitually lazy, idle person (primary sense, from the late 14th century): go to the ant, thou sluggard, as Proverbs commands. This biblical verse made the word immortal: the sluggard is held up as the negative example in the wisdom literature read at every hearth for centuries. The bridge is the slug analogy — the man moves through his duties as the mollusc moves through the garden, leaving a trail and reaching nothing.
  • A moral type, the embodiment of sloth (biblical and homiletic): preachers from Langland to Spurgeon anatomised the sluggard's day: late rising, long meals, the turning in bed as the door turneth upon his hinges. The bridge carries a barnyard insult into theology, where laziness becomes a sin with eternal wages, and the sluggard stands beside the fool and the scorner in the rogues' gallery of Proverbs.
  • A slow, lumbering thing generally (transferred, rare): writers occasionally call a slow ship, a dull horse, or a lagging season a sluggard. The bridge generalises the human idler to anything that drags: winter is a sluggard when spring is late, and a becalmed vessel plays the sluggard on a glassy sea.
  • An affectionate tease among intimates (modern, softened): wake that sluggard Fatty itself leans this way — the speaker plainly loves Fatty even while scolding him. The bridge is tone: shouted with laughter among friends, the moral sentence becomes a pet name, and the sting is drawn by evident fondness.
  • Adjectival use, sluggard-like (marginal): sluggard rest, sluggard hours appear in older verse, transferring the noun to the sleep and seasons it loves. The bridge is the standard noun-to-modifier drift of English, letting the idler's quality stain the time he wastes.

Register and connotation: Sluggard is archaic, hearty, and faintly comic — a word from sermons and storybooks rather than offices, which is why it survives mainly as a joke or a wake-up shout. Nobody is dismissed as a sluggard in a performance review; the modern manager says low performer. But the old word keeps a Shakespearean relish the euphemism lacks: it conjures blankets, crumbs, and noon sunshine on an unmade bed, and condemns with a grin rather than a spreadsheet.
