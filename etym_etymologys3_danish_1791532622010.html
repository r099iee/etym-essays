The name of the Danes is older than the kingdom of Denmark. It appears first in the sixth century, in the Getica of the Gothic historian Jordanes, who lists the Dani among the peoples of the far north. From that tribal name came the country, the language and the adjective that English uses today. The history of Danish is therefore a history of how a name on the edge of the known world became a familiar European label.

Earliest attestations

Jordanes, writing in Latin around 551 CE, mentions the Dani as a people distinguished for their tall stature and their ferocity. The name also appears in early medieval Latin and Old Norse sources, and it is preserved in the English Dene, the Old Norse Danir and the Danish danskere. The kingdom of Denmark itself is named in Old English as Denemearc and in Old Norse as Danmǫrk, both meaning the "borderland" or "march" of the Danes, with the second element related to mearc "boundary, border".

  • Jordanes, Getica — c. 551 CE, the earliest secure mention of the Dani.
  • Old English Dene — the plural tribal name, used in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and in poetry.
  • Old English Denemearc — "the march of the Danes", the origin of Denmark.
  • Old Norse Danmǫrk — the Scandinavian form of the same compound.

The meaning of the root

The etymology of *Daniz is not certain. The most widely cited proposal connects it with a PIE root *dhen- "low, flat", which survives in Old High German tenni "threshing floor" and Old English denu "valley". On this view the Danes would be "the people of the flat land", a fitting description of Jutland. Other scholars have suggested a meaning such as "border people" or have treated the name as a pre-Indo-European tribal label that cannot be analysed further. The flat-land etymology is attractive but should be presented as a hypothesis rather than a fact.

Denisc and the -ish suffix

Old English formed the adjective Denisc from Dene plus the suffix -isc, which descends from Proto-Germanic *-iskaz and is cognate with German -isch, Dutch -isch and Latin -iscus. The same suffix gives English English, Scottish, Spanish and Finnish. Denisc therefore meant "of the Danes", and it is the exact formal ancestor of Danish.

  • Old English -isc — the adjectival suffix, pronounced /iʃ/ after palatalisation.
  • Middle English -ish — the same suffix in its later form, as in English and Scottish.
  • German -isch and Dutch -isch — cognate suffixes in the neighbouring Germanic languages.
  • Latin -iscus — a parallel suffix, showing the wider Indo-European pattern.

Sound history

Old English Denisc was pronounced with a short /e/ and a palatalised /sk/ that had already become /ʃ/ by the Old English period, the same change seen in fisc to fish. The word therefore had the shape /deniʃ/. In Middle English the final vowel weakened, and the modern form Danish emerged. The modern /eɪ/ in the first syllable is irregular from a purely phonological point of view and is generally explained by analogy with Dane, whose long vowel developed in an open syllable and then underwent the Great Vowel Shift. The spelling Danish follows the English -ish pattern rather than the Danish dansk.

The Danelaw and English history

The word entered English experience as the name of an enemy. From the late eighth century Scandinavian fleets raided the English coast, and in the ninth century they conquered and settled large parts of eastern and northern England. The area of Danish law and custom became known as the Danelaw (Dena lagu), and the adjective Denisc appears in the Chronicle, in laws and in poetry. The eventual assimilation of the settlers left a deep mark on English: place names ending in -by, -thorpe and -thwaite, and everyday words such as sky, egg and they, are among the legacies of the Danish presence.

From ethnicity to culture and commerce

In the early modern period Danish became a neutral geographical adjective for the kingdom and its Lutheran monarchy. The nineteenth century added a literary and aesthetic dimension: Hans Christian Andersen's tales were translated as Danish stories, and Danish design and education (the folk high school movement of Grundtvig) acquired a reputation for humanity and simplicity. In the twentieth century the word acquired two very different commercial lives: Danish bacon and Danish blue cheese in Britain, and the "Danish" pastry in the United States. The pastry is a particularly clear case of semantic relocation: a Viennese technique, naturalised in Denmark as wienerbrød, was exported to America and renamed after the country of export rather than the country of origin.

Modern usage

Today Danish is a stable, unmarked adjective with a wide collocational range. It is used in politics (the Danish model, Danish opt-outs), in design (Danish modern, Danish oil), in food (Danish pastry, Danish bacon) and in linguistics (Danish, Old Danish, Danish dialects). It can also be used absolutely as a language name ("she speaks Danish") and, in American English, as a count noun for a pastry ("a Danish"). The word has never acquired a strongly pejorative colour, though in the Viking age it was certainly a name to fear; its modern connotations are neutral to positive, with a persistent association of clean design, social welfare and cosy domesticity.
