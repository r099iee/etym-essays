- c. 800s-1100s (Old English): Forsacan meant to dispute, deny, refuse, oppose, or reject an accusation or alliance - Formal legal and contentious sense.
- c. 1150s-1400s (Middle English): Forsaken evolved into the primary verb for abandoning persons, renouncing worldly sins, or deserting religious vows - Central in medieval morality plays and biblical translations.
- c. 1500s-1700s (Early Modern English): Flourished in high theological, poetic, and essayistic prose (Shakespeare, King James Bible, Milton, Addison) with third-person forsakes / forsaketh - Peak of poignant emotional gravitas.
- c. 1800s-Present: Retained in elevated literary, religious, and formal prose for profound abandonment or renunciation.
