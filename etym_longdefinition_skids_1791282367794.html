Skids as inflected here is the third-person singular present of the verb skid, meaning to slide sideways or forward without proper traction, typically of a wheel, vehicle or foot losing grip on a slippery surface, and by extension to slip or decline. It can also be the plural of the noun skid meaning a sliding movement, a mark left by sliding, or a support for sliding. The core image is momentum continuing while control is lost.

The primary literal sense is vehicular: cars, bikes and trolleys skid on ice, oil or wet leaves. A second literal sense is bodily: boots skid on mud. A third technical sense survives in handling: to skid logs or pallets means to slide them on supports. Figuratively, prices, reputations and lives skid, meaning they slide downward out of control. The tone is dynamic and cautionary.

Polysemy and secondary meanings include:

  • To slide without traction: the bus skidded across the lane. This is the dominant modern verbal sense, born of motoring. The bridge from timber beams is that loads slid on skids, then wheels slid as if on skids.
  • To slip sideways in motion: aircraft skid in a turn, sledges skid. A specialised aeronautical and sporting extension where lateral slide matters.
  • Noun: sliding movement or mark: a skid, skid marks, anti-skid brakes. The event and its trace nominalised from the verb.
  • Noun: support or pallet: a timber skid, a skid of goods. The oldest sense, still alive in shipping and logging, where a skid enables sliding.
  • Figurative decline: to be on the skids, to hit the skids. American slang from logging skid roads leading down to poverty districts, generalised to any downward slide. The bridge is physical sliding reinterpreted as moral or economic fall.
  • Child slang: to skid as to evade: marginal playground use, to slip away. Minor extension of slipping.

Grammar and usage sharpen meaning. As a verb skid is often intransitive with adverbials of surface and direction: skid on ice, skid into a ditch, skid to a halt. As a noun skid collocates with brake, tyre, mark, row and road. The source sentence uses the motoring frame literally for a brain: after years of speeding, steering fails and thought skids into a ditch, a comic image of mental breakdown as a car crash. Register is standard informal-technical, at home in driving manuals, news reports and jokes alike.

Learners should master its argument structure: vehicles and people skid, surfaces cause skids with on and into, and results follow with into, across and to a halt. The noun-verb link is transparent, so skid marks, skid pan and anti-skid all remain analysable. Figurative extensions keep the same schema of control lost on a slippery moral or economic surface, which is why financial and sporting prose reaches for skid whenever decline feels sudden and friction-free. Review cue: picture locked wheels sliding past the steering line. That is the whole lesson.
