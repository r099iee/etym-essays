Linear derivation chain (single root word; onomatopoeic — no PIE ancestry; the chain begins with the sound): Imitative base *fis- "a soft, escaping hiss" (echoic; the source of the buzz; attested across Germanic as an autonomous sound-symbolic formation, with no shared Proto-Indo-European root) │ ├─> Old English fīsian "to break wind" (attested in early glosses) │ └─> Middle English fisten "to break wind quietly" (attested) │ └─> English fise (dialectal) "to break wind silently" (attested dialect verb) │ └─> English fizzle "to break wind without noise" (first attested c. 1530; the earliest recorded sense) │ ├─> 19th century: fizzle "to hiss or sputter feebly, as damp gunpowder, a wet fuse, or a dying flame" (the transfer from the scatological to the acoustic is documented from the 1840s) │ │ └─> fizzle out (American English, 1848) "to fail after a promising start — of a speech, boom, campaign, or career" (the dominant modern sense) │ │ └─> fizzling (present participle) "making a weak, sputtering hiss; failing or dying out" — 'a fizzling torch' │ │ └─> FIZZLING (the exact form of this card) │ └─> English fizz "to hiss like an effervescing liquid" (attested from 1660; a close cousin formation, not a direct parent) │ └─> fizzy (c. 1825), fizzing, fizz-bang └─> Sister imitative words of the Germanic *fis- cluster (all echoic, independent formations, no shared PIE root): ├─> Old Norse fisa "to break wind" → Icelandic físa, Swedish fisa ├─> German fisten (dialectal) / fisa (Low German) └─> Dutch fiesen (obsolete), Middle Low German visen Morphology note: fizzle is built on fizz + the frequentative suffix -le (as in sparkle, wriggle, crackle), giving "to fizz repeatedly and feebly"; fizzling adds the present participial -ing. The -le is the only morphological machinery; no stage is invented, and the c. 1530 first-attestation date and the 1848 first-attestation of fizzle out are fixed by the written record. The word's entire family is onomatopoeic, so no Proto-Indo-European ancestor is claimed or reconstructed.
