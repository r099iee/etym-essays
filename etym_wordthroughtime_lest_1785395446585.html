- [Old English formula (þу læs þе, “by that less that”)] - A literal comparative of reduction: the precaution “lessens” the danger by a degree; utterly ordinary spoken grammar of Old English, not yet a single word.
- [Middle English (les te / leste, 12th–14th c.)] - Now a fused conjunction meaning “for fear that” or “unless,” still interchangeable with its sibling unless; an everyday word in Chaucer's England, emotionally neutral and purely functional.
- [Formalization in written English (16th–18th c.)] - Fastened to the subjunctive (lest he fall) and adopted by the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, legal and official prose; begins to sound grave, solemn and literary rather than colloquial.
- [Modern register (20th–21st c.)] - Has vanished from everyday speech, replaced by “in case” and “so that … not”; survives as a deliberate, bookish, slightly archaic choice and in the fixed national phrase “lest we forget.”
