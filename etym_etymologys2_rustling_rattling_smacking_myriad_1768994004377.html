Four root words — four independent chains: 1. RUSTLING — Germanic, imitative (onomatopoeic), no PIE ancestry: - Old English hristlian / hrūstlian, "to make a soft, sibilant noise, to rustle" (also hristan "to shake, to brandish") (attested c. 8th–11th c.), built on an imitative Germanic base *hrus-/*hris-, "to shake with a soft sound". NO PIE ANCESTOR: these sound-words are imitative creations formed afresh within Germanic; nothing secure reconstructs earlier. - Middle English rustelen, "to rustle" (attested 14th c.) — the regular development of the Old English verb. - English rustle, verb "to make a soft, dry, muffled sound of friction" (of leaves, silk, papers, reeds) (14th c. onward). - English rustling, noun and present participle/adjective — the form on this card (the -ing from Old English -ung/-ing, the Germanic progressive-nominal suffix). NOTE ON THE SECOND SENSE: the American sense "cattle rustling / to rustle cattle" (19th c.) derives from a dialectal "to hurry, to hustle, to rustle up", NOT from the sound-word — a homonymic merger, not a semantic extension of the sound. Final node: RUSTLING. 2. RATTLING — Germanic, imitative (onomatopoeic), no PIE ancestry: - Old English hrætel / hrætele (also hratele), noun "a rattle" — originally the name of the plant "rattlewort" (so called from its rattling seeds) (attested c. 10th–11th c.), built on the imitative base *hrata-, a repetition of a percussive syllable. NO PIE ANCESTOR: the word is created afresh in Germanic as sound-symbolism. - Middle English ratelen, verb "to rattle" (attested 14th c.) — developed from the noun or from the same base. - English rattle, verb "to make a rapid series of short sharp noises" (14th c.); rattle, noun "a rapid series of sharp sounds; a toy; the tail-appendage of a rattlesnake" (16th c. for the toy). - English rattling, noun and present participle/adjective — the form on this card. The family proliferated: rattler (a rattlesnake), rattlesnake (17th c.), death rattle (18th c.), and the colloquial intensifier "a rattling good yarn". Final node: RATTLING. 3. SMACKING — two distinct etymons merged in one form: - Chain A (the taste-word): Old English smæc / smacc, noun "taste, flavour, savour" (attested c. 9th–11th c.) < Proto-Germanic *smakkaz, "taste, flavour" (possibly related to PIE *smeg-, "to taste"; the deeper link is uncertain and debated). Middle English smak "taste" → English smack, noun "taste, flavour, a small quantity or suggestion" ("a smack of salt"). - Chain B (the sound-word): Middle Dutch smacken / Low German smakken, verb "to strike (the lips) sharply, to smack" (an imitative formation, attested 15th c.), borrowed into English as smack, verb "to strike smartly, to open and close the lips with a sharp sound" (16th c.). NO PIE ANCESTOR: this is a fresh sound-imitation. - English smacking, noun and present participle — the form on this card, from the imitative Chain B; the sense "to smack one's lips" (16th c.) names the audible sign of relish, cf. Hindi चटकारा (chatkārā, "lip-smacking"). Final node: SMACKING. 4. MYRIAD — Greek numeral-word, non-Indo-European origin in its numerals: - Greek myríoi, "countless, numberless; ten thousand" (Homeric, 8th c. BCE; the numeral sense standard in Classical Greek) — the origin of the numeral is UNKNOWN / possibly Pre-Greek; no secure PIE etymology is established for this formation (stated, not guessed). - Greek myriás (μυριάς), genitive myriádos, noun "the number ten thousand; a countless host" (Classical Greek, 5th c. BCE onward) — a formation from myríoi with the abstract suffix -ás. - Latin myrias / myriades, "ten thousand; an indefinitely large number" (borrowed from Greek in classical and post-classical Latin; used in astronomy and mathematics). - English myriad, noun "ten thousand; an indefinitely great number", and adjective "countless, numberless" (16th c., 1550s), borrowed from Greek via Latin; the noun sense "ten thousand" was still literal in 16th–17th c. mathematics and astronomy (via the French/Latin tradition), while the "countless" hyperbole became the living sense. Final node: MYRIAD.
