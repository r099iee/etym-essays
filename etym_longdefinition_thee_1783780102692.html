thee (pronoun) — the objective (accusative and dative) singular form of the second-person singular pronoun thou, grammatically the object counterpart of "thou" and equivalent in function to modern "you" in object position, but restricted today to archaic, poetic, liturgical, dialectal, and deliberately archaizing registers. Core grammatical senses. In the old two-case system of English personal pronouns, thee carries every oblique function that modern "you" performs without distinction: (1) the direct object (accusative) of a verb, as in "I give thee my word of honor" or "Does this truth please thee?"; (2) the indirect object (dative), as in "I give thee a ring" — the "to you" of "I give a ring to you"; and (3) the object of a preposition, as in "with thee," "to thee," "before thee," "unto thee." The subject counterpart is thou ("thou art"), and the possessive set is thy before consonants and thine before vowels or standing alone ("thy book," "thine eyes," "it is thine"). In Old English the dative (þē) and accusative (þēc / þec) were still partially distinct, but by the Middle English period they had merged into a single oblique thee, so the modern word is a unitary object form. Register and social charge. Before roughly the sixteenth century thee was simply the ordinary singular object pronoun, used for friend and stranger alike. With the rise of the "T/V" politeness system (modelled on French vous), the plural you invaded singular address, and thou/thee became socially marked. The pronoun then signalled intimacy, equality, condescension downward, or contempt: Quakers used it as egalitarian "plain language"; masters used it to servants and children; lovers and friends exchanged it in private; and address to God — "to thee I speak" — made it the pronoun of prayer. The King James Bible (1611) and the Book of Common Prayer preserve it as the reverent second person of worship. Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Poetic and literary "thee": in verse from Spenser, Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth, and later poets, thee is a metrically convenient, dignified, or archaic object form. Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) opens by addressing the heavenly Spirit that "dost prefer / Before all Temples th' upright heart and pure" — "Delight thee more" — where thee frames the invocation with reverence and intimacy.
  • Liturgical "thee": the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, and modern liturgical English keep thee in prayers and hymns ("woe unto thee," "I call upon thee," "we give thanks unto thee"), lending worship a solemn, archaic register.
  • Quaker plain speech: since the seventeenth century the Society of Friends used thee (often with a third-person verb, "thee is") as the egalitarian pronoun of everyday address, rejecting you as flattering and hierarchical; the practice survives in some Friends' meetings today.
  • Regional dialect: parts of northern England — Yorkshire, Lancashire, Cumberland, Durham — kept thee (and the subject form tha) in ordinary colloquial speech well into the twentieth century ("I telled thee," "come wi' thee"), where it is a marker of dialect authenticity rather than poetry.
  • Fixed phrases and idioms: "get thee gone," "get thee behind me," "woe unto thee," "fare thee well," and the expression "thee-and-thou" (to address someone with the familiar form: "he thee-and-thou'd the duke").
  • Denominative verb "to thee": from the pronoun English forms a verb meaning "to address (someone) as thou/thee," a sociolinguistic action word: "Do not thee me, sirrah!"
  • Period and archaizing use: historical fiction, fantasy, and parody use thee to evoke old times; there it is a stylistic marker of solemnity or quaintness rather than a grammatical necessity, and misuse (e.g., using thee as subject) is a common parody device.
Historical semantic bridge. The full story is not semantic drift but grammatical and social retreat. The word's core meaning — the singular "you" in object position — never changed. What shifted was its register: from default to marked. The bridge runs through the sixteenth-century collapse of the singular/plural and informal/polite distinctions into a universal you, which pushed thee out of neutral usage into the niches of intimacy, religion, dialect, and poetry, where it survives as a living archaism with a strong affective charge.
