- [1650s: the loose wheel] - wabble enters English from Low German wabbeln, purely physical: “to move unsteadily from side to side,” a wheel rocking on a bent axle, a loose joint — no figurative reach yet.
- [1690s–18th century: the vacillator] - the noun wobble (“an unsteady rocking motion,” 1690s) appears, and the figurative extension arrives: to wobble is to be unstable, to vacillate, and the word is applied to opinions, prices, and governments — the waverer's word.
- [19th century: bodily wobble] - the -o- spelling wobble prevails (1851) and wobbly (1850s) is coined; the fanciful collywobbles (1823), a blend of colic and wobble, carries the unsteadiness into the churning stomach, the body's most intimate wobble.
- [20th century: the tantrum] - British slang “to throw a wobbly” (1968) makes the unsteadiness a fit of temper; science borrows the word for the Chandler wobble of the Earth's axis, a planet wobbling on its pivot.
- [Contemporary: jelly and governments] - wobble spans mechanics and politics, dessert and policy; Tolkien uses it at its purely physical oldest — legs rocking loose on slack joints in the sun, involuntary oscillation of a body no longer under command.
