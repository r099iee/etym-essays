The biography of RECOMMENCED runs from a root about going to a word about beginning again, and each stage of the road is a small chapter in the history of Latin. At the bottom lies the PIE root *h₁ey-, "to go," one of the most widely attested motion roots in the family: Sanskrit éti "he goes" and yāti "he goes," Avestan aēiti, Greek eîmi "I go," Lithuanian eĩti, Old Church Slavonic iti, and Latin īre all continue it, so the root has survived in recognisable shape for roughly five thousand years. In Latin, īre proved extraordinarily productive, generating a family of prefixed verbs and their nouns: exitus "a going out," iter "a journey" (a going), and comes "companion" — literally "one who goes with" — which, through Old French comte, gave English count, the nobleman, and county, the district. The most important derivative for this word is inīre, "to go into, to enter upon," and its verbal noun initium, "a going in," which Latin extended to mean "a beginning": the Romans conceived of beginning as entering upon a road, and that metaphor is sealed into the word.

From initium, Latin formed initiāre, "to begin, to initiate," and from this the everyday Latin of the empire produced a strengthened compound, *cominitiāre, "to begin with, to commence," built with com- "with, together." This passed into Old French as comencier, the ancestor of modern French commencer, and was borrowed into Middle English around 1300 as commencen — English "commence." From the first it was a formal word: one commences an undertaking, a war, a ceremony, never a casual action, and English kept that register — "commencement" is still the ceremonial word for a graduation. The verb's core is the "go" root; the older condensed tree's attempt to ground it in PIE *wert- "to turn" is simply wrong and has been set aside.

The second ingredient is the prefix re-, "back, again," inherited by Latin from a source that is itself uncertain — the OED cautiously labels its origin obscure, so no PIE root is asserted for it. What is certain is its function: re- marks repetition or return, and attached to commence it makes "to begin again." The English verb to recommence appears in the sixteenth century, and the form recommenced, the past tense and past participle, dates from the same period. From the beginning it was a word of formal re-beginnings — of proceedings, of undertakings, of ceremonies — the verb of a restart performed with dignity rather than haste, and it is that dignity the word has never lost.

In the social history of the word, recommence belongs to the language of institutions. The law recommences proceedings; the theatre recommences a play after an interval; a match recommences after a stoppage; and in formal narrative prose the word is used to give gravity to any resumed action, from a journey to a scrutiny. It is this institutional and ceremonial weight that David Hawkes exploits in the scene in Hong lou meng: Bao-yu moves over again, draws a chair up beside Dai-yu, and "recommences his scrutiny" — the ceremonious verb makes a deliberate, almost ritual undertaking out of what is, in fact, a boy staring at a girl. The comedy of the scene lies precisely in the disproportion between the grand verb and the small act, and the narrator's stately register makes the reader smile at the boy's solemnity. The word thus does what it has always done: it signals that an action is being undertaken afresh, as an act of will, with full ceremony.

By the later modern period the word had begun to recede from ordinary speech. Everyday English prefers "restart" or "resume," and "recommence" now reads as stiff, deliberate, even faintly ironic — a ceremonious way of saying "begin again" that carries an old-fashioned gravitas which can tip into pomposity. It survives most naturally in formal, legal and literary contexts, exactly the contexts that preserve its meaning best, and its Latinate architecture — re- + com- + initiāre, "again + together + to go in, to enter" — remains visible to any speaker who looks: to recommence is to enter upon a road once more. The word is a small museum of its own history, from the most ancient PIE verb of motion to a ceremonious joke in a great eighteenth-century Chinese novel, and its long journey shows how a language builds formality: the deeper the roots in "going" and "entering," the grander the simple act of doing something again.
