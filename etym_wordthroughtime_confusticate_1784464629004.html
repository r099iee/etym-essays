“Confusticate” was coined in the mid-19th century as a playful mock-Latin verb — a made-up flourish that sounded like scholarship while meaning only “confuse or confound,” profanity with the profanity removed.
“Confusticate” thrived in Victorian comic papers and the libretti of W. S. Gilbert, where respectable characters needed curses that thundered without swearing.
“Confusticate” by the 20th century had become a fossil of family-friendly comic diction, cherished in children’s literature and gently dated in speech.
“Confusticate” lives on in fantasy English through Tolkien’s Bilbo, who uses it as the grand, pseudo-learned half of a two-part oath (“Confusticate and bebother these dwarves!”).
