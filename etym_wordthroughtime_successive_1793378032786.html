- Early 15th Century (c. 1420s) - Borrowed via Middle French *successif* from Medieval Latin *successīvus*; originally confined to legal, ecclesiastical, and royal records concerning the hereditary succession of crowns, titles, and property; perceived as a formal, statutory term.
- Late 16th to 17th Century - Expanded into humanistic literature, natural philosophy, and drama (e.g., Shakespeare, Francis Bacon, John Locke); used to describe the uninterrupted flow of time, natural seasons, and the train of human thoughts; perceived as an elegant marker of chronological order.
- 18th to 19th Century - Heavily adopted in military and naval doctrine (tactical attacks by successive divisions or columns) and in geology and paleontology (successive rock strata and fossil records); symbolized continuous empirical progression.
- 20th Century to Present - Universally utilized across standard English, mathematics (successive approximations), ecology (successive stages of community succession), and everyday journalism to denote consecutive items in a chain.
