Linear derivation chain for SHAFTS (root word: shaft): 1. Proto-Indo-European *skep- (also reconstructed as *skap-/*skabʰ-) "to cut, to scrape, to shave" — the root behind the "shave" words; the "shaft" noun is understood as "something scraped or shaved straight". The exact grade and suffix are reconstructed, not attested in writing. 2. Proto-Germanic *skaftaz "shaft, pole, staff", literally "something scraped or shaved into shape" (1st half of the 1st millennium CE); cognates include Old Norse skapt, Old Saxon skaft, Old High German scaft, Gothic *skafts, and Latin scāpus "shaft, stalk" (a separate Italic formation from the same PIE root, via Greek skápos). 3. Old English sceaft "staff, pole, spear-shaft, arrow-shaft", attested from the 8th c. (e.g., in the glosses and in Beowulf, where sceaft names a spear-shaft among war-gear). 4. Middle English shaft "spear-shaft, arrow-shaft, staff, pole" (12th–15th c.), continuing the Old English word with the regular sound changes (initial /sk/ palatalized to /ʃ/, so spelled sh-). 5. Modern English shaft — with the core weapon sense continuing, and from the 16th–17th c. the extended senses multiplying: a shaft of light, a mine shaft, a column shaft, and figurative "shafts of wit". The plural form SHAFTS is the ordinary plural of the same word, used throughout. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGE: the derivation from PIE *skep- "to cut, scrape" via a Germanic "scraped/shaped wood" is the standard account (so the OED and Etymonline, comparing Germanic *skaftaz with Germanic *skaban- "to shave"), but the derivational morphology is debated: some scholars derive *skaftaz instead from a root meaning "to support, prop" (cf. Latin scāpus), and the precise PIE vocalism (*skep- vs. *skap-) and suffix (*-to-) are reconstructed rather than attested. The PIE stage is therefore presented as the standard reconstruction, with the caveat that no PIE word is ever written down; the Germanic, Old English, and Middle English stages are solidly attested.
