The name Constantine is a Latin proper name whose internal formation is transparent: the imperial name Cōnstantīnus is built from the adjective cōnstāns, "steadfast, constant" (the present participle of the verb cōnstāre, "to stand firm"), plus the suffix -īnus, "belonging to," giving literally "belonging to Constans," i.e., "the man of the steadfast one." Beneath that Latin surface lies one of the most productive roots of the Indo-European family, the verbal stem *steh₂- (traditionally written *stā-), "to stand, to set upright, to be firm."

The PIE root and its far-flung family. Proto-Indo-European *steh₂- "to stand" branched across nearly every major branch of the family. In Indo-Aryan it gave Sanskrit sthā- "to stand" (sthāti "he stands," sthāṇa- "standing place"), whence modern Hindi sthān "place" and sthiti "state, standing," and Odia sthāna. In Iranian it produced Avestan hištaiti "he stands" and, much later, Persian istādan "to stand." In Greek it yielded hístēmi "to set up, stand," with the famous nominal children stásis "a standing, a faction" and stēlē "an upright inscribed slab." In the Baltic branch it gave Lithuanian stóti "to stand up"; in Slavic, Russian stojátʹ "to stand." In Germanic it formed the strong verb *standan, attested as Gothic standan, Old Norse standa, and Old English standan — our English "stand" — while related zero-grade formations underlie "stead," "still," and "stall." In Italic the root produced the stative verb stāre "to stand," whose Latin descendants are legion: status "state," statua "statue," statio "station," stabulum "stable," and the compounds constāre, astāre, restāre, obstāre, and praestāre.

The Latin compound and the Roman virtue. From stāre, Latin built cōnstāre — con- "together" + stāre — literally "to stand together with," hence "to hold firm, to remain fixed, to be consistent." Its present participle cōnstāns "standing firm, steadfast" fed an abstract noun cōnstantia, "steadfastness," which the Romans ranked among the cardinal virtues; constancy was the moral quality of remaining at one's post, unshaken by fortune. As a cognomen, Constans/Cōnstantius named the man of this virtue, and the possessive formation Cōnstantīnus, "belonging to Constans," became a family name of the imperial house: Constantius I Chlorus, father of Constantine; Constantine the Great himself (c. 272–337); Constantius II; Constans; Constantinus II.

From name to world-historical emblem. The name's destiny was set by the bearer. Elevated at York in 306, victor over Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge in 312, master of the whole empire by 324, Constantine legalised Christianity (Edict of Milan, 313), summoned the Council of Nicaea (325), and in 330 refounded the Greek city of Byzantium as his new capital, Constantinople — Kōnstantinoúpolis, "the city of Constantine." The name thereby left the narrow channel of personal onomastics and entered geography, theology, and historiography. Every later Byzantine emperor bore it in deliberate echo, down to Constantine XI Palaiologos, who died on the walls in 1453. In the West, Latin Cōnstantīnus became Old French Constantin and Middle English Constantyn; the Greek Kōnstantĩnos survived in the Orthodox world as Konstantinos (Russian Konstantin); the Romance forms are Costantino and Constantino.

The name in literature and modern usage. In the present context, Macaulay (following the older historians) uses "the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine" as the synecdoche of the late-imperial court. Gibbon and later historians made the same pairing: Diocletian's Tetrarchy and Constantine's monarchy are the two reorganisations of the late empire, and their courts — still exhibiting the pomp of the ancient world in the slowly fading Eastern provinces — are exactly what Macaulay evokes. Modern scholarship keeps the name alive through "Constantinian" (his dynasty, his architecture, his church-state synthesis) and the phrase "Constantinian shift," the technical shorthand for the moment Christianity fused with imperial power. The adjective and the concept both descend directly from the personal name, and both retain the etymological resonance of the firm root: stāre "to stand," the emperor who stood firm, the church that stood with the state.

  • c. 272–337 — life of Flavius Valerius Constantinus; the name is imperial and victorious.
  • 312–330 — Milvian Bridge, Edict of Milan, Council of Nicaea, founding of Constantinople; the name becomes synonymous with Christian empire and with the eastern capital.
  • 4th–15th c. — Constantinian dynastic reuse; the name is borne by emperors until 1453.
  • 8th c. — the Donation of Constantine forges the papacy's western claims in the emperor's name.
  • 15th c.–present — humanists expose the Donation; Gibbon and Macaulay make Constantine (with Diocletian) the synecdoche of late-imperial splendour; "Constantinian" becomes a technical theological term.
