- Mid-16th c. — "twang" is coined as an imitative, echoic English word for the sharp, ringing vibration of a taut plucked string; noun and verb appear almost together, and "twanged" is the verb's crisp single event — the string snapped and sung in one beat.
- 17th–19th c. — the sound-idea transfers from strings to the voice: a "nasal twang" is speech squeezed through the taut membranes of nose and throat, and whole regional accents are said to twang; the word keeps its thin, high, ringing quality and takes on a faintly rustic, comic flavour.
- 20th c. — the banjo and the guitar make "twang" a word of popular music and of American regional character; in narrative prose it stays percussive and precise — the one-syllable report of a weapon or instrument being released, brief as the sound it names.
- Contemporary — "twanged" still names the sharp resonance of a plucked string (a bowstring, a guitar string), exactly one syllable long and exactly as long as the sound; its brevity is still the point — the whole noise lived and gone in an instant.
