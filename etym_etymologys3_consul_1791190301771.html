The evolution of consul begins with the Latin verb consulere, to deliberate, take counsel, consult, and to decide after consultation. Roman antiquarians already linked consul to consulere and to consilium, counsel. Modern historical linguistics accepts this connection: the consul is etymologically the one who consults together with colleague and Senate and then acts. The deeper pre-Latin formation joins the prefix con-, together, with a base related to taking or getting counsel, conventionally traced to Proto-Indo-European *sel-, to take, seize, grasp, in the specialized sense of taking counsel. This is the same broad root family that yields Latin consilium, consultus, and English consult, counsel, consultation.

In early Rome the title replaced kingship. Tradition dates the first consuls to 509 BCE after the expulsion of Tarquin the Proud, initially called praetores or judges before the name consules stabilized. The change was ideological: two colleagues, elected annually by the centuriate assembly, with mutual veto, twelve lictors each alternating monthly fasces, and accountability after office. The consul commanded the legions, convoked Senate and people, executed decrees, and performed public auspices. The eponymous dating formula, Marco Tullio Cicerone et Gaio Antonio Hybrida consulibus, made the names everyday time-markers throughout the Mediterranean.

  • Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Italic: root *sel-, to take, seize, with prefix *kom-, together, yields a compound idea of taking counsel together. Italic sound laws preserve s and vocalism that surface in Latin -sul-.
  • Old and Classical Latin: consulo, consulere, consului, consultum, to deliberate, consult oracle or Senate, look to interests of; noun consul, consulis (m.), agent noun of deliberation; adjective consularis, office consulatus. Attested from the Twelve Tables and early fasti through Cicero, Livy, Tacitus.
  • Imperial transformation: Augustus retains the consulship while concentrating power; emperors hold it repeatedly; suffect consuls multiply; by the third century it is largely honorific. Constantine and later emperors use ordinary consulships as imperial prestige; barbarian kings seek the title from Constantinople.
  • Late Latin to Old French: Late Latin consul persists in administration, church, and learning. Old French borrows consul, consule by the 13th century for Roman magistrates and for contemporary southern municipal magistrates and merchant Law of the Sea judges.
  • Middle and Early Modern English: consul enters Middle English in the late 14th century (Trevisa, translations of Latin histories) for Roman magistrates; by the 16th century it extends to resident agents for merchants abroad in the Levant, North Africa, and later worldwide, as in Hakluyt and state papers.
  • Modern diplomacy: 19th-century professionalization creates career consular services; the 1963 Vienna Convention codifies consular functions, immunity, and the distinction between consular and diplomatic staff. Napoleonic First Consul revives the Roman aura briefly for executive rule.

Phonetically the development is conservative because the word travelled as a learned borrowing rather than through continuous popular speech. Latin consul with short o and u yields Old French consul with nasalized vowel and Modern English with anglicized stress on the first syllable and reduced second vowel. Spelling remains stable across Latin, French, and English, reinforcing the sense of unbroken Roman prestige.

Semantically the drift is from action to office to person to building and district. Consulere as activity produces consul as actor, consulatus as tenure, consulate as office, building, and jurisdiction. Literary attestations fix each stage: Livy and Cicero for republican ideology, Suetonius and Cassius Dio for imperial hollowing, medieval charters of Arles, Pisa, and Genoa for communal revival, Shakespeare and Jonson for English awareness of Roman consuls, and modern treaties for consular law. The ivory cane in your passage belongs to the first stage, when the staff literally embodied the right to consult auspices, command, and judge.
