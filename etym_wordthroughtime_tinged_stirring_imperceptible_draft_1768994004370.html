- tinged
- 16th c. - the verb tinge enters English from Latin tingere, "to dip, soak, dye"; its past participle tinged first means simply "dipped or stained," a physical act of dyeing cloth.
- 17th–19th c. - the dipping-and-dyeing sense retreats into metaphor: air, voices, and memories are "tinged with" something; the word comes to mean a small, diffused addition that changes the whole without dominating it, and it picks up a faint hint of contamination ("tinged with regret").
- 20th–21st c. - almost exclusively metaphorical and literary ("sky tinged with pink," "voice tinged with sadness"); a word of edges and undertones, favoured when the colour or feeling seems added from outside rather than intrinsic.
- stirring
- 13th–14th c. - stir enters English from Old English styrian, "to move, agitate, rouse"; the noun stirring names the first faint motion out of stillness.
- 16th–18th c. - the word extends from physical motion to emotional arousal: "a stirring speech," "a stirring of hope"; it acquires its poetic-to-formal register and its sense of an awakening out of stasis.
- 19th–21st c. - stirring keeps its double life - the very first tremor of movement and the rising of feeling - and in literary prose it names motion at the threshold of sensation, before anything can yet be called a wind.
- imperceptible
- 16th c. - imperceptible enters English from Late Latin imperceptibilis, "not graspable" (in- "not" + percipere "to seize, grasp"); it first describes whatever the senses cannot take in.
- 17th–19th c. - it becomes a key word of philosophy and science for changes too fine for observation - imperceptible atoms, imperceptible gradations - its register formal and analytical.
- 20th–21st c. - standard in scientific and literary description alike; it sets the very scale of perception, naming a change so fine that it can only be noticed in hindsight, or half-believed while it happens.
- draft
- 14th c. - the noun (British draught) enters English meaning literally "that which is drawn" - a pull, a load, a haul - from the verb to draw.
- 16th–17th c. - the current-of-air sense appears: a stream of air "drawn" through a chimney or a doorway; the spelling draft emerges and becomes standard in American English.
- 19th–21st c. - draft settles as the everyday word for a small, localised, often unwelcome current of air indoors, felt as a contrast to surrounding stillness; it also names a first sketch, a money order, and conscription, all things "drawn up" - but here it is purely the breath of the cave against the skin.
