The history of neurosis represents one of the most consequential conceptual transformations in the history of medicine, psychiatry, and modern culture—evolving from an 18th-century bodily diagnosis into the core psychological landscape of 20th-century human consciousness.

1. Indo-European Foundations and Greek Anatomy:

  • The word is built on the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)neh₁- or *(s)new-, meaning "to spin, twist, sew," which yielded words for cords, threads, and sinews across Indo-European languages (compare Latin nervus, English snare, and Sanskrit snāvan-).
  • In Ancient Greek, this root produced neûron (νεῦρον). In early Homeric Greek, neûron meant "sinew, tendon, or bowstring." Only later, through the anatomical dissections of the Alexandrian physicians Herophilus and Erasistratus in the 3rd century BCE, was the distinction between tendons and true neurological fibers (nerves transmitting sensation and motor control) first established, fixing neûron as the anatomical term for a nerve.
  • The medical suffix -ōsis (-ωσις) was standard in Greek for describing a dynamic physiological process, diseased state, or abnormal condition (as in stenosis, thrombosis, and necrosis).

2. William Cullen and the 18th-Century Coining (1769):

  • The Neo-Latin compound neurosis was formally coined in 1769 by the influential Scottish physician and chemist **William Cullen** (1710–1790) in his medical text Synopsis Nosologiae Methodicae.
  • Cullen, seeking an empirical taxonomy of diseases, created the class Neuroses to categorize all illnesses characterized by disturbances in sensation and voluntary movement that were not accompanied by fever or localized tissue inflammation. In Cullen's original physicalist framework, neuroses included epilepsy, chorea, asthma, tetanus, and melancholia, all attributed to subtle imbalances in the physical "nervous fluid."

3. The Psychoanalytic Revolution (Freud and Janet):

  • In the late 19th century, French neurologists Jean-Martin Charcot and Pierre Janet began studying hysteria, demonstrating that many neurotic symptoms could be induced and cured through hypnosis, proving their psychological origin.
  • Sigmund Freud radically transformed the concept in the 1890s and early 1900s. Freud distinguished between "actual neuroses" (physical tensions caused by somatic sexual frustration) and **psychoneuroses** (hysteria, obsessional neurosis, phobias), which he proved were generated by unconscious psychic conflicts. In Freud's model, the Ego represses traumatic memories or unacceptable sexual/aggressive impulses, which subsequently re-emerge in disguised, symbolic forms as neurotic symptoms.
  • Through Freud, Carl Jung, Alfred Adler, and Karen Horney, neurosis became the dominant framework for understanding human personality, neurodivergence, and cultural symbolism throughout the first half of the 20th century.

4. Modern DSM Demise vs. Semiotic and Literary Elevation:

  • In 1980, the American Psychiatric Association removed "neurosis" as a formal diagnostic category from the DSM-III, replacing it with descriptive syndromes (Anxiety Disorders, Mood Disorders). In quantitative psychology, however, it remains fundamental as the **neuroticism** trait (emotional instability and threat-sensitivity) in the Big Five personality model.
  • In literary and critical theory, Roland Barthes in The Pleasure of the Text (1973) re-evaluated neurosis not as an impairment, but as the essential subjective vulnerability that makes literature erotic and compelling. For Barthes, a text devoid of neurosis is cold, polite, and sterile (frigid); it is only when the author's idiosyncratic neurosis and desire puncture the language that the text catches fire and produces blissful readerly communion.
