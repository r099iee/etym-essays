Bracket looks plainly English but arrives through a risqué French detour: it begins as a word for trousers and codpieces. The architectural corbel reminded Renaissance builders of the forked cloth bracket of breeches, and the name crossed from tailoring to masonry in the sixteenth century. From a wall-prop the word then wandered, by pure shape-analogy, into printing-houses, offices, clinics, and sports pages, becoming one of English’s handiest grouping words.

  • Gaulish-Latin bracae: trousers of the barbarians. Latin borrowed bracae (singular braca) from Gaulish for the loose trousers of Celts and Germans, a garment Romans first mocked then adopted for soldiers on northern frontiers. The word is Celtic in origin, with no deeper Indo-European pedigree beyond the clothing itself; it survives in French braies and English breeches / britches (the same root by Germanic route). Meaning at this stage is purely sartorial: baggy northern leg-wear.
  • Old–Middle French brague, braguette: codpiece and small clasp. From bracae came Old French brague “knee-breeches, drawers,” and its diminutive braguette (modern spelling braguette) meaning the front flap or codpiece of hose, then by extension any small forked clasp, mortise, or corbel-tab in joinery — the little forked piece that holds two members together. The semantic step is shape-metaphor: a forked cloth flap looks like a forked wooden tab. Dates: brague 12th–13th c., braguette 14th–15th c. in tailoring and building accounts.
  • 1570s–1610s: English bragget / bracket as architectural prop. English masons and joiners borrowing French building terms take braguette as bragget, bracket: a small forked support jutting from a wall to carry a shelf, cornice, or bust. Spenser (1590s) and building contracts use it for stone corbels; by the 1610s the spelling bracket stabilises. The codpiece sense never crosses the Channel — English keeps only the hardware meaning, bleached of any bawdy echo, which is why modern speakers hear nothing indecent in a shelf-bracket.
  • 1600s–1750s: shelves, lamps, clocks, gun-fittings. The support lends its name to what it supports: wall-brackets holding candles (gas-brackets after the 1810s), hanging bracket-clocks, picture-brackets, ship’s brackets and gun-carriage brackets in naval carpentry. Coachbuilders — the Long Acre men of the source passage, c. 1820s — belong here: fitting a private post-chaise with a folding “bracket” jump-seat and door “pockets” for a family’s two-month summer tour. The verb to bracket (“furnish with brackets”) is recorded in this workshop sense alongside the joiner’s.
  • 1750s–1800s: printers see brackets on the page. The curved marks ( ) used since the 1500s to set off asides had been called parentheses; printers now nickname ( ) and [ ] brackets (1750, 1760s) because each mark looks like a wall-corbel in profile. The verb follows: to bracket words = to enclose them in such marks (1760s–1790s), then figuratively to bracket persons or things together = to couple them in thought (Coleridge, 1810s; Macaulay, 1830s). This is the ancestor of every modern grouping use.
  • 19th–21st c.: taxes, teeth, tournaments, artillery. Victorian administration gives price/age/income bracket (the interval “held” between two limits); dentistry (1900s) cements brackets to teeth to hold wires; gunners speak of bracketing a target with short-and-over ranging shots; American sport (1920s onward) prints elimination brackets whose drawn lines literally look like nested shelf-supports. Computing adds angle brackets for tags and curly brackets for code blocks. The child’s carriage-seat and the programmer’s { } are thus cousins, both descended from a French tailor’s word for trousers via a mason’s wall-prop.

Note on debated mediation: dictionaries agree on the bracae → brague → braguette → bracket spine but differ on whether the immediate donor was braguette (codpiece/clasp) or a parallel joiner’s braguet (little corbel); Etymonline and the OED favour the braguette diminutive route, with Spanish bragueta as a cognate witness rather than the donor. The trouser-origin itself is secure.
