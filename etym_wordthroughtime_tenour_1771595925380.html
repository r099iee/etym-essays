- [14th-15th century continuous course] - [The -our spelling of tenor enters English from Latin tenere, 'to hold', meaning a holding or continuous course; connotation of a steady onward thread]
- [16th-17th century legal and formal] - [Used of the 'tenor' of a document — its exact wording, purport, or sustained character; connotation precise and legalistic]
- [18th-19th century literary drift] - [Ruskin's 'the tenour of the work' names the book's steady inner bearing — the continuity of purpose against which passages are measured; connotation formal, literary, gently defensive]
- [20th-21st century retreat of the -our form] - [The British -our spelling falls out of general use and 'tenor' becomes standard; 'tenour' survives mainly as period flavour, while the word still means the settled direction or purport of a work, life, or remark]
