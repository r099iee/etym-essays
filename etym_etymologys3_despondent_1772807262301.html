The biography of despondent begins not with sadness but with ritual. The root that lies beneath it, reconstructed as PIE *spend-, referred to the solemn act of pouring a libation — an offering of wine, oil, or milk poured out to the gods as the sealing of a vow. In a world where a promise was made sacred by a libation, the root carried two ideas fused together: pouring and pledging. That fusion is visible across the family. In Greek it produced spondein, 'to pour a drink-offering,' and spondē, 'libation,' and from the slow two-long-syllable chant that accompanied such rites came the metrical term spondee, a foot of two long syllables — so that even English poetry preserves the echo of the sacrificial pouring. In the Near East, the Hittite ishpanti-, 'to pour a fluid sacrifice,' has been connected to the same root, showing how old and how widely spread the ritual concept was. On the Italic side the root developed in a remarkable way. Proto-Italic *spondejo- 'to pledge' became Latin spondēre, 'to promise solemnly, to pledge oneself.' From this single verb Latin generated an entire family of commitment words, and through them a large part of the modern English vocabulary of obligation: sponsus, 'one pledged' — a betrothed person — gave spouse and espouse; sponsor, originally a surety or guarantor (and later a godparent who pledges for a child), gives English sponsor; respondēre, 'to promise back,' to answer for something, gives respond, response, and responsible; correspondēre gives correspond. All these words carry, under their different prefixes, the same image of a solemn pledged word, and all descend from the ritual of the poured offering. The word on this card, however, took the opposite road from its siblings. Latin built dēspondēre by adding the preverb de-, 'away, off,' to spondēre. The literal meaning was 'to promise something away' — to hand over a claim, to betroth a daughter, to resign a right, to give up an argument. From this legal and social sense a powerful figurative extension arose: to 'promise away' not an estate or a bride but one's own spirit. The phrase animam dēspondēre, 'to pledge away one's soul,' crystallised in Latin prose the idea of surrendering hope and resolution — giving up courage as one gives up a claim. The present participle dēspondēns, dēspondentem, 'giving up, losing heart,' carried this meaning into the adjective that English would borrow. English borrowed despondent in the late seventeenth century, in the middle of a religious and moral literature deeply concerned with despair. The verb despond, 'to give up hope,' appears in theological writing in the 1650s, and despondency follows soon after as a named condition: the quiet, inward collapse of the spirit after repeated disappointment, distinct from the dramatic agonies of despair because it was resigned rather than raging. The adjective despondent, 'having lost hope or courage,' settled into the vocabulary of what the eighteenth century called melancholy, and from there passed into the psychological and literary language of the nineteenth century. In modern English despondent still carries its etymology in its meaning. It does not simply describe low spirits; it describes the state of someone who has, as it were, promised away their hope — who has stopped expecting a good outcome and handed over their courage. That is why the word is apt in the Red Chamber sentence: Ying-lian's despondency is not a passing low mood but the quiet surrender of expectation after years of repeated betrayal, the moment when the spirit is pledged away. The word family remains small and coherent — despond, despondent, despondency, despondently — and all of them say the same thing in different grammatical shapes: hope has been given up, solemnly, as a thing promised away. A word that began with wine poured to the gods ends by describing a girl who has stopped believing in the future — a long road from libation to resignation, travelled entirely through the Latin idea of the pledged word.
