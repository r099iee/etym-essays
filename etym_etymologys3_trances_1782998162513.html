TRANCES: FROM 'I GO ACROSS' TO THE MIND'S OWN PASSAGE The noun trance is a disguised Latin verb. Its history begins with one of the oldest and most fundamental roots of Proto-Indo-European, *h₁éy-, 'to go' — the same root that gave Latin īre, Greek eîmi 'I go', Sanskrit éti 'he goes', Lithuanian eĩti 'to go', and, astonishingly, the old English preterite ēode 'went'. In Latin this tiny verb proved extraordinarily productive: the Romans prefixed it with adverbs and prepositions to build an entire vocabulary of going — exīre 'to go out', redīre 'to go back', transīre 'to go across', obīre 'to go to meet, to die'.
  • The root and its reflexes: *h₁éy- shows up across the family with remarkable consistency — Latin eō 'I go', Greek eîmi, Sanskrit éti, Lithuanian eĩti; its English reflex survives only in the preterite wend/went, which took over the past tense of go. The root is so old that its 'go' meaning is agreed in every branch.
  • The prefixed verb: trans- + īre = transīre, 'to go across, pass over', and from it the noun trānsitus, 'a going across, a passage'. It is this transparently physical idea — a crossing, a passing over — that the whole modern word rests on.
When the Romance languages took over the verb, Latin transīre became Old French transir, and here the meaning took a dark turn. Transir came to mean 'to pass away, to die', and also 'to be chilled, benumbed', because death and intense fear were experienced as the body's passage out of life and as a freezing of the blood. The Old French noun transe was formed from this verb to name the event itself: the passage from life to death, the death-agony, the swoon, the crisis of dread. A word that began as 'I go across' became, within a few centuries, the name of the moment of dying.
  • Semantic darkening: the shift 'go across' → 'die' is a general European figure (Latin obīre, 'to go to meet' one's death; French trépasser, 'to pass beyond'); dying is the final crossing, and fear freezes the body like cold. Old French transir held both the death-sense and the chill-sense, and transi as an adjective meant 'dead, numb, chilled'.
  • The noun: transe (also transe in Old French texts) named the swoon, the fit, the agony — the visible event of the body being carried out of itself.
Middle English borrowed the Old French noun as traunce in the fourteenth century, and for its first centuries in English the word meant a swoon, a fit, a state of extreme fear, or a near-death suspension of the senses. Chaucer uses the word of characters who faint or are stunned; the Wycliffite Bible and early religious prose use it of prophetic vision. The modern spelling trance settled in the sixteenth century.
  • The softening: in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, as the language of mysticism, Neoplatonism, and enthusiasm spread, trance began to shed its terror. The 'trance' of the mystic — the state in which the soul leaves the body to meet God (a concept translated from the Greek ékstasis, 'standing outside oneself') — turned the old swoon of dread into a rapture of absorption. Acts 10:10 in the King James Bible, 'he fell into a trance', shows the prophetic sense in full dignity.
  • Wordsworth's plural (1805): The Prelude's 'Trances of thought' is the endpoint of this softening: the word has been emptied of fear and filled with contemplative power. The plural makes the states recurrent visitations, and the old resonance of the body's passage now describes the mind passing out of ordinary time.
The nineteenth century gave trance a new technical life. The rise of mesmerism and hypnotism made the 'hypnotic trance' a clinical and theatrical fact — a state artificially induced, used in surgery before anaesthetics and on the music-hall stage. The medical sense of apparent death ('death trance', catalepsy) kept the ancient fear alive in forensic folklore.
  • The modern genre: in the early 1990s the name migrated again — to 'trance music', the electronic genre of hypnotic, pulsing synth lines and four-on-the-floor beats, so named precisely because it aims to induce a trance-like state in dancers. The word has come full circle from the death-passage to the dance floor, while never quite losing its undertone of being carried out of oneself.
  • Everyday residue: in ordinary modern English, to be 'in a trance' or 'trance-like' means to be rapt and oblivious — absorbed enough to be cut off from the world — which is, in the end, exactly what Wordsworth's 'trances of thought' describe.
