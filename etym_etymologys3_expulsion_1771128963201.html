The story of expulsion is a study in how a verb of brute physical force became the legal, medical, and political vocabulary of civilised life — without ever losing its original violence. It begins with a Proto-Indo-European root, *pel-, whose meaning was entirely muscular: to drive, push, strike, thrust. This was a root of hands and herds and weapons, of the shove that moved a stone and the blow that drove an enemy back. When the root entered Latin as the verb pellere, 'to drive, push, strike,' it became one of the most productive engines of meaning the language possessed, because Latin loved to modify a simple verb of motion by bolting prefixes onto it. Pellere gave the language appellare (drive to, call upon, address — whence 'appeal'), compellere (drive together, compel), propellere (drive forward, propel), repellere (drive back, repel), and the beating, striking noun pulsus, which became the beating of the heart itself — modern 'pulse' and 'pulsate.' Every English verb ending in -pel, from compel to expel, is a survivor of this Roman habit. The particular compound that concerns us is expellere, formed with the prefix ex-, 'out': to drive out, to thrust beyond a boundary. The Romans used it with a wonderfully practical sweep — to drive out an enemy, to expel a magistrate, to expel breath, to expel the noxious humours of the body. From the participle stem came the noun expulsiō, 'a driving out,' and it is worth noticing that in Latin, as in English later, the word was equally at home in the battlefield and the sickroom. The physical and the institutional were never separated; expulsion was always the same act, performed at different boundaries. When the Roman physicians and the Roman lawyers said 'drive out,' they meant the same verb, and that double inheritance travelled with the word into the modern languages. The word reached English by the standard medieval route: Latin expulsiō → Old French expulsion → Middle English expulsioun, first recorded in the 14th century. Its earliest English life was as much medical as anything else. In the medicine of the humours, expulsion was a therapeutic virtue: the body expelled its waste and its evil matter, and a good purge assisted the expulsion of the offending humour. It is this world the card's sentence inhabits — 'the sudden and violent ______ of the evil humour left the patient weak but finally on the path to recovery' — where expulsion is at once a medical event and a moral one, the body casting out its corruption in a decisive, even dramatic, crisis. The same centuries saw the word in theology: the expulsion from Eden, the casting out of demons, the exclusion of the sinner from the community of the faithful. In both registers expulsion carried the idea of purification through ejection — the community, like the body, is restored by driving out what has gone wrong. As the institutions of the modern state grew, so did the word's public career. Expulsion from parliament, from the university, from the party became the formal language of discipline; the expulsion of diplomats and of foreign populations became the language of international politics; the expulsion of waste gases and spent cartridges became the language of the machine. In every case the old force of the Latin verb is still faintly audible: something is being driven out, across a boundary, by a power that will not be argued with. The word has kept its kinetic charge through six centuries of English, and no synonym quite equals it because none of them — not ejection, not dismissal, not eviction — carries both the physical violence of the push and the finality of the judgment. Expulsion is the word for the act that can never be undone, and it owes that character to a root that meant nothing grander than to push.
