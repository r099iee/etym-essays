- Late 1590s–1640s: Renaissance Latinate Classical Borrowing - Adopted into Early Modern English directly from Latin *squalidus* to denote physical roughness, scale-like crustiness, and neglected, unkempt bodily appearance.
- 1650s–1780s: Poetic & Natural Desolation - Extended in neoclassical literature to describe wild, uncultivated, desolate landscapes, arid wastes, and gloomy, neglected ruins.
- 1800s–1880s: The Industrial Slum & Victorian Public Health - Connotation shifted decisively toward urban misery as journalists, novelists (like Dickens), and public health inspectors used it to describe the foul, overcrowded tenements of industrial cities.
- Late 19th–20th Century: Lexicographical Benchmark for Extreme Filth - Formally classified in dictionaries as an essential synonym of 'dirty', 'filthy', and 'foul', defining the exact intersection of poverty and severe environmental grime.
- Late 20th–21st Century: Metaphoric Expansion to Moral & Political Degeneracy - Frequently deployed in political commentary, investigative journalism, and legal discourse to condemn corrupt scandals, sleazy backroom intrigues, and ethical decay.
