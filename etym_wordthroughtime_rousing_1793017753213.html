- Late 15th c. — the falconer's word: rouse first describes a hawk shaking and ruffling its feathers, a specialist term of hawking manuals with no wider meaning yet.
- 16th c. — hunters and soldiers borrow it: the stag rouses from its lair, troops are roused to arms; Shakespeare's age makes it the verb of sudden bestirring, from sleep or from hiding.
- Late 16th c. — the adjective is born: rousing begins describing whatever wakes and stirs — rousing music, rousing strains — carrying the falcon's shiver into the concert hall.
- 17th–18th c. — sermons and reveilles: preachers rouse sleepy consciences and officers rouse sleeping crews, so the word gathers a moral and disciplinary colour — to rouse is to call back to duty.
- 19th c. — the age of oratory and anthems: political meetings, revival hymns, and concert critics make rousing the standard praise for speeches and choruses that lift whole halls to their feet.
- Today — praise for collective energy: overwhelmingly the warm adjective of crowds, finales, welcomes, and victories — with the literal waking sense kept alive in alarm calls, headlines, and the verb beneath it.
