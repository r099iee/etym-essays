Unbeknown means "without the knowledge or awareness of (someone)": not known, not suspected, kept from the understanding of a particular person or group. In the literary context of this note, from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the narrator reveals that in the tunnels and caves the goblins have made for themselves "there are other things living unbeknown to them that have sneaked in from outside to lie up in the dark." The word's nuance here is quietly sinister and ironic: it places the reader inside the goblins' ignorance while hinting that these intruders — older than the goblins themselves, slipped in from the outside world — inhabit the darkness wholly without their hosts' knowledge. The tone is that of an omniscient storyteller confiding a secret to his audience, generating dramatic irony and a pervasive sense of hidden, uninvited life stirring in the corners of the underground realm.

Grammar and syntax. Unbeknown functions as a predicative adjective (and adverbially) in the fixed construction unbeknown to + person, roughly equivalent to "without X knowing." It does not appear before a noun as an attributive — one does not say "an unbeknown visitor" — but always in the pattern "unbeknown to him/her/us/them," where the to-phrase names the party left in ignorance. This fixed, preposition-governed grammar is the word's defining feature and separates it from the free-standing adjective unknown.

Secondary uses and variants.
  • The variant unbeknownst: from the mid-19th century (c. 1848) an excrescent -st was added on the model of amongst and against, giving unbeknownst; the two forms coexist, with unbeknownst the commoner in American English and unbeknown the older and more conservative form.
  • Register: the word is archaic and dialectal in flavour, typical of older English and of storytelling; Tolkien, a professional philologist steeped in older English, deliberately employs it to keep his narration close to the cadence of folk-tale.
  • Semantic scope: unlike unknown (anything not known, by anyone), unbeknown is always relative and bounded — it marks a specific party kept in the dark, whether deliberately or accidentally.

Formation. The word is transparently built: the privative prefix un- + the past participle beknown "known, acknowledged," from the obsolete verb beknow (Old English becnāwan), itself be- (intensifier) + know. The modern sense therefore rests on an old verb that meant "to recognize, to acknowledge," and the negative participle preserves that meaning: what is unbeknown to someone has not been recognized by them at all.

Nuance in the present sentence. Two details make the word's use here specially fine. First, it is paired with the clause "that have sneaked in from outside," so that unbeknown to them stands in deliberate tension with the deliberate stealth of the intruders: the creatures themselves act with purpose (they sneaked), yet their hosts remain wholly unaware of them. Second, the word is placed in the narrator's own voice, not in a character's, which transforms what might be plain reportage into confidential disclosure: the reader is admitted to a secret the goblins do not possess. The result is a sentence that does three things at once — it advances the plot, deepens the atmosphere of hidden life, and establishes the narrator as a quiet, knowing presence standing above the ignorance of the underground dwellers. This is the word's characteristic literary function wherever it appears: it manufactures dramatic irony out of a mere prepositional phrase.
