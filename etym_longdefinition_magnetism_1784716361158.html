MAGNETISM — noun. Core meaning: the invisible force of attraction and repulsion exerted by magnets and magnetic materials; and, by extension, the intangible, quasi-physical force of personal attraction exercised by one individual over others. In its figurative sense — the one active in the present context — magnetism is a compelling presence, eloquence, and self-command that draws an audience toward the speaker almost against its will.

In the parliamentary context given — "He was not a conventional success in the House; as a speaker he lacked magnetism" — the word carries its full figurative weight. The subject is not being accused of weak argument or faulty logic; he is being charged with a failure of presence. In the House of Commons, where reputations are made by the ear, a speaker can be procedurally sound, well-informed, and industrious — a "conventional success" in every outward respect — yet still fail to move the chamber because he lacks the personal magnetism that turns a competent address into an electrifying performance. The nuance is precisely the distinction between doing one's parliamentary duty and commanding the House: magnetism is that invisible current of personality that makes men lean forward in their seats.

Full polysemy. The word spans a cluster of related senses, each traceable to the lodestone's power of attracting iron:

  • Physics (the original scientific sense): the phenomenon of attraction and repulsion exerted by magnets, electric currents, and magnetic materials; the force described by magnetic fields. Subfields include electromagnetism, ferromagnetism, paramagnetism, and diamagnetism.
  • Personal / charismatic attraction (the figurative sense of this context): an inexplicable, quasi-physical power of attraction that a person exercises over others — the "magnetic personality," the speaker who draws a crowd. This is the dominant modern figurative use.
  • Animal magnetism (historical): the 18th-century theory of Franz Mesmer that living bodies emit an invisible fluid capable of influencing others; the theory underlying mesmerism and, later, hypnotism. Now historical or pseudoscientific.
  • Earth and rock magnetism (geophysics): the magnetic field of the Earth (geomagnetism); the record of past fields frozen in rocks (paleomagnetism, remanent magnetism); the magnetic striping of the ocean floor used as evidence for plate tectonics.
  • Technology and materials science: magnetism in engineering — magnetic recording, magnetic storage, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetostriction, magnetic levitation; technical jargon built directly on the physics sense.
  • Organisational and social metaphor: "magnetic leadership," the magnetism of a place or a cause — any centre of gravity that pulls people, money, or attention toward it; a broad modern extension of the personal sense.
  • Literary and rhetorical use: the magnetism of a story or an image, i.e. its compelling power to draw and hold attention; a metaphoric shift from persons to things.

Semantic bridges. The history runs from the mineral to the human. The ancient Greeks observed that a dark iron ore found near the district of Magnesia attracted iron; the stone was called the "Magnesian stone," and by the time of Plato it was already a metaphor for the transmission of inspiration from poet to audience. The literal scientific sense was established in the seventeenth century with the study of the lodestone. In the late eighteenth century Mesmer's theory of "animal magnetism" carried the word from the laboratory into medicine and the parlour, describing an invisible influence of one person upon another. From there it was a short step to the nineteenth-century phrase "personal magnetism" — the force of attraction that a great speaker or leader exercises over a crowd. The physical and the figurative senses have coexisted ever since, and the metaphor is now so embedded that the word is often used with no thought of iron filings.

Register and usage notes.

  • Physics/technical: magnetism is the precise scientific term for magnetic force; the figurative sense is excluded from formal scientific writing.
  • Figurative/colloquial: magnetic, magnetically, "a magnetic personality," "she radiates magnetism" — of presence and attraction.
  • Historical: animal magnetism, mesmerism — dated pseudoscience.
  • Distinction: magnetism implies a forceful "pull" of presence; charisma implies a halo of grace or aura.
