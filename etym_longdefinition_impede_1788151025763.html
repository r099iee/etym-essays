impede (transitive verb) — to hinder, obstruct, or delay the progress, movement, or achievement of something or someone; to get in the way. The core image of the word is literal and physical: Latin impedīre means "to shackle, to entangle, to catch by the feet," literally "to put something on the feet" (in- "in, on" + pēs, pedis "foot"). An impeded army is an army whose feet are caught — in mud, in fetters, in its own baggage. From that concrete picture the verb generalizes to every kind of obstruction, whether material ("fallen trees impeded the rescue vehicles"), temporal ("heavy traffic impeded the journey"), or abstract ("a culture of fear impedes innovation").

Primary sense — to slow or block the course of something. Impede takes as its object either the thing in motion ("impede the flow of water") or the agents at work ("impede the investigators"). It differs from "stop" in degree: to impede is almost always to hinder or delay, not necessarily to bring to a halt — the obstacle slows the progress without extinguishing it. This is the dominant modern register: formal, journalistic, legal, and administrative prose ("parliamentary delay impeded the reform"), where the word is valued for its precision and its neutrality of tone.

Secondary senses and extensions.

  • Legal and administrative use. In law and public administration, impede denotes actions that obstruct the operation of justice, inquiry, or process: "attempting to impede a federal investigation," "practices that impede competition." The bridge from the physical sense is transparent — justice, like a traveler, can be tripped and slowed — and the word functions as a precise, chargeable verb: obstructing justice while a proceeding is pending is a defined offense.
  • Physiological and medical use. Older medical and physiological prose used impede freely of the body ("impede digestion," "impeded circulation," "a growth impeding the airway"). The usage survives in scientific and clinical writing about flow, conduction, and movement, and it retains the root metaphor of the body as a system of passages that can be blocked.
  • Technical transfer into electronics. The related noun impedance — coined from the same verb via French impédance by Oliver Heaviside in 1886 — names a circuit's opposition to alternating current. From that coinage, "impede" itself is borrowed back into engineering prose for "oppose the flow of" (a capacitor impedes current at low frequencies). This is a genuine modern science branch of the family, and it preserves the ancient image of blocking the flow.
  • Figurative and psychological use. "Impede one's own recovery," "impede understanding," "fear impedes learning": the verb transfers freely to mental, emotional, and social processes, always with the sense of an obstacle slowing movement toward a goal rather than destroying it.

Semantic reasoning — the historical bridge. The entire family — impede, impediment, impedimenta, impedance — is built on the foot. Roman impedimenta were literally the baggage that hobbled an army's march; an impedimentum was anything that encumbered. Every modern abstract use is a metaphor of that same march: whatever slows the procession of goods, thought, justice, or electrical signal is an obstacle tied to the feet. One image explains nearly everything — from the wedding liturgy's "cause or just impediment" to the engineer's impedance matching.

Grammar and distribution. Always transitive ("impede + object"); passive use ("the rescue was impeded by weather") is idiomatic and common; stress on the second syllable /ɪmˈpiːd/. Register: formal-to-neutral; in ordinary conversation English prefers "hinder," "hold up," "slow down," or "get in the way." Antonyms: facilitate, expedite, promote, assist, advance, further, speed. Related words: impediment ("a hindrance; historically a legal bar," e.g., to marriage); unimpeded ("unobstructed, free-flowing"); impedimenta ("camp baggage, encumbrances"). The same Latin ped- "foot" underlies pedestrian, pedal, expedition ("unfettered feet" speeding forward), and impede's natural opposite, expedite.
