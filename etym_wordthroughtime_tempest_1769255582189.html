- 13th century, Middle English tempeste: the word enters English from Anglo-Norman already meaning a violent storm - it arrives as the grand, bookish term for weather in chivalric romance and scripture, far above the plain speech of everyday skies.
- 1600s: Shakespeare's The Tempest (1611) fixes the word's magical and dramatic halo - the tempest becomes the storm that carries meaning, at once meteorological and theatrical, an upheaval with a plot of its own.
- 1700s-1800s: the figurative life widens - a tempest of anger, passion, or applause, 'tempest-tossed' ships and souls, and Emma Lazarus's 'homeless, tempest-tost' (1883), where the storm stands for exile and the ordeal of arrival.
- Contemporary: the idiom 'a tempest in a teapot' (American) or 'a storm in a teacup' (British) ironizes the word for a great fuss over nothing, while in ordinary speech tempest retreats to the elevated, literary, and metaphorical — the plain default is now simply 'storm.'
