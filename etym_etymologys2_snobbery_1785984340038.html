Old Norse snápr —
dullard, idiot, charlatan
(9th–13th centuries; the traditional but SPECULATIVE etymon of snob)

↓

English dialect snob —
shoemaker; cobbler; cobbler’s apprentice
(18th century, especially at Cambridge)

↓

Cambridge slang snob —
townsman; a non-university man; one of the lower classes
(late 18th – early 19th century)

↓

English snob —
a person who vulgarly admires or apes the upper classes
(early 19th century; popularized by Thackeray, The Book of Snobs, 1848)

↓

English snobbery —
the behaviour, attitude, or practice of a snob
(snob + suffix -ery; attested from the 1840s)

↓

SNOBBERY —
the present word

Notes on debate: The origin of snob is genuinely uncertain. The Old Norse snápr derivation is often repeated but speculative; alternative theories include a shortening of Latin s.nob. (“sine nobilitate”, without nobility) supposedly written at Eton, which is folklore, and links to dialect verbs of sneering. The true earliest etymology is unknown, and scholars say so explicitly.
