Tramp in this line is a noun of sound and motion: the heavy, rhythmic tread of feet, footfalls imagined as doom's own march. It is not yet the vagrant person, not the cargo ship, not the verb — it is the sound of weight coming. The dwarves, they heard the tramp of doom turns auditory trampling into prophecy: what approaches is heard as a weight on the ground before it is seen, and that weight is named doom. The monosyllable itself thuds — the word's sound is its meaning, and it has been since the medieval English verb “to tread heavily” was coined on the model of the footfall it describes. Primary senses of tramp (noun)
  • The sound of heavy, rhythmic footsteps — “the tramp of soldiers’ feet”; “the tramp of doom”; the deep, regular, ground-shaking cadence of marching or trampling.
  • A long, hard walk or tramping expedition — “a tramp over the moors”; a wearying journey on foot.
  • A person who travels on foot and lives as a wanderer or vagrant — “tramp” in the social sense, now dated and often offensive.
  • A tramp steamer — a cargo ship that sails irregular routes wherever cargo offers, named for wandering like a foot-traveller.
Polysemy and secondary modern meanings
  • Vagrant: from the 17th–18th centuries, “a tramp” named the wandering poor, a person who walked the roads in search of work or charity; the sense hardened socially and is now widely considered a slur.
  • Tramp steamer / tramp shipping: a merchant vessel that operates without a fixed schedule or route, “wandering” where trade takes it — the walking image transferred to ships in the 19th century.
  • To tramp (verb): to walk heavily or wearily, to tread, to trample — “to tramp the streets”; “to tramp through the mud.”
  • Trampoline: a French invention derived from trampolino, a springboard, itself from trampolo “stilt” and ultimately related to the same heavy-treading verb-family; the bounce is a distant, airborne descendant of the heavy tread.
  • Trample: the frequentative form of tramp, meaning to tread repeatedly and crush underfoot — the same verb intensified.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge of the senses The word begins as pure sound-symbolism: Germanic speakers created a verb for heavy, rhythmic stepping directly out of the sound of heavy feet — the imitative family that also gave trample, trampoline, and the German trampen. The noun “the tramp” (the sound or act of heavy treading) came first and stays central: doom itself is heard walking. From the repeated heavy walk came the long hard journey, and from the long hard journey came the human traveller — the tramp as vagrant — and from the wanderer came the wandering ship. Every modern sense, from the social slur to the tramp steamer, is the ancient heavy footfall seen through a different lens: the tread, the walker, the wanderer, and the wanderer's ship. In the song, the oldest sense alone matters: the tramp of doom is doom itself walking with weight. How tramp differs from its near-synonyms
  • Tread — the general act and sound of stepping; neutral and broad, where tramp is heavy, rhythmic, and massed.
  • Stamp — a single forceful impact of the foot; one blow, where tramp is a repeated cadence.
  • March — ordered military stepping; disciplined and organized, where tramp is massed weight without the parade-ground order.
  • Footfall — any sound of a foot landing; lighter and neutral, singular or slight, where tramp is heavy, repeated, and ominous.
  • Trudge — slow, weary walking; stresses effort and fatigue, where tramp stresses weight and rhythm.
  • Stomp — heavy, angry stepping, often protest or dance; more deliberate and theatrical than the natural heavy tread of tramp.
In the card's context The line uses TRAMP as a noun of approach: the mountain smokes beneath the moon, and the dwarves hear — not see — doom coming, its weight already in the ground. The word's imitative origin makes the sound real, and its long history of heavy, rhythmic treading makes the prophecy physical. Keep the footfall in mind, not the vagrant and not the ship: the tramp of doom is doom walking, and English gave that sound a word made of the sound itself.
