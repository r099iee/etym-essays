The evolution of baccarat is unusually short, well-documented and honestly mysterious — a 19th-century aristocratic invention whose name has no secure ancestor. The Centre National de Ressources Textuelles et Lexicales (CNRTL) states it plainly: mot probablement emprunté au provençal bacarra, jeu de cartes originaire du midi de la France (Mistral); origine inconnue. Etymonline concurs: from French baccara (19th c.), of unknown origin; Baccarat the town noted for glass-making is a homonym. This candour matters: many popular books claim Italian baccarà "zero" (because tens and face cards count zero) or 15th-century Italian origin, but the game is not reliably recorded before Van-Tenac's Album des jeux (1847, pp.84–97), and French antedates Italian in the record, making an Italian loan improbable (Panzini is 1863–1939, Battaglia cites later).

What is secure is the Provençal channel. Frédéric Mistral's Tresor dóu Felibrige records Provençal bacarra as a card game originating in the south of France. French dictionaries attribute introduction to the Italian wars period as legend, but the French word itself is only attested in the 19th century: 1851 (CNRTL ex.1) and Van-Tenac 1847 for the game baccarat banque. Whether Provençal bacarra continues an older Occitan word, a nickname, or a table-cry is unknown; no Latin or Germanic etymon has been found. The final -t in baccarat versus baccara is orthographic: French often adds mute -t (cf. gat / gât), and English fixed the -t form after 1848.

Game evolution fills the lexical gap. Scholars (Ethier & Lee 2015, Parlett 1991, Dormoy 1873, Bertrand 1889, Borel 1924) reconstruct:

  • Baccarat banque (1847): three-person zero-sum parlor game, banker against two punters. Likely earliest form, played exclusively in aristocratic circles, explaining late attestation.
  • Baccarat chemin de fer / tournant (1870s–1880): simplified two-person zero-sum game, rotating bank, called railway by 1880 (Badoureau 1881). Studied mathematically by Dormoy, Bertrand and Borel — the last launching game theory.
  • Club commission (pre-1907): five per cent on Banker wins to fund clubs before casino legalisation in 1907, creating a non-zero-sum game.
  • Casino chemin de fer (1920s–40s): Banker strategy gradually constrained to fixed draws; Scarne 1949 shows American illegal casinos already fixing.
  • Punto banco (1940s–50s Havana, Vegas 1959, Macau 1962): house-banked, no skill, bets on Player or Banker, Player forced to draw on 5 (Tommy Renzoni at Capri c.1956). Now 90%+ of Macau revenue — US$41bn in 2013.

Linguistically the word travelled with the table: French baccara → English baccarat 1848 → worldwide casino English. Pronunciation settled as /ˈbækərɑː/ ~ /ˌbækəˈrɑː/ in RP, [bakaʁa] in French. A secondary argot sense, baccara "bankruptcy, failure" (Simonin 1935, Voilà taxi!), derives from losing at the table — to go baccarat is to go bust. The crystal sense — Baccarat glass from the Meurthe-et-Moselle town (founded 1764) — is a pure homonym, though casinos happily use Baccarat chandeliers above baccarat tables. Ian Fleming's Casino Royale (1953) cemented the word's tuxedo aura: Bond plays chemin de fer, not poker. Aunt Dahlia playing baccarat at Cannes thus signals exactly the right milieu: rich, reckless, Riviera, slightly disreputable — the perfect Wodehouse vice.
