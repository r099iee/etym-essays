Ochils — short for the Ochil Hills — is a crescent range of rounded volcanic hills in lowland Scotland, sweeping some twenty-five miles from the upper Forth near Stirling north-east through Clackmannanshire and Perthshire to the Tay near Perth. In the source passage — Ruskin in Praeterita remembering childhood summers spent every other year near Perth, reading Scott's Abbot at Kinross and The Monastery in Glen Farg, and believing that Scott's White Lady had lived by the streamlet in that glen of the Ochils — the word is a child's romantic geography: the dark blue hills seen from the post-chaise windows, already peopled by monks, ladies, and queens. The plural with -s is essential; Scots say the Ochils as the English say the Downs or the Fells, a collective landscape, not one peak.

Physically the Ochils are a fault-block escarpment. Their bold south-facing scarp rises abruptly 400-700 metres above the Hillfoots villages — Menstrie, Alva, Tillicoultry, Dollar, Muckhart — to a rolling plateau whose highest points are Ben Cleuch (721 m), Andrew Gannel Hill, and King's Seat. The rock is Lower Devonian andesite and basalt lavas and tuffs of the Ochil Volcanic Formation (c. 415-400 million years old), tilted north and cut by the great Ochil Fault. Glaciation smoothed the tops into whalebacks, dumped drumlins in the carse below, and left corries such as Alva Glen and Dollar Glen as deep wooded gashes — the very glens of the Ochils where Ruskin placed his streamlet. Mill lades once took hill water to power wool, paper, and whisky; silver was mined at Alva in the 18th century; the famous Dollar Glen is now National Trust woodland.

  • Core geographical sense: the hill range itself: e.g. a walk in the Ochils, snow on the Ochils, the glen of the Ochils. Always plural, always definite (the Ochils), always capitalised. Ordnance Survey prints Ochil Hills; common speech shortens to the Ochils. The bridge to all other senses is metonymy: the hills stand for everything on and around them.
  • Geological sense: Ochil-type volcanics: geologists speak of Ochil lavas, the Ochil Fault, the Ochil Volcanic Formation. Here Ochil (singular attributive) is a type locality: andesitic Old Red Sandstone volcanism studied worldwide. E.g. an Ochil andesite sill. The semantic shift is place-to-rock, standard in stratigraphy.
  • Literary-romantic sense (source sense): Scott's Border imagination relocated — The Monastery is set in Melrose, not Glen Farg, and its White Lady of Avenel belongs to the Tweed, yet the child Ruskin confidently moves her to that glen of the Ochils. The Ochils thus mean haunted, covenanting, picturesque Scotland: mist, burns, ruined towers, and spirits. Victorian tourists bought the same vision in guidebooks and engravings.
  • Historical-economic sense: Hillfoots industry: the Ochils imply mills, mines, and sheep; e.g. Ochils wool, Ochils whisky (Alva, Glenochil distillery), Tillicoultry quarries. Hill water is soft and pure, ideal for textiles and distilling; hill pasture fed Blackface sheep; hill burns drove two hundred mills by 1800. To know the Ochils economically is to know Lowland industrial Scotland watered from above.
  • Administrative and modern recreational sense: Ochils survives in ward, school, and club names — Ochils ward (Clackmannanshire), Ochils Mountaineering Club, Ochils Landscape Partnership — and as a walking, paragliding, and conservation area (Site of Special Scientific Interest, ravens, peregrines, upland hay meadows). E.g. the Ochils Traverse, Ochils hill race. The connotation is accessible wildness: hills a bus-ride from Glasgow and Edinburgh, loved, path-worn, and protected.
  • Grammatical note: plural-only collective: one says an Ochil glen or an Ochil hill but never an Ochil for a peak; the range has named summits but no single Ochil. This mirrors the Cheviots, the Grampians, the Pentlands. Outsiders who write Ochil for the whole range betray themselves at once.

Connotation is affectionate and homely rather than sublime. The Highlands awe; the Ochils console — blue, near, walkable, storied, visible from motorway and railway as the green wall above the Forth carse. Ruskin, who would later moralise the Alps, first learned hills as a Lowland child: high roads and cross-roads seen through post-chaise windows, Perth summers, Scott in hand, and the Ochils holding a White Lady by every streamlet. Modern geology and rambling guides keep the same tenderness under science: a fault-scarp that is also a fairy glen.
