lunacy is a noun formed in English in the 16th century from lunatic plus the suffix -cy. Its original meaning is “the condition of being a lunatic”: insanity, unsoundness of mind, especially as recognised by law. In the source sentence — “the mind would be perpetually hesitant, and would soon take refuge in lunacy” — the sense is the older, strong one: a collapse into madness, imagined as a place of refuge from unbearable hesitation. The word is deliberately dramatic and slightly archaic, and its moon-derived history is part of the effect.

The core senses

  • Medical-historical: insanity, mental derangement, especially of a kind supposed to be intermittent. This is the dominant sense from the 16th to the 19th century and is now obsolete in clinical use.
  • Legal: unsoundness of mind sufficient to remove a person's legal capacity or responsibility. A commission of lunacy could inquire into a person's sanity; a lunacy inquisition could place their property under protection.
  • Figurative: extreme folly, reckless irrationality, behaviour that seems insane. “It would be lunacy to drive in this weather.” This is now the ordinary sense in journalism and conversation.

Law and institutions

English law used lunacy as a technical category for centuries. The Lunacy Act 1845 created the Commissioners in Lunacy and a national system of licensed asylums; the word “lunatic” appeared in statutes governing detention, property and marriage, and “lunacy” named the jurisdiction exercised by the Court of Chancery over persons of unsound mind. The terminology was gradually replaced in the 20th century by “mental illness,” “mental disorder” and “lack of capacity”: the Mental Treatment Act 1930 allowed voluntary admission and discouraged the word “lunatic,” and the Mental Health Act 1959 completed the change in England and Wales. Traces remain in older statutes and in the idiom lunatic fringe, coined by Theodore Roosevelt in 1913 for the extremists at the edge of a political movement.

Why the moon?

The word inherits an ancient belief that the moon governs madness, epilepsy and sleepwalking. Greek had selēniazesthai, “to be moon-struck,” used in the New Testament of a boy suffering convulsions; the Latin Bible calls such sufferers lunatici. Medieval and early modern medicine repeated the idea that the brain, being moist, was especially sensitive to lunar phases, and that a “lunatic” was mad at the full moon and lucid at the wane. The theory is false as a general account of mental illness, but it left its mark on the vocabulary of every European language: French lunatique, Italian lunatico, Spanish lunático.

The figurative shift

Once madness became a medical condition rather than a moral or lunar visitation, “lunacy” lost its clinical authority and was free to become a metaphor. The shift follows a familiar pattern: a technical term for a feared condition becomes a loose term of abuse for any behaviour the speaker thinks absurd. “The lunacy of war,” “financial lunacy,” “sheer lunacy” — the word now judges actions, not people, and usually implies that the course proposed is not merely unwise but incomprehensible.

Register and sensitivity

Applied to a person with a mental illness, lunacy and lunatic are now widely regarded as offensive, and mental-health style guides advise against them. Applied to an idea, a policy or a situation, they remain idiomatic and forceful, though even there the metaphor depends on a stigmatised image of madness. The source sentence uses the word of a hypothetical mind, not of a real patient, and its tone is literary rather than clinical.

Grammar and collocations

Lunacy is normally uncountable: lunacy, not lunacies, though the plural occurs in older legal writing for separate findings. It takes the prepositions of and in: “the lunacy of the scheme,” “a moment of lunacy,” “take refuge in lunacy.” Common intensifiers are sheer, pure, utter and downright. The adjective is lunatic, the adverb lunatically (rare), and the derived noun lunatic for the person.

Literary company

Shakespeare's line in A Midsummer Night's Dream — “The lunatic, the lover and the poet / Are of imagination all compact” — fixes the older adjective in the English imagination, grouping the madman with the lover and the artist as people whose minds are not governed by ordinary reason. The noun lunacy belongs to the same semantic field but is colder and more institutional: it names the condition rather than the person, which is why it could serve as a legal instrument.

Related modern vocabulary

Lunar retains the neutral astronomical sense; lunation is a synodic month; lunatic survives mainly in fixed phrases. The word family thus splits into a calm scientific branch and a heated figurative branch, with lunacy firmly in the second.

Why the word still works in the source

The sentence sets routine against lunacy as two ways for a mind to move: one along prepared grooves, the other into a shapeless refuge. “Lunacy” supplies the archaic, absolute alternative — not mere confusion but a settled condition of unreason — and its legal and asylum history gives the sentence a faint institutional chill that “madness” alone would not.
