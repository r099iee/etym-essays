cargoes is the plural of cargo, a noun denoting the goods, merchandise, or freight transported by a ship, aircraft, train, or other vehicle — in practice most often the commercial load of a vessel or aircraft. As a plural it refers to multiple loads or shipments: "the fleet returned with cargoes of silk and spice."

Primary meaning: freight transported for trade.

  • Historically and fundamentally, a cargo is the load carried by a merchant ship: bales, barrels, crates, bullion, and bulk commodities.
  • In modern logistics the word has been generalised to any transported goods — air cargo, rail cargo, road-haulage cargo — but shipping remains the conceptual home of the term.
  • Cargoes (plural) emphasises plurality of loads: several ships' loads, several consignments, or mixed varieties of goods ("cargoes of grain and timber").
  • The singular is both countable ("a cargo of wool") and uncountable/collective ("the plane carried cargo, not passengers").

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Bulk cargo: unpackaged commodities (coal, ore, grain) loaded loose into a ship's hold.
  • General cargo: packaged goods — crates, drums, bales, containers — stowed individually.
  • Cargo ship / cargo vessel / cargo liner: a vessel built to carry freight rather than passengers.
  • Cargo bay / cargo hold / cargo net / cargo manifest: the spaces, equipment, and paperwork of freight-handling on ships and aircraft.
  • Cargo cult (anthropology): a Melanesian religious movement whose adherents expected manufactured goods ("cargo") to arrive by ancestral or spirit intervention — a striking transfer of the shipping term to an ethnographic concept.
  • Figurative: "a cargo of troubles" or "a cargo of worries" (rare, literary) — a burden, echoing the older Spanish sense of a "charge" or load to be carried.

Semantic history and bridge.

  • English took cargo from Spanish in the 1650s, through seafaring trade with Iberia and the New World.
  • Spanish cargo means "burden, load" (from cargar "to load," from Late Latin carricare "to load a cart"); the English borrowing narrowed the general "load/burden" to a commercial shipload.
  • Spanish cargo also means "office, duty, charge" (a responsibility one "carries"), but that abstract sense did not transfer to English, which kept cargo purely concrete.
  • The plural cargoes (with -oes) follows the pattern for nouns in consonant + o that have become fully naturalised: echoes, heroes, potatoes, tomatoes, torpedoes.

Grammar and usage notes.

  • Countable plural cargoes; occasional plural "cargos" appears in logistics jargon but the traditional and standard form is cargoes.
  • Uncountable collective "cargo" coexists: "load/cargo space," "to carry cargo."
  • Common collocations: "cargoes of X," "ships' cargoes," "precious cargoes," "cargo holds."
  • Register: standard and neutral in commerce, shipping, aviation, and logistics; in casual speech "loads," "shipments," "freight," and "goods" compete, while "cargo" is preferred in professional and maritime contexts.

The word in The Hobbit.

  • Tolkien's Master of Lake-town gives "his mind to trade and tolls, to cargoes and gold" — the plural heaps shiploads of goods together with mercantile wealth, crystallising commerce in a single noun.
  • Lake-town is built on trade with the Elves and the lake; "cargoes" are precisely the shiploads of wood and food that sustain it.
