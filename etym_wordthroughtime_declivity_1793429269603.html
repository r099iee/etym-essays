- Early 17th c. — Renaissance natural philosophy: borrowed into English from French déclivité and Latin dēclīvitās during the great expansion of scientific vocabulary, used by early topographers and translators to describe terrain descents.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment travel narratives & hydrography: adopted by Jonathan Swift and marine chroniclers to record coastal gradients, soundings, and picturesque topography with mathematical detachment.
- Late 18th–19th c. — Romantic landscape aesthetics & geology: celebrated by picturesque theorists and Victorian geologists (such as Charles Lyell) to describe valleys, moraines, and eroded geological strata.
- 19th c. — Figurative moral expansion: widely deployed in Victorian essays and historical prose as a metaphor for moral degradation, civilizational decline, or artistic degeneration.
- Today — Sophisticated academic & literary register: preserved in geomorphology, civil engineering, and elevated prose as an evocative term of classical landscape precision.
