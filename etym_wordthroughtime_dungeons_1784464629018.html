“Dungeon” first meant the great tower or keep of a castle — the lord’s strongest stronghold (from the same root as donjon), a place of power and defence rather than punishment.
“Dungeon” in later medieval and Tudor English shifted downward and inward: the keep’s lower chambers became prison rooms, and the word came to mean a dark underground cell or vault of confinement.
“Dungeon” through the 18th–19th centuries was fixed by the gothic imagination as the castle’s terrifying underground prison — chains, damp stone, and forgotten captives — and that sense became the default.
“Dungeon” today conjures legendary under-mountain depths in fantasy — as in the dwarves’ “dungeons deep and caverns old” — while its original tower-meaning survives only in the historical “donjon.”
