Uncouth: the biography of a stranger

Uncouth is the biography of the stranger — a word that began as a statement of fact and ended as a judgement of manners. Its deepest root is the Proto-Indo-European verb *gneh₃-, 'to know,' one of the most productive roots in the family.

  • Worldwide cognates: in Indo-Iranian the root produced Sanskrit jānāti, 'he knows,' and the noun jñāna, 'knowledge' — the source of Hindi gyān (ज्ञान) and of the common verb jānnā (जानना). In Greek it produced gignṓskō, 'to know,' and gnôsis, 'knowledge,' the ancestor of English gnosis, diagnosis ('a knowing apart'), prognosis ('a knowing beforehand'), and gnomon — the sundial's pointer, literally 'the knower,' the thing that knows the hour. In Latin it produced (g)nōscere, 'to come to know,' whose family grew wide: cognōscere, 'to get to know,' gave recognize and cognizant; nota, 'a mark, that by which a thing is known,' gave note and notion; and gnārus, 'knowing,' gave narrāre, 'to make known, to tell' — English narrate.
  • The Germanic branch: the root became Proto-Germanic *kunnaną, 'to know, to know how' — with Grimm's Law turning the initial *g into *k — which yielded Old English cunnan, the ancestor of the modern modal can, and its past participle cūþ, 'known, familiar, well-known.'
From cūþ Old English built a pair of formations: the negative uncūþ, 'unknown, unfamiliar, strange,' and the abstract cȳþþ, 'acquaintance, knowledge,' which survives in the phrase kith and kin (where kin itself descends from a different root, *gen-, 'to beget' — the phrase accidentally pairs a 'knowing' word with a 'birth' word).

  • The semantic journey (epistemology → etiquette): in Middle English uncouth still meant 'unknown, strange,' but the social machinery of the word was already turning. What is unknown is strange; what is strange is foreign; what is foreign is alien — and what is alien to the cultivated circle is, by that very fact, unpolished. By the 16th–17th centuries uncouth had completed its migration: it meant rude, uncultured, unrefined, without grace, while the old 'unknown' sense withered away, surviving only in the word's etymology and in dialects.
  • The modern irony: in the 19th century English coined couth (1896) as the opposite of uncouth, forgetting that the negative once contained the positive — what is 'couth' is only 'known.' The back-formation is a small monument to the migration: the same root that gave 'can' and 'cunning' ('knowing, skilful') now stands for refinement.
  • The literary career: the word carried different weights in different ages. In Old English literature uncūþ was a word of exile and strangeness (an uncūþ land is an alien shore, the home of monsters); in Middle English romance it meant the unfamiliar marvel; in early modern drama and prose it became the comic and satirical label for the boor; in the 19th–20th centuries it settled as an old-fashioned word of manners, capable of a comic shrug ('how uncouth!') or of the older, bleaker force — the rude as the unknowing.
  • In the passage: 'They were uncouth and prematurely aged.' The double force is everything: physically the carvers are rough, weathered, gnarled — their hands and faces cut by stone dust and want; socially they are unknown, excluded from the cultivated knowledge of the Earl's world, cut off beyond the wall in a life so alien that 'all radiance [is] gone.' The word fuses appearance and status — the unpolished as the unknown — and 'prematurely aged' adds the note that their hardness is not natural but inflicted, the rough surface of an exile.
Sound changes along the chain: PIE *gneh₃- → Proto-Germanic *kunnaną (Grimm's Law: *g → *k); *kunnaną → Old English cunnan; cunnan → cūþ (the participle in *-þ-, with the long *ū from the zero grade of the root); cūþ + un- → uncūþ; uncūþ → Middle English uncouth (the voiced dental þ becoming the unvoiced th, spelled ); and the modern pronunciation /ʌŋkˈuːθ/ keeps the hard k and the grating th — a sound, the word seems to say, for the roughness it names.
