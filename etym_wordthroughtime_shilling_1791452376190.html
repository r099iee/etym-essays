- Old English (9th to 11th century) - A purely notional unit of account used to price wergild, livestock and land; no shilling coin yet existed, and regional values varied from about four to five pence.
- Middle English (12th to 14th century) - A familiar reckoning unit of twelve pence, spoken of in law courts and taverns alike; the word feels administrative rather than glamorous.
- Tudor and Stuart (16th to 17th century) - A real silver coin at last, minted from 1504; "a shilling" becomes a tangible sum, the price of a meal, a book or a night's lodging.
- Georgian and Victorian (18th to 19th century) - The workhorse coin of ordinary life and of charitable respectability; "take the King's shilling" means enlist, and "shilling shocker" brands cheap sensation fiction.
- Edwardian and interwar (early 20th century) - Still respectable but shrinking in real value; in Wodehouse a shilling is the smallest gentlemanly wager, a trifle offered for a favour.
- Post-war and pre-decimal (1950s to 1971) - Increasingly small change, jingled in pockets and fed into gas meters; the coin is abolished on Decimal Day, 15 February 1971.
- Contemporary (1971 to present) - Archaic in Britain except in idiom ("not the full shilling") and historical writing, but fully alive abroad as the currency of Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Somalia and South Sudan.
