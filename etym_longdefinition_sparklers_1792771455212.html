Sparklers, the plural of sparkler, are in their dominant modern sense small hand-held fireworks: stiff wires or sticks coated in a slow-burning pyrotechnic mixture that, once ignited, throw off a branching shower of brilliant white-gold sparks with a soft crackle, burning down steadily without exploding.

In the source sentence the word sits inside a catalogue of festive fireworks — squibs, crackers, backarappers, sparklers, torches, dwarf-candles — where it carries exactly this celebratory sense: wands of light meant to be waved by hand, the gentlest and most intimate member of the firework family, enjoyed at Diwali, Bonfire Night, weddings, and New Year celebrations across the world.

Core meaning in detail: A typical sparkler is a metal wire (or bamboo splint) dipped in a composition of metallic fuel such as iron filings or aluminium, an oxidiser such as barium or potassium nitrate, and a binder. Colours come from metal salts: strontium for red, barium for green, sodium for gold. Because the mixture deflagrates (burns rapidly) rather than detonates, the sparkler is classed among the safest fireworks, though its sparks still burn fiercely hot and demand care.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hand-held firework (primary modern sense, from 1906): the wire-stick firework described above; the sense every modern reader reaches for first.
  • A person or thing that sparkles (earliest sense, 1560s, now literary): originally anything brilliant, glittering, or vivacious — a sparkling wit, a glittering ornament. The bridge is the verb sparkle plus the agent suffix -er: literally ‘that which sparkles’.
  • A sparkling gemstone, especially a diamond (colloquial, 20th c.): as in ‘she showed off her new sparkler’. The bridge is visual: a cut diamond throws flashes of light exactly as a sparkler throws sparks, and the slang belongs to the same register as calling diamonds ‘ice’.
  • Sparkling wine or a fizzy drink (rare, dated): a glass of ‘sparkler’ for sparkling wine. The bridge runs through sparkling — bubbles rising in wine were likened to sparks rising from fire — then clipped to the noun.
  • A vivacious, dazzling person (figurative): ‘the sparkler of the debating society’. The bridge is metaphorical: just as the firework draws every eye, so the lively person outshines quieter company. This revives the 1560s sense in modern dress.

Register and connotation: sparklers is a warm, festive word — childlike delight rather than menace. Even its slang extensions (diamonds, vivacious people) carry admiration and affection, never contempt. Grammatically it is almost always plural when it means fireworks, since sparklers are bought, lit, and waved by the handful. Safety notes always add that the wire stays scorching long after the sparks die, so a bucket of water or sand is the sparkler's traditional companion.
