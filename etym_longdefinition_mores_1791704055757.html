Mores (pronounced /ˈmɔː.reɪz/ or /ˈmɔː.riːz/) refers to the fundamental moral customs, social conventions, and deeply ingrained ethical standards of a particular community, culture, or historical epoch. Far more than superficial rules of etiquette or passing fads, mores are regarded by members of a society as indispensable to its collective welfare, social cohesion, and moral order. Violations of a society's mores do not merely cause mild awkwardness or polite amusement; they provoke profound moral outrage, communal condemnation, intense social ostracism, or severe taboos.

In modern sociology and anthropology, the definitive theoretical status of mores was established by pioneer American sociologist William Graham Sumner in his seminal 1906 treatise Folkways: A Study of the Sociological Importance of Usages, Manners, Customs, Mores, and Morals. Sumner formulated an enduring tripartite hierarchy of social norms that remains foundational to social science:

  • Folkways: Informal, routine conventions of daily behavior, traditional habits, and casual etiquette (such as table manners, styles of dress, greetings, or queuing norms). Violating a folkway may mark an individual as eccentric, clumsy, or ill-mannered, but it carries no moral stigma and threatens no institutional foundation.
  • Mores: Folkways that have been elevated to central moral significance because the community has come to believe that adherence to them is vital to societal survival, spiritual salvation, or public order. Mores govern fundamental human boundaries—such as sexual behavior, honesty in commerce, taboos against incest and cannibalism, respect for sacred institutions, and familial obligations. Violating mores results in righteous moral wrath, shunning, exile, or violent communal retribution.
  • Laws: Formal, institutionalized, and explicitly codified norms enforced by state coercion, judicial bodies, and specialized police power. In healthy societies, statutory laws represent the codification and legal backing of pre-existing mores; when laws diverge too drastically from communal mores, they are widely resisted and collapse into unenforceability.

Beyond academic sociology, the word mores functions across multiple intellectual, legal, and literary registers:

  • Legal Jurisprudence (*Boni Mores* & *Contra Bonos Mores*): In Roman civil law and modern legal systems rooted in the continental tradition (as well as common law public policy doctrine), contracts, agreements, or commercial practices that are contra bonos mores ("against good morals / offensive to public decency") are legally null and void. The legal concept relies directly on the unwritten, prevailing moral consensus of decent citizens.
  • Cultural Anthropology & Relativism: Anthropologists analyze how mores vary across geographical boundaries and historical epochs. What one civilization venerates as sacred duty (such as filial piety or ritual sacrifice), another may condemn as barbarism. Anthropological analysis traces how material conditions, economic structures, and religious revelations shape the specific mores of a civilization.
  • Cultural Critique & Historical Transitions: Cultural historians frequently speak of "the changing mores of the Victorian era" or "the sexual mores of the 1960s" to chart sweeping generational revolutions where established taboos disintegrate and new ethical consensus emerges.
  • Grammatical Form & Syntactical Nature: In modern English, mores is a plural noun (borrowed directly from the Latin plural of mōs), construed with plural verbs ("contemporary mores are shifting") and lacking a functional singular form in general English prose (since singular mos was never adopted into vernacular English).
