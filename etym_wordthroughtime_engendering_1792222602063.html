- 14th c. (Middle English) — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman and Old French (engendrer), initially dominated by the literal biological sense of procreating, siring offspring, and physical generation, as immortalized in the opening lines of Chaucer's Canterbury Tales.
- 15th–16th c. (Late Middle to Early Modern English) — Rapidly expanded into abstract, moral, and psychological causation; theological and philosophical treatises described vices, virtues, and spiritual states as being "engendered" in the soul.
- 17th c. (Jacobean & Restoration Eras) — Widely utilized in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (including Shakespeare) to denote the hatching of plots, deep emotions, or intellectual constructs ("It is engendered! Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world's light").
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Eras) — Literal biological procreation receded from polite conversation, increasingly replaced by beget or clinical terms, firmly cementing engender as an elevated, formal causal verb for social sentiment, political movements, and artistic effects.
- 20th c. to Present — Standardized across academic prose, literary criticism, sociology, and political analysis to articulate organic, systemic, and structural causation.
