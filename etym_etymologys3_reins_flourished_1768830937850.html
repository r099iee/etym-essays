THE EVOLUTION OF REINS The chain of descent. 'Rein' enters modern English from Old French resne / reine, which continues Latin retina, 'a cord, band, leash, bridle-rein' — a noun derived from the verb retinēre, 'to hold back, restrain, retain,' itself a compound of re- 'back' and tenēre 'to hold.' The deepest attested source is the Indo-European root *ten-, 'to stretch,' which also gives Latin tendere and English thin, tender, tense. The semantic spine of the whole history is the idea of holding back by means of a stretched cord: the word moves from 'stretch' to 'hold' to 'hold back' to 'that which holds back a horse.' The riding world that made it. The noun retina belongs to the Roman world of cavalry and chariot, where control of the horse was literal and manual, and to the medieval French world of mounted warfare, where the rein became a technical object of the highest importance — the heavy cavalryman must control his horse with one hand while fighting with the other. English takes the word in the fourteenth century, and the earliest attestations (c. 1300s) name the rider's paired straps: the two leather lines held in the hands, the direct, taut link between the rider's will and the horse's mouth. The political metaphor arrives at once. Almost within a generation of the word's arrival, English begins to use the reins as the image of government: 'the reins of the realm,' 'the reins of the empire,' 'to hold the reins' are all attested from the fifteenth century. This is one of the earliest and most enduring metaphors of political power in the language, and it transfers naturally from kings to statesmen, generals, and managers. The semantic logic is simple and physical: to steer a horse you hold the reins, and the man who holds the reins steers the state. The idioms consolidate. By the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries the figurative machinery is complete: 'take the reins,' 'hand over the reins,' 'a free rein,' 'to rein in,' 'to give rein to.' The word's literal home narrows to riding and driving, while its daily life becomes the master figure of command by direct, hand-held restraint. The plural form is the rule, the pair of straps treated as a single instrument of authority. In literary usage the image runs unbroken from Shakespeare's political drama through the statecraft prose of the eighteenth century to the corporate language of the present day; a modern chief executive still 'takes the reins' of a company exactly as a medieval king took the reins of a realm. The sound and form. The spelling shifted through Middle English reyne and rene before settling on rein in the early modern period; the plural remained the ordinary form throughout, so that even today one speaks of 'the reins' where a single strap is meant, the matched pair being conceptually one instrument. The word's evolution is an object lesson in how a concrete, physical instrument becomes, within a few centuries, the standard abstract vocabulary of control. THE EVOLUTION OF FLOURISH From flower to fortune. The verb 'flourish' descends from Latin flōrēre, 'to bloom, to be in flower, to prosper,' built on the noun flōs, 'flower,' from the Indo-European root *bʰleh₃-, 'to bloom, thrive' — the same root that gives English bloom, blossom, and flower itself. In Latin the word already carries a double life: it describes literal plants in flower and, by an ancient extension, the 'flowering' of a person or state — the prime, the best years, the height of fortune. 'To flourish' is thus from the beginning both botanical and social. The inchoative machinery. Old French florir continues the Latin verb with an added twist: the present tense is built on the inchoative stem floriss-, from Latin *flōrīscere, 'to begin to bloom,' and this is the stem English borrowed. That is why the verb shows -ish- in 'flourish, flourished, flourishing' even though no modern speaker thinks of 'beginning to bloom' when saying it. The inchoative -sc- is a fossil in the verb's body. Blooming in English. English takes the word in the thirteenth century with the full dual meaning: plants blossom, fortunes thrive. By the fourteenth century 'flourish' is standard for prosperity — 'a flourishing realm,' 'flourishing trade' — and its literary range is broad from the start, applied to kingdoms, arts, health, and crops. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the noun 'flourish' breaks away to mean an ornamental stroke of penmanship, a decorative turn in music, a heraldic device, a fanfare: any embellishment conceived as the 'flowering' of the line. The verb follows — 'to flourish a letter' is to embellish it — and this ornamental sense is the hinge on which the martial meaning turns. The blade blooms. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the swordsman flourishes his weapon: he sweeps it in a wide, showy, theatrical arc, a gesture of display, self-assertion, and intimidation before the blow is ever struck. The flourish of the blade is the flowering of the blade — the same word that made a pen-stroke beautiful now makes a saber threatening. It is a striking piece of semantic history: an instrument of death described with the vocabulary of a flower, which is exactly the usage on this card, where the general flourishes his saber at the troops. The modern double life. Contemporary English runs the two senses in parallel without effort: a business flourishes while a conductor flourishes his baton. The root image — something opening outward into vivid display, a flower, a pen-stroke, a blade — still binds them. The word's evolution is a textbook case of semantic widening driven by a concrete metaphor that kept its poetry for seven centuries.
