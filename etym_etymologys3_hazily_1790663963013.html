Hazily is a transparent modern adverb — hazy + -ly — but the adjective it rests on has one of the murkiest origins in the dictionary, as if the word itself were lost in haze. The OED bluntly marks haze origin obscure, and that obscurity shapes the whole essay: we trace a probable path while confessing the fog.

The likeliest deep source is PIE *kes-, grey or pale, which gives Old English hasu (grey, ashen, dusky — used of eagles and hair) and haswig (greyish). Phonologically hasu to haze requires voicing of s to z and a vowel shift whose details are irregular; competing proposals invoke Old Norse höss (grey, of the head) or Middle Dutch analogues. What is certain is the Middle English noun hase for hoarseness and the early-fourteenth-century noun haze for thin mist or obscurity of air, soon extended to confusion of mind. To be in a haze was to be unable to see or think clearly, whether from weather, drink, or bewilderment.

  • c. 1300–1600: haze as noun. Sailors and countrymen speak of haze at sea and in summer fields — not thick fog but a thin obscuring veil. Figuratively, minds are in a haze after blows, fever, or strong emotion. The word stays concrete and meteorological, with moral overtones of confusion rather than deceit.
  • 1625: hazy appears. The adjective hazy, full of haze, is first recorded in the seventeenth century for misty weather and for dim memories and notions. Sailors log hazy horizons; divines preach against hazy notions of duty. The extension from air to ideas is immediate and permanent: what is hazy cannot be made out distinctly.
  • 18th–19th c.: hazy matures. Romantic poets love hazy distances — Turner paints them, Wordsworth muses in them — giving haze an aesthetic, nostalgic value alongside its negative vagueness. Empire sailors, soldiers, and opium-eaters report hazy recollections; school memoirs recall lessons hazily. The adverb hazily forms regularly and is recorded for seeing, remembering, and understanding in a hazy manner.
  • 1900–present: the psychology of hazily. Modern prose uses hazily almost more for memory and comprehension than for weather: hazily recalled rules, hazily remembered faces, hazily understood theories. It names the common adult condition the source sentence diagnoses — grammatical commandments learned by rote, never grounded, now floating in half-memory. Photography and film add a technical sense: shot hazily, through soft focus or literal haze.

Morphologically nothing could be simpler: Germanic -y (full of) makes haze into hazy, and Germanic -ly (in the manner of) makes hazy into hazily, with regular y → i spelling change. The adverb is fully productive: hazily seen, hazily recalled, hazily defined. Pronunciation follows: RP /ˈheɪzɪli/, two syllables of haze plus unstressed -ily, the z voiced throughout. No rival spellings survive; hasey and haizy appear only as early-modern variants.

Literary attestation is atmospheric in both senses. Shakespeare has mist but not yet haze in the modern sense; seventeenth-century sea journals do. Austen characters see prospects hazily through tears; Dickens Londoners live in literal and moral haze; Conrad exiles recall home hazily; modern textbooks, like the source passage, diagnose citizens who recall schooling hazily. The debated etymology is itself often cited as a charming self-illustration: lexicographers confess the origin of haze is hazy, lost in the very obscurity it names — a rare case where not knowing is part of the word charm.
