The biography of brigandage tracks a word from raw physical force and fortified height to the systematic criminality of the dispossessed — and from a piece of military jargon to a legal and literary term for state-defying violence.

Earliest horizon — Celtic and the Indo-European inheritance:

  • The most widely accepted starting point is the Celtic word briga, attested in the toponymy of Roman Gaul and Iberia (Brigantium, Brigantia). Its meaning fused 'strength, might' with 'fortified height, hill-fort' — a place of power. Some scholars connect it to PIE *bʰerǵʰ- 'high, elevated' (the root of German Berg and English burg/borough), so that 'strength' grew out of 'high place.' This stage is only reconstructed indirectly, and it must be flagged as probable rather than proven.
  • Whatever its deeper ancestry, briga entered the speech of the Roman provinces as *briga 'strife, quarrel, contention.' The semantic drift is from 'strength/power' to 'the exercise of strength against another' — i.e. 'a fight, a row, trouble.' This is a common pathway: a noun for force slides into a noun for conflict.

Italian and the mercenary interlude — the key turning point:

  • In Italy the base produced the verb brigare 'to strive, contend, scheme' and then the participle brigante. In the 14th and 15th centuries a brigante was not a thief: he was a lightly armed irregular soldier, a skirmisher, a vital if undisciplined part of mercenary companies.
  • The decisive semantic degradation occurred after the wars. Disbanded briganti kept their weapons and their appetite for plunder; with no army and no pay, they preyed on the countryside. The 'irregular soldier' became the 'armed highwayman,' and the word hardened into a term of outlawry from which it never recovered.
  • France borrowed brigand in the 14th century and then created the abstract noun brigandage with the suffix -age (Latin -aticum), which forms nouns of activity and system — compare piracy, vassalage, outrage. By the 15th century brigandage designated the whole trade of armed bands, not a single robbery.

English borrowing and the romantic-legal career of the word:

  • English took brigandage from French in the early 18th century, during a period of intense travel literature about the bandit-ridden roads of Italy. The word arrived already carrying both a criminal-legal weight and an exotic, almost picturesque aura.
  • The 19th century split the word in two directions. In law and administration, 'brigandage' became a formal crime category: the post-unification Italian state fought a bitter, decade-long 'war on brigandage' (brigantaggio) in the south, using army units and martial courts; British administrators used the term for armed resistance in India and colonial territories, conveniently criminalizing rebellion as mere robbery.
  • In literature and travel writing, meanwhile, brigandage was romanticized — the handsome bandit, the Robin Hood of the mountains, the klephts of Greece, the hajduks of the Balkans, the gaucho outlaws of the pampas. This double life — feared as crime, cherished as folklore — kept the word vivid through the 19th century.
  • In the 20th century the word retreated. Journalism now prefers banditry, armed robbery, insurgency, or organized crime, and brigandage survives mainly in history books, legal writing, and period fiction, where it sounds deliberately archaic and scholarly — precisely the register it carries in the present card's passage about famine, mutiny, and state repression.

Linguistic observations:

  • The phonetics are Romance throughout: the initial br-, the mid-word -g-, and the abstract -age suffix are stable from Italian to English; the word never passed through Germanic sound changes.
  • The key semantic law visible here is degradation by association: a professional category (irregular soldier) is degraded by its post-war behaviour into a criminal one, and the abstract noun preserves that degradation frozen in the lexicon.
  • The suffix -age is the same one that yields pillage, plunderage (rare), and forage — a family of words for living off the land and taking what is not yours.
