- [Late 16th c. Elizabethan Era] — borrowed from Middle French as a strictly military and diplomatic term denoting the formal capitulation, surrender, or handing over of a besieged castle, town, or military garrison — [perception: understood as a solemn, high-stakes military act of treaty capitulation]
- [17th c. Early Modern English] — broadened in scholastic and theological writing to signify the verbal translation, paraphrase, or scholarly rendering of a foreign Latin or Greek text into English — [perception: respected as a rigorous intellectual exercise in faithful linguistic transfer]
- [18th–19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] — underwent a major aesthetic transformation, becoming an established term in theatrical, musical, and artistic criticism for a performer's distinctive, heartfelt interpretation of a musical piece or dramatic role — [perception: celebrated as a mark of creative virtuosity, interpretive originality, and refined sensibility]
- [20th c. Modern Era] — universalized across performing arts, cinema, and visual design to denote any artistic presentation, cover song, or graphical rendering of a classic piece — [perception: regarded as a standard, high-register term of cultural and aesthetic evaluation]
- [Late 20th–21st c. Post-Cold War & Post-9/11 Era] — acquired a severe, controversial geopolitical and legal connotation through the phrase "extraordinary rendition", referring to covert extrajudicial state abductions and secret detentions — [perception: sharply bifurcated between refined artistic praise in concert halls and dark human rights discourse in international law]
