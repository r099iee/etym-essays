- 13th–16th centuries — “Lithuanian” means subject of the Grand Duchy, a pagan then Christian polity expanding eastwards; it is a political and dynastic label, not an ethnic one.
- 1569–1795 — Under the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth the word denotes the Grand Duchy’s elite and institutions; Polish is the court language, and “Lithuanian” can coexist with Polish or Ruthenian identity.
- 1795–1863 — After the partitions, the word survives mainly as a regional term under Russian rule; the Lithuanian language is largely a peasant vernacular.
- 1864–1904 — The ban on Latin-script printing turns “Lithuanian” into a smuggled, dissident identity; “knygnešiai” (book carriers) make the language a resistance symbol.
- 1918–1940 — The word becomes the name of a sovereign republic and a modern national identity, with Vilnius and Kaunas as rival cultural centres.
- 1940–1991 — Under Soviet rule “Lithuanian” is officially a regional Soviet identity; in the diaspora it becomes a badge of exile and anti-communism.
- 1990–2004 — Independence and EU/NATO accession make the word a marker of a small, modernising European state; the archaic language becomes a point of national pride.
- 2000s–present — “Lithuanian” is routine in EU affairs and diaspora networking, while pop-culture and tourism branding lean on amber, basketball and the baroque old town of Vilnius.
