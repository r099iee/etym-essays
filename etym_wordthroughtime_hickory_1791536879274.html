- Early 17th Century - Borrowed by Virginia colonists from the Powhatan word for an oily nut-milk beverage ('pawcohiccora'), rapidly transferring to denote the nut and the tree itself.
- Late 17th–18th Century - Established throughout American colonial botany and timber trades as the premier wood for carriage wheels, tool handles, and wagon spokes due to unmatched shock resistance.
- Early 19th Century - Elevated to a cultural symbol of rugged American frontier grit, toughness, and endurance through Andrew Jackson's popular moniker 'Old Hickory.'
- Late 19th to Early 20th Century - Connoted rural schoolroom discipline ('taught to the tune of a hickory stick') and timberland labor ('hickory shirts'), alongside classic sporting equipment like golf shafts.
- Contemporary Usage - Widely celebrated in culinary and barbecue culture for its rich, authentic smoke flavor, and still revered as the gold standard in woodworking for heavy impact tool handles.
