The compound overall is born of a marriage between two ancient Indo-European roots, and its complete evolution must be told as three movements: the biography of over, the biography of all, and the fusion of the two on English soil.

Movement 1 — the descent of over: The first element descends from the PIE root *upér, an adverbial formation upon the base *upó, “up from under”, which marked position in vertical space — above, over, beyond. The root is one of the most prolific in the entire Indo-European family, and its branches show how a single spatial notion diversifies across a continent. In the Hellenic world it produced Greek hupér, “over, above, beyond”, the source of the English prefix hyper- and of hyperbole, the “over-throwing” of language; in Indo-Iranian it became Sanskrit upári, “above, over”, from which Hindi ūpar and Odia uparē still name the space above the speaker; in Italic it yielded Latin super — showing the s-mobile, the wandering initial consonant that distinguishes super from Greek hupér — the ancestor of super, supreme, and insuperable. In the Germanic world the same root gave Proto-Germanic *uber, Old English ofer, and modern English over, together with the great family of over- compounds — overcoat, overdo, overlord, overrule, oversee — in which the prefix oscillates between physical covering and moral excess. That oscillation is decisive for the garment: the “over” of “overall” means simultaneously “above” (a layer laid above the clothes) and “beyond measure” (the totality that exceeds particulars). In Old English, ofer eall was a phrase of spatial totality: a net cast over a field lies ofer eall the field; an eye passing over a page moves ofer eall the page. The phrase fused in Middle English as overal, an adverb meaning “everywhere, altogether, in every direction” (14th century), then generalised into the language of summary and judgment: “overall length”, “overall impression” (15th–16th centuries). The socio-cultural engine here is bureaucracy and accountancy — the need of estates, courts, and merchants to hold a whole in a single glance produced the adjective of total assessment that governs “overall winners” and “overall performance” to this day.

Movement 2 — the ascent of all: The second element descends from PIE *al-, an adverbial root of “beyondness” whose most famous semantic leap made the word for “other” into the word for “every”. In Greek, *al- gave állos, “other” — the source of allegory, “speaking other”, and allopathy, the treatment by the opposite of a disease; in Latin it gave alius, “other”, the ancestor of alias, alibi (“elsewhere”), and alien. In Germanic it produced the adjective *allaz, “whole, entire, all”: Old English eall, German all, and English all, also (“all so”), already, and albeit (“all be it”). The semantic shift from “beyond” to “the whole” is a classic instance of completion: what lies beyond every particular is the totality, so the word that meant “other” came to mean “the whole”. This is why the compound “over-all” is not merely “above everything” but “embracing everything”: the “all” of the compound already carries totality, and “over” adds the gesture of the encompassing sweep that takes every part into view at once.

Movement 3 — the fusion: When English fused ofer and eall, the compound moved from phrase to word, and then made the astonishing jump from abstraction to cloth. In the 14th century overal was an adverb (“everywhere, altogether”); by the 16th century it was an adjective of summary (“total, comprehensive”). Then, in 1759, American usage coined the noun over-alls for heavy protective trousers with a bib and braces worn by farmers and railwaymen: the abstract word of totality became the workingman's cloth, worn over all the other garments. In the 20th century the British singular overall named the one-piece suit covering trunk and limbs — the livery of factory, garage, and museum. The semantic bridge is perfectly visible: a garment worn over all the others is, literally, the “over-all” garment — the last layer that encloses the whole person, exactly as the adjective encloses a whole subject. In Titus Groan, Rottcodd's overall therefore carries the word's entire history in a single syllable boundary: humble working cloth that is nevertheless clothed in the totalising abstraction of “over-all” completeness — the one man in whom the Hall of the Bright Carvings is summed up, “over all” its dust, “over all” its carvings, “over all” its seven candles, the keeper who is literally “over all” of it. The word thus spans the paper of management and the cloth of the caretaker in one continuous historical line, from PIE *upér and *al- to the grey garment enveloping the keeper of the wing.

Literary and cultural attestations across time: Chaucer's generation used “overal” adverbially for “everywhere”; sixteenth-century surveyors and merchants used the adjective to price “the over-all value” of an estate; the 1759 American attestation of “over-alls” appears in descriptions of frontier labour; Dickens and the Victorian industrial novelists put the workingman in his overalls as a badge of class; and in the twentieth century the managerial registers of “overall performance” and “overall responsibility” re-abstracted the word in boardrooms and statistics offices. Every shift — spatial phrase, fused adverb, adjective of totality, American bib-and-brace trousers, British one-piece suit — is documented, and each is a re-materialisation or re-abstraction of the same core idea: the sweeping gesture that takes in everything above and beyond the particular.
