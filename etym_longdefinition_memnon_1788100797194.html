Memnon binds two great antiquities into one name. In Greek myth he is the son of Eos, the goddess of the Dawn, and of Tithonus, the Trojan prince; he is king of the Aethiopians, the dark-skinned peoples of the world's southern rim in Greek geography. In the final year of the Trojan War he came to the aid of Troy with a great Eastern army, performed dazzling deeds, killed the Greek hero Antilochus — Nestor's beloved son — and was himself slain by Achilles. His death, mourned by the goddess of the morning, made him the Trojan cycle's noblest foreign champion: the brilliant, doomed ally whose fall darkens the victor's glory and prefigures Achilles' own death.

His story and cult. The lost epic Aethiopis told of his coming and his fall; the Odyssey already refers to him as the son of the Dawn (Book 11), a place where ancient readers identified him. Later tradition — most fully Quintus of Smyrna's Posthomerica and Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 13) — added his afterlife: Zeus granted the slain hero a measure of immortality; his mourning retainers were turned into the Memnonides, miraculous birds that rose from his funeral pyre and fought yearly over his ashes at his tomb. Virgil's Aeneid (1.489) remembers "the arms of black Memnon" among the famous dead, and Greek vase-painting repeatedly staged his duel with Achilles — the Ethiopian prince in armour, black against the Greek.

The Egyptian Memnon. The Greeks gave the same name to Egypt's wonders: the two colossal seated statues of Pharaoh Amenhotep III (14th century BCE) at western Thebes were called the colossi of Memnon, and the neighbouring Ramesside temple was the "Memnonium". One of the colossi, shattered in an antique earthquake (traditionally dated c. 27 BCE), emitted a musical note at sunrise as the warming fractured stone expanded — the fabled "vocal Memnon". The sound became a tourist attraction of the Roman Empire: Strabo described it; Tacitus (Annals 2.61) reports that Germanicus heard it in 19 CE; Pausanias likened it to the snapping of a lyre-string; and hundreds of visitors scratched Greek and Latin verses into the stone — including the imperial court poet Julia Balbilla, who inscribed epigrams during Hadrian's visit in 130 CE. After Septimius Severus repaired the statue, around 199 CE, the song ceased; silence has reigned since.

Wilde's Memnon. The Happy Prince fuses these strands into "the God Memnon", enthroned on granite at the Second Cataract of the Nile: all night he watches the stars, and when the morning star shines he "utters one cry of joy, and then he is silent". That cry is the colossus's legendary dawn-song, transplanted from Thebes to Nubia; the star-watching is the son of Eos greeting his mother's light. Wilde's Memnon is a Romantic invention — half dawn-god, half singing stone — with the throne of a Nile god-king borrowed from the sun-cult of the river.

Modern afterlife. Today the name survives in three registers: classical allusion (a noble, doomed outsider-warrior who hymns the dawn), art history (the duellist of countless vases), and tourism — the Colossi of Memnon, two quartzite giants over 18 metres tall, still front the Theban plain and are among the most photographed monuments of ancient Egypt.

  • Myth: son of Eos and Tithonus; king of the Aethiopians; slayer of Antilochus; victim of Achilles.
  • Cult: the Memnonides birds; a tomb tradition in the Troad; Zeus's gift of immortality.
  • Egypt: the vocal colossi of Amenhotep III at Thebes; the Memnonium; dawn-song records from Strabo to Julia Balbilla.
  • Literature: Odyssey 11; Virgil Aeneid 1.489; Ovid Metamorphoses 13; Quintus of Smyrna; Wilde's god of the Second Cataract.
