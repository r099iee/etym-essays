Etymological Evolution: From Ancient Hebrew Monotheism to Ivy League Patronage

The proper name Elihu represents one of the most intellectually compelling theophoric constructions in Northwest Semitic onomastics, embodying a direct grammatical confession of monotheistic faith that traveled from ancient biblical poetry through the English Puritan naming revolution and into the foundational lore of American higher education.

Semitic Linguistic Architecture: The Theophoric Confession

The morphology of Elihu is constructed from two distinct lexical elements in Biblical Hebrew:

  • The Divine Element (ʾĒlī): Derived from the common Semitic root for divinity, *ʾil- (found in Ugaritic ʾil, Akkadian ilu, and Arabic ʾilāh). In Hebrew, אֵל (ʾĒl) denotes "God" or "the Mighty One." When augmented by the first-person singular pronominal possessive suffix -ī (י-), it produces אֵלִי (ʾēlī), meaning "my God" (famously echoed in the Psalmic and Gospel lament Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani).
  • The Pronominal Element (Hūʾ): Derived from Proto-Semitic *huwa, yielding Hebrew הוּא (hūʾ), the third-person masculine singular personal pronoun meaning "He" or "that One." In Hebrew theological syntax, hūʾ frequently functions as a predicate of divine self-existence and identity—declaring that God alone is truly existent, constant, and supreme (as in Deuteronomy 32:39, ʾănī ʾănī hūʾ, "I, even I, am He").

When compounded, the resulting name אֱלִיהוּא (ʾĔlīhūʾ) or its shortened variant אֱלִיהוּ (ʾĔlīhū) forms a complete nominal clause: "My God is He" or "He is my God." It stands as an assertive onomastic confession of personal allegiance, distinguishing the bearer's household as worshippers of the one true God rather than the surrounding Canaanite deities.

Biblical Attestations: The Mysterious Interlocutor in Job

While several minor figures in the Old Testament bear the name—including Samuel’s great-grandfather (1 Samuel 1:1), a chief warrior of Manasseh who defected to David at Ziklag (1 Chronicles 12:20), and a temple gatekeeper (1 Chronicles 26:7)—the name achieved immortal literary and theological prominence through Elihu son of Barachel the Buzite in the Book of Job (chapters 32–37):

  • The Dramatic Interlude: After Job and his three elder companions (Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar) exhaust their philosophical debates in a bitter stalemate, Elihu suddenly emerges. Introduced as a young man burning with righteous anger because the elders found no answer yet condemned Job, Elihu delivers four uninterrupted, highly poetic speeches.
  • Theological Uniqueness: Elihu’s discourse rejects the crude, mechanical retributive theology of the three friends, arguing instead that suffering can serve as divine pedagogy—a corrective, medicinal chastening to purge human pride. Linguistically, his speeches feature a remarkable density of Aramaicisms and archaic poetic vocabulary, leading many modern biblical scholars to view his chapters as a profound, deliberate insertion bridging human philosophy and the climactic appearance of Yahweh out of the whirlwind.

Transmission: From Septuagint to the Puritan Revolution

In the third century BCE, the Jewish translators of the Septuagint in Alexandria transliterated the Hebrew name into Hellenistic Greek as Ἐλιούς (Elioús) or Ἐλιού (Elioú). In the fourth century CE, St. Jerome normalized the spelling in the Latin Vulgate as Eliu and Elihu. Following the Protestant Reformation in England, the Geneva Bible (1560) and the King James Version (1611) fixed the modern English spelling as Elihu.

During the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, English Puritans initiated a sweeping onomastic revolution. Convinced that traditional English names (like William, Richard, and Henry) were contaminated by feudalism and Catholic saint veneration, Puritan parents systematically selected names directly from the Hebrew Scriptures. Names declaring theological allegiance—such as Elihu, Ebenezer, and Nathaniel—became widespread across East Anglia, London, and the newly established Massachusetts Bay Colony.

Elihu Yale and the Immortality of Yale University

It was into this devout Puritan culture that Elihu Yale was born in Boston, Massachusetts, on April 5, 1649, the son of merchant David Yale. Although the family returned to London when Elihu was three years old, his New England heritage endured. After amassing his colossal fortune in Madras as an East India Company nabob, Yale was approached in 1718 by the prominent Boston Puritan minister Cotton Mather. Mather wrote a shrewd, flattering solicitation suggesting that if Yale aided the impoverished Collegiate School in Saybrook (which had just relocated to New Haven), the academy would gladly perpetuate his family name:

"Sir, though perhaps you may have some reason to think that you have done enough for the world, yet if what is now proposed should find favor in your eyes, it may be an ambition not unworthy of you... that there may be a Name better than of sons and of daughters to you, in that Academy."

Yale responded with a generous shipment of 417 books, a royal portrait, and nine bales of luxury textiles, whose proceeds funded the construction of a collegiate hall. In gratitude, the trustees officially voted in September 1718 that the institution be named Yale College. Through this act of colonial patronage, the ancient biblical name Elihu became inextricably woven into American academic mythology, giving rise to the universal collegiate nickname for Yale students ("Elis"), the historic university battle cry ("Down the field with Eli Yale"), and the prestigious senior society Elihu, founded on the campus in 1903.
