The etymology of bonnet spans the transition from Late Latin textiles to medieval chivalric headwear, Celtic military pride, Victorian feminine fashion, and modern automotive terminology.

1. Late Latin and Old French Textile Origins (c. 4th – 13th Century):

  • The word is traced to Late Latin bonna or abonnis, a term attested in early medieval glossaries denoting a cap or headband. By the 11th century, Medieval Latin texts record bonetum, which designated a specific kind of fine, supple cloth used exclusively for making headdresses.
  • In Old French, the phrase chapel de bonet ("hat made of bonnet-cloth") underwent ellipsis: the noun *chapel* (hat) was dropped, leaving bonet to signify the cap itself. At this early stage, a *bonet* was worn primarily by men—noblemen, scholars, and burghers—as a soft, brimless indoor cap.

2. Middle English Entry and Scottish National Adoption (14th – 17th Century):

  • The word entered Middle English in the late 14th century (c. 1380s). While in southern England it designated various soft caps, in Scotland it became deeply entrenched as the universal term for the national men's headgear: the Scotch bonnet.
  • Scottish wool-waulkers produced dense, water-repellent felted wool bonnets dyed in blue, black, or clan tartans. The broad *blue bonnet* became the defining symbol of the Scottish Covenanters, Jacobite rebels, and Highland soldiers, giving rise to songs like "Blue Bonnets Over the Border."
  • In Scottish Highland regiments, the feather bonnet—a tall, elaborate headpiece adorned with black ostrich plumes and a red hackle—became a worldwide military icon of martial valor.

3. The Victorian Feminine Fashion Revolution (18th – 19th Century):

  • In 18th-century England and France, female milliners transformed the bonnet into the defining accessory of feminine modest fashion. Unlike hats with circular brims, the Victorian bonnet framed the face while concealing the hair and profile from casual male gaze, fastened securely under the chin.
  • Simultaneously, in detective and period literature, the "Scotch bonnet" retained its distinctive masculine, rugged character. In Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle" (1892), Holmes observes Henry Baker wearing a "Scotch bonnet," immediately providing a vital clue regarding the suspect's modest means and distinctive attire.

4. 20th-Century Automotive Transference:

  • With the birth of the British motor industry at the turn of the 20th century, engineers drew upon familiar wardrobe terminology to name vehicle components. Just as the passenger cabin had a *boot* (luggage trunk), the engine housing was named the bonnet, because it sat like a protective cap over the mechanical "head" of the automobile.
  • This automotive usage became standard throughout Great Britain, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, India, and South Africa, standing in contrast to American "hood."
