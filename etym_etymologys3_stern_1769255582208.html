The adjective stern begins in the physical language of stiffness. Its Old English ancestor styrne is attested with a fully abstract meaning — "severe, strict, hard, cruel, inflexible" — applied to God, to laws, and to hard-hearted rulers. But the word family around it shows where that meaning came from. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *ster- meant "stiff, rigid," and its descendants across the family describe things that do not bend:

  • Greek stereós, "stiff, hard, solid" — surviving in modern English in stereo and stereotype (the solid printing plate) and in scientific words such as cholesterol (the "solid" bile-substance);
  • Germanic reflexes still vividly physical in English: stark (rigid, bare), starch (the stiffening substance), stare (to look fixedly, with a stiffened gaze), and start and startle (to leap stiffly up, to bristle).

The adjective on this card is the member of that family that moved earliest and furthest into the moral realm. Old English styrne and its Germanic relatives mean "rigid, unbending" applied to persons, faces, and laws, so that sternness is simply moral and emotional rigidity — the human posture of a thing that will not yield. Because the adjective is first recorded only in Old English, its precise reconstruction is cautious: scholars propose a Proto-Germanic *sternjaz (or *starnjaz, or *sturnijaz) for the "severe" sense, and the connection to *ster- "stiff" is presented by the OED and standard dictionaries as probable rather than certain. What is certain is the metaphor, which the word has carried for more than a thousand years: to be stern is to be stiff. A stern face is a face held rigid, without the soft play of sympathy; a stern law is a law fixed fast, without the bend of mercy.

In Middle English, sterne was a favourite of moral and religious writers, who used it of God's judgements, of death, and of hard-hearted authority; sternness reads there as the posture of a thing held rigid against every appeal. In early modern English the adjective broadened across the whole range of authority — faces, looks, teachers, captains, judges, laws — while keeping its cold, unsmiling temperature. Shakespeare uses stern of persons, of the elements, and of the sea; Milton's God in Paradise Lost is judgement without warmth. The word became the stock epithet of the severe ruler and the stern parent, and it remains a staple of historical and literary prose to this day.

Meanwhile an unrelated homograph settled beside the adjective. The nautical noun stern, "the rear of a ship," comes from Old Norse stýri, "rudder, steering" — the back of the boat, where the steering gear is fixed — through Middle English sterne, and is ultimately related to steer and to starboard. The two words, the stiff adjective and the steering noun, have shared a spelling since Middle English but never a meaning; "from stem to stern" belongs to the noun alone. A third English word, sternum, "breastbone," from Greek stérnon, adds a final homograph. This trio is a standing warning to every etymologist: identical forms can have entirely independent histories.

Today stern remains in firm use for persons, voices, institutions, and laws that show no indulgence. Its register is formal and slightly old-fashioned: everyday speakers reach for the cooler, more neutral strict, reserving stern for the grim and unrelenting. It has also acquired a dignified, almost heroic ring in fixed phrases — "stern resolve," "stern duty," "stern necessity." That is exactly why C. S. Lewis chooses it for the laws of an unhappy Narnia, where high taxes, stern laws, and a cruel king combine into a picture of hardness that does not bend: the old stiffness of the root, translated into the unbending severity of a kingdom.
