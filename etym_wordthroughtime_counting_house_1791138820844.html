- Late 1400s–1600s: counting house as practical ledger room - Tudor merchants separate money-paper from home and warehouse; perception is efficient, respectable, modern for its day.
- 1700s: emblem of probity and industry - Defoe praises a handsome well-kept counting-house as credit itself; seen as honest trade, neither grand nor mean.
- 1840s: Dickensian gloom - Scrooge's counting-house fixes the image as cold, miserly, Christmas-denying; public perception darkens to greed and chill.
- Early 1900s: obsolete survival - offices with telephones and typewriters replace quills and stools; counting-house sounds quaint, spoken with nostalgia or scorn.
- Present: heritage word - heard in Dickens adaptations, historic plaques, and moral metaphor; perception is top-hat London, ink and ledger, a museum of capitalism rather than a workplace.
