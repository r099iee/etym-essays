- Late 17th Century: French fairy-tale coinage and literary debut - Popularized by Charles Perrault in 1697 (Histoires ou contes du temps passé) as a grotesque man-eating forest giant.
- Early 18th Century: English adoption and nursery-tale sensation - Translated into English in the 1720s, rapidly embedding itself alongside native giants and bugbears in British folklore.
- 19th Century: Figurative political invective and societal personification - Applied to tyrannical monarchs, merciless schoolmasters, and coercive institutions (Napoleon was demonized as the 'Corsican Ogre').
- 20th to 21st Century: Subversive popular culture revision and bureaucratic metaphor - Reimagined sympathetically in post-modern fiction (e.g., Shrek), while remaining an evocative metaphor for oppressive, faceless corporate or state apparatuses.
