Introduction. Mound is a young word with an old problem: it appears in English only around 1500, and its origin is genuinely obscure. Yet in five centuries it has grown from a Scottish word for a hedge into one of the language's most useful topographic nouns — the word for burial barrows, castle mottes, pitcher's hills, ant hills, and dragon-hoards of gold. Its history is chiefly semantic: a word of enclosures became a word of earthworks, then of hills, then of any heaped mass — each step a widening of the same image of raised, protective, heaped material.

Phase 1 — the obscure birth (before c. 1500).

  • Mound is first recorded in Scottish texts of about 1500 in the sense "a hedge or fence; a boundary enclosure". The OED marks the origin as unknown, and the two main theories illustrate how uncertain the case is.
  • The most repeated theory links the word to Old English mund "hand; protection, guardianship" (and Middle Dutch mond(e) "protection, defence"): the mound would be "that which protects" — first a protective hedge or fence around a field, then a protective bank of earth around a fort. Under this theory the ultimate source would be the Indo-European root *man- "hand", since the hand is the original instrument of protection (the same root gives Latin manus "hand" and English manual, manage, manure). But the sound development from mund to mound is not regular, and the theory, though often printed, is not proven.
  • A minority view treats mound as an independent coinage for a raised bank, with no protective metaphor at all. Either way, no secure pre-English chain exists; the word's history in English begins at c. 1500.

Phase 2 — from hedge to earthwork (1500–1600).

  • In the 16th century the word moves from enclosure to defence: a mound is an earthen rampart, a raised bank thrown up around a town, fort or camp. Military engineers and chroniclers use it for the earthworks of sieges and the defensive banks of castles.
  • The semantic bridge is simple and physical: a protective hedge and a protective bank of earth do the same job — they mark and defend a boundary — and English let the word follow the material, from thorns to earth.
  • The period also records the word for raised ground in general — the bank of a ditch, the raised bed of a garden — the generalisation that would carry it everywhere.

Phase 3 — the burial and the hillock (1600–1800).

  • By the 17th–18th centuries mound is the standard word for a raised heap of earth, and the age of antiquarianism attaches it to the ancient monuments: burial mounds, barrows and tumuli are "mounds", and the word acquires its lasting undertone of buried kings and hidden graves.
  • The natural sense also firms up: a mound is a hillock, a small rounded rise — the topographic sense that makes "grassy mound" a normal phrase. The word now spans the whole range from natural hillock to artificial rampart to grave.
  • The verb to mound ("to heap up") follows in the 18th–19th centuries, completing the word's grammatical range.

Phase 4 — America and the Mound Builders (1800–1900).

  • The 19th century gives mound its most spectacular chapter in American archaeology: the great earthworks of the Ohio and Mississippi valleys — temple mounds, effigy mounds, conical burial mounds — are attributed to the "Mound Builders", a vanished prehistoric people. The term and the debate dominated 19th-century American archaeology; Squier and Davis's Ancient Monuments of the Mississippi Valley (1848), the first publication of the Smithsonian Institution, is the founding work.
  • The word enters American geography permanently: Mound City, Mound Builders State Memorial, Indian Mound — place names across the Midwest carry the archaeology.
  • Everyday English meanwhile naturalises the heap-sense: ant mounds, molehills, mounds of snow and of provisions are ordinary speech by 1900.

Phase 5 — baseball and the modern senses (1900–present).

  • In the early 20th century the pitcher's mound becomes standard baseball vocabulary — the raised earth in the centre of the diamond from which the pitcher throws; "on the mound" is now the everyday phrase for the pitcher on duty, and the word is one of the most familiar sporting nouns in American English.
  • The figurative heap-sense expands: "a mound of paperwork", "a mound of evidence", "a mound of data" — any large accumulation, the earth-heap image transferred to work, evidence and information.
  • In fantasy and adventure fiction the treasure-mound is a stock image: dragon-hoards are heaped "in mounds", and the dwarves of Tolkien's The Hobbit lift old treasures "from the mound" — the word's ancient burial undertone makes the gold mound feel like a grave being opened.

Phase 6 — today.

  • Mound is fully ordinary English, but its five centuries of meaning are all still active: the hedge is gone from the sense, but the protective earthwork survives (castle mottes), the burial mound survives (archaeology), the hillock survives (landscape), the heap survives (a mound of gold), and baseball has given the old word a new permanent home.
  • Its tone ranges from neutral topographic noun to deliberate evocation of ancient earthworks — writers choose mound over heap or hill when they want the weight of raised, buried, ancient material.

Summary. A word of unknown ancestry that began as a Scottish hedge: in five centuries mound grew from fence to rampart, from rampart to barrow, from barrow to hillock, from hillock to heap — and finally to the pitcher's hill and the dragon's gold. Its history is a single widening circle of raised earth, still carrying the memory of everything English has ever heaped up.
