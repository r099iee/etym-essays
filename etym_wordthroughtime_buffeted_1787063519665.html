- 13th century - the noun buffet 'a blow, a cuff' enters Middle English from Old French bufe - physical violence with the hand or fist
- 14th century - the verb to buffet 'to strike, knock about' is established; Chaucer and his contemporaries use both noun and verb for blows
- 17th century - extends to wind and waves: ships and shores are 'buffeted by the storm'; the Authorised Version (1611) uses 'buffeted' for being struck and harassed, and Milton applies it to fate
- 18th-19th centuries - the figurative sense is fixed: a person 'buffeted by fortune' or 'buffeted by the world' is knocked about by life itself
- 20th-21st centuries - the modern triple life: literal (wind, weather, seas, crowds), journalistic-figurative (governments, companies, celebrities 'buffeted by' criticism, recession, scandal), and literary; 'bruised and buffeted' becomes a fixed pair for thorough knocking about
