• Distended: Similar because both mean stretched outward by internal pressure; differs because distended is clinical and suggests hollow organs stretched tight (a distended stomach), where swollen covers flesh, water, wood, and figures alike.
  • Engorged: Similar because both mean swollen with fluid; differs because engorged specifies the fluid (usually blood) and the organ (a blood-engorged spleen), a medical-technical word without swollen's wide reach.
  • Bloated: Similar because both mean puffed beyond normal size; differs because bloated implies gas, overeating, or a corpse's decay — a sickly or unpleasant fullness — while swollen is neutral and can be clinical, watery, or proud.
  • Puffed (up): Similar because both mean enlarged by air or inflation; differs because puffed (up) is lighter and often figurative of vanity ("puffed up with pride"), less weighty and less medical than swollen.
  • Inflated: Similar because both mean blown up beyond normal size; differs because inflated implies gas or rhetoric (an inflated ego, inflated prices) and carries deliberate excess, where swollen suggests natural or pathological pressure.
  • Enlarged: Similar because both mean made larger; differs because enlarged is a general, neutral word for any increase in size (an enlarged heart), without the specific image of pressure and distension that swollen carries.
  • Expanded: Similar because both mean grown larger; differs because expanded is the abstract, technical member (expanded metal, expanded text), with none of the bodily or watery imagery of swollen.
  • Puffy: Similar because both describe soft swelling; differs because puffy suggests light, soft, superficial swelling (puffy eyes), while swollen can be hard, heavy, and dangerous.
  • Tumid: Similar because both mean swollen; differs because tumid is the learned word, used medically of tissues and rhetorically of inflated style ("tumid prose") — rare in ordinary speech where swollen rules.
  • Turgid: Similar because both mean swollen; differs because turgid is the technical word for fluid-filled congestion (and for swollen, pompous style) — more scientific and more critical than swollen's neutrality.
  • Oedematous / Edematous: Similar because both describe fluid-caused swelling; differs because oedematous is the strict clinical term for oedema (fluid retention), restricted to medicine, while swollen is the everyday word for the same condition.
  • Bulging: Similar because both mean protruding beyond the normal surface; differs because bulging emphasizes outward protrusion of a part (bulging eyes, a bulging sack), while swollen emphasizes overall distension by internal pressure.
  • Tumescent: Similar because both mean swollen or becoming swollen; differs because tumescent is the technical term (used of erectile tissue, and of 18th-century "tumescence" in rhetoric) and, in modern popular usage, has a specifically sexual register that swollen never has.
  • Exaggerated / Magnified: Similar because both, like the figurative "swollen", mean made greater than the truth; differs because they name the act of misrepresentation, while "swollen" in such uses still roots the inflation in the old image of a body or river pressed large — more concrete, less deliberately false.
