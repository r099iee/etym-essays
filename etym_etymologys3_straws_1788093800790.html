1. The root and the meaning “what is strewn”. Straw descends from PIE *ster- ‘to spread out, strew, scatter’, one of the most productive roots in the family: it yields Latin sternere ‘to spread, strew’ (whence English stratum, prostrate, substrate), strāmen ‘straw, litter, bedding’ (strāmentum), Greek στρώννυμι (strōnnymi) ‘to spread’, στρῶμα (strōma) ‘bedding, mattress’ (whence struma, and the modern sense of mattress via Arabic), and the Slavic spread-words (Russian straná ‘country’, originally ‘spread-out land’). In Germanic the same root, extended with -w-, gives the verb strew (OE strēowian) and, in its o-grade, the noun: PGmc *strawa- ‘straw, that which is strewn’, whence Old English strēaw, Old Saxon strō, Old High German strō (modern German Stroh), Old Norse strá. Etymologically, then, a straw is at bottom ‘the strewn thing’ — the stalks spread out under animals, on floors, or over roofs.

2. Old English. In Old English strēaw (plural strēaw(u), eventually regularised) is an everyday word for the threshing residue, appearing in the glosses and in agricultural and housekeeping contexts: strewn as litter under horses and cattle, mixed into fodder, used as thatch and as cheap bedding; the cognate verb strēowian ‘to strew’ is frequent. The diphthong ēa (a front-back glide) is the regular development of the Germanic o-grade *straw-, and medieval scribes will later spell the word streawe, straue, stro.

3. Middle English: the age of proverbs. Middle English straw(e) keeps the material senses and adds the emotional value that dominates perception ever after. The cheapness of straw makes it the standard measure of worthlessness: to care “not a straw” is proverbial by the 13th century (the variant “not worth a stree” is common in Chaucer's circle), and Chaucer himself uses straw as the type of the worthless and the disposable. Straw also appears as the type of fragility — a straw bends and breaks, and the medieval similes of the weak body (thin as a straw; a leg like a straw) begin here; it is from this stock that the Tolkien simile of the present card ultimately comes.

4. The 16th century: politics of the wind. Two modern idioms fix their roots here: “a drowning man catches at a straw” (recorded in English by the 1530s as a proverb about desperate and useless hope) and “straws in the wind”, the scattered stalks showing the direction of the wind and hence, metaphorically, of events (late 16th century). Both use the plural form and both are meteorological or maritime in imagery, matching the straw's lightness: it is blown about, carried, revealing. Meanwhile “not worth a straw” remains current in parliamentary and religious polemic of the Reformation, where opponents' arguments are dismissed as straw.

5. The 17th–18th centuries: the straw man and the straw hat. The 17th century adds the compound “straw man” (or “man of straw”): in legal slang, a person with no real means who is set up as a surety or nominal litigant — a hollow stand-in, like a scarecrow; by the 19th–20th centuries this becomes the rhetorical straw man, an easily demolished misrepresentation of an opponent's argument. On the fashion side, the straw-hat industry booms (the boater becomes a gentleman's classic); straw-yellow becomes a colour name; and straw appears in viticulture (straw wine, grapes dried on mats).

6. The 19th century: the drinking tube and the last straw. The decisive innovation is the tube. Hollow rye-grass stems have served as drinking conduits for millennia (Sumerian beer straws of gold and reed from Ur, c. 2500 BCE; Chinese grass-stem noodles), but the commercial straw is a 19th-century product: “straws” (properly rye straws) are advertised and sold for sipping from the 1850s, and Marvin Stone's spiral-wound paper straw, patented in January 1888, industrialises them. The same century fixes “draw straws” (decision by lots, pulling varying lengths) and “the last straw (that breaks the camel's back)” — Dickens uses “the last straw” in Dombey and Son (1848) — binding the plural firmly to fateful, fragile contingency.

7. The 20th–21st centuries: plastic, then its backlash. Plastic straws conquer the world from the 1950s–60s (the flexible bendy straw was patented in 1937 by Joseph Friedman), and “straws” become the default term for the disposable sipping tube — a box of straws, straws in the soft-drink glass. The environmental movement of the late 2010s reverses the perception: from icon of convenience to icon of waste, prompting bans and paper/bamboo/metal/glass alternatives. Throughout, the agricultural and proverbial lives of the word continue unchanged, and the simile of the present passage — legs like straws — remains the standard image for limbs that will no longer hold a body up.
