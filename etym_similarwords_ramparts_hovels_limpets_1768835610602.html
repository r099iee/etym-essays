• Parapet (ramparts): Similar because both are part of the defensive works of a fortress; differs because a parapet names only the low protective wall at the top of a rampart or along a ledge, not the whole fortified circuit.
  • Wall (ramparts): Similar because both are vertical barriers of stone; differs because wall is generic and neutral, lacking rampart's military mass, earthwork, and preparation-against-attack.
  • Bulwark (ramparts): Similar because both are defensive structures against attack; differs because bulwark in modern use is mostly metaphorical, standing for any source of protection, whereas a rampart is a physical embankment.
  • Earthwork (ramparts): Similar because both denote raised embankments of soil; differs because earthwork is an engineering term for any mound, while a rampart is specifically the defensive embankment of a fortification.
  • Bastion (ramparts): Similar because both belong to fortress defences; differs because a bastion is a projecting angle of a fortification designed for crossfire, while a rampart is the continuous surrounding embankment.
  • Fortification (ramparts): Similar because both are defensive constructions; differs because fortification is the general term for any defensive work or the art of fortifying, while rampart names the encircling wall itself.
  • Breastwork (ramparts): Similar because both are shoulder-high defensive banks; differs because a breastwork is a hasty field fortification of earth or sandbags, while a rampart is a permanent stone-and-earth circuit.
  • Shack (hovels): Similar because both are crude, rough dwellings; differs because shack is neutral or slightly picturesque (a beach shack), while hovel carries the judgment of squalor and wretchedness.
  • Hut (hovels): Similar because both are small simple dwellings; differs because hut is a plain functional shelter (a mountain hut), while hovel implies ruin, dirt, and barely human habitation.
  • Shanty (hovels): Similar because both name the dwellings of the poor; differs because shanty belongs to improvised settlements and slums, while hovel stresses the ruinous smallness of a single dwelling.
  • Shed (hovels): Similar because both are rough outbuildings; differs because a shed is a working or storage building, while a hovel is a dwelling degraded to the level of a shed.
  • Slum dwelling (hovels): Similar because both describe wretched housing; differs because slum names the district or the condition of overcrowded housing, while hovel is the single contemptible dwelling.
  • Pigsty (hovels): Similar because both name filthy, animal-grade housing; differs because pigsty is deliberately insulting and implies filth as a moral condition, while hovel is the chronicler's colder verdict.
  • Barnacle (limpets): Similar because both are marine creatures that cling to rocks and ship hulls; differs because a barnacle is a crustacean that fixes itself by cement, while a limpet is a snail that grips by muscular suction.
  • Mussel (limpets): Similar because both are clinging shellfish of the shore; differs because mussels fasten by byssus threads in clusters, while a limpet is a single conical shell that clamps flat to the rock.
  • Sucker (limpets): Similar because both describe gripping adhesion; differs because sucker is a slang term for a gullible person, while limpet keeps the patient, immovable tenacity of the animal.
  • Clingfish (limpets): Similar because both grip rocks tenaciously; differs because clingfish are fish with sucker-like fins, while limpets are snails with a single muscular foot.
  • Mine (limpets — the limpet mine): Similar because the limpet mine is an adhesive explosive attached to ship hulls and named for the mollusc's grip; differs because mine is the general term for explosive devices, while 'limpet' specifies the clinging, underwater type.
