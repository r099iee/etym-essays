- 8th century, Old High German - brōt names the baked loaf and, by extension, the food that sustains life; it is the everyday staple of the monastic and peasant table.
- 12th–14th century, Middle High German - brōt covers both the loaf and the means of living; courtly texts use bread as the mark of humble fare against aristocratic meat.
- 16th century, Reformation - Luther's Bible fixes “Unser täglich Brot gib uns heute” and “Der Mensch lebt nicht vom Brot allein”, giving the word its permanent sacramental and scriptural charge.
- 17th–18th century - Brotberuf, Brotneid and Brotgeber make the word a normal term of trade and subsistence; Brot und Spiele enters political rhetoric as a rendering of Juvenal.
- 19th century, industrialisation - bread prices become a revolutionary issue; Brot stands for the minimum wage of survival, and Brotkorb and Brotgetreide enter the language of agricultural policy.
- early 20th century, war and inflation - rationed bread and the Brotkarte make the word a memory of scarcity; Abendbrot consolidates its meaning as the evening meal.
- late 20th century, consumer society - industrial sliced bread and the health-food movement split the word between convenience and authenticity; Vollkornbrot and Sauerteigbrot become markers of taste.
- 2014–present - German bread culture is inscribed on the national list of intangible cultural heritage, and artisanal sourdough turns Brot into a symbol of craft, tradition and local identity.
