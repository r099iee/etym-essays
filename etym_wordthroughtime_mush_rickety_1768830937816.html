- 17th century – 'mush' appears as an English dialectal variant of 'mash' (from Old English māscan, 'to crush into pulp'), meaning boiled cornmeal porridge — a plain, rustic American breakfast food.
- 19th century – 'mush' becomes a staple of the American frontier and camp cooking; alongside the cereal, the noun spreads to mean any soft, pulpy, shapeless mass — matter that has lost all internal structure, that yields, flows, and leaves a stain.
- 20th century – the figurative extension arrives: 'mushy' means cloyingly sentimental, a smothering softness of feeling, and 'mush' denotes mawkish romance or undemanding material; the food sense remains, but the connotation now runs from cozy to contemptuous.
- 21st century – 'mush' survives as regional porridge and as metaphor for anything pulpy, formless, or soft-headed; the word has completed a drift from plain frontier food to a term for shapelessness of matter and of feeling.

- rickety: 17th century – from 'rickets', the children's disease of softened, bowed bones; 'rickety' first means affected with or characteristic of that disease.
- 19th–21st centuries – the medical sense recedes and the commonest sense takes over: a structure, body, or piece of furniture that is loosely jointed, weak, unsound, and liable to collapse — the bony weakness of rickets transferred to wobbly objects and shaky institutions.
