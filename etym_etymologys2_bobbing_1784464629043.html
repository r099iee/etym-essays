Deep stage: unknown; no secure PIE reconstruction exists for the motion verb. Middle English bobben is of obscure origin; the mainstream account treats it as imitative or expressive — the short, quick, bouncing motion or a light blow — reinforced by the noun bob (a blow; a hanging bunch or pendant). [DEBATED — see note below] → Middle English bobben (to strike, buffet, pound; to move jerkily; also to mock, deceive), attested from the 14th century CE; the noun bob (a blow; a rounded lump or hanging bunch) reinforced the verb → Modern English bob (to move up and down or to and fro quickly and lightly; to cut hair short; to make a quick curtsy), from the 16th century CE → Modern English BOBBING — the card's target form: the present participle and gerund expressing ongoing short, quick, repeated up-and-down motion. NOTE on the debate: a Celtic origin (e.g. Irish bob "a bunch, cluster, tassel") has been suggested for some bob senses, and an Old French influence (bobe/bobance "mockery, deception") has been claimed for the "mock, deceive" senses of bobben, but no single source is proven for all uses. The various bob senses (float on water, cut hair short, plumb-bob, curtsy, slang shilling, bobbin) share the notion of shortness, quickness, or a compact hanging object, but they branch in different directions; the motion sense is a native Middle English development. The word is unrelated to French bon or to the personal name Bob.
