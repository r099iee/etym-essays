Glum is an adjective applied to a person, or to a face, expression, or demeanour, meaning sullen, morose, dejected, and silently gloomy: low in spirits, downcast, and given to a heavy, staring, brooding silence rather than to spoken complaint. It denotes the quiet, self-contained form of unhappiness — the mood of one who has nothing to say and says it with a darkened brow. Derived forms include the adverb glumly and the noun glumness, and the word is characteristically used in collocations such as glum silence, a glum face, or to look glum. In register it is a plain, demotic word of the middle ground between formal and slangy speech, and it retains a faintly northern, dialectal flavour inherited from its Scottish and Low German connections.

Polysemy: the full semantic range.

  • Primary sense (personal demeanour): sullen, morose, dejected, silently gloomy; low in spirits with a darkened, downcast look — "a glum expression", "to sit glum", "a glum face".
  • Sense applied to things and scenes: of weather, light, rooms, or atmospheres, dull, gloomy, and lowering — a "glum" sky or a "glum" landscape partakes of the word's original sense of darkness, though this usage is rarer than the personal one.
  • Collocational senses: glum silence, a glum face, to look glum, to sit glum — the word's fixed companions all stress the silent, passive quality of the mood; a glum person is not complaining, merely brooding.
  • Derived and colloquial forms: glumly (adverb), glumness (noun), the colloquial extension glump, the glumps "a fit of sulkiness", and the Scottish glunch (a sulky, glum look or fit).

The sense in the context. In the passage from "Roast Mutton", Tolkien writes: "There they all sat glum and wet and muttering, while Oin and Gloin went on trying to light the fire, and quarrelling about it." The three adjectives are carefully ordered: glum names the inner state — the sullen, silent dejection of thirteen drenched dwarves — while wet gives its physical cause and muttering its only audible outlet. Glum is placed first precisely because it is the atmospheric key to the whole sentence: the company has been soaked by the storm, the fire will not catch, and the travellers have subsided into a passive, brooding misery in which even quarrels are reduced to muttering. The word thus anchors the reader's sense of the scene: not angry, not despairing, but quietly, heavily, soddenly out of sorts, sitting in the dark and the damp with nothing better to do than mutter.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. Glum is usefully distinguished from its near-synonyms. Sullen implies a resentful, hostile brooding that may break out in sulks or bad temper; morose suggests a bitter, ill-tempered gloominess that colours one's dealings with others; dejected is sadder but softer — the spirits cast down by disappointment, without the heavy, lowered look that glum implies; melancholy adds a pensive, reflective tinge; gloomy leans toward darkness of atmosphere as much as of mood; downcast focuses on the dropped gaze; dour is Scottish and implies a grim, unyielding severity; lugubrious is theatrical in its mournfulness; crestfallen pictures the visibly deflated, dejected posture. Glum sits among them as the plainest, quietest, most silent word — unhappiness without complaint, a temporary sourness of spirit rather than a lasting darkness.
