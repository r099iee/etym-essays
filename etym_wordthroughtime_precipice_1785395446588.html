- [Borrowed c. 1590 from Middle French precipice - “a sheer cliff”] - From the first a grave, dramatic word naming a steep place where one falls headlong; the Latin falling head (praeceps, “head-first”) is felt inside it from the moment English takes it up.
- [17th century - the figurative sense grows] - “On the precipice of” ruin, war or disaster appears beside the literal cliff; the word begins to describe any situation balanced on the brink of catastrophe.
- [18th–19th centuries] - The metaphorical life increasingly dominates; precipice becomes a favourite word of Romantic and sublime landscape writing and of the vocabulary of epic heights and abysses.
- [Modern register] - The figurative sense may now lead: the word is as likely to describe a bank's balance sheet as a mountain wall; it remains formal, literary and epic, while ordinary speech prefers cliff or drop and reserves precipice for writing that wants gravity.
