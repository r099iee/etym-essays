The story of highth begins with the Indo-European root that lies behind the adjective high itself. Comparative evidence from the Germanic branch — Gothic hauhs, Old Norse hár, Old English hēah — points back to a Proto-Germanic adjective *hauhaz "high," which in turn is usually traced to a PIE root *kowk- "to swell, become strong." The idea is that "high" originally named something swollen up or raised above its surroundings, a physical image of protrusion. It must be stressed that this root survives clearly only in Germanic and in a few Baltic words (Lithuanian kaũkas "a bump, swelling"); its reconstruction is therefore less secure than that of roots with many daughter reflexes, and some handbooks treat the shape *kowk- as a Germanic-internal formation. The sound shifts involved are instructive: Grimm's Law turned the PIE voiceless stop *k into the Germanic fricative *h, and the vowel *ow leveled to *au, giving *hauh-. This is one of the earliest sound changes in the history of English, and it is preserved invisibly in every form of the word.

From the adjective the Germanic languages created a noun meaning "the state of being high" by adding the abstract-noun suffix *-iþō: Proto-Germanic *hauhiþō, which yields Gothic hauhiþa and Old Norse hæð as well as Old English hēahþu (West Saxon) or hīehþu (Anglian). The suffix *-iþō is the ancestor of the English -th in length, breadth, width, depth, strength, warmth, and it is precisely this suffix that makes highth visibly "high + -th." In Old English the word is fully productive: hēahþu means both the concrete "height, high place" and the abstract "exalted condition."

Middle English was a period of radical phonological and orthographic upheaval, and hēahþu emerged as a tangle of spellings — heihte, heghte, heighte, hyht, hihte — as the final -u was lost, the diphthong simplified, and the spelling system (largely French-trained scribes) hunted for ways to render the Old English sound. Out of this turbulence two competing lines crystallized. One, the regular descendant, is modern height, which has the smooth, reduced vowel and consonant cluster of the ordinary sound-change track. The other is highth, a re-formation made by speakers who, in effect, looked at the adjective high and simply glued on the familiar -th suffix the way they did for breadth and width. Analogy of this kind is one of the great engines of English morphology: the transparent pattern "adjective + -th = abstract noun" overrode the irregular historical form.

Both spellings competed in print for more than a century. Shakespeare uses highth ("on the highth of our mirth," The Tempest), and the Authorized Version of the Bible, printed in 1611, uses it too. But the most famous instance is Milton's, in the opening of Paradise Lost (1667), where the poet prays to be raised "to the highth of this great Argument." Here the archaic spelling is not an accident of printing but a choice: Milton, whose spelling habits were conservative and deliberate, uses highth to give the word an antique, dignified elevation matching the "highth" of his theme — the very pinnacle of Christian epic above the pagan Helicon. The form thus became permanently associated with solemn, archaic, elevated diction.

Why did highth lose, and height win? In part the victory of height reflects the general Early Modern regularization of spelling as dictionaries and printing houses standardized forms; in part it reflects the spelling height's preservation of the historical medial consonant, which kept the word aligned with the attested Germanic line. By the 18th century highth was a conscious archaism, and Dr. Johnson's dictionary (1755) recorded height as the standard while noting the older form. In the centuries since, highth has had a purely literary afterlife: it surfaces in archaizing verse, in scholarly editions of Milton and Shakespeare, and in titles that reach for a 17th-century flavor.

Summary of the evolutionary chain:

  • Sound change: PIE *k → PGmc *h (Grimm's Law); *ow → *au; OE breaking *au → ēa; Middle English monophthongization and vowel reduction; modern loss of the final fricative cluster in the standard form.
  • Morphology: *-iþō abstract suffix → OE -þu → ME -t(e) → modern -t; and, in the rival line, the analogical re-attachment of -th to high.
  • Semantic drift: "swollen, raised" → "tall" → "the state/degree of being tall" → "summit" (metonymy) → "utmost degree" (spatial metaphor) → "sublime height of an argument" (literary register).
  • Socio-cultural factor: the 17th-century print standardization of spelling, and the 18th-century dictionary tradition, fixed height as the standard while highth became a marker of deliberately archaic, poetic style.
  • Key attestations: Old English hēahþu (Beowulf-era prose); Chaucer's heighte; Shakespeare's highth; Authorized Version 1611; Milton, Paradise Lost I.24 (1667); Johnson's Dictionary (1755).
