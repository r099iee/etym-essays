Gobs as used in the Wodehouse sentence means large amounts, heaps, or plenty of something, especially of an intangible quality such as talent, courage, money, charm, or stuffing in the sense of ability and character. When someone says he has the stuff in gobs, they mean he possesses it abundantly, in overflowing measure, far more than the ordinary share. It is an emphatically colloquial, hearty, slightly comic intensifier that belongs to informal British and Anglo-American slang of the early to mid-twentieth century.

The singular form gob has two major modern noun senses that converge in the plural. The first and oldest in English is a lump, clod, mass, or mouthful, as in a gob of butter, a gob of spit, or gobs of paint. From this concrete sense of a shapeless lump comes the extended quantitative sense of a lot, a large indefinite amount. The second major sense is the mouth itself, especially in British slang: shut your gob, meaning shut your mouth. This mouth sense also yields gob as spittle, and in naval and working-class slang, gob as a sailor, originally gob-shite and gob meaning mouth, hence one who uses his mouth for shouting orders or swallowing. The plural gobs therefore carries several layers at once: mouthfuls, lumps, spit-balls, sailors, and by metaphorical inflation, abundantly large amounts.

In contemporary usage, gobs occurs almost always in the plural and in fixed collocations:

  • Gobs of something: gobs of money, gobs of talent, gobs of time, gobs of food, signalling lavish abundance, often with a slightly vulgar, exuberant energy. Example: She has gobs of charm.
  • Gobs as exclamation of quantity: He has it in gobs, They made gobs, meaning in great profusion, without specifying units.
  • Concrete lumps: gobs of clay, gobs of grease, gobs of phlegm, retaining the original physical sense of sticky lumps or expectorated masses.
  • Mouth-related slang: less common in the plural, but gobs can still evoke mouths in phrases like stuff your gob or shut your gobs in dialectal use.
  • American naval slang: gobs as plural of gob, a sailor, especially a US Navy enlisted man in World War I and World War II era slang, as in a bunch of gobs on leave.

The polysemy bridge from mouth to lump to lot is natural and well-attested in lexical history. The mouth is the measurer of mouthfuls: what fills the mouth is a gob, a gobful, a gobbet. A mouthful is inherently a lump, an irregular bolus of food, hence gob acquires the sense of any lump or clod resembling what could be held in the mouth. Once gob means lump, pluralising and inflating it gives gobs, meaning many lumps, then by hyperbole, any large indeterminate mass. This is the same cognitive path seen in words like lots, heaps, piles, wads, chunks, and masses, where a concrete pile becomes an abstract measure of abundance. In Wodehouse, stuff in gobs therefore plays on schoolboy and clubman slang where physical heartiness, the stuffing inside a fellow, is imagined as a substance that can be ladled out in spoonfuls or mouthfuls.

Register and connotation are crucial. Gobs is low-colloquial, masculine, hearty, anti-pretentious, and slightly comic. It would be out of place in formal prose, academic writing, or solemn elegy, but perfectly at home in comic novels, barrack-room talk, sports commentary, music-hall patter, and affectionate praise. It conveys admiration without reverence, abundance without precision. To say he has brains in gobs is warmer, more slangy, and more emphatically English than to say he is highly intelligent. It also implies a kind of unrefined plenty, abundance that is almost wasteful or overflowing, not carefully counted. In the Wodehouse universe, where understatement and slangy overstatement alternate for comic effect, gobs signals the narrator is a cheery insider, a man of the world who measures character in handfuls rather than in psychological jargon.

Domain-specific nuances include mining and industrial use, where gob or goaf means waste material, rubble, or caved-in refuse left in a mine, and by extension any dumped mass. In printing and nautical contexts, gob can mean a small mass of ink, tar, or grease. In American English, gobs often appears as gobs and gobs, a reduplicative intensifier meaning extremely large amounts. The phrase gob-stopper, a large hard candy that fills the mouth and stops chatter, preserves the same nexus of mouth plus lump plus large size. Understanding gobs therefore requires holding together mouth, lump, sailor, and lot as facets of one exuberant slang family, with Wodehouse selecting the lot sense but letting the hearty, mouthy resonance colour the praise.
