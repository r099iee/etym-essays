muslin is a lightweight, plain-weave cotton cloth with a soft, matte, open, and breathable texture, made from finely spun cotton yarn. In its classic form it is fine, slightly translucent, and drapes softly; it has served since the 17th century as everything from a luxury dress fabric to a practical household and medical textile. The word descends from the name of the city of Mosul (Arabic al-Mawṣil) in present-day northern Iraq, the great medieval trading hub through which these fine fabrics reached European markets — an example of a word born not of a root but of a place.

Contextual reading: in this card's passage — 'The cottage casement with choice muslin gleams' — the muslin is the fine curtain cloth at the window, its soft gleam standing for the fleeting elegance and refinement that the poem sets against cobwebs, abandoned halls, and the grave. It is a fabric of light and delicacy, present to underline what is lost.

Core senses:

  • Textile sense — plain-weave cotton cloth: a finely woven, light cotton fabric used for dresses, blouses, curtains, underlinen, babies' clothing, and embroidery grounds; weights range from sheer 'book muslin' to firmer 'jaconet' and 'mull.'
  • Dressmaking and theatrical sense: cheap or unbleached muslin is the standard material for pattern-making and mock-ups (French toile), and for theatrical backdrops, scrims, and wings.
  • Culinary sense (French mousseline): in cooking, mousseline denotes a light, airy preparation — a sauce made frothy with whipped cream or egg white, and mousseline potatoes (an ultra-fine purée); these extensions arrived through French haute cuisine, using the fabric's name to mean 'as light and fine as muslin.'
  • Household-utility sense: open-weave muslin is used for straining liquids, filtering, covering dough, making bags, bandaging, and as a gauze substitute in cooking, cheesemaking, and brewing.
  • Historical sense — luxury Indian cotton: in the 17th and 18th centuries the finest Bengali muslins, especially those of Dacca, were celebrated as 'woven air' — extremely fine, semi-transparent, and extravagantly expensive; muslin was then a princely import, not a utility cloth.
  • Figurative and cultural sense: in 19th-century Britain 'muslin' became a byword for young women and feminine gentility — 'the muslin ranks,' 'a bit of muslin' — carrying connotations of modesty, lightness, innocence, and transient beauty.

Semantic bridges:

  • The toponymic bridge: fabrics bought in the markets of Mosul were named after the city by Italian merchants (mussolo, mussolina), and the name stuck even after Europeans learned that the cloth was woven far away in India — the market, not the loom, gave the word.
  • The luxury-to-utility bridge: industrial cotton spinning in the late 18th and 19th centuries cheapened muslin from an exotic import into an everyday commodity, and the word broadened to cover coarser plain-weave cottons and utility grades.
  • The culinary bridge: French mousseline applied the fabric's name to airy sauces and purées, so that in cookery the word means 'whipped, frothy, feather-light.'

Register and collocations:

  • Common collocations: 'white muslin,' 'fine muslin,' 'muslin curtain,' 'muslin dress,' 'muslin bag,' 'muslin gauze.'
  • Modern usage: the word remains in everyday use for a light cotton cloth, but its luxurious register now survives mainly in historical and dressmaking contexts.
