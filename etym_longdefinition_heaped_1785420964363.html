HEAPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to heap: gathered, piled, or accumulated into a mound or mass; loaded or covered in abundance. The core image is that of many things thrown or laid together in a single, usually tall and somewhat disorderly pile — a heap — rather than arranged in a neat order. A table heaped with books, a plate heaped with food, coals heaped on a fire: the participle insists on bulk, generosity, and often a mild excess. In the passage from William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, HEAPED appears in the phrase "heaped up and published": the young scholar proposes that all the possible mistakes along his line of inquiry ought to be gathered into one great, conspicuous pile and published, so that one could sit back and wait to see which were the real mistakes later on. The tone is deliberately playful and unceremonious — the very opposite of guarded academic caution — dumping theoretical errors into a heap rather than selecting, sorting, or concealing them. The senses of HEAPED branch into a clear family:
  • Piled high (literal). Objects placed or thrown together in a mass, especially a tall, rounded, or disorderly one: leaves heaped along the fence, stones heaped in a cairn, snow heaped by the plow.
  • Loaded or covered (with). The passive construction "heaped with" means laden or piled over with abundance: the table was heaped with manuscripts, a plate heaped with rice, the altar heaped with offerings.
  • Measured (cooking and commerce). In recipes, "a heaped spoonful" or "a heaped teaspoon" is a measure piled well above the rim of the spoon, the opposite of a level or rounded measure — a precise, quasi-technical culinary sense.
  • Figurative abundance (praise, blame, gifts). To heap praise, blame, abuse, honours, or gifts upon someone is to pour them out in overwhelming quantity: she heaped praise upon the orchestra, heaps of criticism.
  • Accumulation of abstract things (errors, dangers, evidence). To heap up mistakes, problems, or arguments is to gather every instance into a single mass, often to expose or evaluate them — exactly Empson's "heaped up and published."
  • As noun phrase. "A heap of" informally means a large amount: a heap of trouble, a heap of work.
Modern jargons and metaphoric extensions:
  • Computing. A "heap" is a region of dynamically allocated memory, and "heap corruption" or "heap overflow" are programming terms; the image of an unorganized pile of allocated blocks preserves the word's core disorder.
  • Computer science. A "binary heap" is a tree-based data structure, named for the pile-like ordering it maintains; "heapsort" is the sorting algorithm built on it.
  • Colloquial and gaming English. A "corpse heap," "junk heap," or "scrap heap" names a mound of discarded things; "at the top of the heap" and "bottom of the heap" metaphorize social rank as a pile.
  • Archaeology and funerary practice. A heaped mound of earth over a grave is a tumulus or barrow; "kitchen midden" names a heaped refuse dump, and heap is the standard word for such mounds of debris and burial.
The historical bridge from "pile of matter" to "multitude" and "abundance" is ancient: Old English hēap already meant both a physical pile and a crowd or troop of people, so that the word carried the abstract idea of a large aggregate — a heap of men — nearly from the start. In Empson the image of heaping errors into print dramatizes intellectual courage: expose everything at once, in public, in a pile, and let the real mistakes reveal themselves later.
