• blast: Similar because both are mild imprecations of annoyance ("blast it!", "drat it!"); differs because blast keeps a residual violent image, literally summoning an explosion or a blight, whereas drat, a bleached "God rot", has no destructive image left at all.
  • bother: Similar because both are mild exclamations of irritation; differs because bother is milder, more patient, and less archaic, the exclamation of mere inconvenience ("bother it!"), while drat is comic, theatrical, and period-flavoured, with the fossilised shape of a real curse.
  • confound: Similar because both are used as imprecations ("confound it!", "confound him!"); differs because confound is a learned-sounding, heavier word that genuinely means to bring to confusion or ruin, and thus keeps more force than the wholly harmless drat.
  • confusticate: Similar because both are playful, euphemistic pseudo-oaths, and Tolkien himself uses "confusticate him!" in the same chapter of The Hobbit; differs because confusticate is a jocular nonsense coinage, mock-Latin modelled on confound and confuse, with no real source, whereas drat is a genuine, if disguised, descendant of "God rot".
  • damn: Similar because both are curses, and drat is historically a bleached substitute for a damn-like imprecation; differs because damn is a true profanity invoking eternal punishment, while drat is a euphemism that has stripped the oath of all religious or destructive force.
  • darn: Similar because both are minced oaths formed from stronger swearwords, darn from damn; differs because darn is American, modern, and still used as a mild intensifier ("darn it!", "darn good"), while drat is British-flavoured, archaic, theatrical, and confined to gentle mock-cursing.
  • dang: Similar because both are mild minced oaths of annoyance; differs because dang, from damn, is modern colloquial and chiefly North American, with no literary or period flavour, whereas drat carries the costume of Victorian comedy.
  • curse: Similar because both can denote an utterance of anger toward someone; differs because curse is the strong, general word for invoking harm or evil, capable of real malevolence, while drat is a deliberately weakened pseudo-curse, the form of an imprecation drained of its content.
  • swear: Similar because both involve uttering oaths; differs because swear is the broad, neutral verb for profane or solemn utterance, while drat is a specific euphemistic interjection, one particular species of safe swear.
  • gosh / gee: Similar because both are minced oaths, gosh for God, gee for Jesus, that preserve an exclamatory gasp without profanity; differs because gosh and gee are exclamations of surprise or mild emphasis rather than of directed annoyance, and never take an object, whereas "drat him!" has no gosh or gee equivalent.
  • fiddlesticks: Similar because both are dated, comic exclamations dismissing something as nonsense or annoyance; differs because fiddlesticks is an interjection of dismissal or disbelief, "nonsense!", rather than an imprecation aimed at a person, and has no verb use.
  • botheration: Similar because both are Victorian-sounding exclamations of mild irritation; differs because botheration, bother + -ation, is a jocular pseudo-formal word of mere inconvenience, while drat is a disguised curse aimed at someone or something.
  • hang it: Similar because both are dated mild oaths of annoyance; differs because hang it is a separate minced or euphemistic imprecation, from a literal hang image and ultimately a euphemism, while drat descends specifically from "God rot".
  • tarnation: Similar because both are euphemistic pseudo-curses, tarnation from damnation; differs because tarnation is American, dialectal, and comic in a different, frontier flavour, while drat is British stage-comic and urban.
