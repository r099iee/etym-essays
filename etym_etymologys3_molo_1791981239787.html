Μόλων is attested as a Greek personal name, borne by several historical figures besides the Rhodian rhetorician, most notably a Seleucid general of the third century BCE who governed the satrapy of Media and rebelled against Antiochus III. The name belongs to a large and productive Greek class: masculine names ending in -ων with a genitive in -ωνος, the same pattern as Πλάτων (Plato), Ζήνων (Zeno), Στράβων (Strabo) and Ἀρίστων (Ariston). The suffix was used both to form descriptive nicknames and to make hypocoristic shortenings of longer compound names, which is why the class is so crowded and why individual etymologies within it are so often irrecoverable.

The problem of the name's meaning. Greek personal names usually say something — Ἀπολλώνιος “belonging to Apollo”, Δημοσθένης “strength of the people” — but the element Μολ- is not a transparent root. Ancient and modern attempts to connect Μόλων with μῶλος, “toil, battle, the press of war” (a Homeric word), run into a phonological obstacle: μῶλος has a long omega, while the name has a short omicron. A connection with μόλις, “with difficulty”, or with a Pre-Greek substrate word, has been suggested but not demonstrated. The honest position is that the name is Greek in phonology and morphology but of unknown semantic content — a common situation with personal names, which are often inherited without their original sense.

From Greek to Latin. Roman writers transliterated the name straightforwardly: Cicero writes Molon and Molonem in the oblique cases, keeping the Greek stem and the final nasal. The Latin poetic and prose tradition therefore fixed the form with -n, and it is this form that passed into the modern scholarly languages: Italian Molone, French Molon, German Molon, English Molon. The shorter Molo arose in English, where Greek names in -ων were sometimes truncated by analogy with Latinised forms and with Italian usage, in which final consonants are regularly dropped.

The person behind the name.

  • Republic: Molon is known chiefly through Cicero, who studied with him on Rhodes in 79 BCE and who credits him with curing the exuberance of his early style. The relationship is one of the best-documented teacher–pupil relations of the late Republic.
  • Empire: Quintilian and later compilers of rhetorical doctrine cite Molon as an authority on style and delivery, often at second hand. Josephus names him among the Greek writers hostile to the Jews, which shows how far his reputation extended beyond technical rhetoric.
  • Renaissance and Enlightenment: editors of Cicero and historians of eloquence treat Molon as a type of the Greek master who civilised Roman prose. Eighteenth-century essays on education invoke him alongside Quintilian.
  • Modern scholarship: the name now appears in prosopographies of Greek rhetoricians, in commentaries on Cicero's Brutus, and in studies of Rhodes as a centre of learning. His lost works are reconstructed from fragments and testimonia.

Sound and spelling in English. English pronunciation varies between the Latinate /ˈməʊ.lɒn/ and the italianate /ˈməʊ.ləʊ/, the latter matching the spelling Molo used in this deck. Neither is wrong: classical names in English have always been pronounced by the conventions of the borrower's schoolroom, and the nineteenth-century habit of anglicising Greek names produced exactly this kind of oscillation.

Unrelated homographs. The string molo also exists independently. In Italian it means “pier, jetty, mole”, from Latin moles “mass, massive structure”, and appears in the names of countless waterfront streets. It is a place name in Kenya (Molo, in Nakuru County) and a district of Iloilo City in the Philippines, and it occurs as a surname in Italy, the Philippines and parts of Africa. These are homographs, not cognates; nothing in them illuminates the Rhodian's name, and the lexicographer should keep them apart.

What the name carries today. In classical prose Molon or Molo denotes a single, sharply defined figure: the Greek rhetorician who taught the two greatest Latin prose stylists of the late Republic. The name functions as a shorthand for that transmission — for the moment when Roman education became, in its highest reaches, a Greek education conducted in Latin.
