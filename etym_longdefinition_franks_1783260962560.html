Franks (plural proper noun) — a confederation of West Germanic tribes who entered Roman history on the lower Rhine in the third century, conquered Roman Gaul under the Merovingian king Clovis in the late fifth century, established the Frankish Empire, and gave their name to France. On this card the word appears in Edward Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, where "the land of the Franks" is the continental homeland across the Channel from Britain, from which, in the dark mythic geography of the pagan North, the spirits of the departed were ferried at midnight to their desolate resting place.

Sense 1 — The tribal confederation and its history. The Franks were not a single tribe but a shifting confederation of West Germanic peoples (with roots among the Salian Franks and the Ripuarian Franks) who coalesced on the lower Rhine in the third century CE. In the late Roman period they served as foederati (federated allies) of the empire while also raiding across its frontiers. Under Clovis I (r. c. 481–511) the Salian Franks conquered most of Gaul, and Clovis's conversion to Catholic Christianity (traditionally dated to about 496) aligned the Franks with the papacy and with the Gallo-Roman population — an alliance that set them apart from the Arian Goths and Lombards. The dynasty he founded, the Merovingians, gave way to the Carolingians, whose greatest king, Charlemagne, was crowned Emperor of the Romans in 800. The Frankish Empire thus stands as the direct ancestor of both France and Germany, and the name Frank was the source of the very word France (from Francia, "the land of the Franks").

Sense 2 — The ethnonym and its "frank" adjective. The tribal name carries a second life as the English adjective frank, "free, candid, open, outspoken". In Late Latin, francus came to mean "free" — a meaning born of the social reality that the conquering Franks, as the ruling class of Gaul, were the free, non-taxed men of the land — and from that Latin word English took frank "free; candid" (as in "to be frank", "frankly speaking"), the verb frank "to stamp a letter free of postage", and franchise (the freedom of a city or trade, via Old French franc). The semantic bridge is literal: the Franks were "the free people", and their name became the word for freedom and then for the outspokenness that freedom licenses.

Sense 3 — The Franks of the East: Faranji. In the medieval Islamic and Byzantine world, "the Franks" (Arabic ifranj / faranji, Greek phrankoi) became the generic name for all Western Europeans — Crusaders, merchants, and pilgrims alike — because the first great Western crusading armies in the 11th–12th centuries were predominantly Frankish. Even today, in the traditional vocabulary of the eastern Mediterranean and South Asia, "Franks" or "Franji" can denote Europeans. This register extends the ethnonym from a single Germanic people to a label for the entire Latin Christian West.

Sense 4 — The name in history-writing and legend. In Gibbon and in the historical tradition, "the Franks" functions as a byword for the vigorous, free, and warlike Germanic peoples who renewed the West after Rome — the founders of the kingdom that became France. In the dark folklore that Gibbon evokes on this card, the "land of the Franks" is the pagan North's land of the living, from which the dead are ferried to the isle of the departed — a mythic geography in which the Franks stand for the unconverted Germanic world beyond the reach of Roman civilization.

Distinguishing note — frank vs frankincense. The adjective frank (free, candid) is genuinely from the Franks' name via Latin francus "free". The unrelated word frankincense (the aromatic gum resin) comes instead from Old French franc encens, "noble incense", where franc means "pure, noble, first-rate" — a different semantic path built on the same Latin base. The two should not be confused: the Franks' name underlies frank "free" and frank "candid", while frankincense rides on the general sense "noble, fine".
