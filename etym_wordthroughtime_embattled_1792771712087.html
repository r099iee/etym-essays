- 14th c. — castle architecture: embatailled first describes walls and towers crowned with battlements — stone prepared for war.
- 16th c. — armies in array: ‘embattled hosts’ march through chronicles and Shakespeare — troops marshalled rank on rank, ready to fight.
- 17th–18th c. — poetic heights: Milton's ‘embattled angels’ and Gray's churchyards keep both senses alive — architecture and armies in verse.
- 19th c. — Emerson's shot: ‘the embattled farmers stood’ (Concord Hymn, 1837) fixes the citizen-soldier image for the English-speaking world.
- 20th c. — the headline sense: newspapers stretch the word to anyone under fire — embattled presidents, embattled currencies, embattled coaches — until the metaphor swallows the battlefield.
- Today — trouble is the battle: nine-tenths of use means ‘beset by critics, lawsuits, or losses’; the castle and the army survive in fantasy, guidebooks, and Tolkien.
