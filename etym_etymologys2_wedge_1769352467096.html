Single root word: wedge (English noun, from Old English wecg, and later also a verb formed by zero-derivation from the noun). The family descends from a Germanic word for a wedge, probably related to a small Indo-European group of words for tapering wooden pins, though the deeper connections are debated and are stated explicitly. Derivation chain: 1. Probable Proto-Indo-European base *wogʰ- / *wag-, "wedge, peg, tapering pin" — reconstructed with caution. NOTE: this root is not securely reconstructable; it is inferred chiefly from the Baltic and Germanic forms and from the Slavic family (e.g. Old Church Slavonic vagy, Russian vaga, "a lever, balance-beam"). Pokorny lists the base, but its exact form, meaning, and even its status as a single PIE root are debated; some scholars treat the Germanic and Baltic words as an independent, possibly expressive group. This is the central debate in the etymology. 2. Proto-Germanic *wagjaz (also reconstructed *waggjaz), "a wedge, a tapering piece of wood or metal" — reconstructed, Proto-Germanic era, c. 500 BCE–c. 200 CE. Attested descendants: Old English wecg, Old Saxon weggia, Middle Dutch wegge, Old High German weggi (modern German Weck is disputed), Old Norse veggr (Swedish vigg, Danish vigge). Baltic cognate: Lithuanian vágis, "a wooden peg, wedge." 3. Old English wecg, "a wedge; also a mass or ingot of metal (a bar of silver or gold struck out into a wedge shape)" — attested from the 8th c. onward (e.g., in glossaries and in the phrase for a "wecg" of precious metal, used as a unit of payment). The metal-ingot sense is a notable Old English development: a bar of silver was literally a wedge of metal. 4. Middle English wegge, "a wedge; a wedge-shaped piece" — attested c. 13th–14th c.; spelling varies (wegge, wedge, weg). 5. Modern English wedge (noun), "the tapering tool; any V-shaped or wedge-shaped object" — the spelling stabilises in the 16th c.; the verb to wedge, "to split or fix with a wedge, to jam tightly," is formed by zero-derivation from the noun and attested from the 16th c. onward. Final node: wedge. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two points are genuinely debated. (1) The deeper Indo-European ancestry: the Germanic and Baltic wedge-words are usually grouped under a base *wogʰ- / *wag-, but whether this is a true PIE root, an independent expressive formation, or connected to PIE *wegʰ- "to move, carry" (the root of way, wagon, weight) is disputed; the OED treats the Germanic group as of uncertain ultimate origin. (2) The Old English metal sense: the "wecg of silver" usage is well attested, but whether the money-sense developed independently in Old English or reflects a wider Germanic pattern is debated. No other stage is in dispute.
