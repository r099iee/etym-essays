- Frizzle (verb) enters English (1590s) as a frequentative of "friz," a French loanword for tight curling of hair — a fashionable, neutral styling word.
- Because curls were once made with hot tongs, "frizzled" slides from "curled by heat" to "scorched so it curls and shrivels" — the singeing sense; by the 19th century it is the ordinary word for heat-shrivelled, singed hair and skin.
- In 1838 the word jumps kitchens: "frizzled" now describes bacon and thin meat fried crisp and curled — the scorch-and-curl image converted into a sought-after texture; the word becomes homely, appetising, faintly comic.
- 20th century: "frizzled" stays firmly informal — hair burned and crusted, or food crisped in the pan; onomatopoeic and playful, it rarely appears in formal writing.
- Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), "singed and frizzled right down to the skin," fixes the older scorching sense in modern readers' minds, keeping the singed-hair meaning alive beside the culinary one.
