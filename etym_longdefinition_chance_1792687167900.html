chance (noun, adjective, and verb) — The unpredictable, uncontrollable occurrence of events without apparent human design, divine intention, or foreseeable causal necessity; luck, fortune, or hazard; as an adjective, occurring unexpectedly, fortuitously, or haphazardly; and, as a verb, to happen by accident, befall, or to take a risk upon an uncertain outcome.

Primary Lexical Senses and Philosophical Dimensions:

  • Metaphysical and Causal Sense (Indeterminacy / Fortune): In philosophy and cosmology, chance denotes the apparent absence of deliberate purpose or teleological direction in the universe. It describes events that occur through the intersection of independent causal chains that cannot be predicted by an observer. In ancient and medieval thought, chance was personified as Fortuna or Lady Luck, spinning her fickle wheel of prosperity and ruin, representing the precarious vulnerability of human endeavor to external circumstance.
  • Mathematical and Statistical Sense (Probability / Likelihood): In mathematics, actuarial science, and statistical mechanics, chance is stripped of superstition and quantified as probability. Here, it denotes the mathematical odds or relative frequency of a particular outcome occurring within a defined sample space (such as the chance of rolling a six on a balanced die, or the statistical chance of genetic recombination). The "theory of chances," pioneered by Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat in the seventeenth century, laid the foundation for modern probability theory.
  • Pragmatic and Human Opportunity (Opening / Prospect): In everyday human affairs, a chance is an opportunity, an opening, or a favorable set of circumstances that makes a desired objective possible. To "seize a chance" or "give someone a second chance" treats the unpredictable flow of events as an asset to be leveraged rather than a peril to be feared.
  • Peril, Hazard, and Speculation (Risk): Conversely, taking a chance implies exposing oneself to hazard, danger, or failure. In gambling, games of chance depend entirely on randomizing devices (dice, shuffled cards, roulette wheels) rather than player skill.
  • Adjectival Sense (Fortuitous / Accidental / Casual): When used as an adjective, chance characterizes an encounter, circumstance, or tool that appears without advance preparation or planning. A chance encounter brings old friends together unexpectedly; in Wordsworth's phrase "the chance equipment of that hour," it denotes whatever improvised materials or casual gear happen to be immediately available.

Specialized Technical and Cultural Domains:

  • Evolutionary Biology (Genetic Drift and Mutation): In modern Neo-Darwinian synthesis, chance operates at the molecular level through random genetic mutations and neutral genetic drift. While natural selection provides non-random directional filtering based on reproductive fitness, the underlying variations upon which selection operates are generated by chance molecular copying errors.
  • Quantum Physics (Objective Indeterminacy): In quantum mechanics (the Copenhagen interpretation), chance ceases to be merely a reflection of human ignorance and becomes an intrinsic, objective property of subatomic reality. Radioactive decay and wave-function collapse occur with fundamental quantum indeterminacy, prompting Albert Einstein's famous resistant objection: "God does not play dice with the universe."
  • Avant-Garde Art and Music (Aleatory Composition): In twentieth-century aesthetics, artists such as Marcel Duchamp and composer John Cage integrated chance into creative composition. Cage's aleatory music utilized coin tosses from the I Ching to determine notes, durations, and dynamics, intentionally abdicating human artistic control to the random flow of the universe.

Semantic Bridge: The unifying semantic bridge across all these senses is the ancient kinetic metaphor of the falling die. The word derives from Vulgar Latin *cadentia ("that which falls out"), from Latin cadere ("to fall"). In ancient and medieval taverns, players tossed knucklebones and dice from a leather cup onto the gaming board. The way the dice "fell" determined whether a player won a fortune or was ruined. This physical tumble of the cube—utterly beyond the thrower's control once airborne—became the universal human metaphor for unpredictable fortune, mathematical probability, unforeseen opportunity, and casual accident.
