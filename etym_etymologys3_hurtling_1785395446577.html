The family biography of hurtling opens in the collision-yards of Old French. The ancestor verb, hurter, meant to strike, knock, run into, or collide — a word of clashes and blows, probably echoic in origin or borrowed from the Frankish battlefield vocabulary of the battering ram, and with no secure Proto-Indo-European ancestry behind it. Modern French preserves it essentially unchanged as heurter, still the verb for knocking against something or running up against an obstacle. When the Normans brought French to England, hurter entered Middle English as hurten, and its first sense was physical: to strike something hard enough to wound it. From that single verb English bred three separate words, each an independent English development.

The first was hurt itself. In Middle English, hurten's sense of striking shaded into harming — to hurt someone was to hit them hard — and the verb then detached from its collision-origin entirely, losing its physical blow and keeping only the damage, so that today hurt is the ordinary word for physical and emotional injury, and its etymological crash has long been forgotten. The second line was hurl. English made a frequentative by adding the -el/-le suffix of repeated small action, the same suffix in sparkle and wriggle, producing Middle English hurlen, originally 'to strike repeatedly', then 'to fling violently' — the thrower's word, transitive, hurling stones, spears, and abuse. From it come hurley, the stick of the Irish game, and hurly-burly, the reduplicated word for tumult that Shakespeare set in Macbeth. The third line was hurtle. Alongside hurlen, English formed hurtelen, also frequentative, and for its first two centuries it meant 'to clash, collide, dash against' — transitive, a verb of impact. But in the sixteenth century hurtle made the great pivot: from 'to cause to collide' it moved to 'to move with such force that collision is inevitable', turning from transitive to intransitive and shifting its centre of gravity from the blow to the flight that precedes it. The old transitive senses fell away, leaving the modern verb: to move at enormous, uncontrollable speed with the constant threat of impact.

The phonetic form of the word records this history. The frequentative suffix -el (later -le) is what distinguishes hurtle from hurt and gives the verb its sense of repeated, intensified, drawn-out action; the same suffix separates sparkle from spark, wriggle from wrig, hurtle from hurt. The spelling with -le, and later the modern -tle, fixed the frequentative shape in the written language. Meanwhile the semantic split between hurl and hurtle hardened over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries into the clean modern opposition: transitive thrower's verb versus intransitive flight verb. It is precisely because they share a single Middle English frequentative ancestor that the two remain so easy to confuse, and usage guides have been policing the boundary since the eighteenth century.

Hurtling then found its natural home in high-speed narrative:

  • Sound and form: the -ing participle caught the word in mid-flight, making it the standard form for describing an object in violent motion — a hurtling meteor, a hurtling train, a hurtling body.
  • Technological register: the railway and then the automobile age adopted the word for runaway engines and careening vehicles; science and astronomy use it of asteroids, comets, and debris moving through space at terrifying speeds.
  • Literary register: from epic and romantic poetry to adventure prose, 'hurtling down' supplies the language of unstoppable descent; Tolkien's use in The Hobbit (1937) — 'he came hurtling down like a thunderbolt' — fixes the word for generations of readers as the verb of mass, speed, and doom.
  • Modern idiom: a culture of speed has kept the word current: runaway trains hurtle, asteroids hurtle, vehicles hurtle toward catastrophe; the frequentative's old sense of repeated collisions lingers as the sense of a chain of impacts about to happen.

The whole career of the word, from the Old French thud of collision to the modern asteroid's flight, is a single story of violence transferring from the blow to the journey: to hurtle is to be already launched, and the crash is the only possible ending. In Tolkien's sentence that is precisely the point — the Great Goblin does not fall but hurtles, because his whole weight has become the blow.
