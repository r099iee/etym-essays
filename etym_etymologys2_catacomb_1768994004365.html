Compound word — two elements, each traced separately, then the compounding and the English borrowing. CHAIN 1 — the first element, Greek katá "down": 1. Greek katá (preposition/adverb) "down, down along, throughout, over, against, according to" — attested in Mycenaean Linear B as ka-ta (c. 1400–1200 BCE) and throughout classical Greek from Homer (8th c. BCE) onward. 2. Origin: DEBATED. The word has no secure Indo-European etymology; it is often connected with PIE *kṃt-/*kom (compare Latin com-, "with, together"), but many scholars regard the Greek form as at least partly Pre-Greek/substrate. This is stated explicitly: the first element's deeper origin is unknown, though its meaning "down, beneath" is certain. CHAIN 2 — the second element, Greek kymbē "hollow": 1. PIE *kumb(h)- / *kemb- "to bend, be hollow" (reconstructed; a widely suggested but not universally accepted root). 2. Greek kymbē "a hollow vessel, a cup, a bowl" (attested from the archaic period, 8th–7th c. BCE), with related kymbos "cup, bowl" and kymbion "small cup." Cognates: Sanskrit kumbha "pot, jar, water-vessel" (Vedic; source of Hindi kumbh, the pot of the Kumbh Mela), Avestan xumba- "pot, jar." The root's core image is concavity — something cupped, hollowed, made to hold. CHAIN 3 — the compounding and the Latin leg: 3. Greek kata kymbēs / katakymbē "at the hollows; the down-hollow" — a compound of "down" + "hollow," applied to the subterranean hollows and quarry cavities of the Appian Way district near Rome. 4. Late Latin catacumbae / catacumbas "the underground burial galleries" — attested from the 4th–5th centuries CE as the name of the great Christian burial complex beneath the Basilica of San Sebastiano on the Appian Way; the district's low-lying volcanic hollows gave the site its name. 5. Italian catacomba (singular), French catacombe (16th c.) — the Romance intermediaries. 6. English catacomb — borrowed (usually as the plural catacombs) in the 17th century; generalized from the Roman site to any underground burial galleries, and metaphorically to any buried honeycombed network. Debate note: the exact composition of the Latin form is debated. The traditional account takes catacumbae from Greek kata kymbēs "at the hollows," with kymbē "hollow vessel" as the base; some scholars prefer a derivation from an otherwise unattested Greek *katakumbē "underground hollow" formed with the same elements, and a Pre-Greek origin of kymbē itself has also been suggested (compare the Mediterranean "cup" words). Every uncertain step is flagged rather than smoothed over.
