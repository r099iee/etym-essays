Thumping is the present participle and verbal noun of the verb thump, "to strike or beat with a heavy, dull blow," and as a participle it denotes continuous or repeated heavy striking — the sound of a fist on a table, boots on a wooden floor, or two heavy bodies grappling on the ground. Beyond this core verbal sense the word has developed a rich family of secondary meanings that must be carefully separated: as a participial adjective, chiefly in British colloquial use, thumping means "very large, emphatic, striking" (a thumping majority, a thumping great lie); of the heart or pulse it means "beating hard or violently" (a heart thumping with fear or exertion); as an adverb it functions as a rough intensive (thumping good, thumping big); and as a noun thumping names a beating or the sound of heavy blows (to give someone a thumping). The word is imitative, or echoic, in origin: its sounds — the voiceless th, the short low vowel, the closing plosive cluster -mp — reproduce the dull, low-frequency impact of a heavy object falling on a solid surface, and it was coined precisely because it sounds like what it names. The sense in Tolkien's The Hobbit, Chapter 2. In the narrator's account of the trolls' brawl the word appears in its oldest, most physical register: "rolling nearly into the fire kicking and thumping, while Tom whacked at them both with a branch." The pairing is acoustic as well as narrative. Thumping describes the heavy, clumsy, monotonous percussion of the two trolls' fists and boots as they grapple on the ground — dull impacts, the sound of great weight moving without grace — while whacked, a sharper and crisper word, describes the ringing crack of Tom's branch against their hides. The contrast of the two sound-symbolic verbs is Tolkien's comic soundscape for the scene: two gross bodies pounding the earth while a third beats ineffectually upon them, all in vain, since the blows "only made them madder than ever." Primary senses of thumping
  • Verb (present participle of thump) — striking or beating heavily and repeatedly so as to produce a dull, resounding impact; also used of pulsing or throbbing force.
  • Participial adjective, chiefly British colloquial — very large, emphatic, striking, or remarkable: a thumping majority, a thumping great lie, a thumping fine victory.
  • Adverb / intensive, colloquial — used to intensify adjectives, especially before negative or emphatic nouns: thumping big, thumping great.
  • Of the heart, pulse, or blood — beating hard and violently with fear, excitement, or exertion: her heart was thumping.
  • Noun — a beating or a series of heavy blows; also the dull sound of such blows: to give someone a thumping.
  • Agent formation thumper (20th century, colloquial) — a remarkably large specimen of something, a "whopper."
Polysemy: how the senses developed
  • The physical sense came first. The 16th-century echoic verb thump named a heavy, blunt, ungraceful blow and the dull sound it makes — the sound of a fist or cudgel, of a fall, of a heavy object dropped.
  • The participial form thumping, in use from the 16th century for continuous striking, took a comic semantic step upward in the 18th and 19th centuries: from "striking heavily" it came to mean "strikingly large or emphatic" — a thumping lie is a thumping big one, and the semantic bridge is the idea of weight and force applied to magnitude rather than to blows.
  • In the 20th century the intensive adjective hardened into a fixed political idiom in British English: a thumping majority is an electoral victory so large it resounds, the parliamentary equivalent of a dull, emphatic blow to the opposition.
  • Meanwhile the heart, in figurative and sentimental prose, began to thump — to beat hard with fear, joy, or exertion — so the word acquired an emotional register alongside its physical one, a use recorded from the 18th century onward.
Distinctions from near-synonyms Thumping must be separated from its neighbours, and the acoustic discriminations are precise. Pounding is heavier and more regular, like a pestle or a drummer's fist; beating is the general, neutral word for repeated striking; hammering suggests tool-like, insistent repetition; battering implies savage, damaging force; smacking is the sharp slap of an open hand; banging is loud, clattering, and hollow; knocking is the sharp rap of a hard object on a door or window; and whacking, its partner in Tolkien's sentence, is the resounding crack of a stick. Thump sits between them all: heavier than whack, duller than bang, more weighty than knock, and always weighted toward the low, blunt, ungraceful end of the sound spectrum.
