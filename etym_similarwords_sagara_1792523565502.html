• Bhagīratha: Similar because both are illustrious Solar dynasty monarchs of Ayodhyā intimately linked to the epic legend of the ocean and the descent of the Gaṅgā; differs because Sagara is the great ancestor whose sons excavated the ocean bed and were destroyed by their arrogance, whereas Bhagīratha is the patient, pious descendant whose superhuman penance brought the Gaṅgā down from heaven to purify their ashes.
  • Sāgara: Similar because Sāgara is the direct grammatical patronymic derivative of Sagara; differs because Sagara is the personal name of the emperor (meaning "born with poison"), whereas Sāgara is the secondary *vṛddhi* geographical noun denoting the cosmic "ocean / sea" excavated by his sons.
  • Samudra: Similar because both *Samudra* and *Sāgara* (derived from Sagara) are the two primary Sanskrit terms for the ocean; differs because Samudra (from sam- + udra, "gathering together of waters") is an ancient Vedic physical and mythic term for the gathered watery deep, whereas Sāgara is the epic patronymic term rooted specifically in King Sagara's dynastic lore.
  • Aṃśumān: Similar because both are heroic kings of Ayodhyā belonging to the same immediate family; differs because Aṃśumān was Sagara's dutiful grandson who entered the underworld cavern, pacified sage Kapila with humble devotion, and successfully retrieved the sacrificial horse so Sagara could complete his rite.
  • Asamañja: Similar because both are figures in the immediate royal household of Ayodhyā; differs because Asamañja was Sagara's eldest biological son (born of Queen Keśinī) whose cruelty toward the citizens forced Sagara to banish him from the kingdom, contrasting with Sagara's commitment to imperial justice.
  • Chakravartin: Similar because Sagara represents the quintessential classical *Chakravartin* ("universal sovereign whose chariot wheels roll unimpeded across the earth"); differs because Chakravartin is an abstract, overarching imperial archetype across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions, whereas Sagara is the specific legendary monarch.
  • Aśvamedhin: Similar because King Sagara is famous as a great performer of the Horse Sacrifice (*Aśvamedha*); differs because Aśvamedhin is a generic titular descriptor meaning "one who has successfully performed the horse sacrifice", whereas Sagara is the individual ruler.
  • Dilīpa: Similar because both are celebrated ancestral kings of Ayodhyā in Lord Rāma's lineage; differs because Dilīpa is renowned for his selfless, humble protection of the divine cow Nandinī and his devotion to sage Vasiṣṭha, whereas Sagara is famous for his martial reconquest of his realm and his sixty thousand sons.
  • Daśaratha: Similar because both were mighty emperors of Ayodhyā in the Solar dynasty whose intense love and sorrow regarding their sons formed pivotal turning points in the Rāmāyaṇa; differs because Daśaratha was the immediate father of Lord Rāma whose fateful boons to Kaikeyī caused Rāma's forest exile, whereas Sagara lived epochs earlier in the Satya/Tretā Yuga.
  • Jaladhi: Similar because both *Jaladhi* and *Sāgara* designate the ocean in Sanskrit poetry; differs because Jaladhi is a purely descriptive compound meaning "receptacle / reservoir of waters", entirely lacking the historical and genealogical royal myth of King Sagara.
  • Mahāsāgara: Similar because it incorporates Sagara's name into modern geographical nomenclature; differs because Mahāsāgara is a modern technical term specifically designating the vast oceans of the planet (e.g., *Hinda Mahāsāgara* = Indian Ocean), whereas Sagara is the ancient king.
  • Varuṇa: Similar because both are intimately associated with the ocean; differs because Varuṇa is the ancient Vedic god of cosmic waters, moral law (*ṛta*), and the subterranean ocean realm, whereas Sagara is a mortal Kshatriya emperor whose sons physically dug the ocean basin.
