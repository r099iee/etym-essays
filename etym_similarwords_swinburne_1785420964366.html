• Tennyson: Similar because he is a Victorian master of melodic, musical verse, Swinburne's nearest rival in sound; differs because Tennyson pairs melody with a moralizing, elegiac restraint foreign to Swinburne's pagan, antinomian excess.
  • Shelley: Similar because both are poets of radical, anti-conventional feeling, sensuous imagery, and lyric intensity; differs because Shelley remains a Romantic aiming at idealist sensuous immediacy, while Swinburne subordinates meaning to a rarefied musical surface.
  • Keats: Similar because both are poets of sensuous richness whose diction rewards close reading; differs because Keats's Ode to Melancholy works by organic imagery and sober meditativeness, whereas Swinburne works by incantatory rhythm and formal intoxication.
  • Browning: Similar because both offer verbal complexity and intellectual challenge to the reader; differs because Browning pursues dramatic psychological realism and colloquial energy, while Swinburne lets sound-structure itself carry the meaning.
  • Bridges: Similar because both are late-Victorian and Edwardian masters of recondite metres — the two names are paired in Empson's schoolroom-handbook sentence; differs because Bridges's prosodic experiments are austere, restrained, and classical, while Swinburne's are luxuriant, pagan, and incantatory.
  • Arnold: Similar because both are Victorian poets engaged with the claims of high culture and criticism; differs because Arnold is a poet of elegiac moral seriousness and classicism, whereas Swinburne is a poet of Dionysian excess and formal daring.
  • Hopkins: Similar because both were Victorian innovators of rhythm — Hopkins's sprung rhythm and Swinburne's anapaests both broke the iambic norm; differs because Hopkins's innovation serves religious intensity and compressed meaning, while Swinburne's serves sheer musical flow.
  • Rossetti: Similar because both were associated with Pre-Raphaelite and Aesthetic circles and with the revival of sensuous medieval and classical themes; differs because Rossetti is painterly, mystical, and inward, while Swinburne is rhetorical, kinetic, and outward-flowing.
  • Wordsworth: Similar because both are canonical English poets whose claims were revalued by modern criticism; differs because Wordsworth is the poet of plain speech and "the real language of men," the antithesis of Swinburne's gorgeous, musical artificiality.
