- c. 1300: visage enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman, from Latin visus, “the seen thing, sight” — a courtly but neutral word for the face and appearance, with no elevation yet.
- 14th–16th c.: the standard literary word for the face and, more broadly, the outward look of anything — used in romances and chronicles simply as “the face, the look” of a person.
- 16th–17th c.: the poets elevate it — Shakespeare and his age use visage for the face as the emblem of character and identity, while plain ‘face’ does the work of speech, so the word rises to a formal, almost heraldic dignity.
- 18th–19th c.: it becomes the monumental face-word of poets and Gothic novelists — the face as a composed, statuesque whole (“grim-visaged”), read from outside like a public record rather than a collection of features.
- 20th–21st c.: rare, literary, and deliberate; to say visage today is to view a face as a document or monument — an archive of the blood — never the casual word of the street, which is exactly the weight the passage gives it.
