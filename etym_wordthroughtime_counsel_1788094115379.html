- After 1066 — “counsel” enters English from Old French conseil as the word for advice, deliberation and plan; the native “rede” retreats.
- 12th–14th centuries — Middle English conseil covers advice, a deliberative assembly, and secret intention; Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde returns constantly to the duty of keeping “conseil” (counsel) secret.
- 14th–16th centuries — the spelling splits: “council” is fixed for the assembly (from Latin concilium), “counsel” for advice and advisers (from Latin consilium); the legal sense consolidates as barristers become “counsel”.
- 16th–17th centuries — “to keep one's own counsel” becomes a fixed idiom; “counsel of perfection” enters the language from the theology of the evangelical counsels.
- 18th–19th centuries — constitutional vocabulary: the American Sixth Amendment guarantees “the right to counsel”; “counsel” is standard court-room usage.
- 20th century — “counselling” shifts from general advice to professional psychological and pastoral guidance (the counselling movement); the noun keeps its legal and formal registers, while in ordinary speech “advice” has taken over.
- Modern — in Tolkien's archaic prose, “this counsel be good or bad” deliberately recalls the word's medieval gravity: deliberation, wisdom and warning in one noun.
