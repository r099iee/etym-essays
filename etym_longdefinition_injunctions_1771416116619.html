Injunctions in this passage are not legal orders but the affectionate, authoritative life-directives of an elder. When Grandmother Jia asks Dai-yu her age, whether she has begun her studies, and then charges her—“You musn’t feel homesick here. If there’s anything you want to eat or anything you want to play with, just come and tell me. And you must tell me if any of the maids or the old nannies are nasty to you”—the narrator says that Dai-yu “made appropriate responses to all of these questions and injunctions.” The word gathers those little commands into a single category: instructions issued from a position of established authority, wrapped in warmth, that the younger party is expected to acknowledge and obey. The tone of the scene is tender, but the structure is not egalitarian—the injunctions flow downward, and Dai-yu’s “appropriate responses” certify that she has heard and will comply. Her compliance is a form of social calibration: it proves she is teachable, well-mannered, and ready to be absorbed into the rules of the household. The word must be separated from its neighbours. An order is blunt, direct, and often peremptory, with no need for relationship; a command carries military or sovereign force and expects instant obedience; a directive is bureaucratic and procedural, the language of offices and memos; an edict is the public decree of a ruler; an instruction is neutral, a how-to; a commandment has a quasi-religious weight, as in the Ten Commandments; while a warning threatens consequences. An injunction uniquely combines the moral and the procedural: it “joins on” to a person’s conscience, binding conduct through authority and affection at once, and it is the word a legal system uses when a court orders someone to do or refrain from doing a specific act (a “prohibitory injunction”). In the novel it is precisely the right word for advice that is also a requirement—a matriarch’s instructions that her granddaughter must accept as part of joining the family. In register, injunction is formal, legal, and homiletic. It is at home in court orders (“the judge granted an injunction against the strike”), in religious and moral discourse (“the injunctions of the Decalogue”), and in literary narrative describing elder authority. It collocates with issue, grant, seek, obey, violate, and heed (“she heeded her grandmother’s injunctions”), with the adjectives paternal, maternal, strict, express, and final. Its plural here is gently legalistic, elevating a grandmother’s kind commands into a small body of household law—an apt summary of how an orphan girl is folded into the customs and obligations of a great family.
  • Primary sense (general): An authoritative warning or order, a solemn command laid upon someone’s conscience.
  • Legal sense: A judicial order compelling a party to do something (mandatory injunction) or to refrain from doing it (prohibitory injunction), a remedy of the equity courts.
  • Religious/moral sense: A binding charge, as in the injunctions of scripture, of conscience, or of an elder.
  • Register: Formal, legal, and homiletic; the plural elevates a set of commands into a small body of rules.
Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings: In jurisprudence, injunction is the technical name for a court order that restrains or compels conduct—interlocutory or interim injunctions are granted before trial to preserve the status quo, and permanent injunctions conclude a case; “injunctive relief” is the remedy itself. In modern media and privacy law, the “super-injunction” (a gagging order) has added a contemporary, contested flavour. In general discourse, an injunction remains an authoritative moral command, and the plural injunctions collects a set of such directives—exactly the note struck in the novel, where a grandmother’s kindness is codified into household law. The related verb enjoin (“to command or forbid”) and the noun enjoinment share the same Latin stock. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: Latin iniungere (in- “on” + iungere “to join”) means literally “to join on, to impose,” so an injunction is an order attached to a person like a yoke; the legal sense developed in the equity courts of the sixteenth century while the general moral sense continued the older image of a charge bound to the conscience. In the passage, the word makes Dai-yu’s welcome a set of obligations she joyfully accepts—advice that is simultaneously requirement.
