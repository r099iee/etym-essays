Daunted, the past participle of the verb daunt, describes someone whose courage has been subdued — made fearful, discouraged, or disheartened by difficulty, danger, or magnitude. To be daunted is to feel one's resolve shrink before an obstacle: the climber daunted by the wall, the applicant daunted by the odds, the traveller in the source sentence pointedly not daunted by the distance.

In the source sentence the narrator insists he was not daunted by the distance — the negative doing all the work. Denying fear asserts courage: whatever the miles, the perils, or the loneliness of the road, his spirit did not quail. The construction is classic understatement, the traveller's quiet boast, and it only lands because daunted names a real inward shrinking that every reader recognises.

Core meaning in detail: Daunt sits between frightening and discouraging: it is not the sudden jolt of startle nor the lasting wound of traumatise, but the steady pressure of something formidable that makes courage ebb. Its grammar is almost always passive or participial — one is daunted by distances, tasks, opponents, reputations — and it governs the grand and the abstract more readily than the trivial: nothing so small as a spider daunts, while exams, mountains, and august assemblies daunt routinely.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Disheartened or intimidated (primary modern sense, from the 13th c.): daunted by the scale of the task, nothing daunted, the team was visibly daunted. The bridge from taming is direct: as a wild creature is subdued, so courage is subdued by the formidable.
  • Subdued or tamed — the older literal sense (archaic): medieval writers speak of daunted steeds and daunted rebels, meaning broken to obedience. The bridge is the Latin root itself: domitare, to tame thoroughly, applied to beasts and insurgents before it softened into feelings.
  • Undaunted as fixed idiom (set phrase, 16th c. onward): undaunted courage, she carried on undaunted. The bridge is negation as praise: English honours fearlessness so often through this word's negative that undaunted now outranks its positive in frequency and has effectively become its own adjective.
  • Daunting prospects (participial adjective of the active side): a daunting task, daunting odds, the daunting prospect of surgery. The bridge turns the verb's effect into a property of the cause: what would daunt us we call daunting, so the participle pair maps both ends of the encounter.
  • Literary and heraldic colour (stylistic): poets write of daunted looks and daunted hosts on battlefields real and amorous, where the word lends antique weight. The bridge is register: a Latinate pedigree makes daunted sound graver than plain scared, so ceremony reaches for it.

Register and connotation: daunted is dignified and inward — the vocabulary of memoirs, citations, and eulogies rather than playgrounds. It flatters even as it admits weakness: to be daunted is to have recognised something genuinely formidable, and to be undaunted is quiet heroism. The source sentence exploits exactly this dignity, turning a long walk into a small epic by declaring the distance failed to do what distances honourably do.
