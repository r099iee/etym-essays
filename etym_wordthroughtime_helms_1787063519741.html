- Old English (8th century): helm meant helmet and also any protective covering; kings and lords were called the helm of their people — protection and defense, concrete and figurative at once.
- Middle English (13th–15th centuries): the great helm of the knight; helm was the ordinary word for the warrior's helmet, while the separate helma (steering gear) developed at sea.
- 16th–17th centuries: as "helmet" (a French-derived diminutive) took over everyday speech, helm the head-armor word became poetic and archaic; the steering sense rose, giving "to take the helm" = to take command.
- 18th–20th centuries: helm as helmet survived only in literary, historical, and fantasy prose; helm as steering and leadership became a common business and political idiom ("at the helm").
- Today: "at the helm" is a fixed idiom of leadership; helms as helmets is an archaic/literary plural revived by fantasy prose like Tolkien's — a knight's helmet and a captain's command in one word.
