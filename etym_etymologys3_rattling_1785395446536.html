RATTLING is a purely echoic word — a phonetic imitation of the sound it names — and like its cousin clatter, its history is less a story of semantic drift than of acoustic invention and steady reinforcement. It was coined in Middle English around the year 1300, directly out of the noise of hard things knocking together, and it has changed remarkably little in the seven centuries since. Its biography is the biography of a sound-impression that found a shape in language and then colonized, through metaphor and metonymy, an astonishing range of mechanical, bodily, and figurative territory. THE ACOUSTIC ORIGIN The word is built on the echoing skeleton r-...-t-...-l. The initial r- supplies a vibrating, trilling onset; the -t- is a sharp alveolar stop, the sound of a single hard knock; and the -l- tail gives the word its rapid, rippling motion, as if one knock were elided into the next. The whole is a small onomatopoeic machine for producing, in speech, the very rhythm of a rattling noise. Because it was coined from the sound, there is no earlier ancestor: no Latin, Greek, or Indo-European word stands behind it. Middle English ratelen (c. 1300) is simply the first recorded form of the sound-impression, and the reconstruction of an earlier base *rat-t- / *rat-l- is a description of the acoustic pattern, not an attested etymon. The word is thus a rare and valuable object for the etymologist: a piece of pure sound-symbolism whose birth is visible in the historical record. THE FIRST CENTURIES In the 14th and 15th centuries ratelen and rattle meant the making of a rapid succession of short, sharp sounds — chains dragged, gates shaken, carts and wagons jolting over rough roads, gunfire, hail. The word was homely and exact, and it spread through the vocabulary of daily mechanical life. It described the noise of the medieval world's moving parts: the iron-bound wheels of a cart on cobbles, the rattle of a loose shutter, the chattering of harness and metalwork. By the 16th century the noun rattle had appeared, naming the instrument that rattles: the baby's toy rattle, whose purpose is to make precisely this sound; and the watchman's or alarm rattle, a ratchet that produced a warning clatter to raise the town. From the sound of the thing, the word had become the thing itself. THE FIGURATIVE SHIFT The most productive development was figurative. In the 16th century, to rattle a person came to mean to disconcert or alarm them — to shake their composure as a chain is shaken — and this sense has remained vigorous ever since, so that we still say a scare "rattled" someone, and modern idiom gives us "to rattle someone's cage", which keeps the image of shaking the bars of a cage. The same century saw the intensifier use, "a rattling good yarn" or "a rattling pace", in which the speed and vigour of the rattling motion supplied a general sense of brisk excellence; the phrase survived into the 19th century and is still recognized, if dated, today. And the verb learned to speak: "to rattle off" captured the fluency of someone reeling off names, dates, or verses without pause, and "to rattle on" the garrulous flow of talk. In each case the body or the voice is treated as a mechanical thing that rattles, and the transfer is so natural that it needs no comment. THE BODY AND THE SNAKE In the 17th century the word reached the body and the New World at once. The death-rattle — the terminal, involuntary throat-sound of a dying person — was fixed as a grim collocation, one of the most sombre uses of an imitative verb in the language, and it survives today in medical and literary usage alike. And the newly encountered rattlesnake was named for the horny rings at the end of its tail, which it vibrates as a warning; the name is pure metonymy, the sound standing for the animal that makes it. That the same word could name a baby's toy and a fatal serpent's warning organ shows how completely the acoustic core of rattle had saturated the culture: once the sound had a name, everything that made the sound took the name. MECHANICAL MODERNITY The industrial and mechanical centuries were kind to the word. Rattling became the sound of the machine age: trains rattling over points, engines rattling, typewriters rattling, and later printers, pumps, and loose machinery of every kind. The word was ready-made for the modern world because the modern world is full of hard, loose, rapidly moving parts, and rattle is exactly the sound they make. In contemporary English the register runs from machines, weather, and alarm — rattling windows, chains, trains, hailstones — to the mechanical percussion of a mountain-side shaking itself apart. Unlike many imitative words, rattle has never tired: its sound and sense have remained in perfect lockstep because the world keeps producing the noise it names. SISTER SOUNDS, NOT RELATIVES Because rattle was coined from sound, the languages around it coined their own versions from the same sound. Middle Dutch ratelen, Middle Low German retelen, Old High German razalōn, and modern German rasseln and rattern are parallel creations on the same r-...-t-...-l skeleton, not relatives of the English word. Latin crepitāre "to crackle, rattle" (English crepitate) and Greek krotéō "to knock, clatter" are independent imitations in the same field. Two speculative links are sometimes floated — the Old Norse verb hrata "to reel, totter", and the rodent name rat, possibly named from its chattering squeak — but neither has ever been proved, and the honest conclusion is that rattle is an independent Germanic coinage, repeated wherever the sound needed a name. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses rattling at the climax of the landslide: "in a fearful confusion of slipping, rattling, cracking slabs and stones." The participle is chosen for its mechanical, metallic texture: the mountain is described as if it were a vast machine coming apart, its parts striking one another in a rapid, unceasing percussion. Slipping gives the movement, cracking gives the fracture, and rattling — the imitative core — gives the sound of the whole catastrophe. A purely onomatopoeic word, with no inherited pedigree, here carries the full weight of an epic disaster, proving that the newest kind of word-formation can be as powerful as the oldest, and that a sound coined seven hundred years ago can still shake a mountainside.
