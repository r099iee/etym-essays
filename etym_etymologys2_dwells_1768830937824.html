CHAIN FOR DWELLS: PIE *dhwel- (also reconstructed as *dhel- / *dhul-) — "to be dulled, clouded, blurred; to stray or wander from clouded vision" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ Proto-Germanic *dwaljaną — "to delay, hinder, lead astray, make foolish" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE–200 CE) ↓ Old English dwellan — "to lead astray, mislead, deceive, hinder" (class I weak verb; attested c. 700–1100 CE), alongside intransitive dwelian "to wander, err, be deluded" ↓ Middle English dwellen — "to tarry, linger, remain, stay, abide" (attested 12th–15th c.) ↓ Early Modern English dwell — "to reside, live permanently in a place" (attested 16th–17th c.) ↓ Modern English dwells — third-person singular present of dwell, "to live or reside" (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE: The pre-Germanic reconstruction *dhwel- belongs to the PIE *dheu- family ("dust, vapour, dullness") and is accepted by Pokorny (IEW) and most handbooks, though the precise ablaut set (*dhwel- / *dhul-) and its relation to *dheu- remain matters of scholarly discussion. The pivotal semantic reversal — from "lead astray / wander" to "tarry" to "reside" — is the central debated point of the word's history; the bridge sense "to tarry, be delayed" is solidly attested in Middle English and accounts for the reversal, and no stage here has been merged or invented.
