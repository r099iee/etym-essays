Conveying is the present participle of the verb convey, and its master image is that of carrying something from one place or mind to another, whether the load is furniture rattling on a cart, water running through a pipe, or a meaning passing from a speaker into a hearer.

In the source sentence the word appears in its oldest and most literal sense: two covered carts go off laden to Buckland, conveying the furniture and goods that Frodo has not sold to his new home by way of the Brandywine Bridge. Here conveying means physically transporting household belongings, the carts serving as vessels and the road as the channel, with a gentle formal flavour that carrying or carting would lack.

Core meaning in detail: At its heart convey joins Latin com-, with or together, to via, the way or road, so that conveying literally pictures accompanying something along the way. From escorting travellers the sense widened to transporting goods, then to transmitting anything that flows — water, heat, sound, electricity — and finally to communicating thoughts and feelings, which travel from mind to mind as surely as carts travel from shire to shire.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Transporting goods or people (primary literal sense, from the 14th c.): carts conveying furniture, buses conveying passengers, lifts conveying shoppers between floors. This is the source-sentence sense, and every other meaning grows out of it.
  • Conducting or transmitting a flow (physical extension, from the 16th c.): pipes conveying water, wires conveying current, nerves conveying impulses. The bridge is the channel: the pipe is a road for water as the highway is a road for carts, so the verb slides from vehicles to vessels.
  • Communicating meaning, feeling, or information (figurative extension, from the 17th c.): words conveying sympathy, a glance conveying reproach, figures conveying the scale of a disaster. The bridge runs through the metaphor of ideas as objects that travel: a sentence is a cart, and understanding is delivery at the far end.
  • Transferring property in law (technical legal sense): a deed conveying an estate, a will conveying title to an heir. The bridge is formal delivery: ownership passes from hand to hand exactly as goods pass from cart to doorstep, and lawyers kept the Latinate verb because its dignity suits parchment and seals.
  • Carrying sound or tribute (refined social sense): to convey compliments, to convey thanks, to convey greetings. The bridge is the escort sense revived in manners: the speaker accompanies another person's goodwill along the road of conversation and delivers it intact.
  • Behaving or conducting oneself (archaic, now rare): older writers speak of conveying oneself nobly or basely. The bridge turns the verb inward: instead of carrying goods along the road, one carries one's own body and reputation through the world.

Register and connotation: Conveying is the genteel elder sibling of carrying: estate agents convey property, chairmen convey thanks, engineers convey fluids, while porters simply carry. It sounds official, careful, and slightly old-fashioned, which is why Tolkien chooses it for the formal removal of Frodo's goods. The progressive form adds ongoing motion, picturing the carts mid-journey, wheels turning, with the load still aboard and Buckland still ahead.
