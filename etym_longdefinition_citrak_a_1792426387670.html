Citrakūṭa (proper noun; Sanskrit: चित्रकूट, citrakūṭa, literally "the many-colored peak," "the picturesque summit," or "the hill of wondrous beauty") — A celebrated sacred mountain, forested pilgrimage region, and modern district straddling the border of northern Madhya Pradesh and southern Uttar Pradesh in central India. In ancient Sanskrit literature, particularly Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa and Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa, Citrakūṭa is renowned as the idyllic, serene forest sanctuary where Lord Rāma, Princess Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa established their initial wilderness hermitage (parṇaśālā) following their banishment from Ayodhyā, and where the poignant reunion between Rāma and his brother Bharata transpired.

Primary epic narrative sense — the idyllic forest haven of the Rāmāyaṇa. In the Ayodhyākāṇḍa of the Rāmāyaṇa, after crossing the sacred confluence of the Gaṅgā and Yamunā at Prayāga, Rāma is directed by the great sage Bharadvāja to take up residence on Mount Citrakūṭa. Bharadvāja describes the mountain as an enchanted sanctuary filled with towering flowering trees, blooming lotuses, singing peacocks, friendly herds of deer, clean mountain streams, and caves inhabited by saintly hermits. Here, on the tranquil banks of the sacred Mandākinī River (now often identified with the Payasvinī), Lakṣmaṇa constructs a leaf-thatched cottage where the divine exiles experience profound domestic harmony, living in communion with untouched nature away from the toxic political intrigues of the capital.

The historic meeting of Bharata and Rāma (Citrakūṭa-Milāpa). Citrakūṭa is the dramatic setting for one of the most emotionally sublime and philosophically elevated confrontations in world literature. Learning of his mother Kaikeyī's treachery and Daśaratha's death, Prince Bharata marches to Citrakūṭa with the entire royal court and army of Ayodhyā. He falls at Rāma's feet, pleading with him to return and accept the imperial crown. Rāma steadfastly refuses to violate their deceased father's solemn pledge. In the climax of their encounter, Rāma gives Bharata his wooden sandals (pādukās); Bharata places them upon his head and vows to govern the realm merely as Rāma's trustee, placing the sandals upon the royal throne at Nandigrāma while awaiting Rāma's return.

Spiritual sanctity, hermitages, and regional toponymy:

  • Hermitage of Atri and Anasūyā — At the foot of Citrakūṭa, Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa pay homage to the ancient sage Atri and his venerable, chaste wife Anasūyā. Recognizing Sītā's unshakeable virtue, Anasūyā presents her with divine, unwithering garlands, radiant celestial cosmetics, and immortal garments that keep her immaculate throughout fourteen years of harsh forest wandering.
  • Sacred pilgrimage landmarks — The modern geographical pilgrimage site of Chitrakoot encompasses several revered locations mentioned in epic and medieval devotional traditions: Kāmadgiri (the sacred forested hill circled by millions of pilgrims performing parikramā), Rāmaghāṭ (the sanctified bathing steps along the Mandākinī River), Sphatik Śilā (the quartz boulder where Rāma supposedly adorned Sītā's hair with wild jasmine), and Hanumān Dhārā (a natural spring cascading down a sheer cliff).
  • The sanctuary of Saint Tulsīdās — In medieval Bhakti history, Citrakūṭa is renowned as the primary retreat of the 16th-century saint-poet Tulsīdās, who composed portions of the Rāmacaritamānasa here. A celebrated devotional couplet describes how Tulsīdās, while sitting on the banks of Rāmaghāṭ preparing sandalwood paste, was blessed with a direct divine vision of Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa as youthful princes.
  • Toponymic cognates and regional fortresses — In South Asian historical geography, the name Citrakūṭa appears elsewhere as a mountain fortress or natural marvel. Most notably, the legendary fortress of Chittorgarh in Rajasthan derives its ancient name from Citrakūṭa-durga, originally founded by the Mori Rajput ruler Citrāṅgada Mori. Similarly, the dramatic horseshoe-shaped Chitrakoot Falls on the Indravati River in Bastar, Chhattisgarh, takes its name from this sacred toponym.

Aesthetic and cultural symbolism. In classical Sanskrit poetics, Citrakūṭa functions as the supreme archetype of the tapovana (penitential forest grove). It represents a liminal paradise poised between civilization and wilderness: unlike the decadent, faction-ridden city of Ayodhyā or the bloodthirsty, demon-infested depths of the southern Daṇḍaka forest, Citrakūṭa embodies perfect pastoral innocence, spiritual contemplation, and absolute fidelity to moral duty (dharma).
