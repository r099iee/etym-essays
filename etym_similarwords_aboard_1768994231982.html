• On: Similar because aboard is historically and functionally a spatial preposition meaning "on"; differs because on is the neutral general preposition (on the boat merely says location), while aboard adds the sense of being within the vessel's operational space, one of its company, committed to its voyage.
  • Onboard: Similar because it means "on or within a vessel or vehicle"; differs because onboard is chiefly the modern adjectival/adverbial form of the same idea, and the verb to onboard ("induct a new employee") has split it into a corporate term, while aboard stays spatial and nautical in feel.
  • On board (phrase): Similar because aboard is literally the contraction of "on board"; differs because the open phrase can be used adjectivally ("all hands on board") and idiomatically ("take on board" an idea), while aboard is a single word that remains almost purely spatial and prepositional.
  • Into: Similar because both express entry into an enclosed space; differs because into is the general preposition of motion toward the interior of anything, while aboard specifically marks entry into a vessel or vehicle and carries the nautical/committal sense that into lacks.
  • In: Similar because both can describe being inside something; differs because in would be right only for a cabin or compartment, not for being on a deck or within a craft's company — aboard covers the whole vessel and its voyage, where in is too narrow.
  • At sea: Similar because both belong to the nautical register; differs because at sea refers to the open water (being away from land), not to being on the vessel, while aboard is always about being on or into the craft itself.
  • Embarked: Similar because both describe boarding a vessel for a voyage; differs because embarked is a formal verb of the completed act of boarding (with its figurative sense "to begin an undertaking"), while aboard is a spatial preposition/adverb describing the state or motion, not the formal act.
  • Alight / on board a craft: Similar in the register of entering or being on a vehicle; differs because alight means to get down/off or (of a bird) to settle — the opposite direction of motion from going aboard — and never carries aboard's sense of committed membership in the vessel's company.
