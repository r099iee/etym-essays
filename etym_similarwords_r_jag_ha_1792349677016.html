• Girivraja: Similar because both serve as ancient designations for royal capital fortresses surrounded by mountains (specifically the Kekeya capital in the Ramayana and the Magadhan capital in the Mahabharata); differs because Girivraja explicitly highlights geological topography ("enclosure/circle of hills") rather than monarchical habitation.
  • Rājadhyakṣa-bhavana: Similar because both describe the physical palace or administrative residence of a royal authority; differs because Rājadhyakṣa-bhavana denotes a bureaucratic or superintendent's palace, whereas Rājagṛha carries comprehensive urban, dynastic, and imperial sovereignty as a citadel-city.
  • Citadel: Similar because both denote a heavily fortified, elevated defensive strongpoint anchoring a sovereign capital; differs because citadel is an Anglo-Romance military architectural term focusing solely on defensive ramparts and garrison bastions, whereas Rājagṛha denotes an organic royal city, political court, and spiritual center.
  • Metropolis: Similar because both represent a primary municipal and political hub of a kingdom; differs because metropolis derives from Greek concept of "mother city" regarding colonial expansion and commercial density, whereas Rājagṛha specifically signifies the sacral and physical seat of royal rule.
  • Capital: Similar because both identify the central seat of government and throne of a monarch; differs because capital is an abstract political term designating the administrative head of a territory, while Rājagṛha embodies an ancient fortified palace-enclosure and specific historical toponym.
  • Pura / Rājadhānī: Similar because rājadhānī is the standard Sanskrit term for a royal capital city; differs because rājadhānī emphasizes the city as the holding-seat of the kingdom's reign (*dhānī*), whereas Rājagṛha literally designates the palace-citadel itself (*gṛha*).
  • Acropolis: Similar because both denote an ancient fortified hill-top royal and sacred urban complex; differs because acropolis is tied specifically to the Greco-Hellenic polis centered on civic deities and defensive heights, whereas Rājagṛha is rooted in Indo-Aryan monarchical court culture.
  • Stronghold: Similar because both represent fortified locations capable of withstanding prolonged siege; differs because stronghold highlights military invulnerability without necessarily encompassing a royal court, civil population, or philosophical school.
  • Bastion: Similar because both imply defensive security and sovereign resilience; differs because bastion is a specific geometric projection in fortification works or a metaphorical anchor, whereas Rājagṛha is an entire populated seat of sovereign governance.
  • Seat of Power: Similar because both refer to the geographic locus where political decisions are enacted; differs because seat of power is an abstract modern idiom, whereas Rājagṛha is a concrete physical compound and historical proper noun.
