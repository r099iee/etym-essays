STURDY is a linguistic paradox: a modern term of unalloyed praise that began as a description of drunkenness, and whose ancestry runs back — if the traditional account is right — to the humble thrush. The story opens in the prehistoric vocabulary of Proto-Indo-European with the root *trozdo-, the ancient word for the thrush, a bird whose name survives across the Indo-European world with remarkable consistency: Lithuanian strazdas, Russian drozd, Old English þrysce (modern English thrush), and Latin turdus. From Latin turdus, popular Latin built the verb *exturdire — intensive ex- plus turdus — meaning "to stun, daze." The folk observation behind it is charmingly specific: thrushes gorge on fermenting grape remnants at the wineries and stagger about as if drunk. Even today Italian tordo "thrush" can mean "simpleton," and French still says soûl comme une grive, "drunk as a thrush." The chain passes into French. Old French estordir / estourdir means "to daze, stun, stupefy," and its past participle estourdi first means "dazed," then, by an easy psychological leap, "violent, rash, headstrong" — the behaviour of one whose wits are befuddled. Spanish atordir "to stun, daze" shows the same family in Romance. English borrowed the participle around 1300 as sturdi, and here is the surprise: the earliest English senses are anything but complimentary. In Middle English sturdi means "hard to manage, reckless, violent; fierce, cruel; stern, ill-humoured; disobedient, rebellious." A sturdy beggar (c.1400) is a vagrant strong enough to work and hence, in the logic of the poor laws, a culpable idler. The word in its first English life describes exactly the kind of person a modern speaker would call troublesome, not dependable.
  • The great semantic reversal (late 14th century). Then the meaning rotates on its axis. By the late fourteenth century sturdy begins to describe a person as "solidly built, strong and hardy"; around 1400 it is applied to objects — trees, walls, and other constructions; in Middle English it can even qualify rough waves and raging rivers. The violent energy of the headstrong sense is reborn as physical firmness: fierceness becomes fortitude, recklessness becomes reliability. The pivot is psychologically plausible — the same tough, hard-to-bend quality that made a man rebellious could equally make him unshakeable — and it is one of the most dramatic about-faces in English lexical history. Modern French étourdi keeps the old "giddy, thoughtless" sense, and Spanish atordir still means "to stun," so English alone completed the march from "dazed" to "solidly built."
  • The disputed etymology. The thrush story is traditional but not secure. The Oxford English Dictionary brands the derivation from *exturdire "open to grave objection," chiefly because a rare and semantically odd formation is asked to carry a great deal of weight. The Century Dictionary preferred a link with Latin torpidus "dull, numb" — an alternative *extorpesco "to become dull" — which would give the word a quite different but equally plausible ancestry. Neither hypothesis can be proven; the honest lexicographical position is that the ultimate source of sturdy is uncertain, and that the thrush is the best-known story, not the established fact.
  • Socio-cultural life. Once the word turned positive, English society put it to work. The sturdy beggar remained a legal and moral category through the Elizabethan poor laws, always contrasting the able-bodied "sturdy" pauper with the genuinely impotent poor. Meanwhile the adjective became the stock praise of farmers, sailors, and soldiers — sturdy oak, sturdy frames, sturdy yeomen — and by the 19th century it was a favourite of imperial and industrial prose for anything reliably built to last.
  • The veterinary relic. English quietly preserved a fossil of the old "dazed, giddy" sense: sturdy (or gid) names the brain disease of sheep caused by a tapeworm larva, which makes the animal stagger and turn. The same word that praises a solid pony also names a disease of tottering sheep — a tiny etymological time-capsule in which the "dazed" meaning survives untouched after centuries.
  • Modern register. Today STURDY is wholly positive: strongly and solidly made, firm and resistant to wear, whether of furniture, construction, or a compact little pony. The old recklessness is gone, replaced by quiet, compact reliability — and in Tolkien's sentence, the adjective's very modesty is what makes the pony's loss felt.
From a drunken thrush to a dependable pony: few English words have travelled so far, and few have reversed their moral polarity so completely, as sturdy.
