The plural noun shafts denotes long, slender, roughly cylindrical objects: the straight wooden poles that form the body of a spear, arrow, or lance; narrow columns of light; mechanical rods; and deep, narrow passages sunk into the earth or through a building. The singular shaft and its plural shafts share one conceptual thread — length, straightness, and a purpose for which the object has been shaped — and the word's history, going back to a verb meaning "to scrape, shave, shape", is written into that thread.
  • Primary sense (weapons): the body of a spear, arrow, javelin, or lance — the straight staff to which the head is fixed. In the present context, goblins waving their spears and clashing the shafts against their shields, the word names the spear-staves themselves, and its emphasis on length, straightness, and deliberate craft makes the weapons feel real, handmade, and numerous. The plural form is itself expressive: not one weapon but a host of them clashing together, each shaft a small unit in a larger, organized menace.
  • Sense of light: a shaft of light (or sunbeam, of moonlight) is a long, narrow, roughly cylindrical column of light falling through an opening — through a window, a chink in a roof, the canopy of a forest. The metaphor transfers the shape of the weapon-staff to the shape of the light: long, straight, and bounded.
  • Mechanical and engineering senses: a shaft is any rotating or fixed rod transmitting motion or power (a drive shaft, an axle shaft, a propeller shaft), and by extension any long rod or bar forming part of a machine or instrument.
  • Mining and construction senses: a shaft is a deep, narrow, vertical or inclined passage sunk into the ground (a mine shaft, an elevator shaft, a ventilation shaft) — the earth made into the same long, bounded form as the spear-staff.
  • Architectural and anatomical senses: in architecture a shaft is the body of a column between base and capital; in anatomy, the shaft of a bone is its long central portion; in feathers and tools, the shaft is the stiff central stem or handle. Everywhere the root idea is the same: the long straight part.
  • Figurative senses: "shafts of wit", "shafts of satire", "shafts of criticism" are sharp, pointed remarks thrown like missiles — the word keeps its weapon-shape but is aimed in words; "to shaft someone" (slang, chiefly American) means to cheat or treat harshly, from the image of being struck by or stuck with a weapon, or from the older sense of a bar/stake used to obstruct.
  • Jargon uses: in engineering (drive shaft, jackshaft, output shaft), mining (exploration shaft, winze), architecture (column shaft), photography and stage design (light shaft, gobo shaft), and athletics (a javelin's shaft), the word is a standard technical term; in gunsmithing and archery it is the arrow's or bolt's staff.
The boundaries against neighbouring words sharpen the sense. A pole or a stick is any piece of wood, often rough or casual — a stick is found, a shaft is made; a rod is slender and rigid but usually a machine-part or a cane; a staff is a walking or ceremonial support, thick and blunt; a spear or arrow names the whole weapon — point, shaft, and fletching — whereas shaft isolates only the staff, the part the hand grips and the shield hears; a beam is a broad horizontal structural timber or a wide column of light, wider and more massive than a shaft; a bar is solid and often short or rigid in a different way; a column (of light or of architecture) is thicker and more massive; a ray is a line of light without thickness, whereas a shaft has a visible width; and a stave is a rod or staff, especially a fragment or a musical staff, closer to shaft in craft but narrower in sense. The unifying idea of shaft is a long piece deliberately shaped for a purpose — shaped by scraping and shaving, as its etymology tells us — and that craft is what separates it from the accidental stick and the broad beam. In the warlike context of the present passage, the word carries the full weight of made things: weapons shaped, carried, and struck together, and the reader hears in shafts the clashing of an armed host.
