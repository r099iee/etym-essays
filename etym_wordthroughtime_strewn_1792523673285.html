- [Old English Period (c. 8th–11th c.)] — used as strēowian / ġestrowen in domestic life, describing the physical spreading of rushes and straw across hall floors — [perception: understood as an essential domestic activity for insulation, warmth, and hall hygiene]
- [Middle English & Medieval Religious Custom (c. 12th–15th c.)] — entered sacred biblical translations (Wycliffe) for Palm Sunday, recording the ceremonial spreading of garments and palm branches before Christ — [perception: venerated as a ritual gesture of sacred honor, royal homage, and festive joy]
- [Early Modern English & Poetic Flourishing (c. 16th–17th c.)] — immortalized by Shakespeare and Milton in both tragic funereal lamentations ("strewn thy grave") and idyllic pastoral scenes — [perception: acquired immense poetic resonance, balancing romantic bridal abundance with melancholy mortality]
- [18th–19th Century Romanticism & Landscapes (c. 18th–19th c.)] — standardized in spelling over strown, describing dramatic natural scenery, battlefields, and ancient ruins — [perception: celebrated as an evocative aesthetic marker of natural wildness, sublime ruin, and historical memory]
- [Modern & Contemporary Usage (20th–21st c.)] — extended into planetary astrophysics ("strewn fields" of meteorites) while serving as a versatile compound modifier (litter-strewn, star-strewn) — [perception: universally employed across technical science, vivid journalism, and literary prose to describe dispersed abundance]
