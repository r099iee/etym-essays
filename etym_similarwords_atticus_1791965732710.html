• Attic: The English adjective from the same root, meaning "of Attica or Athens" and, by extension, "refined, classically pure"; it describes a place, a style or a dialect, whereas Atticus is the Roman's personal agnomen and, in this reading, names a man.
  • Attica: The Greek region around Athens, from whose name the adjective and the agnomen derive; it is a territory, not a person, and it never became a Roman surname.
  • Atticism: A rhetorical style modelled on the Attic orators, or a turn of phrase characteristic of Attic Greek; it shares the place-name root but denotes a manner of writing, not the man or his role as correspondent.
  • Atticist: One who cultivates Attic purity of language, especially in the Greek rhetorical quarrels of the Roman period; the word is a label for a stylistic partisan, while Atticus is a proper name with no polemical edge.
  • Athenian: The direct English equivalent of the Greek adjective behind the agnomen; it is generic and can describe anyone from Athens, whereas Atticus was a distinction granted to one Roman for choosing to live there.
  • Titus Pomponius Atticus: The full Roman name of the same man; it is the formal, three-part version used in reference works, while the bare Atticus is the familiar name used by Cicero and by historians.
  • Quintus Caecilius Pomponianus Atticus: His name after adoption by his uncle Quintus Caecilius; it denotes the same individual in his changed legal identity, and its extra elements record a family transaction rather than a different person.
  • Herodes Atticus: A second-century CE Athenian sophist and benefactor, also called Atticus, who is frequently confused with Cicero's friend; he lived two hundred years later, was a Greek rather than a Roman, and is remembered for his building programme in Athens.
  • Cornelius Nepos: The biographer whose Life of Atticus is the main source for the man's career; he is the author, not the subject, and his name belongs to the same circle of late-Republican literary friendship.
  • Pomponius: The nomen of Atticus's gens, shared by his relatives and by later bearers of the family name; it identifies a clan, whereas Atticus identifies the individual and his adopted city.
