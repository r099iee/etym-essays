1. The Latin formation. English incongruous carries its whole history in its morphology. At the base stands Latin congruere, “to come together, meet, agree”, formed with the prefix com- “together” and a verbal base that the Romans themselves connected with ruere, “to rush, fall, hurl oneself”. The picture is kinetic: things that agree are things that run together and coincide, like two lines that meet and continue as one. From congruere Latin made the adjective congruus, “agreeing, fit, suitable” — first used of numbers, then of character and behaviour — and from congruus the negative incongruus, “not agreeing”, which travelled straight into English in the seventeenth century as incongruous. The history of the base -gruere is not fully settled; the equation with ruere is the traditional account (and matches the sense “run together”), but some scholars treat the word as built without a clear extra-family etymology, and the silence of ancient grammarians leaves room for doubt. What is not doubtful is the semantic architecture: in- negates a word for “coming together” — incongruity is, quite literally, a failure of coincidence.

2. From geometry to manners (17th–18th c.). The earliest English uses are learned and technical. In geometry, figures are congruous when they can be laid one upon the other and coincide exactly; in logic and natural philosophy, arguments and observations are congruous when they agree. The geometric sense survives unbroken in modern mathematics — congruent triangles, congruence modulo n — and the abstract noun congruence still carries the exact meaning of “agreement, correspondence” that the same family gave to John Locke and the Newtonians. In the eighteenth century the adjective broadens from the lecture room into criticism and manners: a style, a costume, a ceremony can now be congruous or incongruous with its context. Samuel Johnson, in his Dictionary, registers incongruous as “unsuitable; not agreeing with” — already the full modern double sense of logical inconsistency and social impropriety.

3. The Victorian ironist’s word (19th c.). The nineteenth century made incongruity a literary instrument. Charles Dickens builds whole scenes out of incongruous juxtapositions — the funereal furniture and the mercantile energy, the grand manner and the mean object; Jane Austen had already exploited the incongruous speech of the vulgar; and the criticism of the age, from De Quincey to Ruskin, uses the word to police the harmony between style and subject. Pater’s own use in the passage before us is precisely of this critical kind: he is defending the formal economy of his book — the Winckelmann essay, an eighteenth-century subject inside a Renaissance series — and the word “incongruous” names the objection before rebutting it: the essay is “not incongruous with the studies which precede it” because Winckelmann, though late in date, “belongs in spirit to an earlier age”. The word is doing double work: architectural (the fit of parts in a book) and spiritual (the real date of a mind).

4. The modern spectrum. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries incongruous has settled into a stable spectrum that still runs from the logical to the comic. At the logical end it continues to signal formal inconsistency; in the middle, social and aesthetic mismatch (an incongruous reply, an incongruous setting); at the comic end, the absurd juxtaposition that makes incongruity the technical foundation of humour theory — from Kant’s “laughing at the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing” to modern “incongruity theory”, which holds that humour arises precisely when two frames that do not fit are forced together. The word has thus never lost its root: something incongruous still refuses to coincide, geometrically, logically, socially or comically, with the shape that surrounds it.
