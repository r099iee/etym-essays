startled:
- A frequentative of 'to start' (16th c.) - 'startle' is built on the verb start, 'to leap up, jump suddenly', with the repeated-action suffix -le: a startle is a jump, a flinch, a bodily leaping
- Reflex before thought (17th c.) - the earliest scenes are physical: startled horses and startled deer leaping; the word belongs to the world of hunting before it belongs to people
- The mind is added (18th-19th c.) - 'startle' extends from the body to the mind - to surprise, shock, or alarm - but keeps its involuntary core: a startle is a leap the body makes before the mind decides
- Contemporary - the verb of the betrayed body: the moment discipline fails and the reflex leaps, the spasm of shock that no warrior can suppress

saddle:
- The rider's seat (Old English onward) - a native word for the seat strapped to an animal's back, from the beginning the fixed point from which all a horseman's actions are launched
- The seat of command (16th-17th c.) - 'in the saddle' means in command; to 'unseat' someone is to deprive them of power; the rider's posture of seated control becomes the figure for authority
- Technical widening (19th-20th c.) - the saddle shape spreads to every load-bearing frame: a bicycle saddle, a saddle roof, a saddle joint in engineering
- Contemporary - both the literal rider's seat and the general figure for a supportive, load-bearing base; the word still rests on the idea of sitting, and of control from a fixed point
