• cowardly: The plainest synonym and the closest in sense; it names fear of danger or pain directly, whereas pusillanimous adds the idea of a small and mean spirit and belongs to a much more formal register.
  • faint-hearted: Nearly identical in meaning — a heart that fails at the moment of trial — but it is gentler, more literary and slightly archaic, and it can even be used with sympathy, which pusillanimous never allows.
  • timorous: Describes a nervous, fearful temperament; it is about susceptibility to fear rather than about moral failure, and it carries no accusation of meanness or pettiness.
  • craven: The strongest of the group, implying abject and shameful cowardice; craven is more violent and more archaic, while pusillanimous is colder and more analytical, and can also mean merely petty.
  • spineless: Colloquial and physical in its metaphor, condemning a lack of backbone; the source passage uses it alongside pusillanimous, and the difference is register — spineless is blunt, pusillanimous is learned.
  • dastardly: Archaic and melodramatic, blending cowardice with villainy; it implies a base act as well as a base nature, whereas pusillanimous need not involve any act at all.
  • lily-livered: An archaic colloquial phrase for cowardly, from the old belief that the liver was the seat of courage; it is colourful and jocular, where pusillanimous is grave.
  • chicken-hearted: Informal and mildly childish; it names fear without the moral and intellectual weight that pusillanimous carries.
  • mean-spirited: Shares the "small soul" idea and the sense of pettiness; it targets meanness and grudging behaviour rather than fear, and it does not imply cowardice.
  • ignoble: Condemns a failure of dignity and generosity; it overlaps with the "unworthy" element but not with the specific charge of timidity.
  • unmanly: An older, gendered term of abuse for cowardice; it is now dated and offensive in a way pusillanimous is not, and it says nothing about the size of the soul.
