1. From ‘mind’ to ‘omen’ to ‘show’. The chain begins with PIE *men- ‘to think, remember, be mindful’, the root of Latin mēns ‘mind’, of English mind, mental, memory, and of Greek μένος (ménos) ‘mind, spirit, strength’. Latin builds from it the causative verb monēre ‘to remind, warn, advise’ — the source of monitor, monition, admonish and monument ‘something that reminds’. The Romans then derived monstrum from monēre: originally ‘that which reminds or warns’ — a divine sign, an omen, a portent; by extension any prodigy or unnatural creature, which is the meaning that modern English monster preserves (via Old French monstre). The denominative verb monstrāre ‘to show, point out, demonstrate’ then grew from monstrum: to show someone an omen, to point something out. English demonstrate, remonstrate and the Catholic monstrance (the glass case that ‘shows’ the host) all descend from this verb. Crucially, muster and monster are therefore doublets — one humble, one terrifying, both descendants of the noticing mind.

2. Old French: showing, then reviewing troops. In Vulgar Latin and early Gallo-Romance, monstrāre became Old French mostrer ‘to show, display, exhibit’ — with the -n- of the cluster absorbed before -st- (modern French restored it: montrer). The feminine noun mostre meant ‘a display, a show’, and a particular display quickly came to dominate: the military review. Medieval lords and communes displayed their armed men before campaigns and contracts, both to intimidate and to count; a mostre of troops is a formal showing at which every man answers to his name and receives his pay. This is the exact moment at which the meaning of the word contracts from ‘showing anything’ to ‘showing soldiers for inspection’.

3. Middle English (c. 1300–1400). Via Anglo-Norman (moustrer, mustrer) the word enters English as the verb mustren and noun mustre. From the start it is a word of war and administration: kings muster their armies before expeditions (the chronicles of Edward III's campaigns, later the language of the Hundred Years War and its English captains), sheriffs muster the fyrd-like levies, and the muster roll — the parchment register of names and equipment — becomes a fixed document of military accounting. The gerund musteryng/mustering appears in the same records (15th-century payrolls and commissions of array). Even the world of ceremonial display remembers the old sense: the 15th-century collective-noun tradition gives a muster of peacocks — birds shown off together.

4. The 16th–17th centuries: judgment and the inner world. The inspection frame hardens into idiom: to pass muster (by the 1570s) means to come through the review accepted — the word now carries standards, scrutiny and the possibility of being found wanting. Elizabethan drama is full of mustering armies (Shakespeare's histories repeatedly muster troops and muster spirits), and the same period sees the crucial figurative leap: one can muster courage, muster arguments, muster a smile — gathering scattered inner resources as if calling the reserves to parade. Both branches (drill-ground and psychological) remain alive and mutually reinforcing for the next four centuries.

5. The 19th century: empires, armies, cattle. The American Civil War makes a technical phrase of mustering out: the formal, ceremonial discharge of a regiment or soldier, with final rolls and final pay — the word's second great institutional meaning after mustering in. Simultaneously, colonial Australia and New Zealand give the word its most colourful modern extension: stockmen muster cattle and sheep out of the bush and back-country paddocks into the yards, where the mob is counted; a muster is the round-up, a musterer the rider who does it, and mustering camps dot back-country stations. The word now spans human and animal assemblies, war and peace.

6. The 20th–21st centuries: safety, politics, and the ominous massing. Maritime safety law (in the wake of the Titanic and the SOLAS conventions) fixes the muster station: the designated assembly point where crew and passengers are counted in an emergency, with muster drills rehearsing evacuation. The usage spreads to oil rigs, factories, hospitals and offices — ‘report to your muster point’ is standard signage worldwide. In politics and business, mustering support, opposition, votes, funds or evidence implies disciplined, deliberate organisation. And the gerund retains the slight menace of its martial ancestry: to speak of an enemy's mustering — as the eagles do of the goblins — is to say that scattered forces are being drawn together, counted and made ready, and that their showing will soon be visible to all. The card's word, in short, has travelled from the remembering mind to the reviewing army to the cattle yard and the ship's rail, without once losing the core idea of an ordered gathering that is meant to be seen.
