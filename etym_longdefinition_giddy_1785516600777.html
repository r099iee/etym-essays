Giddy describes a sensation of whirling, dizziness, or lightheadedness — the feeling that the world is tilting, that one has lost one's footing or one's balance — and, by extension, a state of excited or frivolous high spirits. In Tolkien's sentence, "At the best of times heights made Bilbo giddy," the word does double duty: it names the hobbit's physiological vertigo at altitude and quietly summons the word's older, stranger history, in which a giddy person was not merely dizzy but possessed. SENSE 1 — PHYSIOLOGICAL DIZZINESS (the dominant modern sense): - A sensation of spinning, unsteadiness, or lightheadedness; the reeling of the senses, vertigo of heights, the feeling after spinning around or standing up too quickly. - In the card's context the word captures Bilbo's constitutional unsuitability for the vertical extremes of Middle-earth: a creature of snug holes and gentle Shire hills, he is undone by precipices and eagle-borne flights. The giddiness is character-defining — part of what makes Bilbo Bilbo. - Clinical boundary: unlike vertigo (which suggests inner-ear or neurological pathology), giddy in ordinary use suggests transient, non-pathological unsteadiness. SENSE 2 — EMOTIONAL EXCITEMENT (positive valence): - "Giddy with joy / delight / anticipation / excitement" — a pleasurable lightheadedness of high emotion, in which happiness is so overwhelming that it feels physical. This sense emerged in the 18th–19th centuries and is now fully standard. SENSE 3 — FRIVOLOUS, FLIGHTY, LACKING SERIOUSNESS (character trait): - "A giddy young thing"; "giddy behaviour." Describes a person or action that is silly, impulsive, changeable, or morally light. This sense predates the physical one in English literature and descends directly from the Middle English "foolish, mad" sense; it can carry gentle moral disapproval of wantonness and instability. SENSE 4 — ARCHAIC: MAD, CRAZED, DIVINELY POSSESSED: - Old English gydig meant "possessed by a god, divinely inspired or divinely mad, insane." This sense is extinct in ordinary use but preserved in the word's etymology and in literary echoes; Shakespeare still uses giddy for "fickle, inconstant" ("the giddy round of Fortune's wheel"), and the modern word retains a faint archaic echo of those depths. HISTORICAL BRIDGE: The word travelled from divine possession to physiological dizziness through the shared idea of loss of control. A god-possessed person has lost command of the mind; a dizzy person has lost command of the balance and spatial orientation. Middle English broadened the word from "mad" to "foolish, rash," supplying the character sense; the modern emotional sense then grew out of the physical one. One modern word therefore spans the sacred, the insane, the silly, and the dizzy — an unusually complete semantic fossil of a thousand years. USAGE NOTES AND DIFFERENTIATIONS: - Giddy vs dizzy: giddy retains the emotional and flighty overtones; dizzy is more narrowly physiological (vertigo, low blood pressure, inner-ear disturbance). - Giddy vs vertiginous: vertiginous is the formal, medical, or literary term for a spinning sensation; giddy is the older, earthier, more everyday Anglo-Saxon word, carrying emotional and even moral weight alongside the physical sensation. - Giddy vs lightheaded: lightheaded suggests faintness, near-swooning, and is colourless and clinical; giddy can carry gaiety, excitement, or frivolity. - Idioms: "giddy heights" (literally dizzying altitudes; metaphorically, morally vertiginous or dazzlingly successful positions), "giddy with success," "a giddy whirl" (a rapid succession of events). The horseman's cry "giddy-up" is unrelated, from "get up.
