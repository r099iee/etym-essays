INSOLENT is an adjective meaning rude, arrogant, and contemptuously disrespectful, especially toward those in authority, rank, or age; and, by extension, of behavior or speech that flouts the respect expected by a superior. The insolent person does not merely fail to show respect — he or she openly, brazenly shows its opposite: haughty disregard, contempt, or defiant cheek toward anyone entitled to deference. Because the word's Latin ancestor meant "unaccustomed, unusual," the adjective carries an interesting double history: it once described anything out of the ordinary, and only by way of "contrary to custom" did it come to mean "arrogantly out of line."

Core senses
  • Arrogant disrespect toward superiors (primary sense): An insolent tone, answer, or demeanor treats an authority figure — a parent, teacher, officer, judge, monarch — with open contempt. The stress is on defiance of rank and of the expectation of deference.
  • Haughty, presumptuous behavior: Insolence includes the pride that oversteps its place — the servant who presumes, the junior who talks down, the upstart who acts as if entitled to what belongs to his betters.
  • Historical sense (now rare, from Latin): In Latin, insolens meant "unaccustomed, unusual, immoderate, excessive" — anything contrary to the customary. This older sense survives in English only as a ghost: an "insolent novelty" or "insolent rarity" in early texts simply meant something out of the ordinary.
  • Military and institutional discipline: In military and workplace law, "insolence to a superior" is a specific disciplinary offense — insubordination of the tongue — grounds for reprimand, demotion, or, in armed forces, court-martial.
  • Legal and court register: Contempt of court often grows out of "insolent answers" to a judge; historically, insolent words to a monarch could be treated as lèse-majesté or sedition.
  • As noun (rare): "an insolent": The adjective has a rare substantive use — "an insolent," a rude, contemptuous person — found mostly in older literary English.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge
Latin insolens is formed from the negative prefix in- and solens, the present participle of solere "to be accustomed." Its literal meaning was "unaccustomed, not customary," and it was used of anything unusual or immoderate. The bridge to arrogance runs through custom: someone who is "unaccustomed to" proper behavior, or who acts "contrary to custom," is behaving in a way that exceeds the bounds of what society expects — and from "out of bounds" to "overstepping one's rank, contemptuous of convention" is a short step. By classical Latin, insolens already meant "arrogant, haughty, immoderate"; French and English inherited that later sense, and the adjective entered English in the 14th century already meaning contemptuous disrespect.

Related words and grammar
  • insolence (n.): the quality or an act of being insolent; rude contemptuous behavior.
  • insolently (adv.): in an insolent manner.
  • insolent (n., rare): an insolent person.

Register and use
Insolent is a strong, formal-to-literary word. In ordinary speech, rude or cheeky are lighter; insolent is chosen when the disrespect is arrogant, contemptuous, and aimed at authority, and it is a staple of schoolmasterly, military, and legal English. It collocates with answer, tone, remark, child, soldier, and behavior, and it almost always implies a power relationship — someone insolent to someone entitled to respect.
