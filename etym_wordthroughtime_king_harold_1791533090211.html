- Before 1066 — “Harold” is an ordinary high-status English name, and Harold Godwinson is “Earl Harold”: the king’s brother-in-law, the richest magnate in the land, not yet a royal figure.
- January–October 1066 — “King Harold” is a constitutional claim: the witan’s elected monarch, crowned at Westminster, defending the realm against Norway and Normandy. The phrase is official and contested at once.
- After 14 October 1066 — Norman writers turn the name into a byword for perjury and divine punishment; in their chronicles “Harold” is the oath-breaker whose death proves God’s favour for William.
- 12th c. — English chroniclers and hagiographers rehabilitate him as a legitimate king and a national martyr; Waltham Abbey promotes his memory, and the arrow-in-the-eye story begins to circulate.
- 13th–15th c. — “King Harold” becomes a figure of romance and lament; the last English king of the old line, remembered in ballads and local legends, including the tale that he survived as a hermit.
- 16th–17th c. — Reformation antiquaries take an interest in the Saxon past; Harold is used as evidence of English liberties destroyed by Norman tyranny, and the phrase acquires a political, anti-Norman edge.
- 18th–19th c. — Romantic and Victorian nationalism makes him a tragic hero; paintings, poems and school histories fix the image of the doomed king with the arrow in his eye.
- 20th c. — 1066 becomes a schoolroom staple and a heritage industry; “King Harold” appears on pub signs, in re-enactments and in the titles of popular histories, often with a note of English underdog sympathy.
- 21st c. — Historians emphasise contingency, logistics and the mixed composition of Harold’s kingdom; the phrase survives as a compact way of naming the last stand of Anglo-Saxon England.
