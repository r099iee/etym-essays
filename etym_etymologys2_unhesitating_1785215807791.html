PIE *ghais- 'to adhere, stick fast, cling' (traditional handbooks; NOTE: Latin haerere has no fully certain cognates outside Italic — the root is tentative; see note) → Latin haerere 'to stick, cling, adhere, be fixed to a spot' (haereō, haesī, haesum; attested from Plautus, 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin haesitare 'to stick fast; to falter, stammer, be at a loss' (frequentative of haerere, with the repeated -tāre suffix marking habitual action) → Latin haesitātiō 'a stammering, hesitation, doubt' (Cicero, 1st c. BCE) → English hesitation (c. 1570, via Latin haesitationem and French hésitation); English hesitate (c. 1622, from Latin haesitatus and French hésiter) → English hesitating (present participle, 17th c.) → un- + hesitating → English unhesitating (first recorded c. 1767; adverb unhesitatingly c. 1800). Note: the traditional Indo-European root *ghais- 'to adhere' is proposed in the older handbooks, with tentative Baltic and Norse cognates, but modern etymologists treat Latin haerere as an Italic form without secure Indo-European connections; the chain from haerere onward is solidly attested. Note also that Latin haereticus 'heretic' belongs to Greek hairein 'to take, choose' — a different root — and must not be merged into this family.
