Chain 1 — the noun leaf (plural leaves):
↓
PIE (c. 4500-2500 BCE)
*lewbʰ-
"to peel off, strip, break off"
(semantics debated)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE - 200 CE)
*laubą
"leaf, foliage"
↓
Old English (7th-11th c.)
lēaf
"leaf, foliage;"
"sheet of parchment"
↓
Middle English (12th-15th c.)
leef (sg.) / leves (pl.)
"leaf; page;"
"thin sheet of metal"
(voiced f→v in the plural)
↓
Modern English (to present)
leaves
"plural of leaf; thin sheets"

Chain 2 — the verb leave (3rd person singular leaves):
↓
PIE (c. 4500-2500 BCE)
*leyp-
"to stick, smear, remain"
(sense "remain" debated)
↓
Proto-Germanic (reconstructed)
*lībaną
"to remain, stay"
↓
Proto-Germanic (causative) (reconstructed)
*laibijaną
"to cause to remain,"
"to leave behind"
↓
Old English (8th-11th c.)
lǣfan
"to leave, bequeath,"
"let remain"
↓
Middle English (12th-15th c.)
lēven / leven
"to leave, depart from,"
"allow to remain"
↓
Modern English (to present)
leave
"to go away; "
"cause to remain"
↓
Modern English (3rd sg.) (present)
leaves
"goes away; causes to remain"
(identical spelling to the plural of leaf)

Notes: the derivation of Germanic *līb- "to remain" from PIE *leyp- "to stick" is the standard view but rests on semantic inference, and the older link to Greek leípein "to leave" (PIE *leykʷ-) is phonologically impossible in the Germanic labial forms; a third homograph, the noun leave "permission" (Old English lēaf), comes from a different root meaning "pleasure, allowance" (cf. German erlauben "to permit").
