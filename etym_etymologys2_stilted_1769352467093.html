Single root word: stilted (the English adjective formed from the noun stilt plus the adjectival suffix -ed). The whole family descends from the Germanic noun for a walking-pole, itself built on the Indo-European idea of setting something upright. Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *stel-, "to place, set up, stand, erect" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Ancient Greek stéllō "to set in order, equip, send" and stélē "upright stone pillar," Latin stolidus "stiff, dull, immobile," and the Germanic family of standing supports (still, stool, stall, German stellen "to place"). NOTE: the connection of the walking-pole word to this root is the traditional account and is generally accepted, but the exact intermediate formation is not directly attested and is therefore debated. 2. Proto-Germanic *stelt- (noun *steltijǭ / *steltǭ), "a walking pole, a stilt, a wooden prop with a footrest" — reconstructed, Proto-Germanic era, c. 500 BCE–c. 200 CE. Attested descendants cluster in North Sea Germanic: Middle Low German stilte, Middle Dutch stilte / stelte (modern Dutch stelt), Old Frisian steltera, and, in a parallel form, German Stelze (with its verb stelzen, "to walk on stilts"). 3. Middle English stilte, "a walking pole, a stilt, a prop or support for walking raised above the ground" — attested c. 1300s; the OED treats the English noun as borrowed from Middle Low German or Middle Dutch stilte via North Sea coastal trade, though some scholars allow a native development of the same Germanic stock. This borrowing-versus-native development is the minor nuance in the record. 4. English stilt (noun), "one of a pair of tall poles fitted with footrests for walking raised above the ground; a supporting post or pile; by extension the long-legged shorebird" — attested from the 14th c. onward. 5. English stilt + the adjectival suffix -ed —> stilted, "raised or built on stilts, supported on posts" — the literal architectural and physical sense attested from the early 17th c. (c. 1620s); the figurative sense "artificially elevated in style, pompous, stiffly formal" develops from c. 1775–1820. Final node: stilted. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The pole-noun is solidly Germanic but appears only in North Sea Germanic (Low German, Dutch, Frisian, English) and in German's parallel Stelze. Whether it derives directly from PIE *stel- "to set up" via a suffixal formation or from a separate expressive Germanic base is debated; no attested intermediate form can be reconstructed with certainty. The OED's position is that the English noun was borrowed from Low German or Dutch rather than inherited natively.
