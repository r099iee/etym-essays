Decrees is the plural of decree, a noun (and, less often, a verb) whose core sense is an authoritative order or decision that has the force of law. It names something pronounced rather than deliberated: a ruling issued by a sovereign, a government, a court, or a religious authority, binding on those it addresses because of the office behind it rather than because of any consent it has won. The word is formal and faintly archaic in ordinary speech, yet it remains standard in law, canon law, and political journalism.

In the source passage the word is doing precise work. The writer contrasts two ways in which law comes into being: “common law” grows up out of the customs of a group and is derived from those customs, while “positive law” is handed down by the government. Decrees names the instruments of that second, enacted kind of law — the formal pronouncements through which an authority imposes rules on a society that has not evolved them for itself. The plural matters: law of this kind arrives as a series of discrete acts, each datable and each attributable to a ruler, a cabinet, or a council, rather than as an organic growth out of habit and dispute.

Primary senses

  • Sovereign or governmental order. An official pronouncement by a head of state or ruling body that carries legal force, often without prior legislative debate: a royal decree, a presidential decree, governing by decree. This is the dominant modern sense in political reporting, and it is the sense closest to the source passage.
  • Judicial decision. In the English Court of Chancery and in Scots law a decree was the final order of a court determining a suit — hence the Scottish spelling decreet and the phrase decree of the Court of Session. English procedure replaced “decree” with “judgment” and “order” in the Judicature Acts of 1873–75, but the word survived in matrimonial causes as decree nisi and decree absolute until those terms were themselves replaced in England and Wales by conditional order and final order in 2022.
  • Ecclesiastical legislation. A decree of a Church council or pope is a binding doctrinal or disciplinary ruling. The decrees of the Council of Trent (1545–63) reshaped Catholic practice for centuries, and Gratian's twelfth-century Decretum — a private collection that became the foundation of canon-law study — takes its familiar title from the same word.
  • Roman-law technicality. In classical Roman law a decretum was specifically the emperor's decision when he sat as a judge, one of the forms of imperial constitutio alongside edicts, mandates, and rescripts.
  • Verb. To decree is to ordain or command by decree: the king decreed that the tax be raised. The verb extends easily to fate, Providence, or any impersonal force: as fate had decreed, heaven decreed otherwise.

Figurative and extended uses

Because a decree issues from a single will, the word readily becomes a metaphor for any irresistible or unaccountable dictate. The decrees of fashion, the decrees of custom, or the decrees of the market transfer the political image to social pressures that feel as binding as law and as little open to argument. The tone in such phrases is usually ironic or resigned. A related idiom, by decree, signals government without consent — rule imposed from above — and is almost always pejorative in democratic politics.

Why the senses hang together

Every branch descends from the Latin notion of deciding. Decernere meant to sift a matter, settle it, and bring it to a determination; a judge's decretum and a king's decretum are both, etymologically, “things decided.” The drift from “decision” to “command” tracks the drift of power itself: what begins as a ruling on one dispute becomes, in the hands of a sovereign, a general rule for the future. That is why a single word can name both a court order in a private lawsuit and an instrument of state policy.

Register, connotation, collocations

  • Strongly formal, and rarely neutral in modern politics: “the government decreed” implies speed, secrecy, and the bypassing of parliament.
  • Frequent collocations: issue, sign, publish, revoke, or defy a decree; royal, presidential, emergency, military, and consent decree; decree nisi, decree absolute.
  • A consent decree in United States law is a court-supervised settlement to which the parties agree — an odd hybrid in which the “decree” is negotiated rather than imposed.
  • The verb is largely confined to formal or literary registers; everyday speech prefers order, rule, or lay down.
  • The implied contrast is with statute (enacted by a legislature after debate) and with custom (grown by usage). A decree is law made by an authority acting alone.

Grammar and formation

The noun pluralises regularly as decrees, and the same form serves as the third-person singular of the verb. The past tense is decreed and the participle decreeing, with the final ee simply taking the endings rather than doubling a consonant. Related derivatives include decretal (a papal letter settling a point of canon law, and the adjective from it), the rare adjectives decretive and decretory, and the abstract noun decretal in its legal-historical use.

In this passage

Read against its context, decrees carries a sociological argument as well as a legal meaning. Customary law is described as growing, positive law as being handed down; the decree is the visible moment of handing down. The sentence quietly distinguishes societies in which law is immanent in habit from societies in which law is the command of a chief, and it uses the plural to suggest that positive law accumulates as a body of separate enactments rather than as a single code.
