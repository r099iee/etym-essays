The adjective residuary descends from the Latin verb of sitting. PIE *sed-, to sit, is among the most stable roots, giving English sit, seat, settle, sessile, Greek hedra (seat), and Latin sedēre (to sit). With the prefix re-, back, Latin forms residēre, to sit back, remain seated, or stay behind when others go — the posture of what is left. Its neuter participle-noun residuum means what remains, the balance, the leftover. Roman accountants, jurists, and tax farmers use residuum for arrears and balances; Ulpian and the Digest use it for estate remainders, the ancestor of probate usage. Late Latin keeps residuus as adjective, remaining, left over.

  • PIE *sed-: sitting as staying, the semantic seed of remainder as what stays put.
  • Latin sedēre: to sit, settle, sink down, with compounds preside, subside, supersede.
  • Latin residēre/residuum: to sit back, and the noun for balance or arrears, administrative and legal from the Republic onward.
  • Old French residue: borrowed into Anglo-Norman law as residue, remainder of an account or estate.

Middle English residue enters in the fourteenth century through law French, first as exchequer jargon for arrears, then as probate jargon for what is left after legacies. Chaucer and manorial rolls already know residue in this double sense. The adjective residuary is a seventeenth-century English formation on residue plus the Latinate suffix -ary (as in testamentary, pecuniary), first recorded in legal treatises and will forms. Residuary legatee becomes a fixed collocation by the early eighteenth century, when Chancery reports teem with disputes over residuary bequests, lapsed shares, and void gifts falling into residue. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) and Blackstone's Commentaries (1765–69) entrench it for lay and professional readers: Blackstone explains that the residuary legatee takes the surplus after debts and legacies, a doctrine Dickens could assume his readers knew.

  • Law-French phase (c. 1300–1600): residue as term of art, residue of issues, profits, and goods.
  • Early Modern formation (c. 1600s): residuary coined to qualify legatee, estate, and devise with precision.
  • Georgian litigation: Chancery battles over residue make the word familiar to newspaper readers.
  • Victorian wills: printed will forms with a blank for residuary beneficiary spread the term to the middle class; Dickens uses it as instant characterisation of lonely wealth.
  • Modern statutes: Administration of Estates Acts and American probate codes retain residuary clause, residuary trust, residuary devise, pronounced with the yod /dj/ in RP.

Phonetically the development is borrowing without violent change: Latin residuum to French residu to English residue /rɛzɪdjuː/, then suffixation to residuary with stress shift to the second syllable. The s voices to /z/ between vowels, the u palatalises to /dj/. No serious debate surrounds the chain; *sed- to residuum is textbook. The only nuance is the distinction lawyers insist upon between residuary and residual: both from the same Latin source, but English split them, reserving residuary for wills and constitutions and leaving residual for science and economics, a division dictionaries record from the nineteenth century onward. That split is why Scrooge is a residuary legatee and never a residual one: law keeps its own leftover word.
