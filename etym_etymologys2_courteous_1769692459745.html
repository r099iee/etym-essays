PIE *gher- "to grasp, enclose, surround" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin hortus "enclosed garden, yard" (attested 1st c. BCE; the root's enclosure sense kept in English garden via Germanic *gardaz) → Latin cohors, genitive cohortis "enclosed yard; a company of men, retinue, military unit" — com- "together" + hortus "enclosure" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE; source of English cohort) → Vulgar Latin *curtis / *cortis "courtyard of a farmstead or manor; the estate and its retinue" — syncopated contraction of cohors (c. 5th–8th c. CE) → Old French cort / curt "royal court, palace, assembly of the king's household" (9th–11th c. CE) → Old French curteis / corteis (adjective) "of the court, courtly, polished, well-mannered" — cort + suffix -eis (11th–12th c. CE; also in Anglo-Norman) → Middle English curteis, curteous "having courtly manners, gracious, polite" (13th c. CE) → Early Modern English courteous — deliberate 16th-century "etymologizing" respelling aligning the word with court + -eous (c. 16th c. CE) → courteous ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The 16th-century change from curteis to courteous is a learned respelling, not a sound change; the medieval form curteis survives independently in the surname Curtis and in the archaic curteous. The Latin chain cohors → *curtis is the standard account, but note that curtis and cortis are reconstructed Vulgar Latin forms — the exact intermediate is inferred rather than directly attested.
