- 13th–14th c. 'A dwelling, residence, or habitation' — entered Middle English from French with the simple sense of a place where one stays or dwells.
- 15th–17th c. 'A lord's landed estate' — grows into the feudal legal unit: the lord's house, demesne lands, tenant holdings, and attached manorial rights.
- 18th–19th c. 'A great country house and its grounds' — in the Regency period the word signals landed-gentry status, rank, and the right to hunt and rule the surrounding countryside.
- Contemporary 'A large country house; (British informal) one's home territory' — both a stately-home label and a slang term for the area a local officer 'runs'.
