- [Old English Roots of 'Ever' (c. 8th–11th c.)] — flourished as the uncontracted adverb ǣfre in Anglo-Saxon verse (Beowulf) and laws — [perception: understood as a solemn, binding temporal term meaning at all times or perpetually]
- [Middle English Period (c. 12th–15th c.)] — evolved into ever / euere; early poets and scribes began occasionally clipping vowels in rapid verse delivery — [perception: functioning as a core Germanic adverb of time and universality in Chaucerian tales]
- [Renaissance & Elizabethan Metrical Codification (c. 16th–17th c.)] — formally coined and codified with an apostrophe (e'er) by Shakespeare and Milton to fit strict iambic pentameter — [perception: embraced as an essential, elegant poetic tool preserving metrical harmony]
- [Augustan & 19th Century Romantic Apotheosis (c. 18th–19th c.)] — widely employed across heroic couplets (Pope) and Romantic nature poetry (Wordsworth, Keats, Bryant) — [perception: revered as a noble, elevated stylistic marker conveying introspective antiquity and artistic grace]
- [Modern & Contemporary Status (20th–21st c.)] — retired from standard prose and free verse, preserved exclusively in historical editions, liturgical texts, and dramatic performances — [perception: recognized as an unmistakable emblem of classic English poetic heritage]
