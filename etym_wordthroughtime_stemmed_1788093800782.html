- 1300s–1400s: Middle English stemmen, a Norse loan: damming streams and stopping flows — practical, hydraulic, a craftsman's and waterman's word of low register.
- 1500s–1600s: extends from water to blood and tears — 'stemming' becomes staunching (medical and barber-surgeon usage); simultaneously the nautical phrase 'stem the tide' arises from the ship's stem (prow) pressing against the current; perception: forceful, effortful resistance in both senses.
- 1700s–1800s: idiomatic consolidation — 'stem the tide/current' becomes a set phrase for resisting overwhelming forces; nautical heroics colour the idiom; medicine continues 'stemming the blood'; in everyday speech the dam-sense and prow-sense of the idiom fuse for good.
- Modern: formal and journalistic — 'stem the onslaught/outflow/panic'; casual speech prefers stop, halt, or staunch, so 'stemmed' now reads slightly official, effortful, barely-held; meanwhile the unrelated botanical 'stemmed' (stems removed, or long-stemmed) and 'stem from' (originate) add homonym noise that dictionaries must constantly sort out.
