[CHAIN FOR thrust]
1. PIE *treud- “to press, push, squeeze” (reconstructed; attested in Latin trūdere, “to push, shove, thrust,” and in the Germanic family of pressure and threat).
2. Old Norse þrysta “to press, squeeze, thrust” (attested; the northern, Germanic branch of the root).
3. Middle English thrusten “to press, push, thrust” (c. 1300), borrowed from Old Norse.
4. English thrust, with past and past participle identical: thrust, thrust, thrust (attested; a native strong pattern). The noun (the thrust of a sword, rocket thrust) is a later formation from the verb.

NOTE ON COGNATES: The same PIE root produced Latin trūdere, whose compounds survive in English as intrude, extrude, protrude, obtrude, and abstruse (from abstrūdere, “to push away, to hide”), and the Germanic branch produced Old English þrēat, “pressure, oppression; a crowd,” the ancestor of threat.

[CHAIN FOR hurled]
1. No PIE ancestry can be established: the verb rests on an imitative Germanic base *hurr-, “rapid motion; roaring sound,” probably onomatopoeic in origin (attested in Low German hurreln, “to throw, to dash,” and East Frisian hurreln, “to roar, to bluster”).
2. Middle English hurlen “to run against, to collide” (c. 1225), then “to throw forcibly” (c. 1300), then “to rush violently” (late 14th c.).
3. English hurled, past of hurl (attested; the noun hurl, “a violent throw,” 1520s; hurly-burly, 1530s).

NOTE ON NUANCE: Since early Middle English, hurl has been confused with hurtle, the frequentative of hurt, from Old French hurter, “to strike.” The OED draws the classic boundary: “in hurtle the notion is forcible collision, in hurl forcible projection.” Keep the two distinct.

[CHAIN FOR sprawled]
1. PIE *sper- “to scatter, spread” (reconstructed; attested in Latin spargere, “to scatter, sprinkle,” and in the Germanic family of spreading). The link to sprawl is probable but not certain; some handbooks treat the Germanic verb as of imitative or uncertain origin.
2. Proto-Germanic *spreaw- “to spread out, sprawl” (reconstructed; attested by Old English sprēawlian, Norwegian sprala, “to sprawl, wriggle,” Danish sprælde, “to sprawl, jerk”).
3. Old English sprēawlian “to move convulsively, to writhe” (attested; the earliest sense is convulsive motion, not posture).
4. Middle English spraulen “to sprawl, to writhe” (attested).
5. English sprawled, past of sprawl (attested; the posture sense — lying with limbs flung out — develops in the 16th–17th centuries; urban sprawl is a 20th-century extension).

NOTE ON NUANCE: The reconstruction from PIE *sper- through Proto-Germanic *spreaw- is standard in handbooks (cf. Pokorny), but the Germanic forms could equally be independent expressive creations; treat the PIE link as probable, not certain.
