- [Late 14th c. Late Middle English] — Formed in Middle English (as chastisour / chastiser) from the verb chastisen, denoting an authoritative moral disciplinarian, parental corrector, or feudal punisher - [perception: recognized as a legitimate, necessary instrument of moral order and social discipline]
- [15th–16th c. Tudor & Elizabethan Literature] — Applied to monarchs, military commanders, and ecclesiastical judges charged with maintaining divine law and putting down insurrections - [perception: viewed with awe as a righteous administrator of justice and defender of the commonwealth]
- [17th–18th c. Scriptural, Epic & Augustan English] — Adopted into high literary and biblical translations as an epithet for divine wrath (God as the chastiser of unrepentant nations) and in translations of classical epics (e.g., Homer, Virgil) and Sanskrit epics (e.g., Valmiki's Ramayana, where epic heroes are termed "chastisers of foes") - [perception: majestic, severe, and heroic designation of righteous martial supremacy]
- [19th c. Victorian Era] — Prevalent in pedagogical literature and domestic treatises regarding paternal authority and corporal punishment, while satirists were characterized as verbal chastisers of societal folly - [perception: stern, uncompromising authority figure enforcing moral and academic rectitude]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Primarily retained in epic fantasy literature, classical translations, and stylized political commentary to characterize an unyielding prosecutor, severe coach, or righteous warrior - [perception: an evocative, dignified agent noun carrying echoes of ancient justice and martial grandeur]
