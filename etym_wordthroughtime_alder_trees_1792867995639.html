- Old English — the wet-wood tree: alor names the riverside tree of piles and shields, dark wood for dark water.
- Medieval — the bleeding tree: cut alder sap runs orange-red, and herbals mark it a tree of omens, whistles, and fairy timber.
- Early modern — the builder's secret: millwrights and merchants learn alder piles never rot underwater, and whole cities are pinned on alder trunks.
- 18th–19th c. — the picturesque fringe: landscape gardeners plant alders to darken water and poets to sadden it; the leaning riverside tree becomes a stock melancholy.
- 20th c. — the ecologist's ally: nitrogen-fixing roots make alder the nurse-tree of restored wetlands and strip-mine spoil.
- Today — compound and simplex side by side: alder in nurseries and papers, alder-trees in poetry and prose where the writer wants the trees to stand and lean.
