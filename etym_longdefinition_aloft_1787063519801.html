Aloft is an adverb (also used predicatively and occasionally as an adjective) meaning "up in the air, high above the ground, or raised and held at a height". It describes both position and motion at a height: a flag flies aloft, a bird rises aloft, a rocket climbs aloft, a sailor climbs aloft into the rigging, and a torch is held aloft to light a dark place. In the source passage, the hobbit crosses the treasure floor "holding his tiny light aloft" — raising his small lamp high so its beam spreads into the darkness around him.

Primary senses

  • In the air; high above the ground: "The kite soared aloft." A positional sense, usually visual and often poetic; what is aloft is suspended in the open air rather than merely placed on a high shelf.
  • Raised or held up high: "He held the torch aloft." The sense of deliberate lifting — a hand, arm, banner, glass or weapon raised above its normal level, often for display, light or signal.
  • Upwards, towards the sky: "The rocket climbed aloft." A directional sense nearly equal to "upwards", stressing movement away from the ground.

Secondary and specialised senses

  • Nautical usage: In sailing, aloft means up in the rigging — among the masts, yards, spars and sails, above the deck. A sailor ordered "aloft" must climb the rigging to set, furl or repair sails. This is the oldest and most persistent technical sense, and it gives the word its flavour of height, skill and risk.
  • Meteorological and aviation usage: In weather reporting, "aloft" refers to conditions in the upper atmosphere ("winds aloft", "temperatures aloft"). In aviation, "aloft" means in the air rather than on the ground ("time aloft", "getting aloft").
  • Figurative uses: "With spirits aloft" — elevated in mood; a person "lifted aloft" by praise or fame. Here the height is emotional or social, not physical: the word suggests elevation, exaltation and loftiness of feeling.

Register, grammar and style

  • Formality: Aloft is more elevated than "up" or "above"; it belongs to literary prose, poetry, journalism and technical registers, and is chosen when the writer wants a two-syllable word that implies height with a touch of grandeur.
  • Grammar: As an adverb of place it commonly follows the verb ("rise aloft", "float aloft", "hold aloft"), and it can follow a noun (a "lantern held aloft"). It takes no object and has no comparative or superlative forms.
  • Fossilised origin: Historically aloft is the Norse preposition á ("on, in") fused with the noun lopt ("air, sky"), now a single unanalysable adverb — an example of grammaticalisation.
  • Antonyms and near-relatives: Opposites include below, beneath, down, low and grounded. Related words built on the same root include loft (a raised space; to hit a ball high), lofty (very high; also haughty), lift (to raise) and elevated.

Phrases and patterns

  • Common collocations: "hold/raise/lift/throw aloft", "soar/rise/climb/fly aloft", "winds aloft", "sent aloft", "reach aloft".
  • The a- prefix (from "on, in") also appears in aboard, abed, ashore, astride, afoot and aground — all originally "on/in" plus a noun, now fused into single adverbs.
  • In fantasy and adventure prose, aloft is a favourite word for lamps, torches and weapons raised in dark or dramatic settings, exactly as Tolkien uses it for the hobbit's light in the dragon's cavern.
