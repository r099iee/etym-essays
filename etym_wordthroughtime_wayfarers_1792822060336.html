- 15th c. — the compound is born: wayfarer (way + farer, one who journeys) names the foot-traveller of the medieval road, owed alms and shelter.
- 16th–17th c. — Pilgrims and players: reformations and persecutions fill the roads with religious wayfarers, while ballads romanticise them as carriers of news and song.
- 18th c. — the suspicious stranger: enclosure and vagrancy laws make every wayfarer a questionable character; charity thins and the locked door multiplies.
- 19th c. — poets reclaim the road: Wordsworth's wanderers and Stevenson's walking tours baptise the wayfarer as the free soul, and chapels, inns, and hymns keep the old kindness alive.
- Early 20th c. — boots for pleasure: hiking clubs and youth hostels turn wayfaring into healthy leisure, and Tolkien walking the Berkshire hills absorbs the romance first-hand.
- 1952 — the sunglasses: Ray-Ban's Wayfarer borrows the road's cool for the motor age, and the foot-traveller's name rides henceforth on millions of car-driving noses.
- Today — fantasy's casting call: strange wayfarers in Tolkien's Shire taught every tavern scene since that the cloaked walker means adventure; hostels, trails, and games keep the feet moving.
