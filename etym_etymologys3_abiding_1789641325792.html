ABIDING is the present participle of abide, Old English ābīdan, compounded from ā-, intensive or completive prefix, plus bīdan, to wait, remain, or dwell. Bīdan continues Proto-Germanic *bīdaną, to wait or endure, cognate with Old Saxon bīdan, Old High German bītan, Old Norse bíða, and Gothic beidan, all meaning to await or endure. Pokorny, Etymonline, and the Oxford English Dictionary derive *bīdaną from PIE *bheydh-, to trust, confide, or await, also seen in Greek peithesthai, to be persuaded, and Latin fidere, to trust. The semantic pathway trust someone → await confidently → endure waiting → remain steadfast explains the whole English range from patience to permanence.

Phonetically, the form is conservative. PIE *bheydh- with aspirated stops yields Germanic *bīd- by Grimm Law, with long ī from compensatory processes. Old English bīdan was pronounced bee-dahn with long vowel and infinitive ending. Prefix ā-, pronounced ah, intensified to ābīdan, ah-bee-dahn. In Middle English, long ī diphthongised during the Great Vowel Shift to modern /aɪ/, giving abide /əˈbaɪd/ with reduced initial vowel. The participial ending -ing replaces Old English -ende (abidende) after the 14th-century merger of -ing and -ende in the south, yielding abiding /əˈbaɪdɪŋ/ in Received Pronunciation with stress on the second syllable. Spelling retains silent e before -ing deletion by rule.

  • Old English ābīdan: to await, expect, remain, endure, suffer — Beowulf and homilies use it for warriors awaiting battle and souls enduring judgement. Physical waiting and moral endurance fused.
  • Early Middle English abiden: to dwell, sojourn, await one time — Ancrene Wisse and chronicles use it for residing in a cell and awaiting death. Domiciliary sense strengthens.
  • Late Middle English and King James Bible (1611): abide in me and I in you (John 15:4), the Lord abideth for ever — abiding acquires mystical permanence, dwelling-with-God. Abiding love, abiding city (Hebrews 13:14, we have here no continuing city) fix the collocation.
  • Early modern legal and everyday abide by: to abide by a decision, to abide by the rules — to stand by and submit to. The particle by redirects endurance into obedience.
  • 18th–19th centuries: literary abiding — abiding interest, abiding memory, abiding principles — Romantic and Victorian prose favours the participle as dignified adjective for what survives fashion.
  • Modern contraction: finite abide retreats to formal, biblical, and legal registers (I cannot abide him, abide by), while adjectival abiding thrives in abiding love, abiding memory, abiding citizen in immigration law.

Morphologically, ā- plus bīdan follows a productive Old English pattern where ā- perfectivises: bīdan to wait becomes ābīdan to wait out, to endure to the end. Cognate formations include arise, awake, and abide itself. The present participle -ing, originally verbal noun, becomes attributive adjective: an abiding love is literally a love that abides, that waits out time. This explains why ABIDING precedes nouns of feeling, memory, and principle rather than objects: we say abiding affection, not abiding chair, because only abstractions can wait.

Socio-culturally, ABIDING carries biblical gravity. The King James phrases abiding love, abiding truth, abiding city saturated sermons, hymns (Abide with me, 1847), and funeral liturgy. To call love abiding in twentieth-century biography, as the source does — His abiding love for poetry and opera — is to borrow that consecrated tone for secular devotion, marking Baker passion as steadfast, almost religious, and exceptional in one of his social background. The class note matters: opera and poetry required autodidact perseverance for a bank clerk son without university or coterie; abiding dignifies solitary fidelity against metropolitan networking.

Literary attestations are dense. Shakespeare: if thou dost love, pronounce it faithfully, or love me with abiding commitment. Wordsworth Prelude on abiding things of nature. Tennyson In Memoriam on abiding memory. George Eliot on abiding principles. Modern collocations — abiding memory of the dead, abiding interest in sport, abiding citizen (United States immigration term for one who continuously resides) — all preserve endurance-through-time. The antonymic pole is fleeting, passing, ephemeral: abiding defines itself against modern transience, which is why obituaries and memorials favour it.
