The plural frills names, first, the physical ornaments: narrow strips of fabric gathered, pleated, or crimped along one edge and sewn to collars, cuffs, shirtfronts, curtains, and trimmings so that the free edge stands out in a rippling flounce. In dress history the frill is a smaller, stiffer cousin of the ruffle. But the word’s modern weight is figurative: frills are the superfluous additions, the needless embellishments, the non-essential conveniences and refinements that can be stripped away without destroying the thing itself — the sense crystallized in the commercial idiom ‘no-frills’ for budget goods, flights, and services. Will Durant’s phrase ‘laces and frills’ uses exactly this double life: the Bedouins may be intelligent, courageous, and noble, but without the continuity of food ‘nothing will remain for the laces and frills, the curtsies and amenities, the arts and comforts, of civilization’ — the frills are the surplus ornament of a settled culture.

Core senses.

  • Fabric ornament: a gathered, pleated, or fluted strip of cloth used as a decorative edging or insertion on garments (collar frills, cuffs), household linen, and drapery; a ruffle-like flounce, usually smaller and more bundled than a ruffle proper.
  • Superfluous ornamentation (figurative): needless decoration, flourish, or elaboration added to writing, speech, design, or procedure; the padded, showy extras of a thing. ‘All the frills’ = every possible extra; ‘without frills’ = plain, direct, essential.
  • Non-essential amenities: comforts, conveniences, or services beyond the bare minimum, especially in commercial contexts: a no-frills airline sells a seat and safety, not lounges and meals.

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Older verb senses: to ‘frill’ something is to ornament with frills, or to curl, crimp, or quiver (of hair, paper, or the tremulous ‘frilling’ of fish fins and bird feathers) — the quivering-edge image behind all the uses.
  • Natural history: the frilled lizard of Australia and New Guinea (Chlamydosaurus kingii, described 1825) raises a wide frill of skin about its neck when alarmed — the membrane itself is called the frill; a parallel is the frilled-neck display of some birds.
  • Photography jargon (early 20th c.): ‘frilling’ is the curling or folding away of the gelatin emulsion from the edges of a photographic plate during processing — the film peeling up like a frilled edge.
  • Idioms: ‘frills and furbelows’ (fussy trimming of every kind), ‘no frills attached’, ‘the frills’ as the extra niceties of life (‘the luxury flats come with all the frills’).

Semantic bridge. The pathway from fabric to metaphor is transparent: a frill is visibly attached to a garment — it does no work, it only decorates, and it can be cut away with scissors without harming the cloth. What can be cut away is by definition non-essential, so by the nineteenth century the word had become the standard name for the dispensable extras of any enterprise: the ornament of prose, the flourish of ceremony, the amenities of a service. The commercial ‘no-frills’ format (airlines, supermarkets, lodging) completed the transfer: it promises the essential function at minimum cost by explicitly discarding the frills. The noun’s origin is unknown (see the derivation note), which only underscores how purely visual the birth of the word was: its whole later career hangs on a flutter of cloth.
