A grate is the metal frame of iron bars fitted inside a fireplace, on which coals or wood are laid to burn while air passes beneath them and ash falls through to the hearth below. The context — “There is no fire in the grate, and hunger has made him faint” — is the classic hearth-and-poverty image: the grate stands for the fire itself, cold and empty. The word descends, through French, from Latin cratis “wickerwork, hurdle”, so that the iron fire-frame and the farmer’s woven hurdle are etymological cousins: the grate is, in origin, a kind of intertwined lattice of bars. Structurally, the traditional fire grate is a set of horizontal fire-bars carried in an iron frame — sometimes with a flat back and raised front (the “hob grate” or range grate), sometimes a simple movable basket — with an ash-pan and fender below, so that the burning coal stands in a current of air, drawing draught upward and dropping clinker and ash through. The same lattice principle reappears in the furnace grate of steam boilers and the grate of a cooking stove, where spaced bars admit combustion air and let out the residue; it is one continuous technical family from hearth to engine-room.

  • Fireplace grate (core sense): the barred iron stand for fuel in a hearth, associated with fender, ash-pan, and poker; in Victorian houses the “grate” was the whole fitted fire surround of the parlour, and “to black the grate” meant to polish its iron with black-lead paste.
  • A grating of bars (general sense): any lattice of iron or wooden bars covering an opening — a drain grate, sewer grate, floor grate, or window grate — where the word keeps its original sense of “grille”.
  • Fire basket: in many modern houses a movable fire basket of bars serves the same purpose as the fixed grate; the words are near-synonyms, the basket being the portable form.
  • Homograph verb (a different word): to grate is to reduce food to shreds by rubbing it against a rough-edged implement (a grater); of metal on metal, to rub with a harsh rasping sound; and figuratively, of an irritation, to “grate on one’s nerves”. This verb is a separate word, from Old French grater “to scrape” (of Germanic origin, related to German kratzen “to scratch”), and is not related to the fireplace noun.
  • Family of the noun: grill (a cooking rack of bars), grille (the ornamental barred screen of a window or car front), gridiron, and lattice and hurdle are the noun’s relatives from Latin cratis and its diminutive craticula (“small gridiron”), passing through French — the same ancient wickerwork image worked in modern metal.

Semantic bridge. The noun’s history is a history of material change: a lattice woven of willow twigs (cratis) became, with the spread of ironwork in the Middle Ages, a lattice of iron bars; when raised fireplaces acquired their bar-frames in the later Middle Ages, the grating word was applied to the fuel-holder, and the hearth sense gradually won out over the window and cage senses. Today the word is split between the old-fashioned fireside (the empty grate, the glowing grate — a staple of Victorian fiction, and of Oscar Wilde’s The Happy Prince (1888), where the little seamstress’s boy has no fire in the grate) and the modern municipal metalwork (the drain grate). Both are the same word: the interwoven lattice of bars.
