Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*nas- + *torkʷ- / *terkʷ-
"nose" + "to twist, turn, wind"
[Compound PIE nominal roots yielding a vivid physical descriptor]
↓
Proto-Italic (reconstructed)
*nās- + *torkʷ-ē-
"nose-twisting, pungent irritant"
[Ancestral Italic formation for sharp, tear-inducing plants]
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
nāsturtium
"watercress; literally nose-twister"
[From nāsus ("nose") + torquēre ("to twist"); attested by Pliny and Varro]
↓
Late Renaissance Botanical Latin (c. 1570)
nasturtium indicum
"Indian cress; Tropaeolum majus from Peru"
[Semantic transfer from watercress to Andean garden creeper based on taste]
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1600)
nasturtium
"pungent South American flowering vine"
[Direct borrowing into English floriculture and herbal medicine]
↓
18th–19th c. Dialectal English (c. 1770)
nasturtian
"cottage garden flower; dialectal variant"
[Phonological assimilation replacing Latin -ium with vernacular -ian]
↓
Modern English / Literary English (present)
nasturtians
"trailing garden nasturtiums (plural)"
[Preserved prominently in English literature by J.R.R. Tolkien]
