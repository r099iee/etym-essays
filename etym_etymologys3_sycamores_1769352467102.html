The word sycamore is one of the great wanderers of botanical nomenclature — a name that has walked across three unrelated families of trees over roughly 2,500 years without changing its sound. It begins in ancient Greece, where sukon (fig) and moron (black mulberry) — both themselves words of uncertain, probably pre-Greek substrate origin, not securely Indo-European — were joined into sukomoros, "the fig-mulberry," naming a real tree of Egypt and the Levant, Ficus sycomorus, which bore edible fig-like fruit beneath broad, mulberry-like leaves. This compound entered Latin as sycomorus and Old French as sicamor, and reached Middle English in the 14th century through the great Bible translations (the Wycliffite version), where it named the tree climbed by Zacchaeus in the Gospel of Luke. For English-speaking readers, then, sycamore was first a biblical word for an exotic eastern tree, freighted with scriptural associations. The semantic drift begins when medieval and Renaissance Europeans, who could not grow the real Ficus sycomorus in their cold climate, started re-applying the name to local trees whose broad leafy canopies reminded them of biblical illustrations:
  • 16th-century Britain: botanists and gardeners transfer sycamore to Acer pseudoplatanus, the great maple of the European mountains, a tree with large palmately lobed leaves and two-winged samara fruits — and so the "fig-mulberry" quietly becomes a maple, the tree of the British schoolyard and park.
  • 17th-18th century North America: English colonists meet the towering native plane trees — Platanus occidentalis in the east, Platanus racemosa in the California west — massive riparian trees with broad maple-like leaves, round hanging seed-balls, and the famous peeling bark that flakes away to reveal a mottled, white, green, and cream trunk. Once again the same old name is fitted to a new tree, and the American sycamore is born.
  • 19th-20th century codification: dictionaries record the regional split — British sycamore (a maple), American sycamore (a plane), biblical sycomore (a fig).
The result is a splendid, documented confusion that botanists must always disambiguate. The London plane, a hybrid of the eastern and western planes, is sometimes loosely called a sycamore in American speech. The name has spread far beyond the trees: sycamore wood (plane) serves for butcher's blocks and furniture, sycamore maple tonewood for violin backs, and the word fills placenames from Sycamore Street to Sycamore, Illinois. The phonetic evolution is small (sukomoros to sycomorus to sicamor to sycomore to sycamore), because the sound changes are ordinary Greek-to-Latin-to-French-to-English adjustments; the dramatic changes are semantic. A fig, a maple, a plane — three trees, one name, 2,500 years of wandering — with the biblical fig as the ghost ancestor still hovering in the dictionaries, and Steinbeck's California sycamores as the latest and most familiar claimant of the name.
