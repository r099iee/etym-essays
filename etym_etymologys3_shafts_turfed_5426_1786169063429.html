Part I: The Etymological Evolution of SHAFTS

1. Prehistoric Roots: PIE *(s)kep-

The biography of shaft begins with a cutting tool. Historical linguists trace the Germanic *skaftaz to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning to cut, scrape, or hew — reconstructed variously as *(s)kep-, *(s)kap-, or *skēp-. The root's presence across the branches is visible in Latin scāpus (the shaft or stem of a plant, the trunk of a column) and Greek skēptron (a staff, whence English sceptre) and skaptein (to dig). Everywhere the root appears it governs the same act: shaping a straight piece of wood or stone by cutting and scraping it down to a smooth, uniform rod.

2. The Germanic Rod and the Weapon World: *skaftaz

In Proto-Germanic the root produced *skaftaz, the shaved rod. Its first life was military: the shaft was the spear- or arrow-handle, the straight wooden spine of the hunter's and warrior's chief weapons. Old English sceaft carried precisely this range — the pole, the spear-shaft, the stock of a weapon — and the word stayed close to the carpenter's and fletcher's bench for centuries. In Middle English shaft still meant the spear or arrow itself, and the archer's shaft was the arrow shot from the bow.

3. From Weapon to Building: The Built World

From the later Middle Ages the word extended outward into the built environment. A shaft became the stem of a column (the trunk between base and capital), the channel of a mine (the straight passage sunk into the earth), the pole of a carriage, and the flue of a chimney. All were the same straight, measurable length of stone or wood — the carpenter's rod translated into architecture and engineering. In the 16th century the great transfer to light occurred: a shaft of light made the beam a solid, penetrative thing, a lance of brightness traveling in a straight line. The metaphor has never faded.

4. The Industrial and Modern Age

Industrialization multiplied the senses. The rotating drive shaft, crank shaft, and axle shaft of machinery were the turned rods of the workshop writ large; the elevator shaft and ventilation shaft standardized the vertical passage. Anatomy borrowed the word for the central body of a long bone. In the 20th century the language turned the shaft against people: American slang made to shaft mean to cheat or betray — the arrow's thrust become social injury — and the shaft (unfair treatment) entered the idiom of the workplace and the street. The word thus carries, in a single syllable, the whole history of the straight line: from the cut rod of the prehistoric carpenter to the beam of sunset on a fictional forest floor.

Part II: The Etymological Evolution of TURFED

1. The Obscure Prehistory of *turb-

The story of turf is, by contrast, the story of the earth itself — and it is one of the few English words whose deepest origin remains genuinely obscure. The Proto-Germanic *turbą (turf, sod, peat) is attested in every Germanic language — Old English turf, Old Norse torf, Old Frisian turf, Middle Dutch turf, Old High German zurba (modern German Torf) — but no convincing Indo-European etymology has been established for it. Some scholars have proposed a link to a root meaning to hold or fasten, or a distant connection to Sanskrit darbhá (a tuft of sacred grass), but both suggestions are speculative, and a number of authorities treat the word as possibly borrowed from a pre-Indo-European substrate of the northern peat lands. The Germanic family is secure; the pre-Germanic stage is not, and honest etymology says so.

2. The Sod in Anglo-Saxon Life

In Old English turf named the cut sod and, above all, the peat of the upland moors. For the peasant household of the Anglo-Saxon and medieval north, turf was fuel: turf was cut, dried, stacked, and burned; a turf-fire warmed the hearth. The word belonged to the material of home — the very ground under the house. To turf a surface was to lay sod, to upholster the earth in living grass, and by the 16th century the verb and its participle turfed were established: a turfed bank, a turfed grave, a turfed lawn.

3. The Turf Becomes a World

In the 18th century the word expanded socially. The Turf — with the definite article — became the horse-racing world and the racecourse, the turf on which fortunes were staked; a turf accountant (from the 19th century) is the bookmaker who takes bets on that ground. In the 20th century the territorial sense broke free of the track: a turf war is a gang's fight over territory, and one's turf is the ground one commands. The slang verb to turf out (to eject) completes the circuit — the image of flinging a sod at someone. Every one of these senses stays anchored to the original sod: the cut piece of ground, the living mat of root and grass, the soil underfoot.

4. Turfed in Literature

In the passage that generated this card — the sunset shafts slanting to lay on the turfed forest floor — the two words stage their etymologies against each other. The shafts are manufactured, straight, cutting, vertical; the turfed floor is native, rooted, soft, horizontal. The author (Jack Vance) chose turfed precisely for its ancient, earthy resonance: a floor upholstered in living sod, the horizontal foil to the piercing light — the oldest word in the language for the ground itself set beneath the most modern of light images.
