Assortment means a varied collection or mixture of different kinds brought together — a box of chocolates with many fillings, a shop’s stock of sizes and colours, a pile of parcels and odd furniture as in the source sentence. The essence is planned or accidental variety within one gathering: not one kind but many, side by side.

In the source sentence the word does exactly this work: Inside in the hall there was piled a large assortment of packages and parcels and small articles of furniture — the splendid muddle of presents and party leftovers heaped together after a great celebration, every shape and size in one pile. Large assortment here is deliciously understated: the heap is enormous and gloriously miscellaneous.

Core meaning in detail: An assortment is defined by heterogeneity with bounds: the items differ (flavours, sizes, types) yet belong to one set (one box, one shop line, one pile). Commerce loves the word — a fine assortment of teas, assorted sizes — because it promises choice without chaos. The near-synonym assorted as an adjective (assorted biscuits) is even commoner in daily life than the noun, doing the same work on packets and menus.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A mixed collection of kinds (primary modern sense, from the 17th c.): an assortment of sweets, of tools, of guests. The bridge is the verb assort — to sort things into kinds and then gather the kinds together — so the noun names both the act’s result and the pleasing variety itself.
  • A retail stock or range (commercial): the shop carries a wide assortment — the full spread of what is on offer. The bridge is mercantile: shopkeepers display one of each kind so buyers can choose, and the display itself becomes the assortment.
  • The act of assorting (older, rarer): seventeenth-century writers use assortment for the sorting itself — the matching of like with like. The bridge is the suffix -ment, which names actions as well as results, though modern use keeps almost only the result sense.
  • A grouping by natural affinity (figurative): an odd assortment of allies at a negotiation. The bridge extends commercial variety to people: like sweets in a box, the allies differ yet sit together for one occasion.
  • Assorted as euphemism for miscellaneous (colloquial): assorted junk in the attic. The bridge is gentle irony: the tidy commercial word dignifies what is really a jumble, exactly as in the source sentence’s towering pile.

Register and connotation: assortment is cheerful and commercial — confectionery, catalogues, and well-stocked shelves. It promises abundance and choice, never menace: even an assortment of problems sounds almost jauntily manageable. The word flatters both seller and buyer — the seller’s stock is rich, the buyer’s taste is broad — and it keeps a faint festive air from its life in chocolate boxes and Christmas hampers.
