- 14th c. - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French and Latin, denoting a classical funerary vessel or water vessel from antiquity.
- 16th & 17th c. - Strongly associated in Renaissance literature and funeral treatises (such as Sir Thomas Browne's Urne-Buriall) with cremation, pagan burial customs, and poetic meditations on mortality.
- 18th c. - Expanded dramatically in Georgian domestic life into the 'tea urn', a luxurious metal table boiler for serving hot tea in polite society, while also becoming a staple architectural ornament in neoclassical landscape gardens.
- 19th c. - Elevated by Romantic poets (notably Keats's Ode on a Grecian Urn) into the supreme aesthetic symbol of eternal art transcending mortal decay.
- 20th c. to Present - Maintained in modern funeral practice for cremains, in commercial catering for large beverage dispensers, and in probability mathematics for idealized sampling models.
