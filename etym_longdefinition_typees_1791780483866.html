Typees (plural noun; historically spelled Typee in the singular by Herman Melville, representing the indigenous Marquesan ethnonym Taipi) refers to the members of an indigenous Polynesian tribe native to the lush, expansive valley of Taipivai on the northeastern coast of Nuku Hiva, the largest island in the Marquesas archipelago. In Western literary history, ethnography, and political philosophy, the Typees occupy a celebrated and controversial position as the archetype of both the uncorrupted "Noble Savage" living in blissful communal harmony and the formidable, warlike islanders reputed for ritual cannibalism.

To fully understand the multifaceted significance of the Typees, their history must be examined across ethnographic, literary, and socio-philosophical dimensions:

  • Ethnographic & Geographical Reality (The Taipi of Taipivai): In their native North Marquesan language (*ʻEo ʻEnana*), the tribe is known as the Taipi, taking their name from the vast valley they inhabit—Taipivai ("water of the swollen tide"). The Taipivai valley is one of the largest, most fertile, and agriculturally productive amphitheater valleys in the Marquesas, nourished by a perennial river and surrounded by sheer, jagged basalt cliffs that rise hundreds of meters, physically separating the inhabitants from neighboring valley clans. Traditional Taipi society was organized into complex extended kinship lineages (*mata'eina'a*) ruled by hereditary chiefs (*haka'iki*), inspired shamans (*tau'a*), and master craftsmen (*tuhuka*). They cultivated extensive breadfruit (*mei*) agroforests, taro terraces, and coconut groves, and constructed monumental stone domestic terraces (*paepae*) and sacred ceremonial complexes (*me'ae*).
  • Herman Melville's Encounter and the Birth of a Literary Masterpiece: In July 1842, twenty-two-year-old American seaman Herman Melville, suffering from brutal conditions aboard the New Bedford whaling ship Acushnet, deserted the vessel alongside shipmate Richard Tobias "Toby" Greene while anchored in Taiohae Bay, Nuku Hiva. Seeking to hide in the uninhabited mountainous interior until their ship departed, the two young men accidentally descended into the forbidden, dreaded valley of the Taipi rather than the neighboring, friendly valley of the Hapa'a. Melville remained in the valley for approximately four weeks (mythologized as four months in his narrative), treated as an honored captive by the chief Mehevi and cared for by his host Kory-Kory and the beautiful Fayaway, before dramatically escaping aboard the Australian whaler Lucy Ann. In 1846, Melville published his experiences as Typee: A Peep at Polynesian Life, which became an immediate international bestseller and established his literary reputation.
  • The Duality of Western Perception (Edenic Harmony vs. Cannibal Dread): Melville's portrayal of the Typees captivated the nineteenth-century Western world because it shattered simplistic colonial stereotypes while simultaneously indulging romantic fascination. On one hand, Melville depicted the Typees as living in an astonishing state of social equilibrium, physical health, and emotional tranquility. As famously highlighted by political philosophers, the Typees maintained flawless internal order without written laws, magistrates, lawyers, prisons, or monetary debt; personal theft was virtually unknown, and disputes were settled through spontaneous communal consensus. On the other hand, Melville underscored the constant, chilling undercurrent of dread: the Typees were legendary warriors who engaged in fierce inter-valley battles against the Happars and the Tei'i, practicing ritual cannibalism upon slain enemy warriors to absorb their spiritual power (*mana*).
  • Socio-Political Influence: Peter Kropotkin and Mutual Aid: The extraordinary social cohesion of the Typees captured the attention of leading social theorists. In his landmark 1902 treatise Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution, Russian naturalist and anarcho-communist philosopher Peter Kropotkin directly quoted Melville's account of the Typees to demonstrate that human beings possess an innate capacity for voluntary cooperation and mutual solidarity that precedes and surpasses the coercive mechanisms of the state. Kropotkin pointed to Melville's observation that "no one was ever put upon his trial for any violence to the public" as empirical proof that ethical social life flourishes through reciprocal trust rather than penal coercion.

Polysemy & Contemporary Usage: Today, the term Typees functions primarily in historical, literary, and anthropological contexts:

  • Literary & Academic Usage: It denotes the characters and cultural subjects of Melville's debut novel, serving as a foundational case study in American Romanticism, travel writing, and postcolonial critique.
  • The Living Taipi Community: In contemporary French Polynesia, the descendants of the historic Typees reside in the peaceful commune of Taipivai, celebrated for their master woodcarvers, stone sculptors, and vibrant participation in the biennial Marquesan Arts Festival (*Matavaa o te Fenua Enata*).
