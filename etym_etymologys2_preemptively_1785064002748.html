Two Proto-Indo-European roots, joined only in Latin: Chain I — the "before" element: Proto-Indo-European *per- ("forward, through, beyond"; reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE) → (in Italic) Latin per ("through, by means of"; attested 1st c. BCE) and its by-form prae ("before, in front of"; attested 1st c. BCE) → prefix prae- / pre- ("before"; the first element of praeemere). Chain II — the "taking/buying" element: Proto-Indo-European *h₁em- ("to take, distribute, receive"; reconstructed; cf. Latin emere and Lithuanian im̃ti "to take"; c. 4000–3000 BCE) → Latin emere ("to take, buy"; attested 1st c. BCE) → Latin praeemere ("to take or buy beforehand," formed from prae- + emere; attested classical) → Late / Medieval Latin praeemptio ("a buying beforehand; right of first purchase"; medieval legal noun from the past participle praeemptus) → Early Modern English pre-emption ("the right of purchasing before others"; c. 1600; the US squatters' right to purchase public land from 1841) → English preemptive (adjective, late 19th c.; "preemptive strike" in 20th-c. strategic doctrine) → English preemptively (adverb, 20th c.; the verb preempt is a 19th-century American back-formation from pre-emption). Note on debate and nuancing: the two chains are independent reconstructions and are securely established as PIE roots, but the exact reconstruction of *h₁em- is debated (some scholars reconstruct *h₁em- with a laryngeal of uncertain coloring, and its Tocharian link yäm- "to do, act" is tentative). The Latin forms are all attested. English preemptively is a fully attested modern formation, added to the adjective preemptive by the standard -ly adverbial suffix; the direction of derivation (noun pre-emption → verb preempt → adjective preemptive → adverb preemptively) is clear, with preempt as a back-formation, not an inheritance.
