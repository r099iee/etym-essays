• academic: Similar because it concerns schools, universities and formal learning; differs because its pejorative use means “merely theoretical”, whereas scholastic as an insult means “over-subtle and hairsplitting”, and only scholastic names a medieval doctrinal tradition.
  • scholarly: Similar because it describes devotion to learning; differs because it is a term of praise for rigour and depth, never pejorative and never institutional.
  • pedantic: Similar because it accuses someone of caring more for formal detail than for substance; differs because it is purely hostile, with no historical, educational or theological content.
  • educational: Similar because it means “relating to education”; differs because it is broad, modern and neutral, covering policy, materials and methods, while scholastic is narrower, more formal and slightly archaic.
  • pedagogic: Similar because it concerns teaching; differs because it points specifically at the technique of instruction and often carries a hint of the schoolroom or the lecture hall.
  • bookish: Similar because it suggests a life spent among texts; differs because it describes temperament rather than doctrine or schooling, and is mildly deprecating rather than hostile.
  • erudite: Similar because it denotes great learning; differs because it praises breadth of knowledge and implies no method, no school and no pedantry.
  • Schoolman: Similar because it names the medieval theologian that scholastic describes; differs because it is a concrete noun for a person, and the usual term in histories of philosophy.
  • doctrinal: Similar because scholastic writing is above all concerned with doctrine; differs because it says nothing about schooling, method or the medieval universities.
