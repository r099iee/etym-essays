The word is a Latin compound of sub- "under" and planta "sole of the foot"; each element has its own chain. Chain for sub-: PIE *upo "(from) under, below" (reconstructed, 4th millennium BCE; cf. Sanskrit úpa "near to, under," Greek hypó "under") → Latin sub "under, beneath, below" (attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions, 7th–6th c. BCE); the PIE form is usually reconstructed with an optional s- prefix (*s-upo), a detail debated by specialists. Chain for planta: PIE *plat- / *pleth₂- "flat, broad, to spread" (reconstructed; cf. Greek platýs "broad, flat," Sanskrit pr̥thú- "broad") → Latin planta "sole of the foot" (attested classical Latin; the same form also meant "plant, shoot, cutting," and scholars debate whether the two senses descend from one root or merged early; the verb draws specifically on the foot sense). Chain for the compound: Latin supplantare "to trip up, to overthrow by placing something under the sole or heels, to cause to fall by attacking the footing" (attested in Classical–Late Latin; used in the Vulgate Bible, 4th c. CE, of Jacob supplanting Esau) → Old French supplanter / souplanter "to trip up; to dispossess; to take someone's place by craft" (12th c.) → Middle English supplanten / supplant "to overthrow, displace, take the place of" (13th–14th c., via Anglo-Norman) → Modern English supplant → SUPPLANTED "superseded, replaced, displaced" (past tense and past participle). Debated points: (1) The exact PIE form behind Latin sub is reconstructed as *upo with optional s-mobile; details are contested. (2) Latin planta has two senses ("sole of the foot" and "plant/shoot") whose relationship is uncertain. (3) The compound supplantare is clearly attested from late republican/imperial Latin onward, but earlier attestation is not documented.
