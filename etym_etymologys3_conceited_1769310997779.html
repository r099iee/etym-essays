The biography of conceited is a study in how a physical act — grasping — became, over two thousand years, the name of a moral failing. Everything begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *kap-, "to grasp, take, seize." The root survives in the Germanic branch as the ordinary verb "to have": Proto-Germanic *habjaną lies behind Gothic haban, Old English habban, German haben, and our English have — etymologically, to have is to hold, and to hold is to have seized. The same root gave the Germanic *haft- "seized, held," visible in English haft, the handle that is grasped, and in German Haft, "custody, arrest." In the Italic branch the root produced Latin capere, "to take," and from that single verb Latin poured forth a flood of English words: capture, captive, capable, accept, receive, perceive, recipe (the imperative "take!"), and the whole family of verbs built by prefixing prepositions to capere.

The decisive step for our word was the prefixed form concipere, literally "to take in, gather together, seize together" — used both of conceiving a child and of taking an idea into the mind. Its past participle conceptus gave the direct Latin borrowing concept, but a second, colloquial path ran through Old French, where concevoir produced the noun conceit — "a conception, a notion, a fancy." English took conceit in the fourteenth century, and the word is a textbook doublet: conceit and concept are the same Latin word arriving by two routes, one through French and one straight from the scholars' Latin. In the sixteenth century the noun had a brilliant literary career: a conceit was a striking, ingenious fancy or image, and the "metaphysical conceits" of John Donne and the poets — the startling extended similes that yoke unlike things together — are the surviving monuments of this sense. An author "full of conceits" was full of inventive fancies, and the adjective built on the noun, conceited, first meant exactly that: "full of ideas," ingenious, witty, fanciful.

The moral turn came with the phrase "full of conceit" applied to the self. A man "full of conceit" came to be understood not as full of clever fancies generally but as full of the particular fancy of his own importance — full of the flattering notion of himself. The adjective conceited therefore slid, within the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, from "ingenious" to "having an excessively high opinion of oneself": the man taken with his own favourite idea, the idea of himself. This is the semantic bridge in full: the word went from praising mental invention to censuring self-invention, from the author's abundance of thoughts to the self-regarder's obsession with a single thought — himself.

By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the old "ingenious" sense of the adjective was dead, and conceited had settled into exactly the idiomatic, everyday censure it carries today. Jane Austen, the novelist of social self-regard, uses it with perfect command — in Pride and Prejudice (the card's context) Mrs Bennet snaps that Mr Darcy is "so high and so conceited that there was no enduring him," and the phrase "he walked here, and he walked there, fancying himself so very great" is the word made flesh: the man full of his own favourable notion of himself, striding about pleased with his imagined grandeur. The word in Austen is colloquial, domestic, and morally indignant rather than ferocious — a pointed but mild accusation of vanity, exactly its register today.

Modern English keeps the adjective firmly in this moral sense, while the noun conceit remains ambidextrous: in the criticism of poetry it still names the ingenious figure (the "concetto" of the Italians, the Petrarchan and metaphysical conceit), while in everyday speech it names the self-regard the adjective censures. The word thus preserves, in its two registers, both halves of its own history — the grasping hand of PIE *kap-, the taking-in of an idea in concipere, the Old French notion of conceit, and the modern man "so high and so conceited" who fancies himself "so very great" — one chain, from the physical seizure to the favourite idea of oneself.
