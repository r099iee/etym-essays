Ilk is one of the most instructive words in English for how meaning changes, because its modern sense was born from a misreading — a centuries-long misunderstanding that became, in the end, the word itself. To tell its story is to follow a demonstrative pronoun from Anglo-Saxon England to the lairds of Scotland and then back into English newspapers as a near-insult. The Germanic foundations: "that-like" The word begins as a compound. The Proto-Indo-European demonstrative *h₁ey-, "that one", combined with the noun-root *leyg-, "body, form, shape" — the same root that produced English like, -like, which, such, and lich. A Proto-Germanic *īlīkaz, "having that form, the same", yields Old English ilca, "the same", attested throughout the Old English period: in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, in Ælfric's homilies, and in the Gospels ("se ilca", "the same one"). The word functioned as a weak demonstrative adjective, and in that role it was utterly ordinary: ilca meant simply "same". Modern scholars reconstruct the details of its formation with care — the alternative reconstruction *igalīkaz assumes a different ablaut grade and is defended by some — but the general picture, a demonstrative glued to "like", is secure. Middle English: ilke the same In Middle English the word survives as ilke. It is common in the religious and legal prose of the twelfth to fifteenth centuries: "this ilke day", "the ilke man". The phrases that ilk and this ilk simply mean "that same", "this same". Nothing in this stage predicts a noun meaning "kind". The word is, in fact, dying: by the end of the Middle English period it is a survivor, kept alive chiefly in the north and in Scots, and it might have vanished entirely had it not found a niche in the legal style of the Scottish gentry. Scots: "of that ilk" From the fifteenth century, Scottish landholding families developed a precise formula. A laird whose family surname was identical to the name of his estate styled himself "X of that ilk" — literally "X of that same (estate, bearing the family name)". Thus "Macintosh of that ilk" meant "Macintosh of Macintosh". The usage spread through charters, deeds, peerage books, and finally into the pages of Sir Walter Scott, whose novels carried Scottish life and Scottish speech into the whole English-speaking world in the early nineteenth century. For Scottish readers the phrase was transparent: ilk = same. For English readers it was opaque — but its position was suggestive, exactly where one would expect a word meaning "class" or "kind". The misreading: English reanalysis The reanalysis is documented with unusual clarity. In the eighteenth century, English antiquaries and lexicographers, meeting "of that ilk" in Scottish documents and novels, glossed it as "of that kind, of that sort". Johnson's Dictionary of 1755 records ilk as "Scotch. Same", but already in the course of the century the noun sense was crystallizing. The decisive modern usage appears in the nineteenth century, when ilk — now detached from "of that ilk" and appearing simply as a noun — means "kind, sort, class". Writers of the Victorian novel and press use it with a knowing, half-amused air, and the formula "he and his ilk" begins its career. From "the same" the word had travelled, by way of a foreign idiom misunderstood, to become a label of contemptuous classification. Semantic and social aftermath
  • The old Scottish sense survives untouched: modern peerage works still print "Macintosh of that ilk".
  • The modern sense has thoroughly displaced "same" in ordinary English: no English speaker outside Scotland now says "that ilk" to mean "that same".
  • The word's pejorative colour developed because it arrived in English as a foreign gentry idiom and was applied, from the first, to groups the speaker wished to dismiss — a tone it has never lost.
  • In modern colloquial use the word is almost always attached to people, not things, and to groups the speaker regards as a mere breed or faction.
So a demonstrative meaning "the same" became, through an eighteenth-century misreading of a Scottish formula, a noun meaning "kind" with a permanent sneer attached. Few words in the language show so plainly that meaning is not fixed by etymology, but by use.
