Part of speech and core sense. Extermination is an uncountable noun of action formed from the verb exterminate. Its core modern sense is “the complete destruction or wiping out of a group of people, animals, or things; utter elimination.” It names a process and its result at once: the extermination of a population is both the campaign against it and the state of its having been destroyed.

In the source sentence the word is qualified by virtual: the Revolution is said to have led to the “virtual extermination of the upper tier of French society.” The phrase is deliberately hyperbolic. The nobility was abolished, its privileges revoked, many of its members imprisoned, executed or driven into exile, but there was no literal programme of mass murder against the entire class. Virtual extermination means “effective elimination, annihilation for practical purposes”—a rhetorical escalation that borrows the word’s full moral weight while the hedge virtual concedes that it is not to be taken literally.

Primary modern senses

  • The killing of a population. The dominant sense today, and the most serious: the deliberate destruction of a people, tribe, species or community. It implies totality, intention and usually a campaign sustained over time rather than a single massacre.
  • The eradication of animals regarded as pests. A mundane, commercial sense: the extermination of rats, termites, cockroaches or mice. This is the sense behind exterminator, the technician who performs the work, and it keeps the word in everyday use, somewhat insulated from its darker history.
  • The destruction of a thing, quality or institution. Used of weeds, disease, heresy, a rival firm, or a practice: “the extermination of smallpox.” Here the word is emphatic but not necessarily violent; it stresses completeness rather than cruelty.
  • Hyperbolic use. In journalism and speech the word is often inflated: “the extermination of the high street,” “the extermination of the middle class.” The exaggeration is usually signalled by context, as in the source's virtual.

Legal sense. Extermination is a distinct crime against humanity in international criminal law. Article 7(1)(b) of the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court lists it alongside murder, and Article 7(2)(b) defines it as “the intentional infliction of conditions of life, inter alia the deprivation of access to food and medicine, calculated to bring about the destruction of part of a population.” Two features matter. First, extermination does not require a protected national, ethnic, racial or religious group, so it is broader than genocide. Second, it can be committed by creating lethal conditions rather than by direct killing—a definition shaped by the famines and blockades of the twentieth century as much as by the death camps.

Historical sense. The Latin verb exterminare meant “to drive out beyond the boundary, expel, banish,” not “to kill.” English extermination entered the language in the mid-fifteenth century in a theological register, naming the driving out of sin or heresy from the soul. The sense “utter destruction” appears in the sixteenth century and gradually crowded out the older meaning of expulsion. The modern word therefore preserves, fossilised inside it, the image of a boundary (terminus) and of pushing something across it, out of existence.

Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge. Expulsion and destruction are close neighbours: a people driven beyond the frontier is, in practice, often a people destroyed, and a rite that expels evil may be imagined as annihilating it. Latin exterminare already wavered between “banish” and “destroy utterly” in late antiquity, particularly in Christian usage where the enemy to be driven out was the devil or sin. Once the object of the verb became a population rather than a vice, the stronger sense became normal. The step from “put out of the community” to “put out of existence” is the whole semantic history of the word in miniature.

Register, connotation and collocation. The word is formal and morally loaded. Applied to human beings it is among the strongest terms available, implying deliberateness, system and totality; it is avoided in neutral description and reserved for atrocity. Standard collocations include mass extermination, systematic extermination, wholesale extermination, extermination camp, extermination policy, near-extermination and virtual extermination. The agent noun exterminator has split off into pest control and popular culture, while exterminate acquired a cartoonish fame through the Daleks' catchphrase in Doctor Who from 1963—a strange afterlife for a word of Roman law.

Boundaries. Extermination is more absolute than elimination and more intentional than extinction: species go extinct through habitat loss without anyone intending it, but extermination implies an agent. It is narrower than genocide in legal scope, since it does not require a protected group, yet broader in that it can apply to pests and abstractions.
