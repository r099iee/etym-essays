Cord begins inside the body. PIE *ghere- meant gut, entrail, intestine — preserved in Sanskrit hira vein, Lithuanian žarna guts, Old English gearn yarn originally made of dried gut, and Greek khordē which meant both tripe to eat and gut-string to play. Greeks twisted sheep gut into lyre strings, bowstrings, and sutures, so khordē slid from anatomy to technology while keeping its material: dried intestine.

Latin borrowed this as chorda catgut, string of a musical instrument, keeping Greek kh- as ch-. Vitruvius uses chorda for catapult rope, Cicero for lyre string. Old French inherited it as corde rope, string, twist, widening from gut to any twisted fibre — hemp, flax, silk — by the 12th century. Middle English borrowed corde around 1300 for bowstring, hangman's rope, and instrument string, as seen in Chaucer. The spelling cord is a 16th-century refashioning that dropped h, while the musical-geometry sense was re-latinised as chord in the 1600s, creating the modern cord/chord split from one source plus one intruder — accord from Latin cor heart.

  • Phonetic shifts: PIE gh- → Greek kh- → Latin ch- /k/ → French c- /k/ before o; Greek -ē → Latin -a; French corde → English corde → cord with loss of final e and shortening of vowel to /kɔːd/.
  • Semantic drift: entrails → dried gut string → any twisted string → tendon by resemblance → bond by metaphor → measured woodpile by measuring rope → fabric rib by resemblance → electrical flex by function.
  • Attestations: c.1300 bowstring and hangman's rope; c.1400 tendon or muscle; late 14th century anything which binds; 1610s cord of wood 128 cu ft; 1776 corded cloth rib; 1872 vocal cords.
  • Cognates and kin: cordon diminutive ribbon, corduroy probably cord + duroy cloth, chordate animals with spinal cord, yarn from same gut root, hernia and haruspex from entrail inspection.
  • Cultural context: gut strings made music, surgery, and warfare possible; hemp cords rigged sailing ships; umbilical and spinal cords gave the word sacred bodily centrality; electrification made cord the lifeline of appliances.

A nuance often confused is chord versus cord. Chord meaning simultaneous musical notes is not from gut at all but shortened from accord, Old French acorder to bring heart to heart, from Latin ad + cor heart, respelled with h after 1600 by confusion with chorda. Thus English has two chords: gut-chord from khordē and heart-chord from cor, now spelled alike but etymologically distinct, while rope-cord kept the h-less spelling. OED and Etymonline agree on this double origin and warn against merging them.

From medieval bell-ropes to modern climbing cords and fibre-optic bundles, the same twisted-length principle governs: many weak fibres plied together make one strong, flexible line, whether of gut, hemp, nylon, or glass, for music, ships, machines, and bodies.

Chaucer, Shakespeare, and the Authorised Version use cord for bondage and affection alike, fixing the moral metaphor that still lets a story strike a cord in the heart.
