The name Armenian is older than the English language and older than the Armenian alphabet. Its earliest unambiguous attestation is in the trilingual Behistun inscription of Darius I, carved around 520 BCE, where the Old Persian version uses Armina for the country and Arminiya for its people. The Elamite version has Harminuya, and the Babylonian version uses Urashtu, the older name Urartu. Greek Armenioi is attested from about the same period, perhaps as early as Hecataeus of Miletus (c. 476 BCE). From Greek the name passed into Latin as Armenius, into Old French as armenien, and into Middle English as Armenian in the fourteenth century.

The origin debate. The deeper origin of Armina is uncertain, and no consensus exists. One old suggestion connects it with the Assyrian toponym Armânum or Armanî, attested in Bronze Age Mesopotamian records and associated with a region near the upper Euphrates or with Aleppo. Another derives it from Urartian Arme or Urme, a district in the Armenian highlands, with the Urartian suffix -ni meaning "inhabitant of": Arme-ni, "inhabitant of Arme". A third reads the Elamite har-mi-nu-ya as "mountainous region of the Minni", connecting it with the biblical Minni and the Mannaeans. Still others invoke the Armens, a hypothetical Bronze Age tribe, or the name Erimena in Urartian inscriptions. Because the sources are fragmentary and the phonology is ambiguous, the question remains open. What is certain is that Armina is the immediate source of the Greek and Latin names, and that it was already an exonym when the Armenians themselves used hay.

The endonym Hay. Armenians call themselves hay and their country Hayastan. The origin of hay is also disputed. One influential theory derives it from the Hittite or Urartian name Ḫatti, the land of the Hittites, through a Proto-Armenian form *hatiyos or *hatyos, with the regular Armenian loss of intervocalic -t- to y. Another connects it with the kingdom of Hayasa, attested in Hittite texts in the second millennium BCE. A third derives it from Proto-Indo-European *poti- "lord, master, husband", comparing Sanskrit pati- and Lithuanian patis. None is proven. The legendary ancestor Hayk, son of Togarmah and descendant of Noah, is a later etiological story, not a historical record. The coexistence of hay and Armenian is a classic case of an endonym and an exonym diverging across millennia.

Language history and sound shifts. Armenian was long treated as an Iranian dialect because of its many Iranian loanwords, until Heinrich Hübschmann demonstrated in 1877 that it is an independent branch of Indo-European. Its phonology is marked by a consonant shift comparable to Grimm's law: Proto-Indo-European voiced aspirates became voiceless, voiceless stops became voiced in certain positions, and the original system was thoroughly reshaped. Other characteristic changes include the loss of final syllables, the development of a complex vowel system, and the change of initial p to h and t to y in some environments. The language has seven cases, no grammatical gender, and a split ergative pattern in the past tense. Iranian, Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Turkish, Russian, and French have all left loanword layers, a record of Armenia's position at the crossroads of empires.

Alphabet and literary attestations. The Armenian alphabet was created by Mesrop Mashtots around 405 CE, reportedly after study in Edessa and with the help of the catholicos Sahak Partew. The invention triggered a golden age of translation and original writing. The fifth-century historians Movses Khorenatsi, Yeghishe, and Lazarus of Parpi; the theological and mystical poetry of Gregory of Narek (tenth century); the medieval romances and chronicles; and the eighteenth-century ashugh poetry of Sayat-Nova form a continuous tradition. In the nineteenth century, Armenian Romanticism and realism produced Khachatur Abovyan, Hovhannes Tumanyan, and Yeghishe Charents. The 1915 genocide, in which an estimated 1.5 million Armenians were killed or deported by the Ottoman state, scattered survivors across the world and made the Western Armenian dialect a diaspora language. Soviet Armenia adopted a reformed orthography in 1922-1924, while the diaspora largely retained the traditional spelling.

Modern socio-cultural context. The Armenian Apostolic Church, one of the oldest national churches, was founded traditionally by Gregory the Illuminator and became the state church in 301 CE, making Armenia the first country to adopt Christianity officially. The church, the alphabet, and the genocide memory are the three pillars of modern Armenian identity. The Republic of Armenia became independent in 1991; the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict and the 2020 and 2023 wars have reshaped its politics. A large diaspora in Russia, the United States, France, Lebanon, and Argentina sustains schools, newspapers, and churches. In English, Armenian is now a neutral ethnonym, but it is unavoidably charged in phrases such as Armenian Genocide, Armenian question, and Armenian diaspora.

  • Language - independent Indo-European branch; alphabet 405 CE; Eastern and Western standards.
  • Modern charge - genocide memory, diaspora, and the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict.
