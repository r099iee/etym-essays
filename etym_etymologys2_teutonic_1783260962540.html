Proto-Indo-European *tewtéh₂ / *tewtā- — "people, tribe, nation, population" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Old Irish túath "people, territory", Oscan touto and Umbrian totam "people, community", Lithuanian tautà "nation, people", Germanic *þeudō); c. 4500–2500 BCE ↓ Proto-Germanic *þeudō — "people, nation" (reconstructed; attested in reflexes: Gothic þiuda "people", Old Norse þjóð, Old English þēod "people, nation", Old High German diot(a)); the northern IE cognate of Celtic túath and Baltic tautà ↓ Germanic tribal name *þeudanōz — "the Teutons" (reconstructed plural ethnonym built on *þeudō, i.e., "the people (of the tribe)"; the name reaches the historical record through Latin as Teutones / Teutonī, the tribe that invaded Roman territory and was defeated at Aquae Sextiae, 102 BCE) ↓ Latin Teutones / Teutonī — "the Teutons" (tribal name, attested in Roman sources from the late 2nd c. BCE in the histories of the Cimbrian War; Roman authors also use Teutonus for an individual) ↓ Latin Teutonicus — "of the Teutons, German" (adjective in -icus; attested from the later Latin period, e.g., Claudian, c. 400 CE, and standard in Medieval Latin: rex Teutonicus, Ordo Teutonicus "Teutonic Order," from the late 12th c.) ↓ English Teutonic — "of the Germanic peoples, languages or culture" (adjective; first attested in the early 17th century, c. 1611, as a learned classical borrowing; generalized to the whole Germanic family in 18th–19th-century philology; used as a noun for the Teutonic language, e.g., Macaulay, 1848) → Teutonic DEBATED / NUANCED: The exact shape of the tribal name is debated — the Latin Teutones may reflect a Germanic *þeudanōz (the "lords" or "chiefs," from *þeudanaz "ruler," cf. Gothic þiudans "king") or simply *þeudōz ("the people"); the evidence does not decide firmly between the two. The PIE root *tewtā- itself is well established, but its exact vocalism (*tewtéh₂) is reconstructed. "Teutonic" is a distant cousin (doublet) of modern German deutsch "German" and English Dutch, which descend from the derived *þeudiskaz "of the people"; English borrowed the Latinized form rather than the native Germanic one.
