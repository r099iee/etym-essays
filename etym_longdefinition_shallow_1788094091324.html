Shallow (adjective; comparatives shallower, shallowest) means “of little depth; not deep,” and from that simple physical core it has developed a powerful figurative career describing everything that lacks profundity. In the source sentence, “On the shallow shore most of the barrels…,” it describes a stretch of shore where the water is so thin that barrels can be grounded and handled — a place where the riverbed shelves up gently and the water barely covers the shingle.

1. Primary physical sense — of little depth. Water is shallow when it can be waded or the bottom is visible; containers (a shallow dish, tray, pan, grave, cup, ditch) hold little; soil and root systems are shallow when they penetrate little; breathing is shallow when it moves little air; sleep and coma are shallow when they are light. The opposite is deep. In navigation, a shallow is a shoal — a place where the water is so thin that shipping must avoid it, hence the related noun shoal (“sandbank”). A “shallow grave” barely covers what it holds.

2. Figurative sense — superficial, lacking depth. Since the sixteenth century, shallow has described people, minds, emotions, arguments, and works of art that lack profundity: a shallow person is one whose thoughts, sympathies, and learning run no deeper than the surface; a shallow argument collapses under the least pressure; a shallow novel trades in surfaces. This is the dominant modern use in educated speech, and it carries a clear moral charge — shallowness is a mild vice, a failure of seriousness.

3. Of penetration and reception — skin-deep. Shallow love, shallow knowledge, shallow reading: the word measures how far a thing penetrates beneath its own surface. A shallow impression is easily erased; shallow roots, literal or metaphorical, do not hold. The physical knowingness of shallow water — “you can see the bottom” — became the very figure of transparency and emptiness: what is shallow is easily seen through, and what is easily seen through is quickly exhausted.

4. Technical and professional senses.

  • Oceanography and geology: “shallow seas” are continental-shelf waters; “shallow-water” deposits and facies; a “shallow-focus earthquake” has its hypocentre within roughly 70 km of the surface, as opposed to deep-focus quakes.
  • Hydrodynamics: the “shallow-water equations” model flows whose horizontal scale dwarfs depth — tides, tsunamis, rivers.
  • Linguistics: a “shallow orthography” (Italian, Finnish, Spanish) spells sound transparently, unlike English’s “deep orthography.”
  • Computing: a “shallow copy” duplicates only the top-level structure of an object, sharing the data it points to, unlike a “deep copy”; “shallow parsing” extracts only high-level phrase structure from text.
  • Finance: a “shallow market” is one with few trades, so prices move wildly on small orders.
  • Machine learning: “shallow learning” (a model with few layers) is contrasted with “deep learning” — the two terms are textbook antonyms in artificial intelligence.
  • Engineering: “shallow foundations” (footings) versus deep foundations in construction.

5. Verb, noun, adverb. The verb to shallow means “to become shallow” (the river shallows downstream) or “to make shallow” (silting shallows a harbour). The noun a shallow is a shoal or shallow place; “the shallows” is the shallow part of a river or sea. The adverb (to lie shallow) is rare.

6. Idioms and fixed phrases. “The shallow end” of a pool is the safe end — and metaphorically the easy, risk-free version of anything (“she stayed in the shallow end of the debate”). A “shallow grave” is standard news English for a hasty burial. “Shallow and hollow” pair naturally, because both words describe emptiness behind a surface.

7. The semantic bridge. Everything follows from littleness of depth: what is shallow holds little, sees to the bottom, supports nothing heavy, and is easily seen through. That one physical bundle — thin, transparent, unsupportive — became the moral bundle of superficiality: thin in substance, transparent in motive, unable to bear the weight of serious thought. Steep is its natural antonym on slopes; deep is its antonym everywhere else.
