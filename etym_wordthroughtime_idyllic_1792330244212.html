- Classical Antiquity (3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Exclusively a formal literary term in Hellenistic Alexandria, where eidúllion (diminutive of Greek eîdos, 'little form/vignette') designated short pastoral poems by Theocritus depicting idyllic Sicilian shepherds, rustic singing contests, and mythical nymphs.
- Renaissance to 18th Century — Adopted into European poetic discourse as idyll (French idylle, English idyll), functioning as a recognized genre of pastoral literature alongside Virgil’s Eclogues, representing an elite artistic escape from courtly corruption.
- Late 18th to Early 19th Century (Romantic Era) — The adjective idyllic emerged in English (c. 1790s–1810s) as Romantic poets and aesthetic theorists shifted emphasis from rigid poetic meter to the emotional contemplation of pristine nature, rustic solitude, and uncorrupted rural landscapes.
- Late 19th to 20th Century (Industrial Era) — Underwent intense semantic romanticization as urbanized and industrialized societies viewed pre-industrial villages, pastoral countrysides, and innocent childhoods through a nostalgic, escapist lens.
- Contemporary Era (Late 20th–21st Century) — Broadened to describe any untroubled, harmonious, or utopian circumstance; simultaneously developed a pervasive cinematic and literary irony, where an 'idyllic setting' serves as a brittle façade masking sinister corruption underneath.
