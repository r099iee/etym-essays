Gusto descends from one of humanity's oldest sensory metaphors: that to know is to have tasted. Proto-Indo-European *geus- meant both to taste and to choose, to try on the tongue and to decide in the mind. The double sense survives in Sanskrit jus- to enjoy, Greek geuesthai to taste, Gothic kiusan to test and choose (source of English choose), and Latin gustus meaning a tasting, a mouthful, and by extension flavour and discernment. Latin authors used gustus concretely for the appetiser course of a meal (the gustatio) and abstractly for aesthetic judgment, as in Quintilian's discussion of literary taste. The verb gustare meant to taste, sip, and sample, giving French goûter and English gustatory.

Medieval Italian kept gusto as both flavour on the tongue and delight in the mind, a duality Dante and Boccaccio exploit: il gusto del pane and il gusto della musica. Renaissance humanism elevated gusto into a critical term for connoisseurship. Vasari praised painters of good gusto; Castiglione's courtier must display gusto in dress, speech, and sport. Spanish Golden Age writers parallelled this with gusto meaning pleasure, will, and caprice — a gusto still means at ease, to one's liking, while dar gusto is to please. English travellers on the Grand Tour in the early seventeenth century brought the word home. First recorded in 1629 in the sense of sense of taste, it quickly narrowed in English (unlike in Italian and Spanish) to energetic, hearty enjoyment in action.

  • PIE *geus- doublet: gusto and choose: English holds both reflexes of the same root without recognising them: choose via Germanic *keus- and gusto via Latin gustus. Both unite tasting and deciding.
  • Gustus to gusto: u to o: Regular Italo-Romance vowel development raised and rounded the Latin -us ending to Italian -o, with no change in stem consonants. Spanish borrowed the Italian form and reinforced it through its own gustar to please.
  • 1629 taste to 1680s relish: Early English citations use gusto for literal taste. By the Restoration, writers like Dryden use with gusto for doing something with spirited relish, shifting from noun of sensation to adverbial of manner.
  • Gusto vs. taste: Eighteenth-century aesthetics distinguished taste as discriminating judgment from gusto as vigorous execution. Reynolds praised Michelangelo's gusto, meaning power and zest, not refinement.

John Milton, Laurence Sterne, and later John Keats and William Hazlitt fixed gusto as hearty, unfeigned delight. Hazlitt's 1817 essay On Gusto defined it as power or passion defining an object, citing Rembrandt's gusto in painting rugged faces. Nineteenth-century music criticism spoke of singing with gusto, and cookery writing of eating with gusto, both implying bodily, unembarrassed pleasure. The phrase with gusto became idiomatic, almost always adverbial: to sing, laugh, applaud, tuck in with gusto. The derived adjective gusty in the sense of vigorous exists but is overshadowed by the meteorological gusty from gust of wind, an unrelated Scandinavian word, causing occasional confusion.

Today gusto in English almost never means literal flavour (that sense survives only in gustatory and in Italian restaurants' gusto branding). It means keen, robust enjoyment expressed outwardly. To take to blood with gusto, as in Durant's sentence, is to embrace it eagerly, noisily, without squeamishness. The irony is that vegetarians, presumed to abstain, do so with the same hearty relish as carnivores. Phonologically Received Pronunciation has /ˈɡʌstəʊ/ with a short STRUT vowel and closing diphthong, distinct from Italian /ˈɡusto/ with pure vowels. The spelling retains Italian -o, marking it as a borrowing that never fully anglicised, like tempo, bravo, and virtuoso, all Grand Tour souvenirs of continental taste.
