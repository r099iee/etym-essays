The etymological evolution of Amoy documents centuries of Chinese maritime history, dialectal divergence, European colonial trade routes, and international cartographic standardization spanning the Ming Dynasty to the modern era.

1. Ming Dynasty Fortification and Chinese Characters (c. 1387 CE):

  • The city was established and fortified in the twentieth year of the Hongwu Emperor of the Ming Dynasty (1387) by the military commander Zhou Dexing.
  • The settlement was named Xiàmén in Chinese characters: 廈 (xià in Mandarin, ē / hē in Min, meaning "mansion, grand building, tall structure") and 門 (mén in Mandarin, mn̂g in Min, meaning "gate, portal, entrance").
  • The poetic name signified "The Gate of the Grand Mansion," conceptualizing the fortified island harbor as a protective gateway defending the imperial southern province of Fujian against Japanese wokou pirates.

2. Southern Min Phonology and Early European Contact (16th – 17th Century):

  • While northern court officials pronounced the characters as Xiàmén in Early Mandarin, the local population of southern Fujian spoke Southern Min (Hokkien), where 廈 is pronounced Ē (or Hē) and 門 is pronounced mn̂g, yielding the local spoken toponym Ē-mn̂g.
  • When Portuguese navigators charted the Fujian coast in the 1540s, they transcribed the Hokkien pronunciation into European script as Emouy or Amoey.
  • In the 1650s, the Ming loyalist general Zheng Chenggong (known in the West as Koxinga) established his primary naval headquarters on the island, renaming it Siming (思明, "Remembering the Ming") and launching his expedition to liberate Taiwan from the Dutch East India Company.

3. British East India Company and the Treaty Port Era (1670 – 1900):

  • In 1670, the British East India Company established direct trade relations with Amoy, securing the regular export of Fujian oolong teas, silks, and porcelain. British merchants recorded the name as Amoy in official factory ledgers and maritime logs.
  • Following the Treaty of Nanking (1842), Amoy was officially gazetted in British statutory and diplomatic records as one of the Five Treaty Ports. Victorian newspapers, travel memoirs, and geography textbooks made *Amoy* a household name across the British Empire.
  • In Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's 1892 Sherlock Holmes story "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle," Holmes references the "banks of the Amoy River in Southern China," reflecting the Victorian geographical convention of naming regional rivers after their primary treaty port harbor.

4. Modern Toponymic Standardization (20th – 21st Century):

  • In 1958, the People's Republic of China introduced the Hanyu Pinyin romanization system, officially transliterating 廈門 based on Standard Mandarin as Xiamen.
  • In 1979, the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the United Nations officially adopted Pinyin for Chinese geographical names, standardizing *Xiamen* worldwide.
  • Nevertheless, the historical form Amoy endures in international historical scholarship, Southern Min linguistics, genealogical records of the global Hokkien diaspora, and established corporate brand names (such as Amoy Food).
