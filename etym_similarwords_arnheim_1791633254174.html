• Gombrich (Ernst Gombrich): Similar because both are towering 20th-century German-speaking émigré scholars who revolutionized the psychology of art; differs because Gombrich (author of Art and Illusion) championed a Popperian model of schema-and-correction rooted in cultural convention and learning, frequently debating Arnheim's reliance on universal, biologically grounded Gestalt perceptual laws.
  • Panofsky (Erwin Panofsky): Similar because both are monumental German-American art historians of the mid-20th century; differs because Panofsky founded iconological analysis, decoding hidden theological, literary, and symbolic historical meanings in painting, whereas Arnheim focused strictly on direct formal perceptual forces, psychological balance, and dynamic visual composition.
  • Wertheimer (Max Wertheimer): Similar because both are foundational figures in Gestalt psychology; differs because Wertheimer was the pioneering founder of the movement (famous for the phi phenomenon and productive thinking) who served as Arnheim's mentor in Berlin, whereas Arnheim extended Wertheimer's laboratory findings into the aesthetics of fine art, architecture, and film.
  • Köhler (Wolfgang Köhler): Similar because both were central Gestalt theorists at the University of Berlin; differs because Köhler conducted foundational animal behavior research on chimpanzee insight and physical Gestalten, acting as Arnheim's doctoral advisor, whereas Arnheim devoted his life to human aesthetic perception and visual creativity.
  • Bazin (André Bazin): Similar because both are considered founding fathers of classic film theory; differs fundamentally because Bazin championed cinema's realist ontology through deep focus and long takes, whereas Arnheim was an unapologetic formalist who celebrated silent film precisely because its technical limitations (black-and-white, two-dimensionality) forced artists to invent creative visual equivalents for reality.
  • Wölfflin (Heinrich Wölfflin): Similar because both analyzed art through formal structural categories rather than biography; differs because Wölfflin constructed comparative historical pairs to chart the cyclical transition from Renaissance to Baroque styles, whereas Arnheim formulated universal, ahistorical psychological laws governing human visual cognition.
