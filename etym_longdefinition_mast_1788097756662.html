A mast is the tall upright spar of a ship that rises from the keel, through the decks, to carry the sails, yards, and rigging. The note’s context — the Swallow that “sat on the mast of a” great vessel — uses the word in its plainest nautical sense. In traditional wooden ships the mast was a single great tree-trunk, or a composite “made mast” built of several pieces, stepped into the keel, stayed by shrouds and forestays, and carrying the horizontal yards to which square sails were bent.

The word is of Germanic age: Old English mæst, cognate with Old Norse mastr, Dutch mast, and German Mast, all descended from a Proto-Germanic *mastaz; beyond Germanic it is cognate with Latin mālus “mast”, both going back to an Indo-European *mazdos, “a pole, staff”. The nautical sense is the original one; the shore-based “mast” of a flagpole or antenna is a later extension of the same word.

  • Ship’s mast (core sense): one of the vertical spars of a vessel — the foremast, mainmast, and mizzenmast of a full-rigged ship; in a single-masted boat, simply “the mast”. Each mast carries its own sails and rigging, and its height governs the sail area and therefore the ship’s speed and weatherliness.
  • Parts and accessories: the masthead (the top of the mast, and the platform or lookout there); the “top”, the platform where shrouds attach; half-mast, the position to which a flag is lowered as a sign of mourning; “before the mast”, the idiom for the life of the common sailor, whose quarters were forward of the foremast.
  • Extended sense — any tall pole for signals: a radio mast, television mast, or telephone mast is a tall lattice or tubular structure carrying aerials; the word left the sea for the land in the early twentieth century with wireless telegraphy and broadcasting.
  • Flag and ceremony sense: a flagstaff on land is often called a mast; flags fly “at the masthead” and are lowered to “half-mast” on mourning days — the nautical term fixed in the language of protocol.
  • Verb use: to “mast” a vessel is to fit it with masts, or to set (step) a mast into position; “masted” describes a ship’s rig (three-masted, etc.).
  • A homonym that is a different word: mast also means the fallen fruits of forest trees (beech mast, oak mast) eaten by swine and game — a separate noun from a different Germanic root connected with “food, fattening”, with a quite separate history; the two masts are unrelated homonyms.

Semantic bridge. The mast is the vertical spine of the sailing ship, the fixed member around which the whole science of rigging is organised, and its function of carrying sail made it the natural symbol of navigation itself. Every extended sense — radio mast, half-mast, masthead, before the mast — preserves that original picture of a pole rising from a ship to catch the wind. In modern eyes the mast has migrated from ships to pylons and antenna towers, carrying the ancient word into the age of wireless telegraphy.
