The root word PATTER has two independent etymologies, one imitative and one learned; each chain is given in isolation.
CHAIN 1 (sound sense — imitative, no recoverable PIE root):
1. Echoic base — Middle English patten “to strike lightly, tap” (14th c.), imitative of a light impact; no PIE ancestor is reconstructable.
2. pat + frequentative suffix -er → Early Modern English patter v. “to strike lightly and repeatedly, to fall with a succession of light taps” (early 17th c.).
3. Noun derived from the verb — patter n. “a rapid succession of light tapping sounds” (17th c.) → PATTER.
CHAIN 2 (speech sense — a homograph from Latin):
1. Latin pater noster “our father,” the opening words of the Lord’s Prayer (Vulgate, Matthew 6:9; the phrase itself from Latin pater “father” < PIE *ph₂tḗr “father,” and noster “our”).
2. Middle English pater / paternoster “the Lord’s Prayer” (from Old English via Latin); by the 14th c. the verb to patter means “to recite the paternoster, to mumble prayers.”
3. Semantic extension — patter v. “to talk rapidly, mutter, jabber” (16th c.); noun patter “rapid glib talk, thieves’ cant” (17th c.) → PATTER.
Note: the two chains converge on identical modern spellings and pronunciations but are unrelated; context distinguishes them. Chain 1 is purely onomatopoeic; Chain 2 descends from the Latin prayer formula.
