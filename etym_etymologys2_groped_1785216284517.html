PIE *gʰrebʰ- / *gʰreyb- "to seize, grasp, reach out" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of grasping and reaching, with a glide-variant *gʰreyb-; cognate in Sanskrit grah- / grabh- (gr̥bhṇā́ti, "he seizes, grasps"), Avestan grab- "to seize."
│
└──▶ Proto-Germanic *grīpaną "to grasp, seize" (strong verb) — from the extended form *gʰreyb-; attested descendants: Old English grīpan (→ English grip, gripe), Old Norse grípa, Gothic greipan, Old High German grīfan (→ German greifen), Dutch grijpen.
────▶ Proto-Germanic *graipōn- "to feel about with the hands" — a derived weak verb built from the same root with the -ōn- verbal suffix and a different (ā-grade) vowel, giving a repeated, habitual flavour: groping again and again for something unseen.
────────▶ Old English grāpian "to feel about, to grope" (attested in early English prose). Cognates: Old Saxon grēpon, Middle Dutch grāpen.
────────────▶ Middle English gropen "to feel about, to grope" (past tense grope → groped).
────────────────▶ Modern English grope, past tense GROPED "to feel about blindly or uncertainly with the hands" (Tolkien, 1937); extended senses: to grope after knowledge (metaphoric, early modern), and, from the late 20th century, to touch sexually without consent.

Note on debate: the reconstruction of the PIE root is debated in its shape and range: the simple form *gʰrebʰ- and the extended form *gʰreyb- are both cited (Pokorny, LIV), and some scholars also connect the family to *gʰrebh- "to scratch, scrape." The Germanic and English stages, however, are fully attested and uncontroversial.
