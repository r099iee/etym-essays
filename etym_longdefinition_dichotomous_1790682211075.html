Dichotomous means divided into two parts, especially two parts that are sharply distinct, opposed, or mutually exclusive. At its most literal it describes physical forking: a stem, vein, or branching system that divides repeatedly into pairs. At its most abstract it describes conceptual division: a choice, classification, or worldview split into two poles with nothing admitted between. The governing image in all senses is a clean cut producing exactly two halves.

In logic and philosophy the term is a term of art. A dichotomous division is exhaustive and exclusive: every member falls on one side or the other, as in true/false, living/non-living, or A/not-A. Logicians value such divisions for their rigour and warn against false dichotomies, divisions that pretend to exhaust the possibilities while suppressing alternatives — the famous ‘either with us or against us’. In this register dichotomous carries methodological prestige when the binary is genuine and censure when it is forced.

In the life sciences the word keeps its older, neutral descriptive force. Botanists call venation or branching dichotomous when it forks in pairs; zoologists and physicians use it for vessels, antennae, or keys that split pairwise. No opposition is implied here, only geometry. From this technical base the adjective has radiated into the social sciences and everyday criticism, where a dichotomous way of thinking means habitually seeing pairs of opposites — mind/body, nature/culture, poetry/prose — and where, in contemporary usage, the implication is frequently that the opposition is too crude for reality.

  • Forked in pairs (botany, biology): branching evenly into two, e.g. dichotomous veins of a fern; purely structural, without evaluative force.
  • Exhaustively binary (logic): dividing a genus into two contradictories with no remainder, e.g. a dichotomous classification; the strict, prestigious sense.
  • Sharply polarised (general): marked by stark opposition, e.g. ‘a dichotomous political culture’; looser, often critical, implying oversimplification.
  • Mutually exclusive (choices): presenting two alternatives that cannot both hold, e.g. ‘a dichotomous decision between exile and submission’.
  • Methodologically binary (theory): in structuralism, linguistics, and computing, organised around paired oppositions (signifier/signified, 0/1); can be praise for rigour or blame for reductionism.
  • Psychological splitting: in popular psychology, black-and-white thinking is sometimes labelled dichotomous thinking, a cognitive style that admits no gradation.

The bridge between the botanical and the logical senses is the ideal of the clean bisection: nature’s forking stems supply the visible model for reason’s exhaustive divisions. The bridge from logic to everyday criticism is the twentieth century’s disenchantment with binaries themselves. To deny, as the source sentence does, that poetry and prose differ in a fundamental, dichotomous way is therefore to use the word in its most current intellectual posture: acknowledging difference while refusing the clean cut.
