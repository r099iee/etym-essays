- - 1590s–1600s: enters English (from French) as a literal verb for metal losing its shine — a neutral, technical term used of coins, plate, and armour.
- - 1600s–1700s: extends from metals to any surface (glass, paint, gilding); the figurative sense for reputation appears and begins to gain ground.
- - 1700s–1800s: the metaphor solidifies; "tarnished reputation", "tarnished name", "tarnished honour" become fixed literary and journalistic idioms.
- - 1800s–1900s: a staple of melodrama and Victorian moralising — a "fallen" woman or ruined gentleman is "tarnished"; the emotional register is tragic and pitying.
- - 1900s–2000s: journalism and politics make "tarnished" the default word for any damaged public image; the literal sense recedes to everyday household language (jewellery, silverware).
- - Today: a dual-life word — literal in product care, figurative in media and brand management ("tarnished brand"); slightly more formal than "stained" but less dramatic than "ruined".
