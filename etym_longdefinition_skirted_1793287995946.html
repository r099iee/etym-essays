Skirted is the past tense and past participle of the verb skirt: bordered, flanked, or edged along the margin, or moved around the outside of something rather than through its middle. A vale skirted by woodlands wears trees along its rim like a hem; a walker who skirted the town passed around its outskirts and never entered its streets.

In the source sentence the word carries its oldest verbal sense, the picturesque sense of landscape description: the vale is skirted partly by woodlands and partly by tilled hillsides, meaning woods and fields run along its borders and enclose it. This is the verb as a painter uses it, drawing the frame rather than the picture, and Cobbett reaches for it constantly because his England is all edges, hedges, copses, and field-margins meeting in view after view.

Core meaning in detail: The verb divides into two great pictures that are really one: (a) the static sense, where a feature lies along a boundary and decorates or defines it (woods skirt the meadow, suburbs skirt the city), and (b) the motion sense, where a traveller follows that boundary line and thereby avoids the interior (to skirt the marsh, to skirt the crowd). In both, the margin is the whole point: the eye or the foot honours the edge and declines the centre, whether from admiration, caution, or mere convenience.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To border, flank, or lie along the edge of something (primary landscape sense, from late 16th c.; the source sense): poplars skirted the canal. The bridge runs straight from the noun skirt as garment-hem: just as a skirt rims the body, so woods or buildings rim a valley, and the verb turns the hem into an action of enclosing.
  • To travel along the edge of an area, or to pass around it rather than through it (motion sense): they skirted the bog by a sheep-track. The bridge extends the static border into a journey: the line that rims the place becomes the path the walker takes, keeping dry feet and a safe distance while still advancing.
  • To go around an obstacle or difficulty instead of confronting it, often deliberately (figurative, very common): to skirt the town centre at rush hour, to skirt bankruptcy. The bridge carries the path around the marsh into life in general: traffic, debt, and danger are all treated as ground too soft to cross, best given a wide berth.
  • To evade or avoid a topic, question, or rule without quite breaking it (idiomatic, 19th–20th c.): she skirted the question, to skirt the law. The bridge narrows the bypass sense onto speech and conduct: the speaker walks the rim of the forbidden subject, touching it nowhere, exactly as the traveller walks the rim of the town.
  • To be situated on the margin of a condition or threshold (stative, journalistic): inflation skirted double digits. The bridge abstracts the edge into a number line: values hover at the brink without crossing, and the verb gives statistics the drama of a cliff walk.
  • To throw, scatter, or skip, or to move in a lively darting way (rare, Scots and dialectal, from a different Old Norse root sense of shooting): stones skirted across water in some dialects. The bridge here is thinner and scholars dispute it, but the image of a stone kissing the edge of the water keeps at least a sound-echo of hems and margins.
  • Noun background — the hem or outskirts from which the verb grows: skirts of a coat, skirts of a wood, the outskirts of Leeds. Every verbal use remembers this clothing image: the edge that flares, flows, and can be lifted aside, whether of a garment, a town, or a vale.

Register and connotation: skirted is graceful and slightly literary in landscape use, the word of travel writers and estate agents who love edges, but shrewd and faintly evasive in its figurative uses, where skirting a question suggests dexterity shading into slipperiness. Armies skirt defences, drivers skirt jams, politicians skirt scandals: in each case the verb admires the agility while hinting the centre was never faced.

Collocations and grammar: framed as skirted by for the passive border (a lake skirted by pines), skirted around or round for the bypass journey, and skirted with for trimming (a cloak skirted with fur in older use). As a participle it often premodifies: the skirted vale, a skirted town, where the edge has become the place's defining feature.
