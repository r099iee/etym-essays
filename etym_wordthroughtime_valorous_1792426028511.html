- [Classical & Late Latin Roots (c. 1st c. BCE–4th c. CE)] - rooted in Classical Latin valēre ("to be strong, healthy, of worth") and its Late Latin abstract noun valor ("worth, value, moral strength"), originally referring to physical vigor and economic or legal validity - [perception: viewed as a practical term of bodily strength and legal-economic worth]
- [Old French & Anglo-Norman Chivalric Era (c. 11th–13th c.)] - transformed in medieval French chivalric literature (the *chansons de geste*, such as the Chanson de Roland) into valor / valur and the adjective valoreus / valorus, signifying the supreme martial virtue of the knight: prowess in battle combined with noble honor and loyalty to one's liege lord - [perception: revered as the crowning aristocratic ideal of chivalric knighthood]
- [Middle English Chivalric Romance (c. late 14th–15th c.)] - borrowed into Middle English during the Hundred Years' War (*valerous*), appearing in works by Chaucer, Gower, and Lydgate to praise Arthurian knights and crusader champions - [perception: celebrated as the quintessential literary epithet for heroic warrior-nobility]
- [Renaissance & Elizabethan Epic (16th–17th c.)] - immortalized across Elizabethan drama and epic poetry, notably in Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene and Shakespeare's history plays (*Henry V*, *Richard III*), celebrating English martial heroes and classical commanders - [perception: admired as a prestigious, resonant adjective of heroic grandeur]
- [Victorian Neomedievalism & Military Honors (19th c.)] - revitalized during the 19th-century Romantic and Victorian medieval revival (Walter Scott, Alfred Tennyson's Idylls of the King) and institutionalized in military citations (most famously the Victoria Cross instituted in 1856 bearing the inscription "For Valour") - [perception: venerated as the ultimate civic and military compliment for conspicuous bravery in the presence of the enemy]
- [Modern & Contemporary Register (20th–21st c.)] - preserved in modern English primarily in elevated literary, historical, and fantasy epic genres (such as Tolkien's legendarium) and formal military decorations - [perception: regarded as an evocative, grand, and archaic-leaning term of deep respect for extraordinary battlefield bravery]
