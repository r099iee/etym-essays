GOWN, noun. At its core, a gown is a long, loose outer garment worn for a particular formal, domestic, or professional purpose — but the word spans a surprisingly wide register, from the most ceremonial attire in the language (wedding gown, coronation robe) to the most utilitarian (hospital gown), and even into the abstract vocabulary of academic life (“town and gown”). No single translation captures all its senses, which is why a full definition must map the whole garment family. In the passage from The Story of the Stone, Bao-yu relaxes into private dress: “He had changed his robe and jacket for a rather more worn-looking rose-coloured gown, sprigged with flowers.” The sentence marks a descent in formality. The robe and jacket are public, structured garments of the great house; the gown is the looser, softer layer of the inner quarters, and the adjective “worn-looking” tells us it has been lived in — this is a garment for comfort, not ceremony. Yet the word keeps its luxury: the gown is rose-coloured and “sprigged with flowers,” so that even Bao-yu’s most relaxed dress is silk-bright and flower-embroidered, an indication that in the Jia household even casual attire is a statement of wealth and taste. The nuance of the word is the body’s release — the shift from the rigid public self to the private, domestic self — and the quiet paradox that this release is itself conducted in garments of beauty. The primary senses of GOWN:
  • A woman’s long formal dress. The dominant modern sense: a full-length dress worn for ceremonial or festive occasions. The evening gown and ball gown are floor-length formal dresses for social events; the wedding gown (or bridal gown) is the bride’s ceremonial dress; a debutante gown marks a young woman’s presentation to society. Here gown is a dress raised to its most formal, floor-length, luxurious form.
  • A loose domestic garment. The dressing gown and nightgown are soft, loose, full-length garments worn at home for comfort — the precise register of Bao-yu’s “worn-looking” rose gown, the garment of the body’s release from the public self.
  • A professional or ceremonial robe. The academic gown worn at universities and at graduation, the judge’s gown in court, the cleric’s gown, the barrister’s gown — garments that mark office, learning, and authority. The phrase town and gown pits the lay townspeople against the university community, gown standing metonymically for the scholar.
  • A utilitarian garment. The hospital gown or patient gown, the surgical gown, and the laboratory gown are functional, often backless or disposable garments worn for hygiene, medical access, or protection — the least ceremonial member of the family, showing how far the word has democratised.
  • Historical and archaic senses. In older English a gown could be any long loose outer garment of a man or woman; a gownsman was a civilian or a scholar (as opposed to a soldier in uniform), and a morning gown was an informal robe worn before dressing.
A secondary, technical family of senses derives from the garment as an emblem of office:
  • Metonymy of authority and learning. Because judges, clerics, and academics wear gowns, the word stands for their offices — the gown meaning the legal or clerical profession, gowned meaning robed or ceremonially dressed. “To follow the gown” historically meant to pursue a legal or ecclesiastical career.
  • The ‘gown’ of a woman’s figure. In tailoring, the loose draped or full-length cut of a garment; in older usage, any woman’s outer dress as distinct from her underclothes.
  • Figurative and transferred uses. By extension, anything that wraps or covers loosely like a gown (a gown of snow, a gown of vines); in embroidery and fashion copy, a loose, flowing silhouette.
The semantic history of the word is a fall and a rise. Etymologically a gown began as a coarse garment of skin or fur — a shepherd’s or monk’s protective outer layer — and climbed the social scale until, in the 18th and 19th centuries, it became the most glamorous word in a woman’s wardrobe. Today the same word that names a bridal masterpiece also names the flimsy hospital gown, and both are equally “gowns”: the word’s genius is that it covers the full social range, from the throne room to the sickbed, held together by the single idea of a long, loose, enveloping garment worn over other clothing. GOWN must be weighed against its garment-neighbours. (1) Robe is the closest relative: both are loose, long garments, but a robe tends to be more formal or ceremonial (judicial robes, monastic robes) and is worn in the West mostly by men, while gown has extended to academic, medical, and women’s evening wear. (2) Dress is the modern everyday word for a woman’s one-piece outer garment; a gown is a dress in its most formal, floor-length form — an “evening gown” or “ball gown” is the dress’s ceremonial cousin. (3) Frock is a dated word for a girl’s dress or a loose smock, and also for a clergyman’s outer garment; it is smaller and less formal than a gown. (4) Habit denotes a religious or riding garment with a fixed, institutional form; a gown is not so prescribed. (5) Tunic and caftan are shorter or differently cut loose garments; a gown is characteristically long and floor-skimming. In Ruskin’s and Victorian usage generally, the gown is the formal garment par excellence, the dress of ceremony, office, and occasion — which makes Bao-yu’s private, worn-looking rose gown a small comedy of privilege: even the informality of the great house is conducted in beauty.
