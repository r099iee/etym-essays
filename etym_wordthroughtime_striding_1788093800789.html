- Old English strīdende — the participle of strīdan 'to stride, straddle, bestride': purely physical long-legged walking and mounting of horses — neutral, kinetic, no attitude.
- Middle English stridynge — the same physical core, now also a noun for the act of striding; still neutral, but increasingly used of imposing or heroic movement in narrative and devotional verse.
- 16th–18th centuries — 'striding' becomes a marker of majesty in elevated prose and poetry (gods striding the heavens, kings striding halls): the word picks up permanent connotations of power, dominion and command.
- 19th century — adventure and imperial fiction fix 'striding' as the gait of the decisive hero (striding over deserts, striding into rooms); the participle gains a muscular, slightly menacing confidence and feeds the progress idioms: 'make strides', 'take it in one's stride'.
- 20th century — 'striding' goes figurative in business and politics ('a company striding ahead'); computing borrows the word for regular spaced motion in memory (strided access); stride piano names a jazz left-hand technique of wide swinging leaps.
- 21st century — the literal sense is now consciously literary or athletic (coaches measure stride length, fitness apps track stride rate); in speech the word survives chiefly idiomatically, while prose keeps 'striding' as its standard image for unstoppable, self-assured advance.
