In Gibbon's biography, Lausanne is not a neutral pin on a map but the decisive scene of intellectual and religious re-formation. Sent there by his father after a youthful conversion to Roman Catholicism, the sixteen-year-old was placed under a tutor who swiftly returned him to Protestantism and immersed him in a rigorous, French-influenced scholarly culture. The name thus carries biographical charge: Lausanne stands for correction without mere humiliation — a forced reorientation that nonetheless equipped Gibbon with languages, habits of study, and a cosmopolitan European outlook essential to the future Decline and Fall. In the passage, "sent to Lausanne" compresses family authority, religious crisis, and the making of a historian into a single place-name.

Core status: a toponym, not a common noun. Lausanne is a proper name for a specific city on the northern shore of Lake Geneva in French-speaking Switzerland. As a proper noun it does not have "senses" in the way an abstract or common word does; its reference is fixed by geography and history rather than by definitional features. What a lexicographic entry can still supply is a rich account of what the name points to, how it functions in different discourses, and why it has accumulated symbolic weight far beyond its denotative function.

Primary and everyday reference: Lausanne is the capital of the canton of Vaud, the fourth-largest city of Switzerland, and the seat of the federal supreme court. It lies on a steep hillside terraced above the north-western shore of Lake Geneva (Lac Léman), facing the French and Savoyard Alps across the water. The city is an important administrative, judicial, banking, and cultural centre, and it is famous today as the headquarters of the International Olympic Committee (IOC), which is why it is sometimes styled the "Olympic Capital."

Secondary senses and metonymic uses:
  • The Lausanne years (biographical shorthand) — in Gibbon studies, "Lausanne" names the period of his residence there, above all 1753–1758 as a young man and 1783–1793 as a settled historian; the phrase signals formation, maturity, and the completion of the Decline and Fall.
  • Educational-religious metonymy — in the context of Gibbon's autobiography, "sent to Lausanne" stands for paternal discipline, reconversion to Protestantism, and scholarly immersion; the city functions as a metonym for a particular regime of correction and study.
  • Topographic reference — "Lausanne" can evoke the lake-and-mountain setting itself, a romantic scene praised by Voltaire, Rousseau, Byron, and later travellers; in tourist and travel discourse it functions as shorthand for the beautiful Vaudois shore.
  • Institutional metonymy — the name stands for the University of Lausanne, the Lausanne Hospital, the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL), or the headquarters of the IOC and many sporting federations, depending on context.
  • Treaty and legal reference — the name is attached to international agreements concluded in the city, most famously the Treaty of Lausanne of 1923, which fixed the borders of the modern Republic of Turkey; "Lausanne" can therefore metonymically name that settlement and its aftermath in diplomatic history.

Historical and cultural load: Lausanne was the seat of a bishopric from the early Middle Ages, passed under Bernese rule after the Reformation and became a Protestant stronghold, was an Enlightenment-era centre of letters on the shores of Lac Léman, and is now a bilingual-facing, polyglot hub of international organizations. Each layer — episcopal, Protestant, Enlightenment, Olympic, judicial — contributes a distinct connotation that a writer can activate. The name is thus dense: it can sound religiously austere, intellectually elevated, administratively neutral, or picturesquely alpine depending on the collocation.

Collocations and register: the view from Lausanne; Gibbon at Lausanne; the Lausanne years; sent to Lausanne; return to Lausanne; the Treaty of Lausanne; the Olympic Capital. Register is proper-name and factual in geography, legal, or administrative prose, but in literary biography and memoir the word accumulates an almost emblematic symbolic value — discipline, Francophone scholarship, and the recovery of a Protestant, worldly identity. For language learners the practical point is that Lausanne is a place-name to be recognised and placed, not a word to be defined by synonyms.
