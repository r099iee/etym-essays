The story of LINEATION begins with a plant and ends with a poem. Its deep ancestry lies in the flax plant — the source of thread, cord, and linen cloth — and the word's whole career is a study in how a concrete object (a string) becomes an abstract idea (a line) and then a technical term in two very different sciences (poetry and geology). Every stage of the journey preserves, in the word's skeleton, the memory of the flaxen thread that started it. Stage one — flax (Greek linon, Latin līnum): The Greek word linon meant the flax plant, its fibre, the thread spun from it, and the linen cloth woven from it. Homer knows it; the ancient world wove its sails, its nets, its shirts, and its shrouds from it. The word came into Latin as līnum, and Latin built a small family on it: linum "flax, thread, linen", lineus "made of flax", and — the crucial step — linea, originally the feminine of lineus, "made of flax", used as a noun for "a flaxen thread". The oldest sense of linea, in fact, is that of a cord: the string a mason or surveyor stretched taut, dusted with chalk, and snapped against stone or plaster to mark a perfectly straight line. From that simple craft — a flaxen string that draws a straight mark — every later sense of "line" in English, and every sense of lineation, descends. The thread had become an instrument of geometry, and the word for the instrument (linea, "the flaxen string") became the word for its product (linea, "the drawn line"). This is one of the clearest cases in the history of European vocabulary of a word migrating from material to tool to result. Stage two — the line in Rome (Varro, Vitruvius): By the 1st century BCE linea was fully established in its transferred senses: a drawn mark, a row, a rank, a verse. The architect Vitruvius, writing in the 30s BCE, used the verb lineare "to draw lines" and the noun lineatio for the ground-plan of a building — the drawing made of lines that sets out a design before it is built. The Latin derivational machinery did the rest: lineatio, from the stem lineat-, names the act and the product of drawing lines. It is a testimony to Roman engineering that the same word family produced both the builder's straight line and the abstract idea of arrangement that later science and criticism would borrow. Stage three — English borrowing (17th century): English took the word directly and learnedly in the 17th century: lineation, first recorded in the 1640s, meant the drawing or marking of lines, the ruling of a page, the setting out of a plan. It belonged to the vocabulary of surveying, drawing, and architecture — the 17th-century sense is thoroughly practical. The pronunciation settled early: /ˌlɪn.iˈeɪ.ʃən/, with the stress on the penultimate syllable, exactly as in nation, generation, and the other -ation abstracts. Stage four — the two scientific careers (19th–20th centuries): In the 19th century the word was adopted by the natural sciences, and in the 20th by literary criticism, and it is worth watching how far the two careers diverged from the same root. In geology, lineation became the standard term for a linear fabric in rock: the preferred alignment of elongate minerals, crystals, pebbles, or fold axes produced when a rock is stretched, sheared, or flowed. A stretching lineation records the direction in which a mountain belt was pulled; an intersection lineation marks the crossing of two planar fabrics. Geologists read lineations like compass needles, and the plural "lineations" is their everyday currency. The bridge from the Roman line is the bridge of drawing: rock that has been deformed is literally drawn out, marked with lines by the earth's forces. In poetics, meanwhile, lineation came to mean the division of a poem into lines — the placement of the line-break. Free verse, which has no metre to dictate where lines end, is defined by its lineation: the poet's choice of where each line breaks is the fundamental act of free-verse composition. Criticism speaks of "the poem's lineation" as the system that enjambment exploits, that caesuras punctuate, and that the reader experiences as shape on the page. The same word that plots the grain of a mountain now plots the breath of a poem. Socio-cultural factors and literary attestations:
  • Ancient craft: the masons' and surveyors' string, the chalked cord that snapped a straight line, is the physical ancestor of every "line" in modern English — the word lineage, the line of a poem, the line of a railway, the line of descent all descend from linea.
  • Vitruvius (c. 30–15 BCE) gives the first recorded lineatio, the architectural plan — the earliest attestation of the word in any language.
  • 17th-century English: the word appears in books of surveying and drawing, marking its learned, practical register.
  • 19th-century science: geologists, crystallographers, and botanists take it up; "the lineation of the rock", "the lineation of the leaf" become ordinary scientific prose.
  • 20th-century criticism: with the rise of free verse and the New Criticism, lineation becomes a central term of poetics — the word that lets critics talk precisely about what Ezra Pound and William Carlos Williams did with the broken line.
Semantic drift in summary: flax (the plant) → thread (the material) → string (the tool) → drawn line (the result) → row/verse (the meaning) → act of drawing lines (lineare) → the drawing itself (lineatio) → English lineation (drawing and layout, 17th c.) → geological fabric (19th c.) → the poetic line-break system (20th c.). The movement is always from the concrete to the abstract, and each new science found the old word waiting for it. LINEATION is thus a small museum of the history of measurement, drawing, and verse: pick it up and you are holding the flaxen thread that the surveyor first pulled tight three thousand years ago.
