- [Hellenistic Antiquity] - Derived from Ancient Greek σωτηρία (bodily safety, preservation from mortal harm, deliverance) and the cultic epithet σωτήρ (savior, applied to gods like Zeus and Asclepius, and Hellenistic monarchs like Ptolemy I Soter) - [connotation: civic deliverance from military disaster, physical healing, royal protection]
- [1st–4th c. CE / Patristic Era] - Adopted into the Greek Septuagint and the New Testament, where the root underwent profound semantic elevation from temporal physical safety to cosmic redemption from sin, death, and spiritual condemnation - [connotation: divine grace, eternal life, theological redemption through Jesus Christ]
- [17th–19th c.] - Anglicized within academic theological treatises and ecclesiastical systematics, primarily in the substantive noun form *soteriology* and the adjectival variants *soterial* / *soteric* - [connotation: scholarly doctrinal classification, systematic study of the work of Christ, dogmatic precision]
- [20th c.–Present] - Migrates from dogmatic Christian theology into continental philosophy, existentialism, and secular literary criticism (e.g. Roland Barthes, Walter Benjamin, Paul Ricœur) to characterize art, writing, memory, or revolutionary action as delivering human consciousness from existential despair and temporal erasure - [connotation: secularized redemption, aesthetic salvation, existential preservation]
