- [Scholarly entry (17th c.)] - Borrowed into learned English usage from Latin "recumbere" (to lie back), describing a person lying down, reclining, or resting; a formal, neutral word of the educated register.
- [Technical specializations (18th–19th c.)] - Adopted by heraldry (a recumbent beast lying down with head raised), by sculpture (a recumbent effigy lying flat on a tomb), and by medicine and natural history for any organ or body lying flat; the word gains a dignified, technical flavor.
- [20th c. popular currency] - Enters everyday life mainly through the recumbent bicycle, whose reclined seating posture brings the once-bookish word into ordinary use.
- [Contemporary sense] - Describes anything lying flat or inclined backward, from a resting figure to a sloped posture; still more literary and clinical than plain "lying" or "reclining," it carries restful, comfortable, and entirely neutral associations.
