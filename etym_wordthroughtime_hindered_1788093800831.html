- [Old English (pre-1100): "to injure, harm"] - Hindrian also means "to injure, harm", rooted in hinder "behind" — harming by striking from behind; a blunt, physical verb of attack and damage.
- [Middle English (13th–14th centuries): "to impede"] - Hindren shifts toward "to hold back, impede, obstruct"; the injure-sense fades; the word becomes a workhorse of legal and moral discourse.
- [16th–17th centuries: the KJV sense] - Biblical usage fixes the modern meaning ("what doth hinder me to be baptized?", Acts 8:36); "hindered" is now purely about obstruction, no longer about injury.
- [18th–19th centuries: the logistics of progress] - Applied to commerce, science, travel and industry; the industrial age makes "hindered" a word of delays, bottlenecks, blocked roads and canals.
- [Contemporary: abstract and administrative] - Fully psychological and institutional: people are "hindered" by fear, bureaucracy, injury or lack of resources; the passive participle "hindered by" is a neutral staple of news and formal writing.
