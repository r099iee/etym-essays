RENDEZVOUS (pronounced roughly "ron-day-voo") is an English noun and verb meaning a meeting, or the act of meeting, at a pre-arranged time and place. It is a spectacularly complete borrowing: English adopted the whole French imperative phrase rendez-vous - literally "present yourselves" or "betake yourselves" - as a single word in 1590, keeping the French pronunciation, the French spelling, and even the French hyphen. The result is a word with a double life: as a noun it names either the meeting itself or the place where the meeting happens, and as a verb it means to meet, or to cause forces to come together, at an appointed spot. Because the note records the base form, it can be used in the full verbal paradigm ("we rendezvous at midnight", "they rendezvoused at dawn") and in the nominal plural (rendezvouses), with the adjective rendezvous also possible in attributive compounds like "rendezvous point".

Primary senses and core usage.

  • Sense 1 - A meeting at an agreed time and place. The core noun sense: two or more people, groups, or forces agree to come together at a stated moment and location. "Their secret rendezvous was discovered by the press". The word differs from a mere meeting in its precision and its drama: a rendezvous is always arranged, always placed, and always slightly charged with purpose - military, romantic, or conspiratorial.
  • Sense 2 - The place appointed for the meeting. The word can name the location itself: "the old oak tree was their rendezvous". This place-sense is historically the oldest in English - the word entered the language as the military term for the designated assembly point where scattered troops were to re-form.
  • Sense 3 - A meeting-place of people or things. A wider, atmospheric application: a place where people habitually gather ("a rendezvous for artists"), or the point where two rivers, roads, or forces come together. This shades into the spatial figure of convergence.
  • Sense 4 - The verb: to meet, or to assemble forces, at an appointed place. The verbal use, most at home in military and technical language: "the fleets will rendezvous offshore", "we rendezvoused with the supply column at first light". On the card the verb appears in the infinitive: "we will __________ with your army at first light" - a general agreeing to join forces at a stated time, the pure military sense.

Secondary, figurative, and idiomatic meanings.

  • The romantic and secretive association. Because a rendezvous is arranged in advance and often kept discreet, the word acquired a strong suggestion of romance, intrigue, or secrecy. "A lovers' rendezvous", "a secret rendezvous" - these collocations dominate modern fiction, so that the word now sits somewhere between military assembly and clandestine assignation.
  • Spaceflight and technology: since the 1960s, "rendezvous" is the standard term in space engineering for the meeting of two spacecraft in orbit - the moment when an active vehicle catches up with and docks with a target. This is a direct modern extension of the military sense: vehicles assembling at an agreed point, now in orbit.
  • Navigation and logistics: "rendezvous point", "rendezvous time" - used for the co-ordinated gathering of ships, aircraft, ground parties, or convoys in the armed forces, emergency services, and expedition planning.
  • Figurative convergence: the word can describe any coming-together of elements, as in "the rendezvous of art and science", where two currents finally meet.

Register, tone, and form. Rendezvous is a colourful, distinctly French-flavoured word kept alive in English for its glamour and precision. It is formal enough for military dispatches and romantic enough for fiction; in casual speech it yields to meeting or get-together. Its plural is rendezvouses (though many writers use the singular spelling for the plural), and the verb forms are rendezvouses, rendezvoused, rendezvousing. It collocates strongly with secret, lovers', midnight, appointed, point, and with ("rendezvous with").

Historical semantic bridge. The word began, in the sixteenth century, as a military instruction - the imperative order "rendez-vous", "present yourselves", given to troops told to assemble. From that command English took the noun for the assembly point, then widened it to any appointed meeting and any habitual gathering-place, and finally to a romantic or secretive assignation. In the twentieth century the military idea returned in a new domain: two spacecraft rendezvous in orbit, presenting themselves to each other at an agreed point. The card's sentence, "we will __________ with your army at first light", uses the verb in the oldest surviving sense - forces meeting at a fixed hour and place - and the French command of 1590 is still audible inside it.
