grunts is the plural noun (and third-person singular verb) form of grunt: a short, low, throaty, inarticulate sound produced with the breath forced through a partly closed glottis — the pig’s oink-grunt, the weightlifter’s uhn, the reluctant hearer’s mmph. As a noun it names both the acoustic event and, by metonymy, the utterance so reduced as to be scarcely linguistic: “monosyllabic grunts” in the source sentence means talk stripped to bare acknowledgment — yeah, uh-huh, hmph — gestures of voice rather than vehicles of proposition. The word’s very sound performs its sense: the consonant cluster gr- growls, the nasal -un- resonates in the nose and throat, the final -t cuts the sound short.

Core and extended senses cluster tightly around this bodily prototype:

  • Animal cry (prototype): the pig’s characteristic call, also of boars, bears, and apes. Bridge: onomatopoeic origin; the word imitates what it names, and pig-grunting remains the dictionary’s first illustration.
  • Human effort-noise: the involuntary sound of lifting, falling, pain, or exertion. Bridge: same articulatory mechanism under strain; the body grunts when breath meets resistance.
  • Inarticulate assent/dissent: “he answered only with grunts.” Bridge: reduction — a grunt carries prosody and attitude (reluctance, contempt, amusement) without lexical content, exactly the attitudes-plus-facts bundle the source sentence says real talk exceeds.
  • Minimal speech (pejorative): “monosyllabic grunts” as caveman talk. Bridge: evolutionary and developmental metaphor; grunts stand for the pre- or sub-linguistic, what language left behind.
  • Military / labor slang ("grunts"): U.S. infantry privates (Vietnam-era) or low-level workers as “grunts.” Bridge: persons metonymized to their supposed inarticulacy and expendability; grunts do the heavy lifting and make the heavy noises.
  • Verb senses: to grunt = to utter such a sound; to grunt out an order = to say it throatily and curtly. Bridge: regular zero-derivation between cry and crying.

Register is informal-concrete and mildly dismissive: to call utterances “grunts” is to deny them the dignity of words, which is why the source sentence uses the phrase as a foil — of course we do more than grunt. Collocates confirm: mere, monosyllabic, inarticulate, guttural, disapproving, appreciative, animal, pig-like; give, utter, emit, answer with, communicate in. Phonetically the singular is /ɡrʌnt/, plural /ɡrʌnts/ with a demanding three-consonant coda that itself requires a small grunt of effort to pronounce. To master the word is to hear how much social meaning can hide in a non-word — and how much more real conversation adds on top.

What makes the source sentence memorable is its deliberate understatement as setup: by granting the reductive picture first — mere gestures and monosyllabic grunts — the author lets the reader feel the poverty of any purely informational theory of language before unfolding the richer account of attitudes negotiated, relationships redefined, and social selves reaffirmed. Grunts are thus doing double duty: denotatively they name minimal vocalization, connotatively they name everything a thin theory of communication mistakes talk for. Recognizing that double function helps the learner use the word with irony rather than mere contempt.
