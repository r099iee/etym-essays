stubbed stands at the end of a short, sturdy lineage that is entirely Germanic and almost entirely about trees. The line begins, probably, with the Proto-Indo-European root *steu-, "to push, strike, knock", but the scholars are cautious: the geminate -bb- of the Germanic base suggests a sound-symbolic or expressive formation, so the PIE attribution is conjectural rather than certain. Whatever its deeper origin, Proto-Germanic *stubbaz meant "a stump" — the short projecting remnant left when a tree is cut down. This passed into Old English as stybb, attested in glosses and place-names, and on into Middle English as stubbe: "a stump, a short blunt remnant". The noun proved remarkably productive. From the fifteenth century English had the verb stub, "to clear land of stumps" — hard, practical, agricultural labour — and the noun extended itself to anything short and leftover: a pencil stub, a ticket stub, the stub of a candle. The verb sense that produces stubbed in the passage above is late: the records show "to strike (one's toe) against something projecting" from the nineteenth century, the image being that the toe collides with a stump-like piece of the world. The twentieth century added the cigarette idiom stub out, "to extinguish by pressing the burning stub against a surface".

Phonetically, the word has been remarkably stable. Old English stybb, with its high front vowel, shifted to Middle English stubbe with a short back vowel — the product of the same front-to-back rounding that affected other words with a following labial — and the modern form stub has kept the short /ʌ/ ever since. The doubling of the -b- in the written form is the relic of a historically long consonant, and the weak past tense in -ed (stubbed) is the standard dental formation that English uses for newly created or regularised verbs: once the "strike one's toe" sense was coined in the nineteenth century, it took the regular weak paradigm without hesitation, giving stub–stubbed exactly as the language forms any new verb today.

Semantically, the shift is from object to action to mishap. First the word names the tree-stump itself — a leftover of human clearing of the forest. Then, because a stump is the very thing you trip on in a freshly cleared field, the verb "to stub" comes to name the collision with such a projection. The step from "to clear land of stumps" to "to strike one's toe on a stump-like thing" is small and natural: the obstacle gives the action its name. By the nineteenth century the verb had detached entirely from trees and could be used of any hard fixed projection — a bedpost, a stone, a threshold — and the idiom took on its modern, homely, faintly comic flavour. The twentieth century extended the crushing-of-a-remnant image to cigarettes, so that "to stub out" now pairs the noun's "remnant" sense with the verb's "press against something hard" sense in a single action.

In society and literature, the word has always lived in the rustic, the domestic, and the unheroic. It belongs to the world of fields and farmyards (stubbing land), of the pocket and the desk (ticket stubs, pencil stubs), and of minor household accidents (stubbing a toe). Tolkien uses it with deliberate lowliness: in a chapter full of peril, the detail that Bilbo stubs his toes "again, several times, on nasty jagged stones" keeps the hobbit human and comic, anchoring the adventure in the small, physical, recognizable clumsiness of everyday life. The word's bluntness — its double -b-, its short vowel, its stumpy associations — is exactly what makes it the right instrument for that effect, and it has been performing the same duty, in one form or another, for over a thousand years.
