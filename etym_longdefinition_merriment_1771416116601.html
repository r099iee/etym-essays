Merriment is light-hearted, cheerful gaiety — the state of being merry — and, in its fullest use, the openly expressed, shared, often noisy amusement of a group at play: laughter, banter, clinking glasses, songs, and high spirits in company. It is a social word; merriment by definition requires an audience and a festive occasion, which is what distinguishes it from the private joy a person can feel alone in silence. In the passage from the Dream of the Red Chamber the word is dramatically well chosen: Xi-feng, who has been performing grief, is ordered by the Matriarch to stop and instantly “exchanged her grief for merriment”. The scene lays bare the performative, outward, crowd-facing character of merriment — it is not here a report of inner happiness but a public show of wit, laughter, and charm switched on at command to lighten the heavy, formal atmosphere of an aristocratic household and to protect the fragile composure of the newly arrived cousin.

Polysemy and secondary senses:

  • Festive gaiety: the dominant sense — cheerful, festive high spirits expressed in shared amusement; “sounds of merriment”, “a scene of merriment”, “to join the merriment”.
  • The cause of amusement: by extension, merriment can name the very thing that provokes the laughter — “the jest was a great source of merriment” — a sense in which the word hovers between emotion and entertainment.
  • Cheerfulness of disposition: a milder sense, close to “high spirits” or “good cheer”, where merriment is a state of being rather than an event — “in a mood of merriment”.
  • Conviviality: the word strongly connotes eating, drinking, and feasting together — the “merry-making” of banquets, taverns, fairs, and wedding feasts — which shades it toward “revelry” and “jollity”.
  • Boundaries of intensity: merriment is milder and warmer than hilarity (extreme, boisterous, near-uncontrollable laughter) but noisier and more social than mirth (wittier, more restrained, possibly silent and inward).
  • Register: merriment carries a slightly old-fashioned, literary, or nostalgic flavour, evoking pre-modern communal celebration — the roasted chestnuts and wassail bowls of an English Christmas — rather than the newer vocabulary of “fun”, “party”, or “entertainment”.

Register and collocation. Merriment is used most naturally in formal, literary, and historical prose. Collocations: “sounds of merriment”, “peals of merriment”, “boisterous merriment”, “festive merriment”, “general merriment”, “join in the merriment”, “exchanged her grief for merriment”. Its near-synonym field is rich: joy is profound, internal, and can be silent; happiness is a broad, sustained state of well-being; glee is a sudden, sometimes slightly mischievous burst; hilarity is extreme and unruly laughter; mirth is lighter, wittier, more contained; gaiety is bright, playful cheer, close to merriment but with less noise and less festivity; jollity and revelry emphasize feasting and carousing. Merriment uniquely names the warm, audible, communal joy of people celebrating together — which is precisely why, in the novel, the Matriarch’s command that grief be exchanged for it is a demand not for private comfort but for a resumption of the household’s public, festive composure.
