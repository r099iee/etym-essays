- Middle English entry - adorn came from Old French adorner, from Latin adornare (to furnish, equip), and carried from the first the sense of fitting something out with what is fine or beautiful.
- Renaissance and classical usage - adorned became the dignified, formal word for beautification by costly and artistic ornament, applied to persons, buildings, and speech in elevated prose.
- Macaulay's ironic use - in the 19th century Macaulay could say Constantinople's buildings were still adorned with the sculptures of Polycletus and the paintings of Apelles, the city wearing the jewellery of a dead world - dazzling to the eye, empty at the core.
- Contemporary sense - adorned is somewhat literary and formal, used of people and monuments decked with fine things, while the everyday neutral word for adding ornament is decorated.
