departed — the past tense and past participle of the verb depart ("gone away, left"), and a fully independent adjective and noun in its own right: as an adjective it means "deceased; dead", and as a plural noun (the departed) it is the standard gentle euphemism for those who have died. On this card it appears in the funerary, mythic register of Edward Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, where "the spirits of the departed were ferried over from the land of the Franks at midnight" — death imagined as a voyage, and the dead as travellers who have gone away.

Sense 1 — The literal verbal sense: gone away, left. As the past tense of depart, the word means simply "left, went away": "the train departed on time", "they departed for Rome", "he departed the room". This sense is entirely spatial and neutral, inherited from the Middle English and Old French verb meaning "to separate, divide, go away". It carries no emotional or funerary weight by itself; the euphemistic sense grows directly out of it.

Sense 2 — The euphemism for the dead. From the sixteenth century onward, departed was pressed into reverential service as a term for the dead: "our departed friend", "the faithful departed", "the dear departed". The semantic bridge is a metaphor of death as a journey of departure — the dying "go away" from this life to another shore. Because departure is a natural, unhurried, and hopeful image (unlike the abrupt fact of "dead"), the word softens bereavement: to say "the departed" is to describe those who have left on a journey rather than those who have ceased to be. This is why the word is at home in obituaries, prayers, memorials, and formal condolence.

Sense 3 — "The departed" as a collective noun. In the plural and with the definite article, the departed names the dead as a class: "we honor the departed", "the spirits of the departed". In religious and liturgical English it is especially the term for the souls of the faithful who have gone to the next life ("pray for the departed", the "faithful departed" of the requiem and All Souls'). On this card the phrase "the spirits of the departed" shows the collective sense fully developed: the dead are a ferry-load of souls on their midnight passage.

Sense 4 — "Departed from": deviation and abandonment. In formal and figurative use, departed (with from) means "deviated from, given up, abandoned": "departed from tradition", "departed from the official protocol", "a man who had departed from the party line". This sense keeps the oldest core of the word — separation, parting from a thing — and shows that the English verb never lost its Latin-derived sense of "dividing, parting off".

Sense 5 — Other senses and cultural echoes. In older and poetic English the verb also meant "to part, separate, divide" (the transitive sense, now archaic). In modern culture the word is famous as the title of Martin Scorsese's 2006 film The Departed, a crime drama whose title puns on the euphemism for the dead, on police officers "departed" from the force, and on the souls lost to violence. As an adjective (departed leader) it retains full formal dignity in eulogy and journalism.

Register and usage. In modern English departed is the most solemn and literary of the ordinary words for the dead. It belongs to obituaries, epitaphs, funeral liturgy, and formal condolence, where its gentleness — refusing to say "dead" outright — is precisely its point. On this card the word performs that gentleness in a dark, mythic key: the dead are not corpses but "spirits", not annihilated but "ferried over", travellers whose midnight crossing from "the land of the Franks" makes the euphemism literal.
