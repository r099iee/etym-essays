Constitution (noun; plural: constitutions) denotes the fundamental nature, structural composition, or aggregate of essential qualities that establish an entity, whether that entity is a human body, a political state, an organization, or a physical substance. The word represents one of the most versatile and influential polysemic terms in the English language, bridging the biological, legal, philosophical, and physical sciences.

The term spans several highly developed domains of meaning:

  • Physiological and Psychological Makeup of an Individual: The innate physical vitality, bodily strength, genetic endowment, and temperament of a person (e.g., "a robust constitution", "a delicate constitution", "courage that grows from constitution"). In historical and 18th-century medical and philosophical prose (as in the provided context by Joseph Addison), constitution specifically refers to this innate somatic and temperamental disposition—the natural balance of bodily humors and nervous vitality that determines a person's natural health, instinctive courage, or susceptibility to disease.
  • Fundamental Law and Political Governance: In political theory and constitutional law, the fundamental principles, established precedents, and organic legal document according to which a state, nation, or sovereign body is acknowledged to be governed (e.g., the Constitution of the United States, the unwritten British Constitution). It defines the distribution of sovereign powers between branches of government, sets the limits of state authority, and guarantees fundamental civil liberties to citizens.
  • Chemical and Physical Structure: In chemistry and materials science, the arrangement, proportions, and bonding structure of the constituent elements, atoms, or molecules that form a substance or material.
  • Act of Establishing or Instituting: In administrative, ecclesiastical, and corporate contexts, the formal act of creating, establishing, ordaining, or founding a body, institution, or set of regulations (e.g., papal apostolic constitutions, imperial constitutions in Roman law, the constitution of a new governing council).
  • General Structural Composition: The manner in which the parts of any complex system, narrative, or abstract entity are combined and organized (e.g., "the psychological constitution of the human mind", "the molecular constitution of water").

Historical Semantic Bridges and Metaphoric Shifts:

The word derives from Latin constitutio, an abstract noun from the verb constituere, formed from the prefix com- ("together") and statuere ("to set up, place, cause to stand", from the PIE root *stā-, "to stand").

  • From Physical Erection to Sovereign Decree: In early Latin, constituere literally meant to cause items to stand together or erect a physical structure. In the Roman Empire, imperial edicts and decrees that "settled" legal matters were titled constitutiones principis.
  • From Humoral Harmony to Bodily Constitution: In ancient and medieval medicine (Hippocrates and Galen translated into Latin), the term constitutio corporis was adopted to describe the unique "setup" or balance of the four bodily humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) within an individual. If a person was born with a sound humoral setup, they had a "strong constitution."
  • From the Human Body to the Body Politic: During the Renaissance and Enlightenment, the medical metaphor of the "body politic" merged these two streams. Just as an individual body requires a balanced humoral constitution to function in health, a nation requires a balanced legal constitution to prevent tyranny and anarchy.
