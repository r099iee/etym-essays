- 1530s to 1570s - [Nautical command to keep distance] - Coined in maritime jargon as *a loof* (to the luff/weather side), a sailing order to steer into the wind to keep off a lee shore or prevent collision with another ship.
- Late 16th to 17th Century - [Physical separation on land] - Transferred metaphorically from naval navigation to physical human space; used adverbially and prepositionally to describe standing physically apart or keeping out of reach of danger or conflict.
- 18th to 19th Century - [Social, psychological detachment & pride] - Evolved into a predicate adjective characterizing cool social demeanor, aristocratic reserve, or deliberate emotional distance; viewed with a mixture of respect for dignified autonomy and suspicion of haughtiness.
- 20th Century to Present - [Chilly reserve & psychological unreachability] - Retains a strong psychological charge denoting individuals who seem emotionally unreachable, socially detached, or superior, frequently paired in literature with words like 'remote', 'distant', and 'unapproachable'.
