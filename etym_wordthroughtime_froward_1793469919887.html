- Late 12th – Early 13th c. — Directional origin: enters early Middle English as fraward / froward, meaning physically moving away from, averted, or departing.
- 14th c. — Moral divergence: rapidly acquires the figurative meaning of moral waywardness, designating those who turn away from God, virtue, and lawful lords in Chaucer and Wycliffe.
- 16th c. — Biblical and theological peak: William Tyndale and the Elizabethan translators establish froward as the definitive rendering for Hebrew and Greek terms describing moral crookedness and perverse rebellion in Proverbs and the Psalms.
- Late 16th – 17th c. — Renaissance literary ubiquity: employed by Shakespeare (e.g., Petruchio taming the ‘froward’ Katharina), Hooker, and Milton to denote contrary disputants, stubborn adversaries, and rebellious appetites.
- 18th – 19th c. — Classical essayistic register: preserved by Johnson, Scott, and the Brontës as an elevated stylistic archaism for petulant disobedience and contrary dispositions.
- 20th c. – Present — Scholarly and ecclesiastical fossil: survives in literary criticism, theological commentary, and historical prose, evoking the austere moral discernment of Elizabethan English.
