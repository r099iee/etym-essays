The word "wound" is a fascinating example of a homograph in the English language, possessing two distinct etymological origins and, consequently, two primary sets of meanings and pronunciations. The context provided, "In the midst of it there wound lazily a dark river...", clearly refers to the past tense of the verb "to wind," meaning to follow a twisting or curving path. This sense is pronounced /waʊnd/.

I. Verb (past tense/participle of 'wind'): /waʊnd/

In this primary sense relevant to the given context, "wound" is the past simple and past participle form of the verb "to wind." The verb "to wind" itself carries several related meanings:

  • To move in a twisting or curving course: This is the most direct application in the provided sentence. A river that "wound lazily" implies it did not flow in a straight line but rather meandered, curved, or snaked through the landscape. This sense is frequently applied to natural features like rivers, paths, roads, or even the trajectory of a projectile. For example, "The mountain road wound its way up to the summit," or "The path wound through the dense forest."
  • To coil or wrap around something: This refers to the action of forming something into a coil or spiral, or wrapping it around an object. Examples include "to wind a clock" (to tighten its spring), "to wind yarn into a ball," or "to wind a bandage around an injury." In this usage, the past tense would be "He wound the rope around the capstan."
  • To turn or twist: More generally, to cause something to turn or twist, often repeatedly. "He wound the key in the lock."
  • To encircle or entwine: To wrap something around another object, often tightly. "Vines wound themselves around the ancient pillars."
  • To make one's way with difficulty or effort: To move in a laborious or circuitous manner. "He wound his way through the crowded market."

II. Noun (injury) and Verb (to inflict injury): /wuːnd/

This second set of meanings for "wound" is entirely distinct in origin and pronunciation, referring to physical or emotional harm. It is pronounced /wuːnd/.

A. Noun: An injury to the body, typically involving a break in the skin or other tissue, caused by an external agent.

  • Physical Injury: This is the most common and literal sense. A wound is a type of trauma that breaks the skin or other bodily tissues. It can be caused by various means:
    • Incised wound: Caused by a sharp object, resulting in a clean cut.
    • Lacerated wound: A tear or rip in the skin, often irregular and jagged.
    • Punctured wound: Caused by a sharp, pointed object penetrating the skin (e.g., a nail, a needle).
    • Contused wound (contusion): A bruise, where blood vessels are damaged beneath the skin without a break in the surface.
    • Gunshot wound: Caused by a projectile from a firearm.
    • Stab wound: Caused by a sharp object, such as a knife, penetrating the body.
    These physical wounds can range in severity from minor cuts and scrapes to life-threatening injuries requiring immediate medical attention. The term is central to medicine, surgery, and forensic science.
  • Figurative or Emotional Injury: Beyond the physical, "wound" is extensively used metaphorically to describe psychological, emotional, or spiritual pain and damage. This can refer to:
    • Emotional trauma: "The loss of his family left a deep wound in his heart."
    • Psychological scars: "Years of abuse left her with lasting emotional wounds."
    • Damage to pride or reputation: "The public criticism was a wound to his professional standing."
    • A source of distress or grief: "The old wound of betrayal still ached."
    In this sense, the "wound" is not visible but is felt internally, often having long-lasting effects on an individual's well-being or a group's cohesion.
  • Damage to an object or system: Less commonly, but still used, to describe damage to an inanimate object or an abstract system. "The scandal left a wound on the company's reputation."

B. Verb: To inflict a wound upon (someone or something); to injure.

  • To cause physical injury: This is the active form corresponding to the noun. "The hunter wounded the deer." "He was wounded in the battle." This verb is often used in a military context or when discussing accidents involving physical harm.
  • To cause emotional or psychological pain: Similar to the figurative noun, the verb can describe the act of hurting someone's feelings, pride, or mental state. "Her harsh words wounded his pride." "The betrayal wounded her deeply." This usage highlights the impact of actions or words on another person's emotional well-being.

The distinction between these two homographs is crucial for correct interpretation and pronunciation. The context of the sentence is always the key to discerning which "wound" is intended. The provided sentence, with its description of a river's movement, unequivocally points to the past tense of "to wind" (/waʊnd/), indicating a twisting or meandering course.
