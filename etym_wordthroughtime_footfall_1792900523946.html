- Old English — the parts wait apart: fōt and feallan live for centuries as separate words before ever joining.
- Early 17th c. — the compound lands: footfall appears meaning the sound or stroke of a footstep, made for poets and dramatists.
- 17th–18th c. — the gothic ear: ghost stories, ballads, and novels adopt it as the sound of approach — lovers, murderers, and apparitions all arrive by footfall.
- 19th c. — nature writing: Thoreau, Hardy, and Hopkins listen to footfalls on leaves, snow, and church floors; silence becomes its partner.
- 20th c. — fantasy and commerce split it: Tolkien’s Elves abolish it, while retailers begin counting it — dread and data side by side.
- Today — hush or statistic: the novelist’s footfall still chills, the analyst’s footfall still charts, and both count the same arrivals.
