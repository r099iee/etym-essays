To hesitate is literally to 'stick fast.' The word reaches English from Latin haesitare, a frequentative verb formed on haerere, 'to cling, to adhere, to be fixed to a spot.' In the Latin mind the image was physical: a foot caught in mud, a tongue that 'sticks' in the mouth, a person who cannot move forward because something holds them in place. The frequentative form haesitare (with its repeated -tāre suffix) added the senses of stammering, faltering, and wavering — a thing that keeps getting stuck — and the noun haesitatio named the state of being stuck, in speech, in motion, or in the mind. When English took the word over in the early seventeenth century (hesitate, c. 1622; hesitation, c. 1570, via French and directly from Latin haesitationem), it imported the whole physical metaphor: the hesitant person is one whose tongue or feet are caught, who cannot proceed smoothly because doubt acts like a snare.

The deeper root of haerere is given in the traditional handbooks as Indo-European *ghais-, 'to adhere, to stick fast,' though it must be said honestly that Latin haerere has no secure attested cognates outside the Italic family; the Germanic and Baltic candidates that have been proposed (for example the Lithuanian gaišti, 'to delay, stay') are plausible but unproven, and cautious scholars leave the word as a Latin isolate. What is certain is the Latin career of the word, which produced a large English family, all built on haerere with different prefixes, each preserving the idea of sticking or clinging:

  • Adhere and adhesion (Latin adhaerere, 'to stick to') — the general verb of sticking to a person, rule, or surface; adherent names the follower who sticks.
  • Cohere, cohesion, coherent (Latin cohaerere, 'to stick together') — parts that hold together, and by extension an argument whose parts hold together.
  • Inhere and inherent (Latin inhaerere, 'to stick in') — qualities that stick in a thing and cannot be removed from it.

In the Romance languages the verb continued directly: Italian esitare and Spanish hesitar still mean 'to hesitate,' with esitazione and hesitación as the nouns. (A false friend must be excluded: heretic, through Latin haereticus, comes from Greek hairein, 'to take, choose,' and has nothing to do with sticking.)

The negative unhesitating was formed in English in the late eighteenth century — first recorded around 1767 — by prefixing un- to the present participle hesitating. The formation belongs to a productive English pattern: un- + participle adjective produces the privative ('not hesitating'), and the word moved quickly from a bare description of manner (an unhesitating step, an unhesitating speech) to a moral and psychological claim (unhesitating obedience, unhesitating belief). The adverb unhesitatingly follows from about 1800. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word was a favourite of moral and religious discourse — the unhesitating faith, the unhesitating duty — which is exactly the register of the biographical sentence on this card, where John Stuart Mill 'proclaims his unhesitating belief' in his wife's intellectual influence. The force of the word in such a sentence is the total absence of the 'stick': the belief is not weighed, not qualified, not delivered with a tremor; it is given whole and at once, the response of a mind that moves as smoothly as a hand that never catches, and in that instantaneity it quietly reverses the portrait of the sealed, dry father that precedes it.
