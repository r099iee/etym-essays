- Late Middle English (15th c.) — purport enters English from Anglo-French as a verb of texts: to have as its meaning, to signify; a neutral term of legal and scholarly exposition.
- 16th–17th c. — the noun purport (“tenor, gist”) flourishes in statute and sermon, and the verb extends from documents to persons, “to profess or claim”.
- 18th c. — legal prose fixes “the purport of an instrument”; the word is dignified, technical and seldom sceptical.
- 19th c. — the participial adjective purported appears in newspapers by the 1830s as a compact way to report a claim without endorsing it; the modern sceptical tinge sets in.
- 20th c. — purported becomes standard journalistic and intelligence vocabulary; “purported to be” is the default hedge for contested identities, documents and sightings.
- 21st c. — in legal drafting “purported” marks an act that claims validity but may be void; online it works as a fact-checking flag, sometimes overused to the point of insinuation.
