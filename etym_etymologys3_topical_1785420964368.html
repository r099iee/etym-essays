The adjective TOPICAL descends from Greek topos (τόπος), "place, position, spot" — a noun whose ultimate etymology remains obscure. Modern scholarship classes topos as a word of uncertain origin, very likely drawn from a Pre-Greek substrate of the Aegean world rather than from a recoverable Proto-Indo-European root, and for this reason no PIE form can be responsibly reconstructed: the honest chain begins at the attested Greek noun itself, which appears in prose from the fifth century BCE onward (Herodotus, Thucydides, Plato) with the concrete sense of a place or spot.

From topos the Greeks built a small and productive family. The adjective topikos (τοπικός) meant "pertaining to a place, local," and compounds such as topographia (τοπογραφία), "the description of places," entered English as topography. It was in Greek rhetoric, however, that the word took its decisive turn. In his treatise conventionally called the Topics (Τὰ Τοπικά, c. 350 BCE), Aristotle treated a "place" — topos — as a standard seat or category where a speaker could find arguments on any subject, like a well-stocked shelf arranged for retrieval. A topos was a "commonplace": a mental location for a kind of argument. This is the hinge of the whole later history. The physical "place" became a "place to look for arguments," and from that image the adjective topikos began to mean "pertaining to a topic or theme of discourse" alongside its literal "local" sense. A "topic" in English still means both a subject of discussion and, archaically, a commonplace.

The two senses travelled together into Latin as topica and topicalis, through Late Latin (topicalis "local") and into Middle French as topique, "local; relevant to a topic." English received both meanings, but at different moments and through different doors. Around the 1580s-1590s the word entered English in its locative and rhetorical senses: a "topical" treatment was one proceeding by topics or commonplaces, and a "topical" remedy was one applied to a particular spot on the body — the medical sense that survives intact in "topical ointment" and "topical anaesthesia," where the ancient topos is still visible as "spot on the skin."

The newsworthy sense is a modern growth. As the noun "topic" settled into meaning "any subject of discussion or interest," the adjective attached itself to the day's live subjects: by the early nineteenth century "topical" meant "of or relating to matters of current or local interest," and by the 1820s-1840s it was the natural word for satire and journalism that fed on the issues of the hour — "topical allusion," "topical jokes," a "topically relevant" article. Empson's usage, regretting the "topical interest of Freud," belongs exactly to this modern tradition, and it carries the faintly dismissive edge the word acquired: topical interest is fashionable, zeitgeisty, ephemeral — a matter of the moment rather than of durable value.

The phonology of the word is a steady wearing-down: Greek topos with initial stress became topikos, the Latin topicalis and French topique dropped the final Greek syllables, and English topical settled on a plain initial-stressed trisyllable, /ˈtɒpɪkəl/. Meanwhile the semantic split produced two well-marked registers: the technical medical use (applied to a spot) and the journalistic use (of the current moment), the second now dominant. It is a rare case of one adjective doing the work of both "local" and "contemporary-news" — a living monument to Aristotle's old metaphor of the mind as a place where arguments are stored.

Summary of the eras:
  • Attested Greek (5th c. BCE): topos "place, spot," of uncertain, likely substrate origin; no PIE root.
  • Greek derivatives (5th-4th c. BCE): topikos "local"; Aristotle's Topika (c. 350 BCE) turns the "place" into a "seat of argument" — the rhetorical sense.
  • Latin and French (Late Latin - 16th c.): topicalis "local"; Middle French topique.
  • English locative/medical sense (c. 1590s): "topical application," "topical ointment."
  • English news-value sense (c. 1820s): "topical" = of current interest; journalism and satire adopt it.
  • 20th-21st century: the news sense dominates; the medical and rhetorical senses survive as specialist terms.
