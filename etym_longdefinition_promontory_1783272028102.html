promontory (noun; plural promontories) — a high point of land or rock that projects outward into a body of water, especially the sea; a headland or steep cliff-like point of land extending into the water. In Shakespeare's Hamlet (Act II, scene ii), the word supplies the card's famous image: the earth, "this goodly frame," seems to the prince "a sterile promontory" — a barren, jutting shelf of rock in a cosmic void. CORE SENSES
  • Geographical sense. The primary sense: a high ridge or point of land projecting into a sea, lake, or river, often with steep cliffs on the water side; a headland. Promontories are landmarks for navigation, sites for lighthouses and fortresses, and dramatic features of coastal geography (e.g. Gibraltar, Cape Saint Vincent).
  • Geological and physical sense. In geomorphology, any projecting mass of rock or land, whether coastal or not; a spur or ridge that juts out from a larger landmass.
SECONDARY AND TECHNICAL MEANINGS
  • Anatomical sense. In anatomy and otolaryngology, a promontory is a prominent bony projection inside the body: most notably the "promontory of the tympanic cavity," the rounded eminence on the inner (medial) wall of the middle ear produced by the basal turn of the cochlea, and the "sacral promontory," the projecting upper border of the sacrum at the base of the pelvis. The term was transferred from coastal geography to the body by anatomists because a promontory is simply a high, jutting bony ridge — the same "forward-thrusting" shape as a headland.
  • Architectural and structural sense. By extension, any projecting part of a structure or formation that juts outward, as a balcony or outcrop; rare in everyday use but regular in technical description.
  • Figurative and literary sense. Following Shakespeare, promontory can denote a barren, isolated, or desolate height — "a sterile promontory" — or any lonely vantage point from which one looks out over a void; the word's elevation and bareness carry the metaphor.
  • Historical US railroad sense (capitalized). Promontory Summit (Promontory Point) in Utah is the historic site where the first transcontinental railroad of the United States was joined on 10 May 1869, the famous "golden spike" ceremony; the name itself is a geographical promontory sense.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE The word comes from Latin prōmunturium "headland," a derivative of prōminēre "to jut out, project forward" (prō- "forward, forth" + minēre "to project, overhang," from the PIE root *men- "to project, stand out, tower"). The original spelling prōmunturium was later remodelled to prōmontōrium under the influence of mōns, montis "mountain" — a folk etymology that made the headland a "forward mountain," reinforced by the fact that mōns itself descends from the same root *men- (via the o-grade *mon-). The anatomical sense is a later, learned extension in which the "jutting forth" of a headland is reapplied to a jutting bony ridge inside the body — a clean example of scientific metaphor built on the root's core meaning of projection.
