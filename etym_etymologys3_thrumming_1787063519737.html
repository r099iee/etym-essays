The sound word and its imitative birth. The verb to thrum — and hence thrumming — belongs to a small class of English words whose origin is deliberately echoic: it imitates the sound it names. The earliest recorded uses, in the 1590s, describe the soft, continuous humming produced when fingers run across the strings of a plucked instrument such as a lute, cittern, or guitar. The word was coined into a linguistic niche: English already had strum, hum, and drone, but thrum captured the specific quality of a vibrating string or vibrating surface whose pulse is felt through the fingers. Because it is imitative, it has no traceable Indo-European ancestor; it is one of the few English sound-words that resist reconstruction all the way to Proto-Indo-European.

Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century attestations. Shakespeare and his contemporaries use the verb in musical and comic contexts, often for amateur, lazy, or clumsy playing. The participle form thrumming quickly becomes the standard way of naming the ongoing sound: a thrumming lute, a thrumming string. By the 1600s the sense broadens from instruments to any low continuous vibration — the thrumming of a drum, the thrumming of the wind, later the thrumming of a bee's wings or an insect's flight. The word entered ordinary descriptive vocabulary as the sound of low, rhythmic, resonant motion.

The separate thread-sense strand. Meanwhile an entirely different word, thrum, had existed since the thirteenth century in the language of weaving: the thrums were the loose ends of warp threads left at the edge of the finished web — waste, fringe, or tufts. From this noun English built a verb to thrum meaning "to cover with thrums, to trim with a fringe of loose threads," and an adjectival past participle thrummed. Nautical English adopted thrums as a practical material: loose rope-yarn worked into canvas (thrummed matting) to deaden leaks, or twisted into mops and fenders. The two words were never etymologically the same — one imitative, one Germanic with uncertain ancestry — but their identical form produced centuries of folk-conflation, and dictionaries still note the difficulty of separating "the sound of plucked strings" from "the loose threads a weaver trims."

Industrial and modern expansion. The industrial age gave thrumming its most common modern habitat: the machine. A thrumming motor, generator, or aircraft is one whose deep vibration is felt through its frame. The word migrated from the musician's fingers to the machine's body, and from there into figurative use — a city thrumming with life, a crowd thrumming with tension, a project thrumming with activity — where the vibration stands for intense, barely contained energy.

Literary afterlife and the dragon's breath. In the twentieth century, thrumming became a favorite of descriptive and fantasy prose precisely because of its physicality. Tolkien's use — a dragon's thrumming breath at the threshold of hearing — is a model of the word's power: it names a sound that is also a tremor, a vibration that is also a presence. Modern writing uses the word for bass-heavy music, insect swarms, machinery, and any atmosphere of latent power, and it retains a distinctly literary, slightly archaic flavor that makes it feel weightier than the everyday word "hum."

Summary. From an imitative coinage for plucked strings in the 1590s, thrumming broadened to cover all low continuous vibration, absorbed centuries of folk-association with the unrelated weaving word for loose threads, and settled in modern English as the standard descriptor of deep, felt, rhythmic sound — from engines to basslines to the breath of sleeping dragons.
