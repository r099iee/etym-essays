Single linear derivation chain for AMENDS (Romance/Latin line):
  • PIE *mend- "physical flaw, defect, blemish" (c. 4000 BCE) — reconstructed from Latin menda and Sanskrit mindā "a bodily defect"; the root itself is small and its deeper shape is debated.
  • Latin menda (or mendum), "a fault, blemish, defect" — specifically a defect in a person, and by extension a textual error in a manuscript (c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE, attested in Plautus onward).
  • Latin emendare, "to free from fault, correct," a compound of the prefix ex-/e- "out of, away" + menda (1st c. BCE; Cicero uses it of correcting texts and reforming morals).
  • Vulgar Latin *amendare, the spoken continuation of emendare with the prefix reshaped to ad-/a- "toward" (c. 4th–7th c.).
  • Old French amender, "to correct, improve, repair, reform" (c. 9th–12th c.).
  • Old French amende, "a fine, penalty, reparation," the nominalised feminine of amender (c. 12th c., in legal and feudal usage).
  • Anglo-Norman / Old French plural amendes, "reparations, damages, court-imposed restitution" — borrowed into English.
  • Middle English amendes, "reparation, compensation," attested c. 1300 in legal and moral contexts.
  • Modern English amends (16th c. onward; today confined almost entirely to the idiom "make amends").
NUANCE NOTES: (1) The PIE root *mend- is small and the traditional reconstruction rests on Latin menda + Sanskrit mindā; some scholars treat menda as of uncertain or non-PIE origin, so this deepest step is flagged as debated. (2) The noun and the verb diverged early in French: the verb amender (to correct) and the noun amende (a fine) were already distinct in Old French; English kept both, giving modern amend (verb) and amends (noun). (3) The native clipped verb mend (Middle English menden, from amenden with loss of the initial vowel) is a sibling, not an ancestor, of the noun amends.
