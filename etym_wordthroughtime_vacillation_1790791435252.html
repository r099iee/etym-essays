- 1st c. BCE - *Classical Latin vacillātiō*: Denoted the physical swaying, staggering, or unsteadiness of a person or structure on the verge of falling.
- 1570s - *Early Modern English*: Borrowed from Latin via Middle French *vacillation*, initially used for physical swaying, wobbling, or staggering.
- 1600s–1700s - *Enlightenment Psychology & Rhetoric*: Transitioned into the realm of the mind to describe intellectual irresolution, moral wavering, and the inability to settle upon a firm conviction.
- 1800s - *19th-Century Politics & Literature*: Widely used in political commentary and Victorian literature (e.g., Matthew Arnold, George Eliot) to characterize weak-willed governance and tragic hesitation.
- Late 20th–21st c. - *Critical Theory & Postmodernism*: Embraced in literary semiotics (such as Roland Barthes's *The Pleasure of the Text*) as an affirmative, fluid state of interpretive openness and anti-dogmatism.
