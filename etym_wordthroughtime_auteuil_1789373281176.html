- 11th to 12th Century - First attested in medieval Latin monastic charters as *Altogilum* (and variants *Autolium*), a tranquil rural village on the western hills overlooking the Seine, dominated by agricultural vineyards and wine presses managed by the Abbey of Sainte-Geneviève.
- 17th to 18th Century - Emerges as the preeminent rural literary sanctuary for Parisian luminaries seeking escape from royal court intrigue and Parisian pollution; home to Molière's country house and the celebrated "Société d'Auteuil" (Boileau, Racine, La Fontaine, Chapelle), followed in the Enlightenment by Madame Helvétius's salon, where philosophers like Condorcet, Turgot, and Benjamin Franklin debated political reform.
- 1860 - Formally annexed into the municipality of Paris under Baron Haussmann's great administrative reorganization, becoming one of the four quarters comprising the prestigious new 16th arrondissement while retaining its lush private villas and enclosed estates.
- Late 19th Century (Belle Époque) - Achieves international fame as a laboratory of cutting-edge residential architecture, particularly Hector Guimard's Art Nouveau masterpieces (Castel Béranger); permanently enshrined in literary history when Marcel Proust was born at 96 Rue La Fontaine in 1871.
- 20th Century to Present - Maintains its status as one of Paris's most coveted and expensive residential enclaves, synonymous with discrete diplomatic residences, elite schools, leafy gated streets, and premier international sporting venues like the Roland Garros tennis complex and the Hippodrome d'Auteuil.
