• Straw: Similar because both are dry plant material ubiquitous on farms and often stored in the same barns or used in the same livestock sheds; differs because straw is the spent stalk of cereal crops after grain harvest — nutrient-poor, used for bedding, thatch, and mulch — whereas hay is intentionally cut forage (grass or legumes) dried precisely for its feeding value. The boundary is nutritional purpose versus residual by-product.
  • Fodder: Similar because fodder is the cover term for plant food given to animals, so every load of hay is fodder; differs because fodder includes grains, roots, silage, oilseed cake, and mixed rations, while hay is one specific preparation method (mow + dry + store). In technical writing fodder is the hypernym and hay a hyponym with fixed processing semantics.
  • Silage: Similar as conserved forage for winter feeding and the direct technological alternative to hay-making; differs because silage is ensiled moist under anaerobic conditions, fermenting rather than sun-drying, with different texture, smell, storage (silo or clamp versus loft or bale), and weather-risk profile. Farmers choose between them by climate and machinery, not by synonymy.
  • Haylage: Similar as a stored forage product made from the same grass and legume crops and part of the hay/silage family; differs because haylage is wilted grass sealed and fermented at a higher moisture content than hay but lower than silage — a hybrid between the two, distinct from the sun-dried hay of the traditional process.
  • Forage: Similar as plant material animals eat, whether grazed or conserved; differs because forage can mean live pasture browse or the general feed resource, while hay always implies the cut, cured, stored product. One forages a field; one makes hay from it. The boundary is process and state (living or pastured versus dried commodity).
  • Herbage: Similar as a formal or literary word for green plant cover used by animals; differs because herbage stresses the growing sward or botanical mass, not the dried artifact. Hay is herbage after human intervention (cutting and curing). Register also differs: herbage is scientific or elevated; hay is everyday agrarian English.
  • Chaff: Similar as dry plant material associated with grain farming and sometimes fed to animals as a roughage; differs because chaff is the husks and debris separated from grain during threshing — a by-product, not a cultivated fodder crop — and has none of hay's status as a deliberately cured feed.
