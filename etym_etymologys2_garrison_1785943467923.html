Derivation chain for GARRISON: 1. Proto-Indo-European *wer- (to cover, protect, ward off, watch) — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE. 2. Proto-Germanic *warjaną (to defend, protect, ward off) and *warōną (to watch, guard) — reconstructed, c. 1st millennium BCE. 3. Frankish *warjan (to defend, protect) — 5th–8th c. CE; loaned into the Gallo-Romance speech of northern Gaul. 4. Old French garir / warir (to defend, protect, preserve; later also to heal, cure, make sound) — 9th–12th c. CE. 5. Old French garison / guerison (protection, defense, safety; provisions and means of subsistence) — 11th–13th c. CE. 6. Anglo-Norman garison (defense, protection, provision; troops stationed for defense) — 11th–14th c. CE. 7. Middle English garisoun / garison (a body of troops stationed in a fort; also defense and provisions) — 14th c. CE. 8. English GARRISON (the stationed defending troops; by metonymy the fortified post itself; verb: to supply a place with troops) — modern senses from the 15th–16th c. CE. Note on debate: Old French garir shows the characteristic w-/g- alternation of Frankish loanwords, in which Frankish /w/ became French /g/ before back vowels (compare warder/guarder, warranty/guaranty); both garir and warir forms are attested. The vowels in garison vs. guerison reflect dialectal variation in northern France. The semantic narrowing from "protection, safety, provisions" to "the body of defending troops" is a metonymy well attested across the medieval period; Modern French guérison means "recovery, healing," preserving the older "to make safe/sound" sense in a medical direction, while English kept the military meaning.
