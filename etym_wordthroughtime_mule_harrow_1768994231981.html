- mule
- Plain animal-name (14th–15th c.) - Arrives with the hybrid beast itself from French/Latin; a workaday word for the animal of transport and hauling, carrying none of the horse's status.
- Proverb & stigma (16th–18th c.) - Grows metaphorical: 'stubborn as a mule,' 'mule-headed' — the animal's caution misread as obstinacy; the mule becomes the emblem of thankless, unglamorous labor.
- Frontier engine (19th c.) - The mule becomes the draft-engine of American agriculture — mule train, pack mule, mule skinner — the work-animal of the sharecropper and frontier farmer, saturated with the socioeconomic history of the poor.
- Displacement & memory (20th c.–present) - The tractor displaces the animal; the word survives as technical memory (the spinning mule), as the stubborn figure of proverbs, and as a loaded literary image of endurance bought with thankless toil.
- harrow
- Borrowed implement (13th–14th c.) - Adopted into Middle English along with the toothed soil-breaker itself; a plain farming word for the frame of iron teeth that crushes clods.
- Homonymic collision (Middle English) - The tool-word merges with Old English hergian, 'to harry, ravage': to harrow gains the figurative sense 'to wound, lacerate, torment,' fixed by the Harrowing of Hell.
- Two lives at once (16th c.–present) - The figurative sense hardens into ordinary language — a harrowing experience tears at the heart exactly as the tines tear at the earth — while the literal tool retreats to rural, technical vocabulary.
