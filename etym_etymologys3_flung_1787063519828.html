THE EVOLUTION OF FLUNG

The story of flung is the story of how a violent, obscure Germanic verb of beating became one of English's most vivid words for throwing. It is a word whose modern identity is inseparable from its energetic physicality, yet that physicality began, not with the arm and an object in flight, but with a whip and a stroke.

1. The Germanic Origins: Beating and Whipping

The earliest recorded relative of fling appears in Old English as flingan, meaning "to whip" or "to beat." The evidence is sparse — it survives chiefly in the compound ofeflangan ("to chastise") — but it connects the word to a family of Germanic verbs associated with striking and sudden motion. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic form is *flinganą, "to beat, whip." Notably, the word is confined to the North and West Germanic branches; it has no secure cognates in other Indo-European languages, and no PIE root can be reconstructed with confidence. This isolation suggests either a dialectal formation within Germanic or a loan from a Scandinavian source, a question that remains open in the scholarship.

2. The Middle English Shift: From Beating to Throwing

In Middle English, the verb flingen underwent a decisive semantic shift. The notion of striking a whip or beating something was reconceptualised as a motion of the whole body — to move or rush violently — and then as the action of hurling an object. By the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, flingen meant "to throw, hurl, cast, or dart." This is a classic example of metonymy: the vigorous arm-motion of striking was transferred to the vigorous arm-motion of throwing. Chaucer and other Middle English authors use the verb for casting people and things about with force, and the modern sense of energetic abandonment begins to take root.

3. The Irregular Strong Verb and the Stabilising of FLUNG

English inherited two classes of verbs: the so-called "strong" verbs, which form their past tenses by changing the root vowel (ablaut), and "weak" verbs, which add a suffix like -ed. Fling belongs to the former group, and its paradigm — fling / flung / flung — reflects the historical vowel gradation pattern. By the Early Modern English period, the participle flung had become firmly standardised, and Shakespeare could write of "flung" things and "flung" people with the full modern meaning. The irregularity of the form, far from being a defect, became a mark of its native, organic ancestry.

4. Figurative Extension in the 19th and 20th Centuries

The modern semantic expansion of the word belongs largely to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. From the concrete sense of throwing, English developed reflexive and metaphorical constructions:

  • To fling oneself — to throw one's body with abandon, as into a chair or onto a bed.
  • To fling oneself into (work, a cause) — to commit wholly and energetically, converting physical force into emotional dedication.
  • To fling caution / restraint / something to the wind — to abandon prudence in a sudden, decisive act.
  • The noun fling — first "a period of unrestrained activity," then specifically "a brief romantic or sexual affair" ("a summer fling"), which became prominent in the twentieth century and gave the word a new, colloquial life.

Each of these extensions preserves the core semantic component of sudden, forceful, unguarded motion. The bridge from hurling an object to plunging into a commitment is built on shared kinetic imagery: both involve a decisive, whole-hearted release of energy.

5. FLUNG in Literary and Popular Culture

Because of its vigour, flung is a favourite of narrative and dramatic prose. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the dwarves "flung their pale torches to the ground" — a gesture that expresses despair, exhaustion, and the abandonment of hope in a single energetic image. The word appears throughout adventure, romance, and gothic fiction precisely because it compresses physical force and emotional state into one verb. In journalism and everyday speech, flung remains the emphatic alternative to threw: "he flung open the door," "she flung herself into the campaign," "words were flung across the courtroom."

6. Conclusion

The history of flung demonstrates how a word can travel from the narrow, violent register of whipping to the broad, expressive register of emotional and physical abandonment, all while retaining a core of sudden force. Its Germanic isolation, its irregular inflection, and its rich figurative life together make flung a vivid example of how English preserves its most energetic native vocabulary at the very heart of everyday expression.
