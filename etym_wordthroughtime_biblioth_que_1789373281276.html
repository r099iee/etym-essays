- 14th–15th Century (Middle French Borrowing) - Adopted into Middle French via scholarly and ecclesiastical Latin from Greek bibliothēkē; used primarily in royal charters and monastic inventories to designate locked vaults of illuminated manuscripts or collections of Holy Scripture.
- 16th–17th Century (Renaissance and the Grand Siècle) - Expands under the flourishing of humanist print culture to encompass grand royal collections (such as the Bibliothèque du Roi under Louis XIV) as well as the domestic furniture (bookcases and cabinets) built to house private aristocratic collections.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment and the Revolution) - Codified as an essential civic and egalitarian institution dedicated to universal education and public enlightenment; the French Revolution seizes aristocratic and clerical libraries, creating the foundation for the modern Bibliothèque nationale.
- 19th–Mid-20th Century (Publishing Collections and Canonical Prestige) - Evolves into a standard designation for curated publishing series and deluxe editions; in 1931 Jacques Schiffrin launches the Bibliothèque de la Pléiade (acquired by Gallimard in 1936), cementing "bibliothèque" as the gold standard of literary canonization.
- Late 20th–21st Century (Digital Architecture and Software Libraries) - Broadens dramatically into computer science to designate software libraries (bibliothèques logicielles) and online digital archives (such as Gallica), bridging ancient book depositories with algorithmic data structures.
