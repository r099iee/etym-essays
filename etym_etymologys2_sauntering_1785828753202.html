CHAIN: FROM OBSCURE MIDDLE ENGLISH TO SAUNTERING (4 stages; origin uncertain)

  1. Origin UNKNOWN — no Proto-Indo-European root and no secure earlier ancestor is attested. The word appears abruptly in 15th-century English as santren / sawtren 'to muse, to daydream, to be in a reverie, to act idly' (first recorded c. 1430–1460). Older dictionaries propose an Anglo-French or Middle French source, but no convincing etymon has been established; the origin is treated as obscure by the OED.
  2. Early Modern English saunter 'to wander, to stroll about idly, to loiter' (16th century; the meaning shifts from mental idleness — musing, dreaming — to physical idleness — wandering, loitering).
  3. English saunter 'to walk in a slow, relaxed, leisurely manner, with a careless or confident air' (17th–18th centuries; the modern polished sense, shed of the old contemptuous overtone of mere loitering).
  4. English SAUNTERING — the present participle of saunter, 'walking slowly and leisurely' (participle and adjective use recorded from the 17th century onward).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The origin is genuinely UNKNOWN: the OED marks saunter as of obscure origin. The traditional folk etymology deriving it from Sainte-Terre 'Holy Land' (a crusader going a-sauntering to the Holy Land, hence an aimless wanderer) is attractive and long-lived — Samuel Johnson recorded it — but it is not accepted by modern scholarship; a derivation from Anglo-French has also been proposed but never proven.
  • The earliest attested sense is mental, not physical: 'to muse, to be in a reverie'. The modern walking sense is a 16th-century development, and the modern positive, confident 'unhurried stroll' reading is an 18th-century refinement.
  • No Indo-European reconstruction is possible: the chain therefore begins with the attested Middle English form and states the unknown origin explicitly.
