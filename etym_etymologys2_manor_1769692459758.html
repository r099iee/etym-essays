PIE *men- "to remain, stay, wait, abide" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; source of Greek menein "to remain"; note that a formally identical PIE *men- "to think" exists and must not be conflated with the "stay" root) → Latin manēre "to remain, stay, dwell, tarry, abide" (attested 1st c. BCE; source of mansion, permanent, remain — via re- "back" + manēre; the perfect stem mans- gives mansus "dwelling") → Latin mansio, genitive mansionis "a staying, a dwelling; a stage of a journey; a resting place" (attested 1st c. BCE/CE; a Roman posting-station or dwelling; source of English mansion) → Old French manoir (verb) "to dwell, stay, remain; to sojourn" and manoir (noun) "a dwelling, abode, residence" (attested 11th–12th c. CE; the noun is the infinitive used substantively) → Anglo-French maner / manour "dwelling, residence; the principal house and estate of a feudal lord" (attested 12th–13th c. CE) → Middle English maner / manor "a landed estate held by a lord; the principal dwelling" (attested c. 1300; reinforced by Medieval Latin manerium, the Domesday-era legal term) → English manor "a large country house with lands; a feudal estate" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The chain is securely attested. Points worth noting: (1) PIE *men- "to stay, remain" is a standard reconstruction (reflected in Greek ménō "I remain," Armenian mnam "to remain," Latin manēre), but it is formally homonymous with PIE *men- "to think" (source of Latin mens "mind"); the two are separate roots, and the "stay" one alone is relevant here. (2) The intermediate between Latin and Old French is partly reconstructed (Vulgar Latin carried the "to dwell" sense into Gaulish Romance), but the Old French noun manoir "dwelling" is directly attested. (3) Medieval Latin manerium "manor" (attested by the 11th–12th centuries) was a parallel legal development from the same verb and reinforced the Anglo-French/English forms; the Domesday Book (1086) records maneria throughout England.
