cynically:
- 16th c. - 'cynical' enters English as a school-of-philosophy word: the Cynics, the 'dog-like' philosophers of Greece who flouted convention, so the adjective means austere, contemptuous of luxury, and snappish
- 17th-18th c. - the word leaves the schoolroom and becomes a character trait: a cynic is one who sneers at honour and believes every act is self-serving; 'cynical' carries a moral charge of bitterness and contempt
- 19th c. - the adverb 'cynically' settles into prose, the natural counterpart of the Victorian debates over faith, sentiment, and progress - cynicism is the shadow of disappointed idealism, the voice that distrusts every noble claim
- Contemporary - the everyday adverb of disillusion: in journalism and politics 'cynically' judges an action as staged for appearance or advantage, and the word has hardened from a strange philosophy into the standard, weary suspicion of motive
