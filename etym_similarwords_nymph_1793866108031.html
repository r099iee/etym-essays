• Naiad: Similar because both represent benevolent, beautiful female nature spirits in classical mythology; differs because a naiad is strictly restricted to freshwater aquatic realms (springs, brooks, fountains, rivers, and subterranean aquifers), whereas nymph is the broader parent category encompassing terrestrial, arboreal, marine, and mountain realms.
  • Dryad: Similar because both designate mythological female spirits personifying the natural environment; differs because a dryad is exclusively an inhabitant and protector of forests, woodlands, and trees (often bonded directly to an individual oak or grove), whereas nymph includes spirits of water, mountains, and seas.
  • Hamadryad: Similar because both are female nature deities intimately connected with living ecosystems; differs because a hamadryad's very life and existence are strictly coextensive with a specific individual tree, perishing the instant the tree dies or is felled, whereas most nymphs are longer-lived and can move freely throughout their wider ecological domain.
  • Nereid: Similar because both are semi-divine female spirits personifying aquatic nature; differs because a nereid is specifically one of the fifty daughters of the marine god Nereus, dwelling exclusively in the saltwater Mediterranean Sea and attending Poseidon, whereas nymphs dwell predominantly in inland terrestrial habitats such as fresh springs and woodlands.
  • Oceanid: Similar because both are mythological female water spirits; differs because an oceanid is specifically one of the three thousand daughters of the primordial river-titan Oceanus, personifying cosmic ocean currents and deep saltwater expanses rather than localized freshwater springs and groves.
  • Oread: Similar because both are mythological maidens embodying the spirit of the natural landscape; differs because an oread is specifically tied to mountains, crags, rocky precipices, and alpine grottos (such as Echo), representing wild upland geography.
  • Sprite: Similar because both refer to supernatural, elusive beings dwelling in nature; differs because a sprite (from Latin spiritus via Old French) is a broader folkloric creature of European fairy lore, often diminutive, mischievous, or incorporeal, lacking the specific Greco-Roman divine genealogy and physical human embodiment of a classical nymph.
  • Sylph: Similar because both denote elemental spirits personifying the beauty of the natural world; differs because a sylph (coined by the Renaissance alchemist Paracelsus) specifically personifies the element of air and the sky, whereas classical nymphs belong primarily to earth, water, and plant life.
  • Fairy: Similar because both denote supernatural female beings associated with nature and magic in European folklore; differs because fairy is a broad Celtic, French, and Germanic folkloric concept encompassing both benevolent and malevolent entities of diverse forms, ages, and courts, whereas a nymph is strictly classical Greco-Roman, perpetually youthful, tied to concrete physical topography, and unwinged.
  • Instar / Larva: Similar because in entomological biology both denote immature pre-adult developmental stages of insects; differs because a nymph applies exclusively to hemimetabolous insects (incomplete metamorphosis, where the young emerges resembling a miniature wingless adult), whereas a larva refers to holometabolous insects (complete metamorphosis, such as grubs, maggots, and caterpillars) that undergo a radically distinct pupal stage.
  • Pupa / Chrysalis: Similar because both designate intermediate insect developmental stages between hatching and adulthood; differs because a pupa is a quiescent, non-feeding, typically enclosed resting stage undergoing dramatic tissue reorganization inside a cocoon, whereas an insect nymph is an active, feeding, mobile organism undergoing progressive external molts.
