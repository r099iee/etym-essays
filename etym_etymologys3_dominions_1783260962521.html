The word dominions is a Roman landlord's word, and its deepest root is the Proto-Indo-European stem *dṓm / *dem-, “house; to build” — one of the family's most secure reconstructions, attested in every corner of the Indo-European world. In Indo-Iranian it gave Sanskrit dámaḥ and Avestan dam-, both “house”; in Hellenic it gave Greek dómos, the dwelling and its rooms, with dôma “roof”; in Armenian it survives as tun, “house”; and in Germanic it took the zero-grade and the sense of building, producing Old English timber (building material) and German Zimmer, “room.” The single root thus means both the physical house and the act of raising one, and it carried into each branch a fundamental social idea: the house as the smallest political unit, the family under one roof. It is from this homely beginning that a word for vast imperial territories would grow, and the growth is a perfect illustration of how private power swells into public dominion.

The Roman transformation. In Latin, domus meant “house, household,” and from it Latin formed dominus, “master of the house” — not merely the man who owned the building but the lord of the people within it, the paterfamilias whose power over his household was, in early Roman law, nearly absolute. From dominus came the abstract noun dominium, which in Roman jurisprudence named the master's right of ownership and control — the fullest legal power over a thing or a person that the law recognised. It is a striking fact that the private law of the house supplied the very model for sovereignty: when Roman lawyers and emperors wanted to describe authority over the provinces, they reached for the language of the dominus. The feminine domina, meanwhile, gave English dame, damsel, and madam, and, through Italian madonna (“my lady”), a whole Romance family of respect; the verb dominārī, “to be lord over, to rule,” gave English dominate, dominant, and domineer. The semantic bridge from household to empire could not be plainer: the master's power over his house became the emperor's power over the world.

Into French and English. Latin dominium passed into Old French and Anglo-Norman as dominion, “rule, sovereignty,” and the word was borrowed into Middle English as dominioun in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. From the start it carried two linked senses: the abstract “lordship, mastery, rule” and the concrete “territory under a ruler.” The territorial sense strengthened with the age of empire: by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries “the dominions of a king” was the standard phrase for the aggregate of his realms, and the plural dominions naturally stressed the composite, additive character of early modern monarchy — many kingdoms, duchies, and colonies under one crown. It is exactly this sense that Macaulay uses of Charles V, whose dominions stretched across Spain, the Netherlands, Germany, Italy, and the Americas, to which Cortés and Pizarro added the conquered realms of Mexico and Peru. The word's master-of-the-house flavour gave the imperial usage its imperious edge: these were territories owned by right of lordship, not merely administered.

The constitutional Dominions. In the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word gained a precise constitutional sense: “the Dominions” (capital D) were the self-governing parts of the British Empire — Canada (1867), Australia (1901), New Zealand, South Africa (1910), Newfoundland until 1949 — communities with responsible government and their own parliaments within the empire, owing allegiance to the Crown but not ruled by Westminster. The status was formalized by the Balfour Declaration of 1926 and the Statute of Westminster of 1931, and the term fell out of use as these countries became fully independent. It is a quiet irony that Macaulay's word for the conquered realms of Charles V should have been reused by the British to describe colonies that were becoming, by free consent, self-governing nations — the house-word of the Roman paterfamilias softened into constitutional liberty.

The doublets. English also inherited two sister words from the same Latin root through French: demesne (from Old French demeine/demaine, “the lord's estate,” land retained in the lord's own hands) and domain (from French domaine, “a territory; a field of authority,” and now the everyday word for an internet address). Dominion, demesne, and domain are thus three English reflexes of one Roman house — a single PIE *dṓm branching into lordship, land, and cyberspace.

  • PIE: *dṓm / *dem- “house; to build” — Sanskrit dámaḥ, Greek dómos, Latin domus, Germanic timber.
  • Latin: domus “house” → dominus “master of the house” → dominium “lordship, right of ownership” (Roman law).
  • Old French: dominion “rule, sovereignty, territory” → Middle English dominioun (13th–14th c.).
  • Imperial sense: “the dominions of a king” (16th–17th c.); Macaulay's “dominions of Charles V.”
  • Constitutional sense: “the Dominions” — the self-governing British colonies (1860s–1930s).
  • Doublets: demesne and domain — three English reflexes of the one Roman house-word.
