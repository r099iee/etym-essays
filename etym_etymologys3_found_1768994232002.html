found — a biography of the path that became a meal. The journey begins in the Indo-European root *pent-, "to go, tread, walk," the root of the path itself. Its children form a small family of ways: Greek pátos "a trodden way, path," from the verb patéō "to tread," which gave English the combining element of words like peripatetic "walking about"; Latin pons, pontis "bridge" — literally a passage or way across water, whence pontoon and pontiff, the "bridge-builder" who is the Pope; and in Indo-Iranian, Vedic Sanskrit pánthāḥ "path, way," whose modern forms include Hindi panth and Odia pantha, and Avestan pantā "path." The image that unites the whole family is the way itself — a trodden track, a ford, a crossing — and "to find" in the deepest sense means to come upon the way, to discover the path by walking it. It is even possible, though debated, that English path (Old English pæþ) is itself a distant loan from an Iranian member of this same root, so that the word for "path" and the word for "finding" may share a single distant ancestor.

Finding by walking. In Germanic the root was rebuilt as a present-stem with a nasal infix — *finþaną "to find," literally "to come upon by going," to find by walking the path. This nasal infix is a classic Indo-European present-forming device: the stem was extended with a nasal (þ from *t under Grimm's Law and Verner's Law) to mark the ongoing action of searching, and the family of cognates is wide — Old English findan, Old Saxon fīthan, Old High German findan (German finden), Old Norse finna, Gothic finþan. English find is the direct line, a strong verb whose paradigm find–found–found is the hinge of this whole story: the past participle found, with its rounded vowel and -nd, hardened over centuries of use. The earliest attested English senses of find are "to come upon, to discover, to encounter" — finding a lost thing, finding one's way — and from "discovering" the sense slides naturally toward "obtaining, procuring, providing."

The participle becomes a noun. In Middle English the verb also develops the sense "to provide, supply, furnish," so that a master "finds" his servants in meat and lodging — the provision sense recorded already in medieval legal and manorial English. Out of this participle, "provided, supplied," the noun grows in the nineteenth century. In the labour markets of the last century a worker was hired "for wages and found" — the maintenance found for him, the board and lodging supplied as part of the bargain. What had been a participle meaning "come upon" became a noun meaning "keep, subsistence," and the word's whole history — treading, discovering, providing — is packed into that bare form. The passage uses it in exactly this historical sense, and the careful reader sees the pun: on the frontier, a man's food and bed are what he finds on the road. The anti-merging guardrail is worth repeating: found here is not the other found, "to establish," which comes from Latin fundāre and its root fundus "bottom, ground" (whence foundation, fundamental, profound). Two English homographs, two unrelated histories — the bare form on this card belongs to the path-finding root *pent-, and it means the keep of the hired man.
