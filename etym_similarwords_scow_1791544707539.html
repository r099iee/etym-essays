• Barge: Similar because both denote large, flat-bottomed cargo vessels primarily used on inland and coastal waterways; differs because barge is a broad category encompassing both powered and unpowered vessels, often with rounded bows, whereas a scow specifically features flat, square-ended rakes at both bow and stern.
  • Lighter: Similar because both are flat-bottomed harbor craft used to transport freight between shore docks and anchored ships; differs because lighter describes the commercial function (lightening a ship's load), whereas scow defines a specific hull geometry (flat bottom, square chines, and rectangular ends).
  • Punt: Similar because both are flat-bottomed craft with square ends designed for shallow water; differs because a punt is a small, light, recreational pleasure craft propelled manually by a single person using a long pole, whereas a scow is typically a large, heavy commercial vessel.
  • Flatboat: Similar because both refer to rectangular, flat-bottomed wooden watercraft used in North American river trade; differs because flatboats were historically temporary, single-use wooden rafts floated downstream on the Ohio and Mississippi rivers and broken up for lumber at their destination, whereas scows are permanent, reusable vessels.
  • Hulk: Similar because both can describe cumbersome, unpowered, or unsightly floating vessels; differs because a hulk is specifically the dismantled, stripped hull of an old ship that has been retired from active seafaring (often used as a prison, storage depot, or salvage vessel), rather than a purpose-built freight craft.
  • Dory: Similar because both are traditional North American wooden workboats; differs because a dory is a small, narrow, high-sided rowboat with a flat bottom and sharp, pointed bow used by offshore fishermen, whereas a scow is broad, blunt-ended, and flat-decked.
  • Tender: Similar because both can serve auxiliary harbor and ship-servicing duties; differs because a tender is a vessel used to service, supply, or ferry passengers to and from a larger ship, often fast and maneuverable, rather than a heavy industrial bulk carrier.
