The participle springing descends from one of the oldest motion-verbs in Germanic: Proto-Germanic *springaną, to leap up or burst forth, living on in Old English springan, which meant to leap, to shoot up, to burst out, and even to grow. The semantic core never wavered — sudden energetic emergence — and from it English grew a whole landscape: springs of water that leap from rock, the spring of day that leaps over the horizon, and by the thirteenth century the season itself, the springing-time when the year leaps back to life, clipped to plain spring.

Middle English multiplied the uses without thinning the force. Knights spring from ambush, rivers spring from hills, doubts spring in minds, and the metal coil — named spring for the leap it performs when released — gives the language springing locks, springing traps, and springing carriages. The industrial age only extended the coil's empire: sprung mattresses, sprung rhythms, and clockwork springs all testify to stored energy waiting for release. Alongside ran the trap-idiom: as snares spring shut, so leaks spring open in hulls and questions spring upon the unready.

Stages of the journey:

  • Germanic leap-verb: *springaną names the explosive jump — warriors, deer, and water bursting from earth — with the debated PIE shadow *(s)prengʰ- behind it.
  • Old English fullness: springan covers leaping, dawning, sprouting, and flowing — every sudden upward emergence the island life offered.
  • The season's promotion: the springing-time becomes spring, fixing the leap in the calendar and carrying optimism into the word's permanent colour.
  • Coils and traps: early modern technology borrows the leap for elastic metal, and springing becomes the adjective of stored-and-released force.
  • Modern vigour: from Gandalf springing to his feet to athletes springing from blocks, the participle remains readiness made audible — energy, alarm, and delight in one bound.

Phonetically the word still performs itself: the initial spr- cluster gathers and compresses, the short vowel tenses, and the final -ing releases the sound forward — a small coiled launch in the mouth. That is why the source sentence needs no other verb. Gandalf does not rise, stand, or get up; he springs, and seventy-odd years of assumed frailty snap like a released coil in a single participle.

Two offshoots deserve mention. The spring tide — the tide that springs or leaps highest — carries the verb into lunar astronomy, while Gerard Manley Hopkins's sprung rhythm borrows the coil's energy for poetry that leaps rather than flows. Idiom adds its own springs: the spring chicken, no longer young; spring fever, restlessness as days lengthen; to spring into action, the very phrase Gandalf performs without uttering. Each keeps the core physics intact — compression, then sudden release — proving that after a thousand years the leap still lands.
