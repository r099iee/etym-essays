- Middle English 14th century - 'clamour' from Old French 'clameur' (Latin 'clamor', from 'clamare' 'to cry out'); first meant a loud shouting or outcry, especially of a crowd, and could also carry a legal shade of a claim or complaint.
- 14th-16th century - develops a verb as well: 'to clamour' is to cry aloud, to demand noisily; noun and verb alike imply voices raised together, purposeful rather than random.
- 17th-18th century - broadens to any loud confused noise, and the phrase 'clamour for' hardens into its modern meaning of an urgent collective demand — for rights, for reform, for bread.
- 19th-20th century - increasingly refined: a clamour is not mere din but coordinated protest, many voices united; it appears in political and journalistic registers with an undertone of grievance and demand.
- Modern - slightly elevated or literary, used of noise that has a collective purpose (a clamour for justice) rather than random sound; the Wargs' howling 'clamour' in Tolkien keeps exactly this sense of a pack demanding something together.
