DWELLINGS (three-stage Germanic chain; the PIE root is secure): 1. PIE *dʰwel- "to wander, go astray; to be confused or deluded" (reconstructed, ca. 4000–3000 BCE; cognates include Sanskrit dhválati "he wanders, staggers", Gothic dwals "foolish", Old English dwola "error, heresy"). 2. Proto-Germanic *dwaljaną "to mislead, cause to go astray, delude" (causative of *dwelaną "to wander, tarry"; ca. 500 BCE–200 CE; Old Norse dvala "to delay, tarry", dvǫl "delay, stay"). 3. Old English dwellan "to mislead, deceive, hinder; to wander, tarry, remain" (attested from ca. 8th c. CE; the original OE sense was "to lead astray, delude", which survives as "to dwell on" = "to linger mentally over"). 4. Middle English dwellen "to remain, stay, abide, sojourn; to reside, live in a place" (ca. 1100–1500; the sense shifts from "tarry, delay" to "settle permanently"). 5. English dwelling (verbal noun in -ing from dwell) "a place of residence, abode" (from the 14th c.; the noun construction hardened from the act of dwelling to the building dwelt in). 6. Modern English dwellings (plural) — the target word dwellings. Note the semantic bridge: a word of errancy and delay ("to wander astray") came, through "to tarry, remain," to mean "to live in a fixed place," and the noun then froze that act into architecture. GLOOM (Germanic chain; the earliest stage is DEBATED — see note): 1. PIE *gʰel- / extended *gʰlō- "to shine, glow, gleam" (reconstructed; yields Germanic *glōaną "to glow" and, with an *-m- formative, a base *glōm- "dim light, twilight"). Cognates elsewhere: Greek khlōrós "green, pale yellow" is disputed here; the "shine" root is seen in Latin helvus "honey-yellow" and English gleam, gold. 2. Proto-Germanic *glōaną "to glow" and the *glōm- derivative "twilight, dim light" (ca. 500 BCE–200 CE). 3. Old English glōwan "to glow" (attested 8th c.) and Old English glōm "twilight, darkness" (attested in glosses ca. 9th c.; also the derivative glōmung "twilight, gloaming"). 4. Middle English glome / gloum "darkness, dimness, shadow" (attested from ca. 1300). 5. English gloom (noun) "darkness, dimness" (15th c.); the emotional sense "deep sadness, melancholy" develops in the 18th c.; the verb to gloom "to look sullen, to be dark" (15th c.) and adjective gloomy (16th c.) — the target word gloom. DEBATED STAGE (stated explicitly): the OED marks the origin of Modern English gloom as "of obscure origin." The most widely accepted derivation links it to Old English glōm "twilight" (from the *gʰel- "shine" family), but some scholars instead treat the noun as a back-formation from Middle English gloumen "to look sullen, frown" (15th c.), which may be of Scandinavian origin (cf. Swedish dialect glo "to stare, glare"); if so, the "darkness" sense would be a secondary transfer from the sullen look to a dark mood and a dark place. Both routes are plausible, and the two may have reinforced each other; the link to OE glōm, while probable, cannot be proven from the surviving record. FETOR (Latin chain; NO PIE STAGE CAN BE SECURED — stated explicitly): 1. The pre-Latin/PIE stage is UNKNOWN: Latin fētēre "to stink" has no certain cognates and no secure PIE reconstruction. It is an isolated, "substrate or opaque" verb of Latin; de Vaan lists it without a confident Indo-European etymology (a connection to *dʰw- "to blow" or to Greek *thýein "to smoke" has been suggested but is not accepted). 2. Latin fētēre "to stink, to emit a foul smell" (verb, attested from the 3rd c. BCE — Plautus uses fetet "it stinks"). 3. Latin fētor (later also spelled foetor) "a bad smell, a stench" (noun, attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE, Cicero, Pliny; the foetor spelling is a hypercorrect Latin variant influenced by the adjective fētidus). 4. English fetor "foul smell, stench" (borrowed directly from Latin, first attested 15th c.; revived in technical/medical use in the 19th–20th c.; the related adjective fetid, from Latin fētidus "stinking", was borrowed in the 1590s) — the target word fetor.
