1. From oblivion to illness: Greece. Behind English lethargy stands the Greek noun lēthē, "forgetfulness, oblivion". In Greek myth, Lethe was the river of forgetfulness in Hades whose water erased the memories of the dead: dying released both soul and trouble into that common forgetting. From the same stem comes the verb lanthanō, "to escape notice, to forget", and its celebrated force in Homer, where gods conceal truths from mortal minds. The step into medicine is characteristically Greek: to lēthargos, "forgetful", the Hippocratic physicians added the sense of a man too sluggish even to remember himself. Lēthargia entered the Hippocratic corpus as a named condition — a heavy, feverless drowsiness that dulled the senses and stole the patient's awareness, poised between ordinary sleep and the death-like stupor the Greeks called kōma.

2. Humoral theory and the Middle Ages. Galen folded lethargy into the theory of the four humours: the brain, cooled and dampened by excess phlegm, sank the whole body into sluggishness. This picture — drowsiness as an internal imbalance, not an invading enemy — governed European medicine for more than a millennium. Medieval Latin kept the Greek term as lethargia, and the Anglo-French letargie carried it into Middle English by the fourteenth century. Here the word was still a disease name: medieval physicians knew that a lethargy was a morbid sleep to be fought with stern aperients, noise, and alarms — not a mood to be indulged.

3. Broadening in the early modern period. From the seventeenth century the clinical noun began to travel. Because the lethargic patient was unresponsive, "lethargy" easily named any unresponsiveness of the mind: dullness, apathy toward duty, the torpor of a generation that would not be roused. Preachers seized on it — a soul in lethargy is a soul that has forgotten God — and political writers applied it to nations sunk in ease and to assemblies that would not act. It is on this long-broadened sense that Will Durant drew in the passage above: the tropical climate that breeds lethargy and disease diverts a people's energy from "the inessentials of life that make civilization", leaving only hunger and reproduction.

  • Renaissance medicine:lethargia remains a standard entry in the casebooks; remedies aim to "wake the brain".
  • 17th-18th c. prose: lethargy becomes a moral and political charge — indolence, negligence, spiritual sleep.
  • 19th c.: neurology and psychiatry naturalise it: nervous exhaustion, neurasthenia, and the languid invalid inherit the diagnosis.
  • 20th c.: the sleeping sickness of 1917-26 is styled encephalitis lethargica; "lethargy of the electorate" becomes a standing phrase of democratic anxiety.

4. A remembered past in every use. What distinguishes lethargy from plain sleepiness is the fossil at its centre: the river of forgetting. Every modern use — a metabolic slump, a sluggish market, an unresponsive bureaucracy — quietly repeats the Greek intuition that when we are lethargic we are not resting but forgetting: forgetting duties, possibilities, or, as Durant puts it, the arts and the mind themselves. The word therefore resists rehabilitation. We may sanction a nap but not a lethargy, and the difference is exactly the oblivion the Greeks built into the name.
