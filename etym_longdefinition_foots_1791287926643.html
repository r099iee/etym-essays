foots is the third-person singular present of the verb foot in its idiomatic sense "to foot (a bill / the bills)", meaning to pay, cover, or bear the full cost of something, especially when the expense was run up by someone else or is large, unwelcome, or recurring. In the Wodehouse sentence, "Uncle Tom foots the Boudoir bills" means Uncle Tom routinely pays the debts and running costs of Milady's Boudoir, Aunt Dahlia's magazine. The subject takes financial responsibility; the object is the bill, costs, or losses.

The core modern sense is financial and agentive: X foots Y = X pays Y in full. It implies completeness — not contributing a share but settling the whole. It frequently carries a rueful or reluctant overtone: the payer did not necessarily enjoy the benefit but ends up liable, as when taxpayers foot the bill for a bailout, or a parent foots university fees. In generous contexts it can be neutral or magnanimous, as when a host foots the bill for dinner. The verb governs direct objects like bill, costs, expenses, losses, tab, and account, and appears in the fixed collocations "foot the bill", "foot the costs", and the now rarer "foot up" (add up to).

  • Primary sense — to pay in full: to settle an invoice or cover expenditure. Example: "The company footed the legal fees." The semantic bridge from "add up at the bottom of the page" to "pay" is metonymic: the person who totals the account closes and discharges it. This is the only live verbal sense in contemporary standard English.
  • Archaic / historical sense — to add up, to total: "to foot up a column" meant to sum figures and enter the total at the foot. Example: "The debts foot up to £50,000." Now largely obsolete except in historical novels and bookkeeping histories, but essential to understanding the idiom; the paying sense grew out of the summing sense.
  • Dance / movement sense of foot (unrelated to bills): the same verb foot also means to dance ("to foot a measure"), to walk or travel on foot ("to foot it across the country"), and to furnish with a foot or foundation ("to foot a stocking", "to foot a wall"). These share the noun root but diverged early; only context disambiguates. "He footed the bill" (paid) versus "he footed it home" (walked) versus "she footed the dance floor" (danced).
  • Domain usages: in business journalism, "to foot the bill" is standard for who bears costs in deals, divorces, government spending, and insurance. In law and finance, "footing" can still appear as a noun for the total. In sport and hospitality, "footing the tab" is the American equivalent. The idiom is informal-to-neutral, acceptable in news prose but slightly colloquial.
  • Register and connotation: mildly informal, vivid, faintly complaining. Unlike neutral "pay", "foot" spotlights the burden and the bottom line. "Uncle Tom foots the bills" suggests a settled arrangement, expected yet faintly wearying — exactly Wodehouse's comic economy of rich backers and extravagant dependents.

Grammatically, foots is regular: foot/foots/footed/footing. It is transitive in the bill sense and cannot drop its object (*"he foots" alone is ungrammatical without understood "the bill"). It contrasts with near-synonyms: pay is neutral and general; defray is formal; shoulder stresses burden; bankroll stresses sponsorship of a venture. Learners should anchor on the image of the total written at the foot (bottom) of a receipt: to foot is to own that bottom line.
