WATERLOGGED is a compound adjective made of water + logged, describing something so saturated with, filled with, or immersed in water that it has become heavy, swollen, inert, and unable to function normally. The image at the heart of the word is of a ship or a timber so water-soaked that it lies in the water as inert and heavy as a floating log. In the Tolkien passage, the dwarf Bombur and his companions, having been swept along in barrels in the river, are so waterlogged — so utterly drenched and weighed down with water — that they seem only half alive, incapable of standing, speaking, or even realising that they are saved.

  • Primary sense (ships and structures): of a ship, boat, timber, or wooden structure, so saturated with water as to be heavy, water-heavy, and immobile; e.g. "a waterlogged wreck", "waterlogged timbers".
  • Of land and soil: ground so saturated with water that its air-spaces are filled; boggy, heavy, and unworkable earth; a major problem in farming, drainage, and civil engineering; e.g. "waterlogged ground after the flood".
  • Applied sense (people and creatures): of a person or animal, drenched and weighed down with water, sodden, exhausted, and almost helpless with wetness — the Tolkien usage; e.g. "waterlogged, half-drowned survivors".
  • Applied sense (objects generally): any object that has absorbed water and become spoilt, swollen, or non-functional — a waterlogged camera, a waterlogged phone, waterlogged leather, a waterlogged mattress; the water has got inside and ruined or disabled it.
  • Of crops and plants: in horticulture, "waterlogged" roots are drowned for lack of oxygen; waterlogged soil suffocates plants and is the cause of many plant diseases.
  • Sport and everyday use: a "waterlogged pitch" is so saturated that play must be abandoned; the word is standard for any surface or object rendered unusable by soaking.
  • Figurative sense (rare but natural): heavy, sodden, inert, or overwhelmed — a person "waterlogged with information" or an organisation too clogged to work; the idea of being weighed down and made sluggish, like a log full of water.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is first attested in English in 1729, in the nautical language of the Royal Navy: a ship was said to be "water-logged" when, after shipping heavy seas or being holed, it lay so full of water that it floated in the sea heavy, sluggish, and barely responding to her helm — in effect, turned into a log that floats but cannot move. The connection to the noun log, "a tree-trunk cut and floating in water", is the whole point: a waterlogged ship floats like a log, immobile and unmanageable. From ships the adjective spread to timber and wrecks, then to land and soil, and finally to people and objects soaked through. The historical bridge is thus concrete and nautical: the word was invented to describe a specific, dangerous state of a ship, and then extended by obvious analogy to everything else that becomes heavy and inert through taking on water.

Register and nuances. Waterlogged is more specific and stronger than wet or damp: it implies saturation to the point of dysfunction — the thing is not merely wet, it is disabled by its water content. It differs from soaked in stressing the heaviness and uselessness that result, and from flooded in stressing absorption of water into the substance rather than water standing around it. In sport, a "waterlogged pitch" ends a match; in horticulture, waterlogged soil suffocates roots; in boating, a waterlogged hull may be in danger of sinking or be too heavy to handle. The word carries a consistent tone of damage, defeat, and helplessness — a waterlogged thing has been conquered by the water.

  • Synonyms: soaked, saturated, sodden, drenched, doused, soggy, dripping, sopping, wet through, swamped, flooded, bogged, marshy, miry, heavy, water-heavy.
  • Near-synonyms with a difference: water-soaked (emphasis on wetness rather than inertness), sodden (of cloth and soaked spirits), drenched (the act of dousing), saturated (the scientific term for maximum absorption).
  • Antonyms: dry, arid, watertight, buoyant, airy, well-drained, parched.

Word-family relations. The compound waterlogged stands at the meeting of two families: water, with its Germanic cognates (German Wasser, Dutch water, Norse vatn) and its Indo-European cousins (Latin aqua, Greek hýdōr, Sanskrit udán); and log, the tree-trunk noun of Scandinavian origin. The participial formation — logged as "reduced to the state of a log" — also yields the American adjective logy (sluggish, dull) and the nautical-to-record chain of log as verb and logbook.
