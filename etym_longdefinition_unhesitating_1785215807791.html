unhesitating is an adjective meaning prompt, immediate, and resolute — performed, expressed, or held without pause, doubt, or faltering. It is the negative of hesitating, and its root image is physical: to hesitate is to 'stick fast,' to have the foot caught or the tongue stuck, so that movement or speech cannot proceed smoothly. To be unhesitating is to be free of that sticking — to move, answer, or decide in one smooth, instant motion, with no moment of weighing and no residue of reservation. The word can be used of persons (an unhesitating reply), of actions (unhesitating obedience), of attitudes (an unhesitating commitment), and of the manner in which a conviction is held (an unhesitating belief).

In the literary context of this card the word is doing emphatic emotional work. John Stuart Mill 'proclaims his unhesitating belief in the intellectual influence of his wife.' The adjective insists that Mill's conviction was delivered instantly and completely — with no moment of weighing, no residue of reservation, no tremor of doubt. Coming in the same sentence as the portrait of his 'unimpressionable' father, unhesitating marks the son as the opposite temperament: where James Mill was sealed and dry, John Stuart Mill pours out an immediate, total, reiterated avowal of the woman who, he believed, had formed his mind. The word's physical root — the image of a foot or tongue 'sticking fast' in hesitation — is inverted: belief here moves forward as smoothly as a hand that never catches.

The senses and uses may be catalogued:

  • Of answers and speech (the core sense): given without pause, hesitation, or qualification — 'an unhesitating reply,' 'she answered with unhesitating clarity.'
  • Of actions and obedience: performed at once and without demur — 'unhesitating obedience,' 'unhesitating support,' 'unhesitatingly volunteered.'
  • Of belief and conviction: held with total, immediate confidence, with no internal reservation — the sense at work in 'his unhesitating belief.'
  • Of persons and character: disposed to act and decide at once, decisive, resolute, the opposite of the waverer; the adverb unhesitatingly (c. 1800) is the most common form in modern prose.
  • Register: chiefly formal and literary — journalism, biography, rhetoric — where it stresses instantaneity of response rather than mere firmness; in casual speech 'without a moment's hesitation,' 'straight away,' or 'no question' carry the same force.

unhesitating must be separated from its near-synonyms. Immediate is temporal, stressing that no time intervenes, but says nothing about resolve. Prompt likewise stresses speed and readiness of response. Ready adds preparedness and willingness but not firmness of conviction. Resolute and firm describe a settled strength of purpose in the face of obstacles, yet they do not necessarily exclude an initial pause before the resolve; unhesitating denies that pause altogether. Unwavering and steadfast emphasize constancy over time — not being shaken later — whereas unhesitating focuses on the absence of the initial falter, the instantaneous delivery of the response. Instinctive suggests an act arising without thought from nature, which is not the right note for an avowed and deliberate intellectual belief. Unfaltering is the closest in feeling — speech or movement that never stumbles — and may be used almost interchangeably, though it leans slightly toward steadiness under strain rather than instantaneous delivery. unhesitating, in short, means that between the question and the answer, between the claim and the assent, there was nothing at all: no pause, no doubt, no stick.
