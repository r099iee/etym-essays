flair (noun) — /flɛə/. A naturally great ability or instinctive aptitude for something; or, in a second, equally common modern sense, stylishness and panache. In the source sentence — "even a flair for the minor forms of art" — the word means an innate talent: the American Indians, says Durant, might possess a genuine gift for minor artistic forms and yet, lacking settled food production, never pass from barbarism to civilization.

I. Primary sense — natural aptitude

  • The core modern meaning is "an instinctive or pronounced talent": a flair for languages, a flair for figures, a flair for the dramatic, a flair for marketing. What distinguishes flair from mere skill is the implication that the ability is inborn or at least deeply internalised — it smells out the right thing without conscious deliberation.
  • Grammar: most often a flair for + noun/gerund; also have the flair to do something. It is normally a count noun in this sense ("a natural flair") but can be used abstractly ("she has flair").

II. Secondary sense — stylishness, panache, showmanship

  • Since the early 20th century flair has also meant "a distinctive and attractive style or manner": dressed with flair, a restaurant with flair, an arranger with real flair. In this use it borders on panache, élan and verve — the quality of doing something with a flourish that makes it memorable.
  • The bridge between the two senses is performance: the person with flair for a task performs it with such natural rightness that the performance itself reads as style. Talent, made visible, becomes elegance — hence "with flair" slides from "with skill" to "with polish."

III. The forgotten earlier senses

  • Odour, scent, the sense of smell (archaic). Borrowed in the 16th century, flair first meant "a scent, an odour," and by extension "the sense of smell," especially as used by animals in the chase. This is the sense that explains everything else: to have flair was to scent or sniff things out.
  • Instinctive discernment (19th century). By about 1825 the word had been abstracted: flair became "keen instinctive discernment," the power of detecting what is of value, of judging at a glance. This is the hinge sense from which both the talent sense and the style sense hang.

IV. Polysemy analysis — how the senses hang together

The semantic core of flair across all its senses is unerring detection without reasoning: the nose that finds the scent is the mind that finds the right answer is the performer who finds the right gesture. Distinguish carefully from near-synonyms:

  • Flair stresses innate knack and (in the style sense) visible liveliness; it is lighter than genius (which implies exceptional, often solitary depth) and more specific than talent (which implies developed ability).
  • Flair is positive and admiring, unlike affectation or show, though a hint of theatricality ("a flair for the dramatic") can tip it toward mild criticism.
  • Unlike gift or talent, flair carries a whiff of the French and of fashionable society; the word’s register is polished, slightly worldly, at home in reviews of art, food and fashion.

V. Confusable spellings

  • flare — a blaze, or a widening (as of a skirt); the two words are homophones in most accents and are frequently confused in writing. Flair has nothing to do with fire; it is about scent and skill.

VI. Register note for the source text

Durant uses flair in its admiring 19th-century sense of "instinctive gift": the American Indians, he concedes, may have a genuine "flair for the minor forms of art," yet this alone cannot lift a hunting people into civilization. The choice of flair rather than talent or gift quietly borrows the word’s aristocratic French aura and its sense of inborn, untaught rightness — exactly what the argument requires: an endowment that is real but not sufficient.
