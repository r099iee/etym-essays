Biff is an imitative or expressive formation — a word invented to sound like what it means — and therefore has no PIE root, no Latin ancestor, and no Germanic pedigree. The OED, Etymonline, and Merriam-Webster agree: imitative origin. Its first life was as an interjection, a comic-strip sound effect: Biff! Bam! Whack! OED's earliest evidence for the interjection is 1843 in John S. Robb's American writing, Etymonline notes sound-effect use by 1847. This was the golden age of cheap American humour, minstrel patter, and later dime novels, where blows needed audible spelling. The vowel i plus final ff iconically suggests a short, sharp impact: high front vowel for suddenness, labial stop for closure.

From noise to action is a single step. By 1877 American slang records to biff meaning to hit, with OED's verb evidence from 1888 in the comic paper Judy and Green's Dictionary of Slang citing 1885 Australian and 1889 New York examples ("biffed in the jaw", "biffed her on the head with a fat club"). Barrère and Leland's Dictionary of Slang, Jargon and Cant (1889-90) already labels it an Americanism meaning to give "a biff in the jaw". The noun — a biff = a blow — follows by 1881. The progression interjection → verb → noun is typical of echoic words: the sound becomes the act, the act becomes the countable hit.

  • 1840s sound: Biff! as pure onomatopoeia alongside Bang! Wallop! Whack! — used in tall tales and stage directions to make a punch audible on the page.
  • 1870s-1880s verbing: to biff = to deliver that sound by hitting; initially low slang, sporting and street, then schoolboy and military slang. Past biffed and participle biffing appear at once.
  • Imperial spread: carried to Britain, Australia, and New Zealand by sailors, soldiers, and popular press; Bulletin (Sydney, 1885) shows it fully naturalised in Australian verse within a decade.
  • Wodehouse register: by 1934 "I'll biff you where you stand" is comic-threat English — bluff, schoolboyish, upper-class slang that makes violence sound farcical rather than brutal. C. S. Lewis (1945) uses the same cosy toughness: "Hit him a biff."
  • Modern afterlife: biff survives as informal British for a light punch ("a biff on the nose"), Australian for dismissal ("to get the biff" = be sacked, by extension of being struck off), and American comics onomatopoeia (Batman TV "Biff! Pow!"). Scientific "BIFF" acronyms are unrelated.

Phonologically biff /bɪf/ is maximally efficient: voiced bilabial onset, short lax vowel, voiceless labiodental coda — a syllable that begins with force and ends with abrupt friction, mimicking contact. No sound change or borrowing complicates it; spelling is stable since the 1840s. The scholarly nuance is precisely the absence of deeper etymology: attempts to link it to buff, beef, or Scandinavian roots are rejected by the OED. It is a 19th-century American invention, expressive, not inherited — proof that new words can be minted from noise and still earn a place in Wodehouse threats, school stories, and cricket commentary ("a biff to the boundary").
