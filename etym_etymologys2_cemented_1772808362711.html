PIE *keh₂id- 'to cut, strike, hew' (reconstructed root, unattested; the precise shape is debated — older handbooks write *kae-id- or *kə-id-, modern reconstructions often *keh₂id-) → Latin caedō, caedere, cecīdī, caesum 'to cut, hew, strike down, kill' (attested from the 3rd century BCE; derivatives give English -cide, incisor, caesura, and, via French, scissors) → Latin caementum 'quarry chips, rough stone for rubble-work, stone pieces for mortar' (derived from caedere as 'that which is cut'; attested in Roman architectural writers such as Vitruvius, 1st century BCE; the plural caementa fed Roman concrete, opus caementicium) → Old French ciment 'mortar, binding paste' (attested 12th century) → Middle English cement, cyment 'binding material, mortar' (attested c. 1350) → English cement (noun) 'binding material; figurative bond' (from the 14th century) → English cement (verb) 'to bind or join as with cement' (verb attested from the late 15th century; figurative use of 'cemented alliances/friendships/unions' flourishing from the 17th century) → English cemented (past participle, adjectival) 'joined together or made firm, as if with cement.' Note on debate: the Indo-European etymon of Latin caedere is not universally settled; the *keh₂id- family is also linked to Sanskrit khidáti 'to press, split' and Greek skhízō 'to split,' but some scholars treat those connections as uncertain, and a minority derive caedere from a separate root. The derivation of caementum from caedere, by contrast, is secure and ancient — Varro and Vitruvius already treat the noun as 'cut stone.'
