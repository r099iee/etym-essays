Eskimo is one of the most contested exonyms in English, and its history illustrates how a word borrowed through colonial contact can move from neutral description to scientific label to slur without changing form. The modern debate cannot be understood without tracking both the linguistic borrowing and the changing power relations around it.

The standard etymology, accepted by the OED, Etymonline and most Algonquianists, derives Eskimo not from the language of the people it denotes, but from the language of their southern neighbours. Proto-Algonquian possessed a root *ašk- / *ask- meaning raw or uncooked and a verbal formative *-imo meaning to eat. In Cree and Montagnais (Innu-aimun) this yields forms such as Cree aski-mowew and Montagnais assime·w, glossed as he eats raw flesh or eater of raw meat. French fishermen, fur traders and missionaries in Labrador and the St Lawrence in the 16th-17th centuries heard variants such as eskimaw, eskimantsic and arachkik from Montagnais speakers referring to the more northerly peoples, and adapted them as Esquimaux. English borrowed the French form as Eskimo, Eskimos, Esquimaw in the 1580s-1740s, initially in travel narratives of Frobisher, Davis and later Hudson Bay Company reports.

  • Algonquian stage: exonym formation. Neighbouring groups routinely label each other by diet, speech or custom. Raw-meat-eating marked a real cultural difference in food preparation, but also carried a judgment of barbarity. The word was thus from birth an outsider term.
  • French mediation: orthographic fixation. French plural -aux / -eaux (Esquimaux) was carried into English and long preserved even in singular use, explaining 18th-19th century spellings Esquimaux, Eskimaux, Eskimoes before regularized Eskimo / Eskimos.
  • English generalization: semantic broadening. Early English use applied Eskimo indiscriminately to all Arctic peoples from Greenland to Siberia, conflating Inuit, Inupiat, Yupik, Cupik, Alutiiq and sometimes even Aleut/Unangan, who are linguistically and culturally distinct.

Phonetically the development is straightforward because it is a borrowing, not an inherited sound change. Algonquian /aškimeːw/ with initial vowel and palatal fricative was perceived by French ears as es-ki-mo, with stress shift to first syllable and nasalized vowel in -maux. English then anglicized the vowels to /ɛs-kɪ-məʊ/, spelled Eskimo with initial E and final -o by analogy with words like albino. No Grimm's Law or Great Vowel Shift operation is involved; the changes are adaptation of an unfamiliar cluster to French then English phonotactics.

Semantically the crucial shift is not in denotation but in connotation and in scientific versus popular registers:

  • 16th-19th centuries: travel and admiration. Hakluyt, Purchas, Ross, Parry and Franklin use Eskimo as a neutral ethnographic label, often accompanied by romanticized praise of hardiness, ingenuity, sledges, kayaks and igloos. Peary, as in the source context Peary asked one of his guides, exemplifies this heroic-explorer frame where the Eskimo guide is indispensable yet subordinate.
  • Late 19th-mid 20th century: anthropological codification. Franz Boas, Stefansson and others institutionalize Eskimo as a culture-area term and, crucially, as a linguistic term: Eskimo-Aleut (Eskaleut) family, divided into Inuit-Inupiaq and Yupik branches plus Aleut. Schoolbooks, museums and dictionaries present it as purely descriptive.
  • 1970s-present: Indigenous reclamation and rejection. In Canada and Greenland, Inuit (meaning the people in Inuktitut) replaces Eskimo as self-designation; the 1977 Inuit Circumpolar Council formally rejects Eskimo as colonial and pejorative. In Canada its public use is now widely considered offensive. In Alaska, by contrast, many Yupik, Cupik and Inupiat people continue to accept or prefer Eskimo as an inclusive term because Inuit does not include Yupik peoples, and the Alaska Native community has retained Eskimo in institutional names.

Literary attestations trace this arc: 1584 French Discours du voyage, 1740s English Esquimaux Indians, 19th-century Dickens and Ballantyne adventure fiction, 20th-century linguistic surveys, and 21st-century style guides warning against use except in historical or linguistic compounds such as Eskimo-Aleut, Eskimo dog, Eskimo roll. The debated alternative etymology from Montagnais ayas̆kimew snowshoe-netter, proposed to rescue the word from the raw-flesh gloss, is itself part of this moral history, showing how speakers seek a less insulting origin when a term becomes stigmatized, though most specialists retain the eater-of-raw-meat derivation while stressing it was an Algonquian exonym, not a European invention.

Today Eskimo thus survives in three distinct niches: as a dated general exonym to be avoided in Canada, as a still-used inclusive self-term in parts of Alaska and Siberia, and as a frozen technical element in compounds. Its evolution is a textbook case of pejoration by association rather than by sound or sense change.
