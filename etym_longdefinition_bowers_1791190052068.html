Bowers is the plural of bower, meaning shady, leafy shelters or recesses formed by trees, vines, or branches bent together, natural arbours, and by older extension any secluded chamber, hut, or dwelling. In the source sentence birds make nests and bowers: some birds weave twig display structures, while more generally any bird leafy nesting nook counts poetically as a bower. The word evokes cool shade, privacy, and rustic craft: honeysuckle bowers, willow bowers, forest bowers where lovers meet or travellers rest. Archaically it also meant a lady private chamber, a peasant hut, or a poetic dwelling-place such as heavenly bowers.

The bridge from room to leafy hut is enclosure plus seclusion: a bower is what encloses and hides, whether walls or leaves. Garden bowers are trellised or tree-formed shelters overgrown with climbers, the pride of Tudor and Georgian landscape design and of pastoral poetry from Spenser to Keats. Wildwood bowers are natural thickets or overhanging boughs forming a roof, inhabited by birds and animals. Ornithological bowers are display structures built by male bowerbirds, avenues or maypoles of sticks decorated with shells, berries, and glass, built for courtship and seduction rather than for eggs. Archaic chambers are inner rooms and bridal apartments in medieval romance, where a bower-maid attends her lady.

  • garden arbour: trellised or tree-formed shelter overgrown with roses, vines, honeysuckle; designed shade.
  • wildwood shelter: natural thicket or bending boughs forming a roof; animal and human refuge.
  • ornithological bower: bowerbird avenue or maypole decorated for courtship; sexual selection textbook case.
  • archaic chamber: inner room, boudoir, bridal apartment in romance and ballad; bower-maid, bower-woman.
  • poetic dwelling: blissful secluded abode: woodland bowers of fauns, heavenly bowers of the blessed.
  • homographs excluded: bower anchor of ships and bower jack at cards are specialized senses rarely meant here.
  • mood and register: idyllic, sheltered, slightly archaic; Shakespearean forest rather than modern shed.
  • source pairing: nests for eggs beside bowers for display; beaver dams, bird bowers, chimpanzee huts as animal models for human building.

Modern use is deliberately literary or horticultural: landscape architects still label a trellis a bower, poets still hide lovers in bowers, and biologists describe bowerbird architecture with the same noun Darwin used. To speak of bowers is to praise nature as builder, fitting the passage where early man copies beaver dams, bird bowers, and chimpanzee huts on the road to human architecture. The plural matters: bowers are many, dappled, inviting, a landscape of recesses rather than a single shed. Garden writers still distinguish the bower as living architecture, grown not built, pruned and trained over seasons, unlike sheds and summerhouses assembled in days.
