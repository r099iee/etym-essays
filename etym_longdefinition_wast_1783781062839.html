wast (v.) — an archaic second-person singular past-tense form of the verb be ("thou wast"), the early modern equivalent of modern "you were." The -st ending marks the verb for the pronoun thou (subject singular), so wast means "you were," with the intimate, reverential, or solemn register that the thou form carries in Early Modern English. Grammatically it is the simple indicative past; the subjunctive counterpart is wert.

Grammatical senses. As an inflected form of the suppletive verb be, wast carries the full range of senses of "to be" in the second person singular past:

  • Existence — "thou wast present": the verb asserts that the addressee existed or was at hand at a past time; the Miltonic sense, of the Spirit present "from the first."
  • Identity and predication — "thou wast a child," "thou wast a true friend": copular use linking a subject to a noun or adjective.
  • State or condition — "thou wast always a true friend": expressing a continuing state, quality, or circumstance in the past.
  • Location — "where wast thou?": expressing where the addressee was at a past moment.
  • Auxiliary (in the passive or progressive past) — "thou wast chosen," "thou wast going": forming the past passive and past progressive constructions in the thou-paradigm.

Historical & stylistic senses. Beyond its verbal content, wast carries grammatical and stylistic meaning:

  • Person, number, and mood: it is unambiguously second-person singular, and it is specifically the indicative past form — wert is the subjunctive. The King James Bible and Milton maintain this distinction: wast for plain fact, wert for condition or hypothesis.
  • Register: the thou forms express intimacy, reverence, or solemn address; wast is thus found in prayer, invocation, scripture, and elevated poetry, addressing God, a single soul, or a beloved.
  • Archaism today: in modern English wast is a fossilized archaism — it appears in biblical quotation, liturgy, and archaizing verse, instantly signalling scriptural or early modern diction.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is transparent in origin: the past stem was- (from Old English wæs, the strong past singular of the verb "to be") plus the generalized second-person singular ending -st. This is a striking example of analogical change, because the verb "to be" is deeply irregular and suppletive: Old English had two separate verbs (wesan "to be, remain" and bēon "to be, become") and its present forms were drawn from yet a third Indo-European root, *h₁es-. The past forms, however, came from *wes- "to remain," and the second person singular past was originally wǣre. During the Middle English period speakers rebuilt this form on the model of the strong verbs: was + -st → wast (13th century), exactly as sat + -st gave satst and did + -st gave didst. The bridge to wast is therefore a morphological analogy, not a sound change: speakers regularized the most irregular verb in the language by analogy with the regular strong pasts.

Current status. In present-day English wast is obsolete outside deliberate archaism. It survives in the Authorized Version, in the Book of Common Prayer, and in quotations from them, and it is a standard example in the grammar of Early Modern English of the indicative thou-form of the past of be, contrasted with the subjunctive wert.
