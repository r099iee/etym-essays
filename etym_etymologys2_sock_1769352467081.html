Because English sock is a pair of unrelated homonyms (the garment and the verb), the derivation chains must be given separately for each. CHAIN 1 — the garment (the older word): Source: UNKNOWN — the word's origin before Greek cannot be established. Greek sýkkhos (σύκχος) is of uncertain, probably non-Indo-European (possibly eastern Mediterranean / Phoenician) origin; no PIE root is attested or reliably reconstructible. → Greek sýkkhos, "a light soft shoe, a slipper" (attested in Greek comedy and daily usage, 5th–4th c. BCE; origin unknown) → Latin soccus, "a light low-heeled slipper" (attested 2nd c. BCE; loanword from Greek sýkkhos; worn by comic actors, hence the symbol of comedy) → Old English socc, "a slipper, a light shoe" (attested c. 8th–9th c.; early loan from Latin soccus, taken in the Anglo-Saxon period) → Middle English sokke / socke, "a sock, a light covering for the foot" (attested 13th–14th c.) → English sock, "a knitted or woven garment covering the foot and ankle" (modern sense, from the 16th c. onward as knitting replaced leather) CHAIN 2 — the verb "to hit" (the sense of this card): Origin: UNKNOWN — the verb appears abruptly in English slang in the late 17th century (c. 1690s) with no securely attested earlier source. The most probable explanation is that it is an imitative (onomatopoeic) English coinage, shaped to echo the dull thud of a heavy blow; an alternative, also unproven, derives it from the sock-garment used as a weighted weapon (a stocking stuffed with coins or a stone, swung in brawls). → English sock (verb), "to strike or punch hard" (attested from the late 17th c., e.g. in slang dictionaries of the 1690s) → English sock (noun), "a hard blow, a punch" (derived from the verb, 19th c.) Note on the two words: the garment sock and the verb sock are unrelated homonyms — they share a spelling and pronunciation but have no common etymology, and the verb has no PIE ancestry of any kind. Both chains above state their unknown stages explicitly rather than inventing links.
