Afresh is a late Middle English adverbial formation, not a single inherited PIE word frozen in place. It is a compound of two native English elements whose fusion happened on English soil, and its biography is the story of how a simple adjective of physical freshness became an adverb of spiritual and procedural renewal.

The second half, fresh, is the older half. It rests on Proto-Germanic *friskaz, meaning “fresh, active, unspoiled,” which yields Old English fersc — used especially of water, of unsalted, clean, drinkable water — and which has clear West and North Germanic cognates: Old High German frisc, Old Norse ferskr, modern German frisch and Dutch vers. The Germanic stage is secure; the Indo-European ancestry behind *friskaz is not. Handbooks offer competing reconstructions — a root meaning “to cut, divide,” with “fresh” understood as “newly cut” (compare freshly cut bread or grass); a root for liveliness or quick motion; or no secure cognate set at all, the word being treated as Germanic-internal or even expressive. The honest verdict is that the word’s pre-Germanic past is unknown, and no confident PIE line can be drawn for it.

After the Norman Conquest the adjective’s English career was reshaped by contact. Old French freis (modern French frais) — “fresh, cool, new” — reinforced and re-shaped the form and currency of the English adjective, so that Middle English fresh/fressh sits at a Germanic–Romance crossroads while remaining a native word in use. The adjective’s oldest English senses were physical: fresh water (unsalted), fresh air (clean), fresh meat (not stale). From “unstale, unspoiled” the word easily climbed to “newly made, recent, not previously used,” and that is the semantic hinge on which the adverb turns.

The first half is the native prefix a-, historically a reduced preposition meaning “on,” “in,” or “to” — the same element visible in asleep (“in sleep”), aside (“on the side”), aboard (“on board”), and aloft. English speakers also used the analytic phrase of fresh (and on fresh) to mean “anew.” At some point around the middle of the fifteenth century — the OED’s first evidence is dated c. 1440 — the phrase fused into a single adverb, afresh: “on a fresh footing,” “over again with newness.” The fusion is purely English; there is no Romance whole-word loan here, only native compounding of a native prefix with a native (French-influenced) adjective.

Semantic history: once formed, the adverb’s meaning is stable and transparent. It packages repetition plus renewed condition: not merely doing something again, but doing it again in a new or clean state. It never develops a strongly abstract or moral sense; it stays close to re-beginning and re-arranging. That clarity makes it useful in narrative stage-directions — tables set afresh, attempts begun afresh, tears flowing afresh — and in modern operational language (“start afresh” in planning, computing, and self-help, where the image of wiping the board clean is explicit).

Timeline of afresh:

  • Proto-Germanic: *friskaz “fresh, lively, unspoiled” (PIE ancestry uncertain and debated).
  • Old English: fersc “fresh; of water, not salt.”
  • After 1066: Old French freis/fres reinforces the adjective’s form and currency.
  • Middle English: fresh/fressh; the analytic phrase of fresh meaning “anew.”
  • c. 1440: the fused adverb afresh is attested.
  • Modern: a brisk, slightly formal adverb of reset, the linguistic badge of a deliberate new beginning.

At Bag End, “set out everything afresh” is precisely the linguistic badge of the dwarves’ household coup: not a second helping on Bilbo’s terms, but a complete re-lay of the feast under their own order in his parlour — the adverb of a new beginning, with the clean, unstale freshness of the word still audible inside it.
