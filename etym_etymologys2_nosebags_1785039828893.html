FIRST ELEMENT — NOSE: PIE root *nas- "nose" (reconstructed; attested in the nasal words of nearly every Indo-European branch: Sanskrit nā́sā, Latin nāsus, Old Irish sróin's kin) → Proto-Germanic *nasō "nose" (c. 500 BCE–500 CE) → Old English nosu "nose" (attested from the 8th century) → Middle English nose "nose" (13th century onward) → Modern English nosebags (plural of the compound nose-bag, formed by joining nose with bag; see second element below) SECOND ELEMENT — BAG (origin uncertain, no secure PIE etymon): Origin unknown — no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon; the word may be a loan from a pre-Germanic or Celtic substrate for a bulging object, or a late coinage → Old Norse baggi "bag, pack, satchel, bundle" (related to bǫggr "load, burden"; Viking Age, c. 8th–11th century) → Middle English bagge "bag, purse, pouch" (c. 13th century; also borrowed into Old French as bague "bundle, package, sack," whence Modern French bagage and English baggage) → Modern English bag "a flexible container for carrying or storing things" → English compound nose + bag → nose-bag "a feedbag strapped over an animal's muzzle" (18th century) → Modern English nosebags (plural: feedbags strapped to the head) Note on the debate: The etymology of bag is genuinely uncertain. The standard view derives Middle English bagge from Old Norse baggi, but baggi itself has no secure Indo-European ancestry — some scholars suggest a Celtic or substrate loan (the image of a bulging, rounded object), and the word's sudden appearance in the 13th century has never been fully explained. The compound nose-bag is an English formation of the 18th century, built transparently from its two elements, so the chains of nose and bag meet only at the final compound node; each element has its own separate history.
