1. The peeling root and the leaf. The chain begins with PIE *leup- ‘to peel off, strip, break off’, a root attested in Greek λέπω (lepō) ‘to peel, strip’ (whence leprosy and lepidoptera) and Lithuanian lùpti ‘to peel, flay’. In Germanic the root yields *laubą ‘leaf, foliage’ — the leaf being, etymologically, the peeled-off, stripped thing: Gothic laufs, Old English lēaf (modern leaf), Old High German loub. From the foliage-word, Germanic then built a word for a shelter woven from leaves and branches: *laubjō-, attested in Old High German louba ‘porch, gallery, arbour’ (modern German Laube ‘bower, garden pavilion’); the Frankish form *laubja ‘shelter of foliage, hut’ was carried into Gallo-Romance. Three modern words descend from that single Frankish item: English lodge (via Old French loge), Italian loggia (via its own vernacular), and English lobby (via medieval Latin laubia). Every “lodge” you have ever slept, skied or hunted in is a distant descendant of a leaf-hut.

2. Old French: lodging and un-lodging. Old French loge (9th–10th centuries) means ‘hut, lodge, arbour, covered walk’, and it remains a living word today (loge = theatre box, porter's lodge; loggia in Italian). From it, Old French builds logier ‘to lodge, to give or take quarters, to encamp’ — a word of travel, war and hospitality — and from logier the privative deslogier ‘to dislodge, to evict, to break camp’. The military flavour is original: an army that desloge abandons its camp; a commander who desloges the enemy drives him out of his billets, his fortification, his position. Modern French keeps the word (déloger: to evict, to dislodge) with the same troops-and-tenants double life.

3. Middle English (c. 1400). English takes the word as disloggen around 1400 (OED's early citations come from the late-14th-century translations of Higden's Polychronicon) with the meaning ‘to drive from a position, to dislodge’. The verb remains rare at first and heavily military: chronicles describe garrisons dislodged from castles, besiegers dislodged from their siege-works, and — with the rise of gunpowder — gunners dislodged from batteries. The prefix is Latin dis- ‘apart, away, reversal’ passed through Old French des-, the same prefix that gives disarm, disband, discharge.

4. The tactical age (16th–18th centuries). In early-modern warfare the word becomes a technical verb of position: ‘to dislodge the enemy from the heights’, ‘from the wood’, ‘from his entrenchment’, ‘from the redoubt’. Manuals, dispatches and campaign histories of the Thirty Years' War, Marlborough's wars and the wars of the French Revolution use it constantly, and the paired noun lodgement — borrowed from French logement, ‘the position, quarters or foothold seized and held’ — completes the pair: you make a lodgement on the beach, and the enemy tries to dislodge you from it. The transferred sense also spreads in the same period to anything fixed: stones, stoppages, tenants, and (in political prose) men and factions.

5. The 19th century: the clinic and the quarry. The century domesticates the word. Surgeons dislodge foreign bodies and obstructions; dentists dislodge teeth and impacted wisdom teeth; geologists describe dislodged boulders, dislodged strata, dislodged scree after landslides; engineers dislodge blockages in drains and mill-races. The verb loses its exclusively martial colour and becomes the standard neutral word for knocking things out of the places where they were stuck or settled — while the military sense remains fully alive in campaign narrative and the language of sieges.

6. The 20th–21st centuries: politics, medicine, mind. Today the word does triple duty. Politically, regimes, incumbents, monopolies and entrenched interests are dislodged by elections, scandals, market forces or war — the tactical image of driving an enemy from ground he holds persists undiminished. Medically, lines, catheters, stents and intra-uterine devices become dislodged (a complication reported in every clinical text), blood clots are dislodged and travel as emboli, and airway obstructions are dislodged by the Heimlich maneuver. Psychologically and intellectually, fixed ideas, habits, prejudices and possessive memories are dislodged by evidence and persuasion. And in plain physical description — the avalanche dislodged boulders, the eagles dislodged the goblins from the mountain-slopes — the word still carries the full weight of its history: what was dislodged was lodged first, settled and dug in, and only force, applied from outside, could move it.
