- Late 16th c.: the word “waistcoat” first appears in English for a kind of under-garment, long and sometimes sleeved.
- 1666: Charles II's court decree makes the waistcoat a mandated element of English male dress — a patriotic political statement against French fashion.
- 18th c.: the garment shortens, loses its sleeves, and becomes the richest item of a gentleman's wardrobe — embroidered silk, gold thread, status display.
- 19th c.: absorbed into the sober three-piece suit; worn daily by Victorian gentlemen as a badge of respectability.
- Early 20th c.: still standard formal wear; also worn by waiters, servants, and card players — a marker of professional or club service.
- Mid–late 20th c.: retreats from everyday dress into formal, ceremonial, and costume settings; American “vest” takes over the garment's name across the Atlantic.
- 21st c.: periodic fashion revivals; the word carries strong period and social-class associations in fiction; Tolkien's “waistcoat of fine diamonds” (1937) remains the word's most famous comic use.
