• Flask: Similar because both are liquid vessels from the same Germanic root; differs because a flask is smaller, often pocketable or laboratory equipment, while a flagon is a large serving vessel.
  • Bottle: Similar because both are containers for drink; differs because bottle is the generic narrow-necked vessel, whereas a flagon is larger, often handled, and archaic or tavern-flavoured.
  • Jug: Similar because both are large handled pouring vessels; differs because a jug is open and for general liquids, while a flagon is often lidded and associated with strong drink and feasting.
  • Pitcher: Similar because both are large pouring vessels; differs because a pitcher is an open-mouthed vessel for water or punch, while a flagon is covered and archaic.
  • Ewer: Similar because both are large handled pouring vessels; differs because an ewer is a decorative water vessel with a wide spout, used in washing rituals, while a flagon holds drink.
  • Carafe / Decanter: Similar because both serve wine; differs because carafes and decanters are elegant open-mouthed glass vessels for serving, whereas a flagon is a bulk, often metal or earthenware, container.
  • Tankard: Similar because both are drink vessels of the tavern; differs because a tankard is a personal drinking cup, while a flagon is the larger vessel that fills tankards.
  • Goblet / Chalice: Similar because both belong to drinking and ceremony; differs because a goblet or chalice is a stemmed drinking cup for one, while a flagon is a bulk container.
  • Amphora: Similar because both are ancient bulk wine or oil vessels; differs because an amphora is a Greek and Roman two-handled storage jar, while a flagon is the medieval European handled serving vessel.
  • Drinking-horn: Similar because both appear in the source context as vessels of a feast; differs because a drinking-horn is an individual, curved horn cup, while a flagon is the large supply vessel.
