LUXURIANCE — "the quality of being lush, thriving, and abundant in growth; rank, exuberant, and richly abundant vegetation or vitality; by extension, extravagant richness or elaboration of any kind." In the supplied literary context, a mansion's gardens are in a state of "luxuriance," with flowers and vines growing in wild profusion: the word captures unrestrained, sensuous botanical excess, nature breaking free of cultivation to choke every inch of space with thriving life.
  • Sense 1 — lush, abundant vegetable growth: rank, thriving, profuse plant life; the primary and most literal sense ("the luxuriance of tropical vegetation," "the garden's luxuriance").
  • Sense 2 — rich or elaborate abundance of style, ornament, or imagery: "the luxuriance of his prose," "luxuriance of decorative detail" — a figurative application to writing, art, and ornament in which the core idea of "growing beyond restraint" is transferred from plants to style.
  • Sense 3 — flourishing abundance of life or health: luxuriance of hair, fur, or the body — thriving, vigorous, abundant growth not necessarily vegetable ("the luxuriance of her dark hair," "the luxuriance of youth").
  • Sense 4 — sensual or sexual excess (now chiefly in the related forms): the underlying Latin "luxuria" meant wantonness, excess, and lust; English "luxury" and the obsolete "luxurie" once meant "lechery, lustful indulgence." The abstract noun "luxuriance" itself was largely laundered of this sense, but the moral undertone of intemperate excess survives as a fringe connotation when the word is applied to human conduct or pleasure.
  • Sense 5 — historically, "wantonness, licentiousness" (obsolete): in older usage "luxuriance" could mean rankness of desire or conduct; this sense is now confined to dictionaries and historical texts, the moral sense having been taken over by "luxury" and "luxuriousness."
Polysemy and historical bridges. Luxuriance is built on the Latin "luxuria," "rankness, excess, luxury," itself from "luxus," "dislocated, extravagant, excess." The word entered English in the seventeenth century, via Middle French "luxuriance," applied first to over-luxuriant growth of plants. The central semantic engine is the image of something growing, bending, or spreading beyond its proper bounds: a plant out of its bed, a joint out of its socket, a passion out of its moral limit. From botanical rankness the word was extended metaphorically, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, to anything that grows, elaborates, or overflows abundantly — prose, ornament, imagination, hair, health — while retaining its warm, sensuous, slightly excessive charge. The moral sense (wantonness, lust) that dominates the Romance descendants (French "luxure," Italian "lussuria," Spanish "lujuria" — all "lust") was detached in English and reallocated to "luxury" and "lust," leaving "luxuriance" as a nearly innocent word for glorious abundance. Register and related words. Luxuriance is a formal-to-literary noun, natural in natural history, criticism, and descriptive prose, rare in conversation. It is near-synonymous with "lushness" but richer and more historical; "abundance" is broader and value-neutral; "profusion" stresses sheer quantity; "opulence" belongs to wealth and ornament. The adjective "luxuriant" (lush, profuse, elaborate) and the verb "luxuriate" (to grow luxuriantly, or to take great sensual delight — "luxuriating in the warm bath") complete the family. Distinguish carefully from "luxurious" (comfortable, expensive — of objects and comforts) and "luxury" (great comfort/wealth, or a costly indulgence): "luxuriant/luxuriance" is about abundance and growth; "luxurious/luxury" is about comfort and expense. The two families have drifted apart since they both sprang from Latin "luxuria." The mnemonic "LUXUR-iance = like LUXURY growth" points the right way: both words share the root of excess, but luxuriance is the flourishing of that excess in growth.
