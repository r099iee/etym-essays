atavistic is a 19th-century scientific adjective built on atavism, which itself revives a precise Roman kinship term, Latin atavus. The Latin word is a compound of nursery atta papa, daddy and avus grandfather, forefather: at-avus, literally the grandfather beyond grandfathers — a great-great-great-grandfather, the fourth ascendant generation, and by extension any remote ancestor or founder of a line. Augustus boasted his atavi; Cicero traced clients back to their atavi. The sense was genealogical pride, not biology.

  • Roots: Avus descends from PIE *h₂ewh₂o- grandfather, ancestor, source also of Gothic awo grandmother, Old Irish aue descendant, and Armenian hav grandfather. Atta reflects PIE nursery *atta, a near-universal baby-word for father found from Hittite attaš to Greek atta, Latin atta, Gothic atta, and Slavic otec by later derivation. Such nursery words are parallel inventions, not single inheritances, which is why they recur worldwide.
  • French atavisme (1830s): French naturalists needed a term for the well-known breeder's observation that grandchildren sometimes resemble grandparents more than parents, or that domestic varieties revert to wild type. They Latinised atavus as atavisme, on the model of organisme, mécanisme. Littré defines it as reproduction of ancestral type after interruption.
  • Darwin and English atavism (1860s–1870s): Darwin in The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication (1868) discusses reversion at length, using atavism interchangeably with reversion and throwing back. Huxley, Galton, and popular science writers followed. The adjective atavistic and rarer atavic appear in the 1870s–1880s in anthropology and criminology.

Phonetically the borrowing is faithful: French atavisme /atavism/ becomes English atavism /ˈætəvɪzəm/, and atavistic follows the Greek-derived -istic stress rule, shifting stress to the penult: RP /ˌætəˈvɪstɪk/. The initial a- remains short, the medial -vis- voices to /vɪs/, and the ending -tic is /tɪk/. Spelling is stable; the only variant is the rarer atavic, now largely displaced.

The word's moral career is darker than its biology. Cesare Lombroso's criminal anthropology (1870s–1890s) seized on atavism to brand criminals, the insane, and colonised peoples as evolutionary throwbacks — atavistic stigmata like low brows and long arms. This racist and determinist misuse, widely popularised in newspapers, gave atavistic its enduring pejorative charge of savagery and backwardness. Twentieth-century genetics quietly retired the technical term in favour of reversion, re-expression, and plesiomorphy, but general English kept atavistic as a vivid insult and image.

Hence the irony on this card: to call one's own concern with rhetorical patterns almost atavistic is to borrow the evolutionist's insult and turn it inward. In an age of global electronic revolution, loving Greek and Latin nomenclature looks like grooming a vestigial tail — endearing, perhaps, but out of step with survival. The almost holds the door open: the author both concedes the charge and invites us to consider whether some throwbacks deserve keeping.
