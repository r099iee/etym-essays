- 16th Century: Borrowed via Middle French from Latin *cohaerentia*, originally denoting physical particles sticking or adhering together.
- 17th Century: Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment philosophers (Locke, Bacon) extend the word to describe logical consistency and sound rational discourse.
- 18th–19th Century: Solidifies in literary criticism, rhetoric, and aesthetics to denote unified artistic composition and organic prose style.
- 20th Century: Formalized in wave optics (laser coherence), quantum mechanics (wavefunction phase coherence), and linguistics (discourse coherence).
- 21st Century: Widely applied across cognitive psychology, artificial intelligence (contextual coherence in large language models), and organizational strategy.
