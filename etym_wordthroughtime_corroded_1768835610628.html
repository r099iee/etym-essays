- 14th century – 'corrode' enters English through Old French from Latin corrōdere, 'to gnaw away wholly'; 'corroded' is used literally of small living gnawers — worms eating wood, ulcers eating flesh — an organic, creeping damage.
- 16th–17th centuries – the word extends from creatures to sharp substances wearing away material, and the moral metaphor is already alive: 'corroding cares', 'corroding envy' — the passions that gnaw the mind from within, as Latin poets had imagined curae rodunt, cares gnawing the heart.
- 17th–18th centuries – the chemical revolution redirects the word's centre of gravity from teeth to acids: corrosion becomes the technical name for the slow chemical eating of metals, rust and verdigris its visible signatures; the sense gains a scientific, quasi-forensic authority.
- 19th–21st centuries – 'corroded' reads as darkly entropic: an object smaller, blacker, and rougher than it was, consumed slowly by acid, rust, or time itself; in gothic literary use it names the mineral devastation of age — Peake's skull 'dark and small like a corroded musket bullet', the invisible teeth of chemistry working from the surface inward.
