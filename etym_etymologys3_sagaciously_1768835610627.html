sagaciously - the biography of a mind that works as a hound's nose works. The story begins in the tracking animal. The PIE root *seh2g- meant "to track down, to scent out" - the verb of the hound on the trail, and by extension of any perception that works by signs and traces rather than by direct sight. It is an olfactory and forensic root, the grammar of the hunter before the grammar of the scholar: to know is to follow a scent. The Germanic branch of the root became Proto-Germanic *sōkijaną, "to seek": Gothic sōkjan (which also meant "to question, to dispute" - the search as legal and theological inquiry), Old English sēcan (English seek, with its emphatic beseech), and Old Norse saka, "to search", which combined with rann, "house", in rannsaka, "to search a house" - taken into Middle English as ransaken and modern ransack: the search turned violent, the pursuit become pillage. The same root that gave the wise word sagacious also gave the violent word ransack - the search and the sack, the trail followed and the trail raided, two poles of the same tracking instinct. The Latin branch took a different road, and it is this road that leads to sagaciously. Sagīre, "to scent, to perceive keenly", specialized the root in the direction of acuity rather than of pursuit. Latin built from it the adjective sagāx - sagīre with the agentive suffix -āx, the same suffix as in audax, "bold", and rapax, "grasping" - and the word's literal meaning was "keen-scented". Latin writers applied it first to hunting dogs: a sagāx canis is a dog with a fine nose, a dog that can scent its quarry through confusion. The transfer to persons followed naturally: a sagāx homo is a man who reads the signs, who scents out the truth, who perceives keenly what others must be told - the shrewd, the quick-witted, the sagacious. The genius of the transfer is that it kept the epistemology of the hound: the sagacious man does not deduce from premises so much as track from traces; he works by scent, by signs, by the infinitesimal evidence of the trail. From sagāx Latin abstracted sagācitās, "keenness of perception, shrewdness", which reached English through Old French sagacité as sagacity in the sixteenth century - the age when classical wisdom-vocabulary was flooding into English through Latin and French channels. The adjective sagacious was then taken in the early seventeenth century directly from Latin sagāx, and the adverb sagaciously follows at once with the suffix -ly, trailing its noun by a generation. In early modern usage the family was the vocabulary of the sage and the tracker: prudent, far-seeing judgment in statecraft, counsel, and letters, wholly positive and dignified - the adverb of the counsellor and the commander. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century political and philosophical prose uses sagaciously generously, always with the connotation of deep, experienced, sign-reading perception rather than bookish learning. The same Latin root generated a prophecy-branch: praesagīre, "to scent beforehand", whose noun praesagium gave English presage - the omen smelled out in advance, prophecy conceived as super-scenting, the hound of the future. It is worth distinguishing the rival Latin root that never met this one: English sage, "wise", descends not from sagīre but from sapere, "to taste, to know" - the sapient man is the man who tastes experience - so that English happens to possess two wisdom-words from two sensory metaphors, the taster (sapient, sage, savvy) and the sniffer (sagacious), a coincidence of great elegance for a word like this. In the nineteenth century sagaciously receded from everyday speech, surviving in formal and literary registers; its sensory root began to feel archaic - discernment as a trail followed, a hunting metaphor nearly worn smooth. And in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries it has become consciously literary and often ironic: used of shrewdness exercised upon the petty and the trivial, as when Peake's dusting-man peers "sagaciously over his glasses down the length of the hall" - sagacity without an object, a hunter's instinct with nothing left to hunt. The word has completed a full circle: from the keen-scented hound to the wise counsellor and back to the comic-pathetic figure whose shrewdness is magnificent and entirely without consequence, the mind still working as a hound's nose works on a trail that leads nowhere.
