In the source passage — “we bid him consider well the claims that have been urged” — the word is used in its oldest literary sense: to command, direct or earnestly request, followed by a bare infinitive (“bid him consider”, not “bid him to consider”). This is the grand, archaic register of the word, the voice of kings and messengers; modern prose would say “we ask him” or “we require him”, and the command sense now survives mainly in literary, biblical and formulaic use.

Bid is a small word with a remarkably wide modern range. Its principal senses are:

  • To command or order (archaic/literary) — “bid him come”, “do as you are bid”; past tense bade or bid, past participle bidden or bid. The source sentence is a textbook instance of this sense.
  • To utter as a greeting or leave-taking — “bid farewell”, “bid welcome”, “bid good night”; the object is the greeting itself, an extension of the old “proclaim, announce” sense.
  • To offer a price (auctions, commerce) — “she bid £200 for the vase”, “to bid against rivals”; the noun bid is the offer made. This sense descends from the ancient meaning “to offer” and was already current in Middle English.
  • To attempt or strive for something — “to bid for power”, “a bid for the presidency”, “the company's takeover bid”; the noun bid here means a determined attempt, an extension of the auction image to ambition and competition.
  • To declare in card games — in bridge and whist, “to bid” is to state how many tricks one contracts to take; the noun is the declaration, and the auction metaphor is structural to the game.
  • To invite (archaic) — “he bid them to the feast”, a sense surviving in dialect and in the Scots noun bid = invitation (“a bid to the wedding”).
  • To pray (archaic, ecclesiastical) — from Old English biddan “to ask, entreat, pray”, preserved in the “bidding prayer” (the prayer of invitation and intercession in church services) and in the old phrase “to bid one's beads”, i.e. to say the prayers counted on a rosary.

Fixed phrases and derivatives. “To bid fair to do something” (to seem likely, to promise well), “to bid defiance” (to offer defiance), “to outbid / overbid / underbid” (to offer more or less than a rival), “bid up” (to raise the price by successive offers). As a noun the word covers auction offers, contract tenders, take-over attempts, electoral campaigns (“her bid for the nomination”) and even sporting efforts (“a bid for the title”).

The semantic bridge. The word's history is a merger of two Old English verbs — bēodan “to offer, proclaim, command” and biddan “to ask, entreat, pray” — whose forms collided in Middle English. Every modern sense can be traced to one or other of the pair: commanding, greeting and price-offering descend from bēodan; asking, inviting and praying descend from biddan. The modern “attempt” sense is the newest layer, born in the 19th century when the auction noun was extended to any ambitious struggle for a prize.
