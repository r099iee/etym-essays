RAGGED (four-stage Norse-to-English chain; NO SECURE PIE ROOT — stated explicitly): 1. NO PIE STAGE CAN BE RECONSTRUCTED WITH CONFIDENCE: the Germanic base *ragw-/*rag- behind "rag" and "ragged" has no accepted Indo-European etymology. Some scholars connect it with the base behind English "rough" (Proto-Germanic *rūhaz, from PIE *h₁rewk- "to dig up, tear out") and with the "rug/rag" family generally, but the connections are speculative, and the Old Norse word is usually treated as an isolated Germanic item. The attested record therefore begins with Old Norse — the honest point of origin for this word. 2. Old Norse rǫgg (also ragg, genitive raggar) "shaggy tuft, coarse hair, tuft of wool or fur" (Viking Age, attested from ca. 10th–13th c.; the word for the coarse, tufted hair of animals, from a Proto-Germanic *ragw- "tuft, shagginess"). 3. Middle English ragge "a torn scrap or shred of cloth; a ragged piece of cloth" (first attested late 13th c.; borrowed from Old Norse rǫgg, with the shift from "shaggy tuft of hair" to "scrap of torn cloth"); English rag (modern noun) "a torn piece of cloth" is the direct heir of this Middle English word. 4. Middle English ragged(e), an adjective formed from ragge with the participial suffix -ed(e), meaning "rough, shaggy, tufted; worn into tatters, torn and uneven" (first attested 14th c.; the form raggede literally means "having rags, furnished with tatters"). 5. Modern English ragged "of cloth or clothes, old and torn; rough and uneven" (the modern spelling ragged by the 15th–16th c.) — the target word ragged. NOTE ON THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE (stated explicitly): the Old Norse source-word meant "shaggy, tufted, hairy" — the coarse surface of fur or wool — and the transfer to "torn cloth" happens in Middle English because a frayed, torn garment, seen at a distance, has exactly the rough, tufted, uneven outline of a shaggy hide. The modern "ragged" thus carries the animal roughness of its Norse ancestor inside its textile sense, which is why it extends so naturally to ragged rocks, ragged voices, and ragged lines.
