PIE *webh- "to weave; to move to and fro" (Pokorny's dual-sense root: the shuttle's motion on the loom is both an interlacing and a to-and-fro; earliest reconstructable stage) to Proto-Germanic *wab- "to move to and fro, to wave" (ablaut grade of *webh-; 1st millennium BCE) to Middle Low German wabbeln "to move unsteadily to and fro" (North Sea coastal dialect; 14th-15th c.) to English dialect wabble "to move unsteadily" (17th c.) to English wobble (spelling settled in the 19th c.; variant of wabble) to English wobbling "moving unsteadily from side to side" (present participle, used as adjective; the rootWord). Note: the cognate weaving branch - Sanskrit ubhnati "he weaves, binds", Greek hyphaínō "to weave", Proto-Germanic *webaną "to weave" (Old English wefan, whence English weave; Old English webb, whence English web, weft; Old High German weban) - is well attested, and the to-and-fro sense also appears in Old English wafian "to wave, waver" (source of English wave and waver). Some dictionaries, however, treat wobble as an independent echoic (sound-symbolic) formation; the *webh- link, supported by Pokorny's dual-sense root, is the best available account but is not absolutely certain, and the intervening Low German stage is the earliest securely attested ancestor of the English word.
