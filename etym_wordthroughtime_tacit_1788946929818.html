- Late 16th to Early 17th Century - Enters the English language directly from Classical Latin *tacitus* via Renaissance scholasticism, functioning primarily as a descriptive adjective denoting that which is silent, noiseless, or conducted without vocal utterance, frequently appearing in ecclesiastical treatises to describe silent prayer or quiet mental communion.
- 17th to 18th Century - Undergoes profound institutional codification in jurisprudence and political philosophy during the European Enlightenment, most notably through John Locke's formulation of "tacit consent" in his *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), establishing that an individual who accepts the protections and infrastructure of a commonwealth silently confers civic legitimacy without an explicit verbal oath.
- 19th Century - Flourishes across Victorian literature, diplomacy, and high society as an indispensable descriptor for refined social codes, polite discretion, and unspoken agreements, where explicit discussion of money, pedigree, or personal allegiance was considered vulgar and ungentlemanly.
- 20th Century - Expands into formal epistemology, economics, and sociology, most decisively with chemist and philosopher Michael Polanyi's 1958 introduction of "tacit knowledge" ("we can know more than we can tell"), distinguishing intuitive, uncodified human skill from explicit textbook rules.
- Contemporary Era - Firmly entrenched in both legal parlance (tacit approval, tacit collusion) and everyday educated discourse to denote shared complicity, implicit permission, or intuitive comprehension operating entirely beneath the threshold of verbal articulation.
