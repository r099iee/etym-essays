analogous in the source sentence — (If you use a signed language, you know a great deal about speech gestures in an analogous way — a parenthesis from an introductory linguistics textbook explaining that signers understand hearing people's gesturing the way hearers understand signing) — means similar in a way that supports comparison: knowing one thing gives you a parallel, transferable grasp of the other. The signer has not learned speech gestures formally, but signing teaches the body-language logic from which speech-accompanying gestures can be read. The similarity is functional and structural, not identical: hands moving in air mean differently in the two systems, yet the experience of attending to hands transfers. That careful similar-but-not-same is the whole work of analogous.

In general use analogous describes any pair where correspondence in relations, functions, or patterns makes comparison illuminating: analogous situations, analogous cases, analogous organs, analogous arguments, an analogous crisis. It is stronger than vaguely similar and weaker than identical or homologous: it promises that reasoning applied to one case will usefully guide reasoning about the other. Lawyers argue by analogy from analogous precedents, doctors from analogous symptoms, engineers from analogous bridges, linguists from analogous sound changes. To call two things analogous is to license inference across them while warning that differences remain.

  • Core general sense (source sense): comparable, parallel, corresponding in pattern: alike in significant respects so that comparison teaches; e.g. the two revolutions faced analogous shortages, his duties are analogous to a trustee's, learning to sign gives analogous insight into gesture. The bridge is proportion: as A is to its context, so B is to its. Everyday prose uses this sense almost exclusively.
  • Biological-technical sense: similar in function but not in evolutionary origin (opposite of homologous): the wings of insects and of birds are analogous (both fly) but not homologous (different ancestral structures); analogous eyes of octopus and mammal. Since Richard Owen (1840s) and Darwin, biology reserves analogous for convergence — different roads to the same job — versus homologous for common descent. Students must keep the pair straight: analogy is about doing, homology about being kin.
  • Linguistic sense: formed or changed by analogy: analogous levelling, analogous plurals (cow/kine giving way to cows on the analogy of regular -s). Historical linguists call a form analogous when speakers remodel it after a dominant pattern: helped replaces holp because walk/walked is the model. The source sentence belongs to linguistics and flirts with this register: gestural knowledge transfers by lived analogy.
  • Logical-theological sense: known by analogy, neither univocal nor equivocal: medieval schoolmen held that good said of God and of bread is analogous — same direction of meaning, infinitely different mode. Analogous predication, analogia entis (analogy of being) remains live in Catholic philosophy. The bridge is proportionality again: creature to Creator as effect to cause.
  • Legal-chemical-administrative extensions: analogous provisions, analogous offences, analogous compounds (sharing functional group and reactions), analogous posts (same grade, different department). Each field uses analogous to authorise transfer of rules: apply the tested regulation to the new case because relations match.
  • Grammar and collocation: analogous to (most formal, with dative to) versus analogous with (rarer, stressing mutuality). Adverbs grade the claim: roughly, broadly, closely, exactly, strictly analogous. Do not confuse analogous (similar) with analog (non-digital, continuous: analog watch, analog signal), though both descend from Greek analogos (proportionate). One kept the logic, the other kept the mechanism.
  • Synonym boundary: similar is the plain word for any likeness; analogous adds mappable correspondence of relations. Two red balls are similar; a heart and a pump are analogous. Prefer analogous when the comparison does work — predicts, explains, transfers method — not when things merely look alike.

Style is formal-Latinate. analogous belongs to textbooks, judgments, papers, and reports; conversation prefers like, similar, comparable, parallel. Its very formality performs caution: the writer signals awareness that comparison can mislead and claims only proportion, not identity. In the source parenthesis that caution is exactly right — signers know a great deal about gesture, not everything, and the pedagogy depends on admitting both the transfer and the gap.

All senses meet in the Greek image of proportion: ana-logos, up according to ratio, as 2 is to 4 so 3 is to 6. Whether wings, words, gods, or gestures, analogous things stand in matching ratios to their worlds. Learn the ratio once, and the second world opens — in an analogous way.
