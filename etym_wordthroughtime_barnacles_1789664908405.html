- Medieval goose-bird sense - barnacle means the barnacle goose, fabled to hatch from driftwood; perception marvellous, bestiary lore, not yet crustacean.
- Sixteenth-century transfer - sailors notice stalked shellfish on timber resembling embryonic geese and call them barnacles too; John Gerard 1597 popularises the goose-tree myth.
- Seventeenth-eighteenth century split - ornithologists separate bird Branta leucopsis from crustacean Lepas; barnacle for the shellfish shifts from marvel to nuisance fouling hulls.
- Darwinian elevation - Darwin 1851-1854 barnacle monographs make the animal emblematic of patience, variation and taxonomy; connotation industrious natural history.
- Contemporary metaphor - to cling like barnacles or scrape off barnacles denotes sticky accretion of weed, bureaucracy or myth; in Baker criticism it evokes half-truths encrusting an unknowable life.
