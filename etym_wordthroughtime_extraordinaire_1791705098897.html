- 17th to 18th Century: Diplomatic and administrative postposition - Borrowed directly from French royal court protocol for special plenipotentiary missions (e.g., Envoy Extraordinary / Envoyé extraordinaire).
- 19th Century: Victorian aestheticism and literary reviews - English authors and Francophile critics adopted the French spelling postpositively in literary essays to signal continental refinement and artistic mastery.
- Mid-20th Century: Theatrical promotion and popular journalism - Broadened into a widespread idiomatic phrase attached to colorful occupations and showmen (detective extraordinaire, showman extraordinaire, chef extraordinaire).
- Late 20th to 21st Century: Affectionate accolades and everyday mastery - Universally recognized badge of consummate skill and enthusiasm; frequently applied affectionately in book blurbs, eulogies, and lifestyle journalism to domestic arts and specialized vocations.
