- [c. 1300–1400] - Middle English *claspen* / *clapsen* emerges, meaning to fasten garments with brooches or buckles, and quickly expands to embracing or holding a person closely in affection - [connotation: practical garment fastening transitioning into tender courtly and devotional embrace]
- [1500–1650] - Early Modern English standardizes *clasp* across literary, theatrical, and theological texts (Shakespeare, Spenser, King James Bible), establishing "clasped hands" as the definitive posture of prayer, pleading, and romantic devotion - [connotation: elevated emotional intensity, piety, and dramatic supplication]
- [1700–1850] - Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose incorporates the word into antiquarian and romantic contexts, especially descriptions of metal-clasped medieval folios, fine jewelry, and sentimental parting scenes - [connotation: romantic nostalgia, refined craftsmanship, and sincere personal fidelity]
- [1900–Present] - Contemporary English preserves *clasped* as an expressive, high-register descriptor for intimate tactile connection, jewelry fastening, and formal gestures of unity or prayer - [connotation: dignified, poetic warmth and secure mechanical attachment]
