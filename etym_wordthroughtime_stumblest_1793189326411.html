- Early 1300s — the trip gets its name: Middle English stumblen appears for catching the foot, probably from Scandinavian seafarers' speech.
- 14th–15th c. — the pulpit adopts it: preachers make stumbling the image of sin and the stumbling-block the image of temptation.
- 16th–17th c. — thou stumblest: the -est ending rides high in Shakespeare, the prayer book, and the King James Bible, dignifying trips and errors alike.
- 18th–19th c. — the grammar retires: thou and -est fade from speech, surviving in worship, poetry, and folktale translation, where riders still scold horses in them.
- 20th c. — metaphor takes the field: economies, governments, and climbers stumble, while the literal hoof-trip lives on in racing reports.
- Today — the form is costume, the stem is common: stumblest appears only as deliberate archaism, but stumble in all its senses is everyday English.
