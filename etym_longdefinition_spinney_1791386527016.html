Spinney is a chiefly British noun meaning a small wood, copse or thicket. It is the kind of word that belongs to the English countryside of hedgerows, field margins and game coverts: a spinney is smaller than a wood and often denser, scrubby and thorny. The plural is spinneys. In American English the usual equivalent is copse or thicket, and the word is marked as regional or literary.

Primary sense. A spinney is a small area of trees and undergrowth, typically a few dozen metres across, often left at the corner of a field or beside a stream. It may be natural or planted. In traditional rural management a spinney was frequently a covert — a patch of cover planted or preserved so that game birds, foxes or hares would shelter there. The word therefore carries a faint association with field sports and estate management, though most modern users simply mean "a little wood".

Landscape and ecological sense. Spinneys are important features of the English lowland landscape. They provide shelter for livestock and crops, harbour woodland plants and insects, and act as stepping stones for wildlife between larger woods. Many are ancient woodland remnants; others are nineteenth-century plantations of oak, ash, hazel, hawthorn, blackthorn and field maple. The thorny character implied by the etymology is often literal: hawthorn and blackthorn are typical spinney shrubs, and the word historically meant a place full of thorns before it came to mean a small wood.

Literary and regional register. Spinney has a distinctly rural, southern English and slightly archaic flavour. It appears in nature writing, poetry, detective fiction and children's literature, where it often signals a hidden, enclosed, slightly mysterious place. It is not a technical forestry term: foresters speak of copses, coppices, plantations and stands, while spinney belongs to the vocabulary of the field and the village.

Related and contrasted terms.

  • Copse: a small wood, especially one regularly coppiced; broader and more neutral than spinney, and more common in American English.
  • Coppice: a wood managed by cutting stems to the ground so that they regrow; a technical forestry term, whereas spinney is a landscape description.
  • Thicket: a dense mass of shrubs and small trees; closer to the original thorny sense of spinney, but with no implication of a managed small wood.
  • Holt: an old word for a wood or grove, especially one used by an animal such as an otter or a fox; more archaic and more specific than spinney.
  • Grove: a small group of trees, often open and ornamental; spinney is denser and more utilitarian.
  • Covert: a thicket planted as cover for game; functionally close to a spinney, but explicitly linked to hunting.
  • Brake: a thicket or clump of bushes, especially brambles; more tangled and less tree-like.
  • Hurst: an archaic word for a wood or wooded hill; more toponymic and less common in modern prose.

Figurative and extended uses. Spinney is rarely used figuratively, but it can stand for any small, enclosed, slightly overgrown place. In place names it survives in field names and minor toponyms, often spelled Spinney, Spinny or Spiney. The word can also appear in the names of houses and streets, where it evokes a rural idyll rather than an actual wood.

Technical notes. The word is count noun: a spinney, two spinneys. It is not usually used for a large forest, and it implies a bounded, manageable patch rather than wild woodland. In ecological writing, "spinney" may be preferred to "copse" when the emphasis is on dense, thorny undergrowth and game cover. The spelling spinny is a common variant, especially in older texts; the pronunciation is the same.

Why the word survives. Spinney survives because it fills a precise gap: it names a small wood that is neither a garden grove nor a commercial plantation, and it does so with a pleasant, old-fashioned sound. Its thorny etymology is a reminder that many English landscape words began as descriptions of vegetation rather than of land use.
