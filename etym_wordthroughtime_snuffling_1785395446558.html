- Late 16th century - 'snuffle' formed from 'snuff' with a frequentative '-le', an echoic word for drawing air audibly and repeatedly through the nose, usually of someone with a congested nose.
- 17th-18th century - extends to animals, especially hounds snuffling along the ground while hunting by scent; the wet, laborious, investigative sound becomes associated with tracking and pursuit.
- 18th-19th century - a social side-sense: to speak in a nasal, affected or whining tone ('a snuffling voice'), often used of preachers and pious talk; the word could mock as well as describe.
- 20th century - 'the snuffles' becomes a homely word for the common cold; literary use (as in Tolkien) leans on the animal sense — iterative, prolonged, predatory nasal searching that turns a pack's hunt into an intimate, patient menace.
- Modern - still strongly physical and onomatopoeic; in narrative prose it is rarely neutral, carrying a predatory or congested, faintly comic undertone.
