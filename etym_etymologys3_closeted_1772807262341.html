1. The fastening root. At the bottom of closeted lies a reconstructed Indo-European root meaning "fasten, bar, lock": PIE *kleh₂u- / *klāu- "hook, peg, nail; instrument for fastening", the same stock that underlies Latin clāvis "key", Greek kleíō "to close", and, in the view of many scholars, Germanic words for closing and locking. The core image is a barrier secured by a bolt or a hook: what is shut is sealed. This is worth stressing, because the whole later history of the word is the history of that sealing — first of doors, then of rooms, then of conversations, and finally of identities. 2. Latin claudere and its family. - Latin claudere "to shut, close, bar, enclose" is attested from early Latin onward (Plautus, 2nd c. BCE). Its participle clausus "closed" and noun clausum "enclosed place" fed a large derivative family: claustrum "bar, enclosure" (source of cloister and of the combining form in claustrophobia), and the compounds English knows as conclude, exclude, include and recluse. - Sound note: the root's -au- (claudere) and the participle stem claus- show the classic Latin alternation; in Gallo-Romance the diphthong later monophthongizes, preparing the French clore/clos forms. The cluster -s- in clausus is the "closed" state made grammatical. 3. Old French: from verb to noun to diminutive. - Old French clore "to close" and its past participle clos "closed; enclosure, enclosed space" were highly productive from the 9th–12th centuries, giving words for walls, gardens, monasteries and any shut place. - The diminutive closet (from clos + -et, "little enclosed place") first meant a small private room: a study, an oratory, a withdrawing chamber — not a cupboard. This architectural sense was borrowed into Middle English around 1330, at a time when manor houses were beginning to multiply small private chambers for prayer and study. 4. English: the private chamber. - In medieval and early modern English, a closet was a small room for study, prayer or confidential business — the king's "closet" where he met his most trusted advisers, the lady's closet where letters were read and secrets kept. It was the architectural counterpart of what we would call a private office or cabinet. - From the noun, English formed the verb to closet, first recorded 1604: to shut someone up in a closet. The past participle closeted thus described the state of being shut away, and "to be closeted with X" became the idiom for a private interview among the powerful — exactly the configuration the translation of the novel uses for Lady Wang and Wang Xi-feng. 5. Semantic drift: from room to conference to concealment. - Political and literary English used "closeted with" of ministers, generals and lovers: enclosure became the architecture of trust and intrigue. In the 17th and 18th centuries the phrase is a fixture of court memoirs and novels of intrigue. - As the physical closet shrank in later domestic English (in American usage it became chiefly a storage cupboard), the word's figurative life expanded. To be "in the closet" came, in the 20th century, to mean concealing one's sexual orientation or other hidden identity — the seal of the old fastening root reapplied to the self. - Bullets on the drift: closed door → private room → secret conversation → concealed identity. The conference sense and the identity sense coexist today, both descending from the image of a shut little room: the first hides a conversation, the second hides a person. 6. The passage and the word's full arc. In Hawkes's translation, Lady Wang is found "closeted with Wang Xi-feng, deep in discussion of a letter which had just arrived from Nanking", with the two women present as messengers. The word stages high-level Rong-guo management: enclosure excludes the household, admits only trusted interlocutors and their carriers, and marks the discussion as confidential council rather than leisure. Etymologically the two women are inside a "little enclosure" descended from Latin shutting and PIE fastening — the barrier that once barred a door now screens a family's response to Nanking news, and the same root will later screen a person's whole identity. Few words carry so cleanly, from hook and bolt to private room to secret self.
