• waves: Similar because both are undulations of a liquid surface; differs because waves are larger, energy-driven, often wind-blown and breaking, while ripples are small, delicate, surface-tension-governed and local.

  • wavelets: Similar because both name small waves; differs because “wavelet” is a technical, self-conscious term (also the basis of wavelet mathematics), whereas “ripple” is the everyday word and adds the concentric, spreading image.

  • undulations: Similar because both denote gentle rise-and-fall motion; differs because “undulation” is formal and describes any continuous wavy motion (of land, of snakes, of melody) without the water-specific ring.

  • swell: Similar because both are movements of water; differs because a swell is a long, broad, slow wave travelling from distant storms — far larger and deeper, the opposite of a local ripple.

  • billow: Similar because both describe water in motion; differs because “billow” names a great rolling mass of water, smoke or cloud, with a scale and drama ripples deliberately lack.

  • breakers: Similar because both are waves; differs because breakers are crashing, surf-pounding crests on a shore, violent and gravity-driven; a ripple never breaks.

  • surge: Similar because both involve liquid movement; differs because a surge is a powerful, sudden forward rush; ripples are weak, gentle and repeated.

  • dimples: Similar because both are small surface disturbances (raindrop dimples on water); differs because dimples are static dents (as in cheeks), not travelling waves.

  • crinkles / wrinkles: Similar because both name fine wavy lines; differs because wrinkles fold a surface and stay put, while ripples propagate across it and vanish again.

  • wake: Similar because both are water disturbance patterns left behind a moving object; differs because a wake is the linear V-shaped track of a vessel — energetic and directional, not concentric and gentle.

  • ripple effect: Similar because it is the modern figurative extension of the same image; differs because as a set phrase it denotes consequences spreading through society, markets or media rather than water at all.
