- Middle English greevously / grevously — perceived as “heavily, painfully, oppressively”; still close to the image of a weight laid on body or spirit; used of afflictions, sins, and harsh conditions rather than casual annoyance.
- Late medieval religious prose — becomes a preferred intensifier for moral failure and divine offense (“sinned grievously”); public perception links the word with solemn confession, penance, and spiritual danger, not mere inconvenience.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — flourishes in legal, medical-ish, and tragic diction: grievously wounded, grievously offended, grievous bodily harm in older legal phrasing; audiences hear it as elevated, courtroom- or pulpitserious English.
- 18th–19th c. narrative and journalism — standard marker of severe injury, injustice, or calamity in histories and novels; slightly archaic flavor already emerging beside plainer intensifiers, which makes it useful for dignified pathos.
- 20th–21st c. — relatively rare in informal speech; when used, it signals formal, literary, or ironic-grand style. Modern readers often hear an emotional and moral overtone (grief + gravity) even when the collocation is purely physical (“grievously injured”). Perception: not just “very much,” but “with tragic weight.”
