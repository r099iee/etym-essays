- 14th c. - Entered Middle English (*chafen*) from Old French, initially meaning to make warm, heat over a fire, or rub cold limbs to restore circulation.
- 15th c. - Developed the physical sense of wearing away, inflaming, or rubbing skin raw through continuous mechanical friction.
- 16th–17th c. - Shifted metaphorically into psychological registers (prominently in Shakespeare), meaning to grow heated in temper, provoked, or indignant under opposition.
- 18th–19th c. - Specialized in the imagery of restraint (e.g. *chafing at the bit*), capturing restless impatience and irritation against boundaries, bureaucracy, or delays.
- 20th–21st c. - Preserved in dual capacity: dermatological/mechanical friction (chafed skin, chafed ropes) and emotional vexation at constraints (*chafed at the rules*).
