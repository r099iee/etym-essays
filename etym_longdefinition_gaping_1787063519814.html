Gaping is the present participle of the verb gape, used both as an adjective and as part of verb phrases. As an adjective it means "wide open," describing an opening so broad — and so striking — that it seems to gape or yawn at you. In the source passage the dwarves pass "through the gaping doors": the great doors of the Mountain stand fully open, a dark mouth in the rock, and the word catches both the physical width of the opening and the eerie invitation of it, so that the travellers look back "with longing" as they leave.

Core sense — a wide opening. A gaping hole, wound, mouth, crack, or door is one stretched so wide that the opening is immediately visible and often alarming. The word is frequently applied to things that should be closed: a gaping wound (flesh split open), a gaping void, a gaping flaw or hole in an argument or story, a gaping doorway on a dark night. The implication is openness beyond the normal or expected — an exposure of what lies within.

  • Of the mouth and face. "Gaping" describes a mouth hanging open — from sleep, shock, wonder, or foolishness. A "gaping idiot" is one whose mouth hangs stupidly open; to be "left gaping" is to be struck speechless with astonishment. Yawns, gasps of alarm, and greedy open-mouthed staring are all "gaping."
  • Of the body and the natural world. A gaping wound or a gaping maw (an animal's wide-open mouth) carries a violent or predatory edge; gaping chasms and gorges dominate landscape writing, the word-family having also produced gap itself. A gaping cavity in a tooth, a gaping fissure in rock, and the gaping beaks of nestlings all belong to this sense.
  • Figurative use. By easy metaphor, anything conspicuously absent or incomplete "gapes": a gaping hole in the budget, a gaping absence in the record, a story with a gaping inconsistency. Here the opening is not physical but logical or structural, and "gaping" stresses how unmissable the deficiency is.
  • Verb senses. To gape is also to stare with the mouth open in wonder ("the crowd gaped at the juggler"), and in older English to gape for or after something was to long or crave for it — a sense preserved in phrases like "gaping for news" and in the noun yawn's near-twin. The participle sits inside these verb phrases: "she stood gaping," "they were left gaping."
  • Register. Gaping is vivid, slightly dramatic, and more specific than plain "open": it insists on width and on the act of opening, and it usually carries a hint of danger, emptiness, or astonishment. In medical writing it is clinical (a gaping wound needing stitches); in fiction it is atmospheric (a gaping doorway swallowing the light).
  • Collocations. The word clings to a small set of favourite nouns — gaping hole, gaping wound, gaping mouth, gaping void, gaping doors/gates, gaping maw — and learners of English are best served by learning these collocations as wholes, since the adjective rarely strays beyond them.

Historical bridge. Gape entered Middle English from Old Norse gapa, "to open the mouth wide," an expressive word probably imitative of the wide-mouthed action it names. The noun gap — a hole or break — comes from the same source, so the idea of an open space and the act of opening the mouth are, at bottom, the same word-family, and both words have grown rich modern lives.
