cup (noun) — A small, open, typically bowl-shaped or hemispherical container designed primarily for holding and drinking liquids, usually equipped with a single curved handle and crafted from glazed ceramic, porcelain, stoneware, glass, or metal. Across centuries of technological and semantic evolution, the word has expanded far beyond domestic tableware to designate hollow concave receptacles, natural botanical calyxes, standardized units of volumetric measurement, athletic championship trophies, and specialized medical or protective equipment.

Primary sense — domestic drinking vessel and ritual beverage culture. In domestic life and dining traditions, a cup is an indispensable vessel for hot or cold beverages. The modern teacup and coffee cup, typically accompanied by a matching saucer, evolved in tandem with the global spread of tea, coffee, and chocolate in the 17th and 18th centuries. Unlike a mug (which is typically heavier, taller, and cylindrical), a cup generally tapers downward to a narrow foot. In historical and religious anthropology, the cup functions as the central vessel of communal fellowship, hospitality, and sacred ritual—from the ancient Greek symposiastic kylix to the Christian Eucharistic chalice and the mythical quest for the Holy Grail.

Sense 2 — botanical cupule and hollow plant structures. In botany and plant morphology, cup designates any concave, cup-shaped involucre or receptacle that encloses or supports a fruit, flower, or seed. Most famously—and directly matching the literary context of the oak tree—the woody, scaly base that clasps and anchors an acorn is universally called its cup (or botanically, its cupule). In mycology, the fruiting bodies of ascomycete fungi belonging to the order Pezizales are commonly designated cup fungi due to their flared, basin-like apothecia that catch raindrops to disperse reproductive spores. Similarly, the fused calyx of certain floral blossoms is routinely characterized in botanical keys as a cup.

Sense 3 — culinary standard and volumetric measurement. In cooking, baking, and recipe composition, a cup is a standardized unit of volume. In the United States customary measurement system, one cup equals 8 fluid ounces, 16 tablespoons, or approximately 236.6 milliliters. In the metric systems adopted by Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and the United Kingdom, a metric cup is defined as exactly 250 milliliters. The ubiquity of the measurement stems from historic domestic kitchen practices, where unstandardized household drinking vessels were used as rough proportional ratios before precision measuring tools were mass-produced.

Sense 4 — athletic championship trophies and competitive silverware. In organized sports and international tournaments, a cup is a prestigious ornamental vessel—typically fabricated from sterling silver, gold, or bronze with ornate handles and an engraved pedestal—awarded to the champion team or individual. Iconic global examples include the FIFA World Cup, the America's Cup in yachting, the Davis Cup in tennis, and the Stanley Cup in professional ice hockey. By metonymy, cup has shifted from designating the physical trophy itself to signifying the entire tournament or championship competition (e.g., "competing in the World Cup"), rooted in the ancient European custom of drinking celebratory wine from a shared victory loving-cup.

Sense 5 — sports protection, anatomy, and surgical orthopedics. In specialized protective athletic gear, a cup (also termed an athletic cup) is a rigid, contoured pelvic shield made of hard plastic, polycarbonate, or carbon fiber inserted into a jockstrap or compression shorts to protect male genitalia from high-impact impacts in sports such as baseball, hockey, lacrosse, and cricket. In human anatomy and medicine, the term identifies concave structures, such as the optic cup (the central depression of the optic disc evaluated in ophthalmology for glaucoma) and the acetabular cup (the hemispherical prosthetic socket implanted into the pelvis during total hip arthroplasty).

Sense 6 — figurative, biblical, and idiomatic expressions. In literature, theology, and colloquial idiom, cup serves as a profound metaphor for one's destined lot, fortune, or emotional experience in life. In biblical literature, it embodies both divine abundance (Psalm 23: "my cup runneth over") and divine wrath or suffering (Christ in Gethsemane: "let this cup pass from me"). In modern colloquial English, the phrase "not my cup of tea" denotes something outside one's personal preference or temperament, while to be "in one's cups" signifies drunkenness.

  • In botany and mycology — acorn cup, cupule, cup fungus: characterizes concave involucres clasping nuts or saucer-like fungal fruiting bodies.
  • In culinary measurements — measuring cup, dry cup, liquid cup: standard volume units essential for recipe ratios across domestic and commercial kitchens.
  • In sports and athletics — cup final, cup winner, protective cup: refers either to high-stakes championship silverware or impact-resistant genital protection.
  • In medical anatomy and surgery — optic cup, acetabular cup, cupping therapy: describes natural concavities in tissue or spherical prosthetic implants.
  • In idiomatic discourse — cup of tea, cup of sorrow, in one's cups: expresses personal affinity, tragic destiny, or festive inebriation.

Register, tone, and semantic versatility. Cup is an ancient core noun of Germanic and Latinate heritage displaying extraordinary polysemy. It transitions seamlessly from intimate domestic breakfasts and kitchen measurements to high liturgical sanctity, grand international athletic triumphs, and surgical precision.
