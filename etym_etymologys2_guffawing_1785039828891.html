No PIE ancestry: guffaw is a Scots dialect coinage built from sound-imitation, not inherited from an older root. → Scots gawf "a loud, noisy laugh; to laugh loudly" (early 16th century; the first recorded ancestor) → Scots guff "a puff of breath or wind; foul air; a whiff" (dialect word whose imagery of an expelled breath cross-influenced the formation; c. 16th century) → Scots guffaw (noun) "a loud, coarse, boisterous burst of laughter" (1720) → Scots guffaw (verb) "to laugh loudly and coarsely" (1721) → Scottish English → Standard English guffaw "a sudden, loud, coarse laugh" (adopted during the 18th–19th centuries) → Modern English guffawing "laughing boisterously and coarsely, in repeated open-mouthed bursts" (present participle) Note on the debate: OED and Etymonline agree that the word is imitative in origin — the syllables gu-ff-aw mimic the deep, hollow, breathy peal of the laugh itself — so there is no Proto-Indo-European etymon to reconstruct and no chain of inherited sound shifts to record; the word was freshly coined in the mouths of Scots speakers. The relation between gawf, guff, and guffaw is one of sound-symbolic cross-influence rather than strict derivation, and the exact path by which gawf became guffaw is partly inferential. The English words cackle, chortle (Carroll, 1871), and snort are independent imitative coinages arising from the same impulse to echo laughter and are NOT related to guffaw by descent.
