Gravel is in its primary modern sense a loose accumulation of small rounded stones and pebbles — the water-worn fragments of rock, roughly pea- to plum-sized, that pave garden paths, ballast railways, bed roads, and floor the Thames terraces Cobbett rode across. In the source sentence it does double duty in a single breath: the soil is a gravel at bottom, the hungry drift subsoil of Middlesex, and a sort of spewy gravel further back, the same drift waterlogged and oozing.

Core meaning in detail: Geologists draw the line by size: sand grains below 2 mm, gravel from 2 to about 64 mm, cobbles above. River gravel like the Thames terrace drift is rounded by tumbling; pit gravel is dug from ancient beds; crushed stone is quarried and broken but sold under the same homely name. Its virtues are drainage, cheapness, and honesty underfoot — the crunch that announces a visitor — and its vices, in Cobbett's farmer eyes, are hunger and cold: gravel subsoil starves crops and chills roots, which is why Middlesex market-gardeners prized the black loam cap and cursed what lay beneath.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Loose small stones, aggregate (primary, from c. 1300): path gravel, driveway gravel, railway ballast. The bridge is the word's birth itself — Old French gravele, little stones of the seashore, carried inland to every road and garden.
  • Gravel disease — kidney or bladder stones (medical, 14th–19th c., now archaic): he is troubled with the gravel. The bridge is likeness: the gritty urinary concretions look and feel like tiny pebbles, so physicians named the ailment for the mason's heap.
  • To gravel — to lay or dress with gravel (verb, 16th c.): to gravel a walk. The bridge is straightforward denominal formation: to furnish a surface with the material, as one paints with paint.
  • To gravel — to perplex, nonplus, bring to a standstill (verb, 16th–19th c., now archaic): Shakespeare's Biron is gravelled for lack of matter. The bridge is the stranded ship: a vessel run aground on a gravel bank sticks fast and helpless, and so does a mind run out of answers — embarrassment as shipwreck.
  • Gravel-voiced, gravel-blind, and compounds (figurative fringe): a voice harsh as grating stones. The bridge is texture carried into metaphor: roughness of stone becomes roughness of sound or temper.
  • Fishermen's gravel — spawning beds (technical): the clean stones where trout and salmon lay eggs. The bridge is habitat: anglers and biologists name the redd by its material, the stones the fish themselves chose.

Register and connotation: gravel is plain, humble, workaday English — the vocabulary of gardeners, builders, and road-menders rather than poets, though poets borrow its crunch when they want honesty. Cobbett wields it as geology-as-moral-judgment: gravel bottom means thin stingy country, whatever golden coaches roll over it toward London. The medical and perplexity senses survive only in period novels and Shakespeare footnotes, giving the word a small archaic halo around its outer modern core of paths and drives.
