Naiads (singular naiad; from Greek Nāïádes, "the flowing ones") are, in their primary and oldest sense, the minor female nature-spirits of ancient Greek and Roman mythology who presided over fresh water — springs, fountains, wells, streams, rivers, and pools. They belong to the broader class of Greek nymphs (Greek nymphē, "young woman, bride"): beautiful, long-lived yet not strictly immortal female divinities of a lower order than the Olympian gods, worshipped locally at the particular waters they inhabited. Greek writers subdivided the Naiads by habitat into the Pēgaîai and Krēnaîai of springs and fountains, the Potamēídes of rivers, and the Limnādes / Limnatides of lakes and marshy pools. This was not mere ornament: to drink, bathe, or draw water from a spring was to enter into a relationship with its presiding nymph, and Greek cult honoured her with offerings of milk, oil, honey, or cut hair left at the water's edge, hoping for health, fertility, and prophecy and dreading her anger — which could bring barrenness, madness, or a wasting disease to those who polluted her stream. The cultic reality underlies the literary image, and it is why the word never entirely loses its sense of a particular, localised, living water.

Boundary distinctions are precise in the ancient system. The Nereids are the fifty daughters of the old sea-god Nereus, nymphs of the open Mediterranean who ride with dolphins; the Oceanids are the daughters of Oceanus, the great earth-encircling river; the Dryads and Hamadryads are the tree-nymphs, born with their tree and dying with it; and the Oreads haunt the mountains. A Naiad, by contrast, is bound to fresh flowing water — the spring, fountain, well, stream, river, or pool. The fixed pairing "Naiads and Dryads" (as in C. S. Lewis's passage, "Naiads and Dryads they were called") is so established in English poetry that it reads almost as a single compound idea: the spirits of the water and the wood, the two witnesses of a world in which every feature of the landscape is alive.

In the passage from The Voyage of the Dawn Treader the word does distinctly literary work: Caspian recalls the lost golden age when "all the animals could talk, and there were nice people who lived in the streams and the trees." Here "Naiads" carries a nostalgic, prelapsarian, half-mythic weight — gentle, personable "nice people" rather than dangerous or ambiguous divinities, guardians of the streams whose companionship was lost when the Telmarines and their blunt, rational world took over Narnia. The plural, capitalised, signals enchanted and innocent nature: water as a presence you could love and be loved by.

Secondary modern senses (technical jargon) show the ancient name colonising the sciences:

  • Entomology — the naiad stage: the aquatic nymph stage of dragonflies, damselflies, mayflies, and stoneflies, so named in the 19th century because, like the mythological nymph, it lives in the water before emerging as the winged adult.
  • Malacology (informal) — "naiads": the large fresh-water mussels of the family Unionidae, again named for their fresh-water habitat.
  • Astronomy — Naiad: a small inner moon of Neptune, discovered in 1989 and named for the nymph, continuing the tradition of naming Neptunian moons for water-deities.
  • Literary and figurative use — "a naiad" can name any graceful female water-spirit or, by extension, a woman swimming or bathing gracefully (e.g., "the pool's resident naiads"), the mythological sense transferred to human swimmers.

The word has thus moved from cult title, to classical poetry, to English learned borrowing, to scientific nomenclature, to a metaphor for graceful swimmers — all flowing from one root image of "flowing." Naiads on this card is the English plural (naiad + -s).
