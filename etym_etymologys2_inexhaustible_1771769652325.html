INEXHAUSTIBLE is built on Latin exhaurire "to draw out, to drain," from the root *h₂eus- "to draw water" (the verb haurire), with the privative prefix in- "not." The chain runs from the PIE root of drawing water through the Latin verb of draining to the English negative adjective. Chain: 1. PIE *h₂eus- "to draw (water), to scoop" (reconstructed; attested in Latin haurire "to draw, to drain," and related to Greek aúō "to draw water" and Tocharian *was-; the root denotes the motion of scooping or drawing liquid; per LIV, de Vaan) — the root of drawing water. 2. Latin haurire "to draw (water), to drink in, to drain" (attested from early Latin; the sense "to drain dry" is developed in classical use, e.g. Vergil, 1st c. BCE) — the simple verb of drawing water. 3. Latin exhaurire "to draw out, to empty, to drain dry, to exhaust" (ex- "out" + haurire; attested classical, e.g. in Livy, Cicero, 1st c. BCE–CE) — "to draw the water out of," hence "to use up entirely." 4. Latin exhaustus "drained, emptied, used up" (past participle of exhaurire; attested classical) — the state of having been drawn dry. 5. English exhaust "to draw out, to drain, to use up completely" (borrowed from Latin exhaustus via past participle, first attested c. 1540; the sense "to drain a supply" is present from the start) — the verb that supplies the stem. 6. English exhaustible "that may be drained or used up" (formed in English with the suffix -ible, 17th c.) — the positive adjective. 7. English in- + exhaustible → INEXHAUSTIBLE "that cannot be exhausted, that cannot be used up" (privative in- "not" attached to exhaustible; first attested 1600s, e.g. 1610s in poetry and prose describing resources and energies that cannot be drained) — the final node of the chain. Note on debated stages: the PIE root of haurire is reconstructed as *h₂eus- on the evidence of Greek and Latin, but the wider cognate set is thin, and some scholars treat haurire as of uncertain origin or connect it instead to *h₂ewh₁- "to wet"; the Latin and English stages themselves are securely attested and uncontroversial. The English formation of the adjective is transparent (in- + exhaustible) and needs no intermediate stage; no stage above is invented.
