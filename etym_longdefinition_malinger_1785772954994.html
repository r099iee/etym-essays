MALINGER — verb meaning "to feign illness, injury, or incapacity in order to avoid work, duty, or responsibility; to pretend to be sick or disabled in order to shirk." The malingerer is not genuinely ill (or is grossly exaggerating a real complaint); the pretense is deliberate and is aimed at escaping obligation. Core sense: feigned incapacity to avoid duty. The central meaning is the conscious simulation of sickness, injury, weakness, or disability for an external purpose — chiefly avoiding work, military duty, exams, punishment, or responsibility. It differs from genuine illness in that the sufferer is not actually incapacitated, and it differs from hypochondria in that the motive is not fear of disease but the concrete gain of being excused. In the card's sentence — "When the time came to haul the heavy cargo up the mountain, several crew members tried to __________ by complaining of sudden back pain" — the context is prototypical: the symptom appears precisely when hard, unwanted work is required, which is the signature of malingering. Polysemy and usage spread. The verb operates across several connected contexts:
  • Military: feigning illness or injury to avoid drills, combat, fatigue duties, or deployment — the historical home of the word; "to malinger" is a charge in military discipline.
  • Employment and labor: faking sickness or incapacity to avoid work, take leave, or claim benefits — a classic management and insurance concern; "sick-leave malingering."
  • Medical and forensic: in medicine, deliberately producing or exaggerating symptoms for external gain (money, drugs, disability benefits, avoidance of work); distinguished from factitious disorder and somatization — a technical psychiatric and legal category.
  • School and social: pretending illness to avoid classes, tests, or obligations; a shirking of responsibility — a common everyday application.
  • Figurative / hyperbolic: (informal, rare) to dawdle or avoid effort under a flimsy excuse — the word is sometimes stretched to any lazy dodging, though its strict sense keeps the medical pretense.
The unifying idea in every sense is the conscious pretense of incapacity as a strategy of avoidance: the person performs sickness for gain. The word therefore always carries an accusation: it asserts that the claimed suffering is not genuine. Register and stylistic nuance. Formal and slightly technical; standard in military, medical, legal, and insurance discourse, and used in literary and journalistic prose when the writer wants the precise, damning term. It is stronger and more precise than plain shirk (avoid duty without pretending illness) or skive (British informal, avoid work by any sly means). The noun forms are malingerer (the person) and malingering (the act; also the clinical category). The tone is condemnatory — the word exists to expose an act of deception. Word boundaries and near-synonyms.
  • Shirk — both mean avoiding duty; shirk need not involve any pretense of illness (one can shirk by disappearing, delaying, or passing work on), while malinger specifically scripts the sickness performance.
  • Feign — both mean to pretend; feign is the general verb of pretense (feign sleep, feign interest) without the medical-dutiful motive, while malinger specializes feigning to incapacity for the sake of avoidance.
  • Simulate / fake — both mean to produce a false appearance; simulate is clinical and neutral (simulate symptoms), while malinger adds the pejorative accusation of avoiding duty.
  • Goldbrick (US informal) — both mean to dodge work; goldbrick is slang for loafing or shirking generally, not necessarily by faking illness, while malinger is the formal word for the sick-pretense variant.
  • Skive (BrE informal) — both mean avoiding work; skive covers any sly avoidance and is casual, while malinger is formal and specifically medical-fraud in nature.
  • Dodge / evade — both mean to avoid something; dodge and evade stress clever avoidance of duty or pursuit, without the staged sickness that defines malingering.
Grammar and derivatives. Intransitive verb, malinger / malingers / malingered / malingering; usually used absolutely (to malinger) or with a purpose clause (malinger to avoid X). Agent noun malingerer; abstract/gerund malingering (used as a clinical and forensic category). The word is a borrowing from French malingre "sickly, ailing" (adjective), which English converted into a verb meaning "to act sickly"; the semantic shift from "being sickly" to "pretending to be sickly" occurred in English military usage of the 18th century.
