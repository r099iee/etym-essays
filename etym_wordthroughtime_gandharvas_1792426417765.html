- [Vedic Era (c. 1500–1000 BCE)] — introduced in the Ṛgveda primarily as a singular, mysterious atmospheric deity (*Gandharva*) guarding the celestial Soma in the heavenly waters and possessing erotic influence over women — [perception: regarded with awe and caution as an esoteric, liminal guardian of divine secrets]
- [Late Vedic & Upaniṣadic Era (c. 900–500 BCE)] — pluralized into a distinct celestial order (*gandharvāḥ*) associated with sweet fragrances and music, while in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad the bliss of a gandharva (*gandharva-ānanda*) became a quantified benchmark for measuring transcendent spiritual bliss — [perception: revered as celestial embodiments of refined aesthetic pleasure and otherworldly happiness]
- [Epic & Classical Sanskrit Age (c. 500 BCE–500 CE)] — fully canonized in the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata as the court musicians of Indra's heaven, paired with apsarases, and established in legal codes (Manusmṛti) as the namesake of romantic consensual marriage (*gāndharva-vivāha*) — [perception: celebrated as the universal cultural epitome of musical mastery, courtly romance, and radiant masculine beauty]
- [Buddhist & Jain Canonical Period (c. 4th c. BCE–8th c. CE)] — integrated into Buddhist cosmology as fragrance-dwelling spirits under King Dhṛtarāṣṭra, while in Theravāda Abhidhamma *gandhabba* became a technical term for the intermediate entity seeking rebirth in the womb — [perception: adapted into philosophical taxonomies of consciousness and lower-tier celestial rebirth]
- [Modern Era (19th–21st c.)] — adopted in comparative Indo-European mythology (comparing Vedic *gandharva* with Greek *kentauros*), while in South Asian classical performing arts the term survives with high honor in the prestigious titles of master musicians (e.g., the title *Kumar Gandharva* or *Gandharva Mahavidyalaya*) — [perception: respected as the ultimate classical emblem of vocal and instrumental virtuosity in Indian music]
