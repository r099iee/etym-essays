A WORD THAT ENTERED ENGLISH IN THE AGE OF BOTANY

Fronds enters English late and formally. The word was taken up in eighteenth-century botany — first recorded in 1784 — when English naturalists needed a name for fern leaves: organs so unlike the leaves of flowering plants, whole and rolled and divided into leaflets, leaf and stem in one, that a new term was required. The model was Latin frōns, genitive frondis, "leaf, foliage, leafy branch" — the word the Romans used for the greenness of trees and for the leafy boughs gathered for garlands — and English took the noun straight from botanical Latin, whose writers had preserved and specialised the old word. The plural fronds entered the scientific register of the language in the age of Linnaeus, when taxonomy was inventing names for every part of every plant.

THE LATIN WORD AND ITS LOST PAST

The Latin word itself is of uncertain origin, and this is one of the rare cases where a European word resists Indo-European reconstruction. Modern authorities — de Vaan in his etymological dictionary of Latin, and Etymonline after him — record no securely reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root for frōns and no certain cognates in Greek or Germanic: the noun appears to be native Italic stock whose deeper ancestry is simply lost, an isolate within the Indo-European family. Older attempts to connect it with Greek words for flowers, or to force it onto a PIE root of breaking or budding, have been rejected. What can be said with confidence is that Latin built a small family from the stem frond-:

  • frondēre "to be leafy" and its inchoative frondēscere "to put forth leaves", giving English frondescence, "the season of leafing";
  • frondōsus "leafy, full of leaves", giving the English botanical adjective frondose;
  • and the noun itself, which late Latin reformed as fronda "leafy branch", passing into French, Italian and Spanish — though the French fronde that means "sling" is a different word from Latin funda.

HOMONYMS AND FALSE FRIENDS

Two lookalikes must be kept apart. Latin frōns, frontis, "forehead, brow", is a completely different word with an unrelated history — it is the source of English front, forehead and frontier, and it has nothing to do with foliage. And French fronde "a sling" comes from Latin funda, not from frons/frondis. The two frons nouns of Latin, one "foliage" and one "forehead", happen to share a nominative spelling but descend from separate sources, a coincidence that has misled etymologists for centuries.

FROM HERBARIUM TO POETRY

In English the word's career has two registers. First, the scientific: botanists use frond for the leaf of any fern, palm, cycad and, by extension, for the leaf-like blades of large seaweeds — a kelp has fronds as readily as a fern. Second, the literary: because the word came in learned, late and slightly foreign, it kept a formal, poetic aura. In the 19th century fronded became a favourite of Romantic nature poetry, and in literary prose fronds imply scale, strangeness and something primeval — foliage older than flowers, foliage that dwarfs the observer. It is precisely this double life that Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit: fronds that rise "right above the hobbit's head" are scientifically exact (fern fronds can stand two metres high) and at the same time poetically overwhelming — pillars in a primeval nave, the perfect word for a forest about to close over the travellers.

SOCIOCULTURAL NOTE

Fronds carries within it the history of the word itself being a specialist's borrowing: it entered English not from the mouths of farmers but from the pages of botanical books, and its learned flavour is part of its meaning to this day. Where "leaf" is homely, "frond" is a little exotic; where "leaf" names the everyday, "frond" names the ancient and the tropical. The word's brevity and its plural ending, fronds (rhyming with "ponds" after the r), give it a crisp, almost metallic sound that suits the rustling stiffness of the plants it names.

SUMMARY

A Latin word of unknown origin — an Italic isolate with no secure Indo-European past — was rescued by botanical Latin, borrowed into English in 1784, and has since done double duty as the scientist's precise term and the poet's word for primeval greenery, from fern-forest floors to the fronds that tower over a hobbit's head.
