- Bronze Age Aegean — the purple sea-snail: porphyra names the murex shell and its costly dye, purple as power itself.
- Classical Greece — purple everything: porphyreos describes cloth, blood-dark seas, and at last the purple-speckled stone from Egypt's desert.
- Rome — the emperor's stone: imperial porphyry paves palaces and coffins Caesars; to be born in the purple is literally to be born in a porphyry-lined chamber.
- Middle Ages — sacred spoil: emperors and popes reuse Rome's porphyry columns and sarcophagi, and the stone becomes sanctity made visible.
- 18th c. — the geologist's texture: porphyritic widens from the purple stone to every two-crystal igneous rock, from Cornwall to the Andes.
- Today — emperors and engineers: art historians mean imperial purple by porphyry while miners mean copper ore, and fairy tales like the source build its grandest palace of it.
