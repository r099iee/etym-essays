weak-kneed means lacking courage, firmness or resolve; feeble, cowardly, easily intimidated or swayed, especially in facing difficulty, danger or moral choice. Literally it pictures knees that buckle instead of locking, and figuratively a person who cannot stand firm.

Core sense is moral-political: a weak-kneed leader, government, response, or friend yields when pressure comes, compromises principle, avoids confrontation. In the source — considered it weak-kneed and unseemly to preserve food — it means unmanly, timid, beneath warrior dignity: thrift is recast as fearfulness. Modern use is similar: a weak-kneed apology, weak-kneed defence, weak-kneed liberals/conservatives (term used by opponents across the spectrum). It always implies disappointment: strength was expected, weakness appeared.

  • 1. Cowardly, faint-hearted: Afraid to act. He was too weak-kneed to complain. Synonymous with lily-livered in this sense, but weak-kneed stresses buckling under weight rather than white liver.
  • 2. Irresolute, vacillating, spineless in policy: A weak-kneed compromise, weak-kneed reform. Here fear is less relevant than lack of backbone, pandering, sentimentality. Political journalism's favourite insult.
  • 3. Feeble, infirm, literally unsteady on the knees: Rare literal use: weak-kneed with age, with hunger, with climbing. More commonly expressed as weak at the knees (love, fear make knees weak). The hyphenated adjective is usually figurative, the phrase weak at the knees is physiological (swooning).
  • 4. Sentimental, overly tender: Victorian sense: weak-kneed philanthropy, weak-kneed humanitarianism — kindness seen as effeminate indulgence that undermines discipline. This paternalistic-imperial nuance survives faintly today.

Register and connotation: Informal to journalistic, critical, masculinist. It shames by implying effeminacy and physical collapse. It is rarely neutral medical description; for that speakers use unsteady, wobbly, infirm. Calling a policy weak-kneed is an accusation, not a diagnosis. It differs from cowardly (which is graver, implies desertion of duty), from indecisive (which can be neutral cognitive), from gentle (positive). Weak-kneed always suggests knees should have held but did not.

Grammar: Almost entirely attributive/predicative adjective, hyphenated before noun (weak-kneed response), open or hyphenated after be (was weak-kneed / weak kneed). Comparative weak-kneed-er rare; more commonly more weak-kneed. Adverb weak-kneedly is vanishingly rare. The image draws on universal embodiment: fear makes knees tremble, lovers go weak at the knees, shock makes knees buckle. The compound lexicalises that physiology as character judgement.

Contemporary extensions: Sports commentary (weak-kneed defending), workplace (weak-kneed management), personal resolve (don't go weak-kneed). The physical metaphor keeps it vivid where spineless is dead metaphor. As gender attitudes shift, the term's macho edge is more noticed, but it remains common in political rhetoric precisely because it pictures collapse.
