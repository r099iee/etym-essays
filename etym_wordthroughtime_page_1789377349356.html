- 13th to 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Both homographs enter English from Old French; *page* (the attendant) denotes a youth serving in a noble household in preparation for knighthood, while *page* (the folio) denotes a leaf of parchment in a manuscript.
- 15th to 16th Century (Renaissance & Print Revolution) - Chivalric page boys become celebrated figures in courtly masques and romantic ballads; simultaneously, the invention of the printing press establishes systematic pagination and indexing of book pages.
- 17th to 18th Century - The attendant page evolves into a liveried domestic servant in grand country houses; the verb *to page* begins to be used for thumbing through books.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Formal institutionalization of legislative pages in the US Congress and state assemblies; hotel page boys in brass-buttoned uniforms become prominent in metropolitan luxury culture.
- 20th Century - Telecommunications introduces electronic "pagers" and the verb *to page* (summon via beeper or PA system); computer engineering adopts *page* for virtual memory address units.
- 21st Century - "Web page" dominates digital communication, while ceremonial and legislative pages persist as living symbols of parliamentary tradition.
