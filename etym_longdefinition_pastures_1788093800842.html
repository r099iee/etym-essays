Pastures (plural of pasture) are areas of land covered with grass and other low herbage on which livestock — cattle, sheep, horses, goats — graze. The word is one of the oldest working terms of European farming, and it carries a surprisingly rich cargo of secondary senses, idioms and literary associations. In its core, everyday sense, a pasture is grazing land, usually enclosed or delimited, distinguished from arable land (which is ploughed and sown) and from meadow (land mown for hay rather than grazed, though the two overlap in ordinary speech).

  • Primary sense — grazing land: “the high summer pastures”; “their woods and fields and pastures and cattle remained undamaged”. The plural is the ordinary form when speaking of a farm's or a region's total grazing area.
  • Sense 2 — the herbage itself: pasture can denote the grass and plants on such land (“the pasture is rich this year”), a usage that preserves the word's oldest recorded English meaning, “fodder, grass eaten by cattle”.
  • Sense 3 — agricultural typology: in agronomy, permanent pasture (long-established grassland) is opposed to temporary or ley pasture (sown grass rotated with arable crops); rough pasture is unimproved hill or moor grazing. “Pasture-raised” is now a marketing label for meat and dairy from animals that actually graze outdoors.
  • Sense 4 — the pastoral mode: through its kinship with pastor (“shepherd”, from Latin pāscere “to feed”), pasture belongs to the vocabulary of pastoral literature — the classical and Renaissance genre idealising rural shepherds. A “pastoral” poem, painting or landscape evokes the world of pastures, flocks and simple virtue; the word's dignified, old-fashioned ring comes from this literary ancestry.
  • Sense 5 — Biblical resonance: Psalm 23's “green pastures” (Coverdale 1535; King James Version 1611) made the phrase a symbol of peace, sustenance and divine care; the collocation “green pastures” still carries that echo in hymnody and devotional prose.
  • Sense 6 — idioms: “greener pastures” (better opportunities elsewhere, from the proverb that the grass is greener on the other side of the fence); “pastures new” (a new field of activity, from Milton's “Lycidas”, 1637: “Tomorrow to fresh woods, and pastures new”); “to put someone out to pasture” (to retire them, originally of horses and cattle too old to work, turned out to graze — hence the idiom's gently dismissive, affectionate tone).
  • Sense 7 — verb: to pasture means to put animals out to graze, or, of animals, to graze (“the sheep pastured on the common”); the verb is attested from the 14th century.
  • Sense 8 — ecology and geography: in ecology, pastures are managed grassland ecosystems; overgrazing, rotational grazing and pasture degradation are standard technical terms. The word also survives in place names and in dialect (cf. leasow, from Old English lǣswe “pasture”).

Semantic bridges: the modern senses all descend from one historical pivot — the shift of Latin pāstūra from “the feeding of animals” (an abstract action) to “the place where they are fed”, and then to “the grass itself”. From that point the word's figurative career began: because pastures feed and rest livestock, they became the image of provision (Psalm 23), of a change of scene (Milton), and of honourable retirement (put out to pasture). “Greener pastures” grafts the ancient grazing image onto the modern hope of a better job or life. The plural pastures is particularly common in these idioms, since “pastures” suggests open, multiple, replaceable opportunities rather than a single fixed field.
