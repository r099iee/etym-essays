Animalcules is the plural of animalcule: tiny or microscopic living creatures, the minute animal life revealed by the magnifying glass and the microscope. The word belongs most characteristically to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when natural philosophers peering through hand-ground lenses discovered whole teeming worlds in drops of water, in pepper infusions, and in the tartar of their own teeth — and needed a name for beings smaller than any animal known.

In the source sentence the word carries a geological comparison: as the limestone of continents is built from infinite masses of shells of animalcules — the chalk downs and coral reefs being literally the heaped skeletons of countless microscopic sea-creatures — so language is built from dead metaphors, images whose living origins speakers have forgotten. The comparison, from Emerson's essay on language, works because both heaps are invisible to the naked eye yet mountainous in aggregate: myriads of tiny shells making continents, myriads of tiny tropes making speech.

Core meaning in detail: an animalcule is strictly a little animal — the Latin diminutive made English — but historically it meant whatever small living thing the microscope disclosed before biology had sorted protists from bacteria, rotifers from spermatozoa: Leeuwenhoek's animalcules included bacteria, protozoa, and human sperm cells side by side, all marvels together. Modern science has retired the word in favour of precise terms (microorganisms, protists, bacteria), leaving animalcules as a period word that instantly evokes periwigs, brass microscopes, and the Royal Society's breathless letters.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Microscopic living creatures in general (primary historical sense, 17th–18th c.): the animalcules in a drop of Thames water, in vinegar, in dental plaque. The bridge is the diminutive itself: Latin animal (living being) + -culum (little) — a little living thing, whatever the lens reveals.
  • Protozoa and infusoria in particular (19th c. natural-history sense): the shelled foraminifera whose accumulations form chalk and limestone. The bridge is geological: the very shells the source sentence invokes — foraminifera, radiolaria — were the celebrity animalcules once their role in building continents was understood.
  • Spermatozoa (early specialised sense, from 1677): Leeuwenhoek's most startling animalcules were the living spermatic animals in seminal fluid, announced to the Royal Society with elaborate apology. The bridge is observational: under the lens they visibly swam like tadpoles, so animal they were named — with consequences for theories of generation that lasted a century.
  • Anything minute or insignificant (figurative, rare): to dismiss opponents as mere animalcules. The bridge is scale-contempt: as the creatures are to man, so are the dismissed to the dismisser — smallness made moral.
  • The poetic plural — invisible builders (Emerson's sense): the shell-heaps that make limestone continents. The bridge runs from natural history to rhetoric: countless unseen toilers, each negligible, jointly monumental — exactly Emerson's image of forgotten metaphors building language.

Register and connotation: animalcules is learned, antiquarian, and faintly marvellous — the vocabulary of popular science before science professionalised. It charms where microorganisms chills: to say animalcules is to stand beside Leeuwenhoek at Delft, exclaiming over pepper-water. Modern writers use it deliberately for period flavour, for wit, or — as in the source — for the grandeur of the minute.
