In the passage from which this card is drawn — "Not least was his inexhaustible sense of wonder and his tolerant curiosity about the foibles of the human race" — the word "inexhaustible" describes a curiosity so deep and renewable that no amount of reading, discovery, or historical labor can drain it dry. The adjective paints intellectual appetite as a spring that never fails: every new detail of human foible or imperial decline only feeds further interest. The tone is admiring and almost hyperbolic — wonder not as a fleeting mood but as a lifelong, self-replenishing resource. The image is drawn from water: to exhaust is to draw dry, to empty a well or a spring, and the "inexhaustible" is precisely that which cannot be emptied, however hard one pumps. In its primary sense, "inexhaustible" means "(of a supply or amount) unable to be used up because existing in abundance" — the sense given in the card's short definition. A supply is inexhaustible if no amount of use, however great or long continued, will exhaust it. The word applies paradigmatically to resources: an inexhaustible supply, an inexhaustible fund, inexhaustible reserves of energy or patience. Two components work together: abundance (there is very much of it) and resistance to depletion (using it does not diminish it). In strict use both are present — the thing is not merely plentiful but is imagined as never running out, like the sea or the stars. The word has a rich set of secondary and extended senses that repay attention:
  • Of material resources: inexhaustible supply, inexhaustible reserves, an inexhaustible source — of minerals, water, fuel, food, or money. Here the word is near-literal: the resource is so vast that practical use cannot exhaust it. Classical and early modern writers apply it to mines, springs, and treasuries.
  • Of human qualities and energies: inexhaustible energy, inexhaustible patience, inexhaustible vitality, inexhaustible enthusiasm — a fund of personal resource that never runs dry. This is the most common modern sense, applied admiringly to people whose energy or patience does not flag.
  • Of intellectual and creative resources: inexhaustible curiosity, inexhaustible imagination, an inexhaustible source of inspiration — the sense on this card, where the "sense of wonder" is the spring. Artists, scholars, and writers are said to possess an inexhaustible imagination or to draw on an inexhaustible fund of ideas.
  • Metaphoric / hyperbolic extension: in everyday and journalistic use the word extends to anything that seems never to end or never to be diminished — "an inexhaustible supply of jokes," "inexhaustible topics of conversation," "inexhaustible charm" — where the claim is rhetorical praise rather than measured fact. Here "inexhaustible" shades toward "endless," "boundless," "unlimited."
  • Technical / mechanical sense: in engineering and resource economics, "inexhaustible" is used of resources (solar radiation, tidal energy) that human use cannot deplete, in contrast to exhaustible or finite resources such as fossil fuels. The word is a working term in the theory of sustainable resources.
  • Literary and religious extension: the inexhaustible is a traditional attribute of the divine — God's grace, mercy, or power as an inexhaustible source — and of nature as an inexhaustible book or treasury. The devotional register is older than the practical one.
The semantic bridge between the senses is the image of drawing water. "Exhaust" comes from Latin exhaurire, "to draw out, to drain," from ex- "out" + haurire "to draw (water)": to exhaust a well is to draw it dry, to exhaust a fund is to spend it out, to exhaust a person is to drain their strength. "Inexhaustible" negates the whole image: it is the well that cannot be drawn dry, the fund that cannot be spent out, the strength that cannot be drained. Every sense of the word — the mineral reserve, the energy, the curiosity, the divine grace — is the same picture of an un-drainable depth, and the word keeps that watery image alive even in its most hyperbolic modern uses. Register and near-synonym boundaries are worth drawing precisely. "Endless" and "infinite" are abstract and mathematical — they deny any end or bound whatever, without the concrete image of a supply being used up. "Boundless" stresses lack of outer limits; "inexhaustible" stresses resistance to depletion from within. "Tireless" usually describes a person who does not tire, a quality of the agent, whereas "inexhaustible" more often describes a fund of energy, patience, or material that does not run out. "Unfailing" means not failing or giving way, at home with loyalty, courage, and supplies, and is close, but "unfailing" stresses reliability rather than abundance. "Abundant" marks present plenty without projecting the plenty into the future; "inexhaustible" projects it indefinitely. "Infinite" is the strongest and least physical; "inexhaustible" is the word that keeps its feet in the world of wells and funds — the spring that no pump can empty, the exact image Gibbon's biographer needed for a sense of wonder that never ran dry.
