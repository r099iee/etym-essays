ramparts
- The embankment of defence (16th c.) - Borrowed from French rempart, 're-fortification'; first a strictly military engineering term for the raised embankment of earth thrown up before a wall.
- The whole fortified circuit (16th-18th c.) - Widens to name the entire ring of fortification — wall, parapet, and walkway together; the word of siege warfare and fortress building.
- From the camp to the page (19th c.) - Extends beyond the literal to dignified literary use — the ramparts of a city, the ramparts of heaven; it becomes the poetic word for any great defensive wall.
- Modern authority - Retains the suggestion of mass and preparation against attack; in literary use an emblem of immovable authority, the skin of stone between the stronghold and the swarm.
hovels
- A shed for cattle (15th c.) - The word first appears as a rough roofed shelter for beasts, crude, dark, and animal-grade.
- Onto the dwellings of the poor (16th c.) - Shifts from the cattle-shed to the miserable houses of the poor, carrying the shed's roughness and darkness with it; a hovel becomes a sub-house, barely fit for habitation.
- The judgment of squalor (17th-19th c.) - Settles as the contemptuous word for a small, mean, ramshackle dwelling; the word itself is a verdict, the architecture of poverty.
- Modern degradation - Remains uniquely degraded among words for poor housing, the chronicler's term when he wants no pity and no romance, the exact antonym of palace.
limpets
- The rock-licker's borrowed name (Old English) - The limpet takes the name of the lamprey, itself called the 'rock-licker'; two creatures seen clinging to rocks are collapsed into one word.
- The shellfish of the shore (Middle English) - Stabilises as the natural-history name of the small conical mollusc that grips tidal rocks with its muscular foot.
- The literature of the shore (18th-19th c.) - Naturalists fix the word to the patellid gastropods; it remains a strictly zoological term in ordinary use.
- Modern tenacity - Its figurative force now outweighs the literal: a limpet is anything that grips blindly, tenaciously, and immovably, to be pried off only with violence; the emblem of stubborn clinging.
