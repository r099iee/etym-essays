MOULDERED (also spelt moldered in American English) is the past participle of the verb to moulder — to decay slowly, crumbling gradually into dust, soft earth, or powder through the corrosive action of time, damp, rot, and neglect. It describes a slow, natural, undramatic process of disintegration: a thing that moulders is not shattered or consumed in a sudden event but quietly returns to the soil from which it came.

In J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the word appears in the passage describing the halls of the Lonely Mountain, where "all the old adornments were long mouldered or destroyed": Thorin and his company find the treasure-house of Erebor stripped of its former glory, its carvings and ornaments rotted away during the long centuries under the mountain, a melancholy testament to ruin and the passing of ages. The word therefore carries in this context a powerful elegiac charge: the physical decay is also a symbol of fallen kingship and lost civilization.

Core meaning: To crumble or rot away into dust; to decay by a slow natural process.

  • Primary sense (physical decay): To turn to mould, dust, or soft crumbling matter. Wood, cloth, stone, bone, leather, and paper can all moulder over time. For example, "the timbers mouldered in the damp cellar". This is the original and dominant sense of the word.
  • Secondary sense (neglect and abandonment): To lie decaying and unregarded; to waste away for want of use or care. Closely related to the idea of things left to rot because nobody tends them. For example, "the plans mouldered in a forgotten drawer".
  • Tertiary sense (figurative decay of reputation, talent, or memory): Used of non-physical things that fall into neglect, decay, or obscurity. A reputation, a book, an ambition, or even a language can be said to "moulder in obscurity". This extends the physical decay into the moral, intellectual, or social realm.
  • Sense of slow stagnation: In some 19th-century usage, to moulder can mean to remain inert, passive, or unused — to stagnate — as in "mouldering in idleness".

Connotations and register: The word is strongly literary and slightly archaic in flavour. In everyday speech, rot, decay, or crumble are far more common; moulder is chosen in prose for its poetic, atmospheric quality and its association with old ruins, forgotten things, graves, and the passage of centuries. It appears frequently in gothic and fantasy literature — in Tolkien, Poe, and the writers of the Romantic period — where it supplies the texture of decayed grandeur.

Grammatical notes: Moulder is a regular weak verb; mouldered is its simple past and past participle. It is normally intransitive ("it mouldered") but is often used in the passive or with a locative ("mouldering in the grave"). The agent noun moulder (a person or thing that moulders) is rare. The British spelling keeps -ould- (mould, moulder, mouldered); the American spelling drops the u (mold, molder, moldered), a divergence fixed by Noah Webster in the 19th century.

Related semantic fields: decay, decomposition, putrefaction, disintegration, erosion, corrosion, weathering, mildew, mustiness, rust, dust, ruin.

In the context of the card: "Though all the old adornments were long mouldered or destroyed" — here mouldered names the natural decay of the dwarven carvings, paired with destroyed (violent ruin) and with befouled and blasted (soiled and scorched by the dragon). The verb groups slow organic decay with the deliberate destruction wrought by Smaug, creating a layered picture of total ruin in which even the proudest work of the dwarves has returned to dust.
