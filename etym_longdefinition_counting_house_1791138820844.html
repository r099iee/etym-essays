A counting-house is a small office, bureau, or room, historically used by a merchant, trader, or firm for keeping accounts, handling money, writing letters, and conducting business correspondence. In Dickens's London it is the gloomy chamber where Scrooge and Bob Cratchit work among ledgers, cash-boxes, and high desks on Christmas Eve: cold, dim, businesslike, shut off from the festive street. The term is now historical or literary; modern speakers would say office, accounts office, or headquarters.

Functionally it combined what we now split among back-office, cashier, and mailroom. There bills were made out, debts collected, rents recorded, wages paid, letters copied, and balances struck. It was not a retail shop where goods were displayed, nor a warehouse where they were stored, nor a factory where they were made, but the paper brain of the enterprise. Typically it held sloped writing desks, tall stools, bound ledgers, inkwells, strongboxes, and a single meagre fire. Privacy mattered: the master had his own box or closet within, clerks sat in an outer tank or open area under watch.

  • Merchant's office (core historical sense): the business office of a trading house. Example: the clerk returned to the counting-house after dinner. Implies 18th–19th-century commerce, quill pens, manual arithmetic. More specific than office, which can be any white-collar workplace.
  • Accounts department in miniature: the place where counting in the literal sense happens. Example: figures were checked in the counting-house. Stresses reckoning rather than managing or selling. Near-synonym to counting-room, accompting-house in older spelling.
  • Moral-symbolic use (Dickensian): emblem of miserliness, cold capitalism, work without joy. Example: his heart was a counting-house. Critics speak of counting-house mentality to mean fixation on profit. The room stands for a soul that only reckons.
  • What it is not: not a bank open to the public, though banks have similar rooms; not a treasury of the state; not a modern open-plan office with computers. It is private, small-scale, paper-based, headed by a master or partners rather than a corporation.
  • Form and pronunciation: hyphenated counting-house, plural counting-houses; sometimes written as two words counting house in older books. Stress on counting, secondary on house. To sit busy in one's counting-house, as Scrooge does, means to be absorbed in money-work.

Socially the counting-house marks class and era. Its master might be wealthy yet work with his own hands among his books; its clerks were lower-middle-class strivers in top hats and tailcoats, poorly paid, cold, and deferential. The coldness Dickens stresses — Scrooge keeps Cratchit shivering — is both literal fuel economy and moral economy: warmth, like charity, is rationed. To modern readers the word therefore smells of ink, coal-smoke, damp wool, and thrift.

Use it today for historical description, Dickens discussion, or deliberate archaism. Say the Victorian counting-house, Scrooge's counting-house, the merchant's counting-house. Do not use it for a present-day bank branch or tech startup unless joking; say office. Keep the hyphen in careful prose, though dictionaries accept open and closed variants. Above all keep the count: a counting-house is defined by accounts, not merely by walls.
