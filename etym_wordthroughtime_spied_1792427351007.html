- [15th–16th c. Early Modern English] — Widespread in narrative and poetic literature for sharp-eyed visual discovery (e.g. spying land from a ship, spying a lover or quarry), alongside institutionalization in political espionage during Tudor conflicts — [perception: celebrated in literature for keen observational acumen; regarded with suspicion in statecraft]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Parliamentary English] — Formalized in parliamentary tradition ("I spy strangers") and expanded in descriptive natural philosophy for discovering celestial or microscopic phenomena — [perception: versatile literary verb denoting sharp ocular discernment]
- [19th c. Victorian & Children's Folklore] — Institutionalized in children's parlor folklore ("I spy with my little eye"), contrasting with dramatic Victorian spy fiction — [perception: developed a cheerful domestic innocence in play while retaining romantic intrigue in historical romance]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary English] — Senses cleanly split: in narrative prose, spied remains a polished literary synonym for visual detection, while in geopolitical journalism it is dominated by covert intelligence and cyber surveillance — [perception: refined literary verb for observant discovery; ominous terminology in intelligence and state surveillance]
