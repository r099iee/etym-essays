Stage 1 — PIE root *ḱerd- "heart" (reconstructed, c. 4500-3000 BCE): attested across nearly every Indo-European branch — Hittite kēr (genitive kardiyaš), Greek kardía, Sanskrit hṛd and hṛdaya, Avestan zərəd-, Latin cor, Gothic haírtō, Old English heorte, Lithuanian širdis, Old Church Slavonic srĭdĭce, Armenian sirt, Old Irish cride. Stage 2 — Latin cor, genitive cordis "heart" (Classical Latin, 3rd-1st c. BCE): the organ of the chest, and by extension the seat of courage, feeling, and life; from PIE *ḱerd- via regular sound changes (palatal *ḱ > Latin c; the genitive cordis shows the zero-grade stem *ḱr̥d- of the oblique cases). Stage 3 — Late Latin cordiālis "of the heart, heartfelt" (Late Latin, 4th-6th c. CE; in Medieval Latin "of the heart, reviving the heart", 11th-14th c.): derived from cor, cordis with the suffix -ālis ("pertaining to"). Stage 4 — Old French cordial "of the heart; heart-reviving, restorative" (12th-14th c.): inherited from Latin cordiālis. Stage 5 — Middle English cordial "of the heart; reviving the heart" (14th c., borrowed from Old French): first used as a medical word for heart-restoring medicines. Stage 6 — Modern English CORDIAL "heartfelt, warm, sincere" (adjective, the feeling sense emerging 16th-17th c.) and "a restorative or sweet drink" (noun, 15th c. onward). Note on the nuance: the modern dominant sense "warm and friendly" is a narrowing of the older "heartfelt" sense onto social manner, while the noun "sweet drink" descends from the medieval medicinal "heart-restoring tonic". The American spelling and the "sweet liqueur" sense developed independently in the 17th-18th c. from the same medicinal stem.
