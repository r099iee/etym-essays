The history of befallen is the history of falling imagined as fate. Its two halves are among the oldest tools in Germanic: the prefix be-, meaning about or upon or thoroughly, and the verb fall, naming the most universal of motions — and their union created a verb in which events do not merely occur but descend.

Falling up the family tree: the root of fall reaches back to a conventionally reconstructed PIE stem *pol-, to fall, whose daughters drop through every Indo-European language: Lithuanian puolu (I fall), Armenian p'ul, and the whole Germanic set — Gothic fallan, Old Norse falla, Old High German fallan, Old English feallan. The Germanic lawyers of sound change note the regular consonant shifts with satisfaction, though they concede the PIE stage is thinner than the Germanic one. What matters is that for four thousand years human beings have used one stable syllable for the body's surrender to gravity — and then, by metaphor, for the soul's surrender to fortune.

The prefix that aims the fall: Old English be- (from Proto-Germanic *bi-, by or about) fastened itself to dozens of verbs to direct or intensify them: besprinkle, bemoan, bethink, beware. In befallan (9th c.) it aims the falling upon a victim: rain falls, but fate befalls — the difference between weather and destiny is one small prefix. The Anglo-Saxons used it of lots cast, judgments pronounced, and accidents suffered, already reserving it for happenings with weight.

  • Lots and scripture (Middle Ages): the great early attestations are providential: the lot befell Matthias in the Acts of the Apostles, portions befell tribes in the Old Testament translations; Tyndale and Wycliffe made befall the English verb by which heaven distributed outcomes, and the participle carried divine postage.
  • Chronicles and Shakespeare (16th–17th c.): Holinshed's chronicles record what befell kings and kingdoms, while Shakespeare's characters ask what has befallen and fear what may; the verb becomes the historian's neutral and the dramatist's dread, dignified enough for thrones and common enough for cottages.
  • The retreat into solemnity (18th–19th c.): as happen (itself from hap, chance) conquered everyday speech, befall withdrew upward into sermons, obituaries, and novels of fate — Jane Austen's characters still ask what has befallen with perfect propriety, but no shopkeeper uses it for a late delivery.
  • Tolkien's hinge (20th c.): adventure befalling Bilbo at fifty gathers the whole providential history into one clause: unasked, sudden, life-changing, half-comic and half-divine. Fantasy after Tolkien wears the word constantly — curses befall kingdoms, prophecies befall heroes — because no other participle makes plot feel so much like weather from the gods.

Grammar of passivity: linguists note the verb's odd one-way valency: things befall people, never the reverse, and the participle almost always leans on had or have — had befallen, has befallen — as if the grammar itself confesses that mortals do not do the befalling but only receive it. That passivity is the theology inside the syntax: mortals stand below, and events fall.

Modern survival: today befallen lives where fate is still reported — disaster headlines (the tragedy that befell the town), memorial speeches, fantasy novels, and the liturgy of bad news — while happen handles everything else. The division honours the prefix: ordinary things merely happen, but the great ones still fall upon us, and we still lack any other word for the weight of their landing.
