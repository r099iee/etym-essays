Contemporaneous is a formal adjective meaning existing at the same time, occurring in the same period, or belonging to the same age as something else. Our contemporaneous ancestry means ancestors who lived at the same time as us in the sense of sharing the present age, or more usually in anthropology, peoples living now who are taken to represent earlier stages — a usage the author warns against with savage and barbarous.

Unlike contemporary, which doubles as noun (a contemporary of Shakespeare) and casual adjective (contemporary furniture), contemporaneous is almost purely adjectival and bookish, preferred in scholarship, law and science for precise temporal co-occurrence. Historians speak of contemporaneous accounts, documents, witnesses — sources produced at the time of the events, hence prized over retrospective memoirs. Geologists speak of contemporaneous strata; lawyers of contemporaneous notes as evidence; psychologists of contemporaneous measurement.

The word stresses overlap, not merely modernity. Two things are contemporaneous with each other; contemporary can mean merely up-to-date. You can say contemporaneous with the war, contemporaneous with Chaucer, contemporaneous records. The with-complement is obligatory for clarity, though absolute use — contemporaneous ancestry — is common, meaning coexisting with the observer.

  • Sense 1 — Core temporal: coexisting in time: Contemporaneous civilizations, contemporaneous authors. Example: Darwin and Wallace were contemporaneous naturalists. Bridge: Latin con- together + tempus time + -aneous adjectival, literally timed-together. No implication of contact, only overlap.
  • Sense 2 — Evidential: produced at the time: A contemporaneous diary, contemporaneous minutes. Here the value is reliability: written then, not remembered later. Courts admit contemporaneous notes as exceptions to hearsay because immediacy guarantees freshness.
  • Sense 3 — Anthropological-ironic: so-called primitive contemporaries: Our contemporaneous ancestry as used in early anthropology to label living hunter-gatherers as living fossils. The author urges sparing use of savage and barbarous precisely because calling living peoples contemporaneous ancestors smuggles evolutionism into description. Modern anthropology rejects the phrase as condescending.
  • Sense 4 — Technical-scientific: synchronous formation or measurement: Contemporaneous lava flows, contemporaneous controls. Means formed together or measured together, allowing causal inference. Distinct from simultaneous (same instant) by allowing broader period overlap.
  • Differentiation from contemporary, coeval, synchronous, simultaneous: Contemporary is stylistically neutral and also means modern; coeval stresses same age; synchronous stresses same instant or phase; simultaneous stresses same moment of action. Contemporaneous is the most formal and period-oriented, favouring years and eras over instants.

Register: academic, legal, museum-label. To say contemporaneous ancestry instead of living peoples or present-day societies signals 19th- to early-20th-century evolutionist anthropology. The warning to use savage and barbarous sparingly belongs to that moment of disciplinary self-correction, still relevant when textbooks call foragers our Stone Age contemporaries.
