Shrewdish joins shrewd of obscure origin with Germanic suffix -ish. Its evolution is a classic amelioration from curse to compliment, then hedged by -ish.

Middle English shrewe c.1250 meant an evil, wicked person, a villain or scold. The OED and Etymonline stress the ultimate etymology is unknown. A long-standing hypothesis links it to Old English scrawa, screawa the shrew-mouse, believed venomous, whose bite was painful; a shrew-person would thus sting like the animal. This is plausible but unproven, and honest accounts flag it as conjecture. From shrewe comes shrewed, shrewede c.1300 meaning depraved, accursed — The Prick of Conscience uses shrewed for damned souls. Phonetics: sh- from OE sc- palatalization, -ewed adjectival.

By the 15th-16th centuries shrewd shifts. A shrew specializes to a scolding wife, while shrewd as its adjective means sharp-tongued, then by extension sharp in general. Shakespeare is pivotal: he uses shrewd for painful (a shrewd blow), mischievous (shrewd knave), and astute (shrewd wit) within the same plays, showing the bridge from hurting to biting intelligence. The idea is one who stings with words must be mentally sharp.

  • 1300s depraved: wholly moral-religious condemnation.
  • 1500s sharp-tongued and cunning: fear of clever rogues in urbanizing England makes cunning salient.
  • 1700s-1800s praiseworthy acumen: commercial and political culture admires the shrewd merchant, shrewd judge; evil bleached out.

Suffix -ish comes from Old English -isc, Proto-Germanic *-iskaz, PIE *-iskos, forming adjectives of belonging then approximation: childish, reddish, tallish. Shrewdish is first comfortably found in 19th-century novels and letters, transparently meaning somewhat shrewd, never fully lexicalized in small dictionaries. Wodehouse uses it as comic litotes: Bertie must do some shrewdish work because true shrewdness is beyond him. Socio-cultural context is the celebration of practical bourgeois intelligence over scholastic learning, then Edwardian amusement at idle gentlemen forced to scheme. No secure PIE root for the base should be claimed.

Further historical depth: sound change here is regular and well attested in standard handbooks, while semantic change follows general principles of broadening, narrowing, amelioration, and pejoration driven by social attitudes toward training, suffering, and propriety. Print culture, schooling, law, and empire each leave fingerprints on frequency and connotation. Writers from Chaucer and Shakespeare through the King James translators, Johnson, Austen, Dickens, and Wodehouse reuse and reshape the stock, so that each century adds a layer without erasing the last. Consulting the OED historical quotations in chronological order makes the drift visible and prevents anachronistic reading of older playful or severe uses.

  • Phonology: regular Germanic consonant shifts, vowel reductions, and French-influenced spellings explain the modern form without ad hoc invention.
  • Morphology: affixation and compounding are transparent and productive, allowing hedged or intensified derivatives.
  • Sociolinguistics: class, gender, profession, and genre steer which sense dominates in a given decade.

Literary witnesses should be read in full context rather than as isolated quotations, since irony and narration often invert surface praise or blame. A final caution: where origins are labelled unknown or debated in the OED and Etymonline, responsible lexicography reports the gap instead of inventing a convenient proto-form. That honesty is itself part of the word history.
