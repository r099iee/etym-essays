The English name Macedon descends from the same Proto-Indo-European root as Macedonia, but through a shorter chain that preserves the Greek nominative: 1. Proto-Indo-European *meh₂k- ‘long, tall, pointed’ (an adjectival root of height and length; Greek makrós ‘long’, Latin macer ‘thin, lean’). 2. Ancient Greek μακεδνός (makednós) ‘tall, slender, tapering’ — the o-grade formation on the root, used of high, slender things. 3. Ancient Greek Μακεδών (Makedōn), genitive Μακεδόνος (Makedónos) ‘a Macedonian; the land of the Macedonians personified’ — the old nominative singular of the ethnonym, attested from the 5th century BC (Herodotus, Thucydides), and also the name of the eponymous hero Makedōn in Greek myth. 4. Latin Macedo, genitive Macedonis ‘a Macedonian’ — the Roman adoption of the Greek nominative, attested in Latin from the 1st century BC (the Roman province of Macedonia was established in 146 BC). 5. English Macedon, attested from the 16th–17th centuries, taken directly from Latin/Greek — the archaizing form used for the ancient kingdom. 6. Macedon — the modern English proper noun, used of the ancient kingdom in classical-historical and poetic contexts. Debated points: the underlying meaning of the ethnonym — ‘the tall ones’ (from makednós) or ‘the highlanders’ — is traditional but a pre-Greek/Illyrian substrate origin has been proposed; the PIE root *meh₂k- is secure through Greek and Latin reflexes, but Sanskrit mahánt ‘great’ is from a different root (*meǵh₂-) and is not a cognate. The English spelling ‘Macedon’ simply drops the Greek case-ending, preserving the bare nominative stem.
