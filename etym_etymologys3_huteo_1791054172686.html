Huteo is a Tahitian vernacular plant name borrowed into English travel and anthropological writing, denoting the nut or tree whose pulp Tahitians used as a fish stupefacient. Unlike Indo-European words with millennia of written attestations, huteo belongs to an oral Polynesian lexicon first written by European visitors in the late 18th and 19th centuries, when missionaries, beachcombers, and naturalists transcribed what they heard with French or English spelling. Variation was inevitable: huteo, hutu, utu, futu, putu appear across dictionaries for what botanists now group largely under Barringtonia asiatica (fish-poison tree, sea putat) and close congeners. The most responsible statement is therefore cautious: Tahitian huteo is securely attested as a fish-poison nut; its deeper Proto-Polynesian or Proto-Austronesian antecedent is uncertain, possibly connected to Proto-Polynesian *futu (Barringtonia) with regular Tahitian f → h shift, but dialect borrowing and identification confusion prevent certainty. The debate note in S2 records exactly this.

Sound and spelling history illustrates transcription rather than regular change:

  • Polynesian base: Many islands call Barringtonia futu (Samoan), hutu (Marquesan), utu (Fijian vutu). Tahitian regularly reflects Proto-Polynesian *f as h, so futu → hutu is expected; huteo with final -eo may record a dialect variant, a suffixed form (hutu + -? ), or a mishearing by a French ear for hutu.
  • French missionary orthography: Tahitian was first standardised by London Missionary Society and French Catholic presses; u, e, o spellings waver before dictionaries fixed them. Nineteenth-century sources write huto, huteo, hutuo interchangeably.
  • English borrowing: Anglophone travellers (Ellis, Melville, later Frazer and Durant) lifted the word unchanged, italicised or lower-cased, always glossed as nut. No plural or verb forms developed; English simply says huteo nuts.

Cultural context is the fish-poison complex widespread from the Indian Ocean to the Pacific. Islanders pounded Barringtonia seeds, Derris (duva) roots containing rotenone, Tephrosia leaves, or Pittosporum bark, wrapped them in leaves, and washed them in dammed pools or lagoons at low tide. Saponins and rotenoids deoxygenate or narcotise gills; fish float belly-up and are gathered by hand, a communal, sustainable harvest when done on small streams. Early European observers, struck by ease and festivity, described it as intoxication: fish drunk, leisurely, willing — the very phrasing of the source passage. Missionaries condemned it as wasteful; anthropologists celebrated it as ecological ingenuity; colonial administrations later banned it as poaching.

Literary attestations are thin but telling:

  • 1820s–1840s missionary journals: Ellis's Polynesian Researches lists hutu among useful trees without pharmacology.
  • 19th-century botanical voyages: Forster, Guillemin, and Drake del Castillo collect Barringtonia specimens, noting vernaculars including huteo.
  • Early 20th-century synthesis: Frazer's Golden Bough and later Durant's Story of Civilization (1935, Our Oriental Heritage) popularise huteo nut and hora plant as a paired example of primitive fishing, the exact sentence preserved in this card.
  • Modern ethnobotany: Petard, Whistler, and Allen identify Tahitian hutu firmly as Barringtonia asiatica; huteo survives only as an archaic variant in reprints of Durant, puzzling modern readers and dictionary-makers.

The modern perception of huteo is therefore obscurity itself: an unassimilated foreignism, lower-case, italicisable, absent from most desk dictionaries, surviving as a test of zero-guesswork lexicography. Its evolution essay cannot be 3,000 characters of sound laws; it is 3,000 characters of transcription variance, botanical detective work, sustainable-harvest anthropology, and the journey from lagoon practice to world-history illustration and back to footnoted mystery.
