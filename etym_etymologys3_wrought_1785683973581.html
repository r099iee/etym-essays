The biography of WROUGHT is the biography of the English past participle of "work," told in the register of the smithy. The Indo-European root of labour. The word descends from PIE *werg-, "to do, make, work," one of the most productive roots of the entire family, a root that encodes the very idea of purposeful human activity — the expenditure of force upon matter. Its reflexes are scattered across the ancient world: Greek érgon, "work, deed," gave energy, organ, organon, surgery, ergonomics, and even orgy; Armenian gorc, "work," hardened the initial w to g; Hittite wark-, "to till, work the land," shows the root already ancient before any Greek text was written; and in the Indo-Iranian world the root produced Avestan varəzi-, "work, tillage," and Sanskrit ūrjā, "strength, vigour, energy" — a sense revived in the modern Hindi and Odia word ūrjā, "energy." In Germanic the root yielded Proto-Germanic *wurkijaną, "to work, make," and the noun *wurką, "work," and Old English inherited both: wyrċan, "to work," and weorc, "work." Even the word wright, "a worker, maker" (as in wheelwright, playwright), is a descendant of the same root, showing how deeply the idea of making was embedded in the Germanic word-family. The strong past and its sound changes. The word wrought is, etymologically, the old past and participle of wyrċan. In Old English the verb belonged to the class that formed its past with a velar fricative: wyrċan had the past worhte and the participle geworht, where the -h- spelled a voiceless velar fricative /x/. As Middle English developed, the r in these forms shifted position — worhte became wrohte, and the participle appeared as wrouȝt, wrouȝte, wroughte — a metathesis of the r, the same kind of reordering that appears in bird (< Old English brid) and horse. The spelling with -ough- fossilised the old /x/ sound, which fell silent in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries exactly as it did in thought, brought, and sought. By then, however, the regular weak past worked had been spreading through the language, and by the sixteenth century it had driven wrought out of every neutral use: wrought ceased to be the ordinary past of work and became what it is today — a deliberately archaic form. The smithy and the Bible. The word's survival is owed to two pressures. The first is the language of craft: in the trades, "wrought iron" and "wrought silver" were the technical terms for metal shaped by the hammer and the anvil, as opposed to cast or merely smelted metal, and this technical use kept the participle alive in the workshops long after ordinary speech had abandoned it. The second is the language of solemnity: the King James Bible and the liturgy of the English church continued to use wrought for "made, fashioned, effected" — "he wrought his wonders," "the works that I have wrought" — and this biblical gravity gave the word its grand, archaic register. From these two sources the word passed into literary prose as a stylistic resource: a writer who says "wrought" asks the reader to feel that the action was a labour, a forging, a shaping of resistant material. The figurative turn. From the smithy the word reached inward. "Wrought up" — one's feelings worked into a knot — dates from the seventeenth century, and "overwrought" carried the same image of emotions beaten out of shape into excessive agitation. In the figurative use that the card's sentence exploits, "wrought a deep and lasting change in his temper," the word describes the defeat as a blacksmith: it has hammered a man's character into a new, durable shape, beaten it into the very grain of his temper. This is the word's peculiar power — it is a verb of manufacture applied to the soul, and it is exactly the register that "worked" or "caused" would flatten. In modern English wrought survives in a small but tenacious set of idioms — wrought iron, wrought havoc, wrought a change, wrought up, overwrought — and in the elevated prose of historians, novelists, and translators who want the reader to hear the anvil.
  • PIE (c. 4500–3000 BCE): *werg-, "to do, make, work"; reflexes in Greek érgon, Armenian gorc, Hittite wark-, Avestan varəzi-, Sanskrit ūrjā, Germanic *wurkijaną.
  • Proto-Germanic (c. 2500–500 BCE): *wurkijaną "to work, make"; *wurką "work"; also wright "worker, maker."
  • Old English (c. 7th–11th c.): wyrċan "to work"; past worhte, participle geworht, with the velar fricative -h-.
  • Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.): worchen; wrohte, wrouȝt(e) — the r metathesizes (worhte → wrohte), spelling fixes as -ough-.
  • 16th c.: worked becomes the regular past; wrought is pushed out of neutral use but survives in craft language (wrought iron) and the biblical/liturgical register.
  • 16th–17th c.: the /x/ sound falls silent (like thought, brought); "wrought up" and "overwrought" extend the smithy image to the emotions.
  • Modern: an archaic, deliberate form; the idiomatic set wrought iron, wrought havoc, wrought a change, wrought up, overwrought; the register is grand, biblical, and craft-flavoured.
