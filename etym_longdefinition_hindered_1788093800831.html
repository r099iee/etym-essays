Hindered is the past tense and past participle of the verb hinder, meaning to delay, impede, obstruct or make difficult the progress of a person, action or process — to hold something back without necessarily stopping it altogether. The participle is extremely common in passive constructions ("progress was hindered by...") and functions widely as a participial adjective describing anything whose advance has been slowed or blocked.

1. Core sense.

  • To create difficulty or delay for (an action, journey, plan, movement): "the storm hindered the rescue", "fear hindered his speech".
  • To obstruct (a person) from doing something: "she was hindered from leaving by the locked gate".
  • As a participle: "hindered by" — the standard modern construction naming the obstacle: hindered by weather, bureaucracy, injury, lack of funds, illness or prejudice.

2. Distinction from stronger verbs. The key boundary is between hinder and prevent/stop: to hinder is to impede and delay — the action may still succeed, slowly and with difficulty; to prevent or stop is to bring it to an end. "The mud hindered their march" (they marched on, slowly) versus "the flood prevented their march" (they did not march). Hindrance is a matter of degree: light hindrance equals delay; severe hindrance approaches blockage.

3. Extended and figurative senses.

  • Physical: hindered movement, hindered vision (something blocking the line of sight), hindered breathing.
  • Institutional and social: progress, growth, trade, research and justice are hindered by rules, corruption, censorship, prejudice, poverty — the favourite register of news, policy and academic writing.
  • Psychological: people are hindered by shyness, fear, doubt, self-consciousness — inner obstacles that slow action from within.
  • Medical: recovery, healing or development hindered by complications — common in clinical prose.

4. Historical sense. In Old English hindrian also meant "to injure, harm" — a sense that died out in Middle English as the "hold back, impede" sense took over. Modern speakers feel only the obstruction meaning; the archaic "harm" sense survives only in the related noun hindrance and in the adjective hinder "rear, back" (hinder legs, hinder parts), which preserves the original spatial meaning "behind".

5. Register and nuance. Neutral and formal; standard in written English of every register from news to literature. It is blunter than hamper (which suggests tangling or encumbrance) and impede (more technical, of processes), softer than thwart (which implies deliberate frustration of a plan) and block (complete obstruction). The passive participle "hindered by" is the default modern form, and the word carries a quiet, administrative tone — hindrance is rarely dramatic, always inconvenient.

6. Summary. To be hindered is to be held back, delayed or made to struggle — physically, institutionally or psychologically — by an obstacle that slows progress without necessarily ending it; the word descends from the idea of being put "behind", pushed back from one's goal.
