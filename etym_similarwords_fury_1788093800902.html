• Rage: Similar because both denote violent anger of the highest intensity; differs because rage is the more clinical, physical word (and names the state of “blind rage”), while fury adds the mythological and elemental overtones of the avenging goddesses and the storm.
  • Wrath: Similar because both mean intense anger; differs because wrath is archaic-biblical and implies slow, righteous, punitive anger (the “wrath of God”), while fury is faster, wilder and less moralised.
  • Anger: Similar because both belong to the same emotional family; differs because anger is the general, neutral term covering all intensities, while fury is its extreme end, implying violence and loss of control.
  • Ire: Similar because both mean anger; differs because ire is literary, cold and dignified (“the nation's ire”), a settled state rather than an exploding one.
  • Indignation: Similar because both can describe angry reaction to wrongdoing; differs because indignation is principled, articulate and self-controlled, the opposite of fury's frenzy.
  • Outrage: Similar because both denote explosive public anger; differs because outrage (from “beyond bounds”) is moral and collective — anger at a specific offence — while fury may be blind and personal.
  • Frenzy: Similar because both describe deranged excitement; differs because frenzy is a state of madness or frantic activity that need not be angry (“feeding frenzy”), while fury is anger specifically.
  • Ferocity / violence: Similar because both name destructive intensity; differ because these describe acts or temperaments, not the emotion of anger itself.
  • Passion (archaic): Similar because “passion” once meant any violent emotion, including anger; differs because passion today means love or enthusiasm, while fury remains anger.
  • Furor / furore: Similar because they share the Latin root furere and mean public uproar or craze; differ because furor is collective excitement, not the individual emotion of fury.
