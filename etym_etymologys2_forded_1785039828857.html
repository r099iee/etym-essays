Linear derivation chain for FORDED (past tense of the verb to ford): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *per-, reconstructed, c. 4500 BCE: "to lead over, pass through, cross"; with the nominal extension *prtu- "a going, a passage, a crossing." Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *furdu-, c. 500 BCE–100 CE: "a going, a passage; a ford" (under Grimm's Law the initial PIE *p- becomes Germanic *f-). Stage 3 — Old English ford, attested c. 8th–11th centuries: noun, "a shallow place where a river can be crossed on foot." Cognates: Old Norse fjoror (fjörðr, later "inlet"), Old High German furt, Old Frisian forda. Stage 4 — Old English fordian, attested in Old English, c. 9th–11th centuries: denominal verb, "to go, pass, cross, ford." Stage 5 — Middle English: the noun ford survives; the verb is carried on and later re-formed in Modern English (attested 1610s) as to ford, "to cross by wading at a shallow place." Stage 6 — Modern English forded, 17th century onward: past tense and past participle of ford, "crossed (water) at a shallow place by wading." Side-branch: the Norse cognate fjörðr was borrowed into English as fjord (19th century) for the steep Scandinavian sea-inlet. Cognate notes: the same PIE root *per- underlies Latin portus "harbour" (English port), porta "gate" (portal, porch), portare "to carry" (porter, portable, report, export, import); Greek poros "passage" (pore, porous); Sanskrit para- "the opposite shore, the far bank" (Hindi pa:r पार "across, the other side"); Avestan peretu- "ford," surviving in the name Euphrates ("good to cross over"). Debated / nuanced note: The root *per- is one of the most securely reconstructed of PIE roots, with reflexes in almost every branch; the Germanic shift *p- to *f- under Grimm's Law is standard. The Old English verb fordian is attested, though the modern verb was independently re-formed in the 17th century after the Old English verb had fallen out of use; both are recorded here without asserting a single uninterrupted line.
