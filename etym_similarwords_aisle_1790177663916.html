• gangway: Similar because both denote a narrow passageway between rows of seats, equipment, or structures; differs because gangway is predominantly maritime, nautical, or industrial in register (such as a ship's boarding bridge or factory walkway) and can also serve as an imperative exclamation meaning "make way!".
  • corridor: Similar because both represent elongated pathways facilitating pedestrian movement between enclosed spaces; differs because a corridor is typically an enclosed hallway bordered by solid walls and doors connecting distinct rooms, whereas an aisle runs open-sided through a single large room between seats or shelves.
  • passageway: Similar because both describe a route for transit through an architectural space; differs because passageway is a broader, generic term that encompasses tunnels, alleys, outdoor paths, and covered halls of any scale.
  • hallway: Similar because both are interior routes for pedestrian foot traffic; differs because a hallway is a domestic or building corridor flanked by rooms, whereas an aisle exists inside open spaces like churches, aircraft cabins, and shops.
  • alley: Similar because both refer to narrow pedestrian lanes; differs because an alley is primarily a narrow backstreet between urban buildings or a dedicated sporting lane (e.g., a bowling alley), rather than an indoor passage between furnishings.
  • walkway: Similar because both designate cleared tracks for walking; differs because walkway typically refers to outdoor, pedestrianized footpaths, skybridges, or elevated industrial platforms rather than seat-delimited indoor lanes.
  • lane: Similar because both describe delineated corridors of movement; differs because lane is primarily used for outdoor vehicular roads, swimming pool divisions, or running tracks rather than interior room subdivisions.
