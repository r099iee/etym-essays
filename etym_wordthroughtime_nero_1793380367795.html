- 1st c. CE — Imperial name and descent into infamy: Begins as an honored Sabine patrician cognomen of the Julio-Claudian dynasty, but ends as the name of the condemned emperor declared a public enemy by the Senate.
- 2nd–4th c. — Christian demonization and the Antichrist: Early church fathers (Tertullian, Lactantius) establish Nero as the first imperial persecutor, while apocalyptic commentary identifies him with the Beast of Revelation.
- Middle Ages — Exemplum of hubris and tragedy: Geoffrey Chaucer’s The Monk's Tale and medieval morality plays treat Nero as the supreme historical warning against tyrannical cruelty and the fickle wheel of fortune.
- 16th–17th c. — Renaissance political and theatrical archetype: Shakespeare (in Hamlet, King John, and Henry VI) references Nero as the quintessential matricide and bloody tyrant; political theorists invoke him as the antithesis of the good prince.
- 18th–19th c. — Victorian aesthetic critique and historical revision: John Ruskin, Thomas Babington Macaulay, and Victorian essayists deploy Nero as a devastating metaphor for decadent vanity, critical malice, and administrative negligence.
- 20th–21st c. — Universal cultural idiom: The phrase 'fiddling while Rome burns' remains a dominant political metaphor worldwide, while computing adopts the name for CD/DVD burning software (Nero Burning ROM).
