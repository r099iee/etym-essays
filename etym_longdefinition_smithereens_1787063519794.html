Core meaning. Smithereens are tiny fragments, shattered bits, or splintered pieces, almost always used after verbs of destruction: something is smashed, blown, shattered, or knocked "into smithereens." In the supplied passage, the ledge and its landmarks disappear "in a jumble of smithereens" — that is, the rock does not break into a few large slabs but into countless small fragments. The word is plural-only and cannot be used of a single piece: there is no "one smithereen."

Primary sense: small fragments from violent destruction. The core meaning of smithereens is "tiny pieces produced by smashing or explosion": "the vase shattered into smithereens," "the bomb blew the house to smithereens," "the windscreen was smashed into smithereens." The word implies two things at once: complete destruction (nothing survives intact) and smallness (the fragments are little more than bits and splinters). It is a dramatic, colloquial word — the informal partner of literary phrases such as "shattered into fragments."

Sense 2: hyperbole and figurative use. Because the word is so vivid, smithereens is constantly used hyperbolically for things that are not literally broken at all: "our plans were blown to smithereens," "the company's reputation was in smithereens," "the argument shattered the family into smithereens." Here the word means total ruin or disarray. This figurative use has made smithereens a standard word in journalism and conversation for complete collapse — economic, political, or emotional — and it works because the literal image (fragments flying everywhere) is so easy to picture.

Grammar and usage. Smithereens is a plural-only (plurale tantum) noun; the singular form smithereen is extremely rare and almost always treated as an artificial back-formation. The word takes a plural verb ("the pieces were in smithereens") and appears in fixed phrases: "into smithereens," "to smithereens," "in smithereens." The most common construction is "smashed/shattered/blown/knocked ... to (or into) smithereens." Because it is a mass of fragments rather than a countable set, it usually follows prepositions of direction ("into," "to") rather than articles ("a," "the").

Register and style. Smithereens is a bright, informal, memorable word — the kind of word a storyteller or headline writer reaches for when "fragments" or "pieces" would sound flat. It has a playful rhythm and an Irish lilt (it genuinely is an Irish word, the diminutive smidirín, "little fragment"), and English speakers have loved it since the 1830s precisely because it sounds like what it means: a shower of tiny shards. In Tolkien's passage the word works at full force: the ledge, the walls, the stones, the grass vanish not into rubble but into smithereens — a jumble of the smallest possible pieces, so that nothing of the camping ground remains to be recognised.

How the word feels in context. Notice the collocation with jumble in the source: "a jumble of smithereens." The two words reinforce each other — jumble supplies the chaos, smithereens the scale of the pieces — and together they give the reader the exact picture of a mountainside reduced to a mixed-up dust of broken stone. This stacking of "mess" words is typical of Tolkien's prose at moments of extreme destruction, and it is worth noting that each word in the chain (rock, wall, cliff, grass, stone, walls, ledge, smithereens, avalanche) does its own work of mounting devastation.
