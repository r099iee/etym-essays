Etymology of English beseech: an intensified native Germanic verb built on the 'seek' word.

1. PIE *sāg- / *sag- 'to track down, seek out, trace by scent or sign' [reconstructed].
2. Proto-Germanic *sōkijaną 'to seek, look for, try to get' [reconstructed] — cognates include Old Saxon sōkian, Old High German suohhen (German suchen), Old Norse sǿkja, Gothic sōkjan, and Old English sēcan.
3. Old English sēcan / sēcean 'to seek, try to find, visit, ask for' [attested from the earliest Old English records, 8th c.] — continues *sōkijaną.
4. Old English besēcan 'to seek out; to apply to; to entreat, beg for' [attested in Old English] — formed with the intensive/applicative prefix be- ('about, thoroughly, at'), giving 'to seek hard / to seek from someone'.
5. Middle English bisechen / besechen 'to entreat, beg earnestly, petition' [attested, 13th–15th c.].
6. Modern English beseech 'to ask urgently and fervently; to implore' [this form]; past tense besought (conservative) beside beseeched (regularized).

COGNATE NOTES
  • Latin sāgīre 'to perceive keenly, to get on the track of' belongs to the same PIE root and feeds English sagacious and presage; Greek hēgeisthai 'to lead the way' is often cited in the wider *sāg- discussion, but that connection is debated.
  • The Germanic be- prefix is the same intensive element seen in beset, bestir, bedeck: it turns 'seek' into 'seek thoroughly / seek upon someone'.
  • The verb's archaic root image is hunting and tracking — following signs until one finds what is sought; the petitionary sense adds the idea of seeking a person out in order to obtain a favour.
