SHEATHS is the plural of the native English noun sheath, which descends in an unbroken line from Proto-Germanic through Old English. The chain: 1) PIE *skei- "to cut, split, separate, divide" (reconstructed; one of the great splitting-roots of the family, source of Greek skhízō "to split" [schism], Latin scindō "to cut, rend" [rescind, abscissa], Sanskrit chinátti "he cuts off," Lithuanian skiedrà "splinter") — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *skaithiz "sheath" (reconstructed) — literally "a split-off piece": a wooden case split or hollowed to hold a blade. The same Germanic word gave Old Norse skeið, Gothic skaidus (attested in the 4th-c. Wulfila Bible), Old High German sceida (German Scheide, "sheath; divide"), Dutch schede. 3) Old English scēaþ "sheath, scabbard" (attested from c. 700 CE; plural scēaþas) — the native word for a blade-cover. 4) Middle English shethe "sheath" (attested 13th–15th c.) — the regular development of Old English scēaþ. 5) English sheath (noun, modern form) and the back-formation sheathe (verb, attested 14th c.) "to put into a sheath." 6) English sheaths (modern plural) — the regular plural of sheath — SHEATHS. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The PIE *skei- "cut/split" etymology of the Germanic sheath-word is the standard and widely accepted one; the key semantic argument is the "split-off wooden case" image, supported by parallel developments in Greek and Latin. (2) The exact shape of the Proto-Germanic form (vowel length, umlaut details) is reconstructed, not attested, but the family is securely attested in every major Germanic daughter. (3) The German cousin Scheide preserves the split/divide sense in Wasserscheide "watershed," showing the root's double life.
