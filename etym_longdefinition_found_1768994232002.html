found — In "working for day wages and found," found is the noun for food and lodging supplied in addition to money wages — the worker's keep, thrown in by the employer. On the frontier and in farm labour of the nineteenth century, to hire on "for wages and found" was the standard bargain: a man's body was housed and fed at the worksite, and that maintenance was itself a part of the pay. The literary tone here is deliberately bare and economical — the phrase is the language of the hiring fair, not of the drawing-room — and it signals a life of hard, transient labour in which a place to sleep and a meal are part of the deal. "Found" is the food and the roof, the subsistence wrapped around the wage.

Primary sense. As a noun, "found" means board and lodging, or food and lodging, provided free in addition to wages — the maintenance a worker receives as part of his hire. It is uncountable, a mass noun, and it always carries the idea of being provided for: the employer "finds" (supplies, provides) the worker in board, so the worker is said to be hired "with board and found." The phrase pairs naturally with wages: "wages and found," "fifty dollars a month and found," "room and found."

Secondary senses and the participle bridge. The noun is a hardened use of the past participle of "find" — "found" meaning "supplied, provided, equipped" (the old sense of "find" = "to provide, to furnish," as in "a well-found ship," "find one's own materials," "they found him in clothes"). Out of that participle the labour-market noun grew in the nineteenth century: what the employer "found" you in was your keep. The semantic bridge is provision: to find someone in something is to supply it, and what was found for the hired man was exactly the food and bed of the bargain.

Register, collocations, and the homograph trap.

  • Collocations: "wages and found," "board and found," "room and found," "free board and found," "a dollar a day and found," "a man's keep." The word is uncountable and historical; it belongs to nineteenth-century labour history, farm diaries, frontier novels, and the mouths of old hands.
  • The homograph trap: this "found" (past participle of "find") must not be confused with the verb "to found," meaning "to establish, to set up," which comes from Latin fundāre and its root fundus "bottom, ground" — a different word entirely, related to "foundation," "fundamental," "profound." Two English homographs, two unrelated histories.
  • Register shift: in the present the noun is archaic and historical, a fossil of the hiring bargain; it survives in dictionaries, historical fiction, and labour history, and in fixed phrases that sound quaint or period-flavoured to modern ears. It carries a quiet pun, because the provisions really are "found" for you: come upon, waiting, part of the deal.

Summary of polysemy. FOUND (this card): (1) the noun — food and lodging supplied in addition to wages; (2) the participle sense from which it grew — "provided, supplied, furnished"; (3) the collocational bundle "wages and found / board and found / room and found," the fossil of the nineteenth-century labour market. The bridge runs through the verb "find" in its old sense "to provide," and the word's whole history — treading a path, discovering, providing, hiring — is packed into one bare form.
