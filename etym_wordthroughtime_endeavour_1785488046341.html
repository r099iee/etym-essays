- [Late 14th–15th century] - From Middle English endeven, "to do one's duty, strive," literally to put oneself in devoir (duty): the word is born carrying obligation — an endeavour is an attempt one owes, not a casual try.
- [16th–17th century] - The noun and verb settle as formal words for earnest, sustained effort; Milton's "life-long endeavour" marks the dignified, heroic register the word commands.
- [18th–19th century] - Standard in law, philosophy, and civic prose for serious collective or individual striving; the British spelling endeavour (-our) becomes the badge of a learned, statesmanlike vocabulary.
- [Modern register] - Endeavour remains the formal, slightly elevated word for a serious, purposeful effort — in official prose, education, and literary criticism — keeping its old dutiful gravity while American English writes endeavor; the "duty owed" colour survives faintly in every use.
