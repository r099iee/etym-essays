• homonymy: Similar because both involve a single phonetic sound or orthographic spelling possessing multiple distinct definitions; differs because homonyms arise from separate, unrelated etymological sources that accidentally converged (e.g., river bank vs financial bank), whereas polysemy involves senses that share a common historical root and conceptual lineage.
  • ambiguity: Similar because both refer to the presence of multiple possible interpretations in speech or text; differs because ambiguity is often accidental, context-dependent, or syntactically generated (leading to potential confusion or vagueness), whereas polysemy is an intrinsic, well-ordered lexicographical property of a word's permanent semantic network.
  • equivoque: Similar because both describe words capable of signifying more than one meaning; differs because an equivoque is an intentional rhetorical pun, witty quibble, or double entendre designed to deceive or amuse, whereas polysemy is a natural, neutral structural feature of language.
  • multivalence: Similar because both describe entities possessing several simultaneous meanings, functions, or values; differs because multivalence is a broad philosophical, semiotic, or psychological term applied to cultural symbols, art, and myths, whereas polysemy is strictly a linguistic and lexicographical concept.
  • amphiboly: Similar because both produce multiple interpretations in communication; differs because amphiboly (or amphibology) arises specifically from structural or grammatical ambiguity in sentence construction (e.g., misplaced modifiers), whereas polysemy resides entirely within the lexical semantics of an individual word.
  • plurisignation: Similar because both involve the coexistence of multiple senses within a single verbal expression; differs because plurisignation is a specialized New Critical literary term reserved for deliberate aesthetic layering and poetic resonance in literature, while polysemy is a descriptive term of general linguistic science.
  • pleonasm: Similar because both relate to complexity and multiplicity within lexical usage; differs because pleonasm is rhetorical redundancy (using unnecessary surplus words, e.g., "cold ice"), whereas polysemy refers to multiple distinct meanings condensed inside a single word.
  • semantic drift: Similar because both concepts illuminate the malleability of lexical meaning; differs because semantic drift is the diachronic historical process by which a word's meaning changes across generations, whereas polysemy is the synchronic result showing multiple related senses coexisting at a single historical moment.
