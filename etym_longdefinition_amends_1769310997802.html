amends (noun, plural in form but singular in use, always appearing in the phrase "make amends" or "make amends for"): reparation, compensation, or atonement made for a loss, damage, wrong, or injury done to another person; the act of repairing a moral or material account that has been broken. Primary sense
  • Reparation for wrong — amends is the concrete or symbolic restoration offered by a person who has caused loss or harm: "He tried to make amends for his past mistakes." The word always presupposes a debt — an injury, a betrayal, a fault — and an act of restitution that is meant to balance the account and restore the relationship.
  • Compensation — in a more material register, amends can mean monetary or practical compensation for damage: "The company paid amends to the victims." The legal-historical core of the word is financial: the earliest amends were court-imposed fines.
Grammar and idiomatic behaviour
  • Plural in form, no singular — amends has no ordinary singular *"an amend" (the singular noun exists only as an archaic or technical variant). It takes a singular verb in classical usage ("amends is made") and is always paired with the verb "make" in the fixed collocations "make amends" and "make amends for."
  • Register — solemn, conscientious, moralising. To "make amends" is a deliberate act of repair, not casual apology; the idiom carries an ethical weight that simpler words like "apology" or "fix" do not.
Polysemy and related forms (the semantic family)
  • The verb amend — "to change for the better, to correct": to amend a law, a constitution, a document, a text. This is the direct verb sibling of the noun. The two diverged in register: the verb governs texts, laws, and behaviour ("amend one's ways"), while the plural noun governs restitution for wrongs. The historical bridge is Latin emendare, "to free from fault," which was applied both to correcting manuscripts and to repairing moral faults.
  • Amendment — the noun for the act of altering a law or text (a constitutional amendment); more formal and procedural than amends, which stays personal and moral.
  • The native verb mend — a clipped derivative of amend, meaning to repair torn cloth, broken tools, or (figuratively) a situation; amends retains the fuller, more dignified form.
  • Archaic "amende" / legal "amercement" — the medieval legal sense of a court-ordered fine survives in technical legal history; modern amends has largely moved from the courtroom to the conscience.
Usage and nuance in context
  • In the example "He tried to make amends for his past mistakes," the word signals active, voluntary, ongoing restitution — the attempt matters as much as the completed repair, which is why it pairs naturally with "tried."
  • Amends is distinct from apology: one can apologise in words alone, but making amends implies tangible or at least sacrificial action meant to undo harm and heal trust.
  • The word is frequently moral-religious (atonement for sin or wrongdoing) but without the explicit theology of "atonement"; it stays at the level of interpersonal and civic repair.
