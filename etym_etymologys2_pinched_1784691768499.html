=== ROOT WORD: PINCH (single root, borrowed from Old French; deeper origin debated) === Stage 1 — Origin DEBATED — the usual hypothesis: Vulgar Latin *punctiāre "to prick, puncture, sting" (late Latin, reconstructed; from Latin punctum "a puncture, a point", past participle of pungere "to prick, sting") — if this hypothesis holds, the chain reaches back to PIE *peuĝ- "to prick, stab, mark" (c. 4000–3500 BCE; the root of Latin pungere "to prick" and of English puncture, punctual, point, poignant) Stage 2 — Latin pungere "to prick, sting" / punctum "a point, a prick" (classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) Stage 3 — Vulgar Latin *punctiāre "to prick, puncture" (late Latin; the presumed source of the French verb — RECONSTRUCTED and DEBATED, not attested) Stage 4 — Old North French / Old French pincier, pincer "to pinch, squeeze, nip" (attested 12th c.) Stage 5 — Middle English pinchen "to pinch, squeeze, nip" (attested c. 1200) Stage 6 — Early Modern English pinch (16th c.) and past participle pinched (16th c.) Stage 7 — Modern English slang pinched "stolen, purloined" (attested from the 17th c.; the sense in the text) => pinched (modern English participle; here in the slang sense "stolen") DEBATE / NUANCE: The origin of French pincer is genuinely uncertain. The OED describes it as "of unknown origin"; the Vulgar Latin *punctiāre derivation is the most commonly offered hypothesis but is not proven — alternatives include a Gallo-Roman or Germanic source, and some connect it to Latin *pinsāre "to pound". The PIE *peuĝ- stage is therefore CONDITIONAL: it belongs to the chain only if the *punctiāre hypothesis is accepted. The English and French history from c. 1200 is secure; the slang theft sense is securely English and dates from the 17th century.
