Derivation chain for precipice (a compound of two Latin roots, prae + caput, presented in isolation): CHAIN FOR THE FIRST ROOT, prae 'before': 1. PIE *per(i) 'before, in front of; through, across' (well attested: Sanskrit pari 'around', Greek peri 'around', Latin per 'through'; the form *per(i) is one of the most solidly reconstructed PIE prepositions). 2. Italic *prai 'before, in front' -> Latin prae 'before, in front of' (adverb and preposition, attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions and texts, c. 6th c. BCE onward; the source of the first element of the compound). CHAIN FOR THE SECOND ROOT, caput 'head': 1. PIE *kaput- / *kauput- 'head' (well attested: Sanskrit kaput- in kapuchala- 'hair of the head', Latin caput, Gothic haubi-th, Old English heafod; the exact vowel shape is debated, and both *kaput- and *kauput- are reconstructed). 2. Latin caput (genitive capitis) 'head' (attested from the earliest Latin; also 'top, source, chief, person'; the source of the second element of the compound). THE COMPOSITE AND ITS CONTINUATION: 3. Latin praeceps (genitive praecipitis) 'headlong, head-first; steep; rash, precipitate' (a compound of prae 'before' + caput 'head', literally 'head before', i.e. falling with the head foremost; attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus and Lucretius). 4. Latin praecipitium 'a steep place, a precipice' (abstract/place noun formed on the stem praecipit- of praeceps with the suffix -ium, literally 'the place of headlong falling'; attested in classical and late Latin). 5. Middle French precipice 'a steep place, a precipice' (12th-16th c.; a learned borrowing from Latin praecipitium). 6. English precipice 'a very steep or perpendicular rock face, a cliff' (borrowed c. 1590 from Middle French; the figurative 'brink of disaster' sense is recorded from the 17th c.). Note on the debate: the PIE root behind Latin caput is secure in outline, but its exact vocalism is disputed (*kaput- versus *kauput-), and some scholars treat the head-words of Latin and Germanic as independent formations on a common base. The Latin compound praeceps itself is transparent (prae + caput) and undisputed; the only real uncertainties lie one stage deeper, in the internal shape of *kaput-. The English word has also competed historically with the doublet precipitance and the adjective precipitous, which derive from the same Latin family.
