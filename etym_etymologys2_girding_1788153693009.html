PIE(reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE)
*gherdh-
"to encircle, enclose, gird"
(extended form of *gher- "to enclose"; cf. Goth. gairdan, ON gyrða, OE gyrdan)
↓
Proto-Germanic(reconstructed, c. 500 BCE – 200 CE)
*gurdan
"to gird, encircle"
↓
Old English(attested 8th–11th c.)
gyrdan
"to gird, surround, invest with a belt"
(cf. gyrdel "girdle, belt")
↓
Middle English(12th–15th c.)
girden
"to encircle, to belt on, to prepare"
(irregular pret. girt; regularised later to girded)
↓
Modern English(16th c. – )
girding
"encircling; bracing oneself for action"
(participial/gerundial form; idiom "girding of the loins" from the Bible)

Note on the debate — the PIE stage is a reconstruction: the root "gherdh-" is inferred from the Germanic and Baltic sets, and the -dh- extension is debated (Pokorny groups it under *gherdh-, while some scholars derive the verb directly from *gher- "to enclose"). No pre-Germanic attested form exists.
