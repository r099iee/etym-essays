Betook is the past tense of betake, and to tell its story one must tell the story of its two parts: the intensive prefix be- and the verb take. Take itself is a Viking word. When Old English had long served itself with the native verb niman for "to take," the Scandinavian settlers of the Danelaw brought with them Old Norse taka, "to take, to grasp, to seize," and within a few centuries the loanword tacan had displaced the native verb altogether — a striking case of one everyday word quietly replacing another in the spoken language. Behind Old Norse taka stands the reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *takan-, and behind that, if the uncertain link is right, the Indo-European base *deǵ-, "to take, to grasp," which may also surface in Greek dékhomai (δέχομαι), "to take, to receive," and possibly in Latin doceō, "to teach," if teaching is understood as making someone take something in. The Oxford English Dictionary, however, warns that the Germanic family of take may have no secure cognates outside itself, so this deeper Indo-European connection must be labelled probable rather than certain. What is beyond dispute is the purely Germanic anatomy of the English word: take is a strong verb (take, took, taken), and its odd past form took, with the vowel shifted by the old class-six alternation, is native to the North Sea speech that English inherited.

On to this solid Germanic base, English added the intensifying prefix be-, the same prefix seen in bestir, belabour, and bedevil. The compound bitaken (Middle English) meant at first "to give up, to hand over, to entrust": one betakes a message, a task, or a person into the care of another. It was a short step, in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, to turn the verb back upon the subject. To betake oneself is to hand oneself over to a place or a course of action — to commit one's whole person to the journey — and from this reflexive construction the word acquired its modern, slightly archaic sense, "to go, to repair to." The strong past betook then followed the model of took with complete regularity. There is a further twist worth recording: Old English already had a native verb betǣcan, "to hand over, to deliver, to commend" (built on tǣcan, "to teach," the ancestor of modern teach), which was so similar in sound and sense to the newer betake that the two histories were long confused. Careful etymology now keeps them apart, but the centuries of confusion themselves show how powerfully the "commit" idea clung to this little cluster of words.

The semantic journey of betook is therefore a journey from physical grasping to moral resolve. In Old Norse, taka was the raw act of seizing — taking hold of a thing in the hand, as a Viking takes a sword or a prize. In the English compound betake oneself, that grasping became self-directed and metaphorical: the subject seizes hold of his own intentions and delivers himself to a destination. The word enjoyed its golden age in the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries as the standard literary verb for solemn departure — betook himself to his chambers, betook to flight, betook to study — dignified and ceremonious in narrative prose, oratory, and biblical English. Its nineteenth-century decline carried it out of everyday speech, so that it survived in novels as a period-flavoured formality, often for resolute or hasty departure; in contemporary use it is archaic and literary, kept for elevated or mock-heroic effect, as when a wit says that he "betook himself to the pub." It is precisely the note of self-committal that the novel wants. When Yu-cun, dressed in his best, betakes himself to the gate of the Rong mansion, the verb tells us that this is no casual stroll but a deliberate act — he has taken himself up, composed his person, and placed himself, ready, at the threshold of the powerful household. The word's archaism only strengthens the effect, lending the scene a formal, almost ceremonial gravity that a plain "went" would have destroyed. Betook, in short, is the past tense of a decision.

  • Old Norse taka "to seize" → Old English tacan — the Viking loan displaces native niman (9th–11th c.).
  • be- + take → Middle English bitaken "to hand over, entrust" (12th–15th c.).
  • Reflexive betake oneself "to commit oneself; to go to" (16th c.) — grasping becomes self-directed resolve.
  • betook, the strong past on the model of took (16th c.) — the completed act of self-committal.
  • betǣcan "to deliver" (from tǣcan "to teach") — the native sister long confused with betake.
  • 16th–18th c. golden age in solemn literary prose; 19th-c. decline; modern mock-heroic register.
