Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*kʷetwóres
"four (numeral)"
[Source of Sanskrit catvāras, Greek tessares, and Germanic *fedwōr]
↓
Proto-Italic (reconstructed)
*kʷartos
"fourth (ordinal numeral)"
[Regular ordinal formation from the cardinal numeral four]
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
quartus → quartārius / quartum
"fourth part, quarter, fourth"
[Basis of Latin castra quadrata: four-quadrant Roman military camps]
↓
Vulgar Latin (c. 5th c.)
*quartāre / *quarticāre
"to divide into four parts; to partition"
[Verbal formation from the fractional noun quartus]
↓
Old French (12th c.)
quartier (noun) / quarteler (verb)
"fourth part; district of city; barracks"
[French cities divided into four administrative quartiers]
↓
Middle English (c. 1350)
quarteren
"to divide into four parts; dismember"
[Adopted into English law for penal execution and heraldry]
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1580–1650)
quarter (verb) + -ed
"billeted in lodgings; divided into four"
[Shift from military quarters to the action of housing soldiers or guests]
↓
Modern English (present)
quartered
"lodged, housed; divided into four parts"
[Describing cooks, dwarves, and guests lodged at Bag End]
