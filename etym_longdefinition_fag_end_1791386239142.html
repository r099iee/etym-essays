The compound noun fag-end denotes the frayed, coarse, untwisted, or imperfect end of a piece of woven cloth or nautical rope, and by extensive figurative extension, the final, worn-out, spent, or least valuable remnant of anything — such as time, an era, an event, a conversation, or a cigarette. In the literary context provided (“There was a __________ of sunset still functioning”), P. G. Wodehouse deploys the term with characteristic stylistic brilliance: he takes a phrase typically associated with discarded cigarette butts, frayed cordage, and exhausted tail-ends, and applies it irreverently to the sublime evening sky. Rather than describing a magnificent, glowing twilight, Bertie Wooster observes merely the spent, ragged, leftover dregs of the sunset sputtering out like an expiring match on the western horizon.

Historically rooted in English manufacturing and maritime trades, fag-end evolved across centuries through several distinct yet interconnected domains of human activity:

  • Textile Manufacturing and Weaving: In traditional handloom and early industrial weaving, the fag-end was the coarse, rough, uneven end of a web or piece of woven cloth. This section was attached to the loom beams, held by the weaver’s clamps, or woven with inferior, discarded yarn to secure the piece. When the bolt of fine cloth was cut free and prepared for high-end retail, the fag-end remained as a frayed, flawed remnant of negligible commercial value, commonly sold at a steep discount to the poor or discarded as waste scrap.
  • Maritime and Seafaring Terminology: English sailors and riggers adopted the term to describe the untwisted, frayed end of a hemp rope, cable, or hawser that had lost its whipping (the protective twine binding wound around the tip). A line whose strands had unraveled into a fuzzy, useless tuft was called a fag-end. Sailors regularly chopped off these damaged tips and unpicked their fibers to create oakum — loose hemp used with pine pitch to caulk the seams of wooden ships.
  • Colloquial British Tobacco Culture: In nineteenth- and twentieth-century British, Irish, and Commonwealth slang, a cigarette came to be commonly known as a fag (likely an elliptical shortening of fag-end itself, referring originally to the paper-wrapped stump or the cheap paper tubes filled with leftover tobacco scraps). Consequently, fag-end developed a ubiquitous domestic meaning as the unsmoked butt, stub, or spent filter of a cigarette or cigar left behind in an ashtray or ground out on the pavement.
  • Social, Political, and Institutional Dregs: Figuratively, the term came to represent the lowest, most degraded, or least reputable element of an assembly, community, or political administration. Writers from Jonathan Swift to Walter Scott wrote of the “fag-end of the ministry” or the “fag-end of the multitude,” referring to the corrupt remnants of an expiring government or the disreputable fringes of a mob. Furthermore, in the traditional English public school hierarchy, where younger boys were forced to act as personal servants or “fags” to senior prefects (performing tedious drudgery), the term carried additional resonance with weary, servile leftover chores.
  • Temporal and Experiential Exhaustion: The most prominent literary usage today is temporal: “the fag-end of the day,” “the fag-end of summer,” “the fag-end of a holiday,” or “the fag-end of an empire.” It conveys an atmosphere of melancholy, depleted vitality, and anticlimax — the weary interval when all excitement, beauty, or productivity has departed, leaving only tired people cleaning up debris or enduring the final dull moments before an inevitable conclusion.
  • Polysemy Bridge: The underlying conceptual metaphor connecting all these senses is fraying at the structural boundary. Whether one considers the physical threads unraveling at the edge of a bolt of linen, the hemp strands coming undone at the tip of a ship’s line, the smoldering stub of rolled tobacco paper, or the last dim sliver of twilight fading before nightfall, the fag-end is always the compromised, inferior edge where the substance of the thing dissolves into nothingness.

In terms of register, fag-end is primarily British, Commonwealth, and Irish English, functioning as a countable noun typically preceded by the definite article (the fag-end of...). While slightly colloquial and informal in speech, it possesses an established literary pedigree in classic British prose, where it injects vivid, slightly cynical realism into descriptions of landscapes, time, or human institutions.
