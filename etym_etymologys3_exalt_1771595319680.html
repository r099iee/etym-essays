The biography of exalt begins, surprisingly, not with height but with the barnyard and the nursery. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂el-, “to grow, to nourish, to feed” — a root of organic increase, of mothering and feeding, closer in spirit to a farmyard sow or a wet-nurse than to a throne. In Latin this root produced the verb alō, alere, “to nourish, rear,” and from it the family of alimentum (food), alimōnia (nourishment — whence the legal word alimony, the money that “nourishes” a former spouse), and alma, the nourishing — so that even the august Latin of Alma Mater, the nourishing mother-university, is the direct descendant of a pig’s swill-bucket. More important for our word is the past participle altus: “grown,” hence “high.” Latin altus was famously ambiguous, meaning at once “high” and “deep” — altum mare, the deep sea — because growth can be measured in either direction. The whole modern geography of height is built from this ambiguity: altitude is a measure of how far something has grown upward. From altus, Latin built exaltāre, “to raise up on high,” prefixing the outward-upward ex-. In classical Latin the verb was literal — to lift a physical thing. But its decisive career was in Christian Latin, above all in the Vulgate Bible, where exaltāre became a verb of worship: exaltabo te, Domine, “I will exalt thee, O Lord” (Psalm 30), and the humbling promise that “he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.” It is this devotional colouring, not any Roman engineering, that Old French exalter and Middle English exalten carried into English in the fourteenth century. From the first, therefore, the English word was double-natured: physically to raise, devotionally to glorify. Meanwhile, along the Germanic branch of the same root, the sense of “grown” hardened into a word for age: Proto-Germanic *aldaz, “grown-up, mature,” gave Old English eald and modern old, with elder, eldest, and alderman — the elder being literally the more ‘grown’ man — and German alt, “old.” In Celtic, Old Irish alim, “I nourish, rear,” and Welsh alu, “to grow, produce young,” kept the root’s original meaning of feeding and mothering alive, while Lithuanian augti, “to grow,” is a distant cousin. The root, in other words, was one of the most ancient and generative in the family — and the word exalt is only one of its many well-dressed descendants. In English the word’s career unfolded along both of its inherited registers. The political and literal sense — to raise in rank or power — flourished in the language of monarchy, government, and society: kings and ministers were exalted to office, the humble were exalted from obscurity, and the word became the standard verb for elevation in the hierarchy of state. The devotional sense — to glorify — dominated religious prose and the language of the psalm, and it is in this sense that the word reached its highest intensity: exalt was the verb for what prayer and praise do to God, the ‘lifting up’ of the divine name. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word also took a technical turn in alchemy and early science, where exaltation meant the refinement or purification of a substance — raising a metal or essence to a higher, more subtilised state — a sense preserved in the now-rare noun exaltation. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word had settled into its modern double life: formal, ceremonial, and literary, used of rank, glory, and praise. It is this double inheritance that John Ruskin exploits in the Preface to Modern Painters (1843). The sentence — “To pour our flattery into the ear of Death, and exalt, with vain acclamation, the names of those who neither demand our praise, nor regard our gratitude” — turns the liturgical verb against the Victorian public. Exalt is the psalmist’s verb, the word of worship and royal elevation; to spend it upon the dead, who can neither hear nor require the praise, is to empty the highest register of the language of honour. The higher the solemnity of the word, the sharper the irony: an act of glorification performed with ‘vain acclamation’ on those beyond hearing is homage at once empty and, because it blinds the public to living merit, actively harmful. Ruskin’s exalt thus carries the entire history of the word — from the feeding of the nursery to the throne of God — and lets that history do its satirical work: the language of genuine reverence, misapplied, exposes the vanity of the age. Today exalt remains a high-register, formal word. Its everyday work is done by raise, elevate, and praise; exalt itself keeps the ceremonial weight of glory, rank, and worship. It survives in the noun exaltation (both the state of being raised in glory and the psychological state of intense joy or rapture), in the adjective exalted (elevated, lofty, as in ‘an exalted position’), and in fixed religious and rhetorical phrases. Its fate is that of many words of liturgical origin: retained for its intensity, and increasingly rare in ordinary speech — yet still the only verb that can carry the full weight of raising someone or something to the very height of glory. The word that began in the mouth of a nurse feeding a child has ended, across three and a half millennia, as the language of kings, psalmists, and — in Ruskin’s bitter sentence — the empty homage of the Victorian public to the deaf ears of the dead.
