pastoral (adjective; by extension a noun). The primary sense relates to shepherds and the tending of flocks: of, belonging to, or concerned with the keeping and grazing of sheep and cattle — a pastoral life, pastoral duties, pastoral land. Etymologically the word is “of the shepherd” (Latin pāstor “shepherd,” from pāscere “to feed, graze”). From that literal sense the word grew its most famous meaning: pastoral designates the literary and artistic tradition, beginning with Theocritus and Virgil, that idealizes the life of shepherds and the countryside — the golden age of singing herdsmen, green Arcadia, and innocent ease. In this sense pastoral is both an adjective (pastoral poetry) and a noun (a pastoral is a poem, play, painting, or piece of music of this kind).

The passage. In the sentence “He wanders west as far as Memphis, a solitary migrant upon that flat and pastoral landscape,” the adjective does something quietly unsettling. The flat Mississippi delta around Memphis is farmland, yes, but the passage denies the reader every consolation the word normally brings: there are no singing shepherds, no green Arcadia, no peace of rural retirement. The landscape is flat, the traveler solitary, and pastoral is stripped of its prettiness and left with only its agricultural bones — land that feeds men, land of work and weather. The tone is elegiac and ironic at once: the novel invokes the pastoral tradition only to show it exhausted, a country that once promised the shepherd his ease now holding nothing for a migrant but emptiness. The word's resonance is that of a broken ideal — the classical dream of rural simplicity recollected over a dead land.

Polysemy and secondary senses. (1) Primary (literal): of shepherds, flocks, and the grazing of livestock; a pastoral farm is one given to pasture. (2) Literary and artistic: of the genre idealizing rural life; a pastoral poem, painting, or symphony (Beethoven's Pastoral Symphony, the pastorale in music). (3) Religious: of the care of souls — pastoral ministry, pastoral care, pastoral counseling — because the minister is the shepherd (pastor) of his flock; the adjective descends directly from the same shepherd word. (4) Attitudinal: rustic, rural, or simple in an idealized way — “pastoral charm,” “pastoral quiet.” (5) Modern irony: the word now often signals a broken or exhausted dream — a flat working farmland stripped of Arcadia — used with deliberate irony of real countrysides. Each sense is a single chain of bridges: the shepherd (Latin pāstor) feeds his flock; the feeding becomes the grazing land; the grazing land becomes the idealized countryside of literature; the shepherd becomes the priest who feeds his congregation; and the ideal, once established, becomes available for irony when it meets the actual land.

  • Primary sense: of shepherds and the keeping or grazing of sheep and cattle; of pasture land.
  • Secondary sense: of the literary/artistic genre idealizing rural life — pastoral poetry, painting, music; the noun names such a work.
  • Tertiary sense: of the care of souls — pastoral care, ministry, counseling — from the pastor as shepherd.
  • Attitudinal sense: rustic, idyllic, quaintly simple; “pastoral charm.”
  • Historical bridge: Latin pāstor “shepherd” → pāstorālis “of a shepherd” → Old French pastoral; the shepherd feeds the flock, so feeding → pasturing → idealized countryside → spiritual feeding of a congregation — one continuous chain of metaphor.
