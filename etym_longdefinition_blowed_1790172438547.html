blowed is a dialectal and colloquial past tense and past participle of blow, surviving in modern standard English almost only in fixed exclamations, above all I’m blowed and blow me (down), meaning I’m damned, I’m astonished, I can hardly believe it. In the source context — Well, I’m blowed — the speaker registers blunt surprise or dismay with a respectable oath: not quite swearing, but borrowing the force of swearing. It is the period equivalent of I’m damned, I’ll be blowed, stone the crows, blow me down, softened for mixed company yet still emphatic.

Grammatically the form is instructive. Standard modern blow (to move air) has past blew and participle blown: the wind blew, the fuse is blown. Blowed is the older regular weak formation (blow + -ed) retained in dialects, in sea and stable slang, and in euphemism, where fixed phrases freeze archaic morphology: I’m blowed if I know, well I’m blowed, blowed if he didn’t. To say blowed therefore marks speech as working-class, nautical, Dickensian, or deliberately period, against the neutral blown of careful prose. Wodehouse puts it in the mouths of butlers, valets, policemen, and startled aunts precisely to voice that register without actual profanity.

  • Exclamatory euphemism sense (dominant today) — I’m blowed / blow me: mild oath of astonishment, dismay, or emphatic denial. Example: Well, I’m blowed, it’s Gussie in a beard; I’m blowed if I’m going to pay for that. The bridge is euphemistic deformation: blow stands in for blast, damn, bugger (blow me for blast me), and the passive I’m blowed imagines the speaker knocked over by news, as wind blows a man down. Nautical blow me down (knock me flat) is the fuller ancestor.
  • Literal winded / inflated senses (archaic and dialectal): out of breath from blowing, swollen with air, wind-blown. Example: the horse was quite blowed after the hill (winded); blowed-up bladder (inflated). The bridge is direct participle of blow before blown fully won: to be blowed was to have had air forced in or out of one. Now largely replaced by blown, winded, out of breath, but still heard in stable and hunting talk.
  • Emphatic negative sense — blowed if: damned if, certainly not. Example: I’m blowed if I know; blowed if he’ll admit it. The bridge is oath becoming conjunction: swearing astonishment hardens into a formula for refusal. Parallel to damned if, hanged if, buggered if, but milder and now quaint.
  • Social-register nuance: in 1820–1890 I’m blowed was respectable working-class respectability — strong enough to show feeling, clean enough for the parlour. Dickens uses it for cabmen, beadles, and Sairey Gimps; sailors for forecastle surprise. In Wodehouse it is lightly comic period furniture: Bertie’s world still says I’m blowed where later generations say no way, you’re kidding, bloody hell. Using it now signals affectionate archaism or deliberate Bertie-voice.
  • Confusable blow verbs: English has three historic blows — blow (air, from Old English blawan), blow (bloom, from blowan), and blow (strike, of obscure origin in blow as hit). I’m blowed belongs unambiguously to the air-verb via blow me down, not to blossom or hitting, though folk etymology sometimes hears a punch in it. The standard participle split (blown for air, blowed frozen in oaths) helps keep them apart.

Phonetically blowed is /bləʊd/, rhyming with flowed, showed, against blown /bləʊn/. It collocates with well, I’m, blow me, blowed if. To write it today is to quote the nineteenth century; to say it is to choose comic understatement over real profanity, letting breath stand in for blasphemy.
