Linear derivation chain for English scorch (single root word; two component ancestries, presented separately, then the attested blend into the modern word). HYPOTHESIS A — the Germanic component (shrivelling, drying, contracting): Proto-Indo-European *sker- "to cut, scrape, shred" (reconstructed root, c. 4500–3000 BCE; the parent of shear, share, shard, shirt, sharp, score, scar, scrape, scrub — all built on cutting or scratching) │ └─> Proto-Germanic *skurpan- "to shrink, shrivel" (a -p- extension of the cut-root, the cutting idea transferred to the drying, contracting, pinched-up look of tissue losing moisture) └─> Old Norse skorpna "to shrivel, dry up, shrink" (attested, said of skin and leaves) └─> (the semantic family also includes Middle Low German schrumpen, Dutch schrompelen, German schrumpfen "to shrink, shrivel" — same semantic family, possibly the same extended root) HYPOTHESIS B — the Romance component (stripping, flaying, searing surfaces): Latin excorticāre "to strip off bark, flay" (formed from ex- "off, out of" + cortex, cortic- "bark, rind"; cortex itself possibly from *(s)ker- "to cut" — bark as the stripped layer) └─> Old French escorchier "to flay, strip the skin from a body" (attested, a violent word of skinning) ├─> Modern French écorcher "to skin, flay; to overcharge" (living descendant) ├─> Italian scorticare "to flay, skin, graze" └─> Spanish descorchar "to uncork" (literally "to strip the cork-bark") THE BLEND into English: Old Norse skorpna (shrivelling, drying) + Old French escorchier (flaying, searing surfaces) └─> Middle English scorchen "to burn the surface, to dry up" (first attested c. 1400) └─> English scorch "to burn and shrivel a surface with dry heat; to parch" (modern verb) ├─> scorched (past tense and past participle; "scorched earth", "scorched grass") └─> scorching (present participle and adjective; "scorching heat") └─> SCORCH (the exact form of this card) Note on the debate: the etymology of scorch is genuinely contested. Hypothesis A derives the word from the Germanic shrinkage-family via Old Norse skorpna, on the strength of the drying, shrivelling sense; Hypothesis B derives it from Latin excorticāre via Old French escorchier, on the strength of the searing, stripping sense and the near-identical sound (escorchier / scorch). Most modern etymologists accept a blend of both — the two streams converging on the single Middle English form scorchen c. 1400 — but the proportion of each is debated, and the OED treats the origin as disputed. The chain above records both ancestries honestly and does not force a single line; every stage cited is attested except the reconstructed PIE root *sker- and the reconstructed Proto-Germanic *skurpan-, which are standard reconstructions. No stage is invented.
