- Ancient India to Medieval Persia - Originates as Sanskrit *nīla* ("dark blue, sapphire, indigo"), shifting in Persian to *nīlak* ("bluish, bluish plant"), naming indigo-tinted flora.
- 16th Century (Late Renaissance) - Borrowed via Ottoman Turkish *leylak* into Arabic and Spanish/Italian (*lila*); introduced to Western Europe c. 1565 by Imperial ambassador Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, who sends lilac specimens from Constantinople to Vienna.
- Early to Mid-17th Century - Enters English (c. 1625–1670) via French *lilas*, initially written as *lilac* or *lelacke*, strictly designating the newly imported ornamental flowering shrub *Syringa vulgaris*.
- Late 18th Century (Georgian Era) - Completes a metonymic shift from the physical plant to the color itself (first recorded as an English color adjective c. 1774), rapidly adopted into high fashion and domestic textiles.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes culturally central: utilized as a mandatory shade for "half-mourning" attire in strict British etiquette; heavily featured in Romantic floriography and canonical poetry (Walt Whitman's 1865 Lincoln elegy).
- 20th to 21st Century - Cemented as an essential palette element in modern cosmetics, interior design, synthetic perfumery accords, and spring garden landscaping worldwide.
