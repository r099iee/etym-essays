Earliest stage to present BLEAT: - Proto-Indo-European *bhle- (also reconstructed *bhlē-) "to howl, cry out, bleat" — an imitative/expressive root; the cry is the sense (c. 5th–4th millennium BCE). - Proto-Germanic *blētijaną "to bleat, make a sheep's cry" — built with the -t- formative common to Germanic animal-cry verbs. - Old English blǣtan "to bleat like a sheep" (recorded 8th–11th c.). - Middle English bleten "to bleat; to cry out feebly" (13th c.). - Modern English BLEAT "to utter the weak, plaintive cry of a sheep or goat; to speak in a feeble, plaintive manner" (verb inherited from Old English; noun "the cry of a sheep, goat, or calf" attested c. 1500). Note on the debate: the family of imitative forms across branches (Ancient Greek βληχή "bleating; wailing of children", Latin flēre "to weep", Old Church Slavonic blejati and Russian блеять "to bleat", Germanic blǣtan/blöken) strongly suggests one inherited expressive base *bhle-, but because the word is onomatopoeic, independent parallel imitations in separate branches cannot be entirely excluded; the exact reconstruction of the PIE form is therefore uncertain.
