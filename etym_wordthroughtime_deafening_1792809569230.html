- Old English — the sealed ear: deaf means unable to hear, and by extension empty, barren, and unfeeling — deaf nuts with no kernel, deaf earth that hears no prayer.
- 14th–16th c. — to deafen: the verb appears, meaning to make deaf by clamour or violence; brawls, bells, and cannon begin their long work on English ears.
- 1590s — the participle as firework: deafening is first used of overwhelming noise — ordnance, tempests, and shouting multitudes in Elizabethan chronicles and plays.
- 17th–18th c. — pulpits and poetry: preachers warn of deafening thunder as heaven’s artillery; poets borrow the word for cataracts, battles, and the applause of crowds.
- 19th c. — industry turns it up: steam hammers, locomotives, and ironworks make deafening the daily adjective of the factory town; the paradox of deafening silence is born in the quiet after expected sound.
- 20th c. — amplification everywhere: rock concerts, jet engines, and stadium crowds push the word past all earlier volumes; occupational medicine gives it a literal billing — deafening work genuinely deafens.
- Today — the compliment of noise: deafening cheers, deafening welcomes, deafening finales — the word now praises what it once merely suffered, and Tolkien’s dragon still sets the standard.
