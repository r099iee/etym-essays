Derivation chain for bear / borne: 1. PIE *bher- 'to carry, bear, bring' (one of the best-attested Indo-European roots: Sanskrit bharami 'I carry', Greek phero 'I carry', Latin fero 'I carry, bear', Armenian berem 'I carry'). 2. Proto-Germanic *berana- (strong verb, class IV) 'to carry, bear, give birth, endure, support' (reconstructed from Old English beran, Old Saxon beran, Old High German beran, Old Norse bera, Gothic bairan). 3. Old English beran (strong verb, class IV, 8th-11th c.) 'to carry, bring, bear, give birth, endure, sustain, produce' - past participle boren, attested throughout the Old English corpus, including Beowulf and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. 4. Middle English beren / born(e) (12th-15th c.) 'to carry, bring forth, endure' - the past participle appears as born, borne, iboren in free variation; by the end of the period the spellings begin to diverge. 5. Modern English bear (verb) and its past participle borne (16th c. onward) - the orthographic separation of born (birth only) from borne (all other senses) was consolidated by 17th-century printers; the modern form shows the regular loss of the unstressed medial vowel of boren (syncope) and the lengthening of the vowel. Note on the debate: the split of born vs borne is purely orthographic and late (16th-17th c.), not phonetic or semantic at the time of the split; the two forms were pronounced alike in Early Modern English and were separated by printers for convenience. The final -e in borne is silent today but serves to mark the participle against the noun and the adjective.
