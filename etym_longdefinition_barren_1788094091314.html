Barren (adjective) describes something that fails to produce what it should: land that grows little or no vegetation, a plant or animal that bears no fruit or young, or — figuratively — an effort, mind, or discussion that yields nothing. Its emotional colouring is negative: barren places are empty, bleak and lifeless; barren outcomes are fruitless and disappointing. In the reading context the word describes land that was once 'green and fair' and has become bleak — a wasteland that no longer yields.

Primary senses.

  • Of land and soil: producing little or no vegetation; infertile, unproductive, unable to support crops or pasture — 'a barren desert', 'barren ground', 'barren soil'. In ecology and geography the barrens (plural noun) are tracts of poor, unproductive land, as in the Pine Barrens of New Jersey or the Canadian Barren Grounds.
  • Of animals and people: unable to bear young; sterile. Historically applied openly to women ('a barren woman'), this sense is now largely replaced by the clinical 'infertile', because 'barren' carries a heavy, often cruel judgement; it survives in biblical and literary register.
  • Of plants: bearing no fruit or seed — 'a barren fig tree'; also barren of flowers or leaves, as with a sterile hybrid.
  • Figurative — of mental or creative activity: producing no results, ideas or profit — 'a barren discussion', 'a barren imagination', 'years of barren research'. Here the failure to 'bear fruit' is transferred from the orchard to the mind.
  • Figurative — of emptiness and bleakness: empty of life, comfort, interest or meaning — 'a barren room', 'a barren existence', 'a barren landscape'. In this sense barren approaches 'desolate', though it stresses unproductiveness where desolate stresses abandonment.
  • Technical senses: in geology and mining, barren rock or barren ground contains no ore; in medicine 'barren' is an older term for infertile; in forestry barren land is treeless; in astronomy a 'barren' planet or moon is lifeless and geologically inert.

Semantic bridges. The thread uniting every sense is the idea of bearing: Old French baraigne meant 'sterile', and the word's history runs from the womb to the soil to the mind. A woman who bears no children, a field that bears no crops, a tree that bears no fruit, a mind that bears no ideas, a landscape that bears nothing at all — each is 'barren' because each fails to bring forth. The modern shift toward places and outcomes (rather than persons) reflects both the clinical replacement of the human sense and the growing use of the word to describe environmental emptiness.

Register and collocations. Barren collocates strongly with land, ground, desert, wasteland, hills, plains, island, rock, tree, womb (literary), discussion, debate, years, effort, imagination, and existence. It frequently appears in doublets with 'bleak' and 'desolate' ('bleak and barren', 'barren and empty'), exactly as in the source sentence. The noun barrenness names the state; the verb to barren (to make barren) is rare and poetic. In the King James Bible the word is prominent — Sarai, Rachel, Hannah and Elizabeth are all called barren — which gives it a scriptural echo even in secular use.
