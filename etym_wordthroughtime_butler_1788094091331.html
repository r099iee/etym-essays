- (c. 1250, Middle English boteler via Anglo-Norman buteler / Old French bouteillier “cup-bearer”) - A household officer in charge of the wine; a position of real trust in castles and manors, and a royal office — in France the Grand Bouteillier was an officer of the crown.
- (14th–16th c.) - The butler’s duties widen from wine to plate, silver, and table service; the office becomes formalised in great houses — “butler” is a rank, not just a job.
- (17th–19th c.) - Golden age of the English butler: chief of the male servants, keeper of keys and cellar book, emblem of the well-run country house; Victorian fiction fixes the type as the discreet guardian of household order.
- (20th c.) - The great houses decline; the butler becomes a rare, elite profession; literature and film turn him into an icon of discretion and stiff upper lip — Jeeves (1915–), Ishiguro’s Stevens (1989), Downton Abbey’s Carson (2010–) — and “the butler did it” enters detective-story folklore.
- (today) - The butler survives in luxury hotels, cruise ships, and private yachts as a personal service attendant; “butler’s pantry”, “butler’s sink”, and “butler service” live on in design and computing — the wine-keeper has become the world’s symbol of impeccable service.
