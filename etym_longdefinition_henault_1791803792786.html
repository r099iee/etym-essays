Henault is a French family name, written in modern French Hénault, and in this reading it denotes one particular bearer: Charles-Jean-François Hénault (1685–1770), magistrate, man of letters and historian, habitually styled le président Hénault from the office he held in the Parlement de Paris. Carlyle's sentence — “President Henault, remarking on royal Surnames of Honour how difficult it often is to ascertain not only why, but even when, they were conferred...” — points to Hénault's historical work, above all the Abrégé chronologique de l'histoire de France (1744), a compressed year-by-year chronicle of the monarchy that became one of the most reprinted historical works of the eighteenth century. The word is therefore a pure proper noun: it has no common-noun senses, no plural of its own, and no figurative life outside the name itself.

In Carlyle the name carries an ironic charge. “His sleek official way” is not neutral praise: Carlyle presents the président as the smooth, well-fed voice of ancien-régime official history, a man who can note the absurdity of royal nicknames while remaining perfectly comfortable inside the system that manufactured them. The quotation is a hinge. It introduces Carlyle's own survey of honorific surnames — le Bien-Aimé, le Grand, le Sage — and quietly asks why the crowd bestows such titles, and how quickly it withdraws them.

Who the bearer was

  • Magistrate. Born in Paris in 1685 into a family of the robe, Hénault became a conseiller at the Parlement de Paris in 1705 and later a président of one of its chambers; the title président was a judicial office, not the presidency of the whole court in the modern sense.
  • Academician and salon figure. Elected to the Académie française in 1723, he was for decades the intimate friend and correspondent of Madame du Deffand, and a familiar of the philosophical and libertine salons that Carlyle elsewhere treats with such suspicion.
  • Historian. The Abrégé chronologique arranged French history by year and reign, mixing political narrative with anecdotes, portraits and bons mots; its readability made it a bestseller, and its anecdotal method is exactly what “his sleek official way” is designed to evoke.
  • Witness to the episode. Hénault's discussion of the surname Bien-Aimé belongs to the same 1740s moment as Louis XV's illness at Metz, the event that links Flanders, Alsace and Metz in this batch of cards.

What the name denotes beyond the man

  • A historiographical label. “Président Hénault” functions in French literary history as shorthand for a whole style of writing history: elegant, anecdotal, court-centred, more interested in character and anecdote than in causes.
  • A bibliographic key. Library catalogues and eighteenth-century studies use the bare surname for the Abrégé and the Nouvel abrégé, so that “Hénault” on a title page may denote the author rather than a subject.
  • No common-noun extension. Unlike eponymous scientists, Hénault has produced no unit, law, syndrome or device; the name never became a common noun, and outside French historical writing it survives chiefly as a surname.

Form, accent and pronunciation

  • Accent. French writes Hénault with an acute accent on the first e; English texts of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries routinely drop it, hence Carlyle's Henault.
  • Silent ending. The final -t is silent in French, and the -ault group is pronounced as a single vowel, so the name rhymes with Renault.
  • Look-alike trap. French Hérault — the river and the département — is written almost identically and is constantly confused with Hénault in catalogues, but it is pronounced with a sounded r and has an entirely different, pre-Latin origin; an English reader who “corrects” Carlyle's Henault to Hérault introduces a real error.

Register and connotation

The name is formal, bookish and slightly antiquarian. In French it evokes the polished, sceptical culture of the Regency and the early reign of Louis XV; in Carlyle's English it acquires a faintly mocking colour, the name of a well-dressed official who notices everything and changes nothing. To use it today is to signal familiarity with eighteenth-century French historiography rather than with the man's own writings, which are now read chiefly by specialists.

Why it matters in this reading

The sentence attributed to Hénault is the interpretive key to the surrounding passage: it turns a list of royal nicknames into a problem of evidence. If even the contemporaries of Louis XV could not say for certain when or why the title Bien-Aimé was conferred, then the epithet is shown to be a social fact rather than an official honour — something the crowd gives and the crowd takes away. Recognizing the speaker as a président of the Parlement, a historian of the monarchy and a salon celebrity clarifies why Carlyle grants him a hearing and then undercuts him in the same clause.
