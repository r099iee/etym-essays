Tsarevna, from Russian царевна, is in its strict historical sense the daughter of a tsar — the feminine counterpart of tsarevich, the tsar's son, and carefully distinct from tsarina, the tsar's wife. It is a dynastic title of blood rather than of marriage, borne by the female offspring of the Russian autocrat, and after Peter the Great's regularisation of court titulature its holders carried elevated styles at court, ranked among the imperial family, and figured as assets of state diplomacy, destined for foreign thrones or great domestic houses, their dowries and conversions matters of cabinet discussion.

In the source sentence the word appears three times as a title prefix — the sisters of Tsarevitch Alexei are named as Tsarevna Anna, Tsarevna Olga and Tsarevna Helena — and here the sense is frankly folkloric rather than bureaucratic. The tale is the Russian wonder-tale of Maria Morevna, collected by Afanasyev, in which a tsar's son quests beyond the blue sea-ocean and his sisters wed the Falcon, the Eagle, and the Raven in disguise. In this register tsarevna means the fairy-tale princess in her tower or pleasant mead-city: beautiful, imperilled, enchanted, and ultimately bestowed as the reward of the hero's trials, a figure as conventional to East Slavic narrative as the prince is to the French conte de fées.

Core meaning in detail: Morphologically the word joins tsar- with the feminine suffix -evna, the same daughter-marker seen in Russian patronymics such as Ivanovna, so that tsarevna parses literally as tsar's-daughter, just as korolevna is a king's daughter. English borrowed it in the nineteenth century through translations of Russian fairy tales by Ralston, Bain, and others, and it has never fully naturalised: it remains a marked, exotic word, always carrying a scent of onion domes, snow, firebirds, and Baba Yaga's hut, and writers reach for it precisely when princess feels too generic, too Disney, too Western.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Daughter of a tsar as a formal title (primary historical sense): the bearer of imperial blood in Muscovy and imperial Russia, ranked, styled, dowered, and married for reasons of state; the sense of court almanacs and genealogies.
  • Fairy-tale princess of Slavic folklore (dominant literary sense): the heroine or prize of the wonder-tale — Maria Morevna the warrior-maiden herself, Vasilisa the Beautiful, the Frog Tsarevna — wise, beautiful, frequently enchanted into frog or duck shape, and won only after impossible tasks. The bridge is direct: real tsarevnas were already semi-mythical figures to the peasantry, glimpsed never, and storytellers made them fully mythical.
  • Stage and ballet heroine (artistic sense, 19th–20th c.): the tsarevna of Rimsky-Korsakov's operas and of the Ballets Russes imagination, a costumed vision of exotic Slavic femininity for Parisian audiences. The bridge runs through the vogue for Russian folklore in art music, where the title itself became stage décor.
  • Pampered or imperious girl or woman (colloquial, ironic, modern): as in she runs the office like some tsarevna, meaning someone who expects service as her due, beautiful or haughty, waited upon. The bridge is caricature: the fairy-tale princess's privilege detached from her rank and pinned, half-admiringly, onto a modern diva.
  • General exotic princess in fantasy writing (extended literary sense): writers of fantasy borrow tsarevna for any northern or eastern princess of an invented realm, trading on its established fairy-tale aura. The bridge is purely atmospheric: the word has become a portable piece of Slavic scenery.

Register and connotation: Tsarevna is learned, literary, and romantic — never bureaucratic in English, never slang except in deliberate irony. It flatters its bearer with beauty, mystery, and lost-imperial glamour, and it flatters the writer with erudition, signalling knowledge of Afanasyev rather than the Brothers Grimm. Unlike princess, which can be briskly modern, tsarevna always looks backward, toward palaces, steppes, and enchanted forests, and even its ironic use depends on that backward glow.
