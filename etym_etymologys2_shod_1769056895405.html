Root core: PIE *skeu- / *skeuə- / *skū- "to cover, to conceal, to wrap" (c. 4500–2500 BCE). The earliest reconstructible stage is a verbal root of covering and wrapping, attested across the family in cover-words: Latin scūtum "shield" ("that which covers the body"), Greek skûtos "skin, hide," Latin cutis "skin," Old Norse ský "cloud" (the "coverer" of the sky). Linear chain to SHOD: 1. PIE *skeu- / *skeuə- / *skū- "to cover, conceal, wrap" (c. 4500–2500 BCE). 2. Proto-Germanic *skōhaz "foot-covering, shoe" (c. 500 BCE–100 CE), formed from the root with the suffix *-ō-, literally "the coverer (of the foot)." Cognates: Old Norse skór, Old High German scuoh, Gothic skohs. 3. Old English scōh "shoe" (attested from c. 8th century CE), the direct inherited descendant of *skōhaz; the Old English verb scōgan "to furnish with shoes, to shoe" (also scōwian) was derived from the noun. 4. Old English (ge)scōd — past participle of scōgan "shod, furnished with shoes" (weak class-1 formation; attested in the Old English period, c. 9th–11th century). This is the attested point at which the covering sense of scōh passes into the completed participle. 5. Middle English shodde, schod, shodd "shod" (13th–15th century), continuing the participle. 6. Early Modern and Modern English shod (16th century onward) — past tense and past participle of the verb to shoe, and the adjective "wearing shoes or footwear"; the modern verb to shoe is rebuilt on the noun shoe, but shod itself continues the Old English weak participle. Debated / nuanced notes: - The linkage of the whole Germanic *skōhaz to PIE *skeu- is standard in the handbooks (OED, Pokorny) but rests on the root's wide "covering" semantics; some scholars regard *skōhaz as a Germanic creation whose ultimate base is less certain. The chain above follows the mainstream reconstruction. - The Vedic Sanskrit verb skunāti "he covers, veils" (from *skeu-) is cited as an Indic reflex but is questioned by some philologists; it did not survive into modern Indic vernaculars and plays no part in the English chain. - The noun and the verb diverge early: Old English had scōh (noun) and scōgan (verb), and the participle was already weak (scōd); there is no evidence of a strong vowel-change past like *scōh-ed. The modern form shod therefore does not continue any vowel gradation but a stable weak participle.
