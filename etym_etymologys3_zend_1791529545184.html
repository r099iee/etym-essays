1. The Indo-European root of knowing. The story begins with PIE *ǵneh₃- "to know, recognize", one of the most productive roots in the family. Its descendants include Sanskrit jānāti "knows" and jñāna "knowledge", Greek gignōskō "I know", Latin (g)nōscō "I know", English know, and Slavic znati. In Iranian the root appears in Avestan zan- "to know" and in the noun zainti- "knowledge, understanding". The semantic core is cognitive: to know, to recognize, to understand. Everything that follows is a series of specializations of that core.

2. Avestan zainti- and the Zoroastrian scholarly tradition. Avestan zainti- belongs to the religious and intellectual vocabulary of the Avesta. As the Avestan corpus became increasingly archaic and its language ceased to be spoken, priests needed to explain, translate and interpret it. The Middle Persian noun zand "interpretation, commentary" descends from this Avestan noun and becomes the name of the exegetical tradition itself. The Zand is not a free composition: it is a translation and commentary on the Avestan text, often preserving an older understanding of ritual and doctrine. The great Pahlavi books of the ninth and tenth centuries, such as the Denkard and the Bundahishn, draw on this tradition and expand it into systematic theology, cosmology and law.

3. From commentary to scripture to language. The semantic path is a chain of metonymies. First, zand is the act or product of interpretation. Second, it is the corpus of commentary transmitted alongside the Avesta. Third, because manuscripts frequently juxtapose Avestan text and Zand, European readers encounter the pair as a single entity, the Zend-Avesta. Fourth, they transfer the name of the commentary to the language of the scripture, and Zend becomes an English name for Avestan. Each step is intelligible, and each step moves further from the original sense. The final step is a misnomer, but it is a misnomer with a long and productive life.

4. European encounter and the making of a misnomer. Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron (1731–1805) travelled to India in the 1750s, studied with Parsi priests, and published his Zend-Avesta in 1771. The work made the Zoroastrian scriptures known in Europe and helped stimulate the comparative study of Iranian languages. The title fixed the compound in European languages, and Zend became the ordinary label for the language. Sir William Jones and others disputed the value and authenticity of the material, but the philological work of the nineteenth century, especially that of Eugène Burnouf and Martin Haug, established Avestan as an Old Iranian language closely related to Old Persian and Sanskrit. As the term Avestan gained ground, Zend was reclassified as the name of the commentary, and the compound Zend-Avesta was recognized as a convenience rather than a precise description.

5. Sound and form. The formal correspondences are regular: PIE *ǵ becomes z in Iranian, giving Avestan zainti- and Middle Persian zand. The English form Zend reflects the French or Persian pronunciation of zand, with the vowel of the English spelling. The word is a proper noun and is capitalized. It has no plural in ordinary use. The variant Zand is used by specialists for the commentary, while Zend is the older European form. The Arabic zindīq "heretic, freethinker" is a distant relative, derived from Middle Persian zandīk, and it shows how the idea of "interpretation" could acquire a polemical religious sense.

6. Literature and attestation. The primary attestation is the Avesta itself, especially the Gathas, and the Middle Persian Zand preserved in Pahlavi manuscripts. The Denkard, a ninth-century compendium of Zoroastrian knowledge, is a major witness to the exegetical tradition. Anquetil-Duperron's Zend-Avesta (1771) is the founding European text, and nineteenth-century works such as Martin Haug's Essays on the Sacred Language, Writings, and Religion of the Parsis (1878) show the term in full Orientalist use. William Dwight Whitney's Language and the Study of Language (1867) records the synonymy of "Zend", "Avestan" and "Old Bactrian", a useful snapshot of the terminology before it stabilized.

7. Historiographical and cultural afterlife. The word Zend was carried into English not only by scholars but by the wider culture of Orientalism. Theosophical writers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries invoked "Zend" and the "Zend-Avesta" as emblems of ancient wisdom, often with scant attention to Iranian philology. The result is a double life: a precise technical sense (the Zand commentary) and a vague popular sense (ancient Persian religion and language). Modern scholarship keeps the first and discards the second. The word is now a historical marker, useful for reading older books and for understanding how European scholars first made sense of Zoroastrianism.

8. Semantic and stylistic summary.

  • Original sense: "knowledge, understanding" in Avestan zainti-.
  • Middle Persian: "interpretation, commentary", the exegesis of the Avesta.
  • European learned sense: "the language of the Zend-Avesta", i.e. Avestan.
  • Modern technical sense: the Zand commentary, distinguished from the Avestan text.
  • Register: archaic and bookish; in modern prose, use Avestan for the language and Zand for the commentary.
  • Common confusion: Zend is not Old Persian, not Middle Persian, and not the Avesta itself; it is a name for the commentary that was once misapplied to the language.

9. Why the word still matters. Zend is a small case study in the history of scholarship. It shows how texts, translations and commentaries travel together, how titles become categories, and how a learned error can become a durable term. It also shows the value of precise terminology: the distinction between Avestan (the language), the Avesta (the scripture) and the Zand (the commentary) is not pedantry but the condition of understanding the Zoroastrian textual tradition. The word's survival in the compound Zend-Avesta is a reminder of that history, and its obsolescence as a linguistic label is a reminder that scholarship can correct itself.
