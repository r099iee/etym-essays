Chain 1 — PASSION:
↓
PIE (c. 4000–3000 BCE)
*peh₁-
“to suffer, hurt”
(cf. Greek πῆμα “suffering, calamity”)

↓
Latin (2nd c. BCE – 1st c. CE)
patior · patī
“to suffer, undergo, endure”

↓
Late Latin (Christian Latin, 2nd–4th c. CE)
passio · passiōnis
“suffering; the Passion of Christ”
(Cicero had used passio for Greek páthos;
ecclesiastical Latin adds the specific sense)

↓
Old French (10th–12th c.)
passiun · passion
“the Passion of Christ; suffering; strong emotion”

↓
Middle English (c. 1225–1374)
passioun
“the Passion of Christ; suffering; emotion”
(Ancrene Wisse; Chaucer)

↓
English (16th c.)
passion
“ardent love, zeal, strong feeling; anger”
(sense widens)

↓
English (1640s)
passion-flower
“the flower symbolising the Passion of Christ”
(translating flos passionis)


Chain 2 — FLOWER:
↓
PIE (c. 4000–3000 BCE)
*bʰleh₃-
“to bloom, blossom, flourish”
(cf. English “bloom”, Latin flos)

↓
Latin (6th–1st c. BCE)
flōs · flōrem
“flower, blossom; the finest part”

↓
Old French (9th–12th c.)
flor · flour
“flower; the finest part”

↓
Middle English (13th c.)
flour · flower
“blossom; also the flower of wheat (fine meal)”
(Chaucer)

↓
Modern English (17th–19th c.)
flower
“blossom”
(“flour” splits off as the meal-word;
spelling restored toward Latin flōr-)

↓
English (1640s)
passion-flower
“the blossom of Passiflora, named for the Passion”

↓
Modern English
PASSION-FLOWERS

Note: the PIE root *peh₁- is reconstructed (reflex: Greek πῆμα); some scholars prefer a root with extended dental, but the Latin chain patior → passio is documented. The naming step is a deliberate botanical calque, first in English in the mid-17th century.
