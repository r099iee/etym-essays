CHAIN — a single root family from PIE to the target:
PIE *kap- "to grasp, take, seize" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the same root underlies Germanic have/haben and Latin capere)
→ Latin capere "to take, seize, grasp" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE)
→ Latin concipere "to take in, receive; to conceive (a child, or an idea)" — con- "together, with" + capere (attested from the 1st c. BCE)
→ Latin conceptus, past participle of concipere, "conceived, taken in, grasped" (attested 1st c. BCE); from the Old French verb concevoir "to conceive" comes the noun built on this participle
→ Old French conceit / conceipt "a notion, thought, idea, fancy" (from the past participle of concevoir; attested 12th–13th c.)
→ Middle English conceit "a notion, thought, fancy, mental conception" (attested from the 14th c.)
→ Early Modern English conceited "full of conceit" — the adjective formed from the noun conceit + -ed; first "ingenious, witty, fanciful" (16th c.), then "having an excessively high opinion of oneself" (16th–17th c.)
→ English conceited ◄ TARGET (present day)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The chain is secure from Latin capere onward, and PIE *kap- is one of the most widely attested roots (Italic capere "to take" and Germanic *habjaną "to have" are generally accepted reflexes). Nuance one: conceit and concept are doublets — both descend from Latin conceptus, one via Old French (conceit, the everyday word), one borrowed directly from Latin (concept, the learned word, 16th c.); the adjective conceited is built on the noun conceit + -ed, not on concept. Nuance two: the semantic shift of the adjective from "ingenious, fanciful" to "vain" is a documented historical change of the 16th–17th centuries, not a reconstruction, and it is the very bridge traced in longDefinition and etymologyS3.
