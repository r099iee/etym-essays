TUMBLED is the past tense and past participle of the strong verb tumble, and in daily English it does two things at once: it reports a sudden, uncontrolled fall, and it describes the resulting state of disorder. A person who has tumbled has pitched headlong, lost footing or balance, and gone over — often rolling once or twice before stopping. The participial form can therefore modify a noun whose condition is the residue of such motion: tumbled rocks are rocks that have fallen, rolled, been knocked about, and come to rest where gravity put them.

Primary sense — sudden helpless fall. The core meaning is a loss of equilibrium ending on the ground or at a lower level, with a rolling component: “he tumbled down the stairs”, “the horse stumbled and tumbled into the gully”. Unlike “fell”, which is purely vertical, “tumbled” always keeps a trace of turning, pitching, or somersaulting.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • Of water: a cataract, torrent or wave “tumbles” when it breaks and rolls downward in agitation — “the tumbling falls”, “tumbling waters”. The bridge is the same rolling, end-over-end motion transferred from bodies to water.
  • Of rocks and stone (geology; the sense in the source passage): “tumbled rocks” are large stones loosened from a cliff or bank, rounded and rearranged by water, frost, or gravity, lying scattered and jumbled at the base of a slope. A “tumbled” rock has weathered, rolled, and lost its sharp quarry edges; the participle carries the whole story of erosion in a single word.
  • Of structures: buildings, towers and walls “come tumbling down” — collapse in fragments; the fixed phrase is a cliché of ruin, literal and figurative (“his empire came tumbling down”).
  • Of prices, markets and fortunes: since the 19th century financial writing has used “tumbled” for a steep, abrupt drop — “shares tumbled”, “the currency tumbled to a record low”. The bridge is a precipitous, out-of-control fall, exactly like a physical one.
  • To catch on, to understand (British informal): “tumble to something” = to suddenly perceive a trick, joke or truth, recorded from the late 19th century. The image is of scales falling, or of suddenly tumbling into comprehension.
  • Acrobatics and sport: “tumbling” is still the technical term for somersaults and floor acrobatics in gymnastics; historically a “tumbler” was a professional acrobat. This is the oldest sense of the English word (Old English tumbian meant “to dance, leap”), and it survives unchanged in arena and gym jargon.
  • Disorder: “the room was all in a tumble”, “a tumble of papers” — a confused heap; the nominal use matches the participle: what has tumbled ends up in a jumble.
  • Sexual slang (dated, vulgar): “a quick tumble”, “a tumble in the hay” — a brief casual sexual encounter; the bridge is the undignified rolling of bodies, re-read as a romp.
  • Natural history and objects: “tumbler” pigeons (17th century) turn somersaults in flight; “tumbler” drinking glasses (17th century) have rounded bases that literally tumble over and cannot be set down dry.
  • Modern technology and culture: a “tumble dryer” rolls clothes in heated air; a “tumbler” or tumbling barrel polishes parts by rolling them with abrasive; and the internet “tumblelog” (whence the platform name Tumblr) is a blog format of rapid, low-friction miniature posts — the same rolling brevity in a new medium.

Semantic bridge in one sentence. Every sense of tumbled — acrobatic, geological, hydraulic, financial, colloquial, sexual, mechanical — radiates from one physical core, the repeated uncontrolled rolling-and-falling of a body, so that the participle always carries, at whatever distance, the image of something that went over and never quite recovered its place.
