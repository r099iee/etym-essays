- 19th-century coinage - blighter is formed from blight, the plant disease that withers and blackens crops, plus the agent suffix -er; literally 'one who blights', it enters the language as a hardish term of contempt for a nuisance or pest
- Late 19th-early 20th century softening - in colloquial British speech the word softens remarkably: it becomes a jocular or pitying disparagement, rarely a true insult, and is tempered by affection in phrases like 'poor blighter' or 'you cheeky blighter'
- 20th century - the word becomes a staple of classless, mock-comic British talk, hovering between mock outrage and genuine sympathy, its precise register read only from context
- Contemporary sense - a mildly contemptible, troublesome or unfortunate person, used with more warmth than anger; almost entirely British, it now carries a dated, bluff, old-fashioned charm
