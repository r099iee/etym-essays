The linguistic lineage of traversez represents a vivid synthesis of two foundational Indo-European roots—one governing the physical crossing over of boundaries and the other governing the mechanical turning and orientation of bodies—which merged into a Roman spatial concept of transverse alignment, evolved into a medieval Gallo-Romance verb of physical transit, and ultimately crystallized as an iconic modern urban imperative on Parisian street signals.

  • Proto-Indo-European Dual Roots (*terh₂- and *wert-): The compound structure of the Latin ancestor of traversez unites two ancient Proto-Indo-European verbal roots:
    • The root *terh₂- (or *treh₂-), meaning "to cross over, pass through, overcome, or reach the end." In Proto-Italic, this produced the preposition *trānts, which crystallized into Classical Latin trāns ("across, beyond, over"). Across other branches, it gave Sanskrit tárati ("crosses over, overcomes"), Old English þurh (Modern English through), and Latin intra- / inter-.
    • The root *wer- (or *wert-), signifying "to turn, twist, bend, or rotate." In Proto-Italic, it produced the primary third-conjugation verb vertere ("to turn"), cognate with Sanskrit vártate ("turns, rolls"), Old English weorþan ("to become, turn into"), and German werden.
  • Classical Latin Compounding (trānsversus and trāversus): In Latin, the prepositional prefix trāns- was compounded with the perfect passive participle versus (from vertere), generating the compound participle and adjective trānsversus (frequently contracted in popular speech and poetry to trāversus). Literally signifying "turned across" or "turned athwart," transversus described that which lay perpendicular or oblique to a direct path. Roman writers deployed it in agriculture (furrows plowed crosswise), military tactics (flanking movements athwart the enemy's line), and architecture (transverse beams supporting a roof).
  • Late Latin and Vulgar Latin Verbalization (trāversāre): During late antiquity, as Classical Latin grammar simplified, speakers transformed the participial adjective trāversus into a first-conjugation active frequentative verb: trāversāre, literally "to place across," which rapidly shifted intransitively and transitively into "to pass across, go athwart, or cross over." This verb was preserved across the Romance languages: Italian traversare / attraversare, Spanish travesar / atravesar, Portuguese atravessar, and Old French traverser.
  • Old French Development and Inflection (-ez): In Old French of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, traverser was widely used in chansons de geste and chivalric romances to describe crossing bridges, rivers, and borders. To form the second-person plural present indicative and imperative, Old French appended the suffix -ez. This ending evolved regularly from the Classical Latin second-person plural verbal inflection -ātis (as in amātis "you love"): the intervocalic dental stop [t] weakened and dropped out in early Gallo-Romance, while the tonic vowel [a] palatalized after coronal consonants and closed into [e], written -ez with the graph z representing an affricate [ts] in early Old French before reducing to simple [e] in Middle French. Thus, traversez meant "cross ye!" or, in the polite formal register that developed in the Renaissance, "cross! [formal singular]".
  • Haussmannization and the 20th-Century Parisian Urban Landscape: During the mid-nineteenth century, Baron Haussmann revolutionized Paris by demolishing medieval alleys and constructing colossal, sweeping boulevards (such as Boulevard Saint-Germain and Boulevard Saint-Michel). These vast thoroughfares transformed urban walking into a complex physical challenge: crossing multiple lanes of horse-drawn carriages, and later speeding automobiles, required pedestrians to negotiate traffic in separate stages. In the twentieth century, the Parisian municipality installed illuminated electronic pedestrian crossing signs featuring the distinctive directive: "traversez en deux temps" ("cross in two stages"), utilizing an elevated traffic island (the median refuge) as an intermediary haven.
  • Proustian Semiotic and Existential Transfiguration: It is this exact municipal street sign that the critic in the prompt context transfigures into a profound literary allegory. When the Parisian traffic light flashes its red warning commanding "traversez en deux temps," it does not merely regulate pedestrians; it mirrors Marcel Proust's central philosophical doctrine of discontinuous time. In Proust's vision, human consciousness cannot cross the terrifying expanse of mortal time in an unbroken, smooth continuum. Rather, like the Parisian flâneur crossing the boulevard, we must cross time in fragments and stages, finding refuge in isolated havens of memory and art before reaching the inaccessible other side of eternity.
