- 15th c. (Late Middle English) — Formed from the Anglo-French phrase *en ure* ("in practice, in operation"); primarily used in legal and administrative contexts for putting statutes, customs, or rights into actual execution.
- 16th c. (Tudor Era) — Expanded into general English literature and military treatises; keeping soldiers or apprentices *in ure* meant keeping them in continuous physical training, active employment, and discipline.
- 17th c. (Jacobean & Restoration) — Underwent a crucial semantic shift: from the active meaning of "practicing a discipline" to the modern sense of "becoming hardened or accustomed to hardship, danger, or severe climate through continuous exposure."
- 18th–19th c. (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) — Fully bifurcated into its two modern spheres: high literary prose for stoic endurance of suffering and physical labor (Milton, Wordsworth, Durant), and precise Anglo-American contract and property jurisprudence (the doctrine of benefits that *inure* to heirs).
- 20th–21st c. — Continues prominently in contemporary tax law and non-profit regulation (the "private inurement doctrine" preventing tax-exempt funds from benefiting private individuals), alongside formal essays describing psychological adaptation to harsh environments.
