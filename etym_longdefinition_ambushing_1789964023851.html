AMBUSHING is the present participle and gerund of the verb to ambush: lying concealed in wait in order to attack, capture, or confront by surprise. In its most concrete and historically primary sense it denotes the deliberate act of hiding troops, hunters, poachers, or assailants along a route where prey or an enemy is expected to pass, then striking without warning. The passage in which this card occurs uses it in exactly this poaching sense: nocturnal hunters ambushing venison in the Saint-Germain forests, that is, lying in wait for deer at night in order to kill and steal them.

At its core, ambushing combines three semantic elements: (1) concealment, (2) waiting and anticipation, and (3) sudden violent or unwelcome action. The concealment may be physical — woods, ditches, doorways, vehicles, riverbanks — or informational, as when journalists, photographers, lawyers, or rival firms conceal their intentions until the moment of confrontation. The waiting implies patience, calculation, and asymmetry: the ambusher knows what will happen while the victim does not. The sudden action implies unfair advantage, shock, and loss of the victim's ability to prepare or escape.

  • Military ambush (primary sense): a body of troops concealed to surprise an enemy column, patrol, or convoy. Ambushing here is a formal tactic taught in infantry doctrine — L-shaped, V-shaped, linear and point ambushes — distinguished from open battle by surprise, short violent contact, and withdrawal. Example: troops ambushing a supply convoy at a defile.
  • Hunting and poaching sense (context sense): lying in wait for game. Legitimate hunters ambush ducks at a flyway or deer at a waterhole; poachers ambushing venison do the same illegally, at night, in a royal or private forest. The object venison here means deer as game animals, and ambushing implies stealth, knowledge of animal runs, and evasion of keepers. This is the sense in Guy de Maupassant's river-poacher vignette from which the context comes: marginal men living by theft, night work, and river salvage.
  • Criminal waylaying: ambushing travellers, motorists, or victims for robbery, kidnapping, or murder. Highwaymen, bandits, and urban muggers ambush victims in car parks, alleys, or isolated roads. The law treats ambushing as evidence of premeditation and aggravation.
  • Figurative surprise attack in debate and procedure: to ambush someone with a question, motion, document, or accusation means to produce it without notice so they cannot respond adequately. Parliamentary ambushes, legal ambushes (late disclosure of evidence), and workplace ambushes (sudden criticism in a meeting) all draw on the military metaphor.
  • Ambush interview (journalism): a reporter lying in wait for a public figure outside a home, court, or studio to force an unprepared statement. Ambushing here is ethically loaded: it suggests harassment versus legitimate accountability journalism.
  • Ambush marketing (business/sport): a brand ambushing an event means associating itself with a major tournament or spectacle without paying sponsorship fees — e.g. stunt advertising outside a stadium. The term, coined in the 1980s, extends the military image to commercial parasitism by surprise.
  • Sport and games: in chess, cards, or video games, ambushing denotes a hidden trap, camper tactics, or a surprise opening novelty prepared to catch an opponent off guard.

Grammatically, ambushing functions both as progressive verb (they were ambushing the patrol), as gerund (ambushing requires patience), and as participial adjective (an ambushing party, more commonly ambushing predator). It governs a direct object (to ambush someone/something) and is frequently followed by locative adjuncts (ambushing in the woods, from behind a wall, along the road). The noun ambush preserves the older form, while ambushment is now archaic. The agent noun is ambusher; the place is ambush site or ambuscade, a sixteenth-century doublet borrowed via Italian/Spanish that survives in literary use.

Stylistically, ambushing is strongly negative when the victim's viewpoint is taken (treachery, cowardice, unfairness) but can be positive or neutral from the ambusher's viewpoint (skill, cunning, resourcefulness). Guerrilla traditions celebrate ambushing as the weapon of the weak against the strong, while regular armies and moralists condemn it as unchivalrous. In the poaching context it carries a double coloration: romantic outlaw freedom and sordid parasitism — men who will not do honest day labour but live by night theft, drowned corpses, and floating rags. To master this card, remember: ambushing always means hidden waiting + sudden strike, whether with rifles in a forest, cameras on a doorstep, or logos outside a stadium.
