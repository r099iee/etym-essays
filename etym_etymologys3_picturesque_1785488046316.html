The biography of picturesque begins five thousand years ago in a root that meant to mark and colour: PIE *peyk-. Its descendants are a painter's family in several languages — Greek poikilos 'many-coloured, embroidered' (the embroidered, dappled quality of fine cloth), Sanskrit pisati 'carves, adorns, paints', and Latin pingere 'to paint, embroider, tattoo', which in early Rome could mean all three, since painting began as marking a surface and the same root covered tattooing the body. From pingere Latin built its painter's vocabulary: pictor 'painter' (from the participle pictus), pictura 'a painting' (source of English picture), and pigmentum 'pigment, colouring matter'. The Italic branch of the family supplied the professional who would eventually give the word its modern shape.

The crucial coinage came in Venice in 1648, when the critic and poet Marco Boschini published a long poem in Venetian dialect, La carta del navegar pitoresco, 'the chart of painterly navigation'. Boschini needed an adjective for the quality of art that shows the free, sketchy, expressive handling of the painter — work in the manner of a painter rather than a polished finished surface — and he built pittoresco from pittore, 'painter', with the suffix -esco, 'in the style of' (the same suffix that gives English -esque, from the Germanic *-isk- that also produced English -ish). For Boschini the accent was on the painter: pittoresco praised the bravura of the artist's touch. The word crossed the Alps into French as pittoresque in the later seventeenth century, still meaning 'after the manner of a painter, lively, expressive'.

Transmission and the great respelling:
  • Latin pictor to Italian pittore (the painter).
  • Italian pittoresco (1648, Boschini) to French pittoresque.
  • French pittoresque to English, first recorded around 1703.
  • Almost immediately, English folk-etymology went to work: the unfamiliar pittoresque was respelled picturesque, fused with the familiar noun picture, and the meaning slid from 'like a painter' to 'like a picture'.
  • The shift of one vowel changed the whole orientation of the word: from describing the artist's handling to describing the scene's appearance — the scene now looked as if it had been composed for a painting.

The consequences of that respelling were enormous, because the eighteenth century was about to make 'picturesque' a technical term. In the 1740s and 1750s the word entered English landscape theory, chiefly through William Gilpin and, later, Uvedale Price. The Picturesque became the third term in a famous triad, sitting between the smooth Beautiful (harmonious curves, gentle beauty) and the terrifying Sublime (mountains, storms, awe): picturesque scenery pleased precisely through its roughness, irregularity, variety, and surprise — the qualities a painter values. The category shaped the taste for ruins, rugged oaks, and winding lanes, and the picturesque garden, the English landscape park, was its monument. Public perception: a term of high art-criticism and gardening.

Semantic drift and social factors:
  • 17th century: 'after the manner of a painter' — a term of praise for art and its handling.
  • 18th century: an aesthetic category of landscape between the Beautiful and the Sublime, valued for roughness and irregularity.
  • 19th century: the word tumbled from the treatises into the guidebook; any scene 'worth a picture' — a ruined abbey, a crooked lane, a village of half-timbered houses — became picturesque, the standard mild compliment of the travelling public.
  • 20th–21st century: the word settled into gently approving, faintly touristic praise, extended from scenery to language (vivid, graphic description) and to anything pleasantly old-fashioned or photogenic; its painterly precision was lost, but its charm survived.

The final stage, PICTURESQUE, thus carries the whole journey in a single syllable's difference: Boschini's painterly bravura, Gilpin's landscape category, and the guidebook's postcard compliment all live on in a word whose English spelling is a folk-etymological accident — a Venetian art-critic's coinage respelled into a picture-word by speakers who saw a picture in every scene.
