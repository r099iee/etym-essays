embroidering: the present participle of the verb embroider, meaning (1) to decorate fabric with ornamental needlework — patterns and pictures sewn in thread — and (2) figuratively, to embellish or exaggerate a story, adding decorative detail that may be untrue. As a participle it works both as the continuous verb ("she was embroidering a dragon pattern") and as a verbal noun or gerund naming the activity ("embroidering is a slow craft"). The word's centre is always the needle and thread working on a surface; the figurative sense is a deliberate extension of that image.

PRIMARY SENSE — ornamental needlework (literal)

To embroider is to ornament cloth, leather, or other material by sewing threads into patterns — flowers, figures, emblems, borders, letters. Unlike plain sewing, which joins or repairs fabric, embroidery is additive and decorative: it enriches an existing surface by projecting coloured thread across it.

  • Surface embroidery — patterns worked on the face of the cloth with a needle, the most common form (crewelwork, cross-stitch, satin stitch).
  • Canvas work / counted-thread embroidery — decorative stitching worked on a stiff canvas grid (petit point, needlepoint).
  • Goldwork and silk embroidery — prestigious court embroidery using metal and silk thread, a marker of status in medieval and Renaissance dress and in ecclesiastical vestments.
  • Embroidery as craft and industry — in the context card, "sewing and embroidering morning, noon and night," the participle names a domestic money-making skill: needlework sold or commissioned to support a household, emblematic of the patient, exploitable labour of women.

SECONDARY SENSE — embellishing a story (figurative)

Since the sixteenth century, embroider has been used of narratives and speech: to "embroider a story" is to add decorative, often fictitious, detail to it — to elaborate and ornament the bare facts, sometimes to the point of untruth. The bridge is the image itself: the storyteller "stitches" extra ornament onto the plain cloth of the truth, exactly as a needleworker stitches thread onto plain fabric. The figurative word is mildly playful or critical: it suggests charming invention rather than crude falsehood, for which English reserves lie.

  • To embroider an account — "the witness was accused of embroidering his account of the events to make himself look better."
  • To embroider the truth — to add decorative detail that makes the truth more colourful and less accurate.
  • To embroider a tale — to elaborate a narrative with invented, ornamental incidents.

TERTIARY AND TECHNICAL USES

  • Natural description — "embroidered" names fine, thread-like markings or feather patterning in nature (as of some birds' plumage), a technical-descriptive extension of the needlework image.
  • Figurative extension to other arts — music, writing, and design can be "embroidered" with ornaments, though the needlework sense remains primary.
  • Participial adjective — "embroidering women" (women who embroider), "an embroidering needle" (a needle suited to the craft).

GRAMMAR AND FORM

  • Progressive: "They were embroidering all afternoon" — the action in progress.
  • Gerund: "Embroidering requires patience" — the activity as a noun.
  • Participial adjective: "the embroidering household" — describing those engaged in the craft.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES

  • vs sewing / stitching — structural, utilitarian joining or mending of fabric; embroidery is decorative and additive.
  • vs weaving — creates the fabric itself from warp and weft; embroidery decorates fabric that already exists.
  • vs embellishing / ornamenting — broad, abstract terms applicable to music, architecture, and speech; embroidering is tied to needle and thread, or to the story-figure.
  • vs darning — repair by re-weaving threads into a hole; embroidery is ornamental, not reparative.
  • vs fabricating — wholesale invention or forgery; embroidering decorates the truth with extra detail rather than replacing it.

HISTORICAL BRIDGES

The literal and figurative senses share a single root-image: working at the edge or border. The Old French source, broder, originally meant to work the edge of a garment — the border was the critical place for reinforcement, which naturally became the place for ornament and display. The word's history, from a Germanic word for "point, edge" to French "to work the edge" to English "to ornament with needlework" and then "to ornament a story," is a continuous chain of additions: thread added to cloth, ornament added to borders, fiction added to fact.
