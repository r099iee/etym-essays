Badgering is the present participle and verbal noun of badger: to pester someone relentlessly — nagging, questioning, demanding, needling — until resistance wears down. It is persistence weaponised: not one request but a campaign of them, the asker returning again and again like a dog at a burrow-mouth until the answer changes.

In the source sentence — ‘You are always badgering me about my ring’ — the complaint is precisely this campaign-quality: not a single inquiry but a standing siege, the ring demanded as a topic at every meeting. The speaker contrasts it with a grievance of the opposite kind — never being asked about the journey's other treasures — so badgering here also carries tunnel vision, an obsession that will not look at anything else. One word accuses the asker of both relentlessness and narrowness of mind.

Core meaning in detail: To badger is to apply repeated verbal pressure over time: asking again after refusal, raising the subject at every encounter, mixing coaxing with reproach. Parents are badgered for treats, officials are badgered for answers, witnesses are badgered in cross-examination. The pressure need not be loud — quiet daily repetition badgers as effectively as shouting — but it must be recurrent: a single demand, however fierce, is not badgering. Duration is the essence of the offence.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To pester with repeated demands (primary modern sense): badgering customer service for a refund, badgered into signing. The bridge is the baiting-pit: as dogs harassed the cornered beast without pause, so the badgerer harasses the victim without pause — relentlessness as the whole definition.
  • To question persistently — journalists and lawyers (professional sense): the minister was badgered over expenses. The bridge runs from domestic nagging to public accountability: the same technique of never letting the subject drop, legitimised when the quarry is power.
  • To tease or bait for sport (older, closer to the root): badgering the new boy — schoolyard torment in relays. The bridge is the shortest of all: tormenting a person the way the dogs tormented the animal, the human victim standing in for the beast.
  • Campaigning and lobbying — organised badgering (collective sense): badgered by charities, canvassers, and call centres. The bridge scales the individual pest up to institutions: mailing lists and phone banks mechanise the burrow-mouth siege, one caller replacing another so the pressure never sleeps.
  • Affectionate badgering — coaxing (softened sense): she badgered him into taking the last cake. The bridge is tone: the same repetition performed with love becomes persuasion rather than persecution — persistence re-read as devotion when the stakes are trifles.
  • The animal and the sport (root senses, now background): the badger itself — the grey burrowing mammal with the white-blazed head — and badger-baiting, the outlawed blood sport of setting terriers to drag it from its sett. The bridge is history entire: without the pit, the verb would not exist, and every modern nagging echoes a cruelty abolished in 1835.

Register and connotation: badgering is always the complainer's word — nobody announces ‘I shall now badger you’; the badgered name the crime. It colours the target as victim and the agent as tiresome, whether the scene is a kitchen, a parliament, or a phone bank. The tone is weary rather than furious: to say one is badgered is to sigh, roll the eyes, and admit the siege is working. Grammatically it takes about (the topic besieged), into (the surrender extracted), and with (the weapon of questions) — badgering me about my ring states all three at once.
