The torch is, etymologically, a twist. Its whole history hangs on a single physical observation made by Latin speakers two thousand years ago: that a serviceable brand is made by twisting straw, rags, or tow around a stick and dipping the bundle in pitch or wax. Name the twist, and you name the flame.

The PIE root — twisting since prehistory: Latin torquēre (‘to twist, wind, wrench’) descends from PIE *terkw-, ‘to twist, turn’. This is one of the great pan-European roots: it gives Sanskrit tarkú- (‘spindle’), Old English þrāwan (‘to twist, turn’), and a thicket of Latin derivatives — torques (the twisted neck-ring of Gaulish warriors, ancestor of English torque), tortus (‘twisted’, ancestor of tort, torture, contortion), and tormentum (originally a twisted rope engine). The torch belongs to this twisted family.

From verb to object — Vulgar Latin *torca: In the spoken Latin of late antiquity, the feminine past-participle form was substantivised: *torca, literally ‘the twisted (thing)’. What twisted thing? The bundle of straw or flax wound round a staff and soaked in resin — the standard portable light of the Roman night watch, the villa corridor, the catacombs. The word names the manufacture (twisted) rather than the function (light), a metonymy frozen for two millennia.

Old French torche (12th c.): The word enters French carrying both senses — ‘twisted bundle of straw’ and ‘torch’ — and rides the Norman Conquest into England around 1300 as Middle English torche. Medieval England lit its castles, monasteries, and pageants with it; the torch-bearer becomes a recognised household office, and torchlight processions mark royal entries and holy days.

Centuries of flame:

  • Medieval–Tudor: the torch is infrastructure — wall sconces in every great hall, link-boys carrying them through London streets. Shakespeare uses torches for wedding nights and conspirators alike.
  • 18th–19th c.: gaslight and candles push the flaming brand toward ceremony and symbol — coronations, funerals, Guy Fawkes — while explorers and novelists keep it alive in caves and Gothic ruins.
  • 1886: the Statue of Liberty raises her torch over New York harbour, and the word's idealistic pole — enlightenment, welcome, freedom — is fixed forever.
  • 20th c.: the Olympic torch relay (revived 1936) makes the flame a global ritual; the electric torch keeps the name for the flashlight; American slang turns torch into a verb of arson.

Tolkien's torches: When Gandalf distributes torches alongside sparklers and dwarf-candles at Bag End, the philologist-professor is handing his hobbits a word that has burned continuously from PIE twist-roots through Roman night watches to the modern party — twenty-five centuries of twisted flame in a single syllable.
