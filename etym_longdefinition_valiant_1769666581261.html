Core Definition

valiant — an adjective meaning possessing or showing courage, determination, or heroism; brave in action and firm in spirit, especially in the face of danger, difficulty, or adversity. The word applies both to persons (a valiant knight) and to deeds and efforts (a valiant attempt), and it carries an elevated, admiring, and somewhat old-fashioned tone.

Primary Sense: Courageous and Heroic

  • Personal bravery: a valiant person acts bravely under threat — a valiant soldier, a valiant defender, a valiant leader.
  • Moral fortitude: beyond physical courage, valiant can mean resolute in duty, honor, or principle — "a valiant stand for justice."
  • Dignified heroism: the word implies not just fearlessness but a worthy, noble, admirable quality; it is a term of praise, not mere description.

Secondary Sense: Heroic Effort Despite Adversity

  • Valiant attempt / effort / try: a determined, brave endeavor that may nonetheless fail — the emphasis is on the courage of the attempt, not its success: "She made a valiant effort to save the drowning child."
  • Valiant but vain: often used of losing struggles conducted with honor — a "valiant last stand," a "valiant defense of a lost cause."
  • Valiant little creature: in fable and children's literature (as in the source passage about a small, brave beast), the word dignifies courage in small or unlikely heroes — the brave mouse, the determined child.

Register and Usage Notes

  • Formal, literary, and admiring in register; rare in casual speech.
  • Common in epic, romance, historical fiction, and journalistic tributes to acts of courage.
  • Related forms: valor (noun, courage in battle), valiantly (adverb), valiance / valiancy (rare nouns).
  • Collocates with knight, deed, effort, attempt, defense, stand, heart, spirit, warrior.
  • The adverb valiantly ("fought valiantly") is standard in praise of effort.

Polysemy Summary

valiant is not heavily polysemous but is subtly nuanced across three registers of courage: (1) martial and physical bravery (the valiant knight); (2) moral and spiritual resolution (valiant in faith, duty, or principle); (3) heroic effort against odds, especially where the outcome is lost or uncertain (a valiant attempt). Underneath all three lies the same historical idea: strength — bodily, moral, or spiritual — shown in the service of what is worthy. The word dignifies courage by tying it to worth (its Latin root valēre means both "to be strong" and "to be worth").

Historical Semantic Bridge

Latin valēre meant "to be strong, healthy, influential, of worth." Old French vaillant (present participle of valoir, "to be worth, to be strong") shifted the meaning from physical strength to brave worthiness in the context of medieval chivalry. Borrowed into English after the Norman Conquest (c. 1330s), valiant arrived fully equipped with the knightly ideal — courage joined to honor and worth — and has kept that dignified, admiring sense ever since.
