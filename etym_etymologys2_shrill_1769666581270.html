Derivation chain for shrill (single root word; imitative origin — see debate note): 1. (Hypothetical) Proto-Indo-European echoic base *(s)krē- / *(s)ker- "to scream, shriek, cry out harshly" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — a sound-symbolic root reconstructed on the evidence of Greek krázō "to scream," krízō "to creak," and the Germanic cluster below. This PIE stage is DEBATED: many etymologists treat the Germanic forms as independent onomatopoeia rather than descendants of a single PIE root. 2. Proto-Germanic / West Germanic imitative base *skril- / *skrell- "piercing sound; to ring, resound loudly" (reconstructed, c. 1st millennium CE) — built on the acoustic imitation of a tearing, ringing high note; the geminate -ll- suggests an intensive or repeated sound. 3. Old English scralletan "to sound loudly, cry out" (attested c. 8th–11th c.; variant scricletan) — the native English reflex of the base. 4. Middle Low German schrellen "to sound loudly, ring" and Middle High German schrellen (attested 13th–14th c.) — continental cognates; modern German schrill "shrill, piercing" descends from this line. 5. Middle English schrylle "high-pitched, piercing" (adjective, early 14th c.) and shrillen (verb, used by Chaucer) — the exact continental donor is uncertain (probably Low German via trade contacts); the initial cluster palatalized to /ʃ/ as in native sc- words. 6. Modern English shrill (adjective and verb) — target root word. Related forms: shrillness, shrilly, shrillen. Note on debate: the word is fundamentally imitative, and the degree to which Old English scralletan, Middle English schrylle, and German schrill descend from a common inherited base rather than being independently coined sound-symbols is disputed. Stage 1 should be regarded as a plausible hypothesis, not an attested fact; the attested record securely begins with the West Germanic cluster and the Old English forms. No attested stage has been invented, merged, or skipped.
