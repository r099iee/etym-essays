PEJORATIVE descends in a single unbroken line from an ancient Indo-European word for "going" through Latin, Late Latin, and French. The chain: 1) PIE *ped- "to go, walk, step, tread" (reconstructed; the same root as Latin pēs/pedis "foot" [pedal, pedestrian] and English foot) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) PIE *ped-yōs "worse; literally 'going badly, stumbling, tripping'" (reconstructed comparative formation on *ped-, the base of the Latin comparative) — the semantic idea: the one who goes worse, who stumbles on the path, is the inferior, the worse. 3) Latin peior "worse" (comparative of malus "bad"; attested from the 2nd c. BCE; also spelled pejor) — the direct ancestor of the modern word; the superlative pessimus "worst" is built on the same base with a -s- form. 4) Latin peiōrāre "to make worse, to worsen" (denominative verb on peior; attested in Late Latin, 4th–5th c. CE; also pejorāre) — the step that turns the comparative "worse" into a verb of making-worse. 5) Latin peiōrātus "made worse" (past participle of peiōrāre, attested in Late Latin) — the participle on which the Romance adjective is formed. 6) French péjoratif "depreciatory, disparaging, tending to make worse" (adjective, formed within French on the Latin participle stem peiōrat- with the suffix -if; attested from the early 16th c.) — the immediate source of the English word. 7) English pejorative "disparaging, depreciatory, belittling" (adjective and noun, borrowed from French; attested from 1882) — PEJORATIVE. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The derivation of Latin peior from PIE *ped- "to go" is the standard and widely accepted etymology — the comparative *ped-yōs- meaning literally "going badly, stumbling" — and it is supported by the Latin superlative pessimus "worst," built on the same base with a -s- form. (2) The deeper stages of the family are uncontroversial in outline: the root *ped- is among the best-attested of all PIE roots, and no serious alternative etymon for peior is accepted. (3) The only nuance is the date of the French adjective: péjoratif is securely attested from the early 16th century, but the related verb péjorer, "to worsen," may be slightly earlier; both derive from the Late Latin peiōrāre family.
