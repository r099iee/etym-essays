FOURSCORE is a compound of two independent English roots, FOUR and SCORE, and each has its own complete derivation chain. The chains are given separately below. CHAIN 1 — FOUR (the "4" element): 1. PIE *kʷetwóres — the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European cardinal for "four"; attested indirectly through all major IE branches (Sanskrit catvāras, Greek téssares, Latin quattuor, Germanic *fedwōr). 2. Proto-Germanic *fedwōr — the Germanic reflex of the PIE numeral "four" (reconstructed from attested Germanic forms). 3. Old English fēower — "four"; attested from the earliest Old English texts (7th–8th century). 4. Middle English four / fower — "four"; attested from the 12th century onward. 5. English four — "4"; modern form, unchanged in meaning. 6. four + score → English FOURSCORE — "eighty," four times twenty; compound attested from late Middle English onward and in biblical/formal prose. CHAIN 2 — SCORE (the "20" element): 1. PIE *sker- / *skerə- — reconstructed root meaning "to cut, shear, split"; attested indirectly through Latin (carn- "flesh," caro), Greek (keírein "to cut"), Germanic, and Indic cutting-verbs. 2. Proto-Germanic *skeran- — "to cut, shear" (reconstructed). 3. Old Norse skera — "to cut, shear"; attested from the Viking Age; also skor "notch, tally-mark," the noun formed from the cutting-verb. 4. Old Norse skor — "notch, incision, tally-mark"; attested in Old Norse texts; the notch cut in a stick to record a count. 5. Middle English score — borrowed from Old Norse skor with the senses "notch, cut, tally, twenty (as marked by notches)"; attested from the 13th century; by the 14th–15th century the sense "twenty" is established because counting was done by twenties, one notch per score of animals or goods. 6. English score — "twenty; also a tally, points in a game, the written music (from the lines/staves cut or drawn)"; modern form. 7. four + score → English FOURSCORE — "eighty," four times twenty; compound attested from late Middle English onward and in biblical/formal prose. Combined: four (PIE *kʷetwóres) + score (via Old Norse skor from PIE *sker- "to cut") = fourscore "eighty" (4 × 20). Both roots are Germanic in their immediate English form; neither is borrowed into English from Romance, though "score" itself is a Norse loan into English.
