- Old English — dyne, a loud sound or crash: thunder, falling walls, the stroke of weapons, a stark acoustic word with no necessary festivity.
- Middle English — dine or dyn: battle noise, storms, and lamentation in romance and chronicle, still overwhelming and usually terrible rather than merry.
- 16th–17th c. — civic and martial widening: the din of drums, bells, ordnance, and street crowds, as gunpowder and growing cities supply new volumes of noise.
- 18th–19th c. — domestication into festivity: the din of the feast, the schoolroom, the factory floor, where industrial and social life make sustained racket ordinary.
- 20th c. — the complaint sense sharpens: the din of traffic and above the din become formulas of urban grievance, while the verb to din into spreads through advertising and propaganda.
- Today — double-faced: beloved din of parties and stadiums versus resented din of machines and neighbours, with the hearer's smile or frown deciding which is meant.
