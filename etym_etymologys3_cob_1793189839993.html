The history of cob is a detective story in which the detectives admit defeat at the first chapter and triumph ever after. Nobody knows where Middle English cobbe came from: it has no certain cognates in any language, no agreed root, and no pedigree to print. What it has, from its first 13th-century appearances, is a magnificent career — lump, loaf, nut, stone, horse, and swan — all visibly descended from one blunt expressive syllable. The word is probably what linguists call expressive formation: sounds that feel round (cob, bob, knob, nob, glob) gravitating to round things, coined and re-coined by mouths that liked the shape of them.

The lump that launched a thousand compounds: Middle English cobbe first meant a rounded mass — a lump of earth, a hay-cock head, a rounded summit — and by extension the head itself and then the chief (the head-man). This head-sense seeded compounds that survive today: the cobnut (round hazel-nut, 15th c.), the cobble (round paving stone, a diminutive cob + -le), the cob loaf (round cottage loaf). Each compound is a small fossil of the day when roundness needed naming and cob was the handiest sound in the dialect. The coal-cob and hay-cob of mining and farming districts show the same naming reflex at work into the industrial age.

The horse enters: in the 16th century the stable-yard borrowed the lump-word for the lumpiest horse in the yard — the short-legged, barrel-bodied, thick-necked riding animal that carried parsons, doctors, and stout squires. The metaphor is transparent and slightly rude, exactly as stable slang should be: the cob is a lump of a horse, and the name stuck because it fits the animal's silhouette at twenty paces. Horsemen then refined what slang began: the cob became a recognised type (about 14 to 15 hands, deep girth, clean bone, steady head), with show classes, hogged manes, and a reputation as the safest conveyance in England. From insult to breed-standard in three centuries — few slang words have been so respectabilised.

Swans, spiders, and false friends: the 17th century added the swan: the male's beak-knob (a textbook cob-lump) gave him the title cob against the female pen. Meanwhile the great false friend must be fenced off: cobweb has nothing to do with this word — its cob is Old English coppe/attercoppe (‘spider’), a wholly separate lineage. And American maize gave the lump-word its most famous modern berth: the stripped ear, a bare cylinder of pith, was so obviously a cob that corncob (and corn on the cob) needed no explanation on either side of the Atlantic.

Key stages and attestations:

  • Middle English cobbe (13th c.): ‘rounded lump; head; chief’ — origin unknown, possibly expressive formation or Celtic; the honest record starts here.
  • 14th–15th c. compounds: cobnut, cobble(-stone), loaf-cob — the lump-sense colonises orchard, street, and bakery.
  • 16th c.: cob as short stout horse — stable slang from the animal's barrel silhouette; the fable's insult belongs to this tradition.
  • 17th c.: cob-swan for the knob-billed male — the beak-lump names the bird.
  • 18th c.: corncob in America — the stripped maize ear joins the lump family and carries the word worldwide.
  • 19th c.: the parson's cob idealised — literature from Trollope to Black Beauty fixes the cob as sense, safety, and Englishness on four short legs.
  • Modern: show cobs, corn on the cob, cob loaves — the 13th-century lump-word thrives in yard, kitchen, and bakery alike.

Sound shifts: almost none to report: cob is too young and too blunt for grand phonology. The doubled -bb- of cobbe simplified to single -b; the short vowel stayed put through the Great Vowel Shift (which spared short vowels); the final -e fell silent and then vanished from spelling. The word's music is not historical but mimetic: the round vowel plus the stopped consonant (o + b) physically enacts the lump it names — say cob and the mouth makes a small round ball and pops it.
