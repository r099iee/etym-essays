First root word: swelling - PIE root *swel- "to swell, to rise up" (reconstructed; c. 5th millennium BCE) — the family of the swell words. A homophonous root *swel- "to burn, to glow" is often kept distinct and appears in Sanskrit svarati "to shine, to resound" and Greek selas "brightness, flash." - Proto-Germanic *swellaną "to swell, to grow, to rise" (reconstructed, Common Germanic period) — the Germanic reflex of the swell sense. - Old English swellan "to swell" (attested, c. 8th–11th c.) — a strong verb of the third class, whose past participle swollen survives in modern English. - Middle English swellen "to swell" (attested, 12th–15th c.) — the medieval continuant. - English swell "to grow in size or volume from within" (attested from Middle English onward) — the modern verb. - English swelling (present participle, attested from the 14th c.) — the participial form required by the passage. - English swelling ("swelling and towering swiftly"). Note on debate/nuance: the PIE *swel- root is complicated by the homophone "to burn, to glow"; whether the two were ever one root is debated and cannot be decided. English svelte (from Italian svelto, ultimately Latin ex- + vellere, "to pull out") is NOT related to swell — a common false friend. The Old English swelan "to smoulder," the burn-homophone's reflex, is noted by the OED as possibly linked to the swell word, but the link is unproven.
Second root word: towering - PIE root *twer- "to turn, to whirl, to stir" (reconstructed) — the family of turning; reflexes include Sanskrit tvarate "he hastens" (one who turns quickly), Latin turma "a squadron" (a whirling troop of riders) and turba "a crowd, a commotion" (English turbulent, turbid, turmoil). - Greek tyrsis (τύρσις) "tower, stronghold" (attested, archaic/Homeric Greek) — the tower-word of Greek; its origin is disputed, and it is widely held to be a Pre-Greek or Mediterranean substrate loan naming the round defences of Aegean citadels, so its connection to *twer- is probable but not certain. - Latin turris "tower" (attested classical) — the form that carried the word to the Romance languages; the ordinary Latin word for the fortified height. - Old French tour / tor "tower" (attested, Old French) — the French descendant of turris. - Middle English tour "tower" (attested, 12th–15th c.) — the English adoption of the French word. - English tower (noun: a tall structure; verb: to rise high) (attested) — the modern noun and verb. - English towering (present participle, attested from the 17th c.) — the participial form required by the passage. - English towering. Note on debate/nuance: the genuinely contested point is the origin of Greek tyrsis. A Pre-Greek substrate origin is probable, and the older proposal tying it directly to PIE *twer- is not universally accepted; if tyrsis is indeed a substrate word, then tower's Indo-European ancestry is at second hand only, entering through the Greek loan into Latin. The name Tyrsenoi, "people of the towers," applied by the Greeks to the Etruscans, is a probable but not certain relative. English turban (from Persian dulband) is unrelated.
