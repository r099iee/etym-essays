condemning is the present participle, gerund, and participial adjective of the verb condemn, meaning expressing strong disapproval, pronouncing guilty, sentencing to punishment, or declaring unfit for use. As a verb form it denotes the ongoing act of judging adversely; as an adjective it describes a look, tone, or evidence that conveys such judgment.

Its range spans law, morality, and administration. To condemn a person is to find them guilty and punish them; to condemn conduct is to censure it; to condemn a building is to declare it unsafe and forbid its use. Condemning captures each of these in progress or as an attribute.

  • Judicial sentencing - pronouncing guilty and punishing: The oldest and gravest sense. Condemning to death, to prison, to slavery means sentencing by authority. The source sentence uses exactly this: slavery was extended by condemning to it defaulting debtors and obstinate criminals, that is, by judicially sentencing them to enslavement as penalty for debt and crime. Bridge: directly from Latin condemnare, to sentence together. Implies formal power and irreversible loss of liberty.
  • Moral censure - expressing strong disapproval: The commonest general sense. Condemning racism, condemning violence, a condemning editorial mean declaring wrong in public. Bridge: from courtroom to conscience; the community acts as tribunal. Often collocates with strongly, utterly, roundly condemning. Does not itself punish but invites shame and sanctions.
  • Declaring unfit, unsafe, or doomed: Administrative sense. Condemning a building, condemning meat, condemning a ship means officially declaring unusable and ordering destruction or seizure. A condemning inspection report triggers demolition or confiscation. Bridge: from person sentenced to thing doomed; both are judged beyond saving. Distinct from mere criticism because it has legal effect.
  • Theological doom - consigning to hell: Historical but still resonant. Condemning souls, self-condemning sinner, condemned to eternal fire mean judging worthy of damnation. Bridge: Roman damnare already meant both fine and doom; Christianity eternalized the loss. Survives in condemned soul as metaphor for one beyond redemption.
  • As adjective - conveying disapproval: A condemning look, tone, silence, review communicates blame without explicit verdict. Example: she gave him a condemning stare that ended the excuse. Bridge: participle becoming attributive; the act colours the agent. Often implies moral superiority resented by the judged.
  • As gerund and noun - the act itself: Condemning is easy, forgiving is hard; much condemning followed the scandal. Here condemning names the practice of censure, often criticized as hasty or hypocritical. Philosophers contrast condemning with understanding, urging inquiry before judgment.
  • Grammar and register: Regular -ing form: condemn, condemns, condemned, condemning, condemnation as noun, condemnatory as adjective. Takes objects and prepositional complements: condemning someone for something, condemning someone to something, condemning something as something. Register is formal and serious; everyday speech prefers blaming, slamming, calling out, while condemn is reserved for weighty, public, or official judgment. Note spelling with -mn-: condemn, condemned, condemning, condemnation.

To understand condemning is to understand judgment as damage: from Roman fines to modern editorials, it always diminishes what it touches, whether liberty, reputation, or a building marked for the wrecking ball.
