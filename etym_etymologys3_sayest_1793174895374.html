The story of sayest is two stories braided: the biography of the verb say, one of the oldest uttering-words in the Indo-European family, and the biography of the ending -est, the little grammatical flag that once announced thou the way a uniform announces a soldier.

Begin with the stem. The reconstruction PIE *sekʷ-, to say, utter, is not the showiest root in the lexicon, but it is among the most intimate, surviving in Hittite shakiya-, to declare, in the Greek word for telling a tale, and in Latin's verbs of testifying and speaking. Germanic inherited it and remodelled it with the weak-verb suffix *-janą, producing Proto-Germanic *sagjaną, to say, the direct ancestor of German sagen, Dutch zeggen, and English say. In Old English this was secgan, a workhorse verb of the chronicles and homilies, conjugated fully: ic secge, þū sægst, hē sægþ — I say, thou sayest, he says.

The ending deserves its own history. Proto-Germanic marked thou with *-izi and he with *-iþi, and Old English wore these as -est and -eth, so that every present-tense verb came in a crisp trio. Middle English kept the system — Chaucer writes thou seyst without a thought — and Early Modern English raised it to high art: the 1611 translators of the King James Bible used sayest hundreds of times, fixing the form in the ears of every subsequent generation, while Shakespeare played it like an instrument, giving sayest to lovers, fools, and kings, and sometimes dropping it for the northern says when he wanted a rougher music.

Then came the great levelling. Between 1500 and 1700 polite England abandoned thou for universal you, and the verb-endings fell with the pronoun: sayest and saith collapsed into invariant say and says. Only the Quakers held the line, keeping thou sayest as the speech of conscience into the modern age, and only the Bible, the Prayer Book, and the stage kept the form before the public. The Romantics revived it as costume — Keats and Tennyson don sayest the way actors don doublets — and hymnody embalmed it: congregations that would never say thou at breakfast still sing it on Sunday.

  • The uttering root: PIE *sekʷ- gives Germanic a verb of declaring that stays phonetically close to its origin for six thousand years — say still begins with s and ends with a vowel, as its ancestor did.
  • The thou-marker: Germanic *-izi hardens into Old English -est, the audible badge of intimacy and inferiority, since thou addressed children, friends, servants, and God.
  • The Authorised Version: 1611 freezes sayest at the height of its powers, so that later centuries meet it first as God's diction, not man's.
  • Thee-and-thou retreat: politeness kills the pronoun in two centuries, stranding its verbs as beautiful fossils, used by Quakers in earnest and by poets for effect.
  • The ironic afterlife: the most solemn conjugation in English becomes a joke-shop crown, worn lightly over group chats and dinner plans.

So sayest is a grammatical ghost with a strangely lively afterlife: dead as inflection, immortal as music, it lets a fairy-tale brother ask his sister a simple question and sound, for an instant, like scripture.
