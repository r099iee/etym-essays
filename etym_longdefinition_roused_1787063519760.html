Roused is the past tense and past participle of the verb to rouse, used in the source context as a passive participle: the dwarves are "Roused by these words", that is, stirred, excited, or awakened into alertness and action by what has been said. At its core, rouse means to bring something from a state of rest, sleep, inactivity, or dullness into wakefulness, activity, or excitement — and roused is the state of having undergone exactly that awakening.

  • Primary sense — waking and stirring: To cause someone or something to wake from sleep or to rise from rest; hence roused means woken, called forth from slumber. A sleeper is roused by a shout; an army is roused at dawn.
  • Secondary sense — emotional excitation: To stir up strong feeling — anger, courage, fear, passion, enthusiasm. A crowd is roused to fury by injustice; soldiers are roused to valour by a speech; an animal is roused to attack.
  • Tertiary sense — starting from cover: In hunting language, to rouse game is to drive or startle it from its hiding place; a deer "roused" is one flushed from cover. This sense still appears in sporting and country vocabulary.
  • Physical sense (historical): The earliest English meaning, from falconry, was of a hawk shaking or ruffling its feathers — "to rouse". This physical shaking image explains the word's basic picture of a sudden or violent stirring into motion.
  • Register: Roused is fairly formal and literary in modern use, common in narrative prose, news, and rhetoric, and rare in casual speech, where "woken", "stirred", or "excited" usually replace it.
  • Grammar: Transitive (to rouse someone) and reflexive (to rouse oneself); the participle roused appears after by in passive constructions exactly as in the source sentence: "Roused by these words, they..." — a participial phrase meaning "because they had been stirred up".
  • Connotation: Usually energetic and positive-to-neutral, suggesting a call to wakefulness, effort, courage, or decisive action; but it can shade into alarm or agitation when the stirring is violent or hostile.
  • Related adjective: The closely related adjective rousing ("a rousing speech", "a rousing welcome") preserves the energising, morale-raising sense of the verb family.

Polysemy explained: All senses of rouse share one image: the sudden, forcible passage from quiet or repose into motion or feeling. The falconry sense (shaking feathers) supplied the physical picture; hunting extended it to flushing animals; everyday use applied it to sleepers and idlers; and from the seventeenth century it was routinely applied to emotions. Roused therefore means, in any context, "brought out of a resting state into active life" — whether the rest is sleep, idleness, fear, apathy, or concealment.

In literature: Roused is a favourite participle of adventure, war, and romance prose. In the source text (Tolkien's The Hobbit), the dwarves are roused by words of urgency and panic — the word captures how language itself can shock a group out of despair into rash action. Across English fiction, characters are roused from meditation by messengers, roused to battle by horns, and roused to love by glances; the participle reliably signals a dramatic turning-point in which passivity ends and the plot begins.
