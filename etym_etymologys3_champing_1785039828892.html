THE BIOGRAPHY OF CHAMPING: A WORD THAT TASTES LIKE ITS SOUND 1. A WORD OF FELT, NOT DOCUMENTED, ORIGINS The verb champing belongs to a class of English words whose origins are more felt than documented. Its first recorded ancestors appear in Middle English of the late fourteenth century as cham, chamb, and champen, all meaning "to bite upon something, gnash the teeth." The words cluster so tightly around the sound they describe that most dictionaries treat them as echoic: cham-cham-cham is the noise of jaws working, teeth closing, and grain being crushed — an imitation of mastication itself. There is no Proto-Indo-European root to reconstruct here, and none should be forced; the word was born in the mouths of people who heard chewing and turned the sound into a verb. What the etymologist can document is the word's later career: from a general Middle English verb of biting, through a Tudor specialization to horses, to a seventeenth-century idiom for human impatience that is still alive in every horse-racing and business story ever told. 2. MIDDLE ENGLISH: THE SOUND OF JAWS AT WORK The fourteenth-century forms cham, chamb, and champen appear in Middle English texts as ordinary verbs for biting and gnashing. The spelling chamb, which keeps a final consonant, hints at the closed, pressing articulation of the jaws — a mouthful of sound that pictures teeth meeting and clamping. The OED cautiously connects the family with jam (v.), "to press tightly," suggesting that both spring from the same sound-symbolic impulse to imitate the gripping, squeezing action of the jaws: to jam is to press shut, to champ is to press and release, press and release, in rapid, noisy cycles. Some scholars have also pointed to Scandinavian and Low German parallels such as Swedish kampa, "to chew, squeeze," arguing that the word may have entered Middle English from the speech of the Viking-settled North; the evidence is suggestive but not conclusive, and the safest scholarly position is that champ is a native echoic coinage of uncertain, probably imitative, origin. 3. EARLY MODERN ENGLISH: THE CHEWING VERB SETTLES Around 1520 the Early Modern verb champ settles into the language with the sense "to chew noisily, crunch." This is the general eating sense, applied to anyone or anything that works food between the teeth with emphasis and noise. The word appears in the prose and verse of the Tudor period describing both humans and animals, and it sits comfortably beside its sound-cousin chomp, which emerges as a heavier, more casual variant and eventually becomes the standard American form. Already at this stage the word's core is fixed: noise plus vigour, the jaws emphatically at work. 4. 1570: THE TURNING POINT — A VERB FOR HORSES In 1570 the word takes the specialization that determines its whole modern character: "of horses, to bite repeatedly and impatiently." This is the turning point of champ's biography. From describing any noisy eater, champ became the verb for a horse working its bit — the animal's eager, jaw-clenching impatience, chewing the iron not to eat but because it cannot wait to move. The image is precise and unforgettable: a horse held back, champing the bit, its whole body straining toward motion while its jaws work in restless, rhythmic bites. The word of feeding became the word of frustration, and the two senses — eager feeding and impatient biting — have lived side by side in English ever since. It is exactly this doubleness that Tolkien exploits when he writes of ponies "champing in their nosebags": the horses are eagerly feeding, but the word's bit-image of gripping, working impatience is still audible underneath. 5. 1640: THE IDIOM TAKES ROOT In 1640 the figurative idiom to champ at the bit enters the language, describing any restless human longing to be off. The transfer is perfect: the bitted horse's physical restlessness becomes the eager person's emotional restlessness, and the idiom has never lost its vividness. It is worth noting that in the standard idiom the verb is champ, not chomp — "champing at the bit" — though the American reshaping "chomping at the bit" is now widespread and increasingly accepted in informal use. Purists still prefer the horse's word. 6. WHAT CHAMP IS NOT: THE FALSE FRIEND FROM LATIN It is worth stressing what champ is not. The unrelated noun champ meaning "field" — as in champagne, from the Champagne region's open chalky fields — descends from Latin campus "field" through French, and the two words are perfect homonyms with nothing in common but their spelling and sound. The chewing verb has no connection to the field noun, and any attempt to link them is a false etymology. The confusion is a standing trap in English, and dictionaries are careful to separate the two entries. 7. MODERN ENGLISH: EAGER FEEDING AND IMPATIENT GRIPPING In Modern English, champing carries the full double inheritance: the feeding sense (ponies champing their oats, cattle champing hay) and the bitting sense (a horse champing its bit, a rider champing to be off). The present participle adds duration and repetition — champing is not one bite but a sustained, rhythmic working of the jaws — which makes it the natural word for the ongoing, homely sound of animals at their feed. In literature it can do both jobs at once: Tolkien's ponies are champing in their nosebags, eagerly feeding, warm, and safe, and the word's hungry, working energy completes the picture of bodily relief after the terror of the storm. KEY STAGES AT A GLANCE. - cham / chamb / champen (ME, late 14th c., "to bite upon something, gnash the teeth") — echoic origin. - champ (EModE, c. 1520, "to chew noisily, crunch") — the general eating sense. - champ (1570, "of horses, to bite repeatedly and impatiently") — the specialization. - champ at the bit (idiom, 1640, "to be restless and eager") — the figurative transfer. - champ (noun, c. 1600, "the act of biting repeatedly"). - champing (ModE present participle, "chewing or biting down noisily and eagerly"). THE HOMELY MUSIC OF SAFETY. Champing is the word in which a sound became a verb and a verb became an idiom — the noise of eager jaws working that English has used for six hundred years to picture appetite, impatience, and, in the dry cave of the Misty Mountains, the cozy relief of animals safe at last.
