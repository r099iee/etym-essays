Hill-top, also written hilltop or hill top, names the summit or crest of a hill: the highest stretch of raised ground, where the upward slope levels off and the view opens on every side. It is the natural throne of the landscape — the place walkers climb toward, beacons are lit upon, and settlements, temples, and fortresses have crowned since prehistory.

In the source sentence the word paints a small bare eminence rising out of woodland: a green hill-top, treeless, lifting like a bald head above the encircling trees. The image works because every reader knows the type — the open crown of grass above the timberline of the slopes, conspicuous, exposed, slightly lonely — and the simile of the bald head turns geography into a face glimpsed through the forest.

Core meaning in detail: a hill-top is defined by three qualities at once: elevation (the highest local ground), openness (typically clearer than the sheltered slopes, wind-swept and sunlit), and outlook (commanding views in all directions). Hence its immemorial human uses: the look-out, the signal-fire, the shrine, the windmill, the gallows, the picnic. What valleys hide, hill-tops declare.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The summit or crest of a hill (primary literal sense): the grassy crown, the rocky pate, the highest few acres. The bridge is pure composition: hill (raised ground) + top (the highest part) — the topmost part of the hill, needing no metaphor to be understood.
  • An exposed, conspicuous position (figurative): to stand on the hill-top of reputation, to be exposed like a hill-top. The bridge runs through visibility: whatever occupies the summit can be seen from everywhere and hides nothing, so the hill-top becomes the emblem of prominence without shelter.
  • A place of vision, worship, or decision (literary and biblical sense): hill-tops as sites of revelation — the Sermon on the Mount tradition, hill-top cities, hill-top temples. The bridge is cultural memory: humanity has always climbed high ground to be nearer the heavens and farther from enemies, so literature inherits the summit as the stage for prophecy, parley, and last stands.
  • The respectable heights of society (social metaphor, as in hill-top houses): fine houses and institutions set on the high ground above a town. The bridge is economic geography: the rich have always built uphill — above floods, smoke, and crowds — so elevation shades into status.
  • Aeronautical and military high ground (technical): hill-top sites for masts, radar, and artillery observation. The bridge is tactical: the same openness and outlook that served beacon-fires now serve transmitters and guns, and staff-college maps still mark hill-tops as ground to seize first.

Spelling and form: all three shapes — hill-top, hilltop, hill top — are current, with the solid hilltop gaining ground in American use (Hilltop neighbourhoods, hilltop schools) and the hyphenated form lingering in British literary prose. The plural is hill-tops or hilltops. Tolkien's hyphenated hill-top belongs to an older descriptive tradition, inventorying landscape feature by feature like a surveyor with a poet's eye.

Register and connotation: hill-top is a fresh-air word — walking guides, novels, hymns, and estate agents. It carries openness, health, and outlook, and in poetry a touch of solitude: the lone tree, the single house, the bare crown against the sky. Unlike peak (severe, alpine) or summit (formal, achieved), the hill-top is modest and visitable — high enough to see, low enough to climb before lunch.
