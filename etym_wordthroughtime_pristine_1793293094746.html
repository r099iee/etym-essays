- Ancient Rome — earlier or former: Latin pristinus means of old times or original standing, without any sense of clean.
- 1530s — English borrows it: pristine first means primitive or original, as in pristine innocence or the pristine church.
- 17th–18th c. — antiquarian use: writers praise pristine customs and pristine purity, the original state as moral standard.
- 19th c. — the clean turn: spotless newness joins originality, and pristine linen or snow means both fresh and unhandled.
- 20th c. — green word: conservation takes it for wilderness never logged, fished, or built — pristine reefs, forests, and coasts.
- Today — mint and moral: dealers grade pristine packaging while essayists mourn pristine worlds, and the source sentence uses the oldest sense made new.
