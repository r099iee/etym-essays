First element — MOCK: Deep stage: unknown. The Old French verb mocquer is of obscure origin; most authorities treat it as expressive or imitative, and a Germanic origin (a verb of murmuring or grumbling, cf. Middle Dutch mokken 'to sulk, mumble' and Middle Low German mucken 'to murmur') has been suggested but not proven. No PIE etymon is reconstructable. [DEBATED — see note below] → Old French mocquer / moquier 'to deride, jeer, mock' (12th century CE) → Middle French moquer 'to mock, deride' (14th century CE) → Middle English mokken / mocken 'to ridicule, deride' (attested from the 15th century CE) → Modern English mock 'to deride, ridicule; to imitate in derision' (verb), and mock- 'sham, counterfeit, imitative' (combining form, established in the early modern period) → MOCK- — the first element of this card's compound mock-politeness. NOTE on the debate: the Old French verb's source is unsettled; the Germanic 'mumble, grumble' proposal is plausible on form and sense but is not established, and the word family (mock, mockery, mocker) is best treated as of expressive origin. Second element — POLITENESS: PIE *pel- 'to beat, drive' (reconstructed; the classic proposal for the rubbing/pounding action behind 'polish') — [DEBATED: the deep root of Latin polīre is not universally agreed; Pokorny's *pel- 'to beat, drive' is the traditional account, but de Vaan and other modern authorities treat polīre as of uncertain origin, so the PIE stage must be read with caution] → Latin polīre 'to polish, make smooth, give a sheen to' (attested from the 1st century BCE, e.g., in Cicero and in technical prose) → Latin polītus 'polished, smooth; (transferred) refined, cultivated, elegant' (past participle, classical Latin) → Old French / Middle French poli 'polished; refined, courteous' (12th century CE onward) → Middle English polite 'polished, refined' → Early Modern English polite 'refined in manners, courteous, well-bred' (15th–17th centuries CE) → English politeness 'the quality of being polite; courteous behaviour' (polite + suffix -ness; attested from the late 16th century CE) → POLITENESS — the second element of this card's compound mock-politeness. NOTE: the modern sense 'courteous, well-mannered' is a transferred use of the physical idea of polish — rough edges rubbed smooth become refined manners. English polity and politics are unrelated (Greek polis 'city') and must not be merged with this chain. Full compound: MOCK- + POLITENESS → MOCK-POLITENESS — the card's target form: a transparent English compound naming courtesy performed insincerely.
