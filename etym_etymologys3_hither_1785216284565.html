Hither is one of the oldest and most instructive words in the English deictic system, and its biography is the biography of "here" itself — how a language points to the place of the speaker. The chain reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European deictic stem *k̂o- / *ki- meaning "this, here", a demonstrative particle that elsewhere produced Latin cis "on this side" and feeds the whole family of "here/there" pointing-words across the Indo-European languages. In Proto-Germanic this stem became the demonstrative *hiz "this", and the Germanic dialects built from it a set of directional adverbs by adding the suffix -drē: the result is the reconstructed *hidrē "hither", directly witnessed in Gothic hidrē and Old Norse heðra. The Germanic six-point deictic system — here/hither/hence, there/thither/thence, and the interrogative where/whither/whence — was thus already in place before any English existed, and hither is one of its earliest attested English members. Old English inherited the word as hider (with a variant hyder), attested from the eighth and ninth centuries in poetry, charters, and glosses. In this period hider was the ordinary, workaday word for "to this place" — the standard directional counterpart of "here", used by everyone from chroniclers to servants, carrying no archaism and no ornament. It combined freely into compounds: hiderweard "hitherward", hidercyme "a coming here". The whole deictic family — hider, þider, hwider, heonan, þanon — was a living, complete system for saying where motion points, and Old English prose and verse used it without self-consciousness. The analogical change of -der to -ther. The most striking phonetic event in the word's history is not a regular sound change at all. Old English hider had a -d-; the modern spelling and pronunciation have a -th-. This happened in the twelfth century, and it happened by analogy: the parallel words thither and whither already had -th-, and the deictic trio was so tightly bound together in the mind of speakers that the hider form was pulled into line with its companions, becoming hither. The same analogical pressure, at about the same time, reshaped father and gather, where an original -d- also became -th-. It is a beautiful example of how whole classes of words can shift together, not by sound law, but by the pull of pattern: the speaker who said thither and whither found it natural to say hither too. By the time Middle English spelling settled, the word wore the -ther shape it has kept ever since. Retreat to the written page. Through Middle English the full deictic system remained intact, and "hither" appears constantly in Chaucer, in the mystery plays, and in the romances, usually in the alliterative or formal phrase "hither and thither". But in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries colloquial speech began to simplify. Ordinary speakers increasingly said "over here" and "this way"; the six-point literary system — hence, thence, whither, thither, whence, and their interrogative kin — contracted under the pressure of a simpler, more analytic spoken idiom. "Hither" withdrew into formal, literary, and Biblical use. The King James Bible (1611) is full of it: "Come unto me, all ye that labour... and I will give you rest" (“hither” figures in passages of gathering and summons), and the phrase "hither and thither" hardens into a stock formula of chronicle-style narrative. Shakespeare uses it with deliberate dignity, and the Authorized Version and the Book of Common Prayer kept the word alive in the ear of every English-speaking worshipper long after the street had abandoned it. Fossilization and the modern paradox. By the nineteenth century the binomial "hither and thither" had frozen into the word's principal survival, and "hither and yon" provided a rarer twin. The adjective "hither" (the nearer side, the near bank) was already archaic. Then, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, a curious reversal: what had been ordinary became valuable precisely because it was odd. The "come-hither" look — a glance of flirtatious invitation, "come here" spoken by the eyes — made the ancient adverb into a term of seduction. And when Tolkien wrote The Hobbit in 1937, he dressed his prose in medieval costume by deploying "hither and thither" for goblins who "ran ________ and thither, falling over one another": a dead adverb suddenly generating living kinetic chaos. Today "hither" survives almost only in that fossilized pair, in the "come-hither" glance, and in jocular or literary play; its register is comic-archaic, and it is prized precisely because modern English has no equally sharp single word for "toward this place". The word's long drift — from everyday utility, to sacred formality, to literary ornament — is a miniature history of how English deixis simplified, and of how a language mourns the precision it has lost.
