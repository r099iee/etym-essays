Compound formation of two ancient Proto-Indo-European stems:

[Stem 1: Prajā — Progeny / Creatures]
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*pro- (forward, forth) + *ǵenh₁- (to beget, produce)
"to produce forth, bring forth offspring"
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (reconstructed)
*pra-źaH-
"offspring, generation, children, subjects"
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500–1000 BCE)
prajā (प्रजा)
"offspring, children, progeny, living creatures, subjects"

[Stem 2: Pati — Lord / Master / Protector]
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*pót-i-s
"master, ruler, husband, owner of the house"
(source of Greek pósis, Latin potis/potens, Lithuanian pàts)
↓
Proto-Indo-Iranian (reconstructed)
*pátiš
"master, lord, husband"
(source of Avestan paiti)
↓
Vedic Sanskrit (c. 1500–1000 BCE)
pati (पति)
"lord, master, husband, sovereign protector"

[Unified Compound: Prajāpati]
↓
Vedic Sanskrit Tatpuruṣa Compound (c. 1200–900 BCE; RV 10.121)
prajāpati (प्रजापति)
"Lord of Creatures; supreme generative deity"
↓
Classical & Epic Sanskrit (c. 500 BCE–present; Rāmāyaṇa)
Prajāpati
"Lord of Creatures; creator deity Brahmā; ancestral progenitor of the solar dynasty"

Note on historical debate: Philologists and historians of religion (such as Jan Gonda, Hermann Oldenberg, and Arthur Anthony Macdonell) have extensively debated whether Prajāpati originated as a concrete functional title applied adjectivally to various early Vedic gods (such as Savitṛ the sun-god, Soma, and Tvaṣṭṛ the divine artisan) before condensing into an independent monolithic deity in the late Rigvedic period, or whether he represents an ancient inherited Indo-European archetype of the Primordial Parent. Gonda established that while the compound elements *pro-ǵenh₁-* and *pót-i-s* are thoroughly Indo-European, the synthesis into a solitary demiurge who creates through *tapas* (ascetic heat) and self-sacrifice is a distinct, brilliant innovation of the late Vedic Brāhmaṇa priesthood.
