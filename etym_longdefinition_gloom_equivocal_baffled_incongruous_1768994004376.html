This card carries four root words from a single sentence of atmospheric horror: "Somewhere in the gloom ahead, they heard a prolonged, equivocal noise that baffled the ear with incongruous elements." Each word contributes a distinct piece of the scene's menace, and together they build a grammar of dread.

(1) gloom — darkness or dim light; also, by figurative extension, a state of melancholy or dejection. In the sentence it names the darkness of a Martian cave, but a very particular darkness: ancient, settled, and dense, an atmosphere rather than a mere absence of light. The gloom is the visual prelude to the aural horror that follows, shadow with a texture one must push through. The primary sense is physical (a dim, shadowy place); the secondary, metaphoric sense transfers the darkness of the world to the darkness of the spirit ("the gloom of depression"), a bridge that became standard in the 18th–19th centuries and survives in idioms like "doom and gloom". It also yields the derived adjective gloomy and the adverb gloomily.

(2) equivocal — uncertain, ambiguous, open to more than one interpretation. Etymologically "of equal voice" (Latin aequus "equal" + vocāre "to call"), the word describes a word, sound, or sign that calls equally in two directions at once. In the sentence the noise is equivocal because it combines two incompatible identities — metal dragged over rock and the smacking of wet mouths — so that the ear cannot classify it. The horror here is not loudness but unidentifiability. The word carries a moral charge in rhetoric and law: an equivocal answer is suspect, evasive, designed to keep two possibilities alive; in modern science an "equivocal result" is one that cannot be classified. The clarifying negation "unequivocal" ("clear, unambiguous") is its everyday counterpart.

(3) baffled — confused, puzzled, frustrated; unable to understand or classify. The verb to baffle has a dramatic history: in the 16th century it meant "to disgrace publicly, to subject to ridicule or infamy"; by the 17th–18th centuries it meant "to foil, frustrate, defeat" (to baffle a plan or an enemy); and by the 19th century the mind entered the picture — to baffle the understanding is to bewilder and confound it. In the sentence, "baffled the ear" is a transferred, slightly poetical use: the noise actively defeats the ear's power of classification, slapping away every attempt to name it. The modern participial adjective baffled names the state of having one's comprehension defeated; a baffling mystery actively dodges understanding.

(4) incongruous — not in harmony, out of keeping, mismatched; incompatible with what surrounds it. Built on Latin in- "not" + congruus "fitting, agreeing", the word describes elements that refuse to fall together. In the sentence the "incongruous elements" are the two incompatible identities of the noise — wet and dry, organic and metallic — whose combination is felt as absurd and sinister. The word has a comic sense in aesthetic theory (the sudden jarring of mismatched things as a source of laughter) and an uncanny sense in horror (an incongruous detail unsettles precisely because it should not belong). Its near-synonyms divide the field: inconsistent (logical contradiction), incompatible (cannot coexist), discordant (clashing sounds), out of place (informal), absurd (violating reason).

Together the four words trace a single arc of perception: the gloom limits sight, the equivocal noise resists classification, the baffled ear fails to grasp it, and the incongruous elements — wet mouths on dry metal — provide the reason the ear fails. Each word is thus a technical instrument of atmosphere, and each also carries a rich secondary life of mood, morality, and psychology beyond the cave.
