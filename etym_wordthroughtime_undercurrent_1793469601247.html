- Late 17th c. (c. 1680–1700) — Maritime and navigational coining: Coined during the birth of modern hydrography and ocean exploration to describe deep subsurface sea currents that flowed counter to surface tides, particularly in the Strait of Gibraltar.
- Early 18th c. — Enlightenment psychological adoption: Moralists such as Samuel Johnson and Joseph Addison immediately adopted the term as a psychological metaphor for subterranean human passions (jealousy, vanity, malice) that govern conduct beneath polite manners.
- 19th c. — Victorian fiction and social history: George Eliot, Charlotte Brontë, and Thomas Babington Macaulay employed undercurrent extensively to describe hidden social undercurrents of reform, religious doubt, and unexpressed domestic longing.
- Early to mid-20th c. — Psychoanalysis and political science: The rise of Freudian subconscious theory and Cold War clandestine politics gave undercurrent new life, describing subconscious drives and covert ideological insurgencies.
- Today — Universal literary and analytical descriptor: Widely used in journalism, cultural criticism, and literature to capture latent tensions, market forces, and the unspoken emotional atmosphere of modern life.
