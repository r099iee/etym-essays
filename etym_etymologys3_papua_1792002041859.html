The etymological trajectory of the name Papua encapsulates five centuries of maritime interaction across the Indonesian archipelago, reflecting the meeting point of Austronesian trading sultanates, Iberian navigational ambitions, high-imperial British and German colonial rivalries, and modern post-colonial statehood. The word stands alongside New Guinea as one of the two foundational European-era toponyms for the massive equatorial island.

Linguists and maritime historians have identified two primary philological origins for the name, both situated in the eastern Indonesian archipelago of the Moluccas (Maluku Islands):

  • The Malay Descriptive Hypothesis (The Standard Philological Consensus): The most widely supported etymology derives the name from the Classical Malay adjective papuah (or reduplicated puah-puah / pua-pua), meaning "frizzy," "woolly," or "tightly curled." In the maritime trading networks centered on the Spice Islands of Ternate and Tidore, Malay and Bugis merchants used the phrase orang papuah ("frizzy-haired people") to describe the Indigenous Melanesian inhabitants of the large, mysterious landmass to the east, distinguishing their tightly coiled hair texture from the straight hair characteristic of Western Austronesian populations. This descriptive term was systematically used in coastal trading records and slave-raiding chronicles throughout the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.
  • The Tidore Political / Spatial Hypothesis: A compelling alternative derivation, championed by several Indonesian historians and ethnographers of the North Halmahera region, traces the word to the Tidore language (a non-Austronesian Papuan language spoken in the northern Moluccas). In Tidorese grammar, the phrase papa-ua translates literally as "not united," "not joined," or "unsubordinated" (from papa, meaning "father / master / unified territory," combined with the negative particle ua, meaning "not"). According to this perspective, the Sultanate of Tidore—which claimed tributary suzerainty over the Raja Ampat islands and the western coastal fringes of the mainland—used papa-ua to designate the wild, unconquered interior and southern lands that lay completely beyond the administrative reach and tribute collection of the Sultan.

The name entered European languages in 1526 through the Portuguese navigator Jorge de Menezes. Blown off course by monsoon gales while voyaging from Malacca to the Moluccas, Menezes made landfall on the western shore of the island (likely the Bird's Head Peninsula or Waigeo Island), wintering among the inhabitants. In his maritime logs, he recorded the land as the Ilha dos Papuas ("Island of the Papuas"), adopting the Malay phrase he had learned in the trading ports of Malacca. Two decades later, in 1545, Spanish navigator Íñigo Órtiz de Retes sailed along the northern coast and christened the island Nueva Guinea ("New Guinea") because he believed the Indigenous inhabitants resembled the peoples of the Guinea coast of West Africa. For the subsequent four centuries, European cartographers used both names side by side, frequently labeling the landmass Papua sive Nova Guinea.

In the late nineteenth century, the word transitioned from a descriptive geographic term into a rigid legal and political border. In 1884, following German annexation of northeastern New Guinea (Kaiser-Wilhelmsland), Great Britain declared a protectorate over the southern coast, officially designated as British New Guinea. In 1902, Britain placed the territory under the authority of the newly federated Commonwealth of Australia, and under the Papua Act of 1905, the colony was officially renamed the Territory of Papua. This established an enduring administrative dichotomy: the southern British-Australian colony was "Papua," while the northern territory (seized from Germany in World War I) became the League of Nations Mandated "Territory of New Guinea."

Following the harrowing battles of World War II—most notably the Kokoda Track campaign of 1942, in which Papuan stretcher-bearers earned legendary acclaim as "Fuzzy Wuzzy Angels"—Australia merged the two administrations into the Territory of Papua and New Guinea. In September 1975, the unified territory gained independence as the sovereign state of Papua New Guinea. Concurrently, in western New Guinea, Indigenous West Papuan political leaders continuously campaigned to restore the historic name Papua in place of the Indonesian military-era names Irian Barat and Irian Jaya, a goal officially realized in 2001 under Indonesia's Special Autonomy Law, which established the provinces of Tanah Papua.
