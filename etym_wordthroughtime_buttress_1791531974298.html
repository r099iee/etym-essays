- 12th–13th c. — Old French bouterez and buteret name the projecting supports of Romanesque and early Gothic churches; the word is purely architectural and local.
- 14th c. — Middle English butres appears in building accounts and descriptions of churches; it is a craftsman’s term, concrete and unliterary.
- 15th–16th c. — The great Gothic churches make the flying buttress a familiar sight; “buttress” acquires associations of height, daring and ecclesiastical wealth.
- 17th c. — The verb extends from masonry to argument and institution: writers begin to “buttress” claims, reputations and governments.
- 18th–19th c. — Antiquarian and Romantic writing treats buttresses as emblems of medieval piety and endurance; the word becomes part of the picturesque vocabulary of ruins.
- 19th–20th c. — Engineering gives the word new precision: buttress dams, buttress threads and buttress roots; the noun remains technical while the verb grows more abstract.
- 21st c. — The verb is common in journalism and policy prose (“buttressed by evidence,” “buttress the currency”); the noun survives mainly in architecture and in the phrase “a buttress of” society or the constitution.
