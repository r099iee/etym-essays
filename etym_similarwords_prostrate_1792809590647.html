• Prone: Similar because both describe lying flat and face-down; differs because prone is the plain positional word (prone on the stretcher, prone to error), while prostrate adds ceremony or calamity — worship, surrender, or collapse.
  • Supine: Similar because both are formal postures of lying flat; differs because supine is face-up, exposed and passive (and figuratively means negligently passive), while prostrate is face-down in reverence or ruin.
  • Prostrated: Similar because it is the same root’s verb form, often interchangeable in the sickbed sense; differs because prostrated stresses the action or fresh event of being laid low, while prostrate names the settled state itself.
  • Kneeling: Similar because both are postures of worship and submission; differs because kneeling keeps the torso upright and is sustainable for hours, while prostrate is total abasement — the whole body surrendered to the ground.
  • Groveling (grovelling): Similar because both picture abasement before power; differs because grovelling is cringing and usually contemptible (the sycophant grovels), while prostrate can be noble — saints and ambassadors are prostrate, never grovelling.
  • Prostration: Similar because it is the noun of the same state (heat prostration, prostration before the altar); differs in grammar only — prostration names the condition or act, prostrate describes the person or thing in it.
  • Recumbent: Similar because both describe lying down at full length; differs because recumbent is restful and monumental (recumbent statues on tombs, recumbent cyclists), while prostrate is urgent — felled by awe or affliction.
  • Abject: Similar because both describe utter lowness and submission; differs because abject is moral and inward (abject apology, abject poverty), while prostrate is bodily and visible — the posture itself, seen from across the room.
  • Vanquished: Similar because the prostrate army and the vanquished army are equally beaten; differs because vanquished names the defeat (the contest lost), while prostrate paints the aftermath — the fallen lying where they fell.
