Methought is the stranded past tense of an impersonal verb that English all but abandoned: Old English mē þyncþ “it seems to me”, with past mē þūhte. Its history is a case study in how a grammatical construction — the impersonal verb — can survive as a lexical fossil long after its syntax has died, and how a word can rise from plain grammar to high literature to camp.

Indo-European and Germanic background

The verb of appearing belongs to a Germanic family built on the PIE root *tong- “to think, feel” (older handbooks also write *teng-). The crucial Germanic split, which modern English has blurred, is between two distinct verbs: the active *þankijaną “to think, remember, be thankful” (source of OE þencan, and of þanc “thought, thanks”, modern “thank”) and the impersonal/intransitive *þunkijaną “to seem, appear” (source of OE þyncan). The two were historically related — the “seeming” verb was the perceived-from-outside counterpart of the “thinking” verb — and their forms collided in Middle English, which is why modern “think” does double duty for both “cogitate” and “have the opinion”. Methought's verb is the second one: a verb of appearing, not of cogitating.

Old English: the impersonal machinery

In Old English this verb was used impersonally with a dative experiencer: mē þyncþ “it seems to me”, mē þūhte “it seemed to me”. It belongs to the same machinery that produced mē līcaþ “it pleases me” (ancestor of “I like”) and mē lyst “it pleases me” (ancestor of “I list”). The system flourished in Middle English: “me thinketh” and “me thought” are routine in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (“me thoughte”, “me thynketh”). The experiencer pronoun was still a separate word (“me thought”), and only in the 15th–16th centuries, as the phrase fused, did the one-word spelling methought become standard — the fusion itself a sign that speakers were re-analysing the construction.

Early Modern English: the literary golden age

By Shakespeare's time the impersonal verb was a remnant, but a dignified one. Shakespeare used “methinks / methought” dozens of times, typically at moments of inward revelation: Hamlet's “Methinks I am a prophet new inspired”, the Merchant of Venice's “Methinks I hear my father's spirit”? Wait — that is Hamlet again. The point stands closer to hand: the King James Bible (1611) uses “methought”, and Milton writes “methought” in Paradise Lost for dream-like vision. In all these the form performs a quasi-prophetic function: it reports a perception arriving from outside the will. That is exactly why Wordsworth, two centuries later, reaches for it in The Prelude: “For I, methought, while the sweet breath of heaven / Was blowing on my body, felt within / A correspondent breeze…”. The impersonal form lets the feeling seem received, not manufactured — the breeze, quite literally, blows upon him, and the thought is breathed into him.

Loss in ordinary prose and life as an archaism

From the 16th century onward the spoken language re-analysed the construction: “me thinketh” was heard as “I think” (the dative me was read as subject I), so the impersonal verb died in everyday registers, surviving only in the fused fossils methinks and methought. Prose writers from Defoe to Dickens almost never use it outside quotation; the 19th-century historical novelists and Romantic poets readopted it as stage diction for wistfulness and vision. Today “methought” is a complete archaism used for historical pastiche, for the Shakespearean tag “methinks…”, and for consciously old-fashioned meditative verse. Its modern perception — quaint, lofty, faintly comedic — is the inverse of its original grammatical status as the plainest possible way to say “it seemed to me”, and that inversion is the whole tale of the word.

  • PIE *tong- “to think, feel” → active *þankijaną (“think”) and impersonal *þunkijaną (“seem”).
  • OE mē þyncþ / mē þūhte — dative experiencer + impersonal verb of appearing.
  • ME “me thinketh / me thought” (Chaucer) — pronoun still separate; fusion later.
  • 16th–17th c.: one-word methought; Shakespeare, KJV, Milton; “I think” re-analysis in speech.
  • Romantic revival (Wordsworth, The Prelude) → modern conscious archaism and comedy.
