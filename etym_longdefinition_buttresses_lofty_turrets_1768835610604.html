buttresses — masonry supports built against or projecting from a wall to take its outward thrust and hold the wall up; the architectural answer to the wall's desire to fall. In Gothic building the buttress, and especially the flying buttress, carries the weight of the vault and transmits it safely to the ground. Etymologically a buttress is a 'pushing' — from the Old French verb bouter, 'to push' — because the buttress is literally the thing that pushes back against the wall. In the sentence, the buttresses of Gormenghast are 'time-eaten': ancient, decayed, crumbling shoulders of stone set against the castle's enormous burden, worn down by the seasons that fall over the irregular roofs. The nuance is one of exhausted endurance: the buttresses have held the castle up for so long that time has eaten into them, and their ruined state is part of the general decomposition of the stronghold.

Polysemy of buttresses.

  • Architectural support (primary): a masonry pier built against a wall to resist its outward thrust; the flying buttress transmits the vault's weight to the ground.
  • Figurative support: anything that props up, strengthens, or defends an argument, institution, or state — 'the buttress of the law'. Bridge: the stone that holds up the wall becomes the image of anything that holds something up.
  • The verb to buttress: to support, strengthen, reinforce — 'to buttress an argument with evidence'.
  • Geological/glacial use: a spur or ridge of rock or ice that reinforces a mountain face (a rock buttress).

Against its synonyms, buttress is more precise than support (generic) or prop (temporary, wooden); it is the masonry term for a permanent structural member. Pillar and column stand vertically and carry load from above; a buttress stands against a wall to resist lateral thrust.

lofty — of imposing height; rising high; also, of a character or tone, exalted and noble — literally 'of the air' or 'of the sky'. In the sentence the lofty turrets are the towers that rise over the broken rooftops — 'broken and lofty turrets' — and the adjective does double work: it gives the turrets physical height and at the same time a remoteness and elevation that set them above the ruin below.

Polysemy of lofty.

  • Physical height (primary): rising high in the air; tall and imposing — lofty towers, lofty peaks, a lofty ceiling.
  • Exalted, noble (of character, ideals, style): morally or intellectually elevated — lofty ideals, a lofty ambition. Bridge: height in space becomes height in value; what rises high in the sky rises high in worth.
  • Haughty, proud (of manner): consciously elevated, disdainfully superior — a lofty bearing, lofty disdain. Bridge: the same vertical metaphor, judged coldly: the man who stands above looks down.
  • Of style or language: grand, elevated, sublime — a lofty style, lofty eloquence.

Bridge: the word is formed from loft (the upper storey or the air itself, from Old Norse lopt) + -y; 'lofty' means literally 'of the loft', of the upper air — and the whole range of senses, from tall to noble to haughty, is the semantic career of the vertical metaphor.

turrets — small towers, usually at the angles or corners of a larger building, often projecting from the wall. In the sentence the turrets of Gormenghast are 'broken and lofty' — ruined yet still high, the little towers of the stronghold rising over the irregular roofs. The word is the diminutive of tower, and it keeps that smallness inside it: a turret is a tower that has been miniaturised, a little tower on top of a big one.

Polysemy of turrets.

  • Architectural towerlet (primary): a small tower at the angle or corner of a building, often containing a stair and projecting from the wall — the lookout and flanking accent of castle, palace, and church.
  • Military turret (of a warship or tank): a revolving armoured housing for guns. Bridge: the little watchful tower of the castle becomes the little watchful gun-platform of the modern warship; the 19th century gave the name to rotating armoured gun mounts.
  • Machine-tool turret: in engineering, the rotating tool-holder of a turret lathe. Bridge: the same idea of a small revolving structure holding instruments of work.

Bridge: all senses descend from Latin turris, 'tower', through the French diminutive tourette, 'little tower'; the diminutive still preserves a smallness within the tower, whether of stone, steel, or lathe.
