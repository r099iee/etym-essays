"To the tonsils" is an informal British idiom meaning thoroughly, completely, or to the utmost limit, almost always used to describe a state of emotional saturation such as being piqued, fed up, bored, or amused to the tonsils. In the source context "piqued to the tonsils," it means thoroughly irritated or nettled, not mildly annoyed but irritated through and through, as if the feeling of pique has risen physically up to the throat and can go no further.

The phrase belongs to a family of bodily-limit hyperboles in English — up to the eyes, up to the neck, up to here, to the back teeth — that map degree onto anatomy. The tonsils, small glands at the back of the throat, serve as a vivid upper-boundary marker: once feeling reaches the tonsils, the body is full. Wodehouse and his contemporaries favoured it for comic understatement, pairing a light verb like piqued with a grotesquely physical terminus to produce a mock-clinical effect. The register is distinctly colloquial, upper-middle-class, slightly dated and playful, never formal or technical.

In modern use it appears almost exclusively in passive participial constructions: to be bored / fed up / piqued / sick to the tonsils. It does not productively combine with neutral verbs; one would not say "to enjoy to the tonsils" without deliberate comic reversal. It implies a culmination after cumulative provocation, not a sudden flash, and often carries a note of long-suffering amusement rather than genuine rage.

  • Intensive-completive sense (core): Thoroughly, utterly — e.g., "I was piqued to the tonsils" = I was thoroughly nettled. This is the dominant sense today. The semantic bridge is metaphorical fullness: emotion imagined as fluid filling the body to the throat. Similar to "fed up to the back teeth" and "up to here."
  • Surfeit / fed-up sense: To have had more than enough — e.g., "fed up to the tonsils with excuses." Here the emphasis shifts from intensity to weariness and excess. The bridge is digestive: as if one has ingested something nauseating up to the throat. It overlaps with "sick to the stomach" and "up to the tonsils in paperwork."
  • Literal anatomical reference (rare, compositional): In medical or descriptive contexts, "to the tonsils" can be literal direction — e.g., "the infection spread to the tonsils." This is not idiomatic but shows the source material from which the hyperbole was built. The historical bridge is transparent: the literal endpoint became a figurative maximum.
  • Comic-hyperbolic Wodehousian usage: A self-consciously extravagant upper-class slang use where the physical grotesque is played for laughs. The humour depends on incongruity between a refined emotion like pique and a fleshy body part. This nuance explains why the phrase survives mainly in humorous retrospective prose and period pastiche rather than neutral contemporary speech.

The phrase is gradable only through modifiers — absolutely piqued to the tonsils, pretty well piqued to the tonsils — but resists comparative forms. One does not say "more to the tonsils." Its force lies in finality: it marks an endpoint, not a point on a scale. Understanding this endpoint logic is key to using it naturally and to distinguishing it from simple intensifiers like very or extremely, which lack its bodily image and its implication of accumulated provocation finally overflowing.
