Snuffbox in its founding and still primary sense is a small, usually lidded box made to hold powdered tobacco — snuff — keeping it dry, fragrant, and ready to be taken as a pinch between finger and thumb and inhaled through the nostrils. The classic snuffbox fits in a waistcoat pocket or a lady's reticule: a hinged case of silver, gold, tortoiseshell, enamel, horn, lacquer, or papier-mâché, often engraved, jewelled, or painted, whose lid shuts tight against damp air.

In the source sentence the word carries exactly this keepsake sense at its most tender: a silver snuffbox left behind with friends as a pledge of remembrance, to be looked on often so that its owner's welfare stays present in their thoughts. Here the box is far more than packaging; it is a portable fragment of the giver, a small shining surety of affection, the kind of personal object that 18th- and 19th-century people actually did exchange, bequeath, and treasure.

Core meaning in detail: Snuff itself is tobacco ground to a fine aromatic powder, often scented with bergamot, lavender, or tonquin, and the box exists to serve the ritual of taking it: flip the lid with the thumb, offer the open box to company, take a pinch, sniff, sneeze into a handkerchief, return the box to the pocket. Because damp ruins snuff and scent escapes quickly, a good box seals closely; because the gesture was public and gallant, a fine box advertised taste and means. The greatest examples — French gold boxes, English enamel Battersea boxes, Russian imperial presentation boxes set with diamonds — are masterpieces of the goldsmith's art, and museums from the Louvre to the Victoria and Albert keep whole cabinets of them.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Small box for powdered tobacco (primary historical sense, from the 1680s): the pocket case described above, in silver, gold, shell, horn, or enamel; the sense every dictionary lists first and the sense of the source sentence.
  • Jewelled objet de vertu and collectible (18th c. luxury sense): presentation snuffboxes given by princes, enamel portrait boxes, commemorative boxes. The bridge is craft escalation: once the box became a public ornament, goldsmiths turned a humble container into jewellery, and today most surviving snuffboxes live as antiques rather than as tobacco furniture.
  • The anatomical snuffbox (medicine, 19th c.): the triangular hollow on the back of the wrist at the base of the thumb, bounded by tendons, where a pinch of snuff was traditionally placed before sniffing it. The bridge is visual and practical: the little depression holds exactly a pinch of powder, so anatomists named the body after the habit, and medical students still learn to feel the radial pulse and the scaphoid bone through it.
  • Generalized small lidded box for pills, mints, or trinkets (modern reuse): antique snuffboxes are routinely repurposed to hold tablets, mints, rings, or earbuds. The bridge is functional analogy: the form — small, hinged, pocket-sized, tightly shutting — outlives the tobacco habit it was built for, so the word stretches to cover any second life of the object.
  • Token of sociability and gallantry (cultural sense): to offer one's box was the 18th-century equivalent of offering a cigarette or a handshake — a choreographed courtesy with its own etiquette of tapping, presenting, and declining. The bridge runs from object to gesture: the box names the ritual, so that keeping or leaving a silver snuffbox, as in the source sentence, means keeping or leaving friendship itself.
  • A pinch or portion of snuff by metonymy (rare, literary): writers occasionally use the container for the contents, as when a character is offered a snuffbox and takes, in effect, a pinch. The bridge is the everyday container-for-contents slide found in words like cup, glass, and pinch itself.

Register and connotation: Snuffbox is a warmly old-fashioned word, faintly perfumed and courtly. It suggests candlelight, waistcoats, and elaborate politeness rather than the modern cigarette's haste. Even people who have never seen snuff taken understand the snuffbox as an emblem of a slower, more ceremonious age — which is precisely why it works so well as a keepsake in the source sentence. The anatomical sense is neutrally clinical, the only cold corner of an otherwise affectionate word.
