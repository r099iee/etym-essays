- 4th–1st c. BCE — praetor is a working title: the elected magistrate who states the law and commands a province after his year in Rome.
- 1st–2nd c. CE — with the edict codified under Hadrian and jurisdiction increasingly handled by imperial officials, the word begins to drift from “active judge” toward “holder of a distinguished rank”.
- 3rd–4th c. — the praetorship becomes a costly honorary dignity attached to the games; praetor now means a wealthy senator performing ceremonial duties, not a working magistrate.
- 10th–11th c. — the title is revived in Byzantium for a provincial governor and judge.
- 15th–17th c. — the word belongs to English antiquarian and legal writing, used of the Roman magistrate, and remains a bookish term of classical reference.
- 19th–21st c. — fixed in classical dictionaries as a technical term, while its adjective praetorian lives on independently in political metaphor for guards, enforcers and military meddling.
