THE DRINKING VERB: FROM PIE *LAB- TO ENGLISH LAP

The core verb lap "to drink with the tongue" descends from a Proto-Indo-European root *lab- "to lick, lap up", attested across the family: Greek láptein "to lap up, sip" (and lápsein "to slurp"), Latin lambere "to lick" (the source of English lambent, "playing lightly over a surface" — remarkably, the same root that gives lapping also gives the poetic lambent flames), Lithuanian lakti "to lap up", and the Germanic line that concerns us. Proto-Germanic *lapōną "to lap, drink with the tongue" produced Old English lapian "to drink with the tongue" (attested from the 8th century), Middle Dutch lapen, Old High German laffan, and German leffen — a coherent family of tongue-drinking verbs. The root's shape (*lab-) and its spread across Greek, Latin, Germanic, and Baltic make it one of the most securely reconstructed "oral" roots in Indo-European; the drinking sense is not in doubt.

PHONETIC HISTORY

Old English lapian is a weak class II verb (lapian — lapode — lapod), so its history is simple: the -ian/-ode inflections were lost in Middle English (lappen), leaving the bare stem lap by Early Modern English; the -ing form lapping on this card is the ordinary present participle. No major sound changes are involved — the word has remained phonetically stable for over a thousand years, which is typical of short, common, sound-symbolic vocabulary. Its stability is itself evidence of its basic, domestic character: this is a word of kitchens, barns, and stream-banks, passed on orally generation after generation.

THE SPLASH SENSE: SOUND EXTENSION OR IMITATION?

The sense "to wash against with gentle splashing" appears in the 14th century (lapping water), and its origin is genuinely debated:

  • The standard explanation (OED, Etymonline) treats it as a transfer from the drinking sense: water against a shore makes the sound of an animal drinking, so the same verb names both.
  • An alternative view treats the splash sense as independently imitative: lap, slap, slop, splash, slurp form a cluster of sound-symbolic liquid words in English, and "lapping" water may simply mimic its own sound.
  • Either way, the two senses have been fused in English for seven centuries, and the sensory bridge is the sound itself — the word names an acoustics before a mechanics. This is a textbook case of onomatopoeia meeting metaphor: the dog's tongue and the wave's lip make the same music.

THE OVERLAP HOMONYM: FROM OLD ENGLISH LÆPPA

Independently, English has a second lap, from Old English læppa "a fold, flap, or skirt of a garment", from Proto-Germanic *lappan- "a rag, flap", probably from PIE *leb- "to hang down, flap". This lap means "the part that folds over": the fold of cloth (hence the lap of a seated person), the verb "to fold, wrap" (lapped in luxury), "to overlap" (edges that lie one over another), and, from overlap, the racing lap — a circuit of a track, so called because in lapping a competitor the runner's path "laps over" the other's. The two laps (drink and fold) are etymologically unrelated but have coexisted since Old English, and the -ing form lapping carries both families today: "lapping waves" (drink/splash family) and "lapping a track" or "lapping a bandage" (fold family).

SOCIOCULTURAL CONTEXT AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • Old English: lapian appears in glosses of the Psalms (drinking with the tongue, of animals) and in everyday vocabulary lists.
  • 14th c.: the splash sense appears; by the 16th century "lapping water" is established poetic property.
  • 16th–17th c.: Shakespeare uses lap of water and the drinking sense; Spenser and the pastoral poets make lapping brooks a stock feature of the ideal landscape.
  • 18th–19th c.: Romantic poetry (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats) fixes lapping as the sound of peace — "lapping waves" are the aural signature of lakes and shores; the verb "lap up" (absorb greedily) appears in the 19th century.
  • 20th c.: "lapping" remains the standard poetic word for gentle water; the racing lap and the technical lap (polishing) spread the form into sport and engineering; "lapped in luxury" keeps the fold-sense alive in journalism.
  • 21st c.: lapping thrives in nature writing, song lyrics, and ocean-side tourism copy; Tolkien's The Hobbit (the source of this card) uses "lapping and bubbling" for the deepest stream at the foot of an inland cliff — the word at its most musical, water murmuring against rock.

SUMMARY OF THE DRIFT

From a barnyard verb for animals drinking, lap grew a water-sense through sound, then a poetic register through Romantic landscape poetry, then a greedy-consumption metaphor in the 19th century, and finally a family of technical homographs (racing, engineering) that ride the same spelling. The chain is: animal drink → liquid sound → gentle water motion → soothing poetic scene → eager absorption → (separately) fold/overlap/circuit. Every stage is attested; none is guessed.
