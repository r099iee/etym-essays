- [early 15th century: the net] - English tonnel borrows Old French tonnelle, a funnel- or barrel-shaped net for decoying birds — a workaday fowler's word.
- [1540s: the tube] - Metaphor takes over: anything long, hollow, and tubular is a tunnel — a pipe, a flue, the narrowest of passages.
- [1660s: under the earth] - The word claims its defining sense: a passage cut under a river or through a mountain, ousting the older native mine.
- [1795–19th century: Victorian engineering] - "To tunnel" becomes a verb, and the great age of tunnelling — the Thames Tunnel, mountain railways — gives the word heroic, industrial, public-works glamour.
- [20th–21st century: covert and digital] - Tunnelling means escape (prison-break tunnels), illegality (smugglers' tunnels), and networks (data tunnelling); "tunnel vision" moralizes it toward narrowness — the word still digs a road, but the road may be metaphorical.
