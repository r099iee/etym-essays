- c. 1300s–1500s - Entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman (*enbaumen*), referring specifically to anointing and treating deceased royalty and saints with fragrant botanical balms and spices to arrest decay and prevent offensive odor.
- 1500s–1700s - Broadened into poetic and theological literature to signify soothing, comforting, or steeping something in fragrant sweetness (e.g., flowers or holy grace *embalming* the soul).
- 1860s–1900s - Transformed into an industrial scientific profession during the American Civil War through the invention of chemical arterial injection (Dr. Thomas Holmes), shifting perception from mystical anointing to scientific sanitation and mortuary commerce.
- 20th Century to Present - Established as standard Western funeral practice for viewings; also used metaphorically or satirically in literature (such as Wodehouse and Waugh) for unnatural rigidity, lifeless preservation, or dull paralysis.
