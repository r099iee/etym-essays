- 16th c. — onomatopoeic "twang" is recorded for the sharp, ringing note of a plucked taut string — a bowstring, a lute or viol string snapping back on release; noun and verb appear together, and to "twang" is to shoot or sound with a vibrating string.
- 17th–19th c. — the sound-idea extends from strings to the voice: a "nasal twang" is speech produced through the taut vibrating membrane of the nose, and regional accents are said to "twang"; the word keeps its sense of thin, high, ringing vibration.
- 20th c. — with the banjo and guitar, "twang" becomes a word of popular music and of American regional speech; in narrative it stays percussive and faintly menacing — the precise, resonant signature of an instrument or weapon being released.
- Contemporary — "twang" still names the string-sound proper (guitars, banjos, bows) and the nasal voice-quality; its sharpness and instantaneity make it the natural word for the bow that has "spoken" beside a listener's ear.
