- 13th–14th centuries: 'remenaunt' — plain word for 'the rest': the remainder of money, time, story or people; no special emotion.
- 15th–16th centuries: the cloth trade adopts it — 'remnant of cloth', the leftover piece of a roll sold cheap; the word narrows and becomes commercial.
- 16th–17th centuries: the English Bible makes 'the Remnant' a theological term — the faithful few preserved through judgment.
- 18th–19th centuries: widens again into elegiac historical use — remnants of armies, empires, civilizations; the 'remnant sale' becomes a Victorian shopping institution.
- 20th century: science recalibrates it — 'supernova remnant', 'remnant population', 'remnant cholesterol'; the emotional register splits between flat technical and solemn.
- Today: mildly formal and bookish; carries a built-in sense of loss — you say 'remnant' when you wish to dignify or mourn what is left.
