Hénault is not a word that can be looked up for its meaning; it is a surname, and a surname is a small historical document. Its interest lies in what it records about the society that coined it: the Frankish settlement of Gaul, the Latin chancery that wrote Germanic names down, the French sound changes that reshaped them, and finally the social history of the robe that made one particular Hénault famous. The name belongs to the great stock of dithematic — two-element — Germanic personal names that the Franks brought into Gaul between the fifth and seventh centuries and that supplied a large proportion of modern French surnames.

The Germanic naming system

A Frankish noble name was normally a compound of two meaningful elements, chosen from a relatively small inventory and varied to honour relatives or to alliterate with a family name. Hag- or Haim- could begin a name; -wald “ruler, power” or -ric “king” could end one. The same stock produced Hagan, Haimo, Hainrich (Henry), Waldhar, Reginwald and countless others. After the Frankish conquest these names spread through every level of Gallo-Roman society, were latinised in charters with endings such as -us and -aldus, and were then inherited as hereditary surnames when French families began to fix their names in the eleventh to thirteenth centuries.

Phonetic and orthographic development

  • Initial h-. Germanic h- was retained in the north, unlike the Latin-derived h- that was eventually lost; it remains audible in the modern French pronunciation only through the vowel that follows.
  • Intervocalic -g-. In Gallo-Romance, -g- between vowels weakened to a yod and then disappeared, which is why the second syllable of the name is a simple -i- or -é- rather than a velar consonant.
  • -ald- to -ault. The group -ald- regularly became -aud- and then -ault in French spelling under the influence of Latinised forms; the l was vocalised before the dental and the final t was added by scribes and never pronounced.
  • The acute accent. The accent on é is a sixteenth-century scholarly device for marking a closed vowel; it distinguishes the name from the otherwise identical Henault of older printing.
  • Final -t. Like the -t of Renault, Arnault and Thibault, it is silent in French and often dropped in English spelling.

The rival route: Hainaut the place

There is a second, equally reasonable derivation. The county of Hainaut, in what is now Belgium, was a familiar political entity, and a man who came from it could be called Hainaut, Hainault or Hénault in the same way that a man from Burgundy was called Bourguignon. The place name itself is usually explained from the river Haine, with a second element of disputed sense; the Dutch form Henegouwen shows gouwen “districts”, which supports the idea of a district named after the river. If the surname is regional rather than patronymic, then the personal-name chain above describes only the majority type and not this particular family. Surname dictionaries list both routes, and without documentary genealogy — a run of baptismal and notarial records — the question cannot be settled for any individual Hénault.

From name to historical label

What changed the status of the word was not linguistics but literature. Charles-Jean-François Hénault made the surname a metonym for a style of history. His Abrégé chronologique de l'histoire de France, first published in 1744 and repeatedly expanded, offered the reading public a monarchy organised into dated entries, seasoned with anecdotes and moral reflections. It was exactly the kind of history that the nineteenth century would come to distrust, and Carlyle's phrase “his sleek official way” registers that distrust while still using Hénault as an authority. In English, the accentless Henault is a printer's compromise: French diacritics were costly and often omitted in London compositing, so the name travelled into English without its accent and has usually stayed without it.

Modern reference and caution

Today the name appears in French literary history, in catalogues of eighteenth-century works, in the correspondence of Madame du Deffand, and in the notes of historians of the Parlement. It is not a common noun: there is no “henault” device, law or unit, and the word has no figurative sense apart from the ironic colour Carlyle gives it. The etymology offered here should therefore be read as a typological reconstruction, not a certified pedigree. The elements hag- and -wald are securely attested in the Germanic name stock, the French sound changes are regular, and the parallel names Renault and Arnault confirm the pattern; but the exact Frankish prototype, the date at which this family acquired the name, and the choice between the personal-name and the place-name route remain undocumented and are flagged here rather than asserted.
