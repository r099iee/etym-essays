• hungrily: Similar because it is the plain, everyday adverb of appetite; differs because hungrily is neutral and unmarked, while ravenously implies near-desperate, animal urgency that overrides dignity.
  • voraciously: Similar because both describe intense eating; differs because voraciously (from Latin vorare, "to swallow") stresses the sheer quantity gulped down, while ravenously stresses the ferocity of pursuit — the eater imagined as a beast that seizes its meal.
  • greedily: Similar because both describe eager, excessive taking of food; differs because greedily shifts the emphasis from biological need to excess desire — wanting more than one's share is a moral failing — whereas ravenously names a genuine, gnawing hunger before any question of fairness.
  • rapaciously: Similar because both descend from Latin rapere and carry the image of predatory seizure; differs because rapaciously stresses force and plunder — the snatch of a predator taking what is not given — while ravenously fuses that seizure with real, gnawing appetite and beats it in raw hunger.
  • wolfishly: Similar because both picture the eater as a wild predator; differs because wolfishly makes the animal image explicit (a wolf's devouring), while ravenously keeps the predatory colouring buried and general rather than tied to a single beast.
  • insatiably: Similar because both describe an appetite that is hard to satisfy; differs because insatiably insists that nothing will ever satisfy the hunger, while ravenously describes the manner of a single desperate feeding.
  • avidly: Similar because both describe eager, intense pursuit of food or desire; differs because avidly (from Latin avidus, "greedy") is milder and commonly used of enthusiasm for books, hobbies, or causes, while ravenously keeps the ferocity of a famished predator.
  • gluttonously: Similar because both describe excessive eating; differs because gluttonously stresses over-indulgence and self-indulgence as a vice (the glutton), while ravenously stresses urgent hunger and predatory seizure rather than mere excess.
