- 16th c. - the word enters English (via French) as a learned classical noun: a tree-nymph of Greek myth, bound above all to the oak; bookish and allusive from the very first, a property of classical scholarship and poetry
- 17th–19th c. - the Romantics and their successors make the dryad a fixture of enchanted nature — a gentle, wistful figure of the living, ensouled grove; the rare adjectives dryadic and dryadine appear among the poets, and the word shades from myth into pastoral longing
- 18th–19th c. - the word migrates into science: taxonomy takes it up (Dryas, a genus of mountain avens; Hamadryas, a genus of baboon), so the once-wholly-poetic nymph also becomes a cold label on museum jars
- Contemporary - the word recedes from general speech, surviving as a literary and fantasy word; set in a list like "Naiads and Dryads," it certifies an older, vanished enchanted world and reads at once ancient, foreign, and wistful — where plain speech would say "tree-spirit"
