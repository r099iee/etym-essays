bequeathed is the past tense and past participle of the verb bequeath: to leave property, money, rights, or — by extension — burdens, traditions, and problems to another person in a will, to take effect after one's death. When Ruskin writes that his father began business with no capital, and a considerable amount of debts bequeathed him by my grandfather, he uses the word with dry irony: what is ordinarily an asset handed lovingly down — a house, a sum, a watch — is here a load of debts. The grammar is dative: debts were bequeathed to him (older English omits to: bequeathed him for bequeathed to him). The father, to his honour, accepted the bequest, and paid them all before saving for himself, earning the epitaph an entirely honest merchant. The legal past thus becomes a moral portrait: inheritance as obligation, not windfall.

In strict law bequeathed belongs to succession. A testator bequeaths personal property (money, goods, shares); lawyers distinguish bequeath (personalty) from devise (realty, land and houses), though everyday speech mixes them. To have bequeathed is to have executed a valid will, survived by legatees, with probate confirming transfer. E.g. she bequeathed her library to the college; he bequeathed £10,000 to each grandchild; the residue was bequeathed to charity. The participle appears in wills themselves (I hereby bequeath... becomes after death as bequeathed by the late...), in probate inventories (effects bequeathed and administered), and in obituaries (the fortune bequeathed by the founder still funds scholarships).

  • Core testamentary sense (head sense): left by will: e.g. debts bequeathed him by my grandfather, jewellery bequeathed to a niece, sums bequeathed for masses. Formal, solemn, final; implies death, document, and lawful transfer. The semantic bridge from the Old English root say is speaking one's last wishes aloud and then in writing: to bequeath is to say who shall have what when you can no longer speak.
  • Figurative inheritance sense (dominant in journalism and history): handed down by a predecessor, whether wanted or not; e.g. a deficit bequeathed by the last government, a climate crisis bequeathed to our children, a repertoire bequeathed by a great conductor, a style bequeathed by Ruskin to Proust. Here no will exists; history itself is the testator. Ruskin's debts already point this way: a moral and financial climate passed on. Modern use is often rueful — problems bequeathed, not treasures — as in the bequeathed burdens of empire.
  • Interpersonal and cultural sense: passed on by teaching, example, or genes; e.g. she bequeathed her stubbornness to her daughter, he bequeathed his notebooks to a pupil. Softer than devise; warmer than transmit. Novelists love it for closure: a grandmother bequeaths recipes, superstitions, and a laugh.
  • Archaic speech sense (etymological, now dead except in quoth): Old English becwethan meant to say, speak to, exhort, blame, and only secondarily to declare one's will. E.g. Chaucerian to bequethe a message (to utter). The general say-sense died out by c. 1300, leaving only the legal survivor — a classic case of a broad verb narrowing to a technical niche because wills kept repeating it. OED calls it an old word kept alive in wills.
  • Grammatical note and confusables: principal parts bequeath – bequeathed – bequeathed (regular; no *bequoth). Do not confuse bequeathed (verb past) with bequest (noun: the thing or act) or legacy/legatee/legator (Latinate parallels). Inherit views the same event from the heir's side (he inherited debts = debts were bequeathed him); devise is land-specific; bestow/grant are lifetime gifts, while bequeath is strictly post-mortem.
  • Register and irony (Ruskin's nuance): because bequeath promises benefit, to speak of debts bequeathed is oxymoronic and gently comic, sharpening the father's virtue: he treats liabilities as solemnly as others treat assets. Victorian readers, steeped in wills, codicils, and entail, would feel the joke instantly. Modern readers meet the same irony in phrases like bequeathed a mess, bequeathed a lawsuit.

Mastering bequeathed therefore means hearing both law and story: a document proved in probate, a voice speaking beyond death, and a son choosing honour over ease. Ruskin compresses all three into one clause, and the rest of the paragraph — payment in full, the granite slab, an entirely honest merchant — is the gloss.
