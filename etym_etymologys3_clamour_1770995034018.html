The history of clamour is the history of the human voice raised in public — from the sacred proclamation of the Roman calendar to the street protest of the modern city. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *kelh₁-, "to call, shout, summon," one of the oldest vocal roots in the family, which appears across the daughter languages in the vocabulary of calling and crying. Ancient Greek kaléō, "I call," lies behind words like ecclesiastic (from ekklēsía, "the assembly of those called out"); Old English hlōwan, "to low," preserved the root in the sound of cattle; and Sanskrit developed onomatopoeic forms like kalakala for confused shouting. The root's semantic core was the voice used to bridge distance and gather people — the shout that summons a community.

In Latin the root split into two closely related strands. The first, calāre, meant "to call out, proclaim, announce solemnly" — the controlled, sacred voice of ritual. The Roman pontiffs, observing the new moon, "called out" the days of the coming month; the proclaimed days were the calendae, the calends, and from this ritual calling-out English derives calendar. The same strand produced concilium, "a calling together," the assembly that gave English council. Alongside this formal strand, Latin developed a louder, more urgent one: clāmāre, "to cry out, shout," attested from Plautus in the third century BCE, and from it the noun clāmor, "a shout, outcry, clamour," used by Cicero and Virgil. Where calāre was the voice of order, clāmāre was the voice of emotion — the battle-cry, the cry for help, the roar of a crowd that refused to be organised.

The noun travelled into Old French as clamour / clamor, attested from the twelfth century, carrying both the sense of a loud cry and, increasingly, of an appeal or petition — the "cry" of a subject to a lord or of the people to a ruler. Middle English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century (Chaucer uses it), keeping the French spelling clamour. In English the word's early life was literary and public: it named the great shout of a crowd, the lamentation of a multitude, the noise of battle, or the raucous clamour of birds. From the fifteenth century the verb developed the modern political sense, to clamour for — to demand loudly and persistently. The phrase became standard in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: reformers "clamoured for" rights, the press "clamoured against" abuses, and the word entered the vocabulary of protest and journalism.

KEY SOUND CHANGES

  • PIE *kelh₁- → Latin: the laryngeal is lost, giving calāre; in the lengthened grade the root vowel appears long, giving clāmāre with its initial cluster cl-.
  • Latin clāmor → Old French clamour: the long Latin ā shortens; the accusative ending is lost; the -or of the Latin noun passes into French as -our.
  • Old French clamour → English clamour: a straight loan; British English keeps -our, American English restores Latinising -or — both pronounced identically.

SEMANTIC DRIFT

  • Ritual proclamation (calāre, the calends) → personal and collective outcry (clāmāre, clāmor) — from the controlled voice of order to the unruly voice of emotion.
  • Loud cry (any loud vocal noise) → appeal and petition — the "cry" to authority in medieval law and politics.
  • Appeal → public demand — "to clamour for" becomes the standard phrase of protest in the 17th–18th c.
  • Modern narrowing — the general "loud noise" sense becomes literary; the dominant sense is collective public demand.

LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • Cicero and Virgil, 1st c. BCE — clāmor for the battle-cry and the outcry of crowds.
  • Chaucer, 14th c. — the English noun and verb, fixed in literary English.
  • 17th–18th c. political prose — "to clamour for rights," "the clamour of the people."
  • Modern journalism — outcry and uproar now compete in headlines, but clamour survives as the formal, literary word for insistent public demand.
