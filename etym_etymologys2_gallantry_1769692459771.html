Proto-Indo-European *wel- "to wish, want, be strong" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; the ultimate source proposed for the Germanic root meaning "well, good"; the PIE stage is a reconstruction, while the Germanic and French stages are attested)
→ Proto-Germanic *wal- / *wala- "well, good, pleasing" (reconstructed; cf. Old English wel "well," Modern English well)
→ Frankish (West Germanic) *wala "well, good" (reconstructed, c. 5th–7th c.; carried by the Franks into the Romance-speaking areas of northern Gaul)
→ Old French gale "rejoicing, festivity, pleasure, mirth" (12th c.; from the Frankish word, with the Germanic w- hardening to g- in French)
→ Old French galer "to make merry, rejoice, live a life of pleasure, display finery" (12th c.; verb built on gale)
→ Old French galant "gay, merry, dashing, courtly, attentive to women" (14th c.; originally the present participle of galer, used as an adjective and noun)
→ French galanterie "courtly courtesy, gallantry, refined politeness toward women" (15th c.)
→ English gallantry "chivalrous courtesy toward women; dashing bravery" (borrowed in the 1590s via French; the spelling with double l is from Italian galante and French galant mixed)
→ Modern English gallantry "polite attention shown by men toward women; bravery" (present-day).

Note on the debate: The Germanic source of Old French gale/galer is widely accepted (the alternative — an unattested Gallo-Roman or Latinate origin — has no support), but the deeper link from Germanic *wala- to PIE *wel- "to wish, want" is an inference about the very distant past. The word has no direct Greek or Sanskrit cognate because it never existed in those branches; its only close relative in English is the native Germanic well.
