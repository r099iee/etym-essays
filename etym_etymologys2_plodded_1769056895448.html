Chain for "plod" (sound-symbolic coinage) — the ancestry of plodded: 1. Imitative / echoic Germanic base *plodd- (reconstructed; no Indo-European root can be established). There is NO Proto-Indo-European ancestor: the word is a sound-symbolic coinage, shaped to imitate the sound of a heavy, flat footstep. OED marks the origin as uncertain and treats it as echoic; Etymonline likewise classifies it as imitative. Because it was coined rather than inherited, it has no reliable cognates in Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, or the other IE branches. 2. Middle Dutch / Low German regional forms of the same echoic family (attested in continental Germanic, medieval to early modern) — words such as Middle Low German forms for heavy walking or thudding, and related imitative verbs in Dutch (plodden, "to plod, to tramp") — form the continental sound-symbolic family from which English took its word. The exact donor form is not directly attested, so name the family rather than a single word. 3. English plod, verb (mid-16th century, first attested c. 1560s) — "to walk heavily, slowly, and laboriously; to trudge". Borrowed from the continental echoic forms (or coined independently in English on the same model) around 1565. Early attestations appear in the 1560s–1570s. 4. English plod, noun (16th–17th century) — "a heavy walk or step"; later (20th century, chiefly British slang) "a police constable" (from the image of the foot-slogging beat officer, popularised as "PC Plod" in children's fiction). 5. English plod, verb — extended senses (17th century onward) — "to work doggedly and without brilliance" ("to plod through a book"), and "to make slow, flat, monotonous progress" generally. 6. English plodded (mid-16th century onward) — regular past tense and past participle of "plod": "walked heavily; toiled doggedly". The regular -ed form adds a thudding extra syllable. plodded IS OUR WORD. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: The point of origin is genuinely uncertain: OED flags "plod" as of uncertain etymology, and the imitative explanation is the consensus but not a proof. It sits in a well-attested family of English pl- sound-symbolic words (plash, plump, plunk, plod), which supports the echoic view without settling it. Do not present any single donor word as certain.
