ACCURSED is historically two stories joined by a prefix: the dark, obscure noun curse, of uncertain origin beyond Old English, and the intensive prefix a- that turned cursen into acursen “to curse thoroughly, excommunicate.” Past-participled as acursede in the early 13th century and respelled with false Latin cc in the 15th, it travelled from church anathema to fairy-tale epithet to everyday execration, keeping its venom at every stage.

  • Old English curs “prayer that evil befall; excommunication” (late OE): Etymonline stresses the puzzle: no certain cognate exists in Germanic, Romance, or Celtic. Old English curs already meant both imprecation and church ban, with verb cursian “to wish evil to; excommunicate.” OED and the Middle English Compendium favour derivation from Latin cursus “course, set of daily liturgical prayers,” extended in the Church to “set of imprecations” in the Great Curse — the quarterly sentence of excommunication read in churches, listing offences that incurred automatic anathema. Rival guesses — Old French curuz “anger,” kinship with cross — are judged unlikely. The honest note is uncertainty beyond Old English.
  • Late 12th-century acursen “to pronounce a curse upon, excommunicate”: To the base cursen English added intensive a- (from OE a-, of-, on-), yielding acursen “to curse out, curse thoroughly.” OED notes an Old English ancestor *oncursian as an alternative source, making accurse of multiple origins: prefixation within English or alteration of an older form. The sense was still ecclesiastical and grave.
  • Early 13th-century acursede “being under a curse” (past-participle adjective): Etymonline dates accursed / accurst to early 13th century as past-participle adjective from acursen. The form meant literally “excommunicated, anathematised,” a spiritual status with social teeth — exclusion from church, community, and salvation. Tales of accursed wanderers and beasts inherit this outcast charge.
  • 15th-century false -cc- by Latin analogy: Scribes doubled the c after a- by analogy with Latinate accuse, accept, accord, producing accurse, accursed. Etymonline calls this unetymological — a mistaken Latinism — since the base has no Latin ad- prefix. The spelling stuck, lending the word a classical look it never earned.
  • 1590s weakening to “damnable, worthy of a curse”: As with damned and cursed, frequent angry use bleached the literal church sense into general execration. One need not believe in curses to call traffic accursed; the curse becomes a speech-act of hatred rather than a rite. Related forms accursedly, accursedness mark the adjective’s productivity in this moral-aesthetic role.
  • Modern tale and comic survival: Scripture and liturgy keep the solemn pole (“accursed be…”), while folk narrative keeps the marvellous pole (“that accursed goose must be bewitched”), and colloquial speech keeps the jocular pole (“this accursed thing won’t work”). All three depend on the same history: a church ban turned folk spell turned swear-word, with the goose standing exactly at the folk middle — stuck, strange, and therefore accursed.

So ACCURSED is a church sentence that escaped into the barnyard: uncertain in ultimate root, certain in English use as the word that damns what thwarts us, from excommunicated soul to unroastable goose.
