- (c. 700–1100) - Old English worhte/geworht: the ordinary past of wyrcan "to work, make, do" — completely everyday; a smith "worhte" a sword exactly as anyone "worhte" a day's work.
- (1100s–1500s) - Middle English wroghte/wrouȝt: still the regular past of werken — Chaucer's craftsmen and the Wycliffite Bible use it for making, doing and performing; no archaism yet.
- (1500s–1600s) - The weak past "worked" begins to replace it in speech; wrought starts its retreat, but the King James Bible (1611) fixes it in solemn prose: "what hath God wrought" (Numbers 23:23) gives the participle a scriptural, wonder-struck dignity.
- (1600s–1700s) - Metallurgy rescues it: "wrought iron" (by the late 17th century) names iron shaped by hammering — the word becomes a permanent technical term of the forge.
- (1800s) - "Wrought-up" (agitated, over-excited) appears in the 19th century; literary prose uses "wrought havoc/changes" for grand impersonal effects — the participle is now a deliberate archaism of weight.
- (1844) - Samuel Morse sends "What hath God wrought" as the first telegraph message — the biblical phrase makes wrought the voice of technological wonder in American memory.
- (today) - Wrought lives in fossils: wrought iron, wrought-up, overwrought, "wrought havoc"; elsewhere it is a chosen archaism for skilled making — mail "wrought for an elf-prince" is precisely the sort of solemn handwork the word was kept alive to describe.
