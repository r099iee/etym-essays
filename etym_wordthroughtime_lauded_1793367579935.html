- Late Middle English (c. 1380–1500) - Borrowed via Old French from Latin; strictly used in devotional treatises and liturgical commentary to signify offering sacred praise, adoration, and thanksgiving to the Almighty; perceived as reverent and holy.
- Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700) - Expanded under Renaissance humanism into courtly poetry, royal panegyrics, and civic rhetoric; used to praise monarchs, military heroes, and classical learning; perceived as an elevated rhetorical ornament.
- 18th & 19th Centuries - Firmly established in literary criticism, academic polemics, and scientific treatises; denoted formal, dignified commendation of intellectual or philosophical achievements without religious overtones.
- 20th Century to Present - Prevalent in journalism and artistic reviews (especially in participial forms like *lauded* and *much-lauded*); retains an elevated cultural tone, though occasionally tinged with journalistic skepticism when describing overhyped ventures.
