Unremitting is a morphologically transparent English formation: negative prefix un- + present participle remitting from the verb remit, used chiefly as an adjective meaning “not remitting.” Its story therefore runs through the long and branched history of remit, from Latin remittere, “to send back,” compounded of re-, “back,” and mittere, “to send, let go.” The prefix un- continues PIE negative *n- via Germanic, the default English negator for adjectives and participles.

  • Latin mittere: one of Latin’s commonest verbs — to send, throw, release, utter — whose deeper origin is disputed; many etymologists connect it to PIE *meyth₂-, “to change, exchange,” though de Vaan and others mark the pre-Latin history as uncertain, a nuance worth stating rather than concealing.
  • Latin remittere: literally “to send back,” whence by natural extension “to give back, return,” “to release, let go,” “to relax, slacken,” and morally “to forgive, pardon, cancel a debt.” All four branches — return, release, relax, forgive — survive in English remit and therefore in the negation unremitting.
  • Old French mediation: Old French remettre carries the forgiving and restoring senses into Middle English remitten (14th c.), first in theology and law: sins are remitted, debts are remitted, sentences are remitted.
  • Middle to Early Modern English remit: the verb spreads from pardon to physiology and effort — fevers remit (abate), workers remit (slacken) their zeal, payments are remitted (sent). Remission becomes the technical term for abatement of disease and for forgiveness of penalty alike.
  • Birth of unremitting: 17th-century writers negate the participle to describe what neither pardons nor pauses: unremitting justice, unremitting application. Theological writers use it for divine judgment that does not remit; moralists use it for diligence that does not remit.
  • 18th–19th-century consolidation: letters, sermons, and novels fix the modern collocations — unremitting toil, unremitting attention, unremitting kindness — where the word can praise stamina or lament burden depending on context.
  • Phonetic and morphological notes: stress falls on the third syllable, un-re-MIT-ting, preserving the stress of remit; the double t marks the short vowel; the -ing form has fully adjectival status, admitting adverbs (“unremittingly hostile”) while rarely admitting comparison.

Debated point: the ultimate PIE source of Latin mittere remains unsettled, with older handbooks confidently citing *meyth- and newer authorities calling the etymology unknown beyond Latin. What is not debated is the Latin-to-English path, which is fully attested: remittere → remettre → remit → remitting → unremitting, a chain in which each link is documented and each sense — send back, release, forgive, abate — contributes to the modern feeling that what is unremitting grants neither mercy nor rest. Keep that quadruple denial in mind and the word never blurs into mere continuous: the continuous merely lasts, while the unremitting refuses — refuses pardon, refuses pause, refuses slackening, refuses return.
