eponymous (adjective) — of a person: giving one's name to something (a place, people, institution, era, book, or film); of a thing: named after a person, or sharing the name of its creator or title subject. Examples: the eponymous founder of a city (Romulus, eponymous founder of Rome); the eponymous hero of a novel (the title character); an eponymous album (a self-titled record). The word can point in two directions — the giver of the name and the thing named — and both senses are standard. CORE SENSES
  • Giving one's name (active sense). The person or thing from which a name is derived: "Romulus was the eponymous founder of Rome"; "the eponymous ancestor of the clan" — the real or legendary figure from whom a community claims descent and derives its name. This is the classical, primary sense and the one used in the card's context, where an eponymous personage is believed to be the ancestor of all members of a community, with the genealogy traced back to a divine source.
  • Named after (passive sense). Of a place, era, book, film, or other entity: named after someone or something. "The eponymous town" = the town named after a person; "the eponymous detective" = the detective whose name is in the title (Sherlock Holmes). In this sense the adjective modifies the named thing rather than the name-giver.
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS
  • Music and publishing (self-titled). In music journalism, an "eponymous album" or "eponymous debut" is an album titled after the artist or the band (a self-titled record); an "eponymous film" is named after its title character or author. The passive sense is now the most frequent in ordinary usage.
  • History and archaeology (eponymous archon). In Athenian history, the "eponymous archon" was the chief magistrate whose name designated the official year — the year was "named after" him, so the list of eponymous archons forms the backbone of Attic chronology. This is the historical-technical sense.
  • Ethnography and genealogy (eponymous ancestor/hero). As in the context, an "eponymous hero" or "eponymous ancestor" is the legendary founder after whom a tribe, city, or people is named and from whom descent is claimed — central to Greek, Roman, and biblical ethnography (e.g. the eponymous ancestors of the ten Athenian tribes; the eponymous patriarchs in genealogies).
  • Linguistics (eponymy). The noun eponym names the person after whom something is named, and eponymy is the process by which a proper name becomes a common noun (sandwich, boycott, diesel); the adjective eponymous describes that relation.
  • Branding and business. An "eponymous label" or "eponymous brand" is a fashion or product line named after its designer or founder — very common in modern commerce (e.g. a designer's eponymous collection).
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE The word is built from Greek epí "upon, after, in succession to" + ónyma "name" (Attic ónoma), hence epṓnymos "giving one's name to; named after." The semantic core is the relation of naming: something is eponymous if it either supplies a name (active) or bears a name derived from something (passive). In antiquity the word did institutional work — the eponymous archon dated the year, eponymous heroes founded cities and tribes — and the passive shift to "self-titled album" is a modern, media-driven extension of the same naming relation, made possible because the adjective's two-directional logic was already present in Greek.
