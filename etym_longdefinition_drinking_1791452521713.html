Primary sense: the act of swallowing liquid. Drinking is the present participle and gerund of drink, and its core meaning is the physical act of taking liquid into the mouth and swallowing it. It is one of the basic biological verbs, as old as the language itself, and it appears in the plainest possible contexts: drinking water, drinking milk, drinking from a stream, drinking a cup of tea.

The gerund: an activity and a habit. As a gerund, drinking names the activity rather than a single act, and English has built a large vocabulary of compounds and phrases around it.

  • Drinking water: water fit for human consumption, a public-health category and a basic human right.
  • Drinking fountain: a fixture supplying a jet of water for public use, a Victorian and American civic institution.
  • Drinking age: the minimum legal age for buying or consuming alcohol, eighteen in the United Kingdom and twenty-one in the United States.
  • Drinking game: a game whose penalties involve drinking alcohol, a staple of student culture.
  • Drinking problem: a euphemism for alcohol dependence, gentler than alcoholism and more deniable.
  • Drinking culture: the collective attitudes and customs a society has toward alcohol.
  • Drinking hole: British slang for a pub or bar, usually a modest one.
  • Drinking buddy: a companion one drinks with, a relationship defined by shared leisure rather than intimacy.
  • Drinking straw: a tube for sucking up liquid, and drinking chocolate, a thick beverage of melted chocolate.

Secondary sense: alcohol by implication. In English, drink used absolutely, without an object, almost always means to drink alcohol: "he drinks" is a statement about a habit, not about thirst. Drinking inherits this euphemistic absoluteness. "He has been drinking" means he is drunk; "she is drinking again" means a relapse into alcohol abuse; "the drinking started early" means the alcohol started early. The mechanism is a familiar one: the narrower, socially delicate sense is allowed to occupy the unmarked form, so that the general verb becomes a polite cover for the specific one. The same pattern gives us a drink meaning an alcoholic one, and the drink as a British term for the sea, by a further and more playful extension.

The figurative sense: drinking in. The phrase drink in means to absorb something eagerly through the senses or the mind: to drink in a view, a piece of music, a compliment, a scene. The metaphor treats the mind as a thirsty body and the experience as liquid. It is an old image, found in the Bible and in classical poetry, and it gives English one of its most evocative expressions for rapt attention. In the Wodehouse passage on this card, the character stands "drinking it in", absorbing the sight of the duck with quiet fascination. The phrase is the exact opposite of the alcohol sense: it is about taking something in, not about losing control.

Related senses and idioms. The verb has generated a dense idiomatic network:

  • To drink to someone: to raise a glass in their honour, a toast.
  • To drink someone under the table: to outlast them in drinking without becoming drunk.
  • To drink like a fish: to drink heavily, an image at least three centuries old.
  • To drink up: to finish one's drink, often as an instruction.
  • To drink away: to spend or lose something through excessive drinking, as in drinking away one's savings.
  • To drink in: to absorb eagerly, as above.
  • The drink: British slang for the sea, and also a collective term for alcoholic drink.
  • Drink-driving: the British term for driving while over the alcohol limit.

Technical and institutional senses. In public health, drinking is a measurable behaviour: units per week, binge episodes, drinking patterns, drinking guidelines. In law, drinking is regulated through licensing, age limits, drink-driving statutes and public-order offences. In medicine, drinking is distinguished from eating and feeding in the assessment of dysphagia, and drinking water is a defined safety standard. In religion, drinking acquires sacramental meaning: the drinking of wine in the Eucharist, the drinking of water in baptismal and purification rites. The word thus stretches from biology to law to theology without ever losing its bodily core.

The semantic bridge. The unifying idea is taking something in through the mouth, and from that single physical act three branches grow: nourishment (drinking water), intoxication (drinking alcohol), and absorption (drinking in a sight). The alcohol sense is a narrowing by euphemism; the figurative sense is a metaphor of thirst. Both depend on the same bodily image, which is why the word feels so natural in all three roles.
