- Antiquity (c. 8th c. BCE to Roman Era) - Originally the Greek astronomical name for the open star cluster in Taurus (*Pleïades*) and the mythological seven daughters of Atlas; subsequently applied by Hellenistic scholars in Alexandria to designate their canon of seven supreme tragic poets.
- 16th Century (Renaissance France) - Adopted in 1556 by Pierre de Ronsard and his brigade of seven poets to champion the dignity, poetic ambition, and vernacular majesty of the French language against classical Latin dominance.
- 17th to 19th Century - Borrowed into English as *Pleiad* or *Pléiade*; utilized in literary and scientific essays to designate any illustrious cluster or constellation of seven great thinkers, dramatists, or philosophers.
- 20th Century (1931 onward) - Transformed forever into the gold standard of literary publishing when Jacques Schiffrin and Éditions Gallimard established the *Bibliothèque de la Pléiade*, cementing the word as the definitive emblem of canonical immortality, India paper, and leather-bound textual scholarship.
- 21st Century - Retains dual prestige in contemporary culture as both the astronomical stellar cluster and the ultimate secular canon of world literature, synonymous with exhaustive philological rigor and enduring artistic majesty.
