Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *kap- "to grasp, hold, seize" (reconstructed; conventionally placed c. 4500–3000 BCE). Stage 2 — Proto-Italic *kap- / *kapj- "to take" (reconstructed intermediate stage of Italic). Stage 3 — Latin capere "to take, seize, capture, contain" (attested from the 3rd century BCE). Stage 4 — Latin recipere "to take back, receive, accept, take in" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; formed as re- "back, again, in return" + capere; the prefix re- is morphological, not a separate root). Stage 5 — Latin receptus "received; a withdrawal, retreat" (perfect passive participle of recipere; the stem recept- underlies the derivative noun). Stage 6 — Latin receptaculum "place or instrument for receiving; container, receptacle" (from recept- + the instrumental/locative suffix -culum; attested in classical Latin, e.g. Vitruvius, with later botanical and technical senses). Stage 7 — Middle French réceptacle "receptacle, container" (attested from the 14th century). Stage 8 — English receptacle "a vessel or container that receives and holds; a place of reception" (attested from the 15th century, via Old/Middle French and Latin), of which the plural is RECEPTACLES. Final node: RECEPTACLES. Notes on debate and nuance: - The Germanic have / Old English habban complex is often linked to *kap-, but the connection is debated by specialists and is not needed for this word. - The Sanskrit verb kapati "holds, contains" is sometimes cited as a cognate of *kap-, but its cognacy is debated in some handbooks. - The suffix -culum in receptaculum is an instrumental/locative noun suffix ("a thing used for, a place for"), the same formation seen in vocabulary such as cubiculum; it marks the noun as a place or instrument of receiving. - The re- prefix means "back, again, in return"; in recipere it yields "take back, recover, receive, admit," the semantic base of the whole receive family (receive, receipt, reception, recipe).
