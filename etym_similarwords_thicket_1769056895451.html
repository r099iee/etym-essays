• copse: Similar because it is a small group of trees or bushes in a wood; differs because a copse is a managed stand of trees grown for wood (cut back on a rotation), while a thicket is an unmanaged, dense tangle of shrubs and small trees that blocks passage.
  • coppice: Similar because it is a small wooded area of dense growth; differs because coppice is the traditional woodland-management term for trees cut to the ground and regrown in rotation, while a thicket is natural, wild and uncontrolled.
  • spinney: Similar because it is a small thicket of bushes and trees; differs because a spinney is a small planted or preserved wood, often thinned, while a thicket emphasises the density and impenetrability — you push through a thicket, you walk in a spinney.
  • grove: Similar because it is a group of trees; differs because a grove is an open, often pleasant stand of trees with room to move beneath them, while a thicket is a packed, low, scratchy tangle you have to fight through.
  • brush: Similar because it means dense low-growing shrubs and small trees; differs because brush is the material or cover itself (as in "brushland"), often dry and spiky, while a thicket is the formed, tangled growth — the place — rather than the plant matter alone.
  • scrub: Similar because it is low, dense, woody vegetation; differs because scrub is the ecological term for poor, dry, low-growing vegetation on poor land, while a thicket is denser and more obstructive — a growth thick enough to hide in and fight through.
  • covert: Similar because it is a dense thicket providing cover, especially for game; differs because "covert" is the hunting term specifically for cover where game hides (often deliberately planted), while a thicket is the general word for any dense bush-growth.
  • brake: Similar because it is an archaic/poetic word for a thicket or clump of bushes; differs because brake is now a literary or dialectal relic ("a brake of fern"), while thicket is the living, everyday word.
