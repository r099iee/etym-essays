- [14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman vigour and Old French vigor (from Latin vigor), denoting supernatural power, divine grace, sovereign authority, or heroic physical strength displayed in chivalric combat — [perception: celebrated as a noble virtue, divine endowment, or martial prowess].
- [15th–16th c. Renaissance & Humanism] — Broadened into natural philosophy and Galenic humoral medicine, designating natural vital heat, youthful blooming, and a flourishing physical constitution resisting disease and aging — [perception: admired as the radiant bloom of youth, health, and harmonious physiological balance].
- [17th–18th c. Classical Rhetoric & Jurisprudence] — Formally institutionalized in legal theory ("in full vigour" signifying statutory validity) and literary criticism denoting cogency, stylistic punch, and expressive boldness in verse and oratory — [perception: hallmark of authoritative governance and intellectually commanding prose].
- [19th c. Victorian Evolutionary Biology & Empire] — Applied to biological populations, racial theories, acclimatization, and athletic discipline (Muscular Christianity); industrial machinery's continuous working capacity — [perception: moralized civic duty and imperial imperative, equating bodily vigor with racial stamina and civilizational vitality].
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Usage] — Codified in plant genetics ("hybrid vigour" / heterosis), geriatric medicine (resilient longevity, healthy lifespan), and political rhetoric; British/Commonwealth spelling vigour retained as a hallmark of standard prestige orthography — [perception: universally positive descriptor of sustained physical wellness, executive drive, and robust institutional capability].
