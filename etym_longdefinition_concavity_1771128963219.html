CONCAVITY is a noun denoting the state, quality, or degree of being concave, that is, curved or hollowed inward toward the interior of an object rather than outward. A concave surface recedes away from the observer, opening its empty interior toward the viewer: the inside of a spoon, the bowl of a ladle, the hollow of a cupped hand, the interior of a basin, and the inner face of a converging lens all exhibit concavity. Geometrically, concavity is the exact inverse of convexity; where a convex form bulges outward, a concave form caves inward, and the two are almost always treated as paired, complementary concepts. In formal geometry and topology, a figure is concave when at least one straight line segment joining two of its points passes outside the figure, so that a concave polygon, for instance, contains a notch or re-entrant angle pointing inward. This pure spatial sense is the root meaning from which every later, specialised sense has grown, and it remains the definition that all the word's technical usages share at their core.

In the supplied literary context - 'The dark thoughts seemed to be stirred within the deep [concavity] of his troubled mind' - the word is deliberately torn out of its neutral, technical register and rebuilt as a psychological metaphor. The mind is imagined as a hollowed-out chamber, an abyssal space carved by sorrow, into which dark thoughts are gathered and stirred as though in a great bowl. It is not a shallow dent or a casual hollow: it is deep, resonant, and empty, an internal architecture of absence defined by what it no longer contains. The metaphor works precisely because a concavity is a space that holds what is put into it while remaining fundamentally void, making it the ideal image of a psyche eroded by grief yet filled with the echoes of its own trouble. The sentence's force depends on the reader retaining the word's geometric coldness at the same time that it is given terrifying emotional depth.

The full polysemy of the word is best surveyed sense by sense.

  • Geometry and topology - The primary modern technical sense: an inward curve or indented region of a plane figure or solid; the property of a line, surface, or region that bows toward its interior (a concave arc, a concave polygon, a concave mirror).
  • Optics and physics - A concave lens or mirror is one whose active surface curves inward; concave mirrors converge light to a focus (as in reflecting telescopes, shaving mirrors, and solar concentrators), while concave lenses diverge it and are used to correct long-sightedness.
  • Engineering and architecture - Concavity describes deliberately recessed structural elements: the inner face of domes and arches, concave facades, the bowls of silos, reservoirs, and reflecting pools, and the curved internal surfaces of combustion-chamber heads.
  • Anatomy and biology - Concavity is used of hollowed bony or organic surfaces, such as the concavity of the acetabulum (the hip socket), the hollow of the thorax, or the concave underside of a leaf or petal.
  • Geology and geography - A concave slope is one that becomes steeper toward its foot; the word describes basins, crater floors, valleys, and other landforms that curve inward toward a low centre.
  • Mathematical analysis - A concave function is one whose graph lies below its chords (opening downward), the mirror-image of a convex function; in economics, concave utility curves model diminishing returns to wealth.
  • Metaphorical and literary - Since the early modern period concavity has stood for interior emptiness, absence, withdrawal, and the receptacle of hidden feeling - the 'hollow' of a wounded mind, the 'void' within which memory echoes, the vessel of dark or unspoken thought.

The suffix -ity, inherited through French -ite from Latin -itas, converts the adjective concave into an abstract noun of quality, giving the word a formal, learned, almost forensic gravity. Whereas everyday 'hollowness' is informal and naturalistic, concavity sounds measured and technical, as if lifted from a treatise on optics or a surveyor's plan. This register is the reason the word could be borrowed again and again by successive sciences: the ancient and medieval optics of Ptolemy and Ibn al-Haytham, which analysed concave burning mirrors; the Renaissance astronomy that built instruments around concave lenses; the eighteenth-century mechanics of arches and domes; and finally modern computer-aided design, where concavity and convexity are routine, precisely defined properties of digital geometry. Across all of these domains the word has preserved its core spatial idea while continually acquiring new technical clothing.

Usage today: concavity is standard in textbooks of optics, anatomy, engineering, and mathematics, and is the preferred abstract noun whenever the inward curvature itself - not the accidental hole - is the object of study. As a general noun for any hollow it is rarer in everyday speech, where cavity, hollow, depression, or dent is more natural; but wherever precision and formality are wanted, concavity remains the precise, unapologetically Latinate choice. In literary prose, as in the supplied sentence, it is deliberately chosen for that very formality, which is then turned against itself to describe something as far from a surveyor's drawing as a mind in turmoil.
