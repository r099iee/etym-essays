The etymology of Tomnoddy illustrates a classic English mechanism of lexical formation: the anthroponymic compound, wherein a common personal Christian name is coupled with an expressive noun or adjective to create a generalized character archetype.

1. The Onomastic Base: Tom:

The first constituent Tom is the hypocoristic (pet) form of the masculine personal name Thomas. The name originated in Aramaic as T'ōmā, literally meaning "twin." Transliterated into Koine Greek as Thōmâs in the New Testament and subsequently into Latin as Thomas, the name became exceptionally popular throughout medieval Europe following the martyrdom and veneration of Saint Thomas Becket in 1170.

Due to its overwhelming frequency in late medieval and early modern England, Tom became the default generic placeholder name for an ordinary male everyman, common laborer, or archetypal figure. This tendency gave rise to an entire family of English compound nouns and expressions: Tom Fool (a professional jester or idiot), Tom Thumb (a miniature person), Tomboy (originally a boisterous boy, later a spirited girl), Tom-cat (a male cat), and Tommy Atkins (the generic British soldier).

2. The Base Noun: Noddy:

The second constituent noddy emerged in the early 16th century (attested in the 1530s) during the Early Modern English period. Historical lexicographers and etymologists link noddy to the Middle English verb nodden ("to nod, bob the head, sway drowsily"), which itself derived from an echoic Germanic root indicating back-and-forth oscillation.

A noddy was originally a person who sat with a nodding head—either because they were sleepy and inattentive, or because they constantly nodded in vacant, uncomprehending agreement with whatever was said to them. By the mid-16th century, noddy was a popular term for a simpleton, a dupe, or a fool, and was also assigned as the name of an early ancestor of the card game cribbage (where scoring involved nodding to the dealer). Furthermore, 17th-century sailors named the brown tropical tern the noddy tern due to its fearless, docile nature and tendency to land on ships where sailors easily captured it.

3. Synthesis, Satire, & Literary History:

Around 1700, colloquial English combined these two elements into the alliterative, rhythmic compound Tom Noddy. In northern British maritime dialects, the name was quickly transferred to the puffin, whose large head, solemn face, and clumsy, head-bobbing waddle perfectly evoked the visual archetype of a harmless, lumbering fool.

In 1840, the term reached widespread literary fame through Richard Harris Barham's satirical collection The Ingoldsby Legends, wherein the poem "The Execution of Lord Tomnoddy" skewered the vapid, thrill-seeking aristocracy of London. In 1937, J.R.R. Tolkien drew upon this deep well of English folk-rhyme and dialect to provide Bilbo Baggins with a taunt against the giant spiders of Mirkwood, mocking their lumbering bodies and comical inability to locate the invisible hobbit in the trees.
