- Old and Middle English — gang means a going or journey in northern and Scots speech, while way is the common road-word everywhere.
- 17th c. — sailors fuse them: the gangway is the going-way aboard, the plank, opening, or deck passage by which feet and cargo cross between ship and shore.
- 18th–19th c. — the word spreads below decks and ashore: deck passages, warship muster stations, and mine roadways all become gangways, anywhere narrow that going must happen.
- Victorian era — theatres and parliaments adopt it: the aisles between seat blocks are gangways, and the shouted Gangway! becomes the stagehand's and stretcher-Bearer [REDACTED] peremptory cry.
- 20th c. — flight takes it up: mobile stairs and telescopic jet bridges are gangways too, while gangway! stays the blunt large-city way of parting a crowd.
- Today — the ferry plank, the jet bridge, the cinema aisle, and the barked command all coexist: one going-word for every tight squeeze between here and aboard.
