Single root word: libertine (from the English noun/adjective libertine). Linear derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *h₁lewdʰ- (also cited *leudh-), "to grow up, to rise, to mount; (by extension) the people, those who have grown up together" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. The root underlies Germanic *leudiz "people" (Old English lēode, German Leute), Slavic *ljudъ "people" (Russian ljudi), and Greek eleútheros "free." NOTE: the root's base meaning is debated between "to grow" (Pokorny *leudh-) and a possibly distinct formation; the collective "people" sense is securely attested across several branches. 2. Latin liber, "free; unrestricted; a free citizen (as opposed to a slave)" — attested from the 3rd century BCE (Plautus); derived from PIE *h₁lewdʰ- via an adjectival formation *lewdʰero-, literally "belonging to the people, of the free tribe," hence "free." NOTE: this derivation (liber < *lewdʰ-ero-) is the standard account but is debated; an alternative connects liber to a root meaning "to rise/to sprout," and the exact preform is not directly attested. 3. Latin lībertus, "a freedman, one set free from slavery" — attested in classical Latin (1st century BCE–1st century CE); formed from liber + suffix -tus (the same family as lībertās "freedom, liberty," attested from Plautus onward). 4. Latin lībertīnus, "pertaining to a freedman; a freedman" — attested in classical Latin (Cicero, Livy, and the Vulgate); formed from lībertus + adjectival suffix -īnus. 5. French libertin, "a freethinker; one who rejects religious and moral restraint" — borrowed from Latin lībertīnus, attested in French from the 16th century (c. 1540s–1560s); the French word carried the neutral sense "freedman" and the new intellectual sense "freethinker." 6. English libertine, "a freethinker in religion (16th century); a man of dissolute, morally unrestrained life (by the 17th century)" — borrowed from French, attested from 1563 in the religious sense; the moral sense ("one who lives without moral principles or restraint") established itself by the early 17th century. TARGET: libertine. Debated / nuanced note: the etymological connection of Latin liber to PIE *h₁lewdʰ- is widely accepted but not entirely settled; the semantic development "freedman → freethinker → dissolute man" is attested but the intellectual-to-moral shift occurred within a few decades of the word's entry into English, partly under the influence of French libertin and theological polemics against religious free thought.
