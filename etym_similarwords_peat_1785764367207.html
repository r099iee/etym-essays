• Turf: Similar because in Irish and Scottish fuel contexts both can refer to cut bog material for burning; differs because turf emphasizes the cut slab, often with surface vegetation, and also means grass sod generally, while peat names the organic deposit and the fuel substance as such, including milled or non-slab forms — in the card’s sentence “peat land” is the bog itself, not the cut sod.
  • Humus: Similar because both are decayed organic soil materials valued for fertility; differs because humus forms mainly under aerobic conditions in topsoils and forest floors, breaking down to a stable dark mass, while peat accumulates under waterlogged anaerobic conditions and remains only partially decayed, with distinct chemistry, acidity, and structure.
  • Muck: Similar because both can denote dark, wet organic earth; differs because muck usually implies mixed mineral-organic sludge, farmyard manure, or messy mud, with little botanical identity, while peat is specifically bog-formed, fibrous or amorphous plant residue with fuel and horticultural uses.
  • Lignite: Similar because both sit on the coalification continuum as carbon-rich fossil fuels in the making; differs because lignite is a soft brown coal, geologically further altered, compressed, and aged, while peat is the earlier, less compacted precursor still forming in or near living wetlands.
  • Compost: Similar because both improve garden soils and are used as growing media; differs because compost is an intentionally aerated and managed human product made by controlled decomposition, while peat is harvested natural wetland matter — and environmental campaigns now oppose the two, promoting peat-free (compost, coir) alternatives.
  • Fen / Bog: Similar because both name the wetland where peat forms and both appear with peat in fixed phrases (peat bog); differs because bog and fen name the ecosystems themselves (bog is acid and rain-fed, fen alkaline and mineral-fed), while peat names the accumulating material they contain.
  • Soil: Similar because both are earthy materials beneath one’s feet; differs because soil is the general term for the mineral-and-organic medium of plant growth, while peat is a specific, distinctively wet and carbon-rich accumulation, sometimes treated not as true soil but as an organic deposit and a fuel.
  • Charcoal: Similar because both are solid carbon-rich fuels of plant origin burned on fires; differs because charcoal is manufactured by the deliberate burning of wood in low oxygen, while peat is a natural geological accumulation of partly decayed bog vegetation that is merely cut and dried before burning.
