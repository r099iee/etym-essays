The biography of canopy begins not with luxury silk but with an insect. Classical Greek named the mosquito κώνωψ (kṓnōps), a transparent compound of κῶνος "cone" and ὤψ "face, eye" — literally "cone-face," describing the insect's pointed head. The word is a product of Greek folk observation, not of high ceremony, and its two ingredients have their own long histories: ὤψ "face, eye" descends with certainty from the PIE root *h₃ekʷ- "to see, the eye," while κῶνος "cone, pinecone" is of more uncertain origin — Beekes, the standard modern authority on Greek etymology, treats it as possibly of Pre-Greek (substrate) ancestry. So even at the root the word is a mixture: a possibly pre-Greek noun wedded to an Indo-European one.

From the everyday pest-name Greek formed a practical noun, κωνωπεῖον (kōnōpeion), attested in Hellenistic texts: an Egyptian-style couch or bed hung with fine mosquito-nets. The object is the key to the whole later history — a technology of climate and disease control, born in the marshy country of the eastern Mediterranean where the night air carried fever. The concrete sense "bed with insect curtains" is the hinge on which all the later luxury meanings turn: the canopy began as a mosquito net before it became a mark of rank.

Latin borrowed the object-name as cōnōpēum (with variant spellings cōnōpeum, canōpēum), still meaning a mosquito net or a couch so equipped. As the object travelled through Roman and then medieval material culture, the referent slowly widened: the protective net became, in wealthier houses, decorative drapery — cloth that still hung over and around a bed or seat, but now signalled rank as much as hygiene. The loanword travelled with the object through the Romance vernaculars, and Old French conopé/canopé carried the sense into everyday use.

Middle English fixed the form. Spellings such as canope, canape, and finally canopy appear from the fourteenth century, meaning first the bed-hanging and then any overhead covering of cloth. The sound changes are modest: Latin cōnōpēum → Old French conopé (loss of the final Latin ending and vowel shifts) → Middle English canope (the o/a alternation reflecting French and English scribal variation) → modern canopy, with stress on the first syllable. By late Middle and early Modern English the dominant image was the ornamental overhead covering of a bed, throne, or processional seat; the mosquito origin was largely opaque to ordinary speakers, though historically inseparable from the Greek pest-name.

Semantic drift summary:
  • Greek κώνωψ "cone-faced mosquito" (5th c. BCE) → κωνωπεῖον "bed with mosquito-curtains" (Hellenistic)
  • → Latin cōnōpēum "mosquito net; curtained couch" (1st c. BCE)
  • → Old French conopé "mosquito net; cloth covering" (12th c.)
  • → Middle English canope/canopy "bed canopy; overhead covering" (14th c.)
  • → modern canopy: bed canopy; ceremonial baldachin; tree/forest canopy; architectural cover; parachute and cockpit canopy; ecological and figurative senses.

The modern life of the word is threefold. First, the domestic sense survives: bed canopies, from the four-poster tester to romantic mosquito-net drapery, still hang in bedrooms. Second, the ceremonial sense endures: a canopy of state, the baldachin over a throne or altar, carried on poles in procession, is still used in the rituals of church and court. Third, the figurative and technical extensions multiply: the canopy of a forest (its upper layer of leaves), the canopy of heaven or of stars, a shop-front awning, the canopy of a parachute, the transparent canopy over an aircraft cockpit, and — in ecology and biology — the technical "canopy" of the forest, all preserving the image of something suspended overhead that shelters and encloses.

The target passage recovers the oldest indoor sense: the canopy as fabric architecture dividing sleeping space, the soft wall in the Jia mansion's suite of beds. The social history matters as much as the linguistic one: in the Red Chamber translation, the canopy marks rank as much as comfort — the same silent status language the word has carried since Roman curtains and medieval court hangings. Linguistically, canopy is a loanword, not a native Germanic inheritance: it has no Indo-Aryan or Germanic cognate family, and German uses unrelated words (Baldachin, Betthimmel) for the same furniture. It is a Greco-Latin word that travelled through French into English, changing its referent from mosquito to monarch, from pest-control to palace, while never quite losing the image of cloth hung overhead to shelter what it covers.
