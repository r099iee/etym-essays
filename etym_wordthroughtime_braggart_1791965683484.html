- 1570s — braggart enters English from French as a contemptuous label for a loud boaster, with strong associations with the boasting soldier of the stage.
- 1590s — Spenser's Braggadocchio fixes the type in English literature, and the braggart becomes a stock comic role in drama and satire.
- 17th–18th c. — applied to duellists, courtiers and military men; the word's force is social contempt for a man who cannot stop advertising himself.
- 19th c. — extended from persons to nations and parties: "braggart patriotism" and "braggart diplomacy" appear in political prose.
- 20th–21st c. — survives as a mildly archaic, mostly literary insult; colloquial speech prefers show-off or blowhard, and braggart now carries a faintly period flavour.
