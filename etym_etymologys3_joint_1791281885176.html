Joint begins in the Indo-European idea of yoking: PIE *yewg- to join, yoke, bind together, which also yields Latin iugum yoke, Sanskrit yuga yoke, and English yoke, yoga, junction, join, adjoint. The Latin verb iungere, present stem jung-, perfect junx-, participle iunctus, meant to join together — to yoke oxen, to clasp hands, to unite peoples. From its past participle iunctus, meaning joined, came Late Latin *iunctus as a noun for a joining place, and Old French joint, joindre.

In Old French by the 12th century, joint is both adjective meaning joined and noun meaning juncture, hinge, articulation. The legal phrase joint tenant, tenants jointly holding, and the anatomical sense of limb articulation pass directly into Anglo-Norman England after 1066. Middle English borrows join as a verb and joint as noun and adjective around 1300. Early uses are concrete: joints of the body, joints in masonry, joints of meat — a division at the joining place.

  • Medieval concrete stage: joint as place where two bones, stones, or timbers meet. Carpenters speak of mortise joints, butchers of a joint of mutton, masons of close joints. Adjective use means shared: joint heirs, joint action.
  • Early Modern extension: joint as shared enterprise — joint-stock company in the 1600s — and as marijuana cigarette much later, from joining ends of paper. The verb to joint means to divide at the joint or to fit together.
  • 19th-century American slang: joint becomes a low establishment: a drinking joint, gambling joint, hamburger joint. The logic is that any shack is boards joined together, or that an opium joint is where smokers join together. First recorded for an opium den in the 1870s, then generalised to any disreputable or humble bar, club, or restaurant.
  • 20th-century neutralisation: the disreputable colour fades. My joint, nice joint, rustic joint can be affectionate for home, flat, or country pub. Hip-hop revives joint for a song or place — a fine joint — completing the shift from contempt to cool.

Phonetically the history is palatalisation and nasalisation: Latin iunctus /junk.tus/ loses its final syllable in French to /ʒwɛ̃t/ spelled joint, borrowed into English with affrication to /dʒɔɪnt/. Spelling joint stabilised early, though joynt appears in Tudor prints. The Great Vowel Shift and loss of French nasal vowel give modern RP /dʒɔɪnt/.

Socio-culturally, joint tracks democratisation. From anatomy and architecture known to scholars, it moves to butchery and carpentry, then to frontier saloons and jazz clubs where a joint was any place you could gather. Prohibition cemented joint as speakeasy slang. By mid-century, a rustic joint means exactly what Wodehouse implies: a hick, out-of-the-way pub or inn, a little shabby but lively. The adjective joint meaning shared never disappeared — joint account, joint effort, joint winner — living alongside the noun without confusion.

Literary attestations are dense: Chaucer has joyntes of limbs, Shakespeare has joint-stools and out-of-joint metaphors, Dickens has joints of meat, Hammett and Chandler have joints as dives. No serious etymological debate affects the main chain; *yewg- to iungere to iunctus to joint is among the best-attested derivations in Romance philology, confirmed by cognates in every Italic and Gallo-Romance dialect.
