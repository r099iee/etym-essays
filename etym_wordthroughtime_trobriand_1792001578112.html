- 1793 — Coined and entered European cartography during the French royal scientific expedition of Rear-Admiral Antoine Bruni d'Entrecasteaux, who named the archipelago in honor of his first lieutenant, Jean François Sylvestre Denis de Trobriand.
- 19th c. (1840s–1890s) — Appeared sparsely in British and French maritime charts, whaling logs, and colonial dispatches as a remote, hazardous coral archipelago in the Solomon Sea, occasionally visited by pearl shell traders and Methodist missionaries.
- 1922 — Rocketed into global intellectual prominence with the publication of Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific, establishing the islands as the world's preeminent anthropological field site.
- 1920s–1950s — Reified in international sociological and psychoanalytic debates (engaging Freud, Ernest Jones, and Marcel Mauss) as the supreme empirical counter-example to Eurocentric assumptions regarding the nuclear family, paternity, and market economics.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Retains revered academic status in anthropology while transitioning politically into the Kiriwina-Goodenough District of Papua New Guinea, with the islanders fiercely defending their traditional matrilineal yam culture against modern commercial homogenization.
