Demosthenes (proper noun) — The Athenian statesman and orator Demosthenes (384–322 BC), generally regarded as the greatest orator of ancient Greece, whose speeches, especially the Philippics against Philip II of Macedon, made his name the standard of political eloquence for all time. Primary historical senses:
  • The orator: Demosthenes was the leading figure of the last generation of independent Athens, the master of the political and forensic speech. His surviving works include the Philippics and Olynthiacs (warnings against Philip of Macedon), On the Crown (the greatest of his speeches, defending his career against Aeschines), and the private and public lawcourt speeches. Ancient critics from Cicero and Quintilian onward rated him the perfection of oratory, the standard against which all speakers were measured.
  • The statesman: As a political leader he championed the defence of Athenian democracy and Greek freedom against the expansion of Macedon, led the anti-Macedonian party in Athens, and after the failure of the cause took his own life in 322 BC when the Macedonian general Antipater demanded his surrender.
  • The name: Dēmosthénēs is a compound Greek personal name meaning "strength of the people," from dēmos "the people" and sthénos "strength, might" — a speaking name of the kind typical of Greek aristocratic families.
Secondary and transferred meanings:
  • Archetype of eloquence: In rhetoric, "a Demosthenes" is a byword for irresistible political eloquence; the Philippics lent their title to Cicero's speeches against Mark Antony, and the term "Philippic" still denotes any bitter invective speech.
  • Triumph over disability: The legend that Demosthenes overcame a stammer and a weak voice by practicing with pebbles in his mouth on the seashore, by speaking with running breath, and by reciting verse running uphill, has made his name the archetype of self-discipline conquering natural handicap — the story is told in every handbook of public speaking.
  • Defender of democracy: In political history, Demosthenes stands for uncompromising civic resistance to tyranny and for the doomed defence of a free city-state against a stronger monarch, a figure invoked whenever small free states resist great powers.
  • Eponyms and technical uses: The name survives in "Demosthenian"/"Demosthenic" (of or like Demosthenes' oratory) and in the modern Greek dēmosthenikós; the "On the Crown" speeches remain set texts in the study of Greek rhetoric, and "Demosthenic" is used of grand, impassioned prose.
Semantic bridge: From the transparent Greek compound "strength of the people" (dēmos "people" + sthénos "strength"), the name was borne by a man whose public speeches made it synonymous with the people's voice; the metonymy then moved from the historical person to eloquence itself, to patriotic resistance, and to the conquest of personal weakness. In the source text: Macaulay names Demosthenes, with Sophocles and Plato, as one of the masterpieces of Greek literature still being "read and interpreted" in the fading Byzantine world — the voice of a freedom that had perished, preserved as a school-text among pedants "destitute of taste, sense, and spirit." The name here stands for the summit of Greek political eloquence, kept alive but emptied of its meaning in an empire that had lost the liberty it defended. Register and collocation: Demosthenes is a formal proper noun of history and rhetoric, invariable and singular. It collocates with the Philippics, On the Crown, the orations of Demosthenes, Demosthenes' speeches, and the phrase "the greatest orator of antiquity"; the adjectives Demosthenian and Demosthenic are learned and rare, and the modern everyday phrase "a Demosthenes" means simply a great speaker.
