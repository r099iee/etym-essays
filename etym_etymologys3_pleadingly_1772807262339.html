The adverb pleadingly stands at the end of a long chain that begins with a Proto-Indo-European root associated with flatness and smoothness: *plā-, 'flat, spread out, broad', with the extended form *plāk-. The physical sense is interesting because it shows how the vocabulary of 'pleasing' grew out of the vocabulary of surfaces: what is smooth, flat, and even is what is agreeable to the touch, and from 'smooth' the root moves to 'agreeable', 'acceptable', 'pleasing'. Latin develops this into the verb plācēre, 'to please, be acceptable, give satisfaction', one of the most productive sources of English vocabulary. From the same family English inherits placid 'calm, serene', placate 'to calm, appease', complacent 'self-satisfied' (originally 'pleasing oneself'), and placebo 'I shall please' — the medical sugar pill that pleases the patient — all preserving the idea of calming or pleasing through smoothness and agreeableness.

From pleasing to decree. Latin plācēre also gives placitum, originally the neuter participle meaning 'that which is pleasing' or 'that which has been agreed upon'. In classical Latin placitum means 'opinion, decision, decree' — the thing laid down and accepted. Medieval legal Latin then hardens placitum into the technical name of a formal plea, suit, or judicial assembly: what is 'laid before' authority for acceptance, the case presented to the court. This is the crucial bridge: the word for what pleases becomes the word for what is pleaded. Old French takes up the legalized sense as plait / plaid (lawsuit, agreement, discussion) and the verb plaidier, 'to go to law, argue a case, plead'. Anglo-Norman and Middle English borrow the verb as plede / plead, attested in the thirteenth century, at first for formal statements made in court.

The widening of plead. Over centuries, English plead widens from courtroom procedure to any earnest request: one pleads for mercy, for time, for a favour, while the legal senses (plead guilty, plead a case, enter a plea) survive beside it. The parallel French development of plaisir from the same Latin base yields English please and pleasure, so the 'please' and 'plea' lines remain sisters under one roof — an elegant illustration of how a single root can branch into emotion and into law. The present participle pleading feeds the adverb pleadingly, formed with the ordinary adverbial suffix -ly, attested from the sixteenth century.

The source text. In Bao-yu's address to Grandmother Jia, pleadingly activates the emotional, interpersonal layer rather than forensic argument, yet the etymology still whispers the older structure of petition before a higher authority. Bao-yu is, in miniature, bringing a 'case' to the household's ultimate judge — using the ancient art of making a request pleasing enough to be granted. The modern student who knows the chain from *plā- 'flat' to placēre 'to please' to placitum 'decree, plea' to plaidier 'to plead' to pleadingly can see in one small adverb the whole history of how a word for smoothness became a word for supplication.
