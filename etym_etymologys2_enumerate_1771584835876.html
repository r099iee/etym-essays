ENUMERATE — single Latin-derived chain, fully attested from Latin onward. • PIE *nem- "to allot, distribute, take one's share" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; the root is attested across the family: Greek némō "to distribute, allot, pasture," némein; Sanskrit námas "homage" (a portion given); Old English niman "to take"; German nehmen "to take"; Old Irish nem "share, portion" is debated). → Proto-Italic *nem- (reconstructed; the Greek side is well attested, the Italic side is inferred from Latin numerus, which is securely connected to the "distribution/portion" semantics). → Latin numerus "a number, quantity, amount; a set, a rank; rhythm, measure" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; the semantic bridge from PIE "allot/share" to "number" is the idea of counting out portions — a number is a share counted out). → Latin ēnumerāre (ex- "out" + numerāre "to count," a denominal verb from numerus) "to count out, reckon up, enumerate, recount one by one" (attested from the 1st c. BCE, in Cicero's rhetorical and philosophical works; the compound is classical, not medieval). → Latin ēnumerātus (past participle) "counted out"; and the noun ēnumerātiō "a counting out, enumeration" — a standard term of Roman rhetoric for the figure of listing points one by one (attested in Cicero and Quintilian). → English enumerate "to count or mention one by one" (verb, borrowed in the early 16th century — OED first records c. 1520 — from Latin ēnumerātus; earlier English also used "number" as a verb in this sense). → English ENUMERATE. Note: all stages from Latin numerus onward are fully attested; the only reconstructed stage is the PIE root *nem- and its Italic continuation, where the "allot/distribute" semantics and the connection to numerus are standard but involve inference (de Vaan accepts the connection of numerus to *nem-).
