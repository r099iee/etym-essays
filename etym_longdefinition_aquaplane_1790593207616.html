Aquaplane as a verb most commonly means to glide or skim over the surface of water on a flat board while being towed, or to skim uncontrollably over a thin film of water, as a car tyre does on a wet road. In the context you know how you aquaplane, the speaker invokes the classic water-sport image: a person standing on a board, holding a tow-rope, lifted by speed into a skimming, planing motion where the board no longer cuts through water but rides on top of it. By extension, it also means to perform the sport of aquaplaning as recreation.

The noun senses help clarify the verb. An aquaplane is first a board itself, a flat wooden or composite plane towed behind a motorboat on which a rider stands or kneels. Early 20th-century aquaplanes were simple planks with a rope handle, precursors to water-skis, wakeboards, and kneeboards. Second, aquaplane, more often spelled aquaplaning in British English and hydroplaning in American English, is the dangerous motoring phenomenon where tyres lose contact with wet tarmac and ride on water, causing loss of steering. The verb therefore covers both intentional play and accidental loss of grip, united by the physics of planing: speed plus flat surface plus liquid film equals lift.

Detailed senses in modern use include:

  • Watersport to ride a towed board: to aquaplane across a lake, to go aquaplaning on holiday. This is intentional, skilful, recreational, and nostalgic, peaking in the 1920s-1950s before water-skiing displaced it. Example: They aquaplaned behind the launch all afternoon.
  • Of a vehicle to skid on water: The car aquaplaned on the flooded motorway. This is unintentional, hazardous, and technical, used in driving manuals, accident reports, and tyre advertising. British aquaplane equals American hydroplane in this sense.
  • Of a boat, seaplane float, or hull to plane: less common, but naval architects speak of a hull aquaplaning when it rises onto the step at speed, reducing drag by riding on dynamic lift rather than displacement.
  • Figurative skimming: rare and literary, to aquaplane over a subject, to skim superficially, borrowing the water image for intellectual superficiality, analogous to skimming or skating over.

The polysemy bridge is physics itself. All senses share aqua, water, plus plane, a flat surface that glides. Whether it is a human on a board, a tyre on a road, or a hull on the sea, the mechanism is identical: forward velocity generates hydrodynamic pressure under a flat or slightly inclined plane, lifting it so friction drops dramatically and control becomes delicate. Intentional aquaplaning exploits this lift for thrill; unintentional aquaplaning suffers it as loss of control. The word thus moves from toy to hazard without changing its core image, only its evaluation from diverting to dangerous. This explains why the same spelling can be nostalgic in a Wodehouse-era seaside anecdote and cautionary in a modern Highway Code booklet.

Register is technical-playful, early-modern, and slightly period. Aquaplane sounds inventively Edwardian, a coined compound from the age when aeroplane, hydroplane, and seaplane were all new marvels. It carries a whiff of varnished wood, white flannels, motorboats, and seaside holidays, as opposed to the sleeker modern wakeboard or the clinical hydroplane. As a motoring term, it is standard British English, neutral and instructional, though Americans prefer hydroplane. Grammatically it is regular: aquaplanes, aquaplaned, aquaplaning, with variant spelling aqua-planing in early sources.

Understanding aquaplane therefore requires holding together water, flatness, speed, and lift. It is not swimming, sailing, or diving, all of which go through water; it is riding on water, defeating immersion by velocity. The Wodehouse-era narrator assumes his listener knows this seaside thrill, the sudden lightness when the board stops dragging and starts flying, and uses it as a shared cultural shorthand for modern, silly, delightful speed.
