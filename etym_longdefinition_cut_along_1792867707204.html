Cut along is a brisk phrasal verb of motion meaning to hurry off, to get moving quickly along a route, with an edge of suddenness, as when Frodo orders Sam to run down to his home and then cut along the Row to meet the others at the gate.

In the source sentence the imperative cut along the Row sends Sam hurrying down the lane of hobbit-holes at full speed: the verb cut supplies the sharp sudden departure, and along supplies the line of travel, together picturing the young hobbit darting away and streaming down the road without delay. The urgency is practical, not panicked — conspirators keeping ahead of prying eyes and pricking ears.

Core meaning in detail: The combination works because cut contributes a whole family of sharp, decisive motions — severing, slicing through, taking a shortcut, cutting across — while along contributes continuous forward motion down a line. Put together, cut along means to slice oneself free of the present spot and pour that energy into rapid travel, arriving sooner because one left instantly and kept moving.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To hurry along a route (primary phrasal sense, the source meaning): cut along the Row, cut along to the gate. The bridge runs through cutting as taking the shortest line: one cuts delay away and gives everything to speed.
  • To leave abruptly, to cut and run (near variant): the cousins cut along before the constable turned the corner. The bridge is the same sudden departure with the destination left vague: going fast matters more than going where.
  • To cut — to sever or slice (root verb sense, c. 13th c.): to cut bread, to cut a cord. The bridge is the knife's edge behind the phrasal energy: every cut along carries a ghost of the blade's swiftness.
  • To shorten, reduce, or abridge (verbal extension): to cut a speech, to cut prices, to cut corners. The bridge generalises severing to removal: cutting words or costs away as a knife cuts crusts.
  • To cross or intersect sharply (spatial extension): the lane cuts across the meadow, the ship cuts through the waves. The bridge turns the blade's path into a traveller's path: motion itself becomes a cutting edge.
  • To absent oneself or dodge (social extension): to cut a lecture, to cut a pursuer, to cut someone dead. The bridge is evasion by severance: one slices the bond of attendance, pursuit, or acquaintance.
  • To make a recording or a striking impression (modern extensions): to cut a record, to cut a fine figure, to cut teeth. The bridge runs through older craft senses: the engraver cuts the disc, the tailor's cut shapes the figure, the infant's tooth cuts the gum.
  • Along as continuance and company (particle sense): sing along, bring along, all along. The bridge is the line itself: along stretches motion, music, or time into a path one travels together.

Register and connotation: Cut along is colloquial, crisp, and faintly old-fashioned, the language of scoutmasters, sergeants, and sensible elder brothers sending someone off at the double. It is urgent without being frightened and informal without being slangy, which makes it perfect for conspiracy: Frodo can hurry Sam without alarming him. The modern ear still hears it in period drama and children's adventure stories, where cutting along beats merely going for sheer dash.
