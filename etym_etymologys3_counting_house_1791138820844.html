Counting-house is a compound that tells the economic history of England in two halves: counting, Romance, learned, numerical, and house, Germanic, domestic, sheltering. Each half travelled for millennia before meeting in a Dickensian office on Christmas Eve.

Counting begins with PIE *kom-, with or together, plus *put-, to reckon, yielding Latin computare, to reckon together, sum up. Roman accountants computed taxes and legions with this verb. Late Latin kept computare for calculation while Old French split it into conter and compter, which meant both to count numbers and to tell or recount a tale — hence our doublet count and recount, and the storyteller's conte. The Normans brought conter to England, where Middle English counten meant to reckon, to account, and by extension to matter or be valued. Counting as a present participle and verbal noun — the act of reckoning — is fully productive by 1500. Its spelling wobbles between count and accompt for centuries because scholars re-Latinized it to accompt after computare, leaving us with accounting beside counting.

  • House half: PIE *ḱey-, to lie or settle, extended with Germanic *-hūsan, yields Proto-Germanic *hūsan, a dwelling where one settles. Old English hūs covers house, household, dynasty, and by extension a firm — the House of Rothschild keeps this mercantile sense. Phonologically hūs with long u shortens and lowers to modern house /haʊs/, with the noun-verb voicing split house /haʊs/ versus to house /haʊz/ preserving an Old English voicing pattern. No French replacement dislodged it; house is core Germanic bedrock.
  • Compounding: Counting-house appears in late Middle English as countyng hous, cowntynge house, denoting the room or building where accounts are kept. The hyphen records its journey from phrase to compound: counting house as two words, then counting-house, then in America often countinghouse or counting room. Parallel forms — custom-house, warehouse, counting-room — show the commercial building boom of Tudor London, when merchants separated ledgers from living quarters.
  • Victorian heyday: By 1843 a counting-house is instantly recognizable: high desks, tall stools, ledgers, inkwells, a coal-box grudgingly unlocked, clerks like Bob Cratchit shivering beside a miser like Scrooge. It is neither a modern office with departments nor a shop with customers, but a small private bureau for money: bills, debts, rents, correspondence. Dickens sets Scrooge busy in his counting-house on Christmas Eve to show capitalism refusing holiday, profit refusing charity, in the very room built for reckoning.
  • Decline and replacement: After 1900 counting-house fades as office, headquarters, accounts department take over. What was once technical becomes period flavour; to say counting-house now summons top hats and quills. American English abandons it earliest, British retains it in historic firm names and Dickens adaptations.
  • Literary attestations: Hakluyt on merchants' counting-houses; Defoe's Complete English Tradesman advising a handsome counting-house; Melville's Bartleby in a Wall Street office that is the American cousin; Dickens's A Christmas Carol as the definitive portrait. Each shows the room as moral stage: honesty or miserliness is counted there.

Together the halves embody a cultural merger: Latinate numeracy housed in Germanic domesticity. To count is to think; a house is to dwell; a counting-house is where thought about money dwells. That is why Scrooge never leaves it even in memory: his soul has moved into his ledger room. From Roman reckoning and Saxon shelter to Victorian gloom and modern nostalgia, the compound counts its own long account without loss.
