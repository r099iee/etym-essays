The biography of urchin is a three-act animal fable: a hedgehog walks into English, lends its name to a seafood and to a street child, and then walks out again, leaving its children behind.

  • Act one: the hedgehog (PIE to Middle English): Latin ēricius (also ericius), “hedgehog,” belongs to the bristle-family: the animal that bristles, from the same Indo-European idea that gives Greek chēr (hedgehog/porcupine words) and the ‘horror/horripilation’ group (hair standing on end). Vulgar Latin reshaped it to *ēriciōnem (accusative, with the -ōnem suffix seen in pollutionem-type nouns), which in northern Gaulish Romance lost its first syllable’s vowel and palatalised: Old Northern French *herichun, central Old French heriçun (modern hérisson). The Norman Conquest ferried the northern form across the Channel as Middle English urchon, irchoun (c.1300) — simply the everyday word for hedgehog. Medieval speakers heard nothing cute in it; hedgehogs were suspected witches’ cattle, milk-stealers, weather-prophets.
  • Act two: the sea-hedgehog (1500s): Tudor fishermen and naturalists, faced with the round spiny echinoderm, did what folk-namers always do and called it after the land animal it resembled: a sea urchin, i.e. a sea-hedgehog — exactly parallel to sea horse, sea cucumber, sea anemone (anemone = flower). The compound is first transparent and then lexicalised; by the 1590s “sea urchin” is the animal’s proper English name. Aristotle’s echinos (Greek for hedgehog and sea urchin) shows the same metaphor is ancient and cross-linguistic: round + spiny = hedgehog, on land or under water.
  • Act three: the prickly child (late 1500s onward): Elizabethan London applied urchin to ragged boys — small, bristly-haired, defensive, apt to ‘stick’ their elders. The metaphor ran through appearance (matted spiky hair, clothes sticking out like spines) and behaviour (cheeky, hard to grasp). Shakespeare’s Tempest (“urchins, ouphes”) preserves the halfway folklore stage where hedgehog-demons and goblin children blur. By the 18th century the hedgehog sense was dying (replaced by the native compound hedge-hog), which stranded the two metaphors as the word’s entire estate: divvy the sea-animal to the naturalists and the street-child to the novelists.
  • Sound changes: the journey heriçun → urchon → urchin shows textbook Anglo-Norman borrowing: French initial h dropped in English mouths, stressed vowel lowered and centred, ending -on/-un levelled to -in (compare mutton, cushion). Modern RP /ˈəːtʃɪn/ with plural /ˈəːtʃɪnz/; “sea” merely compounds it. No further new senses have grown since Dickens; the word is stable, split, and beloved.

So when the source passage lists “sea urchins” among lice, locusts, and lizards, it is unknowingly serving hedgehog: a land animal’s name, drowned, spined, and dished up raw.
