PIE *mezg- “to knit, plait, twist” (reconstructed root; reflected chiefly in Baltic and Slavic: Lithuanian mazgas “a knot”, Old Church Slavonic mozga “a net”; the reconstruction and membership are debated in handbooks, see note below) → Proto-Germanic *maskō “a mesh, an opening in a net; a network” (reconstructed; continued by Old Norse mǫskvi “a mesh, an eye of a net,” Old Saxon maska, Old High German masca, Middle Dutch maesche, Old English max and mæscre) → Old English max “a mesh, a net” and mæscre “(a) mesh, an eye of a net” (attested from the Anglo-Saxon period) — alongside the continental forms in Dutch and Low German that are usually credited as the direct source of the English word → Middle English mesche / mesh “a net; the opening or space between the threads of a net” (attested from c. 1400 CE; generally taken from Middle Dutch maesche or Middle Low German masche, reinforced by the native Old English max) → verb mesh “to catch in a net; to enclose in netting” (16th century CE), later “to become engaged or interlocked, of gear teeth” (late 18th century CE) → Modern English MESHED — the card's target form: the past tense and past participle of mesh (this card) NOTE on the debate: English mesh is usually said to have entered via Middle Dutch maesche or Middle Low German masche around 1400, but a native Old English max “net, mesh” also existed and survived into later English, so the exact route — native survival, continental borrowing, or both converging — is debated. The deeper link of Germanic *maskō to PIE *mezg- “to knit, plait” (Lithuanian mazgas “knot”, Old Church Slavonic mozga “net”) is accepted by some handbooks and treated as uncertain by others; the Germanic-to-English line itself is historically secure.
