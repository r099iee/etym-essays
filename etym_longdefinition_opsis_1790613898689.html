Opsis is a technical term from Aristotle’s Poetics denoting spectacle — the visual, spectacular, staged appearance of drama, including scenery, costumes, masks, stage machinery, dance, and special effects — ranked as the sixth and least artistic element of tragedy.

Aristotle lists six parts of tragedy in descending order of philosophical importance: mythos (plot), ethos (character), dianoia (thought/theme), lexis (diction/language), melos (song/music), and opsis (spectacle). For Aristotle, tragedy’s essence lies in the imitation of action through plot and reasoned speech; opsis belongs to the art of the stage-machinist (sceuopoios) rather than the poet. A play should exert its power even when read, without performance: the effect of tragedy can be seen without performance and actors. Spectacle may thrill and delight, but it is apsychotaton — most soulless, least connected to the poet’s craft — and more properly produces mere wonder than cathartic pity and fear.

In modern criticism, opsis has been revived to diagnose visual culture: film, television, and journalism that substitute showing for saying, sensation for argument, mise-en-scène for dialogue. In the given context — movies moving from dialogue to flash and filigree, to what Aristotle called opsis, setting — the author invokes Aristotle precisely to lament decline: cinema abandoning lexis and dianoia for pure spectacle, just as newspapers abandon text for pictures and schools abandon reading for viewing.

  • Strict Aristotelian sense: stage spectacle as material production — masks, robes, ekkyklema, mechane crane for gods, painted skene. Semantic reasoning: from Greek opsis sight, that which is seen; bridge is literal visibility versus verbal intelligibility.
  • Extended dramaturgical sense: mise-en-scène, blocking, lighting, setting in theatre and film studies. Bridge is analogy: all visual design choices that frame action without being action itself; similar marginal status relative to script.
  • Cultural-critical pejorative: empty visual show, eye-candy, special-effects excess. Bridge is Aristotle’s devaluation: because opsis was lowest, invoking it implies artistic poverty, commercial pandering, dumbing-down for mass appetite.
  • Philosophical-visual sense: in phenomenology and classics, opsis as appearance vs reality, the seen aspect of a thing opposed to its logos. Bridge is epistemological: Greek contrast of seeing vs knowing, retained in words like autopsy (self-seeing) and synopsis (joint seeing).

Domain notes: theatre historians use opsis neutrally to catalogue Greek staging; film theorists use it polemically against blockbuster aesthetics; literary educators use it, as here, to defend textual literacy against televisual illiteracy. Pronunciation in English follows Hellenized /ˈɒpsɪs/. Register is highly learned, appearing almost only in classical scholarship and high-brow cultural criticism, often italicized as a foreign term.
