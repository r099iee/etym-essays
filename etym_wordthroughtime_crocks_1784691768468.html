- Old English "crocc" to Middle English "crokke" - the plain, homely name of the earthenware pot, the coarse, heavy, fired-clay vessel of kitchen, dairy, and cellar for butter, pickles, and preserves; it also named the sooty hook from which such pots hung over the hearth.
- Early modern period - "crock" kept this earthy register, designating coarse utilitarian pottery rather than refined porcelain or china, while dialect and slang transferred its rough physicality to people and animals: an "old crock" was a broken-down horse or a sickly, useless person.
- Modern English - the plural "crocks" is the everyday word for unwashed kitchenware and greasy piles of pots awaiting the wash-up, while American slang turned the singular into "a crock" — a false or nonsensical tale — keeping the same idea of something hollow, heavy, and unrefined.
