The history of gallon is the history of commerce in a single word: a liquid container slowly hardened into a legal unit of measure. Unlike the great PIE-rooted nouns of English, the gallon has no secure Indo-European ancestry. The first traceable form is Medieval Latin galo (accusative galonem), with the variants galeta and galletum, recorded in the administrative documents of Carolingian and later medieval Europe as the name of a jar, jug, or bowl for wine and other liquids. Where the word itself came from is debated: the standard hypothesis points to a Gaulish or Celtic substrate noun *galla, "a vessel, a wooden container," of the everyday kind the Celts bequeathed to Gallo-Roman speech without leaving a trace in Latin literature — the sort of word that survives precisely because it was too ordinary to be written down until the medieval notaries needed it. OED frankly records the origin as uncertain, and no attested earlier stage exists. The gallon therefore enters history as a thing, not a number: a pot for pouring wine, roughly the size a strong man could lift.

The decisive change came when trade began to demand that the container's name stand for a fixed quantity. A merchant selling wine could not sell "a jar"; he had to sell a volume that the buyer could verify, and the natural solution was to standardise the very containers — or the laws about them — so that the word "gallon" named one definite amount. In medieval England, where wine, ale, and grain each travelled with their own vessels, the result was a tangle of overlapping gallons: the wine gallon, the ale gallon, the corn gallon, and the dry gallon, differing by commodity and by region. Royal and parliamentary statute took hold of the problem in a famous series of measures — the 1266 Assize of Bread and Ale and the 1303 Tractatus de Ponderibus et Mensuris ("A Treatise on Weights and Measures") — which sought to fix the gallon as an official unit, standardly defined as the volume of eight pounds of wheat or of wine, and subdivided into four quarts, eight pints, and (from Medieval Latin gillo, "a water pot") the small gill of a quarter-pint. These statutes are the moment the gallon stops being a pot and becomes a law.

The old Northern French form galon crossed the Channel with the wine trade after 1066, entering Middle English as galoun in the late thirteenth century, and the word was never to leave the English-speaking world. Over the following centuries the gallon bifurcated into the two standards that still govern it. Britain redefined its gallon in 1824 as the Imperial gallon — the volume of ten pounds of distilled water at 62°F, equal to 4.546 liters — a rational, water-anchored standard. The United States, however, preserved the older 18th-century English "Queen Anne" wine gallon of 231 cubic inches, which works out to 3.785 liters. The two gallons differ by about twenty percent, a standing trap for engineers, traders, and cooks that is literally written into the word: "Imperial gallon" versus "US gallon" are the fossils of an 1824 divergence in the English-speaking world.

Through all this the word kept its human, everyday life. In the kitchen and the tavern a gallon remained the bulk measure for milk, beer, cider, and later petrol and paint; excise duties made it a unit of taxation, and "gallonage" became the technical term for capacity or tax in gallons. And in popular speech the exact measure dissolved into hyperbole: "gallons of" anything means a great deal of it. John Steinbeck, writing of Depression-era migrant workers, exploits exactly this double life in Of Mice and Men, where George imagines freedom as the right to "get a gallon of whisky" — an impossible, lavish excess for a man who cannot hold a job, the precise unit stretched into pure fantasy of abundance. The gallon thus carries both halves of its biography at once: the strictly standardised, legally defined measure of the market, and the loose, generous "gallon of" of everyday speech. A word that began as an unnamed Gaulish pot for wine became a number stamped in statute — and then, in the mouths of dreamers, a measure of the impossible. Gallon is a container, a law, and a dream all at once.
