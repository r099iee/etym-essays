Pip in the sentence The pathos of the thing gave me the pip uses a very specific, now dated British slang sense: to give someone the pip means to annoy, irritate, depress, or disgust them, to put them out of sorts or make them fed up. I got the pip means I felt miffed, gloomy, or peevish. This is the Wodehouse-Bertie Wooster pip, the comic gentleman's way of saying something got on his nerves or dampened his spirits without sounding vulgar or melodramatic.

To understand it you must separate four homonymous pips that converge in spelling but diverge in origin. The relevant one is pip as poultry disease: a white scale or mucus on a bird's tongue or throat, making it pip, causing listlessness. From sick chickens comes human metaphor: to feel like a pippy chicken is to feel seedy, out of sorts. By the 19th century to have the pip or give the pip meant to feel or cause mild illness, low spirits, or irritation. It is hypochondria made comic — I am as droopy as a sick hen.

Other pips illuminate by contrast. Pip as seed (apple pip, orange pip, short for pippin) is botanical and still current. Pip as spot (pips on dice, dominoes, playing cards; pips on a soldier's shoulder for rank; the BBC time-signal pips) is visual: a small dot or star. Pip as outcry (to pip, squeak) and pip as dismissal (to get the pip/push, the sack, or the pipsqueak insult) overlap in sound symbolism. None of these is the Wodehouse sense, though comic writers enjoy the confusion.

  • Annoyance sense (primary here): it gave me the pip = it irritated me. Bridge: sick-bird droop to human peevishness. He gives me the pip is stronger than he bores me, weaker than I hate him.
  • Depression-low spirits sense: I have the pip = I feel down, seedy, out of sorts. More internal and gloomy than angry. Bridge: physical ailment to mental malaise.
  • Disgust-contempt Edwardian extension: to get the pip with someone can mean to become disgusted or lose patience. It gave me the pip can imply the pathetic thing was almost too much to bear.
  • Botanical pip (different lexeme): seed of apple, citrus. To spit pips. No annoyance implied; included to prevent confusion.
  • Spot-mark pip (different lexeme): dot on dice, star on uniform, radio beep. To count the pips. Technical, neutral.

Register is period upper-class British slang, 1890s-1940s, jocular, deliberately trivializing. A character who says it gave me the pip instead of it devastated me signals stiff-upper-lip understatement: genuine pathos is admitted then instantly deflated into a chicken disease. It is never clinical depression nor violent anger, but the peevish middle — fed-up, miffed, huffed. Modern readers meet it almost exclusively in Wodehouse, Saki, and Punch cartoons; current speech prefers the hump, the blues, annoyed.

Grammar: almost always with definite article the pip, verbs give/get/have. To pip someone is rare; to pip out (die, break) belongs to other senses. Understanding the article matters: the pip is a known comic ailment, like the blues or the sulks.
