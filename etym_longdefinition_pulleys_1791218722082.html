Pulleys is the plural of pulley: small grooved wheels over which a rope, belt or cable passes in order to lift, redirect or transmit force. A single fixed pulley changes direction — well bucket up, window sash down; a movable or compound set multiplies force — block and tackle hoisting an engine, yacht halyards, crane hooks, elevator cables. In the source passage pulleys stand alongside ropes and levers as humanity's first mechanical aids, before sledge, roller, wheel and cart.

In physics a pulley is one of the six classical simple machines — lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, screw — and the only one whose textbook plural students meet as often as the singular. Everyday pulleys are humble and ubiquitous: curtain cords, flagpoles, washing lines, gym cables, car timing belts.

  • Core lifting sense: a wheel-and-rope device for raising weights: well pulley, crane pulley, dock pulley, gym pulley. Example: the pulleys squeaked as the piano was hauled to the third floor. This is the source sense: ropes, levers, and pulleys.
  • Rigging and sailing sense: a block containing one or more sheaves: halyard pulleys, sheet blocks, topping-lift pulleys. Sailors rarely say pulley, preferring block, but the mechanism is identical.
  • Power-transmission sense: a belt wheel: drive pulley, idler pulley, timing pulley, alternator pulley. Here no rope is pulled by hand; a rubber belt links engine to ancillaries. Car manuals speak of pulleys constantly.
  • Anatomical and biological metaphor: the trochlea of the eye or finger tendons is described as a pulley — the superior oblique muscle passes through a cartilaginous pulley. Anatomy borrows the engineer's image for a redirecting loop.
  • Figurative-system sense: the pulleys of power, the hidden pulleys behind decisions — the concealed mechanisms that move people or events as ropes move loads. Journalists use this for backstage influence.
  • Clothing and furniture survival: sash-window pulleys, clothesline pulleys — small brass or nylon wheels now invisible inside frames, still called pulleys by builders.

Grammar and collocations: pulleys pairs with ropes, levers, blocks, tackle, belts, cables and verbs rig, reeves, thread, grease, jam, seize. Adjectives distinguish fixed, movable, compound, grooved, toothed. The singular pulley is /ˈpʊli/, plural /ˈpʊliz/; spelling follows consonant + y → ies.

Polysemy bridge: wheel-with-groove → rope-lifter → belt-driver → anatomical loop → hidden mechanism. Each step keeps the schema of a round redirect that lets a small effort move a larger load or change its path. The car fan-belt pulley no longer lifts, and the political pulley no longer turns, but both inherit the simple machine's promise: redirect force, gain advantage. That is why one plural covers well-heads, engine bays and metaphors alike.

Maintenance matters: sailors grease sheaves, climbers retire worn pulleys, and engineers specify sealed bearings, because a seized pulley multiplies friction instead of force.
