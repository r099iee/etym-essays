PIE *bʰruHg- / *bʰrewg- "to enjoy, to use, to make use of, to have the benefit of" (reconstructed root; c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE) → Latin fungor, fungī, functus "to perform, to discharge, to busy oneself with (a duty, office, or task)" (deponent verb; attested from the 3rd c. BCE) → Latin perfungī "to perform fully, to get through with, to discharge completely" (prefix per- "through, thoroughly" + fungor; attested in Classical Latin, e.g. in Cicero and Pliny) → Latin perfūnctōrius "done merely to get through, superficial, careless in performance" (derived from the past participle perfūnctus + the adjectival suffix -ōrius; attested in Classical Latin) → English perfunctory "carried out with a minimum of effort or reflection" (borrowed from Latin in the late 16th century).

Note on the debate/nuance: The PIE root behind Latin fungor is usually reconstructed as *bʰruHg-, connecting with notions of enjoyment and use, and it is generally accepted as underlying fungor "to perform/use," the frux/frūctus "fruit" family, and English fruit and frugal. The reconstruction and the exact laryngeal shape are matters of scholarly hypothesis, and some authorities treat the "enjoy" connection as a semantic bridge rather than a formally proven identity; the Latin line from fungor onward, however, is securely attested at every stage. The semantic twist — from "perform thoroughly" (perfungī) to "done just to get through" (perfūnctōrius) — happened already within Latin and is what English inherited in the late 16th century.
