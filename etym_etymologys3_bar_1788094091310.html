A Romance word of unknown birth. Every "bar" of rod, barrier, court, tavern and music descends from one word: Vulgar Latin *barra, "bar, beam, barrier", first visible at the dawn of the medieval period. Its origin is genuinely unknown — the great dictionaries mark it "of unknown origin". The strongest hypothesis is a Gaulish (Celtic) substrate word for a point, tip or projecting piece (cf. Welsh bar, "top, branch", and Middle Irish barr, "top, point"); on this view the word entered Latin on the Gallic frontier and spread with the Romance languages of the west — which is exactly what happened in the record: Italian barra, Spanish barra, Portuguese barra and French barre all descend from *barra, a pan-Western-Romance distribution that favours a western (non-Roman) origin. But no text records the word before the early Middle Ages, and the Gaulish theory, though widely repeated, remains a hypothesis. Honest etymology stops at *barra and says so.

Old French and Anglo-Norman: the bar enters English. Old French barre (attested from the tenth–eleventh centuries) meant a beam, a gate-bar, a rail — the movable rod that locks a door or a city gate. The Norman Conquest carried the word into England, and Middle English barre is common from the thirteenth century: the barring of doors and gates is everywhere in chronicles, romances and town records. The reading sentence of this card — the dwarves searching the Lonely Mountain's hidden door for "any sign of bar or bolt or key-hole" — is a perfect medieval scene: the bar is the first and simplest lock, the rod laid across the door from within.

  • The legal bar (13th–16th centuries). Anglo-Norman law French used barre for the railing before the bench — the barrier that separated judges and advocates from the public and the accused — and "at the bar" fixed the image: the prisoner stands at the bar, counsel pleads within it. "Called to the bar" (sixteenth century) named admission to the profession, and barrister (sixteenth century, from bar plus the agent suffix) named the advocate himself. "Bench and bar" — judges and advocates — has been the formula of English and American law ever since; the bar exam, the bar association and the American Bar follow the same thread.
  • The tavern bar (16th–19th centuries). The counter over which drink was served in inns was itself a bar — a rail of separation exactly like the court's — and "behind the bar" still marks the boundary between server and served. By the nineteenth century "the bar" had come to name the whole room and establishment: the pub, the hotel lounge, the cocktail bar. The twentieth-century bar boom — wine bars, oyster bars, espresso bars — made the word universal on every street.
  • Sandbars and toll bars (16th century onward). The barrier-image met navigation and roads: a sandbar ridges a river mouth or harbour entrance — sediment because the water's flow slows at the meeting of river and sea — and a toll bar closed a road until the toll was paid. Both are still live senses: "crossing the bar" passed from the river mouth into the poetry of Tennyson, whose elegy Crossing the Bar (1889) made the sandbar an image of death's threshold.
  • The musical bar (17th–18th centuries). The vertical line that divides written time is a bar — the divider again, the rod laid across the staff — and by the eighteenth century "a bar" meant the measure itself: the segment of time between two bar-lines. Composers' scores and orchestral parts made the word universal in music: "four bars of rest", "a twelve-bar blues".
  • Ingots and confectionery (18th–20th centuries). Bars of gold and iron are the rod-shape measured by weight — the ingot — and the "chocolate bar" and "candy bar" (late nineteenth–twentieth centuries) made the shape a daily consumer object: the bar of soap, the bar of chocolate, the bar of gold, all the same rib of solid material.
  • The modern interface (20th–21st centuries). Bar charts (early twentieth century), bar codes (patented 1952, everyday from the 1970s), the space bar of the typewriter and keyboard, the menu bar, the address bar, the progress bar: every new technology rediscovered the rod that divides and the line that separates, and the word obligingly opened to each. A "bar" is now also a want, a stripe of light (a bar of sunlight), and a stretch of beach (a bar of sand) — the shape keeps producing.

The second word: Greek báros. Entirely separate is the unit of pressure. Greek βάρος báros, "weight", descends from PIE *gʷer- "heavy" — the family of Sanskrit gurú- "heavy", Latin gravis and Greek βαρύς barús "heavy". The scientific bar was coined in the early twentieth century — commonly attributed to the Norwegian meteorologist Vilhelm Bjerknes in 1911 — as a unit of pressure of one million dynes per square centimetre, almost exactly one atmosphere; the millibar (0.001 bar) became the standard unit of weather maps within a decade and held that place throughout the twentieth century. The word thus carries two genealogies that never met: the unknown Gaulish rod and the Greek weight. Barometers and barographs share the Greek lineage — "barometer" was coined in the seventeenth century from báros "weight" + métron "measure" — while the bar of the door, the court, the pub and the score shares the rod's. Two bars, one spelling, two nations of ancestors.

Register today. Context disambiguates everything: "at the bar" (court or drinking house), "bar none" (the excluding verb fossilised), "a bar of chocolate" (the ingot), "the bar lifted" (the weather map) — and the double parentage occasionally surfaces in word games and dictionaries, where the rod and the unit sit as separate entries. It is a compact history of Europe in four letters: a Gaulish beam that became the threshold of justice and pleasure, and a Greek weight that became the pressure of the air itself.
