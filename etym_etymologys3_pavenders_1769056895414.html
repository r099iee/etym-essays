A word with a birth certificate. Unlike every other word in this deck, pavender has a known author, a known date, and a known birthplace. It was coined by C. S. Lewis in 1952 for The Voyage of the Dawn Treader, where it names a fictional fish of the Eastern Sea — a "beautiful rainbow-colored fish" that the company catches, remembers eating at Cair Paravel in the golden age, and happily dines on. This is a literary neologism, a word born in fiction, and its entire "etymology" therefore consists of two things: the act of invention, and the way the word's sound-shape is engineered to pass as an old, established species name. It has no ancestor, no cognates, and no Indo-European inheritance — to force a root upon it would be pure fabrication, and the honest lexicon records it, if at all, as an isolated coinage belonging to a single book.

How the word works. Lewis was a master of the plausible coinage: names like Reepicheep, Tumnus, the Dufflepuds, and the Marsh-wiggles all sound as though they could belong to an old folklore tradition, even though they were born on his pages. Pavender follows the same strategy with a species name. Its two syllables and -er ending sit comfortably among real English fish names — gudgeon, pilchard, prawn, bream — so that the reader's ear accepts it instantly as ordinary Narnian vocabulary. And the rainbow colouring of the fish invites one further, speculative echo: Latin pāvo, "peacock," gives English pavonine, "iridescent like a peacock's train." It is quite possible that the inventor's ear, seeking a name for a rainbow-hued fish, was unconsciously moved by that word; but this is inspiration, not inheritance, and no dictionary could ever list pāvo as the root of pavender.

The word's function in Narnia and beyond. In the novel the coinage does real narrative work. The "excellent catch of pavenders" ties the present adventure to the remembered splendour of Cair Paravel — "which they all remembered eating in the old days" — so that the fish is as much a memory as a meal, a small taste of the golden age recovered. The plural noun rolls off the tongue with the ordinary confidence of a dinner menu, and precisely because the name is invented, it does not distract: no real fish, with its real history and associations, could carry the same blend of beauty, nostalgia, and appetite. The word's "evolution" after 1952 is therefore not a development but an afterlife: it lives only in the Narnia corpus, recognised by readers of the book and used by fans as a shared reference. Any modern use of pavender outside Narnia is a conscious allusion — a wink at the Dawn Treader's feast.

What this teaches about words. The study of a neologism inverts the normal order of lexicography. Most words begin as anonymous speech and acquire history; pavender began as a single deliberate act of naming and acquired only the history of its one book. It is a reminder that a word's power need not come from antiquity: plausibility, sound-shape, and the context in which a name is first heard can make an invented word feel as old and as solid as mackerel or trout. The charm of pavender is precisely that a made-up name can sound old, ordinary, and delicious.

  • "Derivation" summary: no PIE root, no Latin stage, no Middle English stage — the entire "chain" is the coinage (C. S. Lewis, 1952) and the plural pavenders used in the passage.
  • Sound-shape notes: two syllables (PAV-en-der), initial stress, -er final; the cluster and rhythm mimic real English species names so the reader's ear accepts the word as established vocabulary.
  • Speculative echo: pavonine (Latin pāvō, "peacock") is the most plausible source of the rainbow colouring in the name, but it is inspiration, not inheritance — stated as such.
