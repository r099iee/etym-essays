The linguistic history of bill is a remarkable tale of dual ancestry: a native Germanic stream representing sharp instruments and avian anatomy, and a Romance stream representing sealed legal parchments, which converged in Middle English to produce one of the language’s most versatile polysemes.

1. The Germanic Lineage: Cleaving Weapons & Avian Beaks (c. 500 BCE – 11th Century):

  • The anatomical and martial sense derives from Proto-Indo-European *bʰei-d- ("to split, cleave, strike"), which yielded Proto-Germanic *bilją ("cutting iron, sword, cleaver"). This root generated Old High German bil (pickaxe), Old Saxon bil (sword), and Old English bill (sword, broad two-edged blade, hooked weapon).
  • Simultaneously in Old English, the variant form bile (attested as early as the 8th-century Épinal Glossary) was applied to the beak or snout of a bird. The conceptual metaphor was vivid and concrete: the hard, pointed beak of an eagle, goose, or raven was conceptualized as a biological blade or chopping tool designed to split, pierce, and tear food.
  • Throughout the Anglo-Saxon period and medieval warfare, the weapon sense thrived in the form of the "bill" or "billhook," a lethal agricultural tool adapted for infantry combat to unseat armored horsemen.

2. The Romance Lineage: Seals, Charters & Invoices (c. 11th – 14th Century):

  • Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, a completely separate word entered English from Anglo-Norman French: bille, which derived from Medieval Latin bulla ("bubble, stud, amulet," and specifically the lead seal attached to official papal and royal documents).
  • By metonymy, the name of the seal (*bulla* / *bille*) was transferred to the document bearing the seal. In the 13th and 14th centuries, a *bille* in Anglo-French and Middle English denoted a formal written petition to the King or Chancellor, a sealed letter, or an itemized commercial statement of debts.
  • Because Anglo-Norman *bille* and Middle English *bile/bill* possessed identical pronunciation, English speakers seamlessly blended them into a single phonetic envelope while maintaining distinct conceptual branches.

3. Constitutional, Theatrical & Commercial Specialization (15th – 18th Century):

  • In the 15th century, the legal petition evolved into the legislative bill—the draft text submitted to Parliament before gaining the status of an Act. This culminated in foundational constitutional milestones such as the Bill of Rights (1689).
  • In Early Modern London, theater managers pinned printed notices outside playhouses, giving birth to the playbill and the idiom of theatrical billing. Commercial expansion in the City of London solidified financial instruments: merchants settled global transactions through bills of exchange and cargo manifests known as bills of lading.

4. Modern Industrial & Transatlantic Developments (19th – 21st Century):

  • During the 19th century in the United States, paper bank notes issued by federal and state institutions were universally dubbed "dollar bills" (contracted from "treasury bill" and "bank bill").
  • In Victorian literature, both branches operated side-by-side with crystalline clarity. In Arthur Conan Doyle’s "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle" (1892), when Ryder describes prying open the goose's bill to force the stolen jewel down its crop, the text draws upon the ancient thousand-year-old Germanic anatomical meaning, immediately recognized by readers despite the word's ubiquitous commercial applications.
