In the preface to his study of English metre, the author explains that he has 'been keeping off some of the most picturesque byways of English versification' — the side-paths of verse technique, such as syllabic verse, classical imitations, concrete poetry, and a host of individual metrical experiments. Calling these byways picturesque gives them a tone of charming, colourful oddity: they are the attractive, eye-catching fringe of the subject, delightful to contemplate but not part of the central network from which the study departs. The nuance is aesthetic rather than functional: the writer concedes their appeal — their almost painterly curiosity — while politely declining to explore them. The word is doing what it does best in its aesthetic sense: describing something that pleases the eye (or the mind's eye) as if it had been composed for a picture.

Polysemy: the senses of PICTURESQUE
  • Sense 1 — of scenery and places (primary): Visually charming and striking in a way that suggests a picture or painting — 'a picturesque village,' 'a picturesque coastline.' A scene so composed that it looks as if an artist had arranged it.
  • Sense 2 — of language and description: Vividly graphic, detailed enough to call up a picture in the mind: 'His account of the fair was so picturesque that the reader could almost see the booths.' The bridge: the picture shifts from the eye to the imagination.
  • Sense 3 — the Picturesque as a formal aesthetic category (18th c.): In landscape theory, 'the Picturesque' is a distinct category set between the smooth Beautiful and the terrifying Sublime — scenery that pleases precisely through its roughness, irregularity, and painterly variety. The bridge: from a casual compliment to a technical term of art-criticism.
  • Sense 4 — quaint, charming, touristic: In modern everyday use, picturesque commonly means pleasantly quaint and photogenic — the pretty postcard village, the crooked lane — charming rather than overwhelming. The bridge: the guidebook adoption of the aesthetic term.
  • Sense 5 — of persons and things, mildly approving: Applied loosely to anything visually pleasing in an old-fashioned or decorative way — a picturesque figure, a picturesque ruin — often with a faint note of condescension, as of a curiosity worth looking at.

The semantic boundary: Picturesque belongs to a family of aesthetic praise-words and must be kept apart from them. Beautiful aims at ideal, harmonious loveliness; picturesque is more informal, rougher, and less perfect. Scenic stresses the natural landscape itself and is neutral, while picturesque implies the eye of a painter. Quaint adds an old-fashioned, faintly curious charm, often of objects rather than views. Pretty is small-scale and dainty, lacking the painterly grandeur; charming is personal and winning rather than visual. Graphic and vivid apply to language with force and clarity but without the aesthetic 'picture' connotation; pictorial and painterly belong to the art world. Idyllic and bucolic add rural peacefulness, which picturesque does not require; photogenic is the modern, camera-age equivalent for subjects that flatter the lens. Only picturesque combines 'visually pleasing' with 'as if composed for a painting' — which is why it has survived as the guidebook's favourite compliment.
