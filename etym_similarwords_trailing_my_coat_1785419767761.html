• Picking a fight: Similar because both denote deliberately seeking a confrontation; differs because picking a fight is direct, personal and often physical aggression toward a specific opponent, while trailing one's coat is a broader, more public and symbolic dare to any passer-by.
  • Playing devil's advocate: Similar because both involve taking a provocative stance; differs because devil's advocate argues an unpopular position for intellectual or pedagogical reasons without genuine hostility, whereas trailing one's coat aims to bait and irritate opponents into a quarrel.
  • Provoking: Similar because both involve goading someone into a reaction; differs because provoking can target a particular individual with taunts or irritation, while trailing one's coat is an indiscriminate, public gesture of challenge.
  • Riling: Similar because both describe deliberately agitating someone; differs because riling is more colloquial and focuses on annoying a specific person, whereas trailing one's coat is theatrical, performative provocation aimed at a crowd or audience.
  • Courting controversy: Similar because both describe deliberately seeking public dispute; differs because courting controversy emphasises attracting attention and debate, while trailing one's coat has a more belligerent, duelling, combative edge.
  • Baiting: Similar because both involve luring an opponent into a reaction; differs because baiting suggests a patient, predatory tease of a specific victim, whereas trailing one's coat is an open, self-advertising dare.
  • Throwing down the gauntlet: Similar because both are traditional symbolic challenges to a fight; differs because the gauntlet is a formal, honourable challenge to a specific adversary, while the trailing coat is a more insolent, indiscriminate provocation to any comer.
  • Stirring the pot: Similar because both describe deliberately creating trouble; differs because stirring the pot is casual, mischievous troublemaking, often behind the scenes, whereas trailing one's coat is public, confrontational and theatrical.
  • Spoiling for a fight: Similar because both express a state of eager readiness for confrontation; differs because spoiling for a fight describes an internal mood of aggressive eagerness, while trailing one's coat is an outward, performative gesture of provocation.
