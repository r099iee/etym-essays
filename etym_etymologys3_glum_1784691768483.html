The biography of glum begins not with a single attested ancestor but with a cluster of related Germanic words built on the stem *glum-, which carried the double sense of physical murkiness and of a face clouding over like a darkening sky. The earliest English representative is the Middle English verb gloumen, recorded about 1300 in the plain sense "to become dark", and shifted by the late fourteenth century — in the form gloumben — to the human sense "to look gloomy or sullen". The semantic leap is revealing: the face is treated as a small patch of weather, and a dejected expression is a lowering sky made flesh.

  • The North Sea cluster. Around the same period, closely similar words appear across the North Sea: Low German glum "gloomy, troubled, turbid", Dutch gluren "to leer", Norwegian dialectal glome "to stare somberly". It is impossible to say with certainty whether Middle English borrowed from the Continent or developed the same formation independently; the OED and Etymonline treat the verb's ultimate origin as unknown, perhaps reaching back to an unrecorded Old English verb or to a Scandinavian source. What is certain is the direction of the semantic movement: from darkness of weather, to darkness of countenance, to darkness of mood.
  • The sixteenth-century adjective. In the sixteenth century the adjective emerges — glum "sullen, moody, frowning", first recorded about 1540 — alongside a now-obsolete noun glum "a sullen look" (1520s). The noun and adjective grew side by side, the adjective describing the face and the demeanour, the noun naming the look itself. Derived forms followed: glumly, glumness, and the colloquial glump, which produced the glumps "a fit of sulkiness" in the eighteenth century, and the Scottish glunch (1719).
  • The parallel of gloom. The related noun gloom appears in 1590, originally Scottish and meaning "a sullen look", and only later takes on physical darkness — in Milton's poetry in 1629 — and then, from 1744, melancholy, with the adjective gloomy already in use from 1580. Gloom and glum thus share a stem and a semantic history, though they are distinct words; the modern English gloomy "dark, dismal, dejected" is glum's close cousin, not its twin.
  • Socio-cultural colour. Glum belongs to the plain, demotic middle of English speech, with a faintly northern, dialectal flavour inherited from its Scottish and Low German connections. It never became courtly or learned; it stayed a word of the hearth, the tavern, and the street, applied to a face, a mood, or a company of travellers. Its register is part of its meaning: to call someone glum is to describe the quiet, self-contained, rather stubborn form of unhappiness — the mood of one who has nothing to say and says it with a darkened brow.
  • Literary attestations. In the passage from "Roast Mutton", Tolkien writes: "There they all sat glum and wet and muttering, while Oin and Gloin went on trying to light the fire, and quarrelling about it." The three adjectives are carefully ordered: glum names the inner state — the sullen, silent dejection of thirteen drenched dwarves — while wet gives its physical cause and muttering its only audible outlet. Glum is placed first precisely because it is the atmospheric key to the whole sentence: the company has been soaked by the storm, the fire will not catch, and the travellers have subsided into a passive, brooding misery in which even quarrels are reduced to muttering. The word thus anchors the reader's sense of the scene: not angry, not despairing, but quietly, heavily, soddenly out of sorts.

Glum in its modern form is a word of pure atmosphere — a single heavy syllable (like lump, thud, hump, dump) whose very sound suggests weight and flatness, a closed vowel and a closing consonant that leave no room for brightness. Its history is short, its origin obscure, but its meaning is as stable as the English weather from which it was first borrowed: a face gone overcast.
