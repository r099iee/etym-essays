• edict: Similar because it is a formal proclamation by a sovereign or authority with binding force; differs because an edict is essentially a public announcement of a ruler's will, whereas a decree can also be a court's ruling in a particular case.
  • statute: Similar because both are law with binding force; differs because a statute is enacted by a legislature through a deliberative process, while a decree is issued by an executive, a court, or a religious authority acting alone.
  • ordinance: Similar because it denotes an authoritative regulation; differs because an ordinance is typically local or municipal, or a detailed by-law, and carries a narrower, more administrative flavour than a decree.
  • ukase: Similar because it names an imperial decree with the force of law; differs because it is specifically Russian in reference and, in English, usually ironic or pejorative for an arbitrary command.
  • fiat: Similar because it stresses the sheer will behind an order; differs because a fiat is an authorization or sanction rather than a body of enacted rules, and the word is more often figurative (“by fiat of the chairman”).
  • mandate: Similar because it imposes an obligation from above; differs because a mandate usually authorizes or requires action and often derives from a vote or a higher authority's commission, not from a single ruling act.
  • rescript: Similar because it is an authoritative written answer or ruling; differs because a rescript is a formal reply to a specific question, especially in Roman and canon law, rather than a general legislative command.
  • ruling: Similar because it is a decision with legal effect; differs because a ruling is the determination of a court or tribunal in a case and is normally narrower, less political, and less solemn than a decree.
  • proclamation: Similar because it is a public, formal announcement by authority; differs because a proclamation declares or announces something, while a decree actually legislates or commands.
  • diktat: Similar because it suggests an imposed order; differs because diktat is always hostile in tone, implying terms forced on a defeated or weaker party, and it is not a technical legal term.
