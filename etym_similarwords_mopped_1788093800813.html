• Wiped: Similar because it is the generic verb for cleaning a surface or removing moisture with cloth; differs because it names no implement and covers dusting, drying and smears alike.
  • Swabbed: Similar because it means cleaning with an absorbent mass of cloth; differs because it belongs to nautical and medical registers and implies formal, thorough treatment.
  • Sponged: Similar because it means washing or absorbing with a soft absorbent; differs because it specifies the implement and also means 'sponged off' (parasitic living), a sense mop lacks.
  • Dried: Similar because mopping often dries a surface; differs because it is result-focused and may use air, heat or towels.
  • Toweled: Similar because it means wiping or drying with a towel; differs because it is specific to cloth for the body and implies vigorous rubbing.
  • Sopped (up): Similar because it means absorbing liquid, as mopping up does; differs because it is older and dialectal-flavoured, stressing the saturation of the cloth.
  • Mopped up: Similar because it shares the same verb; differs because the phrasal form adds the idea of completion — clearing the last remnants of a liquid, a task, an army or a market.
  • Dabbed: Similar because patting with cloth removes moisture; differs because it is light and repeated, without the dragging sweep of a mop.
  • Cleaned: Similar because mopping is a way of cleaning; differs because it is the broadest term, covering every method, and names no cloth or implement.
  • Scrubbed: Similar because it is vigorous surface cleaning; differs because it implies a brush and abrasive force rather than an absorbing wipe.
  • Rubbed: Similar because mopping involves rubbing a cloth; differs because rubbing can polish, massage or chafe without cleaning anything.
  • Absorbed / Blotted: Similar because they remove liquid from a surface, as a mop does; differs because they are technical and delicate (ink blotted, fluids absorbed) rather than house-scale.
  • Grimaced (obsolete sense): Similar because the old 'mopped' meant to pull a face; differs because the grimace-mop is a different word, unrelated to the cloth verb, and long since obsolete.
