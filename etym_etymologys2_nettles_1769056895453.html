ROOT: PIE *ned- "to twist, to bind, to tie together" (reconstructed; no date; a parallel variant *nedh- carries an extra consonant) → Proto-Germanic *natilōn "nettle; the little binder" (c. 500 BCE–c. 600 CE; built with the old instrumental suffix *-tlō, "that which is used for binding," because nettle-stems yield a flax-like bast fibre for cordage) → Old English netele (also netil) "nettle" (c. 7th–11th c.; /ˈnæ.te.le/) → Middle English netel(e) "nettle" (12th–15th c.; /ˈnɛ.təl/) → Early Modern English nettle "nettle, the stinging plant" (16th c. onwards) → FINAL: English nettles "stinging nettle plants" (plural; the exact form used in the Narnia passage). DEBATED / NUANCED NOTES: (1) The derivation of the Germanic plant-name from *ned- "to bind," via the plant's use in making cordage, is the traditional and OED-supported route; a minority suggestion links the plant-name to the root of "needle" (*nē- "to sew"), but this is rejected because the Germanic form points to *ned-, not *nē-. (2) The same root *ned- with a zero-grade and *-j- produced Proto-Germanic *natją "net," giving English net — a close sibling, not an ancestor. (3) English net "left after deductions" (net weight) is an unrelated word from Italian netto < Latin nitidus "clean, bright." (4) Like most plant-names the stem is extremely old, and the exact pre-Germanic derivational steps are not all directly attested; the chain above is the best-supported reconstruction.
