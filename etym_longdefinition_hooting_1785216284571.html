"Hooting" is the present participle of the verb "hoot", and it means making a loud, hollow, cry-like noise — the word covers two related sounds: the deep, resonant, wavering call of an owl, and a raucous human shout of mockery, derision, or rough merriment. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), the word describes the goblins' noise as they pour down from the trees hunting the hobbit: they are "hooting and hallooing" — braying and bawling in crude, jeering voices, the noise of a mob enjoying its own cruelty. The tone is deliberately subhuman and unnerving: "hooting" suggests not speech but a repeated, animal cry, closer to the owl's mocking tu-whit than to articulate language, and this makes the hunters sound like beasts of the dark — a noise that chills as much as it threatens. Core semantics. The verb has two interwoven strands, both alive in the participle:
  • the animal strand: the owl's cry ("owls were hooting in the dark woods"), from which the compound "hoot owl" (a true owl, as opposed to the screech owl) derives (1826);
  • the human strand: shouting — first neutrally ("to call out"), then with derision ("to hoot at a performer", "to hoot a politician from the hustings"), then with laughter ("hooting in laughter");
  • and the mechanical strand: a horn, siren, or whistle "hoots" — the sound of ships and factories.
The present participle "hooting" keeps all these strands fused: in Tolkien's sentence it is at once owl-like, jeering, and animal — sound reduced to a repeated hollow call, with no words in it, only pursuit. Near-synonyms, carefully separated:
  • hallooing — its exact partner in the sentence: a hunting or hailing shout, from Old French halloer, equally archaic and loud.
  • whooping — a high, triumphant, whooshing cry, a separate imitative word.
  • shouting — a neutral loud cry, without the mocking or animal quality.
  • jeering — the specific note of mockery, without the hollow call-sound.
  • booing — a long, low disapproval-cry, the crowd's contrary to hooting.
  • hollering — loud, informal, chiefly American calling out.
  • shrieking / screeching — high, piercing cries, sharper than the hollow hoot.
  • ululating — a long, wavering, ritual cry, more exotic and formal.
  • calling — the plain verb of summoning or of birdcalls.
Polysemy & register. Beyond the participle, the word family has branched: (1) the noun "hoot", the sound itself ("the hoot of an owl", "the hoot of a ship's horn"); (2) the figurative noun "a hoot" = something hilarious (from 1926: "the play was a hoot"); (3) the idioms "not worth a hoot" and "I don't give a hoot" = worth or caring nothing (from the early 19th century), where the cry shrinks to the smallest unit of value; and (4) dialectal intensives such as "hooting mad". The historical bridge is the sound: "hoot" entered English (c. 1200) as a plain imitation of a hollow shout, was first applied to bird cries in the mid-15th century, became the crowd's weapon of derision by c. 1600, and by the 20th century had dwindled, in idioms of indifference, into a shrug — while the participle "hooting" still carries the full ancient weight of the hollow, jeering cry.
