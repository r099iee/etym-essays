Daresay (also written dare say, and in its normal use attached to the first person) is a fixed conversational formula meaning 'I venture to say', 'I should think', 'I assume it to be probable'. It is a mild, polite, slightly hedging assertion of likelihood — the speaker offers an opinion as something he is willing to risk saying, not as a proven fact. Grammatically it behaves like a modal-style verb phrase: the subject is almost always I, and a following clause completes it (I daresay you will think of something), though it can also stand alone as a soft assent meaning 'very likely so'. In Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bilbo uses it in his host-manner at Bag End — If you sit on the door-step long enough, I daresay you will think of something — and the word is pure comfortable hobbit speech: optimistic, understated, slightly fussy, and socially smoothing, as he steers the thirteen dwarves from the doorstep toward bed and breakfast. The tone is colloquial-genteel, early-modern to modern spoken English; neither slang nor high rhetoric, but today faintly dated, the kind of word a patient host or a Jane Austen character would use.

Polysemy — the range of uses. The word has a small but real set of distinct uses, all built on the same core of 'I dare to say / I am bold enough to venture this':

  • Assertion of probability: I daresay (that)… introduces a prediction or belief the speaker considers likely but cannot guarantee — I daresay it will rain. This is the dominant use and the one in the card context.
  • Standalone assent: I daresay alone, answering a statement, means 'probably so', 'I shouldn't wonder' — a soft agreement that concedes likelihood without full commitment.
  • Parenthetical hedge: inserted into a sentence to soften or qualify — …the news, I daresay, will surprise him — where it marks the speaker's own reservation.
  • Politeness / face-saving: used to venture a suggestion while leaving the hearer room to disagree, the classic function of a hedge — Bilbo's I daresay you will think of something both encourages and carefully avoids commanding.
  • Occasional irony or understatement: the formula can be layered — I daresay you think you're clever — where the mildness becomes pointed, a British pattern of understated reproach.

Grammar and semantic boundaries. The historical bridge is transparent: dare is a modal of boldness ('to be brave enough to'), say is plain utterance, and the fusion I daresay literally means 'I am bold enough to say'. Over time the assertiveness weakened: a phrase that begins as bold speech ('I risk saying this') becomes a polite softener ('I suppose, probably') — a classic hedge, the semantic reversal from boldness to modesty. Distinguish from near-synonyms: suppose is plainer and weaker, pure guesswork; guess is informal and more uncertain; reckon and figure are regional and informal; expect implies reasonable grounds; presume is more formal and can sound colder or more assertive (I presume you have finished); assume suggests taking something for granted; suspect hints at doubt; opine is formal and often pompous; I should think is a close British equivalent but a full clause, not a fused word; and venture to say is the formal, self-effacing equivalent of the formula. Daresay is restricted in modern use almost entirely to the first-person singular, is more British than American, and survives chiefly in speech and light literary narrative — a fossil of genteel conversational hedging that still does its modest work of offering an opinion while retreating from asserting it.
