PIE *kei- "to set in motion, to stir, to rouse" (reconstructed root; c. 4000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *haifstiz "strife, violence, struggle, contention" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-200 CE; the derivation from *kei- is conjectural - see debate note) → Frankish *haifst "violence, impetuosity" (reconstructed; c. 5th century CE; the West Germanic speech of the Salian Franks, which contributed heavily to Old French vocabulary) → Old French haste "haste, urgency, speed" (attested 12th century CE) → Middle English haste "speed, quickness, hurry, eagerness" (borrowed from Old French, attested early 13th century, c. 1200-1225) → Middle English hasten / hastien (verb) "to make haste, to move quickly" - native formation, haste + verbal suffix -en (attested c. 1300; the standard form hasten reinforced in the 16th century) → English hastened "moved quickly; caused something to happen sooner" - past tense and past participle of hasten (present-day rootWord) TARGET Debate / nuance note: The reconstruction of the Germanic noun as *haifstiz "strife, violence" is secure on the strength of Gothic haifsts "strife, contest", Old English hæst "violence, fury, impetuosity", Old Norse heipt "feud, hatred", and Old High German heisti "violent, forceful". However, the further connection of this Germanic root to the PIE root *kei- "to set in motion" is speculative: some scholars treat *haifstiz as an isolated Germanic formation of unknown pre-Germanic origin, and the *kei- link rests on broad root-bundling (Pokorny) rather than on unambiguous evidence. The English verb is a native formation (haste + -en), not a borrowing: Old French had the noun haste but its own verb hastir, from which English did not derive hasten.
