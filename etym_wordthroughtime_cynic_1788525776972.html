- 4th Century BCE – Classical Antiquity - Designated an adherent of the philosophical school of Antisthenes and Diogenes of Sinope, who rejected worldly possessions, luxury, and social conventions to live in austere harmony with nature - Distinct philosophical school denoting radical virtue and asceticism.
- 1st Century BCE – Roman Era - Maintained respect as a rigorous philosophical tradition (Latin cynicus), though often caricatured for shameless street-living, public insolence, and sharp verbal biting of patrician hypocrisy - Philosophical admiration mixed with societal shock.
- 16th–17th Century - Re-entered Early Modern English via Renaissance humanism, shifting from a philosophical adherent to a dispositional label for a sneering, snarling fault-finder who habitually carps at human goodness - Semantic pejoration toward bitter misanthropy.
- 18th–19th Century - Cemented in Enlightenment and Victorian literature (e.g. Jonathan Swift, William Makepeace Thackeray, Oscar Wilde) as a world-weary person who sneers at sentimentality, romance, and moral idealism - Witty aestheticism and social satire.
- 20th–21st Century - Pervasively applied to describe institutional distrust, widespread sociopolitical disillusionment, and habitual disbelief in political or corporate integrity - Dominant psychological and cultural worldview.
