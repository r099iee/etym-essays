The word bubbling descends from the noun and verb bubble, which enters English around 1300 in the Middle English form bobel. The immediate source is probably Middle Dutch bobbel or Middle Low German bubbele, words for a small sphere of liquid filled with air. Like throb and thump, the whole family is judged imitative or echoic: the sequence b-u-b, and the doubled consonant that gives bubble its bobbing shape, reproduces the blub-blub sound of gas bursting through water. Onomatopoeic words of this kind cannot be traced to a PIE root, because their form was motivated by sound, not by descent.

In the 14th century the word is domestic and practical. Boiling water “bubbles” over a fire; the earliest records are from the kitchen and the brewhouse, and the verb (attested from the early 1400s) means simply to rise in bubbles or to throw up bubbles. The participle bubbling appears almost immediately in this literal sense: a bubbling pot, a bubbling well.

The 15th to 17th centuries carry the word out of the kitchen and into the natural world. Springs, brooks, fountains, and streams “bubble” — water emerging from the ground or moving over stones makes the same liquid sound, and the word becomes the standard descriptor for clear, living, self-renewing water. Fermenting beer and wine also bubble, linking the word to the tavern and to the alchemist's still. By the 17th century the word has acquired a distinctly pleasant, musical register: bubbling water is the sound of life and refreshment.

In the 18th and 19th centuries the figurative sense develops. Because a boiling or fermenting liquid bubbles “over”, the language transfers the image to temperament and emotion: people bubble over with laughter, wit, spirits, and joy. “Bubbling laughter” and “bubbling gaiety” become stock phrases, and the adjective begins to describe a person who is irrepressibly cheerful. The 20th century intensifies this through the champagne trade: sparkling wine is called “bubbly”, and by extension a cheerful, effervescent person is “bubbly” too. The economic noun bubble (a market rising like a fragile sphere until it bursts) develops separately from the early 18th century, but it feeds back into the verb's modern sense of precarious, rising pressure.

Contemporary English keeps the full range. Bubbling is still the kitchen word for a noisy boiling pot, the natural-history word for a spring or mud-pool, the sound-design word for a watery gurgle, and the character word for lively effervescence. The modern idiom “bubbling under the surface” names something suppressed but about to break out — tensions, ambition, or a chart act on the edge of success — and preserves, after six hundred years, the image of the liquid that quietly rises until it can no longer be contained. The source context uses the word in its purest older sense: a “sort of bubbling” as a sound in the ears, like a pot “galloping” on the fire — the word's earliest recorded reality.
