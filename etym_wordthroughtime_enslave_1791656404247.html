- 1630s–1680s: Early Modern Coinage & Atlantic Expansion - Formed in Early Modern English by prefixing the causative morpheme *en-* to *slave*, directly describing the violent capture, commodification, and maritime transport of human beings in the burgeoning transatlantic chattel slave trade and Mediterranean corsair galley slavery.
- Late 17th–18th Century: Enlightenment Political Philosophy - Adopted by philosophers such as John Locke, John Milton, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau as a potent political metaphor, using the term to fiercely condemn arbitrary absolute monarchy, divine-right tyranny, and religious dogmatism as forces that 'enslave' freeborn citizens.
- 19th Century: Abolitionist Indictment & Moral Transcendentalism - Became the paramount moral and political lightning rod of transatlantic abolitionism (spearheaded by Frederick Douglass, William Lloyd Garrison, and Harriet Beecher Stowe), while concurrently expanding in Victorian moral psychology to describe how alcohol, narcotics, and unchecked sensual appetites enslave the human soul.
- 20th Century: Industrial Labor Critiques & Totalitarianism - Deployed across political theory and sociological critique to characterize totalitarian concentration camps, forced labor regimes (such as the Soviet Gulag), and modern industrial wage-mechanization that strips workers of creative agency.
- 21st Century: Digital Servitude & Algorithmic Bondage - Extensively adopted in neurobiology, technological ethics, and cultural criticism to define how algorithmic recommendation loops, variable reward schedules, and digital addiction actively enslave human attention and behavioral autonomy.
