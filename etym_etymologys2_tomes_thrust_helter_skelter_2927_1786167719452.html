[ROOT CORE (PIE): *tem- (to cut, slice)] — branch of TOMES
├── Greek: temnein (to cut) ──> tomos (a slice; specifically a severed section of a papyrus roll, hence a volume)
│ └── Latin: tomus (a volume of a larger work)
│ └── French: tome ──> Middle English / Early Modern English (16th c.): tome
│ └── Modern English: tomes (plural) ◄ TARGET
└── Cognates: Greek atomos (uncuttable, "atom"), epitomē (a cutting short, "epitome"), anatomia (cutting up, "anatomy"), tomē (a cut); the same root underlies the medical suffix -tomy and tomography.
Note: The "book" sense arose because long Greek and Roman rolls were physically cut into sections, each roll-section being called a tomos; the heavy, imposing sense of modern English tome is a later specialization of the neutral "volume" sense.


[ROOT CORE (PIE): *treud- / *trud- (to push, press, squeeze)] — branch of THRUST
├── Proto-Germanic: *þrustijaną (to press, force, thrust)
│ └── Old Norse: þrýsta (to press, squeeze, force) — borrowed into Middle English: thrusten (c. 1200)
│ └── Modern English: thrust (verb and noun) ──> thrust (also past tense and past participle) ◄ TARGET
└── Cognates: Old English þrēatan (to press, threaten ──> English threaten), Latin trūdere (to push, thrust ──> intrude, obtrude, protrude), Old Irish trot (to contend).
Note: The English verb was very likely reinforced by Old Norse þrýsta; the modern aerospace sense of thrust (propulsive force) is a 19th–20th century extension of the basic "push" meaning. The identity of form between verb and past participle is a defining feature of this word.


[ROOT CORE (Reduplicative / echoic formation, 16th c., no PIE root)] — branch of HELTER-SKELTER
├── dialectal English verb: skelter (to hasten, scurry, dash about), of uncertain, probably echoic origin
│ └── Early Modern English (1593, first recorded): helter-skelter (rhyming reduplication on skelter, with the nonsense element helter added for the rhyme)
│ └── Modern English: helter-skelter (adverb, adjective, noun) ◄ TARGET
└── Type-mates in English rhyming reduplication: pell-mell, hurly-burly, hodgepodge, topsy-turvy.
Note: The first element helter has no independent meaning; the word is a pure echoic / reduplicative coinage of a type extremely common in Early Modern English. The fairground "helter-skelter" (spiral slide) dates from the early 20th century.
