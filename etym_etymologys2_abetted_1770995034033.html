PIE *bheid- "to split, cleave, divide" (reconstructed root; c. 4000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *bītaną "to bite" - the "splitting with the teeth" specialization of the root (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-100 CE) → Old Norse bita "to bite" (attested; c. 9th-13th centuries CE) → Old Norse beita "to cause to bite, to set dogs on, to make an animal bite; to goad" - the causative formation of bita (attested; c. 9th-13th centuries CE) → Old French abeter "to bait, to hound, to urge on like a dog" - a- (from Latin ad, "to, toward") + beter "to bait" (borrowed from Germanic; attested 12th-13th centuries CE) → Anglo-Norman abetter "to instigate, encourage in wrongdoing" (attested 13th century CE) → Middle English abetten "to encourage, incite, support in wrongdoing" (attested c. 1300) → English abet "to encourage or assist in wrongdoing" → abetted (past tense and past participle) TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The chain is well attested from Old French onward. The only genuinely debated link is the very first step: the connection of the Germanic "bite" family (*bītaną) to PIE *bheid- "to split" is standard and widely accepted, since biting is literally splitting with the teeth (cf. Latin findere "to split", Sanskrit bhinátti "he splits", and English fissure, fission). No serious alternative is proposed for the Germanic-to-French section. Note that the prefix in Old French abeter is the Latin ad- "to, toward", not a separate root; and the noun "aid" in the legal doublet "aid and abet" is an unrelated word, from Old French aider, from Latin adiutare "to help".
