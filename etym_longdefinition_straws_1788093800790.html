Straws is the plural of straw, and in the source passage it is used in the oldest, physical sense: the speaker's legs are “like straws” — thin, dry, brittle, boneless stalks that will not bear weight — the stock simile for sudden dizziness and collapse. The word as a whole now carries three distinct live sense-clusters that must be separated carefully.

1. The material sense: dried stalks of grain. Straw is the dry stem residue of cereal plants (wheat, barley, oats, rye) left after the ears have been threshed. It has served, since prehistory, as animal bedding and fodder-mix, floor litter, thatch for roofs, packing and cushioning material, a binder in mud-brick walls (the biblical image of the Israelites forced to gather their own straw for bricks), and the raw material of straw hats, baskets, mats, wine-bottle jackets (the straw-wrapped Chianti flask) and decorative straw-work. In this sense the plural “straws” appears in agriculture and craft: bundles, bales and loose stalks (“straws scattered on the barn floor”), and as the raw of similes of fragility — “thin as a straw”, “legs like straws”.

  • Bedding and thatch: straw's structural role; also erosion-control matting and straw bale construction (a modern revival of straw as a building material).
  • Colour and appearance: “straw-yellow”, “straw-haired” — the pale blond tone of dried straw, transferred to people and wine (vin jaune, straw-coloured wines).

2. The drinking-tube sense. A hollow stalk is a natural tube: ancient Sumerians drank beer from large jars through long gold and reed tubes (the gold tubes found at the Royal Cemetery of Ur, c. 2500 BCE), and the Chinese used hollow grass stalks for vermicelli-sipping. In the 19th century commercial “straws” were literally rye-grass stems sold for sipping (attested from the 1850s), and Marvin Stone patented a machine-made spiral-wound paper straw in January 1888. The flexible “bendy” straw was patented in 1937; plastic became the default material from the 1950s–60s. Today “straws” in everyday restaurant speech means exactly these tubes (“a box of straws”, “plastic straws”), and the plural is the unmarked form of this sense. Since the late 2010s the environmental movement has made plastic straws a symbol of disposable-waste culture — bans in the UK, EU and many US states have driven paper, bamboo, metal, glass and pasta alternatives, and “straw-free” has become a marketing claim.

3. Figurative and idiomatic senses (worthless, fragile, revealing).

  • “Not worth a straw” — utterly worthless (proverbial from Middle English; cf. the synonymous “not worth a stree”). The dry, valueless by-product of threshing stands for nothingness.
  • “Clutching / grasping at straws” — making desperate, futile attempts at rescue: proverbial from the 16th century (a drowning man catches at a straw that cannot save him).
  • “Straws in the wind” — small early indications of a coming change, from the way scattered straws show which way the wind blows (late 16th century).
  • “To draw straws” — to decide by lots, by pulling stalks of different lengths (attested since the mid-19th century).
  • “To split straws” — to quibble over trifles, to argue about nothing.
  • “The last straw (that breaks the camel's back)” — the final small burden that makes the whole collapse (fixed in the 19th century; Dickens uses “the last straw” in Dombey and Son, 1848).
  • “A straw in the wind” — a single tell-tale sign (16th–17th centuries onward).

4. Attributive compounds. Straw man (a sham opponent or argument set up to be knocked down; originally a figure of straw — a scarecrow or a worthless stand-in — and in 16th–17th-century legal slang a person of no means put forward as surety); straw poll / straw vote (an unofficial test of opinion, 19th-century US politics); straw boss (a lowest-level supervisor, US slang); straw hat (the classic boater); straw wine (wine pressed from grapes dried on straw mats).

The semantic bridge. Everything follows from one property: straw is the dry, light, hollow, brittle residue of the harvest. Dry and hollow → a natural tube → the drink straw. Light, brittle, worthless → the proverbs of worthlessness and of desperate grasping. Thin, dry, weightless → the simile of the frail body (“legs like straws”). Pale → the colours straw-yellow and straw-haired. Propped-up and hollow → the straw man. The plural form “straws” sits at the intersection of all of these: the farm litter, the restaurant tube, and the emblem of weakness in the sentence this card is built around.

Register. “Straws” in the agricultural sense is plain and homely; in the drinking sense it is everyday and now environmentally charged; in idioms it is fixed and neutral. The word has no elevated register — its power lies precisely in its cheapness, which is why similes built on it are so effective at picturing fragility.
