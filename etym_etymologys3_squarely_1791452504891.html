The history of squarely begins with a number. The Latin quattuor, "four", descends from the PIE root *kʷetwóres, one of the most stable numerals in the Indo-European family, and from it Latin built quadrus, "four-sided", and quadra, "a square". The square was not merely a shape to the Romans; it was the organising figure of their surveyors, who laid out farmland and military camps on a grid of right angles, and of their masons, who tested every corner with a set square. The word carried exactness from the beginning.

From quadra to Old French. Vulgar Latin formed a verb *exquadrāre, "to make square, to fit to a square", and this passed into Old French as esquarrer, used of hewing stone and timber to true right angles. The noun esquarre, "a square, a set square", followed, and it is this French word that English borrowed in the fourteenth century as square. The Middle English word covered the shape, the tool and the open rectangular space in a town, and by the sixteenth century it had acquired the adverbial form squarely, meaning "in a square manner".

  • Geometric sense: a plane figure with four equal sides and four right angles, and by extension anything arranged at right angles.
  • Tool sense: the carpenter's or mason's square used to test and mark true corners; the tool embodies the idea of correctness.
  • Spatial sense: an open rectangular area in a town or building, from the Roman forum to the English market square.

The moral turn. The decisive development for squarely is the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century moralisation of square. A square is a shape whose sides are true and whose angles are equal; a "square deal" is therefore a fair one, a "square account" is one that balances, and a "square man" is straight and reliable. The metaphor is architectural and commercial at once: the square is the figure of accurate measurement, and accurate measurement is the basis of honest trade. By the eighteenth century "fair and square" was a settled English phrase, and squarely could mean "honestly, straightforwardly".

Directness and blame. The modern emphatic sense, "directly, without qualification", developed in the nineteenth century and matured in the twentieth, especially in political and legal prose. To place the blame squarely on someone is to put it exactly where it belongs, with no ambiguity and no sharing. The image is still geometric: the accusation is set down squarely, aligned and true, not slanted or evasive. This is the sense that dominates contemporary usage, and it explains why squarely sounds decisive rather than merely accurate.

  • Physical: standing squarely, planted with feet apart and weight balanced.
  • Positional: squarely in the middle, exactly on the centre line.
  • Moral: dealing squarely, acting with fairness and candour.
  • Emphatic: squarely responsible, wholly and unequivocally to blame.

The slang divergence. In twentieth-century American slang square came to mean a conventional, uncool person, the opposite of hip, and this sense gave us "be there or be square". The slang is a curious reversal: the word that once meant honest and true came to mean dull and rigid, because the square person was thought to be bound by rules. Squarely, however, was untouched by this development; it remained the adverb of directness and fairness, and it never acquired the dismissive tone of the noun.

Sound and spelling. Latin qu- became k- in Old French, giving esquarre with initial e-; English dropped the initial vowel and the final -e, producing the monosyllable square. The adverb adds the native suffix -ly, from Old English -līce, and the result is a regular, transparent formation. The spelling has been stable since the Middle English period.

Literary and social presence. The word runs through English literature as a marker of plain dealing: the squire who deals squarely with his tenants, the judge who puts the matter squarely to the jury, the sergeant who plants himself squarely in the doorway. It belongs to the same moral vocabulary as upright, straight and above board, and it retains a faintly old-fashioned decency that makes it useful whenever a writer wants to sound firm without sounding aggressive.
