The adverb effulgently is a word built entirely out of light. Its root is one of the oldest words for shining in the Indo-European language family; its Latin carrier was a verb of lightning and gleaming; and its English career has been confined to the highest reaches of literary prose and poetry. To trace it is to watch a family of light-words radiate out of a single ancient source, and to see how a language mints a new word precisely when it wants a brightness that ordinary vocabulary cannot supply — the brightness of learning, of virtue, of the divine.

1. The Indo-European root of shining. The chain begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *bʰel-, "to shine, flash, burn," one of the most productive roots of light in the entire family. It appears, with various extensions, across the branches: Greek phlégein "to blaze" and phalos "shining white," Sanskrit bhāti "it shines," Lithuanian balti "to grow white," and in the Germanic branch Old English bǣl "fire" and bærnan "to burn." The root specialized differently in each branch: some languages pushed it toward heat and burning (blaze, flame), others toward whiteness (the Lithuanian and Slavic names of the color white), still others toward brilliance of light. The extended form *bʰleg- (or *bʰl̥g-), "to burn, blaze, flash," underlies Latin flagrāre "to blaze" (source of English "flagrant," "conflagration") and, through a parallel but distinct formation, the Latin ful- of fulgēre. The reconstruction of this extended form is standard — the Latin evidence for a shining-and-flashing root is secure — though it involves the well-understood detail of the internal sonorant, and older handbooks sometimes reconstructed a separate root where modern scholarship (de Vaan) sees one family.

2. Latin fulgēre: the verb of lightning. Latin fulgēre, attested from the second century BCE in the epic and comic poets, meant "to shine, flash, lighten" — the verb of lightning (fulgur), of gleaming armour and weapons, of flashing eyes, of gold catching the sun. It is a vivid, poetic verb, entirely at home in the world of Ennius, Lucretius, and Virgil, where the brilliance of the natural and heroic world is constantly being flashed at the reader. From fulgēre Latin built a family of shining: fulgidus "gleaming, flashing," refulgēre "to shine back, glitter" (source of English "refulgent"), and — by adding the prefix ex-, "out of, forth" — effulgēre, "to shine forth, to flash out, to beam out." The addition of the prefix changed the meaning from mere shining to outpouring: effulgence is light that comes out of something, light bursting the bounds of its source. This is the crucial semantic event in the word's history, and it is why, two thousand years later, effulgently still means not merely "brightly" but "brilliantly streaming forth from within."

3. Entry into English (18th century). English borrowed the adjective effulgent in the 1730s, and the noun effulgence and the adverb effulgently followed within the same wave of Latinate importation. The word arrived as a consciously literary coinage — the vocabulary of poets, rhetoricians, and divines — and it has never left that register. The eighteenth century, with its taste for the sublime and the Latinate, took the word up eagerly: the Augustans and the writers of the Gothic and devotional traditions used effulgence for the radiance of the sun, of virtue, of the Godhead, and the word carried with it the theological charge of the Latin and Greek fathers, for whom light was the standing metaphor of the divine nature.

4. The Romantic and Victorian apogee. The Romantic and Victorian periods made effulgence one of their favourite words for moral and celestial brightness. The Romantics needed a vocabulary for light that was not merely physical but inward and transformative — the light of imagination, of the inspired mind, of the sublime landscape — and effulgence, with its built-in idea of light pouring from within, was ideal. For the Victorians, brightness became a moral category, and effulgently came to connote not just light but the radiance of virtue, learning, and character made visible: a face "effulgent with joy," a mind "effulgently clear," a virtue displayed in full blaze. The word's grandeur — its four syllables, its opening vowel, its soft double consonant — made it the natural adverb of the solemn and the aspirational, and it was in this period that the word settled into the combination of the couplet's meaning: brightness as erudition and virtue made manifest.

5. The word in the couplet and today. In the couplet — "May the jewel of learning shine in this house more effulgently than the sun and moon" — the adverb carries every layer of its history at once. The root gives the elementary force of light; the prefix ex- gives the outpouring, the light streaming from within; the participle and its Latinate English dress give the solemnity of a prayer. The "jewel of learning" is asked to do what the sun and moon do — shine — but effulgently, from within, and more brilliantly than the two great natural lights of heaven. Today effulgently remains what it has always been: a rare, deliberately elevated adverb chosen where "radiantly" would be too plain and "dazzlingly" too informal — the word that lets a family's blessing turn a pair of ebony boards into a beacon.
