Derivation chain for BATED (single root word; aphetic form of abated; reconstructed stages are labelled as such, attested stages carry dates):

1. Proto-Indo-European *bhau- (reconstructed, debated) — "to strike, beat"; the primitive sense is physical striking, the blow of hand, flail, or weapon. Handbooks debate the exact laryngeal and ablaut shape of the root.

2. Latin battuere / batuere — "to beat, strike," glossed by the grammarian Festus (c. 2nd century CE); possibly a Gaulish or Etruscan loan rather than a securely inherited Latin verb — an honest uncertainty that handbooks continue to record.

3. Late / Vulgar Latin *abbattuere (reconstructed) — "to beat down," the prefixed form (ad- "to, down upon" + battuere "to beat") from which the Romance verbs descend.

4. Old French abatre / abattre — "to fell, knock down" (attested from c. 11th–12th centuries); the ordinary word for felling a tree or knocking a thing down.

5. Anglo-French abater → Middle English abaten — "to beat down, lessen," 13th century; the verb takes the crucial figurative step from physical felling to reduction of intensity.

6. English abate — "to lessen, diminish, hold in" (13th century onward); the living verb — a storm abates, enthusiasm abates.

7. English bate — "to lessen, hold in," the aphetic (clipped) form of abate, the initial unstressed a- being dropped; 16th century (parallels: mend from amend, sport from disport).

8. BATED — past participle and adjective, "lessened, restrained," fixed in the phrase "bated breath" from Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice (1596); the card's form, BATED.

Notes: the falconry verb to bate (of a hawk, to beat or flutter the wings) is a separate word from the same ultimate root via Old French batre "to beat"; the native Germanic cognate of the root survives in English beat (Old English bēatan, from Proto-Germanic *bautan "to strike, push"); and the common misspelling "baited breath" confuses bate with bait.
