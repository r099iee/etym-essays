Gallic comes from Latin Gallicus "belonging to Gallia/Gauls," from Gallus "a Gaul" + adjectival -icus. Its ultimate pre-Latin source is Celtic and debated, so the chain requires a frank statement of uncertainty at its top.

Latin core. Romans used Gallus (plural Galli) for the Celtic peoples north and west of Italy and Gallia for their territory — Cisalpine Gaul in northern Italy and Transalpine Gaul beyond the Alps. Gallicus is the regular ethnic adjective: bellum Gallicum (Gallic War), ager Gallicus. Caesar's De Bello Gallico (mid-1st c. BCE) fixed the term in world literature. Suffix -icus (Greek -ikos via Latin) forms "pertaining to" adjectives exactly as in civicus, Germanicus. Phonology is stable: Gall- with double -ll-, short -a-, hard -g-.

Celtic substrate — debated. Latin Gallus is almost certainly a borrowing of a Celtic self-designation, but which one is disputed. Common proposals connect it to Old Irish gall "foreigner" (originally "Gaul"), Welsh gall "power, might," or a tribal name Gal-. Some scholars link it to PIE *gal- "to be able, power," others to *ĝhel-. Because Romans applied Galli broadly to diverse tribes who did not necessarily share one name, the OED and de Vaan treat the Celtic antecedent as uncertain. The responsible statement is: from a Celtic ethnic term of disputed meaning, perhaps "powerful ones," borrowed into Latin as Gallus.

  • Greek parallel: Greek Galatai, Keltoi named the same peoples; English Galatian and Celtic are cognate ethnonyms through different transmission routes, showing how one Celtic population received multiple classical labels.
  • Medieval continuity: Late Latin Gallicus survived in ecclesiastical and scholarly use (ecclesia Gallicana, the Gallican Church), preserving the adjective while spoken Gallo-Romance evolved into Old French. The learned form never underwent popular sound changes.
  • Renaissance revival and English borrowing: 16th–17th-century English humanists revived Gallic directly from Latin as a literary synonym for French, first for ancient contexts then wittily for modern ones. Unlike French (from Old English frencisc via Germanic Frank), Gallic is a conscious classicism, which is why it still sounds bookish and arch.
  • Modern differentiation: scholarship now carefully separates Gallic (Celtic/Roman Gaul), Gallican (French Catholic tradition), Gallicism (a French idiom borrowed into another language), and gallic acid (from gall-nut, unrelated — from Latin galla). The "French-neighbours" jocular sense in British prose is a 19th–20th-century newspaper and comic-novel habit.

Thus Gallic is a Roman exonym for Celts, frozen by Caesar, preserved by the Church, revived by humanists, and now used by the English partly to name ancient tribes accurately and partly to call the French something prettier than just French.
