- Late 14th c. — Coalescence in Middle English: formed as the compound phrase in so moche, calquing Anglo-Norman en tant que, used to indicate proportional degree and resultant conditions in chivalric and philosophical verse.
- 15th–16th c. — Biblical and statutory standard: codified in early English translations of the Bible (Tyndale, Coverdale, Cranmer) and Tudor parliamentary acts, establishing insomuch that as the pre-eminent marker of dramatic, miraculous, or legal results.
- 17th–18th c. — Augustan rhetorical staple: deployed masterfully by Jonathan Swift, John Locke, and Joseph Addison to construct ornate, balanced periodic sentences tracing human folly and philosophical causality.
- 19th c. — Gradual marginalization: displaced in everyday speech and journalism by simpler connectors like so that and inasmuch as, retreating into high literature, judicial opinions, and historical narratives.
- 20th–21st c. — Archaic stylistic marker: preserved in legal drafting, ecclesiastical commentary, and deliberate period pastiche to convey antique dignity, rigorous formality, or ironical distance.
