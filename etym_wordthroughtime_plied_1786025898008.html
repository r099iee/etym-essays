- Middle English (c. 1350–1450) - Formed as an aphetic shortening of Old French 'appliquer' (to apply) and merged with 'plier' (to fold/bend), meaning to bend to a task, attend to business, or work diligently.
- Late 15th to 16th Century - Extended to the active handling, wielding, and working of tools, weapons, and mechanical implements (such as oars, looms, and needles).
- 17th Century - Maritime expansion gave rise to the navigational sense of ships navigating or traversing regular shipping channels ('plying the seas').
- 18th Century - Broadened to interpersonal solicitation, such as plying customers for trade, plying cabs for hire, or persistently plying guests with wine and questions.
- 19th to 21st Century - Retained across these rich idiomatic domains: industrious professional craftsmanship ('plied his trade'), regular transport routes ('plied the route'), and social/interrogative persistence ('plied with drinks').
