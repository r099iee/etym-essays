STOKES — an essay. To stoke is to poke, and poking is one of the oldest actions named by the Indo-European languages. The verb's family begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *steig- "to prick, pierce, stick," a root of pointed instruments and piercing motions that left children all over the Indo-European world: Latin īnstīgāre "to goad, incite" (whence English instigate, a goading that is still a kind of pricking), Greek stízein "to prick, tattoo" (whence English stigma, literally the mark pricked in), and in the Germanic north a whole family of sticks and stabs — *stikaną "to stab, prick," ancestor of English stick (both noun and verb) and stitch. From the same root, in its o-grade form *stōkaną, Germanic built the verb of thrusting and poking: Old High German stōhhan "to thrust, poke," and in the Low Countries Middle Dutch stoken, "to poke, to thrust at, to stir a fire," attested from the fourteenth century. The Dutch verb is the immediate ancestor of ours: Dutch stoken meant exactly what stoking still means, to jab at the fuel and push it about so the fire lives, and it also gave Dutch its stok, "stick," the very instrument of the action — stoking, etymologically, is doing to a fire what a stick does.

English borrowed stoken in the late seventeenth century (first attested 1680s), in the age when the domestic hearth was still the center of every household and the tending of fires a daily, universal chore. For a century the word lived in the kitchen and the grate, meaning "to poke, stir, and feed a fire." Then came the age of steam, and the word went industrial. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the stoker became the man who fed the furnace of a steamship or locomotive, and the vocabulary hardened around the job: stokehold and stokehole, the furnace-room of the ship where the stokers worked, and stoker, the man (and later the mechanical device) that feeds coal to the boiler. The industrial stoker is the fire-tender generalized: the same rhythmic feeding and stirring that kept the domestic hearth alive now kept the engines of empire moving, and the word carried the heat of that labor into the modern world. In the twentieth century stoke caught fire figuratively, and this is now its most productive sense. Because an emotion can be pictured as a flame, the verb that feeds a fire became the verb that feeds a feeling: to stoke anger, to stoke anxiety, to stoke fears, to stoke a controversy. Modern journalism runs on the word — politicians and the press "stoke" public passion as a stoker feeds a furnace — and the ancient image of the poked fire is now the standard metaphor for the deliberate intensification of anything hot: stoke demand, stoke inflation, stoke rivalry, stoke a crisis. The passage's use — a boy who "stokes" the fire against a world of snow and wolves — is the root sense, and the whole figurative superstructure of modern politics stands on it.

  • PIE *steig- "to prick, stick" (ca. 3000 BCE) → Greek stigma, Latin instigate, Germanic *stikaną "stab."
  • o-grade *stōkaną "thrust, poke" → Middle Dutch stoken "to poke, stir a fire" (14th c.); Dutch stok "stick" — the instrument of the action.
  • English stoke "to feed and stir a fire" (1680s, borrowed from Dutch).
  • 18th–19th c. industrial age: the stoker, stokehold, stokehole — feeding the furnaces of steamships and locomotives.
  • 20th c. figurative: to stoke anger, anxiety, controversy — feeding emotion as one feeds a flame; the standard verb of political journalism.
  • Modern double life: the humble daily tending of a hearth against the cold, and the deliberate fanning of any heat, literal or figurative.

SCULLERY — an essay. The scullery is, etymologically, the dish-room, and its history runs from the covering skins of prehistoric life to the wet stone sinks of Victorian servants' quarters. The root is the Indo-European *skeu-, "to cover, conceal, protect," one of the most productive in the language: it gave Greek skûtos, "skin, hide" — the covering of the body — and it gave Latin scūtum, "shield," the covering that protects the soldier. In Latin it also produced scutra, "a flat dish, a platter, a shallow pan," and the traditional etymology reads the dish as a kind of covering — a flat surface laid over, protecting the table — though, as noted in etymologyS2, some authorities treat scutra as of uncertain origin and the link to *skeu-, while widely repeated, as probable rather than certain. What is certain is the next step: from scutra Latin made the diminutive scutella, "a small dish, a bowl, a cup," attested in the first century CE in the cookbook of Apicius. The tiny dish became the ancestor of a great family of English table-words, most visibly in the French line: Old French escuele, "a dish, a bowl," the everyday word for the vessel from which medieval France ate, which survives in English as the doublet of our word — and, with a different diminutive, gives English esquire's distant cousin "scuttle" (a broad shallow basket or pan) and the nautical "scuttle" (a small hatch-cover, again the covering sense).

From escuele, Old French made escuelerie with its suffix -erie ("place of, function of"): the escuelerie was at first the office or department of the esculier, the dish-bearer or scullion, the servant whose job was the dishes, and then, concretely, the room where the dishes were kept and washed. English borrowed the word in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as squillerye, skuillerye, modern scullery, and it settled into the great houses as the small back kitchen — the wet, rough room off the main kitchen where pots were scoured and vegetables peeled, the province of the scullery maid, the humblest servant in the household hierarchy. The social geography of the scullery is part of its meaning: it is the downstairs room of the upstairs/downstairs world, the unglamorous heart of servant labor, the place where the house's cleanliness was produced out of sight. With the disappearance of the servant class in the twentieth century the word receded from daily use, surviving in period fiction, in the phrase scullery maid, and in the floor-plans of old houses where the scullery has been turned into a kitchen, utility room, or pantry. Its etymology, however, remains perfectly legible: scullery is "the place of the little dishes," and every wet sink, every stack of scoured pots in every great house, was the direct descendant of a small Latin bowl that once covered a Roman table.

  • PIE *skeu- "to cover" → Greek skûtos "hide," Latin scūtum "shield"; Latin scutra "flat dish" (probable, not certain, link — noted).
  • Latin scutella "small dish, bowl" (1st c. CE, Apicius) — the diminutive of scutra.
  • Old French escuele "dish, bowl" (with regular Romance sound changes) → escuelerie "the office/room of the dish-server, the dish-room" (12th c.).
  • Middle English squillerye/scullery (13th–14th c., via Anglo-French) — the small back kitchen for washing-up.
  • Modern: the scullery of the great house, home of the scullery maid; the word's "place of the little dishes" etymology survives in scuttle and the related table-word family.
