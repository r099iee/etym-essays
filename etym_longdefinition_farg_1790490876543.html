Farg in the source sentence is the second half of Glen Farg, the wooded valley in the Ochil Hills of Perth and Kinross, Scotland: “and the Monastery in Glen Farg, which I confused with ‘Glendearg.’” The boy reads Scott’s The Monastery (1820) while travelling through Glen Farg on the Edinburgh–Perth road and mistakes the real glen for Scott’s fictional Glendearg — the hidden tower-glen of the White Lady of Avenel — because the names chime and the scenery matches: a narrow burn, steep birch-clad braes, a streamlet fit for a spirit. The card therefore tests a place-name fragment that a child half-heard from the road and half-read from a romance.

Farg never stands alone in modern use; it occurs only as the specific element of Glen Farg (also Glenfarg village, Glenfarg viaduct, Glenfarg reservoir, Glenfarg water). The glen carries the old A90 and the modern M90 corridor south of Perth, its burn — the Farg Water (also called the River Farg) — running north to join the Earn near Bridge of Earn. Glenfarg village (church, school, former railway station) sits midway; the spectacular Glenfarg viaducts once bore the North British Railway’s main line. Saying “Glen Farg” thus evokes coaching roads, railway cuttings, and motorway alike — three centuries of travellers threading the Ochils by the same notch.

  • Core toponymic sense: the Farg Water and its glen. “Glen Farg,” “the Farg,” “Farg Water” — the burn and the valley it carved through the Ochils between Kinross-shire and Perthshire. E.g. “we drove through Glen Farg in heavy rain.” The generic glen is Gaelic gleann “narrow valley”; the specific Farg is of obscure, possibly Gaelic origin (see etymology), preserved only in this one water-name. The bridge from water to valley is standard metonymy: the burn names the hollow it drains.
  • Settlement and engineering sense: Glenfarg village and works. “Glenfarg,” “Glenfarg station (closed 1970),” “the Glenfarg viaducts,” “Glenfarg reservoir.” Victorian engineers made the name famous far beyond walkers: two great viaducts (one demolished for the motorway) and deep cuttings announced the glen to every Edinburgh–Perth passenger. The bridge is administrative convenience: hamlets and structures take the glen’s name wholesale.
  • Literary-confusive sense in the source: Farg as Glendearg’s double. Scott invents Glendearg (“glen of the tears/blood” — suggestive Gaelic gleann-dearg, red glen) for the tower where Halbert and Edward Glendinning are raised under the White Lady’s watch. The child, reading The Monastery in real Glen Farg, hears Farg/dearg rhyme, sees a matching burn and braes, and concludes the White Lady “had as certainly lived by the streamlet in that glen of the Ochils” as Mary Stuart on Loch Leven. The card’s Farg is thus inseparable from its fictional twin: one real valley authenticating one invented spirit.
  • Landscape sense: Ochil glen scenery. “That glen of the Ochils” — steep-sided, birch and rowan, curlews and dippers, the Farg burn threading mossy stones. Ruskin’s mature geology and botany begin here: the boy who confuses Farg with Glendearg grows into the man who reads landscapes as texts. E.g. “the streamlet in that glen” is the Farg Water in spate after rain, exactly the habitat a Border spirit would choose.
  • Travel-corridor sense: the road north. “Through Glen Farg to Perth” — the family’s biennial summer route: post-chaise or mail-coach up the glen, over the watershed, down to the Earn valley and Perth. Every other year the same rite, the same novels, the same confusion. Modern drivers on the M90 still climb the same grade, mostly unaware they are re-enacting a Regency reading holiday.

Form: /fɑːrɡ/ (rhotic in Scotland, non-rhotic /fɑːɡ/ in RP); always capitalised as part of the proper name; compound variably spaced (Glen Farg, Glenfarg). The lone fragment Farg looks odd on a flashcard — but that oddity is the point: it preserves the child’s ear catching the strange second syllable of a roadside name-board and rhyming it with romance. Register is neutral toponymic; connotation, for Scott’s readers, romantic and slightly eerie, with the White Lady’s streamlet running through it.
