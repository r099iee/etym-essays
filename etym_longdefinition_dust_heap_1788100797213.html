Dust-heap (also written dust heap or dustheap) is a compound noun — dust + heap — meaning a heap or mound of dust, ashes, cinders, and household refuse: in short, a rubbish dump. Two very old English words meet in it: dust (Old English dūst, "fine particles of earth; the mortal body") and heap (Old English hēap, "a pile, a mass; a multitude"). The compound is plain and physical: throw the sweepings, the ashes, and the broken things aside, and they form a dust-heap. In the story of the Happy Prince it is the place where the dead Swallow lies and where the leaden heart is thrown — the story's final image of discard and oblivion.

Primary sense: the refuse pile, and its Victorian glory. The dust-heap in its most famous form is an institution of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century London. Before municipal waste collection, households' "dust" — the ashes of coal fires, mixed with cinders, rags, bones, and kitchen refuse — was carried away by the dustman (the rubbish collector of the old trades) and dumped in great heaps in the dust-yards on the edge of the city. These dust-mounds were then sifted inch by inch by dust-girls and dust-workers for anything salable: cinders for re-burning, bones for glue, rags for paper, and occasionally, as legend insisted, hidden treasure. The trade was a fortune to some — Dickens's father worked for a dust-contractor, and in Our Mutual Friend (1865) Dickens makes the great dust-heaps themselves the buried wealth of the plot, piles of rubbish concealing a fortune. The dust-heap is thus a distinctly Victorian thing: the mound where the city dumped its consumed fires, sifted by the poor, and dreamed over by novelists as the symbol of value disguised as waste.

Secondary senses and the figurative bridge. From the physical mound the compound grew a powerful figurative life — the bridge is simple: what is thrown on the dust-heap is finished, worthless, and forgotten.

  • The dust-heap of history — the place where failed ideas, regimes, and reputations are consigned; close kin of the famous "dustbin of history" long attributed to Trotsky (1917), and a standard image in political journalism ("consigned to the dust-heap of history").
  • The personal and domestic metaphor — "the dust-heap of memory", "the dust-heap of the mind": the internal rubbish dump where forgotten things accumulate; also a heap of neglected possessions ("every broken toy ended on the same dust-heap").
  • Literal but rural and archaic uses — the cottage or farmyard dust-heap (ashes and sweepings piled for garden use) survives in dialect and older writing; and the biblical "dust to dust" (Genesis 3:19, at home in the old English liturgy) gives the heap of dust a funereal undertone — the body itself returns to a dust-heap.
  • In Wilde's tale — "they threw it on a dust-heap where the dead Swallow was also lying": the compound quietly performs the story's whole judgement — the beautiful Prince's heart and the faithful bird, reduced to refuse, side by side on the mound where nothing is valued.

Grammar, register and boundaries. The word is a countable compound noun (a dust-heap; the dust-heaps; on a dust-heap), neutral in register though now slightly dated or literary in literal use. It should not be confused with ash-heap (ashes specifically), midden (a manure or refuse pile, rural and archaeological), dunghill (animal manure), dump and landfill (modern, mechanised waste disposal), rubbish-tip (British, municipal), or junk-yard and scrap-heap (scrapped metal and machinery). The dust-heap is the oldest, poorest, most human of these: the mound of consumed fires and discarded lives.
