- Middle English (c. 1350–1400) - Coined as sirly / sirlich (from sir + -ly), originally meaning "lordly, haughty, arrogant, imperious, or behaving like a master/sir" with an aristocratic or pretentious connotation.
- 15th to Early 16th Century - Began a classic process of semantic pejoration; the connotation shifted from merely acting high-born to being insufferably domineering, contemptuous, and overbearing toward subordinates.
- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Era) - Underwent phonetic spelling alteration from sirly to surly (partly influenced by confusion with sour); the meaning drifted from haughty arrogance to rude insolence, churlish unfriendliness, and sullen anger.
- 17th to 18th Century - Broadened into literary and meteorological descriptions to denote harsh, menacing, or stormy natural phenomena (such as "surly winter" or "a surly sea" in Shakespeare and Milton).
- 19th Century to Present - Settled into its dominant modern psychological and interpersonal sense: describing an uncooperative, bad-tempered, sullen, and abrasive person or demeanor.
