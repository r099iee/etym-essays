• commentators: Similar because both refer to writers who explain, analyze, and interpret a text; differs because commentator is a broad, common modern term encompassing sports broadcasters, political journalists, and general literary analysts, whereas scholiast is strictly scholarly, historical, and philological, traditionally bound to the specific medium of marginal manuscript notes (*scholia*).
  • annotators: Similar because both compose explanatory notes directly on or alongside a primary text; differs because annotator is a general, neutral functional term applied to anyone adding margin notes, highlighting digital documents, or providing brief editorial apparatus, while scholiast denotes a historical practitioner of learned textual, grammatical, and mythological exegesis.
  • glossators: Similar because both write explanatory notes on difficult manuscripts; differs because glossator (from Greek glossa, "foreign or obsolete word") originally specialized in brief lexical definitions explaining obscure, archaic, or foreign words between the lines or in the margin, especially in medieval Bologna legal schools, whereas scholiasts compose extensive multi-sentence interpretive, historical, and stylistic essays.
  • exegetes: Similar because both provide rigorous, line-by-line interpretation of texts; differs because exegete is an elevated hermeneutical term predominantly reserved for theological, biblical, and sacred scriptural interpretation (*exegesis*), whereas scholiasts historically operated primarily on classical secular poetry, drama, and mythology, as well as philosophical texts.
  • grammarians: Similar because ancient scholiasts were almost always professional teachers of grammar and rhetoric; differs because a grammarian focuses specifically on the formal mechanics of language (syntax, morphology, prosody, and orthography), whereas a scholiast investigates the entire thematic, historical, mythological, and contextual universe of the literature.
  • philologists: Similar because both devote their intellectual lives to the critical study of historical texts and language evolution; differs because a philologist engages in broad linguistic, historical-comparative, and reconstructive science across languages and manuscript trees, whereas a scholiast works as a direct annotator commenting upon a specific author's work.
  • pedants: Similar because *scholiast* has historically been used as a synonym for an excessively meticulous scholar; differs because pedant is entirely pejorative, denoting an annoying, ostentatious bore who flaunts trivial book-learning without understanding real-world relevance, whereas scholiast is primarily an honorable philological title that only acquires pejorative nuance in satirical contexts.
  • lexicographers: Similar because both painstakingly analyze the meanings and archaic uses of words; differs because a lexicographer compiles dictionaries defining words systematically in alphabetical order across an entire language, whereas a scholiast explains words as they appear dynamically in the running lines of a poem or dramatic text.
  • hermeneuticists: Similar because both are engaged in the art and philosophy of textual interpretation; differs because a hermeneuticist studies the abstract theoretical frameworks, methodologies, and epistemological problems of understanding meaning, whereas a scholiast engages in concrete, practical line-by-line textual annotation.
  • textual critics: Similar because both work directly with ancient manuscripts to understand their transmission; differs because a textual critic focuses primarily on establishing an authentic critical text by comparing variant manuscript readings and eliminating scribe errors, whereas a scholiast focuses on explaining and interpreting the meaning of the text.
