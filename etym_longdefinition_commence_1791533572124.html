Commence is an elevated, formal verb meaning to enter upon, start, begin, or initiate an action, transaction, legal proceeding, ceremony, or historical epoch. While virtually synonymous in denotation with the native Germanic verb begin and the common verb start, commence occupies a distinctly higher register within English stylistic hierarchy, carrying connotations of solemnity, procedural formality, administrative authority, or ceremonial dignity.

The core semantic fields and operational usages of commence include:

  • Formal Ceremonies and Liturgical Observances: The word is the standard term used to mark the formal opening of solemn, ritualized, or official gatherings. A commencement address at a university graduation does not merely celebrate completion, but marks the day on which graduates formally "commence" their professional lives and civic responsibilities. Similarly, divine services, inaugural festivities, royal jubilees, and orchestral performances are described as commencing at an appointed hour.
  • Jurisprudence and Statutory Law: In legal drafting, civil procedure, and statutory interpretation, commence functions as a term of art. A lawsuit or legal action does not simply "start"; it is formally "commenced" when a plaintiff files a writ of summons or a bill of complaint with the clerk of the court. Statutes often contain explicit commencement clauses specifying the exact calendar date or royal assent upon which an Act of Parliament or legislative statute commences to have binding force of law.
  • Commercial, Corporate, and Contractual Agreements: Formal contracts, commercial leases, insurance policies, and construction undertakings routinely define the precise date of commencement. The term delineates the exact legal threshold at which liability, insurable interest, tenancy obligations, or commercial covenants take effect.
  • Academic Degree Inception: Historically in medieval and Renaissance universities (especially Paris, Oxford, and Cambridge), commence was the technical verb denoting the formal inception of a scholar taking the degree of Master or Doctor, thereby conferring the license to teach (licentia docendi). This academic origin survives intact today in the North American noun commencement for graduation ceremonies.
  • Military and Strategic Operations: In military protocol, naval despatches, and operational commands, orders are issued to "commence firing," "commence hostilities," or "commence reconnaissance," lending the order an unambiguous procedural authority distinct from the colloquial urgency of "start."

Polysemy, Syntactic Valence, and Semantic Evolution:

Syntactically, commence can function both transitively (taking a direct noun object, such as commence operations or commence proceedings) and intransitively (e.g., the concert commenced at dusk). Like begin, it can take an infinitive complement (commenced to speak), although standard contemporary usage markedly prefers a gerund-participial complement (commenced speaking) or a direct nominal object. In 19th-century American dialectal and colloquial usage, commence to was frequently employed where standard British prose preferred began to, an irony given the word's originally courtly French provenance.

The semantic bridge linking the Latin root initiare ("to initiate into sacred mysteries") to modern administrative usage passed through the Vulgar Latin colloquial prefixation *cominitiare ("to undertake together"). In feudal Norman administration, the verb acquired the prestige of written Chancery records, transforming an everyday Romance verb into a durable English instrument of dignity, officialdom, and institutional ceremony.
