Ruts, the plural of rut in its oldest and most literal sense, are deep grooves or channels worn into soft ground by the repeated passage of wheels — the paired furrows that cart-tracks carve into mud, chalk, and moorland, hardening in summer dust and brimming with winter rain. A single wheel makes a rut; two wheels make the ruts of a lane; ten thousand wheels make the sunken holloways of ancient roads, worn man-deep over centuries. In the source sentence the word is pure landscape poetry: mysterious deep folds discovered in the ground, like the ruts of great giant-wheels — the road itself scaled to mythology, as though gods had driven wagons across the moor and left their tracks in the earth.

The word second great life is psychological and entirely modern: a rut as a fixed, monotonous routine from which one cannot — or will not — escape. To be stuck in a rut is to live by grooves of habit: the same commute, the same quarrels, the same holidays, the wheels of the day turning in hollows they themselves have worn. The bridge from the road to the routine is among the most transparent metaphors in English: just as the cart-wheel, once sunk in its groove, follows it without steering, so the human will, once settled in habit, follows the day without choosing. Self-help literature has made get out of your rut a whole genre, but the image predates it by two centuries and loses none of its force for familiarity.

Core meaning in detail: Physically, ruts form wherever soft ground meets repeated loading: farm gateways, unmetalled lanes, forest rides, construction sites, and the muddy verges of even modern roads. They steer vehicles against the driver will — cyclists fear the tramline rut that grabs the front wheel — and they shape ecology, channelling water, sheltering seedlings, and preserving, in places like the Ridgeway or requisitioned Roman roads, the actual wheel-gauge of antiquity. Engineers distinguish rutting (plastic deformation of the surface) from potholing and corrugation; highway specifications limit permissible rut depth to millimetres. Metaphorically, the rut governs discussions of marriage, career, creativity, and nations: institutional ruts, stuck in a rut, the ruts of custom — always the groove that guides until it imprisons. A necessary warning: English has a second, unrelated rut — the annual sexual season of stags and rams, from Old French ruit, a roaring — which has nothing to do with wheel-grooves and must never be confused with them.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Wheel-worn grooves in the ground (primary literal sense, from the 16th c.): the paired channels of lanes, tracks, and fields — the source sentence sense. The bridge from the Old French road-word is direct: a route travelled becomes a rut incised, the journey carving its own record.
  • A fixed dull routine; a groove of habit (great figurative sense, from the 19th c.): stuck in a rut, the daily rut. The bridge is the steering-wheel image: the cart needs no driver once grooved, and neither, sadly, does the commuter.
  • Routine sexual or reproductive cycling in phrases like the married rut (occasional extended use): domestic life reduced to timetable. The bridge fuses the habit sense with an unconscious echo of the other rut, the animal season — a pun English never quite legislates away.
  • Engineering rutting — measured surface deformation (technical highway sense): the millimetre-graded depression of asphalt under heavy axles. The bridge carries the farm lane into the laboratory: the same physics of repeated loading, now laser-measured and litigated.
  • Fortification and mining grooves — wheel-guides cut deliberately (specialist historical use): the stone-cut ruts of ancient paved ways, the guide-rails of mine waggons. The bridge inverts the accident: where traffic once wore grooves unasked, engineers now cut them on purpose — the rut as ancestor of the railway rail.
  • The animal season homograph — rut as oestrus (separate word, noted for disambiguation): stags in rut, the roaring month of autumn. The bridge must NOT be built: this rut descends from Latin rugitus, a roaring, through French ruit — sound, not road — and shares only spelling with wheel-grooves.

Register and connotation: ruts is earthy and Anglo-Saxon in feel — farmyards, moors, and novels of landscape — even though its pedigree is French. The literal sense is neutral to picturesque: writers love ruts for their antiquity (lane-ruts as history you can walk in) and drivers hate them for their treachery. The figurative sense is reproachful but sympathetic: to tell a friend he is in a rut is criticism edged with concern, and to confess it is the first step of every midlife reinvention narrative. Geology, archaeology, and motoring each keep the literal sense technical and alive, while popular psychology keeps the metaphorical one among the most quoted images of modern discontent.
