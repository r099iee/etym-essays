- [c. 1450–1500: Middle English 'hoise'] - Borrowed from Middle Low German / Middle Dutch nautical terminology ('hisen') as 'hoise', meaning to raise sails or cargo aboard ship.
- [c. 1500–1600: Early Modern English] - Developed an excrescent intrusive '-t' sound at the end of the root (analogous to 'betwixt', 'whilst'), turning 'hoise' into 'hoist'; used extensively in naval commands ('hoist the colours').
- [c. 1600–1700: Shakespearean Era] - Extended to military and proverbial usage, immortalized in Hamlet's phrase 'hoist with his own petard' (blown into the air by one's own bomb, caught in one's own trap).
- [c. 1800–Present: Modern English] - Retains core mechanical/physical lifting sense in maritime, construction, and everyday speech, along with figurative usage for sudden escalation or self-sabotage.
