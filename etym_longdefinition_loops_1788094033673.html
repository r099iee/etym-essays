Loops is the third-person singular present form of the verb to loop, meaning "to bend or curve so as to form a loop; to move in a looping course; to encircle or fasten with a loop." In the sentence "the river loops suddenly east across Dale in front of the ruined town," the word describes the course of a river: instead of flowing straight, the Running River sweeps round in a great bend — a loop of water — and turns east across the plain. The verb belongs to geography here, but the same word drives computing, music, aviation and everyday speech, and its senses all descend from one image: the closed or near-closed curve of a looped cord.

Primary senses — the verb.

  • To form a loop / to bend into a loop: to fold a cord, ribbon, wire or road so that it curves back on itself; "she looped the ribbon through the ring"; "the path loops around the lake".
  • To move in a looping course: of rivers, roads, railways, runners and birds — to travel in a winding or circular course; "the river loops through the plain", "the hawk loops overhead". The context's use is exactly this: the river's course is a great bend, and the verb is chosen because the bend curves so far that it nearly closes on itself.
  • To fasten or encircle with a loop: "to loop the rope around the post" — securing by a looped cord; also in sewing and knitting, "to loop" a stitch.
  • To loop the loop: (of an aircraft or ride) to perform a complete vertical circle in the air; by extension, the roller-coaster inversion — the word enters the vocabulary of thrills from 1903.

Domain senses.

  • Computing: "to loop" — to repeat a sequence of instructions until a condition is met; "the program loops over the list"; a "loop" is the control structure itself (for-loop, while-loop), and "infinite loop" is the famous bug. The programming sense is from the 1940s and is now one of the verb's most common technical uses.
  • Music and audio: "to loop" — to repeat a short passage of sound seamlessly; "tape loops" (1950s–60s electronic music) and "sample loops" in hip-hop and dance production; "looping" is a standard studio technique.
  • Aviation and rides: "loop-the-loop" — the aerobatic manoeuvre and the amusement ride; "to loop" alone covers the manoeuvre in pilots' shorthand.
  • Railways and roads: a "loop line" is a branch that rejoins the main line; a "loop" is a circuit of track or a curved slip-road.
  • Geography and hydrology: an "oxbow loop" is the meander of a river that doubles back on itself; the verb describes the course of any waterway that swings in bends, as in the context.
  • Telecommunications and control: "closed loop" / "open loop" in control theory and feedback systems; "loop" as the circuit of a telephone line ("subscriber loop").

Idioms and figurative uses.

  • In the loop / out of the loop: informed about / excluded from a project or decision — management jargon from the 1970s, the loop being the circle of people who know.
  • Feedback loop: a self-reinforcing or self-correcting cycle in systems thinking, economics and psychology ("a positive feedback loop").
  • Time loop: in fiction, a repeated period of time from which characters cannot escape (popularised by Groundhog Day, 1993).
  • To throw someone for a loop: to surprise or disorient them completely — American, 20th century.
  • To keep someone in the loop: to keep them informed — the standard modern courtesy phrase.

Why the word means all this. The noun loop — a fold of cord that curves back on itself — is the ancestor of every sense. Because a loop is a curve that returns to its starting point, the word naturally came to mean "to make such a curve" (the verb), then "to move in such a curve" (rivers, roads, aircraft), then "to repeat" (because a loop returns to its beginning — the semantic bridge to computing and music), and then "to include in the circle" (in the loop). Each domain — geography, engineering, aviation, programming, music, management — re-borrowed the same image of the returning curve and made it technical, so that today "loops" can describe a river bending east, a program repeating itself, or a DJ cycling a beat, all in perfect consistency with the 14th-century noose the word first named.
