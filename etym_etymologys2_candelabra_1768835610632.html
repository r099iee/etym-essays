The root word candelabra descends from a single PIE root meaning “to shine white-hot”, and its chain runs through Latin to the modern English singular/plural instability.

Stage 1 — PIE *kand-, “to shine, glow, be white-hot” (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE).
Stage 2 — Latin candēre, “to be white-hot, to shine” (attested 2nd–1st century BCE); from it the family candor “whiteness, radiance”, candidus “pure white”, candidātus “white-robed office-seeker”.
Stage 3 — Latin candēla, “a candle” (c. 1st century BCE): literally “a small white flame”, from candēre.
Stage 4 — Latin candelabrum, “branched candle-stand” (1st century BCE): candēla + instrumental suffix -brum (as in arātrum “plough”), literally “instrument for candles”.
Stage 5 — Latin candelabra, the neuter plural of candelabrum (classical form), borrowed into English as a plural in the 16th century.
Stage 6 — Modern English candelabra: reanalysed as a singular in the 19th century (the -a ending read as feminine singular, as in umbrella, arena), giving rise to the new back-formed plural candelabras.

Note on debate/nuance: The reanalysis of candelabra from plural to singular is a textbook case of morphological reanalysis, not a genuine etymology: the classical singular candelabrum continues in formal and technical use, so the word exists in three coexisting forms. The PIE root *kand- is also the source of Sanskrit candrá-, “shining; the moon”, giving Hindi chand and Odia candra — the Indo-Iranian branch of the same root runs to the moon rather than to candles, and the link between the two branches is the shared notion of white-hot shining. The suffix -brum is uncontroversially instrumental; some handbooks note a possible relation to Greek bretas/bránchos but this is speculative and omitted.
