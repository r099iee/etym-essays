- 1580s — a new insult is born: scoundrel appears in Elizabethan England for base, worthless fellows.
- 17th c. — the pulpit and the stage: preachers thunder against scoundrels and playwrights parade them as lying servants and jealous villains.
- 18th c. — politics adopts it: election crowds and pamphlets howl down rival scoundrels; the word becomes standard hustings abuse.
- 19th c. — Dickens and melodrama: scoundrels fill novels and hissable stage villains twirl their moustaches; the affectionate rogue-shade also grows.
- Early 20th c. — slightly dated, still deadly: the word sounds old-fashioned beside gangster and crook, but keeps its moral weight.
- Today — the clean insult: scoundrels survives as the printable, quotable, faintly theatrical denunciation — sharp enough for headlines, safe enough for children.
