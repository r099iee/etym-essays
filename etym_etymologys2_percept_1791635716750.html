Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*per- (through, thoroughly) +
*kap- / *keh₂p- (to grasp, seize, hold)
"to grasp thoroughly, seize completely"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*per-kapiō
"to take completely, seize"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
percipere
"to seize entirely, gather, perceive"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
perceptus (past participle)
"seized, gathered, perceived"
↓
Post-Classical / Scholastic Latin (c. 13th–17th c.)
perceptum (neuter substantive)
"that which is perceived, object of perception"
↓
Modern English (coined c. 1837; popularized 1870s)
percept
"an object or internal mental product
of perception; sensory-cognitive construct"

Note: 'Percept' was deliberately formed in 19th-century academic English by analogy with 'concept' (from Latin 'conceptum', that which is conceived). It was introduced into Scottish common-sense philosophy and metaphysics by Sir William Hamilton in the late 1830s, and subsequently established across psychological science by Herbert Spencer, William James, and C. S. Peirce to denote the subjective mental product of perception as distinguished from both external physical reality and abstract conceptual thought.
