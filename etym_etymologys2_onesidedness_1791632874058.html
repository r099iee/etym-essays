PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*óy-nos + *sēy- / *sī-
"single, unique" + "long, hanging, side"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*ainaz + *sīdǭ
"one, alone" + "flank, side, edge"
↓
Old English (c. 9th c.)
ān + sīde
"one, sole" + "flank, lateral aspect"
↓
Middle English (c. 14th c.)
on / oon + side
"one" + "side, lateral surface"
↓
Early Modern English (17th c.)
one-sided
"having or showing only one side; partial"
↓
Modern English (early 19th c.)
onesidedness
"state of being one-sided; partiality, bias, or unilateral focus"

Note: The abstract nominalisation *onesidedness* (often hyphenated as *one-sidedness*) gained immense intellectual currency in 19th-century British and American letters as a direct loan-translation (calque) of German *Einseitigkeit*, popularized through translations of Goethe, Hegel, and Schiller by Thomas Carlyle.
