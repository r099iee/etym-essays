Damask is a rich fabric — classically of silk, and later of linen, cotton, and wool — in which a decorative pattern is woven directly into the ground weave rather than being printed, embroidered, or applied on top. The weave is turned or reversed: where the ground is woven in satin, the pattern is woven in plain (tabby) weave, and vice versa, so that pattern and ground differ only in how they reflect light. Because the pattern is formed by the threads themselves and not by any added thread, damask is reversible — the design appears in negative on the reverse side — and it is this property, above all, that has made damask, for centuries, the supreme fabric of grand table linen and formal upholstery. One colour, figured by the weave itself, reversible, and originally the famed silk of Damascus: pattern and cloth are one. Primary senses and their boundaries
  • Damask (the fabric) — a figured, single-colour woven textile, classically of silk, in which the pattern is formed by reversing the weave; the standard sense since the fourteenth century.
  • Damask linen / damask napery — table linen (tablecloths, napkins, runners) woven in the same reversible technique; for centuries the standard of formal dining, since a damask tablecloth shows its elaborate design simply by lying on a table.
  • Damask rose (Rosa × damascena) — "the rose of Damascus", a fragrant, repeat-flowering rose of the Syrian plain from which attar of roses and rosewater are distilled; a second borrowing from the same city-name, and the source of a colour name.
  • Damask steel / Damascus steel — the famous patterned high-carbon steel of Syrian blades, in which wavy or "watered" patterns appear in the metal from folded welding; also called damascened steel.
  • To damask (verb) / damascene (verb and adjective) — to weave or ornament with a damask pattern; to inlay steel with gold or silver patterns in the Damascus manner.
  • Damson (plum) — "the plum of Damascus", from Latin damascena; a sister word from the same city-name, crushed into English as damson.
  • Damask (colour) — the deep rose-pink of the damask rose, used in fashion and interiors.
Polysemy and the historical bridge The word is fundamentally a place-name that became a product-name, and its whole family of senses is a chain of metonymies radiating outward from one city. Damascus — one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on earth — was for more than a millennium the western terminus of the Silk Road and a workshop city famed for figured silks, perfumes, roses, and swordsmiths. From the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the city's name was used elliptically in Medieval Latin (damascus), Italian (damasco), and Old French (damas) for the fine figured silk produced there; English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century. Modern technical use has narrowed the fabric sense to a precise weave-definition — single-colour, reversible, pattern in the ground — while the damask rose keeps the colour sense, damask steel keeps the metallurgical one, and the damson plum keeps the fruit. The semantic reasoning for each secondary sense is the same: the thing was associated with Damascus and took the city's name. Register and use in the novel In register, damask is a textile and art-historical term with a long literary life; it carries the fragrance of old houses, grand linen cupboards, and the Silk Road. In the sentence from The Story of the Stone, a smiling maid "came in wearing a dress of red damask and a black silk sleeveless jacket which had scalloped borders of some coloured material." The luxury of the fabric — one of the most expensive of the figured silks, normally reserved for formal occasions — becomes the quiet index of the Jia household's wealth, for even a servant is dressed in pattern-woven silk. Red, the colour of celebration, vitality, and good fortune in Chinese culture, deepens the effect: festival colours are everyday wear, and the maid wears red damask as if it were cotton. For Dai-yu, fresh from her provincial home, the maid's red damask is one more measurement of the gulf between where she has come from and where she now stands. The word collocates with red, green, silk, linen, tablecloth, napkin, curtain, upholstery, napery, and satin, and its near-rivals — brocade (raised, often gold-worked, not reversible), satin (glossy, unpatterned), silk (the material, not the technique), jacquard (the nineteenth-century mechanical loom), tapestry (picture-woven wall-hanging) — all mark the boundary of what damask is: flat, single-colour, reversible, austere, and woven, never printed, never applied.
