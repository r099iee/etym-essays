Part 1 — the root SNAP. The first element of snapdragon is snap, a word whose core is a sudden, sharp bite or crack. Unlike dragon, snap has no grand Indo-European pedigree to recover; it belongs to a different, humbler layer of Germanic vocabulary — the onomatopoeic (sound-imitative) words that mimic the sharp sounds of quick motion: clicking, cracking, snapping, snatching.

Origin. English snap (verb) is first attested in Middle English as snappen, "to bite suddenly, to snatch, to seize quickly" — attested in the 14th century (Chaucer uses the verb of snatching and snapping). The word is not inherited from Old English but borrowed in the Middle English period from the Low German / Middle Dutch word snappen (Middle Low German snappen, Middle Dutch snappen "to snap, to seize, to bite"), where it is attested from the 13th–15th centuries. These Low German and Dutch forms are themselves of imitative origin — the sound of jaws closing, or of a sharp crack. The modern Germanic cognates confirm the family: Dutch snappen, German schnappen "to catch, seize, snap" — all built on the same snapping sound.

Semantic development in English. From "to bite suddenly" the verb extends in several directions:

  • Physical snap — "to break with a sharp crack" (a branch snaps, a rope snaps) — attested from the 17th century.
  • Quick motion — "to move quickly with a clicking or cracking sound" (a trap snaps shut, fingers snap).
  • To snatch — "to seize suddenly" (to snap up a bargain).
  • Verbal snap — "to speak sharply or irritably" ("she snapped at him").
  • Noun snap — the sharp crack or click; a sudden bite; a photograph ("to take a snap", from the 1890s, from the quick shutter action); the card-game snap.

The snap- element in compounds. English uses snap freely as a first element in folk compounds: snapdragon (the flower), snap-bean, snap-brim (a hat), snapshot, snap-bolt, snap-fastener. In the plant name the element is descriptive: the flower, pinched at the sides, springs open and shut like a jaw — it "snaps". The imitative origin remains transparent in the modern word: you can hear the snap in the motion of the blossom.

Attestation. Middle English snappen (14th c.); the plant name snapdragon is attested from the 16th century (the herbals of the 1550s–1560s record the name for Antirrhinum), and the parlor-game sense (snatching raisins from burning brandy) from the 17th century, with a famous allusion in Shakespeare's Love's Labour's Lost. The sound-symbolism of sn- (compare snip, snatch, snack, snare, sneeze) makes the word feel inevitable: English has a whole family of sn- words for sharp sounds and rapid mouth-and-nose actions, and snap is among its most typical members.

Summary. Snap is a borrowed-onomatopoeic Germanic word — Middle English snappen from Middle Low German / Middle Dutch snappen, imitative of a sudden bite or crack, meaning in modern English a sudden sharp action of breaking, biting, clicking, or seizing, and serving in SNAPdragons as the descriptive element for a flower that opens and shuts like a mouth.

Part 2 — the root DRAGON. The second element is the oldest word in the compound and one of the best-documented loan-words in European history. Dragon travels from Greek through Latin into every Romance and Germanic language, carrying with it the image of the great serpent.

Stage one: Greek. The Greek noun is drákōn (δράκων), genitive drákontos, "serpent, dragon, huge snake" — attested from Homer onward (the Iliad and Odyssey use drákōn for monstrous serpents). The traditional etymology connects it to the Greek verb dérkomai (δέρκομαι), "to see, to look with a keen and glittering eye", so that drákōn is literally "the seeing one" or "the sharp-sighted one" — the snake that stares, the watcher. This derivation, though ancient and widely repeated, is not completely secure: the connection to dérkomai is generally accepted but has been questioned, and some scholars allow the possibility of a pre-Greek substrate origin. The safest statement is that drákōn is Greek, traditionally explained as "the staring one", with the exact verbal derivation debated.

Stage two: Latin. Latin borrowed the word as dracō, dracōnis, "a serpent, a dragon" — attested in classical prose (Pliny's Natural History describes dracōnes) and in the Vulgate Bible, where dracō translates the serpent of the Apocalypse. The Latin form keeps the Greek genitive stem (dracōnis), and the Romance forms all descend from it.

  • Greek — drákōn "serpent, dragon" (Homer, 8th c. BCE), traditionally from dérkomai "to see, stare" (derivation debated).
  • Latin — dracō "serpent, dragon" (classical; Vulgate).
  • Old French — dragon (from Latin accusative dracōnem).
  • Middle English — dragoun / dragon (c. 1200; Chaucer).
  • Old English — draca "dragon", an earlier direct loan from Latin (in Beowulf, the treasure-guardian is a draca).

Stage three: into English — twice. English actually received the word twice. Old English borrowed it directly from Latin as draca "dragon, serpent, sea-monster" (in Beowulf, the treasure-hoard guardian is a draca); the modern drake in the sense of "dragon" descends from that Old English form. But the standard modern word dragon came through Old French in the Middle English period: Middle English dragoun (also dragun, dragon), attested from about 1200, from Old French dragon, from Latin dracō. Chaucer uses dragon (of the constellation Draco), and the word is fully naturalized by the 14th century.

Semantics in English. In Old English draca and Middle English dragon the word covers the classical monster-snake and the biblical serpent of evil; with the rise of chivalric romance it becomes the fire-breathing, treasure-guarding beast of legend — the dragon St George fights, the worm that holds the hoard. In Tolkien the word is native heritage: his dragons (Smaug, Glaurung) are the direct literary descendants of Beowulf's draca, and "dragon" is exactly the Middle English-derived word the modern reader meets.

Sound change. Greek drákōn → Latin dracō → (via accusative dracōnem) Old French dragon → Middle English dragoun / dragon → modern dragon /ˈdræɡən/. The spelling settled as dragon in the 16th century.

Summary. Dragon is the classical serpent-word — Greek drákōn (traditionally "the staring one", from dérkomai "to see"), borrowed by Latin as dracō, passed into English twice (Old English draca, and Middle English dragoun via Old French), and meaning in modern English the mythical winged serpent of legend and fantasy — the second element of SNAPdragons.

The compound: snap + dragon. The plant Antirrhinum got its English name from its two-lipped corolla, which opens and shuts when pinched: it "snaps" like a little mouth, and the mouth belongs to a dragon. The name is attested from the 16th century (the herbalist John Gerard uses "snapdragon" for Antirrhinum), and it is a model of folk-botanical naming — transparent, playful, memorable. The same image produced the learned scientific name Antirrhinum from Greek anti- "counter, like" + rhís / rhinós "nose" — "counter-nose", snout-like — a parallel recognition of the same facial metaphor in classical dress.

The parlor-game snapdragon (snatching raisins from a bowl of burning brandy, the "dragon" being the flame) extends the same pair of images: the snatch is the "snap", the fire is the "dragon". The game is attested from the 17th century and is famously alluded to by Shakespeare in Love's Labour's Lost.

In Tolkien, the word returns to pure imagery: Old Took's fireworks "used to go up like great lilies and snapdragons and laburnums of fire". The garden flowers become shapes of exploding light — the bloom's silhouette painted in fire against the twilight — and the plural gathers all the blossoms of a midsummer border into one pyrotechnic catalogue. The compound's transparency is exactly what lets the metaphor work: a snapdragon is already a mouth-like blossom, and in firework form it is a dragon of flame that snaps open in the sky.
