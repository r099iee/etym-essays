The story of strained is the story of a squeeze that travelled from the wine-press to the foreign ministry. It opens with the Proto-Indo-European root *streig-, a hand-word meaning to stroke, rub, and press — the steady pressure of the palm that, hardened one way, gives stiffness and, hardened another, gives force. Latin took this pressure and made it muscular: stringere, to draw tight, to bind, to press together — the verb of bowstrings, of bandages, of throats gripped — with its past participle strictus destined for a whole English family of its own (strict, stricture, constrict).

Through France to the English press: Old French estreindre — to wring, to squeeze, to embrace forcefully — crossed with the Conquest and became Middle English streinen around 1300, a verb of many pressures: to draw a rope tight, to press grapes and cloth, to force a confession, to exert oneself. The past participle strained froze each of these into a state, and the states then went their separate ways.

  • The taut state: first and most literal — strained ropes, strained canvas, ears strained toward sound — the pull still visibly on, the form English keeps in sea shanties and, lovingly, in the hobbits' night listening.
  • The injured state (16th c.): sports, labour, and childbed gave surgeons the strained muscle, back, and groin — fibres pulled past comfort — and the word entered the permanent vocabulary of pain, later refined by the strict medical split between strain (muscle-tendon) and sprain (ligament).
  • The filtered state: pressing liquid through cloth or mesh made strained the cook's word — strained broth, strained honey, strained yoghurt — clarity achieved by pressure, a sense so domestic it rarely meets its rope-hauling twin.
  • The social state (19th–20th c.): novels and newspapers stretched the participle over human bonds — strained relations, strained hospitality, a strained atmosphere — until diplomacy could speak of nothing else; the taut rope had become the standard instrument for measuring how close a friendship is to snapping.
  • Sound and spelling: Middle English streinen simplified its diphthong to modern /streɪn/, spelled strain, with the regular -ed participle pronounced /d/ after the nasal — strained /streɪnd/ — a spelling that generations of schoolchildren have confused with stained, to teachers' despair.
  • Literary witnesses: Shakespeare strains courtesy and throats; the Authorised Version strains at gnats; Austen strains sensibility; and Tolkien, with the hobbits' strained ears above the clop of ponies' hoofs, gave the perceptual sense its most quoted night scene.

What unites the athlete's hamstring, the cook's broth, and the ambassador's relations is one unbroken image of pressure applied — drawn tight, wrung out, stretched thin. Few participles have worked so many trades while keeping the feel of the rope in every one of them.
