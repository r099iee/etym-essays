PROWLING — the patient, hungry circle of the hunter At its plainest, PROWLING is the present participle of the verb prowl: to move about quietly and stealthily in search of prey or plunder — roving with a predator's patient, hungry vigilance rather than with any settled destination. The word always implies three things together: motion (the prowler does not stand still), stealth (the motion is meant to escape notice), and purpose (there is something being sought — prey, loot, a victim, an opportunity). Remove any one of the three and the word dissolves: a march is motion with a destination, a loaf is motion without purpose, a leap is motion without stealth. SENSE 1 — intransitive: to rove stealthily in search. The core sense, attested since the 14th century: to prowl about, to go prowling, of wolves, cats, thieves, and hungry creatures. The prowler casts for anything, moving about (as Tolkien's phrase stresses) rather than closing on a fixed target. SENSE 2 — transitive: to prowl (an area). From 1580: to go stealthily over a stretch of ground in search of prey or plunder — a wolf prowls the forest, a burglar prowls the streets, a patrol prowls the perimeter. The area itself becomes the object of the verb: to prowl the neighborhood, to prowl the corridors. SENSE 3 — the noun: a prowl, on the prowl. The noun names the act — a prowl — and the fixed phrase on the prowl (1803) means engaged in seeking prey, at first of beasts and burglars and later extended to anyone hunting anything. By the twentieth century on the prowl became a standard euphemism for a person, typically a man, looking for sexual conquest — the predator's hunt recast as social pursuit. SENSE 4 — the agent: prowler. A prowler (1510s) is one who prowls. In law-enforcement and crime reporting the word specializes: a prowler is a person lurking or moving about a property with criminal intent, especially by night. From this comes the American police jargon prowl car — a patrol car, a car that "prowls" a beat — and the verb to prowl in police dispatches. SENSE 5 — figurative and extended uses. The verb reaches beyond the literal hunt: a disease prowls a community, a rumor prowls the corridors of power, a virus prowls the network. To prowl the web is to search the internet opportunistically, drifting from page to page in search of something — the hunting circle updated for the information age. A predator "prowling" for victims keeps the full force of the literal sense. POLYSEMY SUMMARY. Prowling is roving, opportunistic hunting with no fixed quarry: literal big-cat patience, furtive burglary, night-time human predation, and — figuratively — any restless, stealthy, purposeful search. The word's constant elements across every sense are motion, stealth, and an undirected hunger for something not yet found. IN THE HOBBIT. Tolkien writes of Gollum: "Not a gourmet, but he liked a bit of fish now and then, or a goblin if he could catch one; he liked them raw and he liked them fresh, so he went prowling about." The comic understatement ("not a gourmet") first strikes an almost domestic, affectionate note; but the word prowling instantly dispels the cosiness. It pictures Gollum as a furtive, beast-like hunter slinking along the shores of his dark underground lake — alert, silent, endlessly patient, seeking prey that can be killed and eaten on the spot. The rawness and freshness he demands are the appetites of a wild animal, and prowling embodies the constant, unending hunger that keeps him moving through black water and echoing rock. The tone is grim comedy shading into menace. NEAR-SYNONYM BOUNDARIES (the stealthy-motion field):
  • Lurk: to stay still and concealed in ambush, not moving in search — prowling is steady, restless motion.
  • Skulk: to move or hide with guilt or fear, cowering from notice, often after something shameful — prowling is a predator's confident hunger.
  • Slink: to move in a sinuous, ashamed, or abject gait, a body trying to pass unseen — prowling is purposeful quartering, not cringing withdrawal.
  • Stalk: to approach one identified quarry deliberately, stalking is focused and directional — prowling roves about opportunistically, casting for anything.
  • Rove / roam / wander: to move without fixed destination and without stealth — prowling adds stealth and a hunting purpose.
  • Patrol: to move over an area systematically on a fixed beat, openly and by duty — prowling is covert and prey-seeking.
  • Range: of animals, to move over a territory in search of food — prowling stresses the silent, hunting vigilance rather than the territory.
  • Sneak / creep: to move furtively to avoid notice — prowling adds the active, patient search for prey or plunder.
