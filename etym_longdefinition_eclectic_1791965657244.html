Eclectic is an adjective, and less often a noun, describing something assembled by selection from a wide range of sources rather than conforming to a single school, style, or system. In the source passage Cicero is “dismissed as a shallow eclectic,” and the word is doing precise work: an eclectic is a thinker who picks doctrines out of different philosophies, and “shallow” adds the judgement that the picking has no principle behind it. The noun is older than the adjective in this philosophical sense, and both descend from a Greek verb meaning “to pick out.”

1. The adjective: drawn from many sources

In ordinary modern use eclectic praises or describes a breadth of reference. An eclectic taste in music ranges across genres; an eclectic library mixes poetry, science, and travel; an eclectic menu borrows dishes from several culinary traditions. The word implies deliberate choice rather than mere mixture: the eclectic person has looked at many options and taken what seemed worth taking.

  • Core lexicographic wording: “drawing on or deriving ideas, style, or taste from a broad and diverse range of sources”; “composed of elements drawn from various sources.”
  • Typical constructions: eclectic taste, eclectic style, eclectic mix, eclectic approach, eclectic range, eclectic collection; “an eclectic blend of.”
  • Degree: neutral to positive in most modern contexts; the positive charge comes from the implied open-mindedness and discrimination of the selector.
  • Grammar: attributive or predicative (“her taste is eclectic”), and gradable (“more eclectic,” “rather eclectic”).

2. The noun: a person who selects from systems

As a noun, eclectic denotes a person, especially a philosopher, who adopts doctrines from several schools instead of adhering to one. This is the oldest English use and the one in the source passage. It can be neutral in the history of philosophy and disparaging in polemic: to call someone an eclectic may mean that they are open-minded, or that they have no system of their own. The adjective “shallow” in the source makes the second reading explicit.

  • Neutral use: “the eclectics of Alexandria” — philosophers of the eclectic school.
  • Disparaging use: “a shallow eclectic” — a borrower without a coherent position.
  • Modern extension: “an eclectic” can also mean a person of varied tastes, especially in music, food, or design.

3. Philosophy: the Eclectic school and Cicero

The philosophical label goes back to the Hellenistic age. Diogenes Laertius records an “Eclectic sect” founded by Potamo of Alexandria, who chose doctrines from every school, and the label was later applied to thinkers of the Roman period who combined Platonism, Aristotelianism, Stoicism, and Scepticism. Cicero is a central case: trained in the Academic tradition, he draws freely on Stoic ethics, Peripatetic psychology, and Epicurean arguments, while presenting himself as an Academic sceptic. Whether that makes him an eclectic or a sceptic is a standing question in Ciceronian scholarship, and the source passage’s dismissal as “a shallow eclectic” belongs to the older, hostile side of that debate.

  • Potamo of Alexandria — 1st c. BCE, traditionally named as the founder of the eclectic sect.
  • Alexandrian eclecticism — the attempt to reconcile Plato and Aristotle, later important for Neoplatonism.
  • Roman eclecticism — the practical habit of taking the most persuasive position from each school, characteristic of Cicero’s philosophical works.
  • Modern scholarship: “eclecticism” is now often a neutral description of method rather than a verdict on originality.

4. Art, architecture, and design

The nineteenth century gave the word its most visible application. Eclecticism in architecture names the deliberate use of historical styles — Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, Egyptian — chosen for effect rather than derived from a single tradition. Garnier’s Paris Opéra, with its Beaux-Arts façade and Baroque interior, is a standard example, and Victorian and Edwardian builders produced an entire landscape of eclectic façades. In interior design the adjective is now wholly familiar: an eclectic room mixes periods, colours, and textures, and the skill lies in making the mixture look intentional.

  • Architectural Eclecticism: a nineteenth-century movement of style-recombination, sometimes called historicism.
  • Interior design: “eclectic style” mixes old and new, high and low, with an emphasis on curation.
  • Art history: eclectic can be neutral or mildly pejorative, implying derivation rather than invention.
  • Music and fashion: eclectic taste signals range and curiosity, and is usually a compliment.

5. Technical and institutional uses

The word has been adopted by several professions. In American medicine, “eclectic medicine” was a nineteenth-century movement, founded by Wooster Beach, that combined botanical remedies with regular medicine and trained practitioners at institutions such as the Eclectic Medical Institute in Cincinnati. In education, McGuffey’s Eclectic Readers took their name from the selection of extracts from many authors, and modern language teaching speaks of an “eclectic approach” that combines elements of several methods. In international business, Dunning’s “eclectic paradigm” combines ownership, location, and internalisation advantages into one framework.

  • Eclectic medicine: a botanically oriented American sect of the 19th c., now historical.
  • Eclectic Readers: McGuffey’s schoolbooks (from the 1830s), named for their selected extracts.
  • Eclectic approach: in language teaching, a principled combination of methods rather than loyalty to one.
  • Eclectic paradigm: in international business, a unified explanation of why firms invest abroad.

6. The semantic bridge: selection as virtue and as vice

Everything follows from the Greek verb “to pick out.” Picking can be praised as discrimination or blamed as mere borrowing; the same act looks like independence when the selector has a principle and like opportunism when he does not. The history of the word is a history of that ambiguity. In philosophy, where systems are supposed to be coherent, eclecticism is suspect. In art and design, where novelty comes from recombination, it is a virtue. In ordinary life, it usually means simply “wide-ranging,” and the question of coherence rarely arises.

7. Register, connotation, and company

  • Connotation: open-minded, cosmopolitan, and curated when used of taste; derivative, inconsistent, and magpie-like when used of thought.
  • Common collocates: taste, style, mix, approach, collection, range, blend, palette; “a remarkably eclectic,” “a deliberately eclectic.”
  • Related nouns: eclecticism (the practice or quality), eclectically (the manner).
  • What to avoid: eclectic is not a synonym for random; the word implies selection, and a random mixture is merely miscellaneous.

8. In this reading

The source passage’s phrase “a shallow eclectic” uses the noun in its oldest and most hostile sense. Cicero is not being praised for range; he is being accused of assembling a philosophy out of other people’s parts and calling it his own. The adjective “shallow” measures the depth of the selection, and the sentence quietly assumes that a thinker ought to have a system rather than a sampler. Reading the word’s Greek etymology sharpens the charge: an eclectic is someone who picks out, and the question is always whether the picking has a principle.
