CHAIN: FROM LATIN TO JEST (6 attested stages — PIE stage uncertain)

  1. Proto-Indo-European source uncertain: Latin gerere "to carry, do, perform" has no securely reconstructed PIE etymon. Older popular sources give *ger- "to carry"; a connection with PIE *h₂eħ- "to drive, lead" (cf. Greek ágein "to lead", Latin agere) has been proposed but is rejected or doubted by de Vaan and other modern authorities, who treat the deeper origin as unknown.
  2. Latin gerō, gerere "to carry, bear, do, perform" (attested from early Latin, 3rd–2nd c. BCE; classical usage bellum gerere "to wage war", gerere magistratum "to hold office").
  3. Latin gestus, past participle of gerere, "carried, performed"; neuter plural gesta "deeds, exploits, achievements" (attested from the 1st c. BCE; title-type of Gesta Romanorum "Deeds of the Romans", a medieval compilation).
  4. Old French geste "deeds, exploits; a story of exploits, an epic narrative" (attested from the 11th–12th c.; the chanson de geste "song of heroic deeds").
  5. Middle English geste, jeste "a tale, story, narrative; a romance of deeds" (attested from c. 1300).
  6. English jest — the comic sense "a joke, witty remark" first attested in the 16th century as the heroic sense weakened; the noun and verb form the modern word → JEST.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The PIE ancestry of Latin gerere is unknown; modern handbooks (de Vaan) treat it as etymologically opaque, so the chain is reliably attested only from Latin onward.
  • The shift of the English word from "a tale of deeds" to "a joke" (documented across the 15th–16th centuries) is a semantic change within English itself, not a change of form.
