Immediately after the gullies come the dark ravines, and the contrast is the whole point: where the gullies could 'almost' be leapt and yet were 'very deep', the ravines defeat both possible responses at once — 'one could neither jump over nor climb into' them. The adjective dark matters as much as the noun: sunlight never reaches the bottom of these gorges, so they read as vertical cracks of shadow in the mountain flank, walls too steep to descend and too far apart to cross. In scale they are the next order of landform up from the gully — a ravine is essentially a gully grown steep, wide, and sheer — and the narrator deploys them to escalate the Company's journey from difficult to nearly impossible. The tone is laconic and final, the understated voice of the tale recording absolute blockage without exaggeration: the travellers do not even attempt these obstacles; they know at a glance that neither strategy exists. Ravines thus name the point at which wild geography stops being a path and becomes a wall.

Polysemy & secondary senses. The noun ravine and its plural ravines have a small but real range:

  • Core geographical sense: a deep, narrow, steep-sided gorge or cleft worn in the earth, typically by torrential water; larger and more impassable than a gully, smaller and steeper-walled than a canyon — the sense here.
  • Historical French-influenced senses: in older English, 'ravine' (or 'ravin') could mean a violent rush of water or a raging flood (recorded 1610, now obsolete); the word came into English twice, once in the greedy sense of plunder (from French ravine 'rapine'), once in the water sense.
  • Related greedy senses: 'ravenous', 'ravening', and the verb 'to raven' (to prey greedily) descend from the same French stock, as does 'rapine' — the plundering family of the same root.
  • Technical/domain uses: in geology and geomorphology, 'ravine' is standard for a steep-sided, water-cut erosional channel, between a gully and a canyon in scale; in civil engineering and fire management, 'ravine' names terrain features that channel water or fire and constrain access.
  • Metaphoric extension: by analogy, 'ravine' can name any deep, impassable gap or division (a ravine between classes, between generations), though this is far rarer than the physical sense.

Nuance against near-synonyms. Against its synonyms the word distinguishes itself precisely. A gully is water-carved but modest, often leapable, as here; a gorge is wider and usually carries a stream along its floor; a canyon is grander still and carries American Southwestern associations (from Spanish cañón, 'tube'); a chasm is a broad rift, more geological than water-made; a defile implies a narrow pass that armies must traverse single-file; and a crevasse is strictly a fissure in a glacier. Ravine occupies the useful middle ground — a deep, steep-walled gorge cut by a torrent, wide enough to bar crossing, sheer enough to bar descent — which is precisely the double impossibility the passage insists upon, and the word's own violent history (it descends from Latin rapere, 'to seize') gives the sheer walls their sense of having been torn, not merely carved, out of the rock.
