- 14th c. "Jacques Bonhomme & peasant simplicity" - French bonhomme ("good man") emerges as peasant archetype; initially feudal condescension, later evolving into the concept of a good-hearted, simple fellow.
- 17th c. "emergence of French bonhomie" - French dictionaries codify bonhomie as unaffected good-nature, candor, and genial simplicity without artifice.
- Early 19th c. "English borrowing" - British essayists (Hazlitt, Scott, Thackeray) import bonhomie into English to capture a warm, clubbable, unpretentious geniality that English lacked a single word for.
- Late 19th c. "Victorian social & diplomatic staple" - widely used in high society and journalism for the expansive cheer of gentlemen hosts, clubmen, and politicians.
- Early 20th c. "comic Wodehousian usage" - P.G. Wodehouse and modern humorists use it affectionately or ironically for unexpected waves of good feeling toward uncongenial characters or creatures.
- Present "cosmopolitan mark of easy warmth" - universally recognized in literate English for genuine or diplomatic affability and genial goodwill.
