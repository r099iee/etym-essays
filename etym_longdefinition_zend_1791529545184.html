Zend (/zɛnd/) is an archaic English proper noun with two related uses. Properly it denotes the Zand, the Middle Persian exegesis, glosses and translation of the Avesta, the sacred corpus of Zoroastrianism. From the eighteenth century onward, European scholars also used Zend as the name of the Avestan language itself, the language in which the Avesta is composed. The second use is now obsolete in linguistics, where Avestan is standard, but it survives in older titles and in the compound Zend-Avesta.

1. The proper sense: commentary. Middle Persian zand means "interpretation, explanation, commentary". In the Zoroastrian tradition, the Avesta was transmitted in an archaic language that later priests no longer spoke; the Zand supplied a translation into Middle Persian together with glosses and exegesis. Manuscripts often present Avestan text and Zand together, and the compound Zand-Avesta reflects this pairing. The Zand is thus a commentary tradition, not a separate scripture, and it is the ancestor of the later Pahlavi books that preserve Zoroastrian doctrine, law and cosmology.

2. The dated sense: the language. European travellers and scholars of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries encountered the Zand-Avesta and assumed that Zend was the name of the language. The usage was reinforced by Anquetil-Duperron's celebrated translation, Zend-Avesta (1771), and by nineteenth-century Orientalists who spoke of "the Zend language" and "Zend philology". Writers such as William Dwight Whitney in 1867 could describe the language of the Zoroastrian writings as "usually called the Zend" and note that it was also styled Avestan or Old Bactrian. Modern scholarship restricts the name to the commentary and calls the language Avestan.

3. The Avesta and its language. The Avesta is the scripture of Zoroastrianism, attributed to the prophet Zarathushtra (Zoroaster). Its oldest layer, the Gathas, is composed in Old Avestan; the Younger Avesta, including the Yasna, Visperad, Vendidad and the Khordeh Avesta, is in a later form of the same language. Avestan is an Old Iranian language, closely related to Old Persian (the language of the Achaemenid royal inscriptions) and, more distantly, to Vedic Sanskrit. It is not Middle Persian, the language of the Zand, and it is not modern Persian.

4. Related terms.

  • Zand: the Middle Persian commentary and translation; the correct technical term.
  • Zend-Avesta: the Avesta together with its Zand, and by extension the European title for the corpus.
  • Avestan: the modern linguistic name for the language.
  • Pahlavi: Middle Persian, the language of the Zand and of the later Zoroastrian books.
  • Zendik / zindiq: a related Middle Persian and Arabic term for a heretic or freethinker, historically connected with zand.

5. The semantic bridge. The word's history is a chain of metonymies: "knowledge" becomes "interpretation", "interpretation" becomes the commentary corpus, the commentary is bound with the scripture, and European readers transfer the name of the commentary to the language of the scripture. The final step is a learned mistake, but a productive one: it gave English a familiar label for Old Iranian before the modern terminology settled. The correction, Avestan, is a nineteenth-century achievement of comparative philology.

6. Modern usage and nuance. In contemporary scholarship Zend is archaic and should be avoided except in historical quotations or in the fixed title Zend-Avesta. It is not a synonym for Old Persian, and it is not the name of the Avesta itself. The Theosophical movement and popular occult literature kept the word alive in the twentieth century, often with little regard for Iranian philology. For a modern reader, the safest gloss is: Zend = the Zand commentary; Avestan = the language; Avesta = the scripture.

7. The Zoroastrian context. Zoroastrianism is one of the oldest continuously attested religions, and its scriptures are central to the linguistic history of Iranian. The Avesta survives in a relatively small corpus, much of it liturgical; the Gathas are dense, archaic hymns, while the Younger Avesta contains ritual instructions, hymns, legal and purity rules, and cosmological material. The Zand grew up as a scholarly and priestly apparatus for understanding this inherited text. Pahlavi literature of the ninth and tenth centuries, including the Denkard and the Bundahishn, preserves and expands this exegetical tradition. To call the language "Zend" is therefore to confuse the commentary with the text, a confusion that early European scholarship inherited and later corrected.

8. Historiographical significance. The career of Zend is a case study in Orientalist philology. Anquetil-Duperron's journey to India and his 1771 publication made the Zoroastrian scriptures available in Europe and helped launch the comparative study of Iranian languages. The term Zend travelled with the texts, and for a time it was unavoidable. As the decipherment of Old Persian and the comparative study of Sanskrit advanced, scholars such as Eugène Burnouf and Martin Haug clarified the relationship between Avestan, Old Persian and Middle Persian. The older label then became a historical curiosity, useful mainly for reading nineteenth-century scholarship. The modern preference for Avestan reflects both linguistic accuracy and the wider professionalization of Iranian studies.
