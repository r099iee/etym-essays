EVOLUTION OF PERCH / PERCHED: FROM A FARMER'S ROD TO THE EAGLE'S LEDGE The story of perched begins not with a bird but with a rod. The oldest attested ancestor is the Latin noun pertica, recorded from the third century BCE, meaning 'a long staff, pole, or rod' - the implement of the surveyor, the vintner, and the labourer, used to measure land and to support vines and trellises. Crucially, pertica is a word whose deeper ancestry is unknown. Modern etymologists - most notably de Vaan in his etymological dictionary of Latin - record no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon, and various guesses linking the word to roots of stretching or reaching have failed to win acceptance. The word appears to be a piece of Italic stock whose ultimate origin is simply lost. What matters for English is not the deeper root but the shape of the word as it entered the Romance languages: in Gallo-Romance, Latin pertica became Old French perche, a word that meant both the measuring rod and, increasingly, the horizontal bar on which birds were set. The birth of the bird's perch The semantic key to the whole later history is the farmyard and the falconer's mews. The perche of Old French was the bar on which hawks were set to rest between flights and on which domestic fowls roosted at night - a pole with a creature on it. That conjunction of rod and bird is the hinge of the word's meaning: a perch is not merely a pole but a pole whose whole purpose is to hold a resting bird. When the word entered English in the thirteenth century as perche, it did so as this ordinary word of the farm and the mews, naming the beam or branch where birds sat, with no elevation of tone at all. The verb followed almost at once: perchen, recorded around 1300, meant 'to set or rest on a perch', and it was used first of fowls and falcons, carrying the bird's own point of view - settling on a narrow, elevated support and gripping it with the feet. The generalisation of the word (14th-17th centuries) From the fourteenth century the verb began to leave the bird-house. Perch and its participles extended from fowls to people (to sit on a raised seat or bench), then to things set at a height (a candle perched on a candlestick, a man perched on a wall), and finally to whole buildings and places (houses and castles perched on crags, villages perched on cliffsides). By the early modern period the word had acquired its permanent charge of elevation, poise, and faint precariousness: the thing perched is high, and it is balanced on a narrow support. Alongside this generalisation, the noun developed its secondary senses. The surveyor's rod gave English the land-measure: one perch (also called a rod or pole) is five and a half yards, and the unit is still used in British land surveying today. In the old theatres a 'perch' was a cheap high gallery seat. And from the nineteenth century the word produced its status idiom: a person perched at the top of an institution sits high and might be 'knocked off their perch', the phrase used to mean the destruction of arrogant superiority. The modern word and its register In modern English, perched is an extremely productive participle that has become a staple of naturalist, travel, and landscape writing. It collocates with the vocabulary of height - perched on a branch, atop a hill, on the edge of a cliff - and it always gives a scene a sense of height and balance. In ornithology it is the ordinary word for a bird's resting posture; in architecture and travel prose it describes buildings in exposed positions; in the literature of the sublime it lends picturesqueness to heights. The word never lost the two ideas in its Latin core: the narrow support and the resting creature. In Tolkien's The Hobbit that double heritage is deployed to precise effect: the eagle is perched close by, a participle that sets the great bird settled and at ease on its ledge while the hobbit scrambles upright, anxious and off balance. The calm altitude of the perched bird against the flustered motion of the hobbit is the whole meaning of the scene, and it is carried by a single word that had travelled from a Roman surveyor's rod to the eagle's eyrie over more than two thousand years.
