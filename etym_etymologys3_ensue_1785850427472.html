ONE OF THE GREAT ROOTS OF FOLLOWING

Ensue belongs to one of the most productive roots in all of Indo-European: *sekʷ-, "to follow". The root is attested across the whole family — Sanskrit sácate "accompanies, follows", Greek hépomai "I follow", Latin sequī "to follow" — and from Latin sequī and its compounds English has drawn an enormous vocabulary: sequence, sequel, consequent, execute (from exsequī, "to follow out, carry out"), prosecute (from prosequī, "to follow forward"), pursue (from prosequī via Old French), suit and suite (things that follow), sect (those who follow), and obsequious (following compliantly). The root is about movement in the wake of something — a follower keeping pace behind — and nearly every one of its descendants is a story of following turned into sequence, consequence, or imitation.

LATIN: FROM FOLLOWING TO PURSUIT

Latin sequī was a deponent verb — active in meaning, passive in form — meaning "to follow, pursue, come after". Its compound insequī, made with in- in the sense of "on, upon, after", meant "to follow closely, to pursue, to attack": the image is the pursuer pressing hard on the heels of a fleeing enemy, close enough to strike. Classical writers use insequī of chasing enemies, of pursuing advantages, of misfortunes that dog a man — a word of active, often hostile following. It is this concrete picture of pursuit, not the calm order of a queue, that stands behind the English word.

THE FRENCH BRIDGE AND THE LOGICAL IDIOM

In the spoken Latin of late antiquity the deponent verb was regularized on the active model — a reconstructed *insequere — and from it Old French formed ensuivre, "to follow after, to result", attested from the eleventh to thirteenth centuries. French developed a characteristic logical idiom out of the word: il s'ensuit que, "it follows that", the standard way in French philosophical and legal prose to state a deduction. English borrowed the verb in the fourteenth century as ensuen, recorded in Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde (about 1374), where it still carries both meanings of its French and Latin ancestors: to pursue a person, and to come after an event. For two centuries the two senses coexisted in English — a man could ensue (pursue) an enemy, and a result could ensue (follow) from a cause.

THE NARROWING TO CONSEQUENCE

From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the active pursuit sense dropped away, and ensue settled into its modern, impersonal form: an intransitive verb used of events that follow in time or result from a cause. The shift is exactly the shift from the concrete to the abstract: from chasing a person to following an event, and finally to being the consequence of an event. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries ensue was the standard literary verb for "happen afterward", used above all of conflicts, debates, panics, and public events — "a great silence ensued", "battle ensued", "confusion ensued" — and it remains a fixture of narrative prose. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries it has become a familiar journalistic formula: "chaos ensued", "hilarity ensued", "a furious row ensued" — two words that sum up a whole aftermath, often with the deadpan irony of "hilarity ensued", which modern usage has made a catchphrase for any situation that collapses into comedy.

SOUND AND REGISTER

Phonetically the word has worn its history into a compact form: Latin insequī → Vulgar Latin *insequere → Old French ensuivre (the compound's prefix en- replacing the Latin in- in its French form) → Anglo-French ensuer, with the -v- of the French infinitive lost in the English borrowing → Middle English ensuen → modern /ɪnˈsjuː/, with the initial vowel reduced to a weak syllable and the -s- pronounced with a following yod. In register, ensue is formal and impersonal: it has no subject who acts, only events that follow — which is precisely its value in the card's sentence, where the failure of the grid produces chaos without any person needing to do the producing. The word remains the most economical way English has to say "happen afterward as a consequence", a compressed verb carrying the whole idea of cause and sequence.
