The story of “unmerited” begins at the moment a harvest or a spoil is divided. The Proto-Indo-European root *(s)mer- meant “to get a share, to allot, to earn” — the primal scene of distributive justice, in which portions are dealt out and each person receives what falls to him. In Greek the root produced méromai, “to receive a share”, and its great noun meros, “part, share”, still visible in modern chemical terminology (isomer, polymer, monomer — compounds built of “parts”); and from the same root Greek made the haunting moîra, “portion, lot, fate”, the allotted destiny of every mortal. The Moirai, the three Fates of Greek religion, were literally “the Apportioners” — Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos, the goddesses who dealt out to each person the portion he could neither refuse nor outrun. Some scholars have even connected the same base with Latin memor, “mindful”, and Greek mérinma, “care”, through the idea of keeping one’s allotted portion in mind — a disputed but suggestive link that shows how deeply the notion of the share reaches into the earliest Indo-European worldview.
  • Latin merēre: in Latin the root produced merēre, “to deserve, to earn, to obtain as one’s due share” — a verb of soldierly service as well as of worth, since merere stipendia meant “to serve, to do military service”, earning one’s pay and one’s due. Its past participle meritus, “deserved”, generated meritum, “what one has earned, a deserved reward, worth”, and also “a service done, a kindness”.
  • The commercial cousin: the same share-root produced mercēs, “wages, pay”, and merx, “merchandise” — what is acquired by exchange — and hence market, merchant, commerce, mercantile, and mercenary (mercenarius, “hired, serving for pay”). The equation of the “share” root with the “trade” root is traditional and generally accepted, though not beyond dispute.
  • Old French and Middle English: Latin meritum passed into Old French as merite, “reward, worth, desert”, and into Middle English about 1200 as merite — at first a theological word, the spiritual reward earned by good works in the medieval economy of salvation, where merit and grace were weighed against each other in the accounts of the soul.
From this religious root modern “merit” grew: the noun, “earned worth”, in the thirteenth century; the verb, “to deserve”, soon after; and then, by the simple addition of the negative prefix un-, “not”, the past participle “unmerited”, “not deserved”. The word therefore carries to this day an ethical and quasi-legal weight: merit is a debt paid by justice, and what is unmerited is what justice does not owe. In the theology of the Reformation the concept became central — unmerited grace, the favour freely given that no work could earn — and from that exalted religious register the word stepped down into ordinary moral prose, weighing praise and blame, reward and punishment, against a scale of desert. In the passage, “not unmerited” uses the word with deliberate litotes: the books have earned their rest, and the double negative both concedes their worth and smiles at the ceremony of the concession. Modern use remains formal and judicial, at home in literary criticism, courtesy, and ethical discussion, and rare in everyday speech — a word that is signalled whenever a writer is weighing a claim of desert, exactly as this sentence does.
