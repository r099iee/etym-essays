Resplendent begins in light. Its root, the reconstructed PIE *splend-, "to be manifest, to shine, gleam," carries a meaning far older than ornament: the primal experience of a thing becoming visible, flashing out of darkness. Handbooks dispute the root's exact shape — *splend-, *splnd-, and *spel- all appear — but its semantics are secure, a sudden, glowing manifestation, and they survive in the Sanskrit sister root sphul-, "to throb, quiver, burst forth, sparkle," whose noun sphuliṅga (whence Hindi sphulinga, "a spark") preserves the image of a point of light bursting into view. The equation of sphul- with *splend- is debated in the handbooks — the initial clusters fit imperfectly — but the family likeness of meaning is unmistakable, and the spark is a good emblem for the whole family's sense of sudden, manifest brilliance. In Latin the root settled into splendēre, "to shine," a verb of steady, high-status brilliance used of polished metal, firelight, and reputation alike. From it Latin built the adjectival splendidus, "brilliant, renowned" — the ancestor of English splendid, borrowed in the seventeenth century — and the noun splendor, "radiance, glory," the ancestor of English splendour, borrowed in the fifteenth. Already in Latin the shining had been metaphorized: a man of splendor was a man of honor, his renown a light, and splendidus could describe both a polished surface and an eminent reputation. The word thus entered Roman moral and political vocabulary as naturally as it entered Roman optics; shine, status, and worth were the same light. The intensifying sense that gives us resplendent enters with the prefix re-. Latin resplendēre means "to shine back, to glow again" — not merely to emit light but to return it, to throw radiance outward in response to being seen. It is exactly the verb for a surface that dazzles because it reflects: gilded beams, enameled rafters, polished gold, a jewel catching the candle. Late Latin's present participle, resplendēns, with its stem resplendent-, passed into Old French as the vivid verb resplendir ("to shine brightly," whence French resplendissant), and English took the adjective resplendent around 1480, in the ornate courtly vocabulary of the late Middle Ages. The word arrived already aristocratic: fifteenth-century English used it of royal robes, heraldic display, and the heavenly light of saints, and its register never descended from that ceremonial height. Meanwhile the plain root continued to shine beside it — splendid, splendour, and the Romance splendere / splendido (Italian), espléndido (Spanish) all descend from the same Latin verb and keep their luminous prestige. In English the adjective's career has been remarkably stable. Through the Renaissance and the age of heraldry it described the glitter of courts and the glory of heaven; in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the Romantics and the Victorians applied it to natural spectacle — resplendent sunsets, resplendent autumn forests — and to the sumptuous interiors of palaces and cathedrals. Its grammar settled into the characteristic "resplendent with + material" construction, a phrasing that foregrounds the source of the glow: the building in Dream of the Red Chamber is resplendent with carved and painted beams and rafters, and the translation uses the word precisely because that construction carries the weight of a great house's crafted light. In the modern period the word also entered the fixed vocabulary of natural science, most famously in the name of the resplendent quetzal, the iridescent bird of Central America whose feathers gave the adjective a permanent home in ornithology. No regular sound change complicated its passage into English: it was a learned borrowing straight from the Latin participial stem, and its spelling and pronunciation have stayed close to the original. What has never changed is the register — resplendent remains a formal, ceremonial word, reserved for brilliance that is staged, ornamented, and returned to the eye. From a spark in Indo-European prehistory to the painted rafters of the Rong-guo mansion, it has always meant light that shines back.
