- c. 1200–1300 — Ecclesiastical entrance: sepulcre enters Middle English via Anglo-French from Latin, predominantly denoting the Holy Sepulchre of Jerusalem in chivalric crusader and pilgrim chronicles.
- Late 14th c. — Biblical and liturgical codification: Wycliffe’s Bible and parish drama employ the word for both Christ’s burial cave and the North-wall Easter sepulchre used during Holy Week.
- 16th c. — Orthographic classicization: The spelling solidifies with an archaizing, Greek-mimicking -ch- (sepulchre), and the King James Bible (1611) forever cements the moral idiom of the ‘whited sepulchre’ for religious hypocrites.
- 17th c. — Jacobean tragedy and poetry: Shakespeare, Webster, and Milton use both the noun and verb to evoke majestic decay, vaulted cathedral gloom, and the enshrinement of memory.
- 18th c. — Moral essayists: Samuel Johnson and contemporaries deploy sepulchre as an ironic contrast between the generous marble monuments lavished on the senseless dead and the envy directed at the living.
- 19th c. — Gothic and Romantic literature: Poe, Byron, and Tennyson use sepulchre (and the adjective sepulchral) to summon damp subterranean horror, gothic dread, and spectral sorrow.
- Today — Elevated literary and theological usage: Preserved in historical scholarship, liturgical contexts, and figurative prose denoting deceptive exteriors hiding inner rot.
