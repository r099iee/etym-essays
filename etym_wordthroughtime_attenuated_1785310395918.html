- [16th century: physical thinning] - Borrowed from Latin attenuare ('to make thin'); the first English senses were bodily — to thin, emaciate, or slenderise a person or substance.
- [17th-18th century: rhetorical and medical extension] - Applied in criticism to the weakening of an argument's force and in medicine to thinning the humours; the core idea of reduction-by-thinning spread outward into figurative use.
- [19th-20th century: the hard sciences] - Entered physics and engineering (the attenuation of a signal as it travels) and microbiology (attenuated vaccines, a pathogen weakened yet still immunising); it became a precise technical word for graded reduction.
- [Contemporary sense] - A general descriptive word for anything reduced in force, intensity, magnitude, or thickness without being destroyed — from sensory-gating capacity to sound to a diluted strain — almost always carrying an implied loss of strength.
