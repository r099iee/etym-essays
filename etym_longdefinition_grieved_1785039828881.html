**GRIEVED** (verb, past tense and past participle of *grieve*) — felt or expressed deep sorrow, distress, or mental anguish; to have been burdened, in body or spirit, by grief or oppression. The word is distinctive in naming both the inward feeling (one grieves for a loss) and its cause (it grieves one that something is so), and in stressing the quiet, weighty endurance of pain rather than its outward display. **1. Primary sense.** To grieve is to feel sorrow so heavy that it presses the spirit down — the word's root is the Latin for 'heavy.' Grieved describes the person who carries that weight: mourning a death, lamenting a loss, sorrowing over ruin or cruelty. It is quieter than any outcry; grief is the private, dignified heaviness of a heart burdened by what it remembers. **2. In The Hobbit (Ch. III, 'A Short Rest').** The word appears in the scene where Elrond examines Thrór's map. The narrator records that although Elrond 'did not altogether approve of dwarves and their love of gold,' he 'hated dragons and their cruel wickedness, and he grieved to remember the ruin of the town of Dale and its merry bells, and the burned banks of the bright River Running.' The nuance is delicate: Elrond's grief is not self-pity but a noble, long-memoried sorrow — moral and elegiac at once. The construction 'grieved to remember' shows grief ignited by memory, and its objects are the small, homely joys that dragons destroy: merry bells and the bright, living river now burned. The word's weight does the narrative work of establishing Elrond's compassion and his hatred of cruelty, aligning him with Bilbo's pity and setting him apart from the dwarves' mere love of gold. **3. Polysemy — full range of senses.**
  • Intransitive: to feel grief. One grieves for, over, or about a loss — most commonly the death of a loved one. Modern usage also includes 'grief work,' the psychological process of adjusting to loss, so that grieving is understood as an active, healing labour rather than a mere state.
  • Transitive (older, now rarer): to cause grief. 'It grieves me to say' — the impersonal construction in which grief is caused. This preserves the word's oldest meaning, 'to burden, oppress, weigh down,' from Old French grever.
  • Noun forms: grief, grievance, grievous. The family includes grief (the feeling), grievance (a cause for complaint, especially in law and the workplace), and grievous (heavily injurious or atrocious). Note the shift: grief internalizes, grievance externalizes — a grievance is a wrong one brings against another.
  • Physical and archaic senses. In Middle English, to grieve could mean to press down physically or to oppress; a 'grievous burden' is a heavy one. The physical sense died out in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as the emotional sense took over.
**4. Boundaries against near-synonyms.**
  • Mourn: to perform grief publicly and ritually, with ceremony and outward display; grieving is inward and can be entirely private.
  • Lament: to voice sorrow loudly or formally, even as a genre of poetry; grieving need not be voiced at all.
  • Bewail: archaic, passionate outcry.
  • Sorrow: a state of sadness rather than the active process of feeling it.
  • Regret: far milder, and concerned with one's own choices or lost opportunities rather than with losses one did not cause.
  • Aggrieved: feeling wronged or injured, with a strong sense of injustice; grieve focuses on sorrow rather than on the demand for redress.
**5. Register and connotation.** Grieve is dignified and inward; it is the word of solemn remembrance, elegy, and the modern language of loss. In summary, grieved means having felt or expressed deep, weighty sorrow — a word that has travelled from the physical burden of oppression to the private endurance of loss, and that in Tolkien's prose carries the quiet compassion of an elf who mourns a town he never saw.
