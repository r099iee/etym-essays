- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1380) - Formed as *deuly* or *dewely* by attaching the adverbial suffix *-ly* to the adjective *dewe* (borrowed from Anglo-Norman *du* / Old French *deu*, 'owed'); originally denoted acting in accordance with moral duty, religious righteousness, or feudal obligation.
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor & Chancery English) - Rapidly institutionalized within English common law and parliamentary rolls; acquired specialized juridical currency in formulas such as *duly summoned*, *duly elected*, and *duly executed*, signifying full legal validity.
- 17th to 18th Century (Augustan Bureaucracy & Enlightenment Jurisprudence) - Solidified in commercial contracts, official diplomacy, and public registers; expanded temporally to signify punctuality (*in due course*, *duly delivered*), while developing formulaic usage in epistolary manners.
- 19th Century (Victorian Administrative Prose & Literary Irony) - Prevalent in official court gazettes, civil service minutes, and narrative fiction (Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde); frequently employed with dry satirical nuance to highlight empty bureaucratic rituals or predictable follies.
- 20th to 21st Century - Maintained with absolute precision in legal drafting, corporate governance, and parliamentary procedure; colloquially popularized in the ubiquitous administrative acknowledgement *duly noted*, which often carries mild comedic or resigned irony.
