- Late 15th c. — Physical and civil retirement: Enters English prose from Latin recedere, describing individuals physically retiring from an assembly, court, or hostile confrontation.
- 16th c. — Territorial and political concession: Applied in diplomatic chronicles and treaties to armies receding from disputed frontier borders and litigants receding from contested claims.
- 17th c. — Astronomical and hydraulic observation: Natural philosophers employ it for the rhythmic movement of oceanic tides receding from shores and heavenly bodies receding in apparent orbit.
- 18th c. — Aesthetic perspective and color theory: Landscape painters and critics adopt the term for vanishing parallel lines, receding pictorial planes, and cool background tones.
- 19th c. — Victorian romantic and temporal reflection: John Ruskin and essayists deploy it metaphorically for departed epochs, youthful ideals, and memories that culminate in radiance as they recede into the past.
- 20th–21st c. — Medical, psychological, and macroeconomic ubiquity: Standardized in colloquial speech for receding hairlines, while remaining indispensable in economic journalism for receding inflation and receding geopolitical crises.
