- Late 16th c. — Physical & spatial borrowing: borrowed into Early Modern English from Middle French insistence and Latin insistentia, initially carrying traces of its physical etymological sense of 'standing firmly upon or resting weight upon' a surface or position.
- 17th c. — Rhetorical & discursive consolidation: shifted rapidly into intellectual, theological, and legal discourse to denote dwelling emphatically upon a subject in speech or writing, or refusing to drop an argumentative point during debate.
- 18th c. — Social etiquette & polite suasion: widely adopted in polite Georgian literature (such as the novels of Samuel Richardson and Jane Austen) to describe affectionate, pressing social hospitality (e.g., urging a guest to stay or dine, where courteous compliance followed gentle insistence).
- 19th c. — Moral reform & programmatic conviction: employed heavily in Victorian political speeches, philosophical tracts, and essays (Carlyle, Ruskin, Mill) to express uncompromising moral and programmatic imperatives for societal improvement.
- 20th–21st c. — Epistemological, critical & institutional usage: standard in academic critique (e.g. an author’s insistence on a specific methodology), institutional compliance (regulatory insistence on standards), and interpersonal psychology.
