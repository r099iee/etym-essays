braggart is a countable noun for a habitual boaster — someone who talks up his own deeds, courage, wealth or descent, usually louder and more often than the facts warrant. The word is always pejorative and usually a little theatrical: a braggart is not simply a man who mentions his achievements, but a man who performs them. In the source passage it stands at the centre of the composite insult "a vainglorious braggart", where vainglorious supplies the diagnosis (the glory is empty) and braggart supplies the behaviour (the boasting is loud and repetitive).

Core sense. "A boastful person; one given to vain and ostentatious self-praise." The noun implies habit, not a single lapse: anyone may boast once, but a braggart does it as a way of being. It also implies an audience — the braggart needs listeners, and his talk is aimed at producing admiration rather than conveying information. And it implies doubt: to call a man a braggart is normally to suggest that the claims are inflated, if not false.

Senses, uses and grammatical behaviour

  • The plain noun: "he was a braggart and a bully", "a loud-mouthed braggart", "the village braggart". This is the dominant modern use.
  • Attributive use: braggart can stand before a noun as a quasi-adjective — "a braggart soldier", "braggart captains", "braggart nationalism". The attributive use is slightly archaic but common in literary prose.
  • The stage type: the braggart soldier is a recognised dramatic role, descended from the miles gloriosus of Roman comedy and the Capitano of the commedia dell'arte: a swaggering officer who claims impossible victories and is exposed as a coward.
  • Predicative and collective extensions: whole institutions can be called braggarts by transference — "a nation of braggarts" — though the usage is rhetorical rather than standard.
  • Adjectival relatives: bragging (the activity), braggy (informal, chiefly American), braggartly (rare, archaic). The noun itself has no verb: one does not "braggart", one brags.

Why the word carries contempt. The contempt is built into its morphology. Braggart contains the suffix -ard, the same depreciatory element found in coward, drunkard, dullard, sluggard, wizard and bastard. Historically this suffix descends from a Germanic element meaning "hard, bold" (German -hart), but in French and English it specialised in labelling people by a vice or defect, often with a sneer. A bragard in French was thus already a type rather than an individual: the man defined by his bragging. English inherited that sneer intact.

Register and connotation. The word is informal-to-literary rather than technical. It is stronger than boaster and weaker than liar: it accuses exaggeration and vanity, not necessarily outright fabrication. It has a slightly old-fashioned, almost Shakespearean ring, which is why it survives best in historical writing, satire and political invective. Natural collocates are loud, vain, drunken, empty, swaggering, military, and the stock phrase braggart soldier.

In this reading. The charge of bragging was aimed at Cicero's habit of celebrating his own consulship. He wrote an epic poem on the subject, De consulatu suo, and one of its lines — "O fortunatam natam me consule Romam" — became a byword for self-congratulation and was still being mocked by rhetoricians and satirists a century later. Roman critics could forgive eloquence but not self-praise, and the figure of the braggart, inherited from comedy, was ready-made for the purpose. In the biography from which this passage comes, the accusation is listed among the stock charges of Cicero's detractors, alongside vacillation, cowardice and intellectual pretension.

Modern life. Braggart remains current but has narrowed: it is used mostly of men, mostly in print, and almost always with a touch of comedy or antiquarian relish. Social media has produced a whole vocabulary of newer, sharper terms — show-off, blowhard, flexer — and braggart survives as the literary, slightly period alternative, the word a novelist reaches for when the character belongs to another century.
