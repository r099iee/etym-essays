• Scaevola: Similar because it is the cognomen by which the man is usually shortened in modern prose, and it names the same jurist; differs because alone it can equally denote the legendary hero or the Pontifex, so it is less precise than the full name.
  • Quintus Mucius Scaevola: Similar because it is the fuller Roman form of the same name, adding the praenomen; differs in that the praenomen identifies an individual within the family, while the two-element form names the family branch.
  • the Augur: Similar because it is the standard disambiguating title for the man meant here; differs because it denotes an office — membership of the augural college — not a name, and could be applied to many other Romans.
  • the Pontifex: Similar because it is the parallel title of his cousin, the other great jurist of the family; differs because it marks a different man, a different priesthood and a different career, and using it of the Augur would be an error.
  • jurist: Similar because it describes what Scaevola was, the greatest lawyer of his day; differs because it is a common noun for any legal expert and carries none of the family's prestige.
  • jurisconsult: Similar because it renders the Roman idea of the iuris consultus, the aristocratic expert who gave legal opinions; differs in being an archaic, technical English term, and in emphasising the advisory rather than the teaching role.
  • Mucius: Similar because it is the family name that identifies the gens; differs because it is shared by every Mucius and therefore cannot pick out this individual.
  • Cicero: Similar because the two names are bound together in the history of Roman law and appear side by side in this reading; differs because Cicero was the pupil and advocate, not the jurist, and his name became a byword for eloquence rather than for legal science.
