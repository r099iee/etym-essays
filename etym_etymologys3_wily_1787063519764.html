The history of wily is the history of a single Germanic word for “craft, trick, device” passing into English, acquiring the adjectival suffix -y, and slowly shifting from a relatively neutral sense of “skilled” to a distinctly pejorative sense of “deceitfully cunning” — a trajectory that mirrors the changing moral evaluation of cleverness in English-speaking culture.

Origins in the Germanic world. The noun wile is first attested in late Old English as wīl, meaning “stratagem, trick, cunning device”. The word is generally thought to be cognate with Old Norse vél (also written vǽl), which meant “craft, art, artifice” and, significantly, “machine, engine” — a revealing semantic range, since an “engine” is literally a device or contrivance, and a “wile” is a mental contrivance. The same root appears in Old Frisian wīl and Old High German wīla. These point to a Proto-Germanic form *wīlō, “a device, a trick”, whose deeper Indo-European ancestry is disputed: some scholars seek a link to an Indo-European root meaning “to turn, wind, weave” (the idea being that a wile “weaves” a plot), but no confident PIE etymon can be reconstructed with certainty. This uncertainty must be stated plainly: the ultimate origin of the Germanic word is obscure, and the Old Norse form may itself have been influenced by, or have influenced, neighbouring northern languages.

Borrowing into English. English shows the word both as a native Old English item and as a Scandinavian-influenced form. During the Viking Age, Old Norse vél circulated in the Danelaw, and scholars debate whether English wile is a direct Old English survival, a borrowing from Norse, or a hybrid of both. Whatever the precise path, by the Middle English period wile was firmly established, appearing in romance, religious and didactic literature as “a trick, a guileful device”. Chaucer uses it, and the phrase “wiles” to denote deceitful stratagems becomes common, especially in religious writing where the “wiles of the devil” are a favourite theme.

The adjectival development. The adjective wily (Middle English wili) is a straightforward derivation: noun wile + suffix -y (“full of, characterized by”). It is attested from the early 13th century, initially meaning “full of wiles; crafty, cunning”. The word is used throughout the medieval and early modern periods in ways that today would be considered relatively neutral or even admiring: a “wily” king or knight is often simply a clever one who outwits enemies through stratagem rather than force — a quality celebrated in the heroic tradition. The Trojan hero Odysseus is the archetype of this admired craftiness, and medieval romance consistently pairs “wily” with “wise”.

Renaissance and Shakespeare. By the Elizabethan period the moral colouring had darkened. Shakespeare uses wily and wiles repeatedly, nearly always in contexts of deceit and entrapment: the “wily” speaker is a schemer. Yet the word retains a theatrical glamour: the wily villain is also the most interesting character on stage. This dual perception — cleverness as both admired and suspect — has remained attached to the word ever since, and it is this very tension that makes it so useful to storytellers.

Seventeenth to nineteenth centuries. During the 17th–19th centuries, wily settled into its modern pejorative register. It became a stock adjective in fables and didactic literature (the wily fox), in adventure fiction, and in political writing where “wily” politicians were contrasted with honest ones. The noun wiliness and adverb wilily appear, and dictionaries from Johnson (1755) onward record the now-familiar senses: “cunning, artful, crafty”. In this period the word lost most of its heroic neutrality and became the property of moralists, fabulists and novelists describing characters of doubtful honesty.

Modern usage. In contemporary English, wily retains a slightly literary, even archaic flavour. It is used of foxes, veterans, politicians, negotiators and fictional tricksters; it can shade from outright condemnation (“a wily con man”) to grudging admiration (“a wily old campaigner”). It is not a high-frequency word in colloquial speech, but it survives robustly in prose, journalism and popular culture, largely unchanged in meaning for the past three hundred years — a rare stability for a word whose moral connotations were, for centuries, in motion.
