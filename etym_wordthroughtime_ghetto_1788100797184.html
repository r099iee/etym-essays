- 1516 Venice - a place-name for the foundry island to which Jews were legally confined; within years it becomes a common noun for the Jewish quarter.
- 16th–18th c. Europe - the obligatory, walled Jewish quarters of Rome, Frankfurt, and Prague; segregation sanctioned by papal and civic law.
- 19th c. England - travel narrative and fiction bring 'ghetto' into English for the Jewish districts of continental cities; mildly exotic, not yet slang.
- 1939–44 - Nazi-occupied Europe turns the ghetto into an instrument of genocide (Warsaw, Łódź); the word acquires its darkest load.
- 1940s–1960s America - sociologists (Wirth, Drake & Cayton, Clark) extend the word to segregated Black urban neighbourhoods; it denotes structural racism.
- 1970s–90s hip-hop era - 'ghetto' shifts from imposed to claimed: ghetto fabulous, ghetto blaster; pride and critique interwoven.
- Today - the slang sense ('cheap, improvised, rundown') is widely judged offensive; careful speakers reserve the word for segregated districts and historic events.
