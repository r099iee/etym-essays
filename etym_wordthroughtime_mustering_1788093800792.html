- Late 14th century (c. 1380–1400) — mustren enters English from Old French mostrer ‘to show’: at first it simply means to show or display, and a mustre is a show, a display, an exhibition — completely neutral.
- 15th century — the word locks onto its military orbit: a muster becomes the formal show-and-count of troops (muster rolls, mustering the garrison); both noun and verb specialise in soldiers assembled for inspection — the semantic core is ‘putting on display to be checked’.
- 16th century — ‘pass muster’ (by the 1570s) makes the inspection sense idiomatic: to pass muster is to survive scrutiny; the word start standing for standards and accountability rather than mere display.
- 17th–18th centuries — expansion into the inner life: mustering courage, strength, arguments — the figurative gathering of personal resources enters the literary language; muster gains a psychological register alongside the drill-ground one.
- 19th century — colonial and American extensions: Australian stockmen ‘muster’ cattle out of the bush (a muster is a round-up and head-count); the US Civil War gives ‘mustering out’ (formal discharge from service) — the word now covers both assembling and disbanding, both people and animals.
- 20th–21st centuries — safety culture rebrands the word: muster stations, muster points and muster drills on ships, rigs and factory floors make ‘mustering’ the everyday word for emergency gather-and-count; ‘mustering support/opposition’ is a staple of political prose — while the old drill-ground solemnity has quietly drained away in all but the most martial contexts.
