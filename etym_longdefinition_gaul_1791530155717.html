Gaul is the English name for the ancient Celtic world of western continental Europe — above all the territory that became modern France — and, by extension, for the peoples who lived there. It works chiefly as a noun ("Caesar conquered Gaul"; "a Gaul of the Arverni") and only occasionally as an attributive, because English normally prefers Gallic or Gaulish in that role. A single syllable therefore carries three superimposed maps: a Roman administrative map, a Celtic ethnographic map, and a modern literary map on which "Gaul" survives as a slightly ironical synonym for France.

Primary sense: the Roman territory. In classical usage Gallia was not one fixed country but a family of Roman designations, and English "Gaul" inherits that looseness. The word can denote the whole transalpine landmass bounded by the Rhine, the Alps, the Pyrenees and the Atlantic, or the much smaller province that Rome actually governed, or even northern Italy. The three most important Roman divisions were:

  • Gallia Cisalpina — "Gaul this side of the Alps", the Po valley of northern Italy, conquered by the Romans in the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE and eventually absorbed into Italia. English "Cisalpine Gaul" preserves the phrase.
  • Gallia Transalpina, later Gallia Narbonensis — "Gaul beyond the Alps", the Mediterranean strip from the Pyrenees to the Alps, organised as a province from 121 BCE and known simply as Provincia, the ancestor of the name Provence.
  • Gallia Comata — "long-haired Gaul", the free interior that Caesar subdued between 58 and 50 BCE and that was afterwards reorganised into Aquitania, Belgica and Lugdunensis.

Ethnographic sense: the Gauls as a Celtic people. "Gaul" also names a population rather than a place. The Gauls were speakers of continental Celtic languages, bearers of the La Tène iron-working culture, builders of fortified oppida, and practitioners of a learned druidic religion that fascinated and alarmed Roman observers. In English the plural "the Gauls" is the ordinary way to refer to them, and the word is often used interchangeably with "the Celts". The two are not identical: Celtic is a linguistic and archaeological family label covering Ireland, Britain, Iberia and Anatolia as well, whereas Gaul is a Roman geographic-ethnic category focused on the Rhine–Alps–Pyrenees block. The most famous Gaul in English is Vercingetorix, the Arvernian leader who united the tribes in 52 BCE and surrendered at Alesia.

Linguistic sense. Gaulish is the extinct continental Celtic language attested in inscriptions from the 6th century BCE to roughly the 4th century CE, written in Greek, Etruscan and Latin alphabets; the Coligny calendar and the Larzac tablet are its best-known monuments. It survives mostly in proper names, in loanwords into French, and in the substrate influence debated by Romance linguists. The adjective Gallic covers both the ancient people and, by transfer, anything characteristically French; the prefix Gallo- builds terms such as Gallo-Roman, Gallo-Romance, Gallo-Italic, Gallophobia and Gallomania.

Secondary and transferred senses. Because Gaul was the classical name of France, English writers from the Renaissance onward used it as a decorative or ironical substitute for "France", and "a Gaul" for "a Frenchman". The tone is usually mock-heroic or gently teasing rather than neutral. The opening sentence of Caesar's De Bello Gallico — Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres, "All Gaul is divided into three parts" — has become a humorous formula for any awkward tripartite arrangement. A further transferred sense lives in French rather than English: gaulois means "bawdy, ribald, earthily frank", a stereotype of Gallic plain-speaking that produced the English phrase "Gallic humour".

Technical and scientific by-forms (careful distinctions).

  • Gallium, element 31, was discovered in 1875 by Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran and named from Latin Gallia, "Gaul" — with a probable secondary pun on Latin gallus, "rooster", echoing his own surname (le coq). It is a genuine derivative of the country name.
  • Gallic acid is unrelated to Gaul: it comes from Latin galla, "oak apple, gall", the growth on oaks from which the acid was first isolated. The homography is a trap for etymologists.
  • Gall (bile; bitterness; to chafe) descends from Old English gealla and is not connected with Gaul at all, despite identical pronunciation in Received Pronunciation.
  • Galatia in Anatolia takes its name from the same Celtic ethnonym family (Galatai), but through Greek rather than Latin, and refers to a different settlement of Celtic-speaking invaders.

Modern and popular-culture senses. Since 1959 the Asterix albums of René Goscinny and Albert Uderzo have made "Gaul" a comic brand: the indomitable village, the potion, the Roman camp. French schoolchildren still learn "Nos ancêtres les Gaulois" ("our ancestors the Gauls"), a formula of Third Republic civic mythology, and the statue of Vercingetorix at Alesia (1865) turned the defeated chieftain into a national icon under Napoleon III. In contemporary English, "Gaul" is rare outside historical writing, translation, tourism and humour; its everyday work has been taken over by "France", "French" and "Celtic".

Register and usage note. "Gaul" is a learned, bookish word. Calling a modern French person "a Gaul" is marked: either playful, archaic, or faintly disparaging. In historical prose it is neutral and standard. The derived adjective "Gallic" is fully naturalised and idiomatic ("Gallic shrug", "Gallic wit"), whereas "Gaulish" is strictly archaeological and linguistic.
