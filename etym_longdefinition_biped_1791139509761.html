A biped is, in its core modern sense, any animal that habitually moves on two feet, with humans, birds, and many extinct dinosaurs as the canonical examples. As a noun it denotes the creature itself; as an adjective (often biped or more commonly bipedal) it describes the two-footed mode of locomotion, posture, or anatomy. The word belongs primarily to zoology, anatomy, palaeontology, and robotics, but it also carries a literary and pejorative life in which “the biped” means man in a detached, zoological, sometimes contemptuous way — exactly as in the source passage where man is called “this marauding biped.”

In biology the term is functional rather than taxonomic: it does not name a clade but a locomotor strategy. Obligate bipeds such as humans, ostriches, and kangaroos cannot move efficiently any other way, while facultative bipeds such as lizards, bears, or chimpanzees can walk on two legs briefly but normally use four. Human bipedalism — habitual upright striding with a vaulting gait, arched feet, adducted big toe, S-curved spine, and enlarged gluteal muscles — is treated as a defining hominin adaptation linked to freeing the hands, endurance travel, thermoregulation, and encephalization. In palaeontology “biped” versus “quadruped” helps reconstruct theropod dinosaurs, hadrosaurs, early hominins from footprints and pelvis morphology, and the famous transition from Ardipithecus to Australopithecus to Homo.

Beyond pure biology the word extends by analogy. In robotics and science fiction a biped is a two-legged walking machine — Honda ASIMO, Boston Dynamics Atlas, or fictional walkers — distinguished from quadruped robots, hexapods, wheeled rovers, and drones. In furniture, surveying, and photography an older secondary sense appears: a two-legged stand or support, contrasted with a tripod, though this is now rare and usually replaced by “bipod” for guns and stands. In colloquial or satirical writing “biped” can mean simply a person, often featherless, clumsy, or destructive, echoing Plato’s jest that man is a “featherless biped.” This is the bridge to the insult use: by reducing a person to locomotor anatomy the speaker strips away reason, culture, and soul.

  • Obligate two-footed animal (noun, primary): a creature whose normal locomotion uses two hind limbs, e.g. “Humans are the only surviving obligate bipeds among primates.” This is the textbook zoological sense and the anchor for all others.
  • Human being viewed zoologically / pejoratively (noun, literary): “marauding biped,” “naked biped,” “clumsy biped.” Similar core — a two-legged human — but shifted in register from neutral description to detachment, irony, or misanthropy. Historical bridge: Enlightenment natural history classified man alongside animals, making “biped” a levelling term.
  • Two-footed / bipedal (adjective): “biped motion,” “biped dinosaurs.” Meaning “having two feet.” Still found in older texts; modern science prefers bipedal. Bridge: direct conversion from Latin bipes adjective to English noun-adjective.
  • Bipedal robot / machine (noun, robotics): a humanoid or bird-like walker on two actuated legs. Shares the two-legged locomotor schema; differs because the “animal” is artificial and balance must be engineered rather than evolved. Emerged in the late 20th century with humanoid robotics.
  • Two-legged support / stand (noun, rare / obsolete): a prop with two legs. Shares only the “two-footed” morphology applied to objects. Now largely displaced by bipod, but attested in 19th-century technical writing.
  • Facultative biped (noun, specialist): an animal capable of short bipedal bursts, e.g. basilisks, cockroaches, gibbons. Extends the primary sense by relaxing “habitual” to “occasional,” important in biomechanics and evolution debates about how bipedalism originates.
  • Mythic / speculative biped (noun, extended): aliens, cryptids, or fantasy races described as bipeds versus quadrupeds or polypods. Shares locomotor description; differs in that the referent may be imaginary. Common in exobiology (“carbon-based bipeds”) and games.

Grammatically, biped is countable (“bipeds”), takes bipedal as its relational adjective, and pairs with bipedalism for the trait and bipedality for the condition. Collocations include “obligate biped,” “habitual biped,” “featherless biped,” “upright biped,” and “biped gait.” Register is formal-scientific as a noun for animals and robots, literary-ironic for humans. To call someone “a biped” in ordinary conversation is odd unless joking or scolding, because the neutral everyday word would be “person”; choosing “biped” deliberately animalizes. That precise distancing force explains the source sentence: quadrupeds, centipedes, bacilli will be freed “from this marauding biped” — man reduced to one more fauna, marauding with weapons and careless feet.
