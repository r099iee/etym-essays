- Medieval — the garden root: turnip feeds peasants from small plots while field names open arable; no compound yet.
- 16th–17th c. — the compound appears: enclosed turnip patches near towns gain the name turnip-field on the model of cornfield.
- 18th c. — Townshend revolution: the turnip-field becomes the famous fourth course of Norfolk husbandry, abolishing fallow and fattening winter cattle.
- 19th c. — landscape and insult: novelists paint huge autumn turnip-fields while slang pities turnip-heads, the crop honouring and mocking the country together.
- 20th c. — roots retreat: tractors, cereals, and silage shrink the turnip acreage, and the word turns nostalgic, a Hardy and Tolkien marker of old mixed farming.
- Today — heritage and fodder: turnip-fields survive as sheep keep, game cover, and table specialities, the compound smelling of damp autumn earth and honest husbandry.
