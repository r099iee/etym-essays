pang is a wholly Germanic word, with no trace of Latin or Greek in its history, and its biography begins in the Low Countries. Its earliest English form is not pang at all but prange, recorded in the middle of the fifteenth century in the sense "a sharp, pinching, throbbing pain". That word was borrowed from the Germanic speech of the Low Countries — Middle Low German prange, "narrowness, distress", also "an instrument of torture" (the press, a squeezing device), beside the verb prangen, "to press, pinch, squeeze". The underlying image, which the etymology preserves perfectly, is of a clamp or a pinch: a pang is literally something that presses the flesh sharply between hard surfaces, and the torture-instrument sense shows how the word lived in a world of physical compression. In the early sixteenth century English underwent the change that produced the modern form: the -r- of prange was lost before the following consonant, giving pange and then pang, first recorded around 1520 in the sense "a sudden sharp spasm of pain".

Phonetically, the change is a classic example of cluster simplification. Middle English prange began with the consonant cluster /pr-/, and the liquid /r/ dropped before the following /n/, a natural easing of articulation that gave the new form pange — attested, interestingly, both with and without a final -e in the sixteenth-century records — and the modern disyllable then fell together into a single syllable as pang with a short /a/ (modern RP /pæŋ/). The loss of the -r- also distanced the word from its source: once the spelling and pronunciation settled as pang, the word no longer visibly belonged to the Low German family that gave it birth, and its English history appeared, to later etymologists, to begin in the 1520s with no obvious relatives.

From the very first the word moved easily between body and mind: by the 1530s English had the pangs of conscience — the mind's pinching remorse — and by the 1580s the pangs of childbirth, alongside the enduring pangs of hunger, love, jealousy, and regret. This early and complete extension from physical to moral pain is the key event in the word's career: pang became the standard English noun for a brief, piercing spasm of feeling, and it has stayed there ever since. Two relatives remain close to the Low German source. English dialect kept prang, "a sharp pain" and "to pinch, squeeze", a survival of the borrowed form; and prong — "a pointed, projecting spike", first recorded in the late fifteenth century — is probably the same Dutch or Low German word for the pinching instrument, repurposed for the prongs of a fork.

In society and literature, the word's career tracks the shift in how pain and feeling were imagined. In the sixteenth century, physical spasm — especially of childbirth — was the natural referent, and the torture-instrument ancestry gave the word an undertone of compulsion: a pang seizes you; you do not choose it. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as the language of conscience and sensibility developed, the moral pang flourished in religious and sentimental writing: a guilty conscience was tormented by pangs, and the virtuous heart felt pangs of sympathy for the suffering of others. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the collocations had multiplied and softened — pangs of hunger, of nostalgia, of envy, of regret — so that the word now names the brief, involuntary, piercing arrivals of feeling more than any sustained suffering.
