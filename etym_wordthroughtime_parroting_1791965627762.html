- 1520s — the bird’s name becomes current in English, probably a familiar pet-name formed from the given name Peter and applied to the talking bird.
- Late 16th c. — The verb appears, meaning to repeat another’s words mechanically; the gerund follows, at first describing a parrot’s mimicry.
- 17th–18th c. — Applied to people, especially pupils and partisans who repeat a master’s phrases; a standard charge in religious controversy.
- 19th c. — Extends to politics and journalism: parroting the party line, parroting a leader’s slogans.
- 20th c. — Educational psychology attacks “parrot learning” and “parroting back” answers as the opposite of understanding.
- 21st c. — Digital usage: parroting talking points, and machines parroting their training data.
