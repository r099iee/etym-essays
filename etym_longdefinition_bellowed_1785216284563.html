bellowed is the simple past tense of the verb to bellow: to emit a loud, deep, resonant cry of great volume — the full-throated roar naturally associated with a bull. In this sentence, “'Look out for the door,' bellowed the captain”, the verb is a speech tag: it tells us not merely that the captain spoke, but that he hurled the warning out at maximum volume, a deep chest-shaking shout meant to be heard over the surrounding din and to jerk his men into instant obedience. The tone is urgent, commanding, and faintly animalistic: the captain does not advise; he roars. The word implies power and authority — a man used to being obeyed — and it suits the chaos of the scene, where an ordinary “said” would sound impossibly calm. Because it is a single past-tense event, it records one dramatic outburst rather than a habit of shouting. In a nautical or military setting the verb is doubly appropriate: a command on a heaving deck must be hurled out at full chest if it is to be heard at all, so a captain who bellows is a captain whose words carry the weight of the elements themselves.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings — the verb has a clear layering:

  • Of animals (the original sense, Old English onwards) — to bellow is the deep, resonant roar of a bull, and by extension of other large beasts (deer in rut, oxen). “The wounded bull bellowed in rage.” The sense is natural, instinctive, and of great volume.
  • Of human speech (from c. 1600) — a person bellows when shouting with a deep-voiced, angry, urgent, or commanding roar, clearly set apart from the higher-pitched shout or yell. “The officer bellowed orders across the parade ground.” This is the sense used in the passage.
  • Of laughter and other vocal bursts — “he bellowed with laughter” is a standard collocation: the deep, resonant, full-chested burst of a large laugh, borrowing the animal cry for human joy.
  • Of inanimate forces (literary) — thunder bellows, the sea bellows, guns bellow: the deep, booming sound of natural or mechanical force. “The cannon bellowed across the harbour.”
  • The noun bellow — a single loud, deep cry: “a bellow of rage”. The noun and verb run in parallel, both attached to the same deep, resonant sound.
  • False-friend family — the sounding instrument bell comes from the same Germanic root (the bell was named for its ringing), but belfry is NOT a cognate: it comes from Frankish *bergfriþu, “watch-tower”, reshaped by folk-etymology after bell.


Among near-synonyms, each shades differently:

  • roared: similar in a loud, deep, powerful cry; differs because roared is more generic — used of fierce animals, rage, or a crowd — and lacks bellowed's specifically bovine resonance.
  • shouted: similar in loud vocal volume; differs because shouted is merely loud speech, lacking the depth of register and animal timbre of a bellow.
  • yelled: similar in a loud raised voice; differs because yelled suggests a higher, thinner, more panicked pitch rather than the deep chest-shaking boom of a bellow.
  • hollered: similar in shouting loudly; differs because hollered is informal and rustic, without bellow's depth of register and command.
  • bawled: similar in loud, unrestrained vocalizing; differs because bawled implies weeping or harsh, uncontrolled crying, while bellowed is deliberate, powerful, and commanding.
  • called: similar in speaking to someone; differs because called is neutral and untroubled, at the opposite end of the volume scale from a bellow.


In register, bellow is vigorous and unmistakably physical yet never vulgar: it sits comfortably in serious fiction, epic narrative, and journalism, but would be eccentric in polite drawing-room conversation, where a plain “said” or “called” would suffice. Its deep, resonant, animal character makes it the perfect verb for command in chaos, and Tolkien uses it for exactly that: the captain's warning is not advice but a roar.
