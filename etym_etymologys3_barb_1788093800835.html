The PIE root: a beard shared across the Indo-European family. The history of barb begins with the reconstruction of a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "beard", standardly written *bʰardʰ- (older handbooks also print *bhardhā; a rival shape *bʰars- is defended by some scholars), extended as the feminine noun *bʰardʰ-éh₂ "beard". The evidence is broad and strong: Latin barba "beard" (whence English barber and barb); Old English beard, Old High German bart (modern German Bart); the Balto-Slavic set Lithuanian barzdà, Russian borodá, Old Church Slavonic brada; and the Celtic set Welsh barf, Cornish barv. The "beard" word is one of the firmest items of Indo-European vocabulary, but its internal shape is genuinely debated: the -s-/-dʰ- alternation across branches has led some linguists to reconstruct *-rs- with later hardening in Italic and Germanic, while others keep *-rdʰ-. The OED and Etymonline both flag the uncertainty; what is certain is the meaning and the family's unity.

Latin barba: from the chin to the point. Latin barba "beard" is attested from the earliest Latin literature (Plautus, 3rd–2nd centuries BCE onward). The Romans already used the word transferred: Pliny and the agricultural writers use barba for the bristling awns and tufts of plants — the "beard" of an ear of corn. The crucial step for English history is the extension of the beard metaphor to weaponry: a projectile point that bristles backward like a beard. This weapon sense is documented in the Romance vernaculars and is fully at home in Old French, where barbe means both "beard" and "the barb of an arrow", because the little backward-pointing spikes look exactly like a chin covered in stubble. Alongside it, Old French developed the verb barber "to shave, trim the beard" and the noun barbier "barber" — collateral descendants of the same word that English also borrowed.

Old French and Middle English: the double career. Old French barbe was borrowed into Middle English around 1300 as barbe, chiefly in the primary sense "beard (of a man or beast)". The same word family gave medieval English the now-rare verb barben "to shave". But the archery sense arrived with the word itself from French military and fowling vocabulary, and English records it in the late 16th century: the OED and Etymonline date "the sharp point of an arrow or fish-hook, sticking backwards" to the 1580s. For three hundred years in English the word led two lives: the plain everyday "beard" (always secondary to the native beard, which had never died out) and the technical "barb of a missile or hook", which soon became the word's main career.

From arrow to feather to wire: the 17th–19th centuries.

  • Fishing and hunting tackle: 17th-century angling and fowling manuals standardise the barb of the hook and the barbed dart; "barb" is now a word of craftsmen, huntsmen and soldiers.
  • Ornithology: from the 18th century, natural historians apply "barb" to the lateral filaments of a feather vane (the "beard" of the quill); the feather barb, with its barbules and barbicels, becomes standard technical vocabulary, and 19th-century study of flight mechanics gives it precision.
  • Botany: the same century extends the word to the recurved spines of burrs, thistles and grasses; "barbed" enters botanical description.
  • Barbed wire (1862): the American Civil War era invents fencing wire armed with sharp points; the term is recorded from 1862 and the famous patents of Glidden and others follow in the mid-1860s. The product transforms the Great Plains, then becomes the emblem of the Western Front in 1914–1918 — the "barbed wire" of no-man's-land — giving the word its permanent modern undertone of deliberate, mass-produced hurt.

The figurative barb: 19th century onward. The migrated metaphor "a barb = a stinging remark" is recorded from the 19th century: speech is an arrow, and the pointed comment is barbed so that it sticks. The usage thickened through the 20th century — political journalism, sports punditry and celebrity gossip all "trade barbs". The verb "to barb" (to arm with a barb; to barb one's words) and the adjective "barbed" (barbed wit, barbed remarks) completed the figurative apparatus. Mid-century slang added a purely coincidental homonym, "barbs" = barbiturate pills, a clipping that shares the spelling but none of the meaning — a useful lexicographic warning that not everything that looks like a barb is one.

Literary test case: Tolkien's arrow. The anchor of this card is J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937): "In it smote and vanished, barb, shaft and feather, so fierce was its flight." The sentence is a lexicographer's gift: it uses "barb" in the precise medieval-archery sense (the point), sets it in the three-part anatomy of the arrow, and shows that the concrete sense never died even while the figurative sense flourished. Tolkien — a philologist by profession — deliberately summoned the old technical register, and the sentence now stands as the modern canonical example of the word in its true habitat.

Summary of the drift. Barb has travelled from the shared Indo-European beard (*bʰardʰ-) through Latin barba and Old French barbe into English as "a beard" (c. 1300), then — by the beard metaphor — became the backward-pointing spike of arrows and hooks (1580s), branched into feather and plant anatomy (18th–19th centuries), armed the frontier and the trenches as barbed wire (1862, 1914), and finally migrated to the tongue as the stinging remark (19th–20th centuries): one continuous metaphor — the bristling beard of the point that refuses to let go — running from the chin of a Roman senator to the "barb" of a modern debate.
