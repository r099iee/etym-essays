- [15th century] - Surreptitious enters English from Latin surrepticius, "stolen, furtive" — first applied to things obtained by stealth or fraud; an edge of illegality is present from the start, used of documents snatched and gains secretly made.
- [16th–17th century] - Widens from outright theft to secret, unauthorized acquisition: surreptitious editions (books printed without permission), surreptitious tricks; the adverb surreptitiously (c. 1600s) appears meaning "by stealth, without authorization".
- [18th–19th century] - Becomes a staple of formal prose and legal language — acts done in secret and taken without permission; the tone shifts from plain "stolen" toward "sly, cunning, hidden from the proper authority," carrying a faintly judicial charge.
- [Modern register] - Formal, literary, and judicial: surreptitiously recorded, copied, photographed, obtained — the academic-integrity and espionage adverb; it still keeps the ancient Latin odor of theft, reporting not merely secrecy but a culpable stealthy intention.
