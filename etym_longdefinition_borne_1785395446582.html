BORNE is the past participle of the verb to bear, meaning carried, transported, supported, sustained, or endured - weight lifted and moved through space, a burden or responsibility shouldered, suffering tolerated, or (with an object) a child brought forth. In Tolkien's scene in The Hobbit, Dori 'was borne off last of all' and Bilbo, 'swinging in the air with his arms nearly breaking', hangs on below: borne captures the aerial rescue in its full passivity - dwarves and hobbit lifted bodily from the burning tumult and carried aloft as cargo of fate. The nuance is helplessness joined to salvation: the borne do not fly; they are weight, and something greater carries them. The participle's register is elevated and faintly archaic - borne aloft, borne on the wind, borne away by death - dignifying the carrying even while it emphasizes the carrier's mastery. Its tone is solemn, passive, final: the borne has surrendered its motion to its carrier, and the sentence's grammar (Dori was borne off) makes the Eagles the sole actors and their passengers pure objects. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Carried / transported (literal): physically lifted and conveyed - borne on a litter, borne by the current.
  • Supported (structural): a weight or load sustained from beneath - the roof is borne by the pillars.
  • Endured / tolerated: suffering or hardship sustained - the pain was borne with patience.
  • Brought forth (of a child): she has borne two children - the participle retains the birth sense of bear.
  • Produced / yielded: fruit borne by a tree; interest borne by a bond (financial usage).
  • Proved / confirmed: borne out by the evidence (the idiom 'to bear out' means to confirm).
  • Negation: insupportable, unable to be borne; insufferable, unbearable.
THE FAMOUS MORPHOLOGICAL SPLIT: BORNE VS BORN The most important fact about borne is its relationship to its twin born. Both descend from the same Old English participle boren, but in Middle English the two forms split:
  • Born is restricted to birth and origin: 'he was born in 1901', 'born and bred'.
  • Borne keeps the full remaining range: the perfect with have ('she has borne the weight'), the passive with by ('borne by the wind'), pregnancy ('has borne a child'), and the abstract senses of endurance and support.
The spelling borne is further distinguished by the final -e that marks the full participle; born drops it. This is the most famous orthographic-morphological boundary in English, and the two forms are still confused by native writers. HISTORICAL BRIDGES The split is not semantic in origin but orthographic. In Middle English the participle was written born, borne, and born(e) interchangeably; the printers of the 16th-17th centuries began to favour borne for the full participle, while born, losing the final -e, became fixed in the birth construction. The result is a single word family with a double face: one sound, two spellings, two destinies. USAGE AND REGISTER Borne is the elevated, literary, slightly archaic participle. In technical and scientific writing it appears in fixed compounds (airborne, waterborne, seed-borne diseases, mosquito-borne); in everyday speech it survives mainly in formal and literary contexts. It is always passive in relation to its subject - the borne thing is acted upon - which makes it a favourite of solemn prose, and precisely the word Tolkien needs for a rescue in which the carried have no control of their own motion.
