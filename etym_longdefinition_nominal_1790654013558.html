Nominal is a versatile adjective whose meanings radiate from Latin nomen "name, noun". In everyday use it means in name only, formal rather than real: a nominal leader, nominal fee, nominal Christian — titled but powerless, token rather than substantial. In grammar and linguistics it means relating to nouns or functioning as a noun: nominal phrases, nominal inflection, nominal predicates. In stylistics it describes noun-heavy prose: a nominal style prefers abstraction, nominalisation, and heavy noun phrases ("the implementation of the regulation") over verbs ("we implemented the rule"). In economics and finance it means unadjusted for inflation or real value: nominal wages, nominal GDP, nominal interest. All senses share the idea of the name as opposed to the thing.

The in-name-only sense dominates journalism and politics. A nominal ally votes with you on paper but not in crisis; a nominal rent of one pound preserves a legal form without economic substance; nominal damages in law vindicate a right with a token award. The implication can be neutral (technicality), dismissive (sham), or precise (form without substance). The grammatical sense dominates classrooms: nominal morphology (case, number, gender), nominal clauses ("that she left surprised us"), nominal groups in systemic-functional grammar. The stylistic sense dominates composition criticism: teachers warn that a hypotactic nominal style — nested clauses packed with abstract nouns — sounds ponderous, bureaucratic, and evasive, however fond it is of balanced flourishes like alliterative isocolon.

Specialised extensions multiply. In economics, nominal vs real is foundational: nominal figures are face-value, real figures correct for price changes. In engineering, nominal size means the designated, approximate size (a nominal two-by-four is not exactly 2×4 inches). In statistics, nominal data are categorical labels without order (colours, names) as opposed to ordinal or interval data. In philosophy, nominalism holds that universals are mere names. In religion, a nominal believer is counted but unconverted. The bridge is always name-versus-reality: the label says one thing, the substance says less, or something else.

  • In name only / token: formally so-called but without substance; nominal head, nominal fee, nominal Christians.
  • Grammatical — noun-related: pertaining to nouns or noun equivalents; nominal number, nominal sentence, nominalisation.
  • Stylistic — noun-heavy: favouring nouns and nominalisations over verbs; dense, abstract, bureaucratic prose.
  • Economic — face-value: not adjusted for inflation or purchasing power; nominal wages, nominal yield.
  • Technical — designated: stated as a norm for identification; nominal voltage, nominal diameter, nominal data.
  • Collocations: nominal value, nominal damages, nominal aphasia (word-finding difficulty), nominal group, nominal style; antonyms vary by sense: real, actual, substantial, verbal, inflation-adjusted.

Register is formal-neutral across domains, but in style guides nominal is often a quiet reproach: call prose nominal and you accuse it of hiding actors behind abstractions. To grasp nominal is to grasp how much work names do — labelling, claiming, masking — without delivering the thing itself.
