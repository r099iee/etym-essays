Creaking is the present participle and verbal noun of creak: producing a thin, harsh, grating sound, high-pitched and usually intermittent, the voice of stiff things moving under strain — unoiled hinges, loose floorboards, old ships working in a swell, knees on cold mornings. Where a squeak is short and a groan is low, a creak is prolonged, dry, and frictional, the sound of two surfaces protesting as they slide or twist against each other.

In the source sentence Old Gaffer Gamgee receives a bottle of ointment for creaking joints, and the participle works as a gentle comic adjective: his knees and knuckles have become like old doors, needing oil exactly as hinges do. Tolkien, writing of an old gardener, reaches for the word that turns bodily ageing into household maintenance, tender rather than clinical, and every reader past forty recognises the morning stair-test the word describes.

Core meaning in detail: Physically, creaking is stick-slip friction made audible: surfaces catch, strain, then slip microscopically, over and over, each slip a tiny click that fuses into the drawn-out grating we call a creak. It needs stiffness plus movement — the new leather shoe, the old wicker chair, the arthritic knuckle — and it usually worsens in the cold and eases with oil, warmth, or use, which is why the ointment in the sentence is exactly the right folk remedy. The -ing form stresses continuation: not one creak but the ongoing habit of creaking, joints that do it every morning.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Stiff joints and bones that grate on movement (source sense, bodily): creaking knees, a creaking back, the Gamgee's joints. The bridge carries the household sound into the body: cartilage worn thin works like a hinge gone dry, so the same verb serves the carpenter and the rheumatologist alike.
  • Hinges, doors, gates, and floorboards (primary everyday sense, from 16th c.): the creaking stair in the ghost story, the creaking gate of the churchyard. This is the sense children learn first, and the sound-effect every haunted house in fiction depends upon.
  • Ships, timbers, and strained structures (nautical and architectural): a creaking hull, creaking rigging, creaking roof-beams in the wind. The bridge is scale: great wooden structures complain in the same voice as small ones, only deeper and slower, and sailors read the creaks as weather reports.
  • A thin grating voice (transferred sense): a creaking whisper, the old man's creaking laugh. The bridge is the throat as instrument: aged or strained vocal cords vibrate unevenly like a warped hinge, so the voice is heard as wood rather than music.
  • Worn institutions groaning under strain (figurative, 19th–20th c.): a creaking bureaucracy, a creaking economy, creaking public services. The bridge is the great metaphor by which states become machines and machines become bodies: an administration that moves stiffly, noisily, and reluctantly is heard as an old joint, and the adjective quietly predicts eventual seizure.
  • Creaking as ominous quiet in narrative (literary effect-sense): the single creak on the stairs that freezes the heroine. The bridge is dramatic: because creaks announce movement, one unexplained creak means someone, or something, is moving, and horror fiction has banked on that inference for two centuries.

Register and connotation: creaking is homely and faintly comic for bodies — creaking joints invite sympathy and a smile, never dread — but eerie for houses, where it belongs to night, emptiness, and suspense. Applied to institutions it turns satirical: to call a government creaking is to call it obsolete while granting it a kind of dogged persistence. The word always implies age plus continued use: what creaks has not yet broken, and the sound is the proof that it still moves.
