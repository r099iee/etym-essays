Every notion you have ever had descends from humanity's oldest intellectual ambition: to know. The trail begins at reconstructed PIE *ǵneh₃-, to know or recognise — one of the great world-roots, the ancestor of English know, knowledge, and notice, of Greek gnosis, of Sanskrit jnana, of Latin's whole knowing family. To know, for the Indo-Europeans, was to have picked something out and marked it — the root also touches note and notion alike — and the Romans built on it with characteristic legal precision. From the verb nōscere (to come to know, to learn by inquiry) they derived the noun nōtiō: literally a knowing, the concept the mind forms when acquaintance ripens into comprehension. Cicero and the Stoics used it as philosophy's working vocabulary — a notio was the mind's legitimate grasp of a thing, the imprint left by experience on understanding.

Through late antiquity and the Middle Ages the word stayed in scholars' hands. Medieval schoolmen counted and classified notiones — common notions shared by all minds, divine notions beyond them — and the word crossed into Old French as notion, meaning idea, knowledge, or acquaintance, keeping its learned bearing. Middle English borrowed it in the 1400s, spelled nocioun in Chaucer's heirs, as a prestige term for conceptions and opinions: to have a notion of theology was to have studied, not to have fancied.

The great loosening came with the age of essays and conversation:

  • Philosophy's high noon (1600s): Locke, in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), made notion and idea the rival counters of epistemology — every theory of knowledge had to define what a notion was, and the word has never since shaken the faint chalk-dust of the lecture hall.
  • The whim shift (1700s): ordinary speakers, unimpressed by epistemology, softened the word toward sudden fancies — to take a notion into one's head — because notions, arriving unasked and unproven, behaved exactly like whims. The thought and the urge merged.
  • Yankee notions (early 1800s): New England pedlars hawked ingenious trifles — tin gadgets, wooden novelties — as Yankee notions, products of Yankee ingenuity, until dry-goods stores labelled whole counters of buttons, threads, and ribbons as notions. The idea had become inventory: small clever things, born of somebody's bright notion, sold by the dozen.
  • The dismissive notion (Victorian era on): precisely because notions are unproven, the word learned to sneer — romantic notions, wherever did you get that notion — a put-down that concedes the thinking while denying the knowledge.

Sound and spelling barely stirred across the whole journey — nōtiō to notion is little more than the wearing-off of a Latin ending — which suits a word whose business is continuity: every notion still claims kinship with knowing, however distant. Tolkien's use catches the word at its most human: But now I've a notion is neither philosophy nor commerce but the mind announcing, with modest pride, that something has just arrived. Two thousand years after the Stoics defined their notiones, a hobbit's bright idea keeps the lineage alive — knowing, caught in the act of beginning.
