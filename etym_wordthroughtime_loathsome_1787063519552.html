- Old English (pre-11th c.) - [Hostile & Hateful Root: *lāð*] - The Old English adjective *lāð* meant hateful, hostile, inimical, or sorrowful (a *lāð* enemy was a hated foe).
- Middle English (13th–14th c.) - [Compound Suffixation: *lothsom*] - Formed by combining the adjective/noun *loth* (hateful/repulsive) with the productive suffix *-som* (tending to cause), shifting the sense from personal hostility to objective disgusting quality.
- Late Middle English to Renaissance (14th–16th c.) - [Visceral Physical Disease & Leprosy] - Extensively used in medical and moral contexts to describe rotting sores, contagious pestilence, rotting corpses, and demonic entities.
- 17th–19th Century (Early Modern to Victorian) - [Moral Depravity & Gothic Horror] - Broadened to describe grotesque villains, depraved crimes, and terrifying monsters in Gothic literature (Mary Shelley, Bram Stoker).
- 20th–21st Century (Contemporary) - [Fantasy, Moral Condemnation & Horror] - Retained as a powerful descriptive adjective in high fantasy (Tolkien's Mirkwood creatures, monsters), ethical denunciation of war crimes, and horror fiction.
