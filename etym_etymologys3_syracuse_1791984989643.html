Syracuse is a name that looks Greek and behaves Greek, but whose root no one has satisfactorily explained — a combination common among Mediterranean place names of the colonial period.

The Greek forms

The city was founded, in the Thucydidean chronology, in the thirty-eighth year before the Peloponnesian War, that is 733 BCE, by Corinth under Archias. The Doric form of the name was Συράκοσαι, the Attic literary form Συράκουσαι, and both are plural — a habit Greek shares with other cities conceived as a union of quarters or of founding groups, as in Ἀθῆναι and Θῆβαι. Latin took the plural over unchanged as Syracusae, and it is the Latin plural that appears in Cicero, Livy and Pliny.

The problem of the root

  • Ancient grammarians connected the name with a marsh near the site which they called Syrako; that explanation is reported, not demonstrated, and may itself be a back-formation from the city name.
  • The ending -ουσαι is a perfectly ordinary Greek feminine plural participle ending, but a Greek ending does not guarantee a Greek stem: the same pattern appears in many place names that Beekes and others classify as Pre-Greek.
  • Attempts to extract a Greek sense — from σῦς “pig”, from σύρω “to drag”, from a hypothetical “swampy place” — have not won acceptance; attempts to find a Semitic root reflecting Phoenician traders are equally unproven.
  • The honest formulation is that the name is pre-Greek or at least non-transparent, that its meaning was already lost to the Greeks, and that the “marsh” explanation is the best-known ancient guess rather than an etymology.

From plural to singular

Late Latin and the Romance languages reshaped the plural Syracusae into a feminine singular, giving Italian Siracusa; the change follows the general drift by which classical plurals of city names were reanalysed as singulars. English did not borrow the Italian form directly but took the name from Latin in the sixteenth century, when Thucydides, Plutarch and Livy were translated and printed; the final -e and the initial Sy- reflect that Latin and French-mediated transmission rather than the Italian Si-.

The city behind the name

The history of Syracuse is a sequence of almost allegorical contrasts. It was a Corinthian colony that became richer than Corinth; a Greek democracy that Athens tried to destroy and failed; a tyranny that produced both the most brilliant engineering of antiquity and the political disappointments of Plato; and finally a Roman provincial capital whose artworks were carried off to decorate temples in Rome. Marcellus' sack of 212 BCE was celebrated in Rome as a triumph of piety — the conqueror wept for Archimedes — and lamented by Greeks as the beginning of the systematic looting of the Greek world.

  • Coins: the Syracusan dekadrachms of the fifth century, signed by masters such as Kimon and Euainetos, are among the most admired of all Greek coins, and the city's name on them is one of the earliest mass circulations of the word.
  • Literature: Pindar wrote victory odes for Hieron; Aeschylus visited the court and his Women of Etna was composed for it; Theocritus set his Idylls in the Syracusan countryside and gave the pastoral tradition its founding landscape.
  • Philosophy: Plato's Seventh Letter describes the Syracusan disasters of Dion and Dionysius II, and the episode shaped the ancient judgement that philosophy and power do not mix.
  • Science: Archimedes' defence of the city became the archetype of applied mathematics in the service of a state.

Medieval and modern fortunes

Byzantine Syracuse fell to the Arabs in 878 after a long siege, and to the Normans in 1085; the earthquake of 1693 destroyed most of the city and produced the late Baroque rebuild of Ortigia that visitors now admire. In the twentieth century the name acquired two further associations: the Allied landings of July 1943, and the modern industrial complexes of Priolo and Augusta that sit beside the ancient quarries.

Modern reference

Today Syracuse is a medium-sized Italian city whose economy rests on tourism, agriculture and a little industry, and whose name in English is increasingly ambiguous, since Syracuse in New York State is a familiar university town. In the reading, though, the name is still doing its ancient work: it locates the tomb, and it quietly measures the distance between a Greek city that produced a mathematician and a Roman official who came to honour him.
