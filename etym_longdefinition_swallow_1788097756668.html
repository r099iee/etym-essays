SWALLOW (the bird) is a small, swift, graceful migratory songbird of the family Hirundinidae, with long pointed wings, a deeply forked tail, and a short, wide bill adapted to catching flying insects on the wing. The barn swallow, Hirundo rustica, the most familiar species, nests under eaves and in barns throughout the Northern Hemisphere and migrates thousands of miles to Africa in winter — exactly the bird of the source context, where “his friends had gone away to Egypt six weeks before”. In British and Commonwealth use the group includes the house martin and sand martin; the word is also a homograph of the unrelated verb and noun swallow (“to gulp down”), which has a different etymology entirely.


Primary sense



  • The bird: any of about eighty species of hirundines. Characteristics: jerky, skimming flight low over water and fields; mud-and-straw cup nests plastered to walls and rafters; twice-yearly long-distance migration; a delicate twittering song; faithful return to the same nest site each spring. The swallow is the archetypal “summer bird” of the English-speaking world — the herald of spring, the bird of eaves, barns, and bridges.


Extended and figurative senses



  • The proverb: one swallow does not make a summer — from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics (“one swallow does not make a spring”), in English from the mid-16th century, still a live caution against drawing conclusions from a single instance.

  • Homograph vocabulary: the verb swallow “to take into the stomach through the throat; to engulf, absorb, accept credulously, retract one's words” and its noun — a wholly separate word (Old English swelgan) that coincidentally shares spelling and pronunciation with the bird name. Swallow dive (British; the American swan dive) — a dive with arms spread wide like wings — and swallow-tail (the forked tail; the swallow-tailed coat of formal dress) build on the bird's shape.

  • Folklore and symbolism: swallows nesting under one's eaves were held to bring luck and protect the house; harming a swallow was unlucky; the bird's spring return made it a symbol of homecoming, renewal, and constancy — hence the sailor's tattoo of a swallow for every five thousand nautical miles sailed, and as the emblem of safe return home.

  • Literature: in Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888), the little Swallow — delayed by his love for the Reed while his friends fly to Egypt — becomes the self-sacrificing hero of the story, giving the bird its most famous literary afterlife; Aesop's fables include the swallow and the other birds; Ovid's Philomela story, retold by Chaucer, makes the swallow the nightingale's sister.

  • Science and conservation: in ornithology “swallow” distinguishes hirundines from the superficially similar swifts (family Apodidae), which differ in wing shape, nesting, and behaviour — a classic lesson in convergent evolution. In modern conservation the barn swallow is a declining farmland species, its numbers monitored as an index of insect and landscape health. Compound names: barn swallow, cliff swallow, house martin, sand martin, swallow-wort (a plant named from its seed shape).


Register: everyday and affectionate in the bird sense; proverbial; literary; technical in ornithology. The word is also a surname and a place-name element (Swallowfield, “the swallow land”). Its double life — bird and gulp — is a standing test of context: the reader must decide whether the sentence is about flight or food.
