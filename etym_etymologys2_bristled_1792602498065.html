Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*bʰórs-ti-s
"point, prickle, projection, bristle"
(from verbal root *bʰers- / *bʰres- "to project, point")
↓
Proto-Germanic (reconstructed)
*burstiz
"stiff hair, bristle, spiky shoot"
↓
Old English (c. 9th–10th c.)
byrst
"bristle, coarse stiff hair of a swine"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th–14th c.)
brustle / bristle
"stiff hair; (verb) to stand stiffly erect like swine hair"
(underwent r-metathesis and acquired frequentative suffix -le)
↓
Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)
bristle
"to erect hair in rage; to be thick with weapons"
↓
Modern English (past tense / participle)
bristled
"stood erect; was densely armed; reacted indignantly"

Note on historical phonetics and cognates: The transformation from Old English byrst to Middle English bristle illustrates classic liquid metathesis in Germanic historical phonology, wherein the rhotic consonant /r/ swapped positions with the preceding vowel (Proto-Germanic *burstiz → Old English byrst → Middle English brustle / bristle). The root is cognate across the Germanic family with Old High German burst (Modern German Borste, "bristle", and Bürste, "brush"), Old Norse burst ("bristle, roof-ridge"), and Middle Dutch borste. Beyond Germanic, philologists connect the base to Proto-Indo-European *bʰórs-ti-s, cognate with Sanskrit bhr̥ṣṭi- ("point, spike, prong, edge") and Latin fastigium ("pointed top, gable, summit", via *farst-).
