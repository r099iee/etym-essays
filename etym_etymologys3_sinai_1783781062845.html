SINAI is a toponym of Semitic origin with no Indo-European ancestry, and although it is one of the most famous place-names in the Bible, its etymology has never been settled. It first appears in the Hebrew text as Sînay, the name of the mountain (and the surrounding wilderness and, later, the whole peninsula) at which, according to Exodus 19–20, God descended in cloud and fire, proclaimed the Ten Commandments, and concluded the covenant with Israel. Scholars have proposed at least four plausible sources for the name, none of them certain. The oldest and most popular connects it to the Semitic moon-god Sîn, the great lunar deity of Mesopotamia, the Sîn of Ur and Harran, so that Sinai would be "the mountain of Sîn" — a view reinforced by the fact that the Bible itself records a "wilderness of Sin" (Exodus 16:1) near Sinai, a name likewise associated with the moon-cult. A second proposal derives the name from a Semitic root meaning "clay, mud" (Arabic ṭīn), making Sinai "the place of clay"; a third, from the root šinn-, "tooth," would make it "the toothed, notched peak," a description suited to the serrated granite summits of the southern peninsula. A fourth etymology, connecting the mountain to the burning bush — Hebrew seneh, the thorn-bush of Exodus 3 — has been popular since antiquity but is generally judged by modern scholarship to be a folk-etymology prompted by the narrative itself. Because the mountain was sacred long before Israel, some specialists further suspect a pre-Hebrew, possibly Amorite or Egyptian, substratum behind the name; nothing is certain. The honest lexicographic verdict, stated plainly, is that the ultimate origin of the name is unknown. What is certain is the word's transmission, which closely parallels that of Sion. The Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament begun in the third century BCE, transliterated the name as Σινᾶ (Sina), and Jerome's Vulgate (c. 405 CE) rendered it Sinai or Sina. Through the Latin Bible the name entered the vernaculars of Europe; English had it by around 1300, carried by the Vulgate, Anglo-Norman, and the earliest English scripture and sermons, and it became standard in every English Bible from Wycliffe to the King James Version (1611). The form settled as Sinai, with the penultimate stress, though the Greek Sina survives in some European languages. The mountain's biblical career fixed the word's meaning. In Exodus 19 the people arrive at the wilderness of Sinai and camp before the mount; the mountain burns with fire to heaven, the trumpet sounds long and loud, and Moses ascends into the cloud to receive the Law. The giving of the Decalogue, the covenant ceremony, and the code of the Law (Exodus 20–31) all unfold at Sinai, and Deuteronomy 5 recapitulates the lawgiving on the same mount. Sinai is therefore, in the religious memory of the West, the mountain of the Law par excellence — the place where a people was constituted, where divine will was codified, and where the sound of the trumpet and the voice of God were heard by an entire nation. The prophet Elijah travels forty days to "Horeb, the mount of God" (1 Kings 19) to hear the still small voice; the Psalmist sings of "the Lord that dwelt in Sinai" (Psalm 68:17); and the Book of Judges, Nehemiah, and the Prophets repeatedly recall the Sinai covenant as the charter of Israel. The New Testament and Christian theology kept Sinai but relocated its meaning. Paul in Galatians 4:24–25 allegorizes Sinai itself as the covenant of bondage — the law given on the desert mountain versus the freedom of the promise — so that "Sinai" becomes a theological term for the Old Covenant, the dispensation of commandment and fear, in contrast to the grace of the gospel. This double valence — the mountain of glory and the mountain of bondage — has governed the word's use in Christian preaching, hymnody, and systematic theology ever since. In literature the name carries its full weight of prophetic inspiration. Milton's Paradise Lost opens by invoking the Spirit "That on the secret top Of Oreb, or of Sinai, didst inspire / That Shepherd, who first taught the chosen Seed" — a line that pairs the two names of the one mountain, registers the traditional identification of Horeb and Sinai, and sets the biblical lawgiver's inspiration against the pagan "Aonian Mount" of the Muses. The desert mount of the Law is, for Milton, the true source of the song he attempts, and the very uncertainty of which mountain Moses climbed becomes an engine of the verse. In the modern period Sinai moved from scripture into geography and geopolitics. Since the nineteenth century the name has designated the entire triangular peninsula of Egypt — the land bridge between Africa and Asia, bordered by the Mediterranean, the Gulf of Suez, and the Gulf of Aqaba — famous for Jebel Musa (the traditional Mount Sinai), the ancient monastic complex of St. Catherine's, and the dramatic granite peaks and canyons of the south. The Sinai Peninsula has been a stage of war and peace in the Arab-Israeli conflicts, a tourist and pilgrimage destination, and the object of continuing archaeology and biblical scholarship. Through every register — the mountain of the Law, the wilderness of the wanderings, the peninsula of modern Egypt, the theological symbol of the Old Covenant, and the Miltonic mount of biblical inspiration — the name Sinai has carried, for three thousand years, the memory of a people hearing the voice of God. Sound and orthographic notes:
  • Hebrew Sînay (סִינַי) shows the final -ay diphthong, which Greek rendered as a (Σινᾶ) and Latin as -ai (Sinai), fixing the English form.
  • English pronounces the name with diphthongized vowels (/ˈsaɪnaɪ/), while the Greek-derived Sina preserves a shorter form in other European languages.
  • The spelling has been stable in English since the fourteenth century; no significant variant competed with Sinai.
