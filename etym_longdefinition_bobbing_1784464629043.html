Bobbing is the present participle and gerund of the verb bob: moving with short, quick, repeated up-and-down (or sometimes to-and-fro) motions — light, buoyant, and rapid rather than smooth, majestic, or heavy. A cork bobs on water, a head bobs with music or nervous energy, a small boat bobs in a harbour, and an anxious person bobs on a doorstep. The -ing form emphasizes ongoing, repeated motion: a continuous jig of small dips and rises rather than a single completed movement. In all its uses the word carries a slightly comic or undignified energy — bobbing is what light things and flustered people do, not what heroes or mountains do. In Gloin's speech — "As soon as I clapped eyes on the little fellow bobbing and puffing on the mat" — the participle freezes Bilbo in a single remembered first impression: a small, agitated figure moving up and down with short breath, all nervous energy, before the dwarves have accepted him as their burglar. The -ing form matters both grammatically and dramatically: it reports continuous observed motion, an ongoing state of agitation, not a single bob. Paired with puffing, it paints unheroic physical comedy on the doormat — exactly the image a skeptical dwarf would retain. Tone: vivid, slightly belittling, kinetic; the participle makes the motion something still in progress in Gloin's memory. Primary senses of bobbing / bob
  • Of objects — to float or move with short, quick, buoyant up-and-down motion on water or in air ("a cork bobbing on the waves"; "buoys bobbing in the bay").
  • Of persons — to move the head or body up and down quickly ("bobbing his head to the music"); to move with a quick dipping gait or gesture.
  • Of greeting — to make a quick, small curtsy or bow ("she bobbed a curtsey"); a light, abbreviated form of deference.
  • Gerund and noun use — the action of bobbing itself ("the bobbing of the buoys"), and the informal noun for a quick dipping motion.
Polysemy: secondary and modern senses
  • Bobbed hair — a short women's haircut; from the verb sense "to cut short," which branched from the same family of "short, quick, trimmed" ideas in the 16th–20th centuries.
  • Plumb bob — a weight on a string used to test verticality; the "hanging, swinging, dipping" image of the same root.
  • Bobbing for apples — a Halloween game of catching apples with the teeth from a tub of water; the apples "bob" on the surface as players lunge for them.
  • Bob (noun, slang) — a shilling (British, 19th–20th centuries); distinct from the motion sense in origin, a reminder that "bob" is an overloaded word with several separate histories.
  • Bobtail, bobcat — "bob" in the sense "short, docked" (a short tail), a related branch of the "shortened" idea that feeds names of animals.
  • Bobbin — a reel or spool of thread; probably connected to the "rounded lump" noun sense, but partly a separate history (from French bobine), so it must not be force-linked to the motion verb.
  • Bob and weave (idiom) — the boxing technique of dodging by dipping the head and body sideways; the compound couples the vertical dip with lateral motion.
Register and collocation Bobbing collocates with cork, buoy, boat, head, apples, and with manner adverbs such as gently and nervously. It belongs to plain, kinetic, slightly comic register — light and quick rather than slow and grand — which is exactly why Gloin uses it to shrink the flustered hobbit to the size of a floating cork.
