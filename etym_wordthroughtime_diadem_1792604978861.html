- [Classical Greece & Persia (5th–4th c. BCE)] - a simple white silk or wool fillet or ribbon tied around the head or tiara by Persian kings; adopted by Alexander the Great to symbolize universal monarchical rule - [perception: revered in the Orient as sacred majesty, but viewed warily by democratic Greeks as an emblem of eastern autocracy]
- [Roman Republic & Early Empire (1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] - strictly rejected by Roman citizens and senators as the abhorrent hallmark of oriental despotism; Julius Caesar famously made a public show of refusing the diadem offered by Mark Antony at the Lupercalia - [perception: heavily stigmatized as an anti-republican badge of tyranny]
- [Late Roman & Byzantine Empires (4th–6th c. CE)] - reintroduced and transformed by Diocletian and Constantine the Great into a pearl-encrusted, gem-studded rigid gold circlet, establishing the imperial diadem as the supreme Christian sovereign badge - [perception: revered as the sacred, divinely ordained insignia of the Roman emperor]
- [Middle English & Medieval Europe (13th–16th c.)] - entered Middle English from Old French, applied broadly to royal crowns, liturgical headpieces, and Christ's celestial crown of glory in mystical poetry - [perception: celebrated in chivalric romance and theological literature as the ultimate badge of royalty and spiritual triumph]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Natural History] - appropriated by Swedish taxonomist Carl Linnaeus and later naturalists into biological nomenclature (e.g., Araneus diadematus, Diadema), while retaining its high poetic role for supreme beauty or achievement - [perception: admired as a prestigious, classical term for crowning elegance and natural pattern]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Usage] - primarily preserved in elevated literature, archaeological history, and metaphorical rhetoric denoting apex achievements or historical royal headwear - [perception: evocative of ancient, opulent majesty and timeless regal power]
