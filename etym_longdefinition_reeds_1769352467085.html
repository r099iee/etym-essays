reeds — the plural of reed, a tall, slender, perennial wetland grass or reed-mace-like plant with a jointed, hollow, upright stem, growing in or near water, typically in dense stands. On this card the word is tested in its botanical sense: "The reeds jerked slightly in the current." The image is of tall, thin, flexible plants standing in water, swaying and quivering with the movement of the water around them. Reeds are among the most ancient of useful plants: their hollow stems made pipes, thatch, mats, baskets, and writing implements, and their botanical name (Phragmites) reflects their character as tall, brittle, shaking stems.

1. Primary sense — wetland grasses (botany)

Tall, slender, hollow-stemmed grasses that grow in or near water — common reed (Phragmites australis), giant reed (Arundo donax), and bulrush-like sedges. They stand in dense clumps along rivers, lakes, and marshes, and are characterized by jointed, hollow stems, feathery or cattail-like tops, and a tendency to sway and "jerk" in the current or wind. This is the sense of the card.

2. Secondary sense — the musical reed (woodwinds)

In music, a "reed" is a thin strip of cane or metal that vibrates to produce sound in instruments such as the clarinet, saxophone, oboe, and bassoon — the "single reed" (one strip against the mouthpiece) and "double reed" (two strips bound together) families. The semantic bridge is direct and ancient: the earliest wind instruments were made by cutting and flattening a hollow reed stem, and the same Arundo donax cane is still harvested today for clarinet and saxophone reeds. The plant gave its name to the vibrating strip that is its descendant.

3. Tertiary sense — pipe organ reed stops

In the pipe organ, "reed stops" are ranks of pipes that produce tone through a vibrating metal tongue (the reed) rather than through air flow alone. The word carries the same logic: a strip of flexible material that vibrates to make sound, named after the plant that first provided such strips.

4. Writing and literature

Because the hollow stem of the reed could be sharpened into a pen, "reed" historically also meant a writing implement — the ancestor of the pen. The Latin calamus and Greek kalamos ("reed, pen") show this usage, and English retains it in poetic and historical contexts. Relatedly, "reed" in ancient Greek (kalamos) gave words like calamus (the reed) and cane.

5. Historical semantic bridge

Old English hrēod meant "marsh grass, reed," from Proto-Germanic *hreudą, possibly linked to a root meaning "to clear (land)" or "shaken, swaying" — the plant of open marshy ground. Because the hollow stem was the ancient material for pipes, pens, and thatch, the botanical word early acquired technological extensions: the shepherd's pipe, the scribe's pen, the thatcher's bundles. Every later "reed" — musical strip, organ tongue, pen — is a functional descendant of the plant that first provided the material.

6. Register, nuance, and modern uses

  • Ecological/landscape: reeds are an icon of wetlands, marshland conservation, and natural beauty — "reed beds" are vital wildlife habitats.
  • Building: thatching with reed ("reed thatch") remains a craft in parts of Europe.
  • Musical: "reed," "double-reed," "reed instrument," "reed pipe," "reed organ," "reed stop" — all live musical vocabulary.
  • Figurative: a "broken reed" is a weak or unreliable support (from the Bible and classic literature); "a reed shaken by the wind" describes someone easily swayed.
  • Related plants: do not confuse with rushes (solid, round, soft stems), sedges (often triangular stems), or cattails (cylindrical brown heads) — though "reed" is used loosely for all of these.
  • Compound: "reed bed," "reed mace" (the cattail/bulrush), "reed pen," "reed pipe."

In summary, "reeds" on this card are the tall, thin, hollow-stemmed wetland grasses that sway and jerk in the current — but the word's family extends into music, writing, building, and ecology, all connected by the hollow stem of a remarkable plant.
