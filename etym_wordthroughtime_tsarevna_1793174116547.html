- Muscovy, 16th–17th c. — the cloistered dynastic daughter: tsarevnas live in the terem seclusion of the Kremlin, unseen, married for statecraft, figures of awe to commoners who never glimpse them.
- Peter the Great, early 18th c. — the Westernised grand duchess: the native title is overlaid at court by European styles, and tsarevna begins its retreat from chancery into nursery and village tale.
- 19th c. — the folklorists' prize: Afanasyev's collections and Ralston's English translations fix tsarevna as the fairy-tale princess of the Frog, the Grey Wolf, and Maria Morevna, familiar to every Russian child.
- Ballets Russes era, early 20th c. — the exotic stage beauty: Paris applauds tsarevnas in opera and ballet, and the word gains a Parisian perfume of Slav exoticism.
- Soviet era — the ironic relic: with no tsar left, the title survives in storybooks and as gentle mockery for a spoilt girl waited on hand and foot.
- Today — the fantasy staple: English fantasy novels and games deploy tsarevna for any northern princess, trading on a century of accumulated onion-dome glamour.
