- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Attested as *glēo* / *glīw*, signifying mirth, entertainment, musical play, jesting, and minstrelsy; central to the Anglo-Saxon court culture of the *glēoman* (bard or harper).
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Spelled *gle* or *glee*, retaining the broad meaning of song, festive sport, and merriment; frequently paired with game (*gle and game*) in chivalric romances.
- 18th Century (Georgian Era) - Undergoes specialized musical narrowing: designates an unaccompanied English part-song for three or more solo voices; formal *Glee Clubs* established across London dining societies.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Migrates to university campuses as collegiate choral ensembles (*glee clubs*); concurrently, in everyday vernacular (Oscar Wilde, Charles Dickens), general usage shifts toward radiant, childlike joy, or mischievous triumph.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retains dual presence in standard English: universal noun for buoyant, demonstrative delight (or malicious satisfaction), and established institutional name for school and university singing choirs.
