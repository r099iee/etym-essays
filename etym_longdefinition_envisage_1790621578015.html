envisage means to form a mental picture of something, especially something future or hypothetical; to conceive, imagine, or expect that something will happen or be a certain way. It combines visualization with intellectual anticipation: you not only picture it but contemplate it as a real possibility, plan, or consequence.

The central modern sense is prospective imagination. To envisage a future, to envisage an information society, to envisage consequences, to envisage a world without war, to envisage living on Mars. Your sentence — develop ways to envisage, imagine, conceptualize the information — captures this exactly: making the abstract visible and thinkable so it can be managed. Here envisage is slightly more formal and deliberative than imagine; it suggests serious consideration by policymakers, planners, scholars, or designers rather than idle fantasy.

A second, closely related sense is expectation and foresight: to envisage difficulties, envisage problems, envisage resistance, envisage success. I envisage no objection means I foresee none. We did not envisage such rapid growth means we did not anticipate it. In business and official prose this sense shades into anticipate and foresee, but retains the visual metaphor of seeing ahead.

  • Visualize / picture in the mind: to envisage a landscape, a building, a face — to call up a quasi-visual image. Retains the French visage face root most directly. Envisage the harbour crowded with sails.
  • Conceive as possibility: to envisage an alternative, envisage reform, envisage independence — to entertain an idea as conceivable. Often in the negative: I cannot envisage a deal without safeguards.
  • Plan / propose for the future: the scheme envisages two new hospitals, the treaty envisages joint control. Here the subject is often inanimate — a plan, law, report envisages — meaning it provides for or contemplates.
  • Expect / foresee: we envisage completion by June, envisaged demand never materialized. Common in forecasts, budgets, timetables. Past participle as adjective: the envisaged cuts, the envisaged expansion.
  • Philosophical / theoretical: to envisage a concept, envisage information, envisage consciousness — to frame abstract entities in a conceptual model that makes them thinkable, as in information economics and cognitive science.

Grammar: transitive verb taking a noun phrase or gerund or that-clause or as-phrase: envisage change, envisage leaving, envisage that prices will rise, envisage the building as a gallery. Often passive: it is envisaged that... British English strongly prefers envisage + -ing (envisage working from home) where American often uses envision. The noun envisagement is rare; envisaged functions attributively.

Register is formal, written, administrative, academic. You envisage scenarios in reports, white papers, planning inquiries, manifestos. Collocates: envisage a future, envisage a society, envisage consequences, envisage difficulties, envisage reforms, envisage a situation where, difficult to envisage, hard to envisage, originally envisaged. Tone is sober and rational — envisaging is what responsible adults do before acting — in contrast to dream or fantasize, which excuse impracticality, and to hallucinate, which pathologizes.

Polysemy bridge: face → seeing a face → facing toward → confronting with the eyes → picturing in the mind's eye → contemplating as future. The visual root guarantees that even the most abstract use retains a hint of seeing: to envisage is to make the invisible faintly visible, to give information a face.
