Tinkle is a sound-word — an onomatopoeia — and its entire history is the story of how languages imitate the small, bright ringing of metal and glass. Because the word is imitative, its history is less about sound changes and more about how a repeated base (tink) acquired a diminutive, frequentative shape and then extended its meaning into the modern world.

I. The imitative base "tink"

The core of tinkle is the base tink, itself an imitation of a sharp, light metallic ringing.

  • Middle English had the verb tinken (attested by the late 14th century), meaning "to clink, to make a ringing sound".
  • The base is essentially universal as an echo of metal-on-metal: compare Dutch tinken/tinkelen, German dialectal tinken and Swedish tingla, all imitative.
  • English also borrowed the Latin-derived tintinnabulation ("a ringing of bells") from tintinnabulum, a bell — built by reduplication of the same kind of "tin(t)-" imitation. The native tinkle and the Latinate tintinnabulation are therefore distant cousins through the same trick of onomatopoeia.

II. The frequentative suffix "-le"

At some point in Middle English, tinken was given the frequentative suffix -le(n), giving tinklen — attested from the 14th century.

  • The suffix -le (from Old English -lian) builds verbs that mean "to do the base action repeatedly, lightly, or with small, quick movements": sparkle (from spark), twinkle (from twin/twink, "to blink"), wriggle, jiggle, dribble, crumble, hobble.
  • The effect on tinkle was to make the sound smaller, lighter and more repeated than a single "tink": a tinkle is a delicate, continuous or repeated ringing rather than one sharp strike.
  • This frequentative formation is exactly parallel to twinkle, with which tinkle is often paired in verse — both are small, light, repeated, and both rhyme naturally.

III. The noun and the broadening of the sense

From the verb a noun grew, and from sound the word spread to related domains.

  • The noun tinkle (the sound itself) is attested from the 17th century.
  • The word's natural habitats: small bells, sleigh bells, piano keys played lightly, glasses or ice, water, and — crucially for the source passage — small pieces of metal or coin striking together, which is why Tolkien pairs "a glint and a tinkle" for gold in the dark.
  • The Biblical "tinkling cymbal" (from the King James version, 1611, translating the Greek of 1 Corinthians 13) gave the word a famous mocking register: a tinkling cymbal is a hollow, attention-seeking noise without love — a sense of empty showiness.

IV. The euphemistic extension

The most remarkable shift came in the modern period.

  • By the early 20th century "to tinkle" (and "a tinkle") had become a childish or genteel euphemism for urinating, based on the light sound of urine.
  • This is an example of semantic narrowing with a register shift: the word moved from a general sound-word into a private, bodily sense, kept alive because the sound itself is genuinely tinkling and because parents needed a soft word for children.
  • Today the sound-sense and the urine-sense coexist; context disambiguates them. Writers of serious prose usually avoid the euphemism for fear of comic effect, which is why Tolkien's "tinkle" for gold ringing in a dragon-hoard is safe: nothing bodily is suggested in a cavern of treasure.

V. Semantic summary

From an imitative Middle English verb tinken, given a light frequentative suffix to become tinklen, tinkle has always meant a small, bright, ringing sound. It broadened from bells to glass, metal and music, acquired a mocking literary register from the Bible, and in the modern era gained an informal bodily sense. It remains one of the most onomatopoeic, delicate words in the language.
