The deep root: dividing time. The ultimate source of tidings is a prehistoric Indo-European root *dā-, “to divide, cut up”, the same root that lies behind Greek daíomai “to divide” and, in later Greek, daimōn “a divider, dispenser, god”. In the Germanic branch the root produced a noun *tīdiz, “time, period” — built on the telling idea that time is something divided into portions: seasons, hours, feasts. This is why the modern English word tide (in “eventide”, “yuletide”, “Whitsuntide”) means “season or period” even today, and why the sea's tide — the rising and falling of the waters — is a later specialization of the same word: the fixed time at which water rises.

Old English: tīd, tīdian, tīdung. Old English inherited *tīdiz as tīd, “time, season, hour, feast-day”, a word of enormous frequency, still familiar in “eventide” and in the church's “tide” of Christmas or Easter. From tīd the Anglo-Saxons formed the class-2 weak verb tīdian, “to happen, to occur” — literally “to come about at a (certain) time” — and from the verb they derived, with the productive abstract-noun suffix -ung, the noun tīdung, “an announcement, news, an event”, attested in late Old English (11th-century glosses and prose). The semantic path is worth pausing on: time → to happen (in time) → a happening → a report of a happening. News, in the Anglo-Saxon mind, was literally “that which has come to pass”, and the word for the report was built on the word for the event.

Scandinavian reinforcement. In the centuries after the Viking settlements, English tīdung met its Scandinavian cousin: Old Norse tiðindi, “events, news”, built on the adjective tiðr, “occurring, frequent” (from the same Germanic root *tīdiz). Norse tiðindi is the ancestor of modern Scandinavian words for “news” — older Norwegian and Danish tidende, Swedish tidning “newspaper”. In northern England, where Norse speech was a daily reality, the two words reinforced each other, and Middle English tiding, tidyng, tydynges emerged, attested from about 1200 in chronicles and romances. The plural form tidings quickly became dominant, since reports typically arrived as a collection of items.

Middle English career. In Middle English, tidings was the ordinary word for news. It appears in the romances and chronicles (“tidynges of the knyght”), and in Chaucer, who uses tidynges repeatedly in The Canterbury Tales and Troilus and Criseyde for the reports that drive his plots — the arrival of a visitor, the outcome of a war, the state of a distant lover. The word was thoroughly everyday: a merchant's tidings were as natural a topic as the weather.

The Bible and the sacred register (16th–17th centuries). The decisive shift of register came with the Reformation translations of the Bible. William Tyndale, in his 1526 New Testament, rendered Luke 2:10 with “tydynges of greate ioye” — and the King James Version of 1611 kept the word: “I bring you good tidings of great joy”. From that moment tidings carried an evangelistic resonance: it was the word of angels, of glad announcements, of the Gospel itself (the very word “gospel” is Old English gōdspell, “good news”, and “evangel” means “good news” in Greek). The collocation “good tidings” became liturgical, and “glad tidings” equally so; the 18th-century carol “God Rest Ye Merry, Gentlemen” fixed “tidings of comfort and joy” in the popular ear forever.

The 18th–19th centuries: ordinary news, dignified tone. Throughout the 18th and 19th centuries tidings remained a normal, if slightly elevated, word for news — found in newspapers (“tidings from the Continent”), in novels (Dickens uses it freely), and in private letters. It never quite lost the gravity it had acquired from Scripture: writers reached for it when the news was weighty, solemn or joyful, and it coexisted comfortably with the newer, shorter “news”.

The 20th century and today: retreat and revival. In the 20th century journalism abandoned tidings for “news”, “reports”, “stories”. The word receded into three refuges: religious and festive language (“good tidings”, “tidings of comfort and joy” at Christmas), literary and historical prose, and genre fiction — above all fantasy, where Tolkien's use of the word (“Far over Mirkwood tidings spread”) deliberately summons the voice of old chronicles. In contemporary English, tidings is archaic by default: used straight, it sounds solemn or festive; used knowingly, it is a gentle archaism. Its survival, against all odds, in a handful of set phrases is a small monument to the Bible translation that gave it wings.
