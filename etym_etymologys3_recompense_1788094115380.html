Recompense is a word whose entire history is written in the language of the balance scale. From the Indo-European root to the modern English noun, the family story is about weighing — weighing metal, weighing money, weighing justice — and the word still carries the scales with it into every sentence it enters.

The Indo-European base. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)pend-, “to pull, to stretch, to draw out”, from which developed the sense “to weigh” — because weighing is drawing or pulling on a balance. The root's best-known offspring in Greek is spéndein “to pour a libation, to make a drink-offering”. In Latin the root produced pendere, “to hang, to weigh”, and with it the whole Roman vocabulary of money, because in the ancient world one paid by weighing out metal: pendere also means “to pay”. Latin pondus “weight” (source of English “pound” as a unit of weight), pondō “by weight”, pensum “something weighed out, a task” (source of “pension” and, through a detour, of “pensive”, the state of weighing thoughts), and expendere “to weigh out, to pay out” (source of English “spend” and “expense”) all radiate from the same root. The intransitive verb pendēre “to hang” is its sibling: to weigh and to hang are the same action seen from either end of the rope, and the twin forms explain why the same family gives us both “pendulum” and “pension”.

Roman accounting. Latin formed a frequentative verb pensāre, “to weigh repeatedly, to ponder, to counterbalance” — the source of English “pensive” (via French) and of “pension”. From pensāre with the prefix cum- “together” came compensāre, “to weigh together, to balance one thing against another, to offset” — the ancestor of “compensate” and “compensation”. The verb belongs to the language of Roman bookkeeping and law: to compensate is to set losses against payments on the scales of the account.

The re- prefix and Late Latin. To compensāre Latin added re- “back, again”, producing Late Latin recompensāre, “to weigh back, to compensate again, to requite”. The word is attested in post-classical Latin legal and ecclesiastical texts, where it carries the double sense of rewarding and punishing — paying back good for good and evil for evil, as justice requires. From Late Latin it passed into Old French as recompenser (attested from the 12th century), “to counterbalance, to compensate”, and from Old French into Middle English around 1400 as recompensen. English spelling wobbled for centuries between “recompense” and “recompence” (the noun, by false analogy with “defence”), and both forms are found in print into the 18th century.

The biblical career. The word's most influential attestations in English are scriptural. The King James Version uses it for divine requital: Deuteronomy 32:35, “To me belongeth vengeance, and recompence”, Hebrews 10:30, “Vengeance belongeth unto me, I will recompense, saith the Lord”, and Proverbs 20:22, “Say not thou, I will recompense evil; but wait on the LORD, and he shall save thee”. In these verses the word carries the full weight of the balance: God's recompense is the perfect weighing-out of every act, reward and punishment together. This theological register gave the word a solemnity it never lost — even in modern secular use, “recompense” sounds like justice being done, not like a transaction.

Modern narrowing. In the 17th–19th centuries the word was ordinary in legal and commercial English: recompense for damages, recompense of services, “in recompense for”. The 20th century saw it retreat: insurance and law now prefer “compensation”, “damages”, “indemnity”, “restitution”; everyday speech prefers “repayment” or “making it up to someone”. “Recompense” survives as the dignified word — in moral argument, in historical and fantasy prose (as in the source sentence, “the recompense of our damage, and aid for our widows and orphans”), and in the religious vocabulary of requital. The semantic journey is complete: from the stretched cord of the Indo-European balance, through the Roman weighing of money, the balancing of accounts, and the requiting of good and evil, to a formal English noun that still, two and a half millennia later, asks what is owed when the scales have been tipped.
