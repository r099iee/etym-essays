PATINA, from the Italian patina and ultimately the Latin patina, is the greenish or brownish film that forms on the surface of old bronze, copper, or brass as the metal slowly oxidizes over decades or centuries. In the world of The Story of the Stone, the "green patina" on the huge antique bronze ding, fully a yard high, is far more than a chemical accident of age: it is the physical signature of survival, the "skin of time" that quietly certifies that this vessel has sat at the centre of a great household through many generations. Its literary tone is reverent and monumental. A patina is not dirt and not neglect; it is a badge of honourable antiquity, the very opposite of the shiny, showy, new-made object. The green bloom on the ding therefore "covers" the bronze the way tradition covers the Jia family — evidence that their power is old, layered, crusted, and durable rather than recent, flashy, and borrowed. The word imports the vocabulary of the connoisseur and the antiquarian into the domestic scene, insisting that even the furnishings of the mansion participate in a long, dignified history.

Polysemy and the full range of senses:

  • The green or brown film on aged metal — the primary, material sense: the stable oxide skin (often cupric carbonate, sulphide, or chloride) on bronze, copper, or brass; a "patina of age" on an antique vessel.
  • Any mellowed surface quality acquired through time and use — the extended material sense: the soft glow of old wood, the worn sheen of well-used furniture, the bloom on old leather.
  • Figurative: accumulated character — the modern metaphor: "the patina of experience," "a patina of respectability," "the patina of history"; a quality that adheres to a person, institution, or idea through long use and age, often pleasantly or deceptively.
  • Conservation and antiquarian usage — in museums, the admired, stable "aged surface" that conservators strive to preserve, as distinct from corrosion that must be removed.
  • In the Romance languages — the Italian and French source words also name the film on metal and the mellowed surface of objects; Italian patina originally meant "shallow dish," a sense surviving in the culinary name of the Italian casserole.

In its precise meaning, patina must be distinguished from the neighbouring words of surface and decay. Verdigris is the actual bluish-green copper compound (basic copper carbonate or copper acetate) that forms on copper and brass; it is the chemical substance itself, and in the eyes of conservators it is often treated as corrosion to be removed, whereas patina is the admired, stable "aged surface" of which the verdigris is only a part. Tarnish is the thin, dull, discolouring film that forms on silver and copper and is regarded as undesirable — it is wiped away by polishing, while a patina, by contrast, is prized and carefully preserved. Rust is the reddish, crumbly, destructive iron oxide that eats away iron and steel and has no aesthetic or monetary value whatsoever: nothing is ever valued "for its rust," while everything is valued for its patina. Glaze and coating are applied finishes, not acquired ones; film is the neutral general word for any thin layer; and veneer is a thin layer added for appearance, carrying an implication of falseness that patina never has. In sum, patina is the one word of the family that turns chemical ageing into honour.
