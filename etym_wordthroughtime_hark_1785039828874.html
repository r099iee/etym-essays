- Middle English - 'hark,' shortened from 'hearken' (Middle English 'herken,' from the 'hear' family), is the everyday verb for listening; but it finds its true home in the hunt, where 'hark!' is the cry that sets hounds and huntsmen in motion.
- 16th-18th century - 'hark' becomes the imperious, attention-demanding imperative — 'hark!' calls the whole company to stillness; it lives alongside 'hearken' and takes on a commanding, ceremonial ring.
- 19th century - the word is largely displaced in ordinary speech by 'listen,' surviving in poetry, hymn, and literary diction ('Hark! the herald angels sing'); its hunting life persists in 'hark back,' of a pack retracing a lost scent.
- Contemporary - 'hark' is archaic and deliberately elevated, reserved for solemn summons, archaizing verse, and the phrase 'hark back'; it commands not merely the ears but the whole body to attend, and its old urgency now sounds spell-like and ritual rather than everyday.
