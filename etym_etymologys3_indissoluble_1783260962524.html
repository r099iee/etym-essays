A WORD BUILT ON LOOSENING At the bottom of indissoluble stands the Proto-Indo-European root *leu-, glossed "to loosen, divide, cut apart." Its oldest concrete senses are almost violent: cutting through, severing, releasing a bound thing. The root is thinly but reliably spread across the family. In Greek it yields lū́ein, "to loosen," the source of the modern scientific lexicon — lysis, analysis, paralysis, catalysis, dialysis, electrolysis. In Latin it gives luere, "to loose, set free, release, pay," and, with prefixes, the great Romance family of solvere, "to loosen away." In Germanic the same root underlies loose (Old Norse lauss), lose (Old English losian "to perish"), and German verlieren "to lose" and lösen "to loosen." What unites the branches is a single image: something bound or joined, cut through and set free. THE ITALIC ENGINE: FROM LUERE TO DISSOLVERE Italic develops the root in a distinctive direction. Latin luere, "to loose, release, pay," takes the prefix se- ("apart, by oneself," from PIE *s(w)e-) to form solvere — literally "to loosen away," hence "to unbind, release, dissolve," and (financially) "to pay," because paying a debt is loosening one's obligation. From solvere Latin grows a rich family: solūtiō "a loosening, solution"; absolvere "to set free from" (hence absolve, absolute); resolvere "to unfasten again" (resolve); and dissolvere, "to break apart, loosen asunder" (dis- "apart" + solvere), attested from the first century BCE. From dissolvere come dissolution, dissolute (morally "unbound"), dissolvent, and — by suffixing -bilis, "able to" — the potential adjective dissolūbilis, "that can be loosened apart," the adjective that names the capacity of being dissolved. The addition of the negative prefix in- then produces indissolūbilis, "not to be dissolved," the direct ancestor of the English word. THE NEGATIVE AND THE LEARNED ROAD TO ENGLISH The double negative is worth stressing: indissoluble is in- (not) + dissolūbilis (capable of being loosened apart). It therefore denies a capacity — the capacity of a bond, substance, or union to be dissolved — rather than describing mere duration. This modal force is what distinguishes it from permanent or everlasting. Latin indissolūbilis was a learned, late, and medieval word, at home in theology (the indissolubility of the marriage bond) and law; Middle French carried it into French as indissoluble in the fifteenth century, and English borrowed it about 1540, in the same wave of Renaissance Latin learning that brought dissolution, dissolve, and solution into the language. From the outset it moved between the chemist's workshop and the moralist's pulpit: the physical sense — a substance that cannot be dissolved in any solvent — provided the metaphor for the abstract sense — a bond that cannot be dissolved by any power. MACAULAY AND THE FUSION OF NATIONS The word's literary zenith is Macaulay's History of England. In the great clause "not merely by legal bonds, but by indissoluble ties of interest and affection," the adjective transfers the chemist's certainty to politics: the Act of Union of 1707, a mere legal bond and therefore repealable, is elevated into an indissoluble tie because it is woven from interest and affection — the two forces Macaulay's Whig philosophy trusted above law and force. The 19th century also used the word of marriage, empire, and confederation — the indissoluble union of the United States was invoked on both sides of the secession debate — and in all these uses the chemistry is the same: dissolution is asserted to be impossible. Today indissoluble is a formal, literary word, rarer than in Macaulay's day but still exact: it names the bond or compound for which no solvent exists.
