At its core, a crevice is a narrow opening, crack, or fissure — most naturally a split in a hard solid surface such as rock, stone, masonry, or a cliff face. The word carries an air of precision and slight literary colour: it is not just any crack, but a thin, deep, natural opening that invites the imagination to wonder what lies within or beyond.

Primary sense (narrow crack in rock or stone)

  • The everyday, dominant use: a small, narrow opening formed by splitting or weathering in a rock face, a cliff, a wall, or the ground — e.g. “a fern growing from a crevice in the cliff”.
  • The opening is understood to be natural, not made by tools; it arises from pressure, weathering, cooling, or settlement over long periods of time.
  • It is typically long and thin rather than round; a crevice is an opening far wider than it is tall, penetrating a surface in a sliver-like fashion.

Secondary and extended senses

  • In masonry and buildings: a joint or gap between stones, bricks, or timbers — the source context speaks of “a joint or crevice” in a wall, meaning any crack or gap through which one might see or through which air might pass.
  • In the ground: a split in the earth’s surface, from dry cracking of soil to a narrow gap in rock strata; this shades toward, but stays smaller than, a crevasse.
  • Figurative openings: “a crevice in the wall of secrecy” or “a crevice of opportunity” — a narrow but exploitable gap in something otherwise solid (systems, plans, arguments). Less common than “crack”, but attested in literary usage.
  • In climbing and mountaineering: any narrow crack in rock used (or avoided) as a hold or route; climbers speak of finger cracks, hand cracks, and chimneys, but a small thin split is still a crevice.

Semantic boundaries and register

  • Distinguished from crack (any break, however broad, shallow, or superficial, in any material) by being narrower, deeper, and almost exclusively natural or geological.
  • Distinguished from crevasse (a deep open fracture in a glacier, or by extension a wide chasm) by scale: a crevice is small enough to be jumped over or stepped across; a crevasse is a serious hazard.
  • Distinguished from fissure (the technical geological term for any elongated fracture) by everydayness: crevice is the common, concrete word, fissure the scientific one.
  • The word is mildly literary; in plain conversation most speakers reach for “crack” first. Crevice appears very often in adventure fiction, nature writing, and geological prose.

Historical bridge and polysemy logic

The word’s history explains its boundaries: it enters English as a plain word for any split or crack (via Old French), and only gradually becomes specialised to rock and stone. The modern restriction to narrow natural openings is the endpoint of a long narrowing: from “any rent or split” (14th c.) to “a split in stone” (18th c.) to “a narrow fissure in rock or cliff” today. The figurative uses are a late extension, borrowing the concrete image of a thin gap in something solid to speak of any narrow opening — in a market, a schedule, or a narrative.

Usage and collocations

  • Typical collocations: “a crevice in the rock/cliff/wall”, “to lodge in a crevice”, “to peer into a crevice”, “every nook and crevice”.
  • Typical verbs: open, widen, fill, conceal, peer into, grow from, nest in.
  • Register: standard to formal, with a literary flavour; no slang or vulgar uses; plural “crevices”.
  • Pronunciation: two syllables, stress on the first: /ˈkrevɪs/.

Common pitfalls

  • Not to be confused with crevasse, a much larger glacial fissure (both descend from the same French root).
  • Not a verb in standard English (“to crevice” is rare and dialectal); the noun form is overwhelmingly dominant.
  • Spelling: crev-ice; the ending is “-ice”, not “-ise”.
