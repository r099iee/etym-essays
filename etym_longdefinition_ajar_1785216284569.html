"Ajar" describes a door, window, or gate that is neither fully open nor fully shut but caught in between — slightly turned on its hinge, leaving a narrow gap through which one might glimpse light, or be glimpsed. The word is a postpositive adverb/adjective, used only after the noun ("the door stood ajar", never "an ajar door"), and its tone is quietly descriptive, almost photographic: it captures a momentary, precarious state rather than a fixed one. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2 ("Roast Mutton"), the nuance is crucial to the suspense: the door "was still ajar", but a goblin "had pushed it nearly to" — the escape route is narrowing by inches while the hobbit is still inside; the word makes the reader picture the shrinking crack of light and the creeping finality of the door's closing. Core semantics. "Ajar" always implies a partial, provisional opening: wide enough to glimpse through, too narrow for easy passage. It often carries a faint frisson of invitation, danger, or interruption — a door ajar is a door that was recently opened and may close at any moment. It is used almost exclusively of hinged closures:
  • doors (the dominant use), windows, and gates, and sometimes shutters or cabinet doors;
  • rarely and jocularly of mouths, jaws, or eyes ("his jaw was ajar" = slightly open in surprise);
  • figuratively, of a boundary or opportunity left slightly open ("the negotiations left the door ajar").
Near-synonyms, carefully separated:
  • half-open — blunter; merely reports the midpoint of a hinge's travel, without the sense of motion or precariousness.
  • cracked open / open a crack — implies only a sliver of light, often of doors and windows shut but not latched.
  • unlatched / unlocked — describe the state of the fastener rather than the position of the panel; a door can be unlocked yet shut, or ajar yet latched.
  • slightly open — the plain modern paraphrase, which loses "ajar"'s hint of being on the turn.
  • agape / wide open — the opposite extreme, flung fully open; "ajar" deliberately straddles open and shut.
  • gaping — a wide, unattended opening, without the delicate narrowness of "ajar".
Only "ajar" carries the root idea of a door "on the turn": the word descends from the Old English verb "to turn", so a door ajar is a door caught in the act of swinging, arrested mid-motion. Polysemy & register. The word has essentially a single sense (slightly open), but it has generated two living metaphoric extensions: (1) the state of being suspended between two conditions — a "door left ajar" in politics or business is an opportunity kept barely open; and (2) the Gothic and horror frisson of the unlatched opening, made famous in ghost stories and detective fiction, where a door ajar signals a presence recently passed through. Its register is literary and domestic, and its small size conceals an ancient history: from a phrase meaning "in the act of turning" (on char), the word compacted into its modern two-syllable form in the early eighteenth century, fusing what had been three words — "on char(e)" — into one.
