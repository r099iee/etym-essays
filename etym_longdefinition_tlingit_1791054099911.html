Tlingit (capitalised) denotes a matrilineal Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast, centred in southeast Alaska with communities in northern British Columbia and southern Yukon; also their Na-Dene language, and as an adjective, anything pertaining to them (Tlingit art, Tlingit fisherman). In the source sentence it identifies the cultural actor: a Tlingit hunter using seal-decoy mimicry. Pronunciation in English is commonly TLING-git; in Tlingit itself, Lingít with a lateral fricative.

Principal senses in contemporary English:

  • The people (plural proper noun): Members of the Lingít nation, organised in Raven/Crow and Eagle/Wolf moieties, clans, and house groups. Example: The Tlingit have fished salmon for millennia. Often used without -s for plural (the Tlingit are), sometimes Tlingits in older texts.
  • The language (mass noun): A Na-Dene language, distantly related to Eyak-Athabaskan, noted for ejectives, lateral fricatives, and tone. UNESCO lists it as endangered but revitalising through immersion schools. Example: She speaks Tlingit with her grandmother.
  • Adjectival / attributive: Tlingit totem pole, Tlingit canoe, Tlingit potlatch. In the passage, Tlingit fisherman means a fisherman who is Tlingit, implying traditional ecological knowledge.
  • Cultural complex: By extension, the art style (formline, Chilkat blankets), social institutions (potlatch, clan crests), and subsistence system (salmon, seal, halibut, eulachon).

Encyclopaedic and domain detail is necessary because this is an ethnonym, not a common noun. Population is about 16,000–20,000 enrolled tribal citizens plus descendants. Federally recognised tribes include the Central Council of Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska. Traditional territory (Lingít Aaní) spans the Alexander Archipelago, Glacier Bay, and the Stikine River. Economy historically rested on salmon weirs, halibut hooks, seal hunting, trade in eulachon oil (grease trails), and later fur trade. Social organisation is matrilineal: clan membership, names, songs, and crests pass through the mother. Spiritual life centres on Raven creation narratives, shamanism, and memorial potlatches. Material culture includes cedar plank houses, dugout canoes, bentwood boxes, Chilkat and Ravenstail weaving, and monumental totem poles that are clan histories, not idols.

Usage nuances and sensitivities:

  • Capitalisation: Always capitalised. Lower-case tlingit is an error.
  • Exonyms to avoid: Kolosh/Koloshi (Russian colonial), Thlinket (archaic misspelling), Indian as a generic substitute. Use Tlingit, Lingít, or specific clan/town names (e.g., Kiks.ádi, Chookaneidí).
  • Primitive framing: The source phrase clear conscience of primitive war reflects 1930s popular anthropology and is now regarded as stereotyping. Modern writing emphasises sophisticated resource management, law (at.óow ownership), and diplomacy.
  • Language vs people: Context disambiguates: speak Tlingit (language), Tlingit elder (person), Tlingit-owned (law).

Polysemy here is not semantic drift but referential extension: people → language → culture → art style. The bridge is metonymy: the name of the people naturally names what they speak and make. No secondary slang or technical jargon exists; the word has not developed metaphoric senses such as tlingit-like. Its meaning has instead narrowed in precision as scholarship replaced vague 19th-century Northwest Indian with specific nations: Tlingit distinct from Haida, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, and Athabaskan neighbours.
