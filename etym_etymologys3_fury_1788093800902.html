1. The Latin verb and its uncertain root. English fury descends from Latin furia “rage, frenzy”, the noun built on the verb furere “to rage, rave, be mad”. The origin of furere is genuinely disputed. The traditional account, followed by Pokorny's Indo-European dictionary and by popular etymological references, connects it with the PIE root *dheu- “to blow, storm, rage” — the same root that lies behind Greek θύελλα (thúella) “storm, whirlwind” and θυμός (thūmós) “spirit, anger”; the image would be of a person blown about, storming, possessed by a wind of madness. But the sound correspondence required (*dʰu- giving Latin fu-) is irregular, and de Vaan's etymological dictionary of Latin (2008) is blunt: “no reliable etymology.” The honest summary is that furere is a word of obscure origin whose association with the Indo-European storm-root is plausible but unproven. The Romans themselves felt the connection between rage and madness: furor “madness, frenzy” belongs to the same family, and furere was used of raving prophets and lovesick poets as well as of the angry.

2. The Furies. The plural Furiae was the Romans' name for the goddesses of vengeance, identified with the Greek Erinyes — the chthonic spirits born, in Hesiod's Theogony, from the blood of the castrated Uranus, who hunted down murderers, perjurers and those who violated kinship and natural order. The Latin name means “the Raging Ones”, and it is the single most important fact about the word's semantics: from the beginning, fury was not merely an emotion but a personified, divine force that pursued and punished. Aeschylus's Eumenides (458 BCE) had already transformed the Erinyes from hunters into guardians of justice, and the Roman poets — Virgil's Aeneid, where the Fury Allecto drives Amata and Turnus to war — made the Furies engines of epic catastrophe. When the word reached English, it carried this entire mythological apparatus with it.

3. The French bridge and Middle English arrival. English fury is attested from the 14th century, borrowed from Old French furie, itself from Latin furia. In Middle English the word is found in religious and didactic texts (the “furie” of God's wrath), in romance (the fury of warriors), and in classical borrowings (the Furies of pagan story). The spelling stabilised as fury, and the plural Furies remained the name of the goddesses — so the word had, from its first English century, a double life: common noun for rage, proper noun for the avenging goddesses.

4. The 16th–17th centuries: personification and gender. The Renaissance, with its passion for allegory, made Fury a stock personified figure (with snakes for hair, torch and whip, as in Spenser's Faerie Queene and the emblem books), and the phrase “a fury” came to mean a raging, violent woman — a usage that persists in melodrama and journalism. The word's gender association is a genuine semantic fact: fury, unlike rage, has a long history of being gendered feminine, thanks to the goddesses behind it.

5. The 17th–19th centuries: the elements. From the 17th century the word extends to nature: “the fury of the storm”, “the fury of the waves”, “the fury of the battle”. This is a personification-transfer: natural violence is imagined as an angry agent, and the classical storm-gods hover behind it. The 19th century adds the colloquial “like fury” (furiously, with great violence), while “furious” settles into everyday intensifying use.

6. The 20th–21st centuries: psychology, war and moral reclamation. In the 20th century psychology and self-help reclassified fury as an emotion to be managed (“anger management”), while thrillers, noir and war writing kept its destructive glamour. Two proper-name careers are notable: Nick Fury, the cigar-chomping Marvel spymaster (created by Stan Lee and Jack Kirby, 1963), and “Fury”, the British nickname for the American M10 tank destroyer, revived as the title of the 2014 film Fury. Meanwhile the moral valence of the word has shifted: “righteous fury”, “public fury”, “internet fury” now describe anger that is presented as legitimate, even admirable — a collective response to injustice — a long way from the blind madness of the Latin furor. The 2015 film Mad Max: Fury Road sums up the modern career of the word: fury as a title of honour for justified rage against oppression.

7. Conclusion. In more than two thousand years of recorded use, fury has moved from an obscure Latin verb of obscure origin, through personified goddesses, medieval wrath, Renaissance allegory, Romantic storms and modern moral rhetoric, without ever losing its core: violence of emotion so intense it seems to come from outside the self — divine, elemental, or both. Tolkien's sentence, “the hatred of their race for the dwarves had been rekindled to fury”, uses the word exactly at that pitch: an emotion that has become a force of nature.
