Trampled comes down to us from the foot itself: the word is an imitation of the heavy, repeated, thudding tread of a walker, and its history is the history of what those feet did. The stem *tramp- is common to the Germanic languages of the North Sea and the Low Countries — Middle Low German trampen, Middle Dutch trampen, Old High German trampen — and its ultimate origin is classified as imitative or expressive rather than inherited: there is no securely reconstructable Proto-Indo-European etymon. The sound of a heavy step, repeated and hammering, is written into the word itself, and the same root went on to produce modern German trampeln "to trample" and Dutch trappelen "to stamp the feet". An occasional attempt has been made to connect the family to the PIE root *drem- "to run" (whence Greek drómos "course" and Sanskrit dramáti "he runs"), on the argument that trampling involves moving feet; but the connection is uncertain and is not the mainstream view. The Germanic stem is best understood as a noise-echo of boots on the ground. The word enters English in the late fourteenth century. Middle English trampen, first attested around 1386 in the age of Chaucer, is a neutral word for walking heavily, stamping the feet — a manner of gait with no victim and no moral colour. One tramps through mud, through a march, through life; the word names the heavy step itself, the rhythm of the march, and the English long retained this noun use, "tramp", for a walker or vagrant who lives on the road. The crucial transformation happens in the mid-fifteenth century. Middle English adds the frequentative suffix -elen (the -le of repeated or continuous action), producing trampelen "to trample", first attested around 1440: to beat something down by continuous treading. With that suffix the word acquires both a victim and a meaning — no longer merely walking heavily, but crushing by repeated stepping, a crop under a herd, a person under a charge. The difference between "tramp" and "trample" is exactly the difference between one heavy step and many: the frequentative form makes the repetition grammatical. From gait to violence. The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries are the age of the word's literal expansion. Cavalry charges, mobs, stampedes, and routs all "trample" the fallen underfoot, and the word becomes the ordinary name for what armies and crowds do to the bodies beneath them — a death without agency, the accident of being under a mass. The tone is bodily and grim, and the Bible translators of 1611 pressed the word into the great condemnations: the wicked "trample" the poor and the righteous as one treads the mire. Shakespeare uses it of contemptuous violence, and throughout the early modern period "trampled" keeps its concrete, heavy, crushing image. The figurative trample of the nineteenth century. The nineteenth century completes the semantic drift. The verb extends from bodies to rights: to "trample on" a law, a feeling, or a principle is to treat it with brutal contempt, as if it were grass under boots. The heavy foot becomes the emblem of tyranny and carelessness — the phrase "trample underfoot" hardens into a formula of moral outrage, and "trampled" now carries a ready ethical charge alongside its physical one. The two senses coexist: a modern reader hears "trampled to death" in a stampede report and "trampled on his rights" in a courtroom, and knows at once that the same repeated, crushing foot is in both. Tolkien and the careless foot. In The Hobbit (1937) Bilbo fears being "bumped into, ________ to death, or caught by feel" — the goblin horde would crush him as carelessly as men trample a law. Tolkien fuses the old bodily weight with the modern moral overtone: the death is an accident, no enemy chooses it, and the goblins' feet do the killing with the same indifferent mass with which "the regime tramples the constitution". The word's history — from neutral gait-word (14th c.) to crushing verb (15th c.) to moral metaphor (19th c.) — is compressed into that single participle, and the reader who knows the road the word has walked hears in it at once the stamp of boots and the tread of tyrants. Contemporary English keeps both strands fully alive: stampede deaths are tramplings, and rights are still said to be trampled underfoot; the core — repeated, heavy, crushing feet — has not shifted in six centuries, only widened its targets from grass to crowds to rights.
