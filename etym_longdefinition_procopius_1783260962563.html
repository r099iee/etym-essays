Procopius — proper noun: the principal Greek historian of the reign of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I (r. 527–565 CE), born c. 490–507 CE at Caesarea in Palaestina Prima; secretary and legal adviser to the general Belisarius, whose campaigns he accompanied and chronicled. The name has become the classic symbol of the double-faced court historian: official panegyrist in his public works, corrosive satirist in his secretly preserved memoirs. 1. Primary sense: the historian of Justinian's wars.
  • Author of the History of the Wars (Hypèr tôn polémôn), eight books narrating Justinian's campaigns against the Persians, Vandals, and Goths (c. 550–557 CE), written in a classicizing Greek style modeled on Thucydides and Herodotus.
  • Author of the Buildings (De Aedificiis, Perì ktismátōn, c. 553–555 CE), a panegyric on the churches, fortifications, and public works raised by Justinian, presenting the emperor as a divinely favoured builder.
  • Author of the Secret History (Anecdota, "unpublished things," composed c. 550 CE but surviving in a single 14th-century manuscript and first printed in 1623), a savage, satirical attack on Justinian, the empress Theodora, and Belisarius, contradicting his own public narratives — the work that made his reputation double.
  • In the source sentence Gibbon cites him as the sober, reliable witness to ancient marvels: "as Procopius had been told" — the name standing for trustworthy ancient reportage of wonders.
2. Secondary and extended senses (polysemy).
  • Historiographical technical term: "Procopian duality" or "the Procopian problem" — the tension between the official panegyric and the private invective of the same author, a stock problem in Byzantine studies.
  • Byword for the double chronicler: in modern scholarship and literary criticism, "a Procopius" (rarely) or "a Procopian account" means a double narrative in which public praise and private denunciation coexist, often as a comment on court historians.
  • Proper name reused: Procopius was also the name of the 4th-century Roman usurper (d. 366 CE, a relative of Julian), of Procopius of Gaza (c. 465–528 CE, a Christian rhetorician), and of several Byzantine saints, including the 3rd-century martyr Procopius of Scythopolis/Caesarea.
  • Literary allusion: in late antique and modern prose the name stands for the last great classicizing historian of antiquity, the terminus of Greek secular historiography before the Byzantine chronicle tradition.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges. The personal name derives from Greek prokopḗ "progress, advancement, success," itself a compound of pro- "forward, before" and kopḗ "a cutting, a blow, a stroke," hence literally "a cutting forward," i.e., "making one's way ahead, thriving, prospering." The name Prokópios therefore meant something like "he who progresses, the thriving one" — an auspicious name of late-antique Greek Christianity. The bridge to the historian is accidental but significant: the bearer of the "progress" name chronicled the most expansionist reign of the Eastern Roman Empire, and his double corpus turned the personal name into a historiographical archetype. The modern associations (double-faced historian, witness to wonders) are metonymic, built on the man's two surviving texts rather than on the etymology of the name. 4. Language, spelling, and pronunciation.
  • Greek Prokópios (Προκόπιος) → Latin and English Procopius, with the Greek o (omicron) of the second syllable retained and the feminine-marking ending Latinized as -ius.
  • In English the stress falls on the second syllable: /pɹəʊkˈəʊpɪəs/.
  • The name is indeclinable in English (no plural form in ordinary use); in Greek it is a second-declension masculine noun.
5. Register and connotation.
  • Learned, historiographical, and primarily written; the name belongs to the vocabulary of ancient history, Byzantine studies, and literary criticism rather than to everyday speech.
  • It carries a mild frisson of scandal because of the Secret History: to invoke Procopius is to invoke both sober reportage and suppressed court gossip.
