WHISTLING is the present participle (and gerund) of the verb whistle: the production of a clear, shrill, musical sound by forcing a stream of air through a small opening — most commonly pursed lips, but also through the teeth, a whistle instrument, or a narrow vent in a machine, pipe, or windway. The participle form is continuous: whistling describes the sound while it is happening, sustained and shrilling on. In the source sentence — "...the waters rose in fierce whistling steam..." — the steam erupts from the dragon's gate with a high, cutting, shrieking hiss: the word gives the escaping vapour a voice, a piercing sound that is simultaneously hot, pressurised, and dangerous.

Primary senses

  • To make the lip-whistle (intransitive): to produce a musical note by shaping and blowing through pursed lips: "he whistled as he worked".
  • To produce a tune (transitive): to play a melody by whistling: "she whistled a folk song".
  • To signal or call: to attract attention or issue a signal with a whistle: "the referee whistled for a foul"; "he whistled for his dog".
  • Of wind, steam, air, projectiles: to make a high shrill sound in passing through or around something: "bullets whistled past"; "the kettle was whistling".

Secondary and peripheral senses

  • As noun: a whistling — a sustained shrill sound; also the wind-instrument, the referee's whistle, the steam whistle, the whistle of a kettle or train.
  • Idioms: "to whistle for something" (to want it in vain), "whistling in the dark" (feigning courage), "whistling past the graveyard" (defiant cheerfulness amid fear), "to whistle Dixie" (idle or deluded talk).
  • Technological: a "whistle" is an engineering device for emitting a shrill sound by steam or air pressure — the railway whistle, factory whistle, dog whistle, referee's pea-whistle; "whistling" describes the sound of such devices and of aerodynamic noise.
  • Aerodynamics/acoustics: "whistling" is the technical term for the high-frequency tonal noise of air moving through narrow passages, gaps, and reeds — from teapots to wind instruments and aerodynamic gaps on vehicles.
  • Modern figurative: "whistle-blowing" — exposing wrongdoing — takes its name from the sports referee's whistle that stops play; "whistleblower" is a wholly contemporary legal and journalistic term.

Semantic bridge between senses: all whistling shares one physical fact — a narrow stream of air forced through a small opening producing a pitched, shrill tone. Whether the opening is lips, teeth, a metal pipe, a projectile's wake, or a teakettle's spout, the acoustics are the same, so the same word serves the singer, the referee, the wind, and the steam.

Connotations and register

  • Cheerful and casual for the human lip-whistle (whistling at work, whistling a tune); piercing and alarming for steam and projectiles; neutral-technical in engineering.
  • The word is mildly pejorative in old proverbs about women — "a whistling woman and a crowing hen are neither fit for God nor men" — a social judgement now archaic and rejected.
  • In Tolkien's sentence whistling is fierce and elemental: the steam is not merely hot but vocal, shrieking as it rises, foreshadowing the dragon's own roaring emergence.

In the source text, whistling is a participle used as an adjective before the noun steam: the fierce, whistling steam rises, and the sound-word primes the reader's ear just before the visual blaze of the dragon's ascent — sound first, then colour and flame.
