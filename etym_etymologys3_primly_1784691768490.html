Primly is a purely English formation: the adverbial suffix -ly attached to an adjective whose ancestry runs back through medieval France to the great Latin word for “first.” The story therefore moves through three languages and some five thousand years, and each stage changes both the sound and the sense of the word.

Stage one: the Indo-European root *per-. At the bottom of the chain lies a Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *per-, meaning “forward, through, before, in front, over.” It is one of the most productive roots in the entire family, and it appears in nearly every branch with directional and temporal senses. In Latin it produced the preposition per “through, by means of” (English per, percent), pro “before, for” (English pro, proceed, promise), and the prefix prae-. In Germanic it gave far (the one who has gone “forth”) and first (a superlative built from the “foremost” sense); in Greek it gave peri “around” (English perimeter, period). The original sense was plainly spatial — movement out ahead, across, or beyond — and out of that spatial “foremost” sense the languages repeatedly built words for priority, excellence, and rank.

Stage two: Latin prīmus “first, foremost, finest.” Latin formed its ordinal prīmus as the superlative of prior “former, first,” so that the word carries within itself the whole “before / in front” root. But from the very beginning prīmus was more than a counting word: it meant “first in rank, first in time, best, chief, most excellent,” and it generated a vast derivative family — primarius “of the first rank” (English primary), prīmus in compounds such as primogenitor “first ancestor,” prīmae litterae “the first letters” (hence primer, a first reader), and prīmās “of the first rank” (English primate). Latin prīmus also produced the later Romance words for “first”: Italian primo, Spanish and Portuguese primero, French premier — and English borrowed many members of the family (prime, primary, primal, primacy, primate, primer, primeval) and, through Italian opera, primo “the leading part.”

Stage three: Old French prim “fine, delicate, slender, neat.” Old French inherited prīmus and developed, beside the ordinary ordinal sense (the numeral was largely taken over by premier), a specialised adjectival sense recorded as prim and prims: “fine, delicate, slender, neat.” How did “first” become “fine”? The bridge is the old equation of priority with excellence and refinement: what is primus is the best, the finest in quality, and from “finest in quality” the word glides to “delicate, slender, neat in appearance.” This development happened on French soil and is the crucial semantic hinge of the whole story.

Stage four: English prim, and the narrowing to manners. English borrowed the Old French adjective in the fourteenth century, at first in the sense “excellent, fine, handsome,” so that prim in Middle English could still be a word of praise. But from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries onward the adjective narrowed sharply, from fineness of quality to fineness of behaviour: stiffly proper, formally neat, demurely exact, with a growing suggestion of prudery and self-conscious correctness. The narrowing is a classic case of specialisation: a general word for excellence becomes a particular word for a fussy style of propriety, and the comic potential of “excessively proper” emerges precisely because the word retains a ghost of its old excellence — the prim person is over-excellent, too good, too tidy to be natural.

Stage five: the adverb primly. To the narrowed adjective English attached the native adverbial suffix -ly, descended from Old English -līce (from līc “body, form,” hence “in the form of”). The formation is recorded from the late sixteenth century, and from the start it served as the all-purpose manner-word of the adjective: to sit primly, to dress primly, to fold, to speak, to disapprove primly.

  • Sound shifts. The chain is phonologically conservative. PIE *per- gives Latin prīmus with the regular superlative -m-; Old French prim keeps the Latin p-r-i-m skeleton with the final -us dropped; English prim is a one-syllable direct loan; and primly merely appends -ly to form a two-syllable adverb. The interest of the word lies in its meaning, not its sound.
  • Semantic drift summary. forward / foremost (PIE) → first, chief, finest (Latin) → fine, delicate, neat (Old French) → excellent (Middle English) → stiffly proper, demure, self-consciously neat (Early Modern English) → adverb: in a stiffly formal and precise manner (16th c. onward).
  • Socio-cultural factors. The narrowing of prim happened in an age (the 16th–17th centuries) intensely preoccupied with codes of courtly and polite behaviour, with decency and comeliness in dress and deportment; the word acquired its mocking edge as conduct books multiplied and propriety became a public performance. In the 19th century it became strongly associated with Victorian respectability — the prim aunt, the prim drawing-room, the primly kept house — and it survives in modern English with that slightly period, gently amused flavour.
  • Literary attestations. The adjective prim is used by Restoration dramatists and Augustan essayists of persons of affected neatness; in the 19th century it is a favourite of Dickensian narration and of the domestic novelists; the fixed collocation “prim and proper” belongs to that century; and Tolkien’s use of “primly” in The Hobbit (1937) shows the adverb doing character-comedy work.

The result is that primly today is a small word with a long pedigree: five thousand years from a spatial preposition to a manner-adverb, a word whose history is written in its meaning — the once-excellent, once-fine adjective fixed forever in the service of demure, self-conscious neatness.
