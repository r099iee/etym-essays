RATTLING is a purely echoic, imitative word. No Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed for it: the word was coined in Middle English as a phonetic imitation of a rapid succession of sharp percussive knocks, built on the echoing skeleton r-...-t-...-l. European parallels in other branches are independent sound-symbolic creations, not cognates. Stage 1 — Echoic base *rat-t- / *rat-l- "repeated sharp knock" (sound-symbolic, no date): the acoustic pattern of a rapid, sharp, repeated percussion; a description of the sound the word imitates rather than an attested etymon. Stage 2 — Middle English ratelen (c. 1300) "to make a rapid succession of short, sharp sounds" (verb): the earliest recorded form of the word, coined directly out of the noise it names. Stage 3 — English rattle (verb, 15th century) "to emit a series of short sharp percussive sounds" (intransitive, of chains, windows, gunfire, hailstones), "to move with a rattling noise", and "to shake a thing till it rattles" (transitive). Stage 4 — Figurative and idiomatic uses (16th century onward): "to disconcert, alarm, unnerve" (to rattle someone); "to rattle on / off" of rapid, glib, fluent talk; the intensifier "a rattling good yarn" (brisk, excellent). Stage 5 — English rattle (noun, 16th century) "the instrument that rattles": a child's toy rattle; a watchman's or alarm rattle (the ratchet); the death-rattle (17th century), the terminal throat-sound; the rattlesnake (17th century), named from the horny rings of its tail that rattle as a warning. Stage 6 — English rattling (present participle in -ing): the exact form of this card, in attributive use ("rattling chains", "a rattling noise") and as a continuous-verb form, from the early modern period onward. DEBATED AND NUANCED POINTS: The echoic origin is secure and generally agreed, but the exact pre-Middle-English base *rat-t- / *rat-l- is reconstructed, not attested. Sister formations across Germanic — Middle Dutch ratelen, Middle Low German retelen/ratelen, Old High German razalōn "to crackle" (German rasseln "to rattle, clatter", rattern "to rattle") — are parallel creations on the same acoustic skeleton, NOT ancestors of the English word. Latin crepitāre "to crackle, rattle" (source of English crepitate) and Greek krotéō "to knock, clatter" are independent imitations in the same field. Two speculative links are sometimes floated but never proved: Old Norse hrata "to reel, totter", and the animal name rat (Middle Low German ratte, German Ratte), possibly named from its chattering squeak; both are kept out of the line of descent. No Sanskrit, Hindi, Latin, or Greek cognate exists in the strict genetic sense.
