Equivocating is the present participle and gerund of the verb equivocate, signifying the deliberate use of ambiguous, vague, or double-edged language in order to conceal the truth, evade a direct answer, or avoid committing oneself to a definite stance without resorting to an outright, technically falsifiable lie. It is the linguistic art of constructing statements that admit two or more plausible interpretations—one innocent or defensible, the other misleading—allowing the speaker to lead the listener into a false conclusion while preserving a technical line of retreat.

In the source passage from Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726), Lemuel Gulliver reflects upon his immersion among the noble Houyhnhnms, whose utter lack of deceit renders them incapable of understanding the concept of lying, which they describe periphrastically as ‘saying the thing which is not.’ Gulliver confesses the extreme difficulty with which he eradicated the ‘infernal habit of lying, shuffling, deceiving, and __________’ endemic to human beings, particularly Europeans. Here, equivocating occupies a distinct tier within Swift’s taxonomy of mendacity: it is not the clumsy invention of falsehood, but the refined, intellectualized vice of bad faith, wherein words are deployed as shields and decoys rather than vehicles of truth.

Core meaning and rhetorical mechanics: The defining characteristic of equivocating is its calculated exploitation of polysemy, syntactic indeterminacy, or context-dependent meaning. While a direct lie manufactures non-existent facts, equivocation manipulates real linguistic signs so that the hearer’s natural inferential process generates the falsehood. The speaker remains formally truthful according to the letter of the utterance, yet morally and communicatively deceptive in its spirit and pragmatic effect.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Deliberate evasion and non-committal discourse (primary modern sense): Using ambiguous language in public, political, or professional arenas to avoid giving a direct answer, taking responsibility, or offending opposing factions. Politicians and corporate spokespersons equivocate when facing controversial questions, producing verbose statements crafted to be interpreted favorably by mutually hostile audiences.
  • Rhetorical dodging and interpersonal hedging (conversational sense): Evasiveness in daily social interaction, such as giving equivocal replies about one’s availability, feelings, or past conduct to avoid conflict, awkward disclosures, or accountability.
  • Theological and casuistical mental reservation (historical doctrine): The practice, prominent in Counter-Reformation Europe and Tudor-Stuart England, of speaking words that are true when supplemented by an unuttered mental qualification (reservatio mentalis). Under severe religious or political persecution, casuists argued that a believer could legitimately equivocate under oath before a hostile magistrate to preserve life or protect fellow believers without committing mortal perjury before God.
  • Philosophical and formal fallacy of equivocation (logical sense): In argumentation and logic, using a word in two different senses within the premises and conclusion of a syllogism, thereby creating an invalid inference masquerading as a valid deduction (e.g., confusing ‘man’ as the human species with ‘man’ as adult male).
  • Psychological defense mechanism (behavioral sense): Maintaining internal ambivalence or sending mixed interpersonal signals to protect oneself from the vulnerability of decisive commitment, emotional exposure, or ultimate rejection.

Register, connotation, and stylistic resonance: Equivocating belongs to an educated, formal, and analytical register. It carries a heavy, pejorative moral weight, denoting cowardice, slick disingenuousness, or scholastic hair-splitting. To accuse someone of equivocating is to charge them not merely with being mistaken, but with prostituting the collaborative rules of human language for dishonest self-preservation.
