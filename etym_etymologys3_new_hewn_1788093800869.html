First element — NEW: one of the oldest words in the language. The story of new begins in the deepest reconstructable level of Indo-European. The root *néwos, "new, fresh, young", is attested across the whole ancient family: Sanskrit náva, Avestan nava, Greek néos (whence English neo-), Latin novus (whence novel, novelty, innovation), Old Irish nuae, and Hittite newa-. No single sound-change is needed to explain these: they are all direct continuations of the same adjective, which already meant "fresh, young, newly made" long before any of the daughter languages split apart. In Germanic the root took the form *niwjaz, and its first-syllable vowel was raised by umlaut to give Old English nīwe — found already in the earliest Anglian and West Saxon texts — which developed through Middle English newe into modern new, pronounced /njuː/ with the characteristic yod of the modern form.

The semantic space of NEW. From the beginning the word was richer than "made just now". It covers: newly made (a new house); newly discovered or arrived (the new world); unfamiliar (a new face); renewed, restored (new moon, new life); and additional (a new chapter). This breadth is ancient — Latin novus and Greek néos had exactly the same range — and it made the word indispensable to religion and politics: the Christian centuries spoke of the New Testament and the new heaven and new earth of Revelation (KJV: "I saw a new heaven and a new earth"), while Rome's novus homo, the "new man" who rose to power without ancestral office, bequeathed English the modern sense of a self-made newcomer.

Compounds — where NEW and HEWN finally meet. New is one of the most productive compounding elements in English history: new-born, new-found, new-fangled, newcomer, new-fallen, new-mown, new-made, new-built, and — with the archaic participial adjective of hew — new-hewn. The interesting history here is a perception shift: new-fangled, coined in the 16th century from "fangle" (to fashion), originally meant simply "fond of novelty"; within a century it had curdled into "unpleasantly trendy, gimmicky", and it is now almost always pejorative. New money versus old money (19th–20th centuries) encodes the same suspicion of the recently arrived as novus homo did in Rome. In new-hewn itself, however, the compound keeps its positive, monumental force: scripture had fixed "hewn" as the participle of divine and royal workmanship — "hew thee two tables of stone" (Exodus 34:1) — and new-hewn inherits that solemnity, describing stone and timber fresh from the axe of builders acting at need, as at the Gate of the mountain in the source passage.

Second element — HEWN: from struck to shaped. The second element has an independent and equally ancient story. English hew descends from Old English hēawan, a class VII strong verb (hēaw, hēow, gehēawen) meaning "to cut, strike, chop; to shape by cutting". Behind it stands Germanic *hawwan-, and behind that the PIE root *kau-, "to hew, strike" — whose daughters include Latin cūdere "to strike, beat" and Lithuanian káuti "to strike, fight". The Germanic family is one of the most visible in modern Europe: German hauen "to cut, strike" (colloquially, abhauen "to clear off"), Old Norse hǫggva, and Dutch houwen all continue it. The root's original centre was the blow itself — hewing as striking — and the transfer to building is the semantic heart of the word: to hew is to bring shape out of raw material by blows of axe or adze, the oldest construction technology after stacking.

The monumental career of HEWN. Old English used hēawan for cutting down trees, striking with swords, and carving stone and timber; glossaries render Latin caedere with it. In Middle English hewen was the everyday verb of building and butchery — one hewed wood, stone, flesh and enemies indiscriminately. The participle hewn (with the strong form preserved in attributive use beside the newer weak hewed) was then lifted to its defining altitude by the King James Bible of 1611: "his own new tomb, which he had hewn out in the rock" (Matthew 27:60); "Look unto the rock whence ye are hewn" (Isaiah 51:1); "they have hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). These verses made hewn the standard participle for human work upon stone — monumental, deliberate, permanent — and rough-hewn (16th century) added the nuance of work left coarse and unfinished. In 19th-century America the verb acquired a new figurative collocation, "hew to the line" — to keep strictly to a course — from the surveyor's or woodsman's practice of cutting straight along a marked line, crystallised in the proverb "let the chips fall where they may".

The union of the two elements. New-hewn is thus a compound of two ancient native words, each with deep Indo-European lines, united in a distinctly literary formation. Sound history is minimal on both sides (regular vowel developments, umlaut in new, the strong-participle retention of hewn); the real history is semantic and social — scripture giving hewn its solemnity, the compound machine of English giving new infinite partners, and the fantasy register of the 20th century (this very passage from The Hobbit) restoring new-hewn stone to the vocabulary of walls raised at need.

  • Phonetic notes: PIE *néwos > Germanic *niwjaz > OE nīwe (umlaut of *a under *-ja-); PIE *kau- > Germanic *hawwan- > OE hēawan (class VII reduplicating strong verb).
  • Register today: new is universal; hewn is archaic-literary; new-hewn is a deliberate archaism with monumental, scriptural resonance.
