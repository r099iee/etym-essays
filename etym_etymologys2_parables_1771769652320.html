Linear derivation chain for the single root word PARABLES: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *gʷelH- 'to throw, strike, hurl'. Reconstructed, not directly attested; securely reconstructed as the base of Greek ballein and its derivatives (see LIV, Pokorny). Stage 2 — Greek (attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE onward): ballein (βάλλειν) 'to throw, cast, hurl' — the verb whose root underlies a vast family of Greek compounds; the noun of action formed from it is bolē (βολή) 'a throw, a casting, a stroke.' Stage 3 — Greek (classical, 5th–4th c. BCE): paraballein (παραβάλλειν) 'to throw beside, to set alongside, to compare' — formed with the preposition para-, 'beside,' + ballein; and from it the noun parabolē (παραβολή) 'a placing beside, a comparison, an illustrative likeness' — in Greek rhetoric an extended simile or comparison set beside a truth to illuminate it (Aristotle uses the word for an illustrative parallel). Stage 4 — Greek New Testament (1st c. AD): parabolē 'a parable' — the word adopted for the teaching stories of Jesus (the sower, the mustard seed, the lost sheep, the prodigal son), where a realistic scene is set beside a spiritual truth; this is the sense that fixes the modern meaning of the word. Stage 5 — Latin: parabola 'a comparison, a proverb, a saying' — borrowed from Greek as a term of rhetoric and grammar; in Vulgar and Late Latin the word broadens to 'speech, word, talk' (the source of the Romance words for 'word': Italian parola, French parole, Spanish palabra, Portuguese palavra). Stage 6 — Old French (attested 12th c.): parabolé 'a parable, a proverb' — the religious sense preserved alongside the 'word' sense. Stage 7 — Middle English (attested 13th c.): parable 'a parable, a moral tale' — borrowed from Old French; the religious sense dominates from the first attestations. Stage 8 — English (modern): parable — 'a short instructive story'; the plural parables (as in Gibbon, who 'translated the catastrophes of nature into parables of human nature') continues the word unchanged from the Middle English borrowing. Note on debated stages and nuances: (1) The PIE root *gʷelH- is secure for Greek ballein, but the precise laryngeal shape is reconstructed rather than attested; some scholars distinguish *gʷelh₁- 'to throw' from other homophonous roots. (2) The development of Latin parabola 'comparison, proverb' into 'speech, word' in Late Latin and the Romance languages is well attested and is a separate branch of the family — it does not lie on the direct ancestor line of the English 'parable,' which preserves the Greek rhetorical-religious sense via Old French parabolé. (3) The English parabola (the conic section, 17th c.) is a separate learned re-borrowing from the Greek mathematical use of parabolē (Apollonius of Perga, 3rd c. BCE) and is a collateral member of the family, not the ancestor of 'parable.'
