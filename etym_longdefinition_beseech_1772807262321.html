Beseech means to ask someone urgently and fervently to do something — to implore, to entreat with the full weight of need. The verb is transitive and hierarchical: the speaker addresses a person who holds power over the outcome, and the asking is marked by intensity, humility, and moral pressure. Unlike the cool legal request or the neutral ask, to beseech is to place oneself at the mercy of the one addressed.

IN THE PASSAGE

In the servant's petition before Yu-cun, beseech is the climactic verb of powerless urgency. He has narrated murder, the power of a wealthy boss, and a year of official neglect; he can now only beseech Your Honour to arrest the criminals and uphold the course of justice. The word is exactly right because it combines deference with a claim on duty: he begs, but he begs for justice, not for a favour. The tone of 'Both the living and the dead will be everlastingly grateful' turns the request into a moral account that the magistrate is being asked to settle. In the world of the novel — of bribes, 'life-preservers', and bought influence — to beseech is what remains when money and connections belong to the other side.

PRIMARY SENSES

  • To implore / beg earnestly (someone to do something): 'I beseech you to reconsider' — the core modern sense, transitive with a person as object.
  • To petition solemnly: in formal, religious, or courtroom rhetoric, beseeching is the appeal of the powerless to the powerful, of the mortal to the divine, of the subject to the ruler.
  • With object + infinitive: beseech someone to do something — the standard construction.
  • Archaic-literal root sense: to seek out, to apply to; this older sense ('to go seeking a person with a request') survives only inside the fossilized petitionary meaning.

GRAMMAR AND FORMS

  • Principal parts: beseech – besought – besought (the conservative, literary, and biblical forms), alongside the modern regular beseeched.
  • The irregular past besought mirrors seek – sought and preserves the verb's ancestry in the seeking family.
  • Older religious English uses thee / thou: 'I beseech thee, O Lord' — fixed in liturgy and in the King James Bible.

REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS

  • Collocations: I beseech you (to)…, beseech someone for mercy / help / pardon / forgiveness, humbly / earnestly beseech, beseech on bended knee (rhetorical).
  • Register: literary, rhetorical, religious, and historical-fiction English. In everyday modern speech people say beg, please, or I'm asking you; beseech is chosen when a writer wants elevation, biblical colour, or the pressure of absolute need.

BOUNDARIES WITH NEAR-SYNONYMS

  • Ask is neutral and everyday, with no built-in intensity.
  • Request is polite and formal, often written, and can sound bureaucratic.
  • Beg is close in desperation but can imply abjection or street-begging; beseech is elevated and literary.
  • Implore stresses emotional distress; entreat earnest asking; beseech adds an older, almost biblical or courtroom solemnity.
  • Plead often implies argument before a court or a special case; supplicate is more ceremonial and prayer-like; pray (in its archaic sense 'to ask earnestly') is closer still to divine address.
