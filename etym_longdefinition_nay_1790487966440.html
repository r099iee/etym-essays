nay is an old-fashioned and still formal word for no, used to refuse, deny, or vote against, and — very commonly in literature including Ruskin — an adverb meaning and indeed, or rather, even when strengthening or correcting what was just said. In the source sentence — they not only did more, but in proportion got less, than other people — nay, that the best of them were even ready to govern for nothing — nay carries this second, corrective sense: what was just claimed is not enough; indeed, the truth is stronger. Kings did not merely take less; nay, they would govern for nothing at all. The dash sets it off as a rhetorical lift, a Victorian preacher's pause before the climax.

The negative sense survives in voting and dialect. Parliamentary tellers still cry ayes and nays, American roll-calls record nays, Quaker and northern speech keeps nay for a blunt no. To say nay to a proposal is sterner and more archaic than saying no; it sounds like oath-taking. Shakespeare is full of it: nay, I know not, nay, hear me out. Children meet it in ballads and Bibles: nay, we will have a king to judge us.

  • Simple negation (oldest voting sense): no as answer, refusal, or vote; e.g. to say nay, a nay vote, nay, I will not. Historically nay answered questions without a negative word (will you go? nay), while no answered negative ones — a distinction lost by 1600. The bridge to modern use is fossilisation: legislatures froze the old contrast in procedure.
  • Corrective-intensifying sense (Ruskin's sense): introduces a stronger restatement; e.g. he is learned, nay, profound; a year, nay, a decade of toil. Equivalent to indeed, or rather, what is more. The semantic bridge is denial of insufficiency: you deny your own understatement (nay, that is too weak) and replace it with more. This is why nay so often follows not only.
  • Prohibitive and discourse sense: nay as interjection checking the hearer; e.g. nay, hear me; nay, do not weep; nay, but tell me truly. Here nay softens command with familiarity, like come now. The bridge is pragmatic: a mild no to interruption becomes a call for attention.
  • Noun sense: a nay as a vote or utterance: a refusal, a negative vote, a denial; e.g. ten ayes and five nays, to meet with a nay, without nay. Plural nays rhymes with ayes /aɪz/. The bridge is nominalisation: the word said becomes the act counted.
  • Verb sense (rare, archaic): to nay-say, to deny or refuse; e.g. to nay a motion, naysaying neighbours. Modern English prefers gainsay, deny, naysay as compounds. The bridge is functional shift, common with interjections.
  • Idiomatic survivals: nay-sayer (one who habitually refuses or predicts failure), nay-saying, the ayes have it over the nays. Pessimists are naysayers even when they never utter nay. The bridge is lexical fossil: a dead everyday word lives in compounds.

Register is crucial. To use nay in conversation today sounds Yorkshire-blunt, Quaker-plain, biblical, or joking-antique. To use it in an essay, as Ruskin does, sounds periodic and climactic — a trumpet before the moral. Learners must not confuse nay with neigh (horse's cry, though homophonous /neɪ/) or with neh slang.

Polysemy thus turns on two pivots: denial of fact (no) and denial of adequacy (nay, more than that). Both deny, but one closes a question while the other opens a stronger claim. Ruskin's kings need the second: ordinary language understates their selflessness, so nay corrects it upward.
