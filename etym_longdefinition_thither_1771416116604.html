Thither is an archaic directional adverb meaning "to or toward that place," and it appears here at the very head of the sentence in David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone: "Thither, having chosen a suitable day on which to commence his journey, and having first taken his leave of Jia Zheng, he now repaired to take up his duties." The fronted position is deliberate and ceremonious: the adverb stands at the march-step of the prose, giving the departure the measured cadence of an official progress. Unlike static there, which names a place one occupies, thither names a place one is moving into — it is a goal-word, allative, full of direction and destination. Paired with the equally formal repaired, it frames the journey not as a trip but as a transition between spheres of influence: the character leaves Jia Zheng's orbit and betakes himself to the seat of his new duties, and the archaic diction lends the move an almost ceremonial gravity, as though the whole household paused to watch him go.

Linguistically, thither belongs to the ancient demonstrative system of English, in which distance and direction were kept strictly apart. The full grid of the old system is worth laying out, because it explains every sense and every boundary of the word:

  • Place vs. direction: here (static, "at this place") stood against hither (motion, "to this place"); there ("at that place") stood against thither ("to that place"); where ("at what place") stood against whither ("to what place").
  • The ablative triple: a parallel set marked origin rather than goal — hence ("from this place"), thence ("from that place"), whence ("from which place"). Thither is thus the exact opposite in direction of thence: thence names a point of departure, thither a point of arrival.
  • Derived forms: the adverbs hitherward(s), thitherward(s), and whitherward(s) ("in the direction of this/that/which place") are rarer, more emphatic variants built on the same stems; thitherward means "toward that place, in that direction," and survives only in the most archaic prose.

Polysemy in the strict sense is limited, because thither has kept a single core meaning for a thousand years; what varies is register and construction, and these are worth detailing exhaustively:

  • Archaic / literary sense: "to that place, toward that place," after verbs of motion — "he thither repaired," "they went thither." This is the primary and, in modern English, almost the only sense.
  • Figurative / extended sense: "to that state, point, or conclusion" — as in "we argued thither" (to that conclusion); a marginal but attested extension of the spatial word into logical or narrative direction.
  • The frozen phrase "hither and thither": "in this direction and that, from place to place, aimlessly," describing restless or disordered movement; this is the phrase in which the word survives most idiomatically in modern English.
  • Purely archaic and dialectal survival: in some regional dialects of England and Scotland thither (often as thithera) persisted as an ordinary word of direction well into the modern period, and deliberate uses in historical fiction, fantasy, and liturgical pastiche rely on this deep archaic resonance.
  • Grammatical note: as an adverb thither does not take a following preposition of its own ("thither to X" is rare and poetic); it commonly stands alone or at the head of a clause, as in the passage above, where its fronting gives the sentence its formal, biblical cadence.

The synonym boundaries are ancient and precise. There is the static locative ("at that place"); thither is the motion-to sense ("to that place"), and modern English has collapsed the two into one, so that "go there" now does the work that once required "go thither." Whither is the interrogative and relative partner ("to what place?"), so that the old triple hither (to this place), thither (to that place), whither (to which place) mirrored the triples hence/thence/whence and here/there/where. Hence is the opposite direction — ablative, "from this place" — where thither is goal-bound: hence marks an origin, thither a destination. Yonder points at something visually distant and in sight, whereas thither names a goal without needing to see it; and the periphrasis to that place is simply the neutral modern equivalent, stripped of all archaic colour.
