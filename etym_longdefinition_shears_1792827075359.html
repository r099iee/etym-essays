Shears are large, heavy scissors: a two-bladed cutting tool with crossed pivoted blades and looped handles, made in sizes from tailor's shears to gardener's hedge shears to the sheep-shearing blades whose clack Sam fills the source sentence with as he works nearer the windows.

The sentence pairs the tool with its music. One never reads of silent shears; the blades meet with a clack, the handles squeak, the cut branches fall. In the dark silent room the garden enters only as sound, and shears carry it: honest steel labour against grave counsel, Sam's peaceable war on the hedge beside the gathering war on Mordor.

Core meaning in detail: Shears differ from scissors chiefly in scale and strength: scissors are light, finger-looped, for paper and thread, while shears are weighty, often with one large bow for several fingers, built to cut hedge, cloth, metal, or fleece. The word is plurale tantum — always plural in form, like trousers or tongs — because the tool is essentially a pair: two blades, one action, a singular made of a double. Garden shears, pruning shears, pinking shears, sheep shears, and tinsmith's shears are the great tribes.

The cutting root — the bridge: Shears descend from PIE *sker-, to cut, the most prolific cutting root in English, father of shear, share, shore, score, sharp, short, and shirt. The bridge from cutting to this tool is specification: of all the things that cut, the pivoted double blade earned the old noun scear, and the plural hardened around it because the blades come only in pairs. To shear a sheep is the same stroke writ large across a flock.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Pivoted double-bladed cutting tool (primary modern sense): hedge shears, tailor's shears, kitchen shears. The bridge is direct inheritance: the Old English cutting implement grown heavier and more specialised with every trade.
  • Sheep-shearing blades (pastoral sense): the spring shears that strip the fleece in one piece. The bridge is the verb to shear at its oldest labour: shearing time, shearers, and the shearling lamb named for its first shearing.
  • Large scissors generally, including dressmaking: a pair of shears on the cutting table. The bridge is scale: dress shears are scissors grown to the measure of long cloth, and tailors keep the grander noun for the grander tool.
  • Metal-cutting snips (industrial sense): tinner's shears, bench shears, the guillotine shear. The bridge carries garden steel into the workshop: same crossed blades, harder stock, longer leverage.
  • A shearing framework or hoist (nautical and engineering): shear legs, two spars lashed at the top for lifting masts and guns. The bridge is visual: the legs open and close like blades, and sailors named the crane for the scissors.
  • The Fates' shears (mythic image): Atropos with her shears snipping the thread of life. The bridge moralises the tool: what cuts wool cuts years, and every shearing carries a shadow of the final cut.
  • A shorn result, a cutting (rare): the shear of wool, the shearling's fleece. The bridge turns action into produce: the blades' work named for the blades, as a cut is named for the knife.

Register and connotation: shears sound honest, rural, and capable — Sam's word, the gardener's companion, smelling of oil and boxwood. Against swords and rings they promise proportion: growth to be trimmed, not enemies to be slain. Even the Fate with shears feels less cruel than the warrior with a sword, since shearing gathers the fleece for use while slaying only spends.
