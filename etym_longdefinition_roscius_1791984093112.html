Roscius is a Latin masculine proper name — more precisely a gentilicium, the hereditary clan name of the gens Roscia — and in English it functions almost exclusively as a historical label. In the source sentence it denotes Sextus Roscius of Ameria, the young Umbrian landowner whom Cicero defended on a charge of parricide in 80 BCE, at the age of twenty-six, in the first criminal case of his career. The passage's phrasing — “having successfully defended Roscius in an important case in the year 80” — compresses one of the most closely studied trials of the late Republic into a single clause.

The name has no ordinary English sense. It is not a common noun, has no plural, and is never used figuratively except in the archaic compliment “a Roscius”, meaning a supreme actor, which belongs to a different bearer of the same clan name.

The case

Sextus Roscius's father was a wealthy equestrian of Ameria (modern Amelia, in Umbria), a man of local standing and Roman connections. He was murdered in Rome, probably in 81 BCE, while the city was still reeling from Sulla's civil war and the proscriptions that followed it. The murder was not a private crime committed for its own sake: it was the first move in a profitable confiscation.

  • The proscription device — after the killing, the dead man's name was entered on the lists of the proscribed, which made his property forfeit and allowed Sulla's agents to sell it. The purchaser was Chrysogonus, Sulla's powerful freedman, who acquired an estate Cicero values at six million sesterces for a token two thousand.
  • The family — two relatives, Titus Roscius Magnus and Titus Roscius Capito, had taken possession of farms, slaves and household property. If the son inherited, they and Chrysogonus would lose everything, so the accusation of parricide was the cleanest way to remove him: a convicted parricide could not inherit.
  • The court — the quaestio de sicariis, the standing court for murder and poisoning reorganised by Sulla's Lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficis of 81 BCE. The prosecutor was Gaius Erucius, a professional accuser of the kind the late Republic produced in numbers.
  • The charge — parricidium, the murder of a parent, the most polluted crime in Roman law. Its penalty was the poena cullei: the condemned man was beaten with rods, sewn into a leather sack with a dog, a cock, a viper and an ape, and thrown into the sea or a river.
  • The defence — Cicero could not attack Sulla directly, so he aimed at Chrysogonus, argued that the purchasers of the estate were the real killers, and produced witnesses that Sextus had been in Ameria when his father died. The speech is a masterclass in attacking a powerful man's agent while leaving the powerful man himself untouched.
  • The result — acquittal. The published speech, Pro Sexto Roscio Amerino, is Cicero's earliest surviving criminal oration and the first public sign of the courage he would later show against Catiline and Antony.

The other Roscius

The nomen is shared with a second famous man. Quintus Roscius Gallus (c. 126–62 BCE) was the greatest comic actor of the late Republic, a friend of Cicero and of Sulla, who raised him to equestrian rank. Cicero later defended him in a dispute with a business partner, the Pro Q. Roscio Comoedo. Because of the actor's prestige, “Roscius” became a byword in English for an unsurpassable performer: eighteenth-century London hailed David Garrick as “the English Roscius”, and nineteenth-century critics applied the name to favourite tragedians. That allusive sense is the only non-literal life the word has had, and it is now archaic.

Etymology and uncertainty

The origin of the gentilicium is not recorded. Roman clan names were commonly formed from a place, a trade, a physical trait or an ancestor's praenomen, and the suffix -ius was the ordinary gentilic ending; but no ancient author explains Roscius, and no secure ancestor can be reconstructed.

  • Latin rūscus or ruscum, “butcher's broom”, a prickly shrub used for brooms, is sometimes floated as a plant-name source; it would make the name a rustic or occupational one, but there is no evidence beyond the resemblance.
  • Latin russus, “red”, is the source of the familiar modern guess that Roscius means “reddish”; the guess rests on sound alone, and the -sc- of Roscius is not explained by russus.
  • An Umbrian or Etruscan origin is plausible because the family came from Ameria, in the old Umbrian territory, where local names of non-Latin shape were common; but this too is inference, not record.

Register and use

The word belongs to classical scholarship, legal history and the history of oratory. It is capitalised, invariant, and normally qualified — “Sextus Roscius”, “the Roscius case”, “the actor Roscius” — because the bare name is ambiguous between the defendant and the player. In modern writing it carries a scholarly, slightly archaic colour and almost never appears outside a discussion of Cicero, the proscriptions or Roman comedy.
