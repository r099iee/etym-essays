praetor, noun (Latin praetor, genitive praetoris, masculine). In Roman antiquity, a senior magistrate of the Roman people, ranking immediately below the consuls and principally charged with the administration of justice; also, under the Empire, a holder of that dignity as an honorary senatorial rank, and, in the Greek East, a provincial governor under the Byzantine name praitor. In the source sentence — “Three years later he took the next step in the sequence of civil honors in Rome when he became praetor” — the blank names a precise rung of the cursus honorum, the office Cicero held in 66 BCE, the year after his curule aedileship and three years before his consulship. It is not a loose word for “judge”: it is a dated constitutional post.

The Republican office

  • Origin: The praetorship was created, according to Livy, by the Licinio-Sextian legislation of 367 BCE, chiefly to relieve the consuls of the burden of jurisdiction in the city. The first holder was styled praetor urbanus, and for a century and a half he was the only one.
  • Expansion: A second praetor, the praetor peregrinus, was added about 242 BCE to hear cases involving foreigners; two more were created in 227 BCE for Sicily and Sardinia, and two again in 197 BCE for the Spanish provinces. Sulla raised the number to eight, Caesar to sixteen, and the early emperors adjusted it further — twelve under Augustus, sixteen under Tiberius, eighteen under Claudius.
  • The cursus honorum: The praetorship sat between the quaestorship or aedileship and the consulship. Under Sulla's regulations a candidate had to be at least thirty-nine; the office conferred imperium and the right to stand for the consulship, and it was therefore the decisive step for any ambitious senator.
  • Insignia and powers: A praetor wore the toga praetexta, sat on the sella curulis, and was attended by six lictors inside the city, twelve outside it. He could command troops, preside over certain assemblies and, above all, speak with the authority of a magistrate of the Roman people.

What the praetor actually did

  • Jurisdiction: He did not try cases himself in the modern sense; he defined the issue. A plaintiff applied to him, and he granted or refused an action by issuing a formula that set out the claim and instructed a iudex to decide it.
  • The edict: At the start of his year each praetor published an album listing the actions he would grant. Since each new incumbent reissued his predecessor's edict with amendments, the edict became a cumulative body of rules — the ius honorarium or praetorian law — by which Roman equity grew.
  • Remedies: Beyond the formula, the praetor could issue interdicta (orders), grant bonorum possessio (possession of an inheritance), order restitutio in integrum (restoration to a former position) and appoint guardians — powers that let him reshape substantively unfair outcomes.
  • Provincial command: After his year in office a praetor was normally prorogued into a province as a propraetor, governing with the same imperium. This is why the term shades into “governor” in imperial usage.

Later senses and the semantic bridge

  • Imperial honorary rank: Once the emperor absorbed the real work of government, the praetorship became a costly dignity. The praetors of Rome still existed in the fourth century, but their chief duty was to fund public games, and the title functioned as a senatorial grade rather than an office.
  • Specialised praetors: New jurisdictions were carved out — a praetor fideicommissarius for trusts in the first century CE, a praetor tutelaris for guardianship under Marcus Aurelius, and two praetores aerarii for the treasury under Augustus.
  • Byzantine praitor: In the tenth and eleventh centuries the Greek πραίτωρ reappears as the title of a provincial governor and judge, a direct continuation of the late Roman use.
  • The bridge between senses: Every extension runs through the idea of a man who goes first and speaks with authority — from general to magistrate to judge to provincial governor to honorary dignitary. The judicial sense is not a metaphor but a specialisation of command.

Derivatives and modern use

Latin praetorium meant a general's tent and then a governor's residence, and appears in Greek as πραιτώριον in the New Testament for Pilate's headquarters. The cohors praetoria, a commander's bodyguard, became the Praetorian Guard of the emperors, and from it come praetorian prefect, praetorianism (the political science term for military interference in politics) and the modern figurative “praetorian guard” for a leader's loyal enforcers. English praetor itself has never developed a living general sense: outside Roman history it is purely bookish.

Register and collocations

The word is formal, antiquarian and capitalised only when it begins a sentence. It collocates with urbanus, peregrinus, edict, imperium, cursus honorum, propraetor and praetorian. Its connotation is neutral and technical; the emotional weight in English sits in the adjective praetorian, which carries a suggestion of praetorian menace.

Why it matters in this reading

The sentence is a career chronology, and “praetor” is its pivot. Cicero had been quaestor in Sicily in 75, curule aedile in 69, praetor in 66 and consul in 63. To say that he became praetor is to say that he had entered the small group from which consuls were drawn — and, for a man from Arpinum with no consular ancestors, that was the decisive promotion.
