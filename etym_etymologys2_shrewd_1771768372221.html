Derivation chain for SHREWD (single root word; no secure PIE ancestor — Germanic–English path only):
1. Old English scrēawa “shrew-mouse” (family Soricidae), attested by the 10th–11th c.; the animal name has NO secure attested Indo-European ancestor and is probably expressive or echoic (the creature’s shrill squeak, its frantic gnawing). Older proposals of a Germanic root *skreu- “to cut, to shred” are speculative.
2. → Middle English shrewe (late 13th c.) “a wicked, malicious, evil person” (figurative transfer from the venomous little beast of folk-belief).
3. → by the 16th c., specialised sense “a scolding, bad-tempered woman” (Shakespeare, The Taming of the Shrew, c. 1590).
4. → denominal verb Middle English shrewen “to be wicked; to curse.”
5. → past participle Middle English schrewed, shrewd (c. 1300) “wicked, evil, malicious, depraved” — literally “cursed.”
6. → c. 1500–1540s semantic turn: “cunning, artful, knowing, sharp” — cleverness re-read as dangerous sharpness (the same path as English crafty, sly).
7. → 16th–17th c.: “keen, acute, sharp-witted, astute.”
8. → Modern English shrewd “astute; keen in practical judgment,” fully positive by the 18th c. — OUR CARD: SHREWD.

Debate / nuance: no PIE root can be responsibly claimed for the Old English animal name scrēawa; the origin is marked obscure in the OED. The semantic journey from “cursed, wicked” to “astute” is the celebrated reversal: the 16th-century admiration of cunning re-valued a word that had meant danger, in exactly the way crafty and sly darkened in the opposite direction.
