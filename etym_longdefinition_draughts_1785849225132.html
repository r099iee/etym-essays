DRAUGHTS is the plural of the noun draught (British spelling; American draft), and every one of its meanings radiates from the ancient verb to draw — "to pull, drag, extract, attract". In the reading passage the word is used in its oldest, most literal plural sense: large draughts of water, whole mouthfuls of liquid drawn into the mouth and swallowed — "To drink heartily, copiously, and with relish in large draughts". A draught is here one complete pull at the drinking vessel, and the plural makes the drinking long, deep, and repeated: each swallow is a separate act of drawing water in, so that the sentence pictures drinking as a series of satisfying pulls. This is the drinking sense that the card's sentence serves, and it is the sense English speakers have used since the fourteenth century, when a "draught" of drink meant a single amount swallowed at one go — the exact mental image of water being drawn (pulled) from a vessel up into the mouth.

Yet the same word, above all in British English, has accumulated an extraordinarily wide family of further senses across seven centuries, all of them still bound to the root idea of something being drawn or pulled:

  • Board game sense: "draughts" is the British name of the game Americans call checkers, played on a chequered 8x8 board with flat round counters that are "drawn" (moved) diagonally forward — the pieces are, literally, draughts that are drawn across the board. The game name is recorded from about 1400.
  • Current of air: a "draught" is a current of cool air that is drawn into a room through a gap, window, or chimney — air being pulled into a space, the same "drawing" image transferred to ventilation; a British household staple of complaint ("shut the window, there's a draught").
  • Nautical sense: a ship's "draught" (spelled "draft" in America) is the vertical depth of water a vessel requires to float — the depth to which the hull is "drawn down" into the sea; recorded from the fifteenth century.
  • Brewing and hospitality: "draught beer" is beer drawn (pulled) from a cask, keg, or barrel through a tap rather than served from a bottle or can — the drink is pulled out of its container, exactly as liquid is drawn in drinking.
  • Medicine and pharmacy: a "draught" is a liquid dose of medicine to be swallowed at one gulp — a measured potion "drawn off" for a single swallow; the sense persists in formal pharmacy.
  • Animal draught: a "draught horse", "draught ox", or "draught animal" is an animal whose strength is drawn upon to pull a plough or wagon.
  • Fishing: a "draught of fish" is a single catch hauled in — fish drawn up in the net at one pull.
  • Engineering and furnaces: a furnace or chimney has a "draught" — the air current drawn through it that feeds combustion.
  • American spelling senses (draft): the same word spelled draft in American English covers money orders (a bank draft), conscription (military draft), preliminary writing (a draft essay, a rough draft — writing pulled together), and sports (player draft, from drawing lots).

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: all of these senses, from mouthfuls of water to board games to bank drafts, descend through the single image of drawing. Proto-Germanic *draganą "to draw, carry, pull" gave Old English dragan "to draw, drag", and the noun draught (Middle English draught, from the past stem of the verb) literally meant "that which is drawn, an act of drawing". From this one noun the language repeatedly extended the word along the same logical route: pulling air gave the ventilation sense; pulling liquid into the mouth gave drinking; pulling water beneath the hull gave the nautical depth; pulling beer from a cask gave draught beer; pulling game pieces across a board gave the game; pulling a net gave a catch; pulling a plough gave draught animals; and pulling together a preliminary document gave the draft. Modern American English simplified the spelling to draft for most senses, while British English kept the historical draught for liquids, air, ships, and the game — which is why the card's sentence, with its "large draughts" of water, reads as distinctly British and slightly literary to a modern ear. The plural DRAUGHTS itself is the form under which the drinking sense and the game sense most commonly appear.
