The history of tumult is a study in the power of a single image to organise meaning across two thousand years. The word descends from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning simply "to swell," and everything it has ever meant — the swelling of a body, the surging of a crowd, the din of a marketplace, the churning of the soul — is a variation on that one physical picture. English keeps all of it alive at once: when we speak of the "tumult of the marketplace," we are unconsciously repeating a metaphor a Roman senator might have recognised.

The root: swelling (PIE and Latin)
  • The chain opens with Proto-Indo-European *teuh₂-, "to swell, be puffed up, surge." The root is admirably well attested across the family: in Germanic it gives Proto-Germanic *þūmô, "the swollen one" — our English thumb; in Hellenic it gives Greek tumbos, "a swollen heap of earth," a burial mound, source of English tomb; in Indo-Iranian it gives Sanskrit stūpa, a mound or heap.
  • Latin inherits the root as tumere, "to swell," attested from the classical period and visibly productive: tumor is the body's swelling (our medical "tumour"), tumescere is "to begin to swell," and tumentia are "swellings." The Roman medical and poetic writers used the verb of swollen wounds, swelling seas, and swelling rage.
  • The important step for our word is the application of the swelling image to society. Roman historians and jurists watched crowds and armies gather, surge, and boil over; the verb for what the crowd did was tumere — the mob swelled. From this came the noun tumultus, "a swelling up into commotion."
The Roman word: a legal and military emergency
  • In Roman public life tumultus was a technical term as much as an everyday one. A tumultus was a sudden, unannounced rising — of an enemy at the frontier, of a mob in the city — that swelled into danger so quickly that the normal procedures of declaring war could not be followed. The famous tumultus Gallicus, the Gallic emergency, forced Rome to levy her citizens in the forum itself.
  • The word thus carried from birth a double meaning that English has kept: it named both the noise and confusion of the rising and the physical surging mass that made it dangerous. A tumultus was loud because it was swollen; it was swollen because it was a crowd or army in motion.
  • Latin writers also applied the image inward. Tumultus mentis, "the tumult of the mind," appears in classical prose for emotional upheaval — the feelings swelling and churning like a mob in the breast. The psychological sense is therefore not an English invention but a Latin inheritance already active in Cicero.
Into French and English
  • Latin tumultus passed into Old French as tumulte in the twelfth century, broadening from the military emergency to any noisy disturbance or public disorder — a marketplace, a quarrel, a riot. The specifically legal weight faded as the word became a general term for commotion.
  • English borrowed tumulte around 1400, first in historical and literary contexts describing battles, sieges, and public disorders. Spenser, Shakespeare, and Milton all use it richly: Shakespeare speaks of the "tumult" of war and of the passions, Milton of the "tumult" of rebellion in Paradise Lost — the swelling chaos of a fallen host.
  • The spelling settled to modern "tumult" by the sixteenth century, and the noun quickly grew its full modern family: tumultuous (from Latin tumultuosus, "full of commotion"), the rare verb to tumult ("to raise an uproar"), and the adverb tumultuously.
The modern range
  • Today tumult occupies a precise place on the spectrum of disorder. It is louder and more physical than "turmoil" (which is inward and confused), more dignified than "racket" (which is just noise), less extreme than "pandemonium" (which is madness). Its signature is the surging mass: a crowd, a sea, an army, a heart — anything that swells and heaves.
  • The marketplace example captures the full range: "The tumult of the marketplace made it impossible to hear anything." The noise is there, but so is the image of the crowd swelling and churning like a flood — the ancient Latin metaphor, still doing its work.
  • Its figurative life is as strong as its literal one. A mind "in tumult" churns like a mob; a "tumult of emotions" surges through the breast. The word has become one of English's most reliable bridges between the physical and the psychological, always carrying the buried image of something swelling past its bounds.
  • Two thousand years after a Roman crowd first "swelled" into an uproar, the word still means exactly that: things swelling, surging, and spilling into noise and disorder — in the street, in the storm, or in the heart.
