- 1851 — "man, fellow" — first recorded in British slang; a low-register, urban word.
- Late 19th c. — "ordinary man" — music-hall and working-class speech give it a matey, unpretentious flavour.
- Early 20th c. — "decent fellow" — a good bloke becomes a moral compliment, especially in Australia and New Zealand.
- World War I — "mate, soldier" — army slang and ANZAC culture entrench the word as a badge of egalitarian masculinity.
- Mid-20th c. — "any man" — the word spreads through British popular culture and loses its specifically lower-class edge.
- Late 20th c. — "blokey" — the adjective marks a self-consciously masculine, beer-and-football style.
- 21st c. — "guy" — global English often substitutes the American word, while bloke survives as a marker of British, Irish, Australian or New Zealand identity.
