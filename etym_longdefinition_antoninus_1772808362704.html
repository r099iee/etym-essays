In Gibbon's first chapter, Antoninus is a chronological pivot rather than a biographical ornament. The sentence — "from the death of Marcus Antoninus, to deduce the most important circumstances of its decline and fall" — makes the death of the emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus (AD 161-180) the hinge on which the whole Decline and Fall turns: everything before that death is the prosperous condition of the empire; everything after is decline. Gibbon deliberately writes "Marcus Antoninus" rather than "Marcus Aurelius": the choice stresses the Antonine dynasty and the tradition of adoptive succession that ended when Commodus, a natural son, succeeded his father. The proper name functions as a milestone in a designed historical argument. Core reference. Antoninus is the cognomen (family-name element) borne by the last of the Five Good Emperors, Marcus Aurelius Antoninus; it is also the family name of the Antonine dynasty, shared with his adoptive father Antoninus Pius (AD 138-161). Polysemy and modern senses. "Antoninus" is a proper name, so its "senses" are the distinct referents the name can pick out:
  • Marcus Aurelius Antoninus (AD 161-180): the Stoic emperor-philosopher, author of the Meditations, the last of the Five Good Emperors; the referent in Gibbon's sentence.
  • Antoninus Pius (AD 138-161): his adoptive father and predecessor; the name Antoninus attaches to the founder of the dynasty's peaceful image.
  • The Antonine dynasty: the line of adoptive emperors (Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius) that Gibbon counts as the "happy period" of more than fourscore years; sometimes widened to include Commodus' reign.
  • The Antonines (plural): standard shorthand for Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius jointly, as in Gibbon's list "Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, and the two Antonines".
  • The Antonine Wall: the Roman frontier fortification in central Scotland (AD 140s), built under Antoninus Pius; the name of the emperor is fixed in archaeology.
  • The Age of the Antonines: a historiographical period-label for the second-century height of the Roman Empire, praised by Gibbon as the period during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous.
  • The Antonine Plague (AD 165-180): the great epidemic of the late 160s that devastated the empire; named after the ruling dynasty.
  • Later imperial bearers: the name "Marcus Aurelius Antoninus" was adopted by later emperors — notably Caracalla (formally Marcus Aurelius Antoninus) and Elagabalus — extending the title into the third century.
  • Onomastic and devotional uses: Antoninus is a Christian saint's name (Saint Antoninus of Florence, 1389-1459, a Dominican archbishop); the Roman cognomen also survives in given names such as Italian Antonino.
  • False-etymological resonances: medieval and popular tradition linked the name to Greek anthos "flower" (hence the unhistorical h in English "Anthony") and to anti- "against"; both are folk etymologies rejected by scholarship.
Historical bridge. The bridge from person to dynasty to period is institutional. Because the Antonine emperors ruled by adoption for a century, the family name Antoninus became a brand of virtuous, stable rule; when Gibbon needs a single date on which the happy period ends, the death of the last legitimate Antonine (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus) is the natural hinge — and the name itself carries the dynastic weight that makes the pivot meaningful. Register and collocation. In historical prose the name collocates with death, succession, and era-boundaries: "the death of Marcus Antoninus", "the age of the Antonines", "the Antonine dynasty", "the Antonine Wall", "Antonine history". Register is formal and classical; modern popular history prefers "Marcus Aurelius", while Gibbonian and eighteenth-century historiography use "Antoninus".
