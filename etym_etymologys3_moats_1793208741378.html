The history of moats is the history of the Norman castle written in earth and water. When the conquerors raised their motte-and-bailey fortresses across England after 1066, each timber tower stood on an artificial mound — the motte, from Old French mote meaning a mound — and the soil for that mound came from a great trench dug around it. Heap and hollow were born together, and the single word covered both: the mound that defenders stood on and the ditch that attackers drowned in. Within a century the ditch sense had won, and by c. 1300 Middle English mote meant what it means today.

Engineering the water barrier:

  • Choosing the site: the finest moats were not dug so much as plumbed — builders sited castles beside rivers and marshes, then dammed and diverted the flow so nature filled the ditch for them, as at Bodiam, Leeds, and Caerphilly with its vast mirrored lakes.
  • Wet versus dry: where water was scarce, engineers dug dry moats with scarped rock sides, trusting depth and steepness alone; crusader fortresses in the east combined both, rock-cut ditches of staggering depth that no siege tower could bridge.
  • Guarding the crossing: every moat funnels the attacker to one point, the drawbridge before the gatehouse, swept by arrow slits and murder holes — the whole science of the castle reduces to defending a single narrow plank over water.
  • The town moat: great cities copied the castle, ringing walls with water-filled ditches fed by rivers; French and Dutch engineers of the 17th century perfected the art, laying whole landscapes under controlled inundation at the opening of a sluice.

Obsolescence and afterlife: gunpowder ended the moat's military career — cannon laughed at ditches and breached walls from beyond them — yet the word refused to die. Star forts kept ditches as one element among many; country houses kept moats as ornament, still water doubling the beauty of brick and stone. Romantic poets seized on the moated grange as the image of seclusion, Tennyson's Mariana (1830) listening to her own voice in a moated hall of the mind. Antiquarians drained, grassed, and promenaded the great town ditches until the moat became heritage: the Tower's dry hollow, photographed by millions, is a moat you stroll through.

The modern metaphor: in the 1990s Warren Buffett revived the medieval fortress for investors, praising companies protected like wonderful castles by deep economic moats — brands, patents, licences, network effects. Business journalism adopted the image wholesale, and today analysts debate moat width, moat erosion, and moat-crossing disruptors with the seriousness of siege engineers. From Norman earthworks to shareholder letters, the moat has kept its single unchanging meaning: whatever stands between the treasure and the taker. Even the travellers of the source sentence read empty country in its terms, seeing wide moats in mere folds of ground — proof that a ditch dug for war has become a measure for the eye itself.
