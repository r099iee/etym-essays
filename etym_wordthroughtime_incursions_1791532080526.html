- 1st c. BCE — Latin incursio is a military noun for a charge or hostile entry; Caesar and Livy use it for the raids of Gauls and Germans across frontiers.
- 1st–5th c. CE — Roman historians apply the word to barbarian attacks on the frontier; it becomes a standard item in the vocabulary of imperial defence.
- 14th–15th c. — Old French incursion and Middle English incursion enter learned prose, chiefly in translations of Roman history and in chronicles of border war.
- 16th–17th c. — The word widens to any hostile or unwelcome entry; writers speak of incursions of the Turks, of pirates, and of error into the Church.
- 18th–19th c. — Figurative use flourishes: incursions into privacy, into rights, into trade; colonial and frontier writing keeps the military sense vivid.
- 20th c. — The word becomes a diplomatic euphemism for border violations and airspace intrusions; ecology and medicine adopt it for species, salt water and pathogens.
- 21st c. — Biosecurity speaks of pest incursions and incursion response; cybersecurity speaks of network incursions; the word remains formal, slightly ominous and usually plural.
