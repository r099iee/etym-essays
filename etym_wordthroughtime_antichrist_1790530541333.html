- 1st Century CE (New Testament Greek *antichristos*) - Coined in the Johannine Epistles (1 John, 2 John) to describe any heretical teacher or apostate who denies that Jesus came in the flesh; applied to a spiritual disposition and multiple contemporary deceivers.
- 2nd to 5th Century (Patristic Era) - Synthesized with apocalyptic visions in Daniel, 2 Thessalonians, and Revelation to formulate the doctrine of a single, literal end-times tyrant who will perform false miracles, conquer the world, and persecute the faithful before Christ's Second Coming.
- Middle Ages (11th to 14th Century) - Developed into a popular cultural and political weapon; rival emperors, popes (e.g. during the Great Western Schism), and invading hordes (such as the Mongols) were routinely labeled the Antichrist.
- Reformation Era (16th to 17th Century) - Luther, Calvin, and Protestant confessions officially designated the Roman Papacy as the Antichrist, while Counter-Reformation theologians developed Preterist and Futurist frameworks to counter the charge.
- 18th to 19th Century (Napoleonic Era & Romanticism) - Secularized and dramatized as a potent political epithet for world-shaking conquerors (notably Napoleon Bonaparte, as famously mirrored in Tolstoy's *War and Peace*) and in literary depictions of charismatic tyrants.
- 20th Century to Present - Proliferated in popular apocalyptic fiction (e.g., *Left Behind*, *The Omen*), political conspiracism, and philosophical discourse (e.g., Nietzsche's *Der Antichrist*), while serving as a figurative descriptor for supreme tyranny.
