- Late 14th–15th c. — Mathematical & architectural borrowing: entered Middle English via Old French from Latin lineamentum, initially denoting a geometric line drawn with a pen, a surveyor's guideline, or an architectural elevation.
- 16th c. (Renaissance era) — Anatomical and artistic shift: adopted by Renaissance draughtsmen and medical humanists to describe the linear contours, bone structure, and anatomical proportions of the human body and face.
- 17th–18th c. — Physiognomic and abstract expansion: broadened under the influence of physiognomy to link facial lines with moral character; simultaneously applied metaphorically to the preliminary outlines or distinctive traits of laws and philosophies.
- 19th c. (Romantic & Victorian literature) — Psychological keyword: extensively favored by Gothic and Victorian novelists (Shelley, Brontës, Dickens) to depict the brooding, expressive, or hereditary facial lines of characters.
- 20th–21st c. — Elevated literary & historiographical analysis: preserved in literary prose for evocative facial portraiture, and widely employed in academic historiography to denote the defining structural contours of historical eras or ideological movements.
