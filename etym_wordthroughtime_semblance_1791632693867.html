- c. 1300 — Middle English adoption from Anglo-Norman: borrowed as semblance / semblaunt, originally meaning one's visual countenance, facial expression, physical appearance, or demeanor, without necessarily implying deceit or falsity.
- 14th–15th c. — Chivalric and courtly literature: featured heavily in Arthurian romances and Chaucer's poetry to describe friendly gestures, gracious social demeanor, and visual heraldic resemblance (e.g., making "fair semblaunt").
- 16th–17th c. — Renaissance dramatic polarization: heavily utilized by Shakespeare and Elizabethan dramatists to contrast deceptive outward show with corrupt inner reality ("there is no vice so simple but assumes some mark of virtue on his outward parts"); the word acquired its lasting cynical edge of illusion and pretext.
- 18th–19th c. — Neoclassical & Victorian aesthetic / political critique: used in aesthetic theory to denote mimetic visual imitation in painting, and in political commentary to describe the fragile, cosmetic preservation of social peace or constitutional norms (e.g., "a semblance of peace").
- 20th–21st c. — Postmodern philosophy, art criticism, and modern usage: deployed by art historians (like E.H. Gombrich) to debate realistic representation versus abstraction; in everyday prose, predominantly used in the idiom "a semblance of [order/normalcy/dignity]" to denote a fragile, partial, or superficial approximation.
