[CHAIN FOR whirled]
1. PIE *kwer- “to turn, whirl, move around” (reconstructed root of rotation; attested indirectly across the branches — and to be kept strictly distinct from *kʷel-, “to revolve”).
2. Proto-Germanic *hwerbaną “to turn, to go around, to wander” (reconstructed; attested in Old English hweorfan, Old Norse hverfa, Gothic hwairban, all “to turn, to go”).
3. Old Norse hverfa “to turn, to go” (attested from the 9th c.) → iterative hvirfla “to turn around and around, to whirl” (attested; the -l- marks repeated, continuous rotation).
4. Middle English whirlen “to whirl” (c. 1300), borrowed from the Old Norse iterative.
5. English whirled, past tense of whirl (attested; the family includes whirlpool, whirlwind, whirligig).

NOTE ON DEBATE: The similar-looking PIE root *kʷel-, “to revolve” (whence wheel, cycle, and Sanskrit carati), is a separate root and must never be fused with *kwer-. The two are sometimes confused in popular etymology.

[CHAIN FOR flung]
1. PIE *plak- “to strike, beat” (reconstructed; attested in Greek plēssō, “to strike,” Latin plangere, “to beat, to beat the breast,” and plaga, “a blow”). The link to Germanic fling/flengja is probable but not certain.
2. Germanic s-extension of *plak- → Old Norse flengja “to flog, whip, beat” (attested) and Old English flingan “to whip, scourge” (rarely recorded; attested in glosses).
3. Middle English flengen “to beat, to flog” → late 14th c. “to throw violently” (the whip-stroke becomes the careless throw).
4. English fling, past tense flung (attested; the strong past was modelled on ring–rang–rung; an earlier form flang is recorded).

NOTE ON NUANCE: The derivation of fling from PIE *plak- is judged probable rather than certain; some handbooks treat the Germanic verb as of uncertain or imitative origin. The violent, whipping semantics, however, are consistent across the Germanic attestations.
