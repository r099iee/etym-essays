From the cart to the ship.

The story of cargo begins on land, with the wheeled vehicle. Latin carrus "wagon, cart" was itself a loanword from Gaulish karros, a Celtic word for a four-wheeled wagon descended from the Indo-European root *ḱers- "to run" — the semantic idea being that a cart is a thing that "runs" on wheels. The same root yields Latin currere "to run" and, through it, English "car," "current," "course," and "career." Caesar, campaigning in Gaul, records the carrus as a Gallic vehicle, and the word entered Latin as a military and agricultural term; the Romans found the Celtic two- and four-wheeled carts efficient enough to adopt both the vehicle and its name.

From carrus, Late Latin formed the verb carricare "to load (a cart), to burden" — the ancestor of Old French charger (English "charge") and of the Romance verbs of loading. This verb passed into Spanish as cargar "to load," with its noun carga "load, burden" and, derived from cargar, the noun cargo "burden, load; charge, duty, office." A telling sign of the word's early life: Spanish cargo carried both a concrete sense (a load to be carried) and an abstract one (an office or responsibility one "carries") — a bifurcation that English cargo would not inherit.

English borrows a seafaring word.

  • English took cargo from Spanish in the mid-17th century (first attestations c. 1650), in the context of transoceanic trade with Iberia and the New World.
  • The English word narrowed the general "load/burden" to a commercial shipload: the freight a merchant vessel carries for hire or trade.
  • Because the borrowing came through ships, "cargo" was firmly maritime for two centuries before spreading to other forms of transport.

Expansion in the 19th and 20th centuries.

  • Steam shipping and the growth of colonial trade made "cargo" a workaday term of docks and customs: cargo manifests, cargo insurance, cargo tonnage.
  • In the 20th century aviation extended the word upward: air cargo, cargo aircraft, the "cargo bay."
  • Logistics generalised it further to rail and road freight while retaining a bias toward large-scale commercial transport.
  • The anthropological term "cargo cult" (Melanesia, mid-20th century) applied the shipping word to movements expecting the delivery of manufactured goods — a striking cultural export of the maritime sense.

Plural forms and usage.

  • The plural cargoes (with -oes) follows the rule for nouns ending in consonant + o that have become fully naturalised: echoes, heroes, potatoes, tomatoes, torpedoes.
  • When the plural means "loads of goods," cargoes is the standard form in both British and American English; "cargos" occurs occasionally in logistics jargon but is less traditional.
  • The collective/uncountable use ("the ship carried cargo") coexists with the countable plural ("cargoes of grain and timber").

Semantic drift and cultural resonance.

  • From "thing loaded onto a cart" to "burden" to "a ship's freight" to "any transported commercial goods" — each step widened the vehicle but kept the core idea of a carried commercial load.
  • The Spanish sense "office, duty" (as in cargo = "post") never transferred, so English cargo is purely concrete.
  • Tolkien's use in The Hobbit — "his mind to trade and tolls, to cargoes and gold" — sets the plural amid mercantile abstraction: the Master's wealth is built from shiploads of goods, the plural heaping trade, tolls, and freight together.
  • Modern resonance: containerisation, airport "cargo," and "cargo shorts" keep the word ubiquitous, while its ship-loaded, Romantic past lingers in literature.
