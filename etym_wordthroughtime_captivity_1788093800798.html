- [Antiquity] - Latin captivitas: the legal-military status of a prisoner of war — a cold status word from the triumph and the slave market.
- [Late Antiquity/Christianity] - Spiritualised: the soul held captive by sin; the Vulgate applies 'captivity' to the Babylonian exile — the word gains scale and sorrow.
- [Middle English] - Arrives in English (14th c.) with both faces: political exile and spiritual bondage; 'the Captivity' becomes the fixed name of Israel's exile.
- [16th–18th c.] - Becomes liberty's shadow in Enlightenment thought; captivity narratives (Mary Rowlandson, 1682) make it a genre; abolition and piracy keep the literal sense raw.
- [19th c.] - Expands sideways: animals 'in captivity' (zoos), 'captive audiences' and 'captive markets' in commerce — the word learns to describe any party that cannot leave.
- [20th–21st c.] - Camps give it its darkest register; conservation gives it a careful positive one (captive breeding); and 'captive portal' brings the ancient seizing to every hotel Wi-Fi login screen.
