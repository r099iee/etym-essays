• cowering: Similar because both describe ducking or shrinking down in fear before a threat; differs because cowering is purely physical and usually has no element of embarrassment or servility — you cower from a storm or a blow, but cringe often carries self-consciousness or humiliation.
  • crouching: Similar because both involve lowering the body in a guarded posture; differs because crouching is neutral and deliberate (as in crouched over a plant or crouched to spring) whereas cringing always implies fear, submission, or discomfort.
  • flinching: Similar because both describe a quick, involuntary recoil from something threatening or unpleasant; differs because flinching is a sudden reflex of a moment, whereas cringing can be a sustained posture or a slow, deliberate shrinking.
  • wincing: Similar because wincing is an involuntary grimace of discomfort or embarrassment close to the modern emotional sense of cringing; differs because wincing emphasises a brief facial and bodily reaction to pain or bad news, while cringing suggests more prolonged fear, shame, or self-abasement.
  • grovelling: Similar because both describe abject, self-humbling behaviour before an authority; differs because grovelling means lying low or creeping on the ground in extreme submission and focuses on outward conduct, whereas cringing centres on the shrinking, fearful posture and, in modern use, on inner embarrassment.
  • quailing: Similar because quailing is trembling with fear and losing courage; differs because quailing is about cowardice and dread, usually of battle or punishment, with no servile or embarrassed component.
  • skulking: Similar because skulking involves moving stealthily and fearfully, hiding out of sight; differs because skulking stresses concealment and furtive movement rather than the crouched, submissive, or embarrassed posture of cringing.
  • blenching: Similar because blenching is an archaic and literary recoil or start aside from fear; differs because blenching is rare, formal, and strictly physical, lacking the modern emotional-embarrassment sense of cringing.
  • squirming: Similar because both describe bodily restlessness produced by discomfort or shame; differs because squirming is wriggling and twisting in place, usually from a guilty conscience or acute embarrassment, whereas cringing involves drawing inwards or away.
