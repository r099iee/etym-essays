TRAWLING is the present participle and gerund of the verb to trawl, and as such it names both a continuous action and the activity itself in two connected spheres: the maritime one of commercial fishing, where a boat drags a large, cone-shaped net (a trawl net) through the water — either across the seabed (bottom trawling) or at mid-depth (midwater trawling) — in order to catch fish in bulk; and the figurative one of systematic, wide-ranging searching, where a person, an investigator, or a computer program sweeps through a vast collection of information, records, locations, or items in the hope of gathering everything relevant. The word always carries the idea of a broad net: nothing is aimed at precisely; instead a wide dragnet is drawn through a medium — sea or data — and everything in its path is gathered up for later sorting. That core image of the sweep is the key to every sense the word has.

Core maritime sense (fishing): In commercial and deep-sea fisheries, trawling is the dominant industrial method of catching groundfish and pelagic shoals. A trawl net is shaped like a cone or funnel, wide at the mouth and tapering to a "cod-end" where the catch collects; the mouth is held open either by otter boards (large doors that plane outward under tow) or by a rigid frame, and the net is towed behind a trawler on a heavy cable. Bottom trawling drags the net directly along or just above the seafloor, catching flatfish, cod, haddock, and shrimp; midwater trawling targets open-water species such as herring, mackerel, and pollock; pair trawling uses two boats towing one net between them. Related terms in this sphere include trawler (the vessel or the fisherman), trawlerman, beam trawl, otter trawl, cod-end, and bycatch. Because the technique sweeps everything from the floor, it is ecologically controversial: bottom trawling disturbs seabed habitats and collects large volumes of bycatch (unwanted fish, crustaceans, and marine life), and the term now carries a contested, critical coloring in environmental discourse even as it remains a neutral technical term inside the industry.

Figurative sense (systematic searching): Since the late twentieth century, trawl and trawling have been applied to any systematic, broad sweep through data or places: investigators trawl through archived documents, analysts trawl email servers, recruiters trawl résumé databases, and shoppers trawl markets and vintage shops. The construction trawl for (trawling for talent, trawling for information, trawling for votes) expresses an unfocused, catch-all hunt whose result is uncertain. This is the sense the card's context uses: "The investigators spent weeks __________ through thousands of archived court documents."

Digital and technical polysemy (data trawling, web scraping): In computing, data science, and OSINT (open-source intelligence), data trawling or trawling the web refers to the automated collection of large volumes of data — scraping pages, harvesting records, crawling databases — so that targeted items can be extracted from the mass afterwards. The metaphor is exact: an algorithm sweeps a wide net through electronic records the way a ship sweeps the sea.

Related senses and shades:
  • Trawling for evidence / fishing expeditions: in legal and investigative idiom, a "fishing expedition" is precisely the trawl — a broad, speculative search for evidence whose precise target is unknown.
  • Distinction from trolling: in fishing, trolling means trailing one baited line or a few lures behind a slow-moving boat; trawling means towing a wide net. On the internet, trolling means provoking anger; data trawling means harvesting data. The two are often confused but are not synonymous.
  • Historical technical narrowing: early English uses of trawl (1560s) referred specifically to the dragnet and to the act of fishing with it; the figurative extension to searching is modern (twentieth century onward).

Grammatical and morphological notes: Trawling is used (1) as a present participle ("the boats are trawling offshore"), (2) as a verbal noun or gerund ("trawling is a controversial practice"), and (3) attributively ("trawling operations", "trawling gear"). The base noun trawl (the net) and the verb trawl are both recorded in English from the 1560s; the agent noun trawler follows shortly after.

Semantic bridge summary: All senses hang on one image — the dragnet. A cone of mesh is dragged through a medium and everything in its path is collected; whether the medium is the North Sea or an email archive, whether the collector is a trawler, a detective, or a search algorithm, the verb is the same because the shape of the action is the same: wide, mechanical, thorough, and slightly indiscriminate.
