spoil centres on two great ideas that English has held together for 700 years: goods taken by force and goodness taken away. As a noun, spoil — usually plural spoils — means booty, plunder, loot stripped from a defeated enemy or seized in contest; Ruskin's kings let their followers divide any quantity of spoil or profit, meaning they surrendered the conqueror's rightful share of treasure, cattle, and land to their men. As a verb, to spoil means to damage, ruin, impair, or decay: to spoil food, to spoil a view, to spoil a ballot, to spoil a child by overindulgence.

The link between taking and ruining is stripping. Latin spolium meant the hide stripped from an animal and the armour stripped from a fallen foe. To spoil an enemy was to strip him; to spoil a thing is to strip it of value; to spoil a child is to strip him of discipline by giving too much. Once the metaphor is seen, the many modern senses cohere: despoilment, decay, indulgence, and electoral excess are all strippings.

  • Noun: war-booty and winnings (oldest, Ruskin's sense): valuables seized in war, piracy, or competition; e.g. spoils of war, to divide the spoil, spoils system. Historians speak of Roman spolia, medieval pillage, and American spoils of office (jobs given to supporters). Always faintly moralised: spoil is not honourable pay but seized surplus. The bridge to verb senses is metonymy: the act of stripping yields the thing stripped.
  • Verb transitive: to plunder, despoil (14th–17th c. core): to rob, pillage, lay waste; e.g. to spoil the Egyptians, to spoil a city (King James Bible). Now literary, preserved in despoil, spoliation. The bridge is direct borrowing from Old French espoillier "to strip".
  • Verb transitive: to damage, ruin, impair (dominant today): to make useless or less good; e.g. to spoil dinner, to spoil the surprise, rain spoiled the match, a spoiled ballot. Food spoils (goes bad), milk spoils, plans spoil. The semantic bridge is stripping of value: a stripped thing is a ruined thing.
  • Verb transitive: to overindulge a child or pet: to harm character by excessive kindness; e.g. a spoiled child, to spoil grandchildren, spoiled for choice. Paradoxically, giving too much strips discipline. Victorian moralists, including Ruskin, worried constantly about spoiling by luxury versus hardening by labour.
  • Verb intransitive: to decay, go bad: of food, temper, or chances; e.g. fish spoils quickly, fruit spoils in heat. American grocers mark spoilage. The bridge is internal ruin without agent: time strips freshness.
  • Technical and idiomatic extensions: spoiler (a plot revelation that strips suspense; also a car flap that strips lift), spoilsport (one who strips fun), to be spoiling for a fight (eager, as if stripped for action), spoil heap (mining waste stripped from earth). Each keeps the stripping image: a spoiler strips the story, a spoil heap is stripped earth.

Grammar helps. Noun spoil is usually plural (spoils) for booty but singular for waste (spoil heap). Verb spoil has two participles: regular spoiled (American, and for children) and irregular spoilt (British, especially for food and chances: spoilt milk, spoilt vote). To be spoiling for something is to crave it aggressively.

In Ruskin's sentence the nobility of kings lies in refusing spoil. Where modern politicians seek profit, Homeric kings surrender loot. To understand spoil here is to understand an economy of honour: spoil measures greed, and renunciation measures kingship.
