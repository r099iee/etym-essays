- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Classical Latin) - The compound noun *supercilium* (from *super* 'above' + *cilium* 'eyelid') literally meant the eyebrow; through natural anatomical association, Roman writers like Cicero and Seneca used it metaphorically for haughtiness, sternness, or aristocratic gravity.
- Early 16th Century (c. 1520–1540) - Borrowed directly into Renaissance English from Latin *superciliōsus*, originally applied in scholarly and medical texts to denote stern, haughty, or censorious facial expressions.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Restoration Eras) - Expands into drama and political essays to characterize arrogant courtiers and haughty prelates whose elevated station made them dismissive of common citizens.
- 18th to 19th Century (Georgian & Victorian Eras) - Achieves canonical literary status in novels of manners (Jane Austen, William Makepeace Thackeray, Charles Dickens, Oscar Wilde); permanently fixed as the quintessential adjective for cold aristocratic disdain and the raised-eyebrow sneer.
- 20th to 21st Century - Widely utilized across literary criticism, psychology, and high-register journalism to describe intellectual snobbery, condescending institutional rhetoric, and elitist contempt.
