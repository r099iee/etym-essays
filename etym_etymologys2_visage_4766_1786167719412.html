DERIVATION CHAIN — VISAGE
R → PIE *weid- 'to see' (also 'to know', since knowing is 'having seen') — the root of Latin vidēre 'to see', Greek idein 'to see', Sanskrit veda 'knowledge', and English 'wit', 'wise', 'vision'.
E → Latin vidēre 'to see' — with its perfect participle vīsus 'seen; sight, appearance'.
D → Latin vīsus + the neuter suffix -āticum → Vulgar Latin vīsāticum 'that which is seen; appearance' — a derivative meaning 'the seen thing', the look, the face.
C → Old French visage (11th c.) 'face, countenance' — from vis 'face, look' (itself from Latin vīsus) + -age; the everyday French word for the face.
B → Middle English visage (c. 1300) 'face, countenance' — borrowed from Anglo-French into the courtly and literary vocabulary of English.
A → Modern English VISAGE 'a person's face or facial expression' — now formal and literary, the face as beheld.
Debate note: None of the steps is seriously disputed. The only nuance is that 'visage' (the seen face) and 'vision' (the act of seeing) both derive from vīsus, and the modern English word retains its original logic — the face as the object of sight — even as it has drifted into a formal, quasi-archaic register.
