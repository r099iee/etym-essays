• gerund: Similar because both are non-finite verbal nouns derived from verbs that retain verbal properties (such as governing objects and adverbs); differs because a gerund in English is morphologically marked by the suffix -ing and functions strictly as a noun in nominal slots (e.g., "Swimming is fun"), whereas an infinitive can function as a noun, adjective, or adverb, and is typically preceded by *to*.
  • participle: Similar because both are non-finite verb forms; differs because a participle (present participle in -ing or past participle in -ed / -en) functions primarily as an adjective modifying a noun or as an element of compound aspectual tenses (e.g., "the running water", "he has written"), whereas the infinitive expresses the base action itself.
  • non-finite verb: Similar because the infinitive belongs to this overarching linguistic category; differs because non-finite verb is the broad hypernym encompassing infinitives, gerunds, and participles, designating any verbal form not conjugated for tense, person, or number, whereas infinitive is the specific base member of that category.
  • base form: Similar because in modern English the bare infinitive is morphologically identical to the base form; differs because base form (or uninflected root) is a morphological concept designating the naked lemma without affixes, while infinitive denotes a specific syntactic category and clause type.
  • root verb: Similar because both represent the fundamental verbal core; differs because root verb refers to the irreducible morphological base from which derivatives are built, whereas infinitive represents an operational grammatical mood or non-finite form.
  • verbal noun: Similar because both can function nominally to name an action; differs because verbal noun is an umbrella term covering all nouns derived from verbs (including formations in -tion, -ment, and -ing), which may lose verbal government entirely (e.g., taking determiners and adjectives: "the quick building of the bridge"), whereas the infinitive always retains its full verbal syntax.
  • supine: Similar in classical Indo-European grammar (especially Latin) because both are non-finite verbal substantives; differs because the Latin supine is a defective verbal noun in the fourth declension used strictly in the accusative (in -um, to express purpose after motion verbs) or ablative (in -ū, with adjectives), whereas the infinitive has universal syntactic distribution.
  • lemma: Similar in lexicography because the infinitive commonly serves as the citation lemma under which a verb is listed in dictionaries; differs because lemma is a meta-lexicographical indexing term for the canonical headword, whereas infinitive is an actual grammatical form within a language's syntactic system.
  • citation form: Similar because the infinitive is the traditional citation form used in Romance, Germanic, and Slavic dictionaries; differs because citation form is a convention of dictionary design (in Latin and Ancient Greek dictionaries, the citation form is the first-person singular present indicative, e.g., amō, rather than the infinitive).
  • unconjugated verb: Similar because both identify a verb free from person and number inflection; differs because unconjugated verb is an informal descriptive phrase, whereas infinitive is the precise, historically grounded grammatical term dating back to classical antiquity.
