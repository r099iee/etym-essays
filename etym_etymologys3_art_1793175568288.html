The biography of art is five thousand years of saying is. Its root is Proto-Indo-European *h₂es-, to be, exist, the most indispensable verb ever reconstructed, whose children still declare existence from Iceland to India: Sanskrit ási (thou art), Greek essí, Latin es, Hittite eš-, all saying to one person you are with the same inherited breath. The root is so old and so constantly used that it is suppletive everywhere, patched together from several prehistoric verbs, because no single stem could survive the wear of being spoken a thousand times a day.

In Germanic the root became *esaną, to be, and its second-person singular took the ancient -t- or -s- endings that marked thou across the family. Old English eart (West Saxon) or earð, arð (other dialects) was thus no poetic ornament but kitchen grammar: þū eart hungrig meant simply you are hungry. The intrusive -r- that turns es- into eart comes from rhotacism and analogy with forms like eom (I am) and the plural earon, welding the paradigm together with a shared r.

Stages of the change:

  • Prehistoric being: PIE *h₂es- conjugates for every person, and its second singular *h₂ési is the seed from which Sanskrit ási, Greek eî, Latin es, and Germanic *arti/*isi all grow.
  • Old English daily speech: eart serves every thou, from kings to cowherds, while sindon/earon serve plurals; no solemnity attaches yet.
  • Middle English levelling: dialectal ert, art, arte compete and art wins, riding with Chaucer's thou through the Canterbury Tales.
  • Early modern double life (1500–1700): Shakespeare puns and prays with thou art some three hundred times, the King James translators enshrine which art in heaven, and meanwhile Londoners increasingly call even intimates you, dooming the form in speech at the moment of its literary triumph.
  • Death and embalming (18th–19th c.): spoken art dies with spoken thou everywhere but Quaker meeting; written art is embalmed in hymns like How Great Thou Art (1885, translated 1949), marriage vows, and Romantic poetry addressing nature and night.

Modern English thus keeps art as a beautifully preserved fossil: grammatically dead, stylistically immortal. Fantasy translators choose Who art thou? over Who are you? for the source sentence for exactly the reason the hymn-writers kept it: three letters of archaism move the speaker out of the present and into a world where words still carry ceremony, where to ask after someone's being is already half a spell. And the old forms still guard their old distinctions: art for the single familiar thou, are for the many and the honoured, so that every Who art thou? quietly counts its addressee as one soul singled out, whether for love, for worship, or for judgment.
