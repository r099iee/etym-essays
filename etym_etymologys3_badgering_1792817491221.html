Of all the words in this batch, badgering has the most visible birth certificate: it was coined from a blood sport within living memory of the printing press, and its whole meaning is the sport remembered. The animal came first. The grey burrower with the white-striped head appears in English as bager in the 1520s, very likely — though debated — from badge (a distinctive mark) plus the pejorative-intensifying suffix -ard (as in drunkard, laggard): the marked one, the badged beast. The white blaze running from nose to skull is the badger's uniform, and Tudor countrymen named it for exactly what it wore.

From beast to verb:

  • The sett and the pit (16th–18th c.): badgers dig elaborate burrow-systems called setts, and countrymen learned to dig the beast out with spades and terriers — the badger-dog, ancestor of the dachshund and the terrier breeds. By the 1600s this had formalised into badger-baiting: the badger placed in a pit or barrel while dogs were set on it round after round, bets laid on endurance. The beast's courage became proverbial — cornered, outnumbered, it fought every round — and the spectacle drew the same crowds as bear-baiting and cockfighting.
  • The transfer (1790s): observers began applying the pit's vocabulary to human torment: to badger someone was to set upon them with the same relay relentlessness — fresh questions like fresh dogs, no pause between rounds. The earliest verb uses still mean physical baiting and teasing (schoolboys badgering a butt, mobs badgering a victim); the sense of verbal nagging for a favour matures in the nineteenth century as the literal sport fades.
  • Abolition without extinction (1835): Parliament's Cruelty to Animals Act killed the legal pit, but the verb survived its parent sport — a common fate of sporting metaphors (to beard, to bait, cockfighting politics). Freed from the barrel, badgering generalised to any repeated pressure: suitors, salesmen, journalists, and inquisitive relations all learned to badger.
  • The professional century (20th c.): mass democracy and mass media gave badgering its modern stages — the lobby badgered, the witness badgered in cross-examination, the minister badgered at the despatch box, the consumer badgered by advertising. Telephone and then email mechanised the siege: one caller replaced by another so the burrow-mouth is never empty.
  • Tolkien's use: the source sentence preserves the domestic original — one person, one question, repeated until love itself feels besieged. It is badgering at its purest: no institution, no technique, just the refusal to let a subject drop, exactly as the dogs refused to leave the sett.

Sound shifts and form: the noun's first vowel levelled from bager to badger with the intrusive d that English inserts between n-adjacent vowels and r-clusters in familiar words (compare thunder from thunor). The verb is a straight zero-derivation from the noun — no affix, just the pit's name turned into an action — and badgering adds the regular -ing, pronounced with the soft dg that keeps the badger audible inside every act of pestering. Say it and the beast is still there, striped head down, refusing to come out.
