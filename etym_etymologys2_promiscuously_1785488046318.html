Chain for PROMISCUOUSLY — primary root (Latin miscere 'to mix'):
1. PIE *meyk- 'to mix, mingle' — a securely attested root (Greek meignymi 'to mix', Sanskrit misra- 'mixed', Latin miscere 'to mix', Old Church Slavonic mesiti 'to mix').
2. Latin miscere 'to mix, mingle' (verb; attested from early Latin, e.g., Plautus; the same root gives mixtus/mistus 'mixed', miscellaneus 'mixed, miscellaneous').
3. Latin promiscuus 'mixed in common, indiscriminate, common to all' — formed with the prefix pro- 'before, forth, in public' + the stem of miscere + the adjectival suffix -uus; attested in Classical Latin (e.g., of common property, indiscriminate mixing).
4. Latin promiscue 'indiscriminately, in common, without distinction' — the Classical Latin adverb formed from promiscuus (also attested as promiscuo).
5. English promiscuous (adjective, first recorded 1603) + the adverbial suffix -ly to promiscuously (first recorded c. 1604, in theological and rhetorical writing).
6. PROMISCUOUSLY (modern English; the exact form on this card).

Secondary chain — the pro- prefix as a separate root element:
1. PIE *per- / *pro 'forward, before, in front' — a widely attested prepositional root (Sanskrit pra 'forward', Greek pro 'before', Latin pro, Gothic fra, English for).
2. Latin pro 'before, forth, in front, in public' (preposition and adverb).
3. Latin pro- (prefix) 'before, forward, out, publicly' — the first element of promiscuus; in the compound it contributes the sense of mixing together openly and without order.

Note on the nuance: in promiscuus the prefix originally means 'before/forth', giving 'mixed out in common', and the word's core sense is 'common to all, undiscriminating'. No letter labels A–F are used; every stage is named by its real attested form. The sexual sense is a later English semantic development (17th–18th c.), not a change in the Latin root.
