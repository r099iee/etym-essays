The etymology of volatility is aerial. Latin volare to fly, possibly from a lost expressive root imitating rushing air, underlies volatilis meaning winged, furnished with wings, then by natural metaphor swift, fleeting, transitory. Roman poets call birds, arrows, and rumors volatilis: whatever moves through air quickly. Late Latin alchemists and physicians seize the metaphor for substances that fly away as vapor: volatile salts, volatile spirits, volatile oils that evaporate at ordinary heat. This chemical sense enters Old French volatil and Middle English volatile by the 1590s, first of medicines and essences that disperse.

Seventeenth-century moral psychology extends flight to temper. A volatile person is one whose mind flies from object to object, lively but inconstant; a volatile wit sparkles and vanishes. Eighteenth-century political writers speak of volatile crowds and volatile republics that will not stay fixed. The noun volatility, formed with abstract -ity from Latin -itas, is attested in English by the mid-17th century for both chemical evaporativeness and figurative fickleness. French volatilité parallels the development, reinforcing learned prestige.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries quantify flight. Thermodynamics measures vapor pressure and defines volatile organic compounds as regulatory category. Finance, from the 1970s with Black-Scholes and computer trading, mathematizes volatility as standard deviation of log returns, then creates indices such as VIX, the fear gauge. Computing borrows chemistry: volatile memory requires power to retain, non-volatile persists, a precise analogy of evaporation as loss. Media theory, as in the epilogue from permanence to volatility, synthesizes all layers: digital text evaporates like spirit, swings like price, flares like temper, and vanishes like RAM when power fails.

  • Phonetics: Latin o and a preserved; English stress on second syllable with reduced vowels, plus -ility with yod and long i, yielding RP pattern with secondary-primary contour.
  • Morphology: vol- fly plus -atilis capable of plus -ity state, literally winged-ness, now instability.
  • Semantic motor: from literal wings to vapor to mood to market to memory to text, each step preserving swiftness plus loss of hold.

Debated nuance concerns the ultimate PIE root. Some connect volare to *gwelh- to wish or to fly, others treat it as isolated Italic with onomatopoeic origin; the uncertainty does not affect the secure Latin-to-modern chain. What matters culturally is persistence of flight imagery: economists still speak of capital flight, chemists of fugitive dyes, psychologists of flightiness, archivists of fugitive texts. To say text moves from permanence to volatility is therefore etymologically exact: from remaining through time to flying through time, from monument to migration. The essayistic power depends on hearing wings in markets, moods, molecules, and screens at once.
