Introduction. Loops, the third-person singular of the verb to loop, is the modern tip of a word whose origins are genuinely obscure — and whose career is a textbook demonstration of how one simple image (a cord that curves back on itself) can be re-borrowed, century after century, by completely different trades: knotting, fortification, railways, aviation, computing, music, and management. Each borrowing kept the core picture — the returning curve — and added a new technical sense, so that the same word now describes a river bending east, a program repeating itself, a DJ cycling a beat, and a colleague being kept informed.

Phase 1 — a shadowy origin (before c. 1400).

  • Loop appears in English around 1400 as loupe, meaning "a fold or doubling of a cord; a noose; a running knot". Its origin is genuinely uncertain: the OED's leading candidates are Celtic (Irish lúb, Scottish Gaelic lùb "a bend, a loop, a fold" — a plausible source given the 14th-century contact between English and the Irish-Scottish world) and Scandinavian (Old Norse hlaup "a leap, a run", if "loop" developed from the idea of a "running" knot that slides along a rope).
  • No PIE root can be reconstructed for either candidate, and the word's early life is therefore unattested — the chain simply begins at the first recorded English form. The noose sense matters: the earliest loops were practical — sailor's knots, snares, the loops of weavers and cordwainers.

Phase 2 — the noun settles (1400–1600).

  • Through the 15th–16th centuries loop is a workaday noun of cord and cloth: the fold of a rope, the loop of a belt, the looped stitch of a seam. The word carries no elevation; it belongs to ships, workshops and kitchens.
  • The same period extends "loop" to openings: a narrow slit or eye — and from that sense comes the famous compound loophole (1590s), the slit in a fortress wall through which defenders shoot while remaining protected.

Phase 3 — loophole and the figurative leap (1600–1800).

  • The 17th century turns loophole figurative: "a way of escape" — first of legal and procedural gaps, and the sense is alive and well today (tax loopholes, regulatory loopholes). The image is exact: a loophole in a wall is a narrow protected opening; a loophole in a law is a narrow protected way through.
  • The 18th century sees loop used of decorative and mechanical circles (button-loops, stirrup-loops), and the word begins to appear in industrial contexts, but the verb still does not exist: rivers do not yet "loop" in English.

Phase 4 — the verb is born (1800–1900).

  • In the 19th century the verb to loop is formed from the noun: "to form into a loop" (of thread, wire, ribbon) and, crucially, "to move in a looping course". Railways, surveyors and geographers adopt it: a line "loops" around a hill, a river "loops" through a plain — exactly the geographic use of this card's sentence, in which the river "loops suddenly east across Dale".
  • The century's engineering culture gives the word its first technical habitats: loop lines on railways, loop roads, loop circuits in telegraphy. The returning-curve image is now thoroughly at home in the industrial landscape.

Phase 5 — the thrill and the machine (1900–1950).

  • In 1903 "loop the loop" enters the language with the age of flight: the aerobatic vertical circle (and the roller-coaster inversion of the same name). The word now carries excitement — the loop is a stunt, a thrill, a circle drawn in the air.
  • In the 1940s computing takes the word over: a "loop" is a sequence of instructions that repeats until a condition is met (for-loop, while-loop); "infinite loop" becomes the classic bug that traps a program forever. The semantic bridge is perfect: a loop is a curve that returns to its beginning — so a looped instruction returns to its start and runs again. No metaphor in computing is older or more transparent.

Phase 6 — music, management, and fiction (1950–today).

  • Electronic music of the 1950s–60s uses tape loops — short recorded passages spliced into circles and played over and over (musique concrète; Steve Reich's tape pieces of the mid-1960s); sample loops and "looping" become standard studio practice in hip-hop and dance production, and the verb acquires its musical sense: "the beat loops".
  • Management English of the 1970s produces "in the loop" and "out of the loop" — informed or excluded from a circle of decision-makers — and "feedback loop" enters systems thinking, economics and popular explanation. The loop as "the circle of people who know" is the same returning-curve image once more.
  • Popular fiction popularises the "time loop" (Groundhog Day, 1993): a period of time that repeats itself, characters trapped in a returning curve — the ancient image of the looped cord, now a structure of narrative.

Phase 7 — today.

  • Loops the verb form covers the full inheritance at once: a river "loops" (geography, the oldest verb sense), a program "loops" (computing), a track "loops" (music), a manager "loops people in" (jargon). The single image — the curve that returns to itself — holds all of them together.
  • Pronunciation and form are stable (/luːps/; loop – looped – looping), and the word shows no sign of losing its productivity: every new domain that needs a word for "returning curve or repetition" reaches for it.

Summary. From a 14th-century noose of uncertain ancestry to the vocabulary of rivers, aeroplanes, programs, tape machines and time itself: loop is the story of an image that never wore out. Every era re-discovered that a curve which returns to its beginning is exactly the shape of a knot, a flight, a repetition, a cycle — and gave the old word a new job.
