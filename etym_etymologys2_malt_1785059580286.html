Proto-Indo-European *mel- / *meld- ("soft, tender; to soften, dissolve, melt"; also with nasal extension *mel-dʰ-), reconstructed full-grade *meld- and zero-grade *ml̥d-ú- ("soft, tender") — no fixed date, reconstructed stage c. 4000–3000 BCE → Proto-Germanic *maltą (neuter noun, "malted grain, that which is softened/steeped"; zero-grade *ml̥-t-ó- of the same root with the -t- extension seen in *meltaną "to melt, dissolve"; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) → Old English mealt / mealtu (neuter, "malt, barley prepared for brewing"; attested in the Laws of Ine c. 690 CE as a unit of legal payment alongside bread and ale) → Middle English malt / mault (14th c., the standard spelling settling by the 15th c.) → Modern English malt (noun "malted grain"; verb "to make into malt"; derivatives malter, maltster, maltose, malted milk). Note on debate: the connection between "malt" and the PIE "soft/melt" family is generally accepted, but the exact form of the Germanic noun is reconstructed (*maltą from *ml̥-t-ó-) on the basis of the attested Germanic forms (Old English mealt, Old Norse malt, Old High German malz, Old Saxon malt); the -t- extension parallels that in *meltaną "to melt," and the semantic link is the shared idea of softening by steeping. No stage between PIE and Proto-Germanic is directly attested, so both the exact zero-grade vocalism and the precise meaning at the intermediate stage rest on reconstruction rather than on records. One minority view derives the brewing word instead from a separate PIE *melh₂- "to grind, crush," on the ground that malt is crushed grain — but the attested cognates all point to softening, not grinding, and this alternative is not favored in current scholarship.
