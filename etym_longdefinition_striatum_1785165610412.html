Striatum (full anatomical name corpus striatum, "the striped body") is a large subcortical cluster of nerve-cell nuclei at the base of the forebrain, belonging to the group of structures called the basal ganglia, and crucial to the control of voluntary movement, habit formation, motivation, and reward. It is called "striped" because a section through it shows a zebra-like alternation of grey cell bodies and white myelinated fibre bundles — the striae, or stripes, that give the structure its name. In the present context the striatum is cast in its most modern role: the passage explains that when your prediction of reality turns out to be accurate, your striatum "releases a micro-dose of dopamine as an internal 'Good job, your prediction of reality was accurate!' reward." The nuance is at once computational and hedonic: the striatum is the brain's prediction-error engine, issuing a small pulse of dopamine when an expected outcome arrives, thereby teaching the organism which predictions to trust and which actions to repeat. The dopamine itself is produced by neurons of the midbrain (substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area) and delivered to the striatum; to say the striatum "releases" it is convenient shorthand for a whole circuit.

The striatum must be distinguished from the structures around it. The basal ganglia is the umbrella group of subcortical nuclei — striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra — and the striatum is its chief input station, receiving projections from nearly the whole cerebral cortex. The striatum itself has two anatomical halves: the caudate nucleus and the putamen, separated in most of their course by the internal capsule; early anatomists named these two together the "striate body," and the term dorsal striatum (caudate plus putamen) is now contrasted with the ventral striatum, whose best-known member is the nucleus accumbens — the structure most closely tied to reward, craving, and the dopamine "micro-dose" of the passage. Where the cerebral cortex computes and plans at the surface, the striatum sits beneath it, converting cortical plans into learned, automatic action and gating the signals that make repetition feel good. It is therefore central to the whole cycle of the passage: a correct prediction is rewarded, the reward teaches the system, and the teaching is engraved as habit.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:

  • Anatomy (primary sense, 17th century onward): the subcortical "striped body"; the word is a noun from Neo-Latin striatum, and the related adjective striatal means "of the striatum" (e.g., striatal circuits).
  • Neuroscience (extended sense, mid-20th century onward): the striatum as the hub of the basal ganglia's motor, habit, and reward systems; in computational neuroscience specifically, the site of reward-prediction error and reinforcement learning — the sense used in the passage.
  • Psychiatry and neurology (clinical sense): the striatum is invoked in disorders of movement and motivation — Parkinson's disease (degeneration of the dopamine pathway feeding it), Huntington's disease (striatal atrophy), addiction (hijacking of the ventral striatum's reward signal), obsessive-compulsive disorder (overactive habit loops).
  • Adjectival "striate"/"striated" (related but distinct): not a sense of the noun, but the adjective striated (from the same Latin stria, "groove, stripe") is used generally in biology — as in striated muscle, whose banded appearance gave skeletal muscle its name by the same logic.
  • Popular science (recent metaphorical sense): the striatum is described in journalism as the brain's "reward circuit" or "habit centre," the neural source of the "Good job" micro-reward of fulfilled prediction.

The semantic bridge runs from a Roman mason's word to modern reward science: stria, the groove or flute of a column, gave striatus, "fluted, striped," and anatomists of the seventeenth century, seeing the alternating grey and white bands in a section of the brain, named the region corpus striatum, "the striped body." The name is purely descriptive; the striping is real and diagnostic, and it has survived three centuries of anatomy because it is precisely the feature that makes the region recognizable. The register of the word is clinical and scholarly — a Latin anatomical label, almost always introduced with a gloss, exactly as the present context introduces it — but its range has widened in recent decades as neuroscience has entered public discourse, so that "striatum" now appears in ordinary articles about habits, rewards, and apps engineered to trigger dopamine. Throughout, the core meaning is fixed: the striped, subcortical structure that turns cortical plans into learned action and rewards the organism for predicting correctly.
