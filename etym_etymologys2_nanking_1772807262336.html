Morpheme 1 — 南 nán 'south' (Sinitic; no PIE ancestry):
Old Chinese (reconstructed) *nˤəm / *nəm 'south' (attested as a directional character in oracle-bone inscriptions, c. 1200–1046 BCE) → Middle Chinese nâm / nɑm 'south' (Sui–Tang period, 6th–10th c. CE) → Mandarin nán 'south' (modern) → in the compound 南京 Nánjīng 'Southern Capital'.

Morpheme 2 — 京 jīng 'capital' (Sinitic; no PIE ancestry):
Old Chinese (reconstructed) *kraŋ 'hill, height; hence the seat on the height, the capital' (attested in oracle-bone and bronze forms, c. 1200–1046 BCE onward) → Middle Chinese kɨæŋ / kæŋ 'capital city' → Mandarin jīng 'capital' → in the compounds 南京 Nánjīng 'Southern Capital', 北京 Běijīng 'Northern Capital', 东京 Dōngjīng 'Tokyo, Eastern Capital' (via the same character).

Compound toponym path to English:
Chinese 南京 Nánjīng, literally 'south + capital' (the city's status as southern capital fixed in imperial geography; the modern English toponym descends from this compound, whose earlier city names included Jinling and Yingtian) → reading of 京 in the southern/Jianghuai pronunciation as kīng, which, combined with the older and Postal Map romanization conventions, gives 'Nanking' in English (the spelling 'Nankin' also appears in French and older English; Postal Map system standardized early 20th c., drawing on established 19th-c. usage) → English proper noun Nanking (city name; attributive 'Nanking families, Nanking silk').

Note on the debate and on accidental resemblance: the '-king' of Nanking is purely a romanization accident matching the older regional reading of 京; it has no connection to the Germanic word king (< Proto-Germanic *kuningaz). Neither morpheme (南 nor 京) has Indo-European ancestry; both are Sinitic elements of the Sino-Tibetan family. The trade word nankeen is a commercial offshoot of the place-name, not its parent.
