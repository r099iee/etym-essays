Torn is the past participle of the strong verb tear: something that has been separated, split, or pulled apart by force, leaving ragged, uneven edges. Fabric ripped open, paper rent in two, flesh and skin lacerated by a sharp or violent agency — all are torn. The word carries a strong implication of violence done to a surface or object, and it distinguishes the resulting damage — irregular, jagged, and beyond neat repair — from a clean cut made with a blade. A cut is made; a tear is forced. Alongside this physical core, torn has long supported an extensive figurative life. Primary physical sense
  • Ripped or rent apart by force: cloth, paper, skin, muscle, or membrane pulled apart with ragged edges. “His coat was badly torn by the jagged brambles.”
  • Damaged beyond neat repair: the emphasis on jagged, uneven damage distinguishes torn from cut or sliced. A surgeon cuts; a bramble tears.
  • Medical and technical use: a torn ligament, a torn muscle — a sudden over-stretching or rupture of tissue; a torn sheet in printing; torn clouds (clouds broken apart by wind).
  • Deliberate fashion sense: torn or distressed jeans, pre-torn by design — a modern commercial sense in which the damage is the aesthetic.
Figurative senses
  • Torn between: pulled in different directions, emotionally divided, unable to choose — “torn between loyalty to her family and her ambitions.” Recorded from the eighteenth century; the same image of physical division applied to the heart or mind.
  • Torn by grief / torn by conflict: a nation, family, or heart “rent” by opposing forces or emotions — the passive participle of a violent act applied to states of feeling or society.
  • Torn apart (intensive): completely and violently separated or destroyed; also of a person devastated by loss.
  • Torn (adjective, archaic/literary): the older strong forms tore (obsolete) and rent are used in poetry and scripture.
Word-use notes and register Torn is the most neutral and inclusive of the words for tearing-damage. Ripped stresses sudden, violent pulling and is the more slangy word; rent is the literary, archaic strong form favoured in verse and solemn prose; lacerated is the precise, often medical word for torn flesh. In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, Bilbo has scrambled up into a thorny bush to escape the trolls, and his “clothes and his skin” are torn — the jagged branches catching at his garments and his flesh while he lies rigid, not daring to move. The participle anchors the scene in bodily reality: every scratch and rent is a trace of his panicked flight, and the word stands in pointed comic contrast to the comfortable, well-ordered life of a hobbit who loves his hole and his larder. Adventure, for a hobbit, begins with ruined clothes and scratched skin. Grammatical note Torn is one of the few English words that is simultaneously a past participle (used with have or be: “has torn,” “was torn”), a passive adjective (“a torn page”), and the base of the modern idiom “torn between.” The verb tear is a strong verb: present tear, past tore, participle torn — the participle preserves the older strong form while the past tore was later reshaped on the analogy of bore and swore. Origin in brief Torn descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *der-, “to split, flay, peel,” through Proto-Germanic *teran- (Grimm’s Law turned PIE *d- into Germanic *t-), Old English teran (with participle toren), and Middle English torn. The same root gave Greek derō, “to flay,” and derma, “skin” — the source of dermatology.
