1. The Latin crucible: fingere "to shape". The deep history of feint begins with a PIE root *dʰeyǵʰ- ("to knead, mold, shape, build"), which in Latin produced the verb fingere (fingō, fingere, fīnxi, fictum) - "to shape, form, fashion". From the very first Latin attestations, however, the verb carries its decisive second life: because what the mind "shapes" need not be real, fingere also came to mean "to imagine, devise, pretend". This is the semantic hinge on which the entire later history turns: the same word describes the potter's clay-shaping and the liar's shaping of a story, and its derivatives - fictio ("a making-up; a fiction"), fictor ("maker; forger"), fictilis ("of clay, earthen"), figura ("shape, figure"), figmentum ("something invented") - show how naturally Roman culture passed from craft to imagination. Roman rhetoric and satire exploited exactly this: to "fingere" an enemy's crime is to shape it, fiction and forgery being, in Latin as in English, two products of the same shaping hand.

2. Old French: feindre and the two faces of feint. In Gallo-Romance, fingere evolved regularly to Old French feindre, "to feign, pretend, dissemble", with the past participle feint ("feigned, false"). But Old French feindre also developed the sense "to shirk, hang back" - feigning was the coward's way of evading duty - and so the participle feint acquired a second, physical sense: "weak, spiritless, faint". The history of English registers both branches: the adjective borrowed around 1300 (spelled feint and faint alike) meant both "false, deceitful" and "weak, lacking strength", and the "weak" branch hardened, via early modern spelling habits, into the separate modern word FAINT, leaving the deceptive branch to its own career. The participle's feminine form, feinte, also crystallised as a noun in medieval and early modern French: "a pretense, a sham", and then specifically "a feigned attack" - the false thrust that fencing masters and drill sergeants had begun to codify.

3. Fencing academies and the codification of the feinte (17th century). The seventeenth century was the golden age of the French school of arms, when the masters of the Parisian salles d'armes systematised swordplay into the categories still used today - attack, parry, riposte, and feinte. The feinte was defined precisely as an attack made to draw a parry: the fencer offers a line, the opponent defends, and the attacker disengages to strike the now-open line. English gentlemen studied the French manner, and the word entered English in the 1680s as purely technical duelist's vocabulary, at exactly the moment when Restoration England was absorbing French military and courtly culture en masse. From the fencing hall it stepped immediately into drill: a feint was also the false demonstration of an assault made by troops to mask their real purpose.

4. From the piste to the field and the drawing-room (18th-19th centuries). The eighteenth century carried feint beyond combat. Military treatises of the age of Marlborough, Frederick the Great, and Napoleon describe feints at every scale, from skirmishes to sieges: a feint against a fortress gate, a feint crossing of a river, a feint to draw the enemy's reserves. Simultaneously the novel - the new literary form par excellence - discovered the word's social utility: characters "make a feint of" leaving, sleeping, agreeing, or falling in love, and the feint becomes an instrument of plot in the great nineteenth-century novelists. In 19th-century prizefighting the feint became a formal part of the boxer's art, and the same decades that produced feint-ruled ledgers (see below) made the word at home equally in the ring, the camp, and the drawing-room.

5. The feint/faint tangle and feint-ruled paper. A curious by-product of the word's divided ancestry is commercial stationery. Because "faint" was long spelled "feint" (the two were one word until the seventeenth century fixed the split), the light, fine lines ruled in ledgers and notebooks are still called "feint ruling" or "feint lines" in the trade - a survival of the older spelling applied to lines that are themselves faint. Lexicographers note the irony: the very word for "dim, weak" in the stationery trade wears the spelling of deception. The tangle also continues to generate errors in student writing, where "feint" for "faint" (and vice versa) is a classic howler - entirely excusable, since the two words were one until quite recently.

6. The feint in modern war, sport, and speech (20th-21st centuries). Twentieth-century military history gave the feint its grandest stage: the great deception operations of both world wars - dummy tanks, sham radio traffic, phantom armies - were feints at the scale of armies, culminating in the D-Day deception of 1944 (Operation Fortitude), which fixed the German high command's reserves on a landing that never came. In sport the feint spread from boxing to football (the step-over, the dummy), rugby, and basketball, and in the fighting games of the video-game age "feints" are engine-level mechanics, coded into the combat systems themselves. In journalism and political commentary "a feint" now means any calculated misdirection - "the tax cut was a feint", "his candidacy is a feint" - always carrying the duelist's implication that a real thrust follows. The word's register has never faltered: unlike "trick" or "ruse", feint demands that the deceiver be in command of space, timing, and the opponent's eye.

7. Phonetic outline. Latin /ˈfiŋ.ɡe.re/ → Old French /ˈfɛ̃n.drə/ (feindre; the nasalised vowel of -in- before -nd-) → French feinte /fɛ̃t/ (final schwa lost) → English /feɪnt/ (noun, 1680s), with the French nasal vowel reanalysed as the ordinary diphthong of "faint" - a phonetic union that kept the doublet feint/faint homophonous for centuries. The verb "to feint" (to make a feint) is a modern back-formation of the twentieth century.
