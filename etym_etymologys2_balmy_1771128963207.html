• Semitic (ultimately non-Indo-European): Hebrew bāśām/besem "balsam, spice, fragrant gum" (or Akkadian burāšu "juniper", a debated candidate): the word-family of "balsam" is generally traced to a Semitic source for a fragrant resinous plant.
  • Ancient Greek bálsamon (βάλσαμον) "balsam, fragrant resinous ointment" (attested 5th–4th century BCE), borrowed from a Semitic source.
  • Latin balsamum "balsam, the balsam tree, its fragrant gum" (Classical Latin, 1st century BCE), borrowed from Greek.
  • Vulgar/Old French basme, baume "balm, fragrant ointment" (10th–12th century), inherited from Latin balsamum.
  • Middle English balme/baume "balm, fragrant healing ointment" (c. 13th century), borrowed from Anglo-French and Old French.
  • Modern English balm "soothing, fragrant healing ointment" — then adjective balmy "bearing balm, soothing, fragrant, mild" formed with the suffix -y (attested 16th century, 1500s).
  • Final node: BALMY.

Debate note: The ultimate Semitic source is debated (Hebrew bāśām vs. Akkadian burāšu "juniper" vs. other proposals); the Greek–Latin–French–English chain is secure. The British slang "barmy" ("foolish") is a distinct word from barm "ale froth", frequently confused with balmy in spelling.
