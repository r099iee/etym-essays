The history of strove is the history of a Germanic quarrel word that detoured through French and returned to English ennobled as moral effort. The starting point is reconstructed Proto-Germanic *striban, meaning to quarrel, to contend, to exert oneself against another, attested indirectly through Frankish *striban and its Old French offspring estriver, to quarrel, and estrif, quarrel, contention. Frankish speakers, Germanic rulers in Gaul, supplied feudal vocabulary of dispute, and Old French estriver meant to brawl, to vie, to go to law with someone, a vigorous, slightly unruly verb of aristocratic contention. Its noun estrif gives modern English strife, preserving the older harsher sense that the verb has softened.

Middle English borrowed striven around 1300 with that contentious sense intact, knights stryven together, winds stryve, neighbours stryve in court. Phonologically the borrowing was nativized to the strong-verb pattern strive, strove, striven by analogy with drive, drove, driven and thrive, throve, thriven, even though French verbs are normally weak. The vowel series i, o, i lengthened under open-syllable lengthening and then diphthongized in the Great Vowel Shift, giving modern /straɪv/, /strəʊv/, /strɪvən/ in RP, with voicing of v preserved intervocalically. Spelling stabilized as strive with silent e marking long i, past strove with o, participle striven with en, a Литurgical-like triplet that feels archaic and dignified today.

  • Medieval quarrel stage: to strive as to fight, wrangle, and litigate, used by Chaucer and Froissart translations for tournaments and lawsuits, connotation of honour and dispute.
  • Early Modern moralization: under Reformation and humanist influence to strive acquires inward sense of labouring toward virtue, salvation, and learning, as in strive to enter at the strait gate, shifting enemy from neighbour to self and sin.
  • Romantic aspiration: 19th-century poets and essayists make striving a philosophy, Goethe's Faust, Carlyle's work ethic, where strove for the ideal becomes noble even in failure, connotation of endless becoming rather than victory.

Modern usage completes the softening, strove requiring no human opponent, so that even a silent world, a text, or a medium can strive for voice. The regularized past strived now competes with strove in American news prose, especially for-kept senses like strived to cut costs, while strove retreats to literary high ground where its irregular vowel signals gravity. To write it strove for voice and rhythm is therefore to summon six centuries of contention turned inward, a word that once meant men brawling now meaning marks on a page straining toward song, preserving in a single vowel change the civilizing of struggle into aspiration.

That civilizing is also visible in schooling and corpora, where strive, strove, striven is taught alongside drive and thrive as a surviving strong paradigm whose irregularity itself signals gravity. Children first produce strived by rule, then meet strove in stories as a mark that what happened mattered and cost something. Usage counts show strove persisting in fiction, obituary, and commencement address, collocating with mightily, valiantly, and tirelessly for ideals rather than quotas. The silent world that strove thus joins that solemn company, effort honoured for dignity even when voice has not yet fully arrived, a past tense that hallows attempt.
