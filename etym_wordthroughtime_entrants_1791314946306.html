- Late 16th–17th c. Early French legal borrowing - borrowed from Middle French entrant; used adjectivally ("entering upon") and as a noun for one who takes formal or physical possession of land, office, or ecclesiastical benefice.
- 18th c. Academic and professional institutionalisation - adopted specifically in Scottish universities (e.g. Aberdeen, St Andrews) as a technical title for incoming first-year students; also used in legal Inns of Court for new pupils.
- 19th c. Sporting and examination boom - with the Victorian codification of horse racing, athletic derbies, horticultural exhibitions, and competitive civil service examinations, entrant became the universal standard term for an enrolled competitor.
- Mid-to-late 20th c. Strategic economics expansion - transformed into a core technical concept of corporate strategy and industrial organization (Michael Porter's 1979 Five Forces framework popularized "barriers to entry" and "threat of new entrants").
- 21st c. Modern ubiquity - pervasive in international competitions, university admissions analytics, talent reality broadcasts, and digital market disruption analyses; thoroughly neutral, precise, and authoritative.
