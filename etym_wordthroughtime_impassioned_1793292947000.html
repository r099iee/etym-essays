- Latin — suffering borne: passio names the suffering of Christ and martyrs, endurance as holiness.
- Medieval — love's suffering: passion widens to any overpowering feeling, above all the pangs of love that make the lover suffer sweetly.
- 16th c. — the prefix strikes: impassioned appears for works and hearts endowed with passion, first of music, verse, and lovers.
- 17th–18th c. — the orator's word: sermons, pleadings, and tragedies are praised as impassioned, feeling raised into art.
- 19th c. — causes catch fire: reformers, patriots, and novelists grow impassioned over slavery, nations, and heroines, zeal made respectable.
- Today — earnestness admired, partisanship suspected: impassioned pleas move juries and charities, while impassioned allegiances warn scholars against choosing sides with the heart.
