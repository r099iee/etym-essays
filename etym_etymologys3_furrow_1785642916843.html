The story of furrow is the story of a plough-cut in the earth slowly learning to read a human face. It begins in Proto-Indo-European *perk-, 'to dig out, to tear out, to pull up' — a root of agricultural turning-over, attested in a wide arc of the family. In Latin the root produced porca, 'a ridge between two furrows', the raised bank of earth that the plough throws up; in the Germanic languages it produced the word for the cut itself. The contrast is instructive: the same root 'to dig up' gave the Latins the name of the ridge that is left and the Germanic peoples the name of the channel that is cut — each branch of the family foregrounded a different result of the same motion of the blade. This is a recurring lesson in etymology: the ancestor word is often more general than any single descendant. The Germanic form was *furh-, and it reached Old English as furh with genitive furge. Old English furh is attested before 1000 in its purely practical sense — the long, straight channel made by a ploughshare in the soil — and it shows every sign of being an old, ordinary, working word of the farm, with no literary pretension. The sound changes involved are clean and instructive. The Indo-European voiceless stops p and k were shifted by Grimm's Law to the Germanic fricatives f and h: PIE *perk- therefore surfaces as Germanic *furh- (with the u-grade vowel from the noun formation), and the final -k became -h, as it did in many Germanic words (compare Latin canis with Gothic hund, 'dog'). The result was a word as angular and earth-bound as the thing it named. Middle English reshaped the word slightly. The old noun had a genitive/oblique form furge, and in later Middle English the word was re-levelled as furwe, forwe, furgh — the oblique form winning out, as happened with many Old English nouns. From this Middle English furwe descends the modern spelling -ow (furrow), fixed by the early modern period. For seven centuries the word stayed exactly where it began: purely agricultural, the straight cut of the share, the elementary unit of the tilled field, so central that it even generated a unit of measure — the furlong, from furh-lang, 'furrow-long', originally the length of a ploughed furrow, the distance a team of oxen could pull the plough before turning, later standardized at 220 yards and still used in horse-racing today. A word for a line in the dirt had hardened into a unit of distance; the furrow is quietly present every time a race is run over 'six furlongs'. The great transformation came in the 16th century, when the word made the leap from field to face. The verb 'to furrow' is first attested in that century, meaning 'to make furrows in the earth, to plough'; almost immediately the image generalized, and furrows began to be spoken of in the skin — the deep parallel creases of the forehead and brow, marks of age, strain, and thought. The transfer is a perfect metaphor: time and emotion plough the face exactly as the share ploughs the soil, cutting straight, deep channels that do not easily smooth over. By the 18th and 19th centuries the expression 'to furrow one's brow' had settled into a fixed literary formula for concentration, displeasure, or anxiety — quieter and more dignified than a scowl, more legible than a mere frown, because it carries the concrete agricultural image of a field being ploughed by feeling. The two senses have lived side by side ever since, but their fortunes have diverged with history. The literal farming sense, once the everyday workhorse, has grown more remote and technical as mechanized agriculture has replaced the horse-drawn plough and as fewer people live close to the making of furrows; in specialist registers the word now also names technical channels — the cleavage furrow of a dividing cell in embryology, grooves in geology, planting furrows in horticulture. The figurative brow sense, by contrast, remains a constant of narrative prose: 'he furrowed his brow' is instant shorthand for a character receiving bad news, weighing a decision, or refusing to concede. In its figurative life the word keeps the whole weight of its history — the plough, the oxen, the straightened furrow of good farming, and the stern, enduring face of the farmer who ploughed it. 'A straight furrow' even entered English as a moral phrase for an honest, upright life, so that the same word that measures horse-races also measures human character.
