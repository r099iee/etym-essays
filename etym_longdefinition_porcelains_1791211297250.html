Porcelains is the plural noun form of porcelain, denoting multiple articles, types or collections of a fine, hard, white, vitrified ceramic ware prized for its translucency, smoothness and resonant ring. In its central modern sense it refers to true hard-paste and soft-paste ceramic vessels, figures and tableware fired at very high temperature so that the body vitrifies and becomes glass-like, non-porous and translucent when thin. A sentence such as they displayed Ming porcelains means they displayed several Chinese porcelain vessels or several categories of porcelain ware, not the material in the abstract.

The singular porcelain also functions as a mass noun for the material itself, while the plural porcelains particularises it into countable objects or varieties. Thus one says porcelain is fragile but these porcelains are from Dresden, parallel to glass versus glasses. The word carries strong associations with whiteness, delicacy, smoothness and luxury, and by extension with pallor and fragility in descriptions of skin or manners.

  • Core ceramic sense: multiple porcelain objects or wares, e.g. a cabinet of Meissen porcelains, export porcelains, blue-and-white porcelains. This is the dominant countable use in museums, auction houses and collecting.
  • Typological sense: distinct kinds, recipes or factories of porcelain, e.g. hard-paste and soft-paste porcelains, continental and English porcelains, meaning different bodies, glazes and manufacturing traditions.
  • Material mass sense (singular base): the vitrified substance itself, white, high-fired, made from kaolin and feldspathic rock, distinguished from earthenware which is porous and opaque and stoneware which is opaque and stone-like.
  • Shell archaism: in early modern texts porcellain could still mean the cowrie shell itself, now obsolete except in historical discussion of the etymology.
  • Attributive and figurative extensions: porcelain skin meaning very pale, smooth, flawless skin; porcelain teeth or doll suggesting glossy artificial perfection; porcelain fragility suggesting social delicacy or brittleness of character.

Secondary and domain-specific usages build on the core. In art history and archaeology, porcelains are classified by paste, glaze, decoration and kiln, as in Kraak porcelains, famille-rose porcelains, Blanc de Chine porcelains. In commerce, porcelains denotes lots at sale and export categories. In dentistry, porcelain veneers and crowns exploit the same sense of tooth-like, enamel-smooth ceramic, and the plural porcelains is occasionally used for sets of dental prostheses. In electrical engineering, porcelain insulators are vitrified ceramic insulators, though the plural usually refers to insulator units. The semantic bridge in each case is vitrified whiteness plus smoothness plus prestige.

Polysemy is therefore limited but systematic. The shift from mass to count, from material to objects to types, follows the general pattern of ware nouns like silver to silvers and china to chinas. The shift from shell to ceramic is historically frozen and no longer productive, but explains why porcelain sits alongside china as a ware name derived by analogy. The figurative shift from ceramic property to human property, smooth, white, cold, resonant, easily broken, motivates porcelain complexion, porcelain delicacy and porcelain composure, each trading on a different salient property of the ware. None of these replaces the literal sense; they layer over it in literary and advertising registers.

In the source context about clay on wickerwork culminating in the perfect porcelains of China, the word marks the endpoint of a technological evolution from crude fireproofed basketry to true high-fired vitrified ware. Here porcelains carries admiration for technical perfection, imperial Chinese mastery and aesthetic refinement, the culmination sense reinforced by perfect. Understanding the plural as both objects and achievement helps the reader feel the historical distance between Neolithic coated wicker and Song or Ming kilns.
