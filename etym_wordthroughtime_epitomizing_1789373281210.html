- Late 16th to Early 17th Century - Coined in Early Modern English as the present participle and gerund of the verb *epitomize* (from Latin *epitome*), strictly denoting the scholarly act of writing an abridgment, summary, or compendium of a lengthy historical chronicle or theological treatise.
- 17th to 18th Century - Began transitioning from literal textual abridgment to figurative compression; philosophers and moralists spoke of an individual whose life or conduct "epitomized" a Christian virtue, vice, or human condition in miniature.
- 19th Century - The semantic center of gravity shifted decisively toward quintessential embodiment; Victorian critics used *epitomizing* to denote that which serves as the supreme, archetype-defining example of an aesthetic style, national character, or moral temperament.
- 20th Century - Textual abridgment became a specialized or secondary sense, while the embodiment sense became universal across journalism, art criticism, and everyday discourse ("epitomizing elegance", "epitomizing modern decay").
- Contemporary Era - Dominates global English as the premier active verb and participle for representing the definitive standard, quintessential manifestation, or aesthetic pinnacle of a trend, brand, or cultural zeitgeist.
