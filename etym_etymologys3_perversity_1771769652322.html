The biography of PERVERSITY is the biography of an image: a turning. The word is built on two of the oldest roots in the Indo-European family. The first is *per-, "through, forward, across," the preposition that gave Latin per and survives in Sanskrit pári and Greek perí; in composition it contributes the sense of "thoroughly, completely." The second is *wert-, "to turn, to bend," one of the family's great verbs of rotation, attested in Sanskrit várte, "I turn," in Latin vertere, "to turn," and — remarkably — in Old English weorðan, "to become," which originally meant "to turn into" something. The two roots come together in the Latin verb pervertere, "to turn thoroughly, to turn upside down, to overturn, to corrupt": a verb whose basic physical meaning is tipping something all the way over — a cart, a stone, a state — and whose moral extension is tipping a mind or a life out of its proper course. From the very first, the Latin word carries the double register that still defines it: a physical toppling and a moral twisting. The classical history of the word is the history of a moral vocabulary forming itself. Latin already had perversus, the past participle of pervertere, meaning "turned the wrong way, inverted, contrary," and from it the language of the Republic and Empire formed perversitās, an abstract noun in the productive suffix -tās, meaning "perversity, wrong-headedness." In the Latin of the early Church and the Fathers — Tertullian, Ambrose, Augustine — perversitās becomes a word for the corruption of the soul, the willful turning of the creature from the Creator, the "crooked" way of the sinner set against the straight way of God. This is the sense that passes into the Romance languages: Old French perversité, attested from the twelfth century, means "perversity, wickedness, depravity," and the moral weight is already fully present when the word reaches England. Middle English took the word from French in the fourteenth century. The earliest English uses are religious and moral: perversite is the quality of the perverse, the wicked, the obstinate in sin. Chaucer and his contemporaries use the family in sermons, devotional prose, and the moralizing poetry of the age; to speak of a man's perversite was to speak of his willful turning from truth and virtue. For the next three centuries the word leads a stable double life. In theological writing it remains a serious word for moral corruption; in everyday and literary use it softens toward "contrariness, stubbornness, waywardness" — the quality of a person who is not necessarily wicked but simply determined to go against the grain. It is this lighter, secular sense that dominates the eighteenth century, and it is exactly this sense that Gibbon's sentence uses: the "perversity of tastes" that the observer witnesses "with delight" is a delightful, amusing contrariness, not a sin. The connoisseur of humanity enjoys the crooked turnings of taste as a naturalist enjoys a curiosity. The nineteenth century then darkens the word decisively. Under the combined pressure of moral earnestness and the new sciences of mind and body, perversity — and far more its close relative perversion — acquired a specialized and ominous sense: a deviation from the natural, the proper, or the normal, and above all a deviation in the sphere of sexual desire. The new psychology of the later nineteenth century, from the French school of sexology onward, borrowed the old moral word and gave it clinical authority: perversion became the technical term for sexual conduct or desire judged to be contrary to nature. Perversity, while never quite losing its broader sense, absorbed some of this darkness; in ordinary modern use it now suggests a tainted or unwholesome bent more often than the playful contrariness of Gibbon's age. The word's whole history — physical toppling, moral corruption, everyday contrariness, clinical-sexual deviation — is thus a single long turning, from the cart tipped over in a Latin field to the dark modern judgment, and back again to the delighted literary irony in which "the perversity of tastes" can still be witnessed with pleasure. Register is everything: the same word that condemns in a clinical case-note may still charm in a historian's sentence, and that ambivalence is precisely what has made perversity so durable a word for humanists to describe the willful twist in human nature.
