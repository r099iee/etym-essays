COARSE is an adjective meaning, first, “rough in texture or grain — not fine or smooth”, and, by extension, “of low or common quality; vulgar, rude, or lacking refinement in manner, language, or taste”. In the source context (“she has ___, red hands, all pricked by the needle, for she is a seamstress”) it describes hands roughened and thickened by hard needlework — the texture sense applied to skin. The word is a homophone of course, and historically it IS the adjective use of that noun: in Middle English, goods and fare “of course” meant those of the ordinary, usual, everyday run — and “ordinary” slid down the scale of value to “common, base, rough”.

Primary senses in modern English

  • Texture: composed of large, thick, or harsh particles or fibres; the opposite of fine — coarse sand, coarse salt, coarse cloth, coarse hair, coarse skin, coarse-ground flour. This is the oldest living everyday sense and applies across materials, foods, and bodies.
  • Quality: of low, common, or inferior grade — coarse food, coarse fare, coarse goods; not choice, refined, or fine.
  • Character and manners: lacking refinement, delicacy, or polish — vulgar, rude, indelicate — coarse manners, a coarse fellow, coarse laughter.
  • Speech and obscenity: coarse language, coarse jokes, coarse humour — crude, indecent, earthy, at the edge of the obscene; the polite word for what is now called “blue” or “vulgar”.

Secondary, technical, and domain senses

  • Coarse fishing (British): angling for freshwater fish other than salmon and trout — roach, bream, carp, pike — so called because such fish were once “coarse”, i.e., common and unfit for the gentlefolk's table; a survival of the old “common” meaning.
  • Coarse salt / coarse grind: culinary and industrial grades with large crystals or particles — coarse-ground coffee, coarse sea salt, coarse pepper.
  • Coarse thread / coarse pitch: screw threads with a large pitch, chosen for strength in soft materials; engineering antonym of fine thread.
  • Coarse adjustment: the large, rapid-focus control of a microscope, opposed to the fine adjustment.
  • Coarse aggregate: the gravel-sized stone fraction of concrete; coarse-grained describes rocks of large crystal size, photographic film of visible grain, and, figuratively, a blunt and simple approach to problems.
  • Coarse (archaic, of weather): stormy or rough — an obsolete sense paralleling “rough” on the sea.

The semantic bridge: from “of the ordinary course or run” (15th century) the word first meant “common, everyday”, then “of common quality — not choice”, and slid on the one hand into “rough, harsh, thick-grained” and on the other into “rude, vulgar, unrefined”. The texture sense and the moral sense reinforce each other: roughness of grain and roughness of manners were felt to be the same coarseness. Modern English keeps both strands alive while the parent noun course has long since gone its own way, leaving coarse and course as one of English's great spelling traps.

Register: neutral for texture and quality; mildly formal or euphemistic for indecency (“coarse language” remains politer than its referents); technical in fishing, engineering, microscopy, photography, geology, and cookery.
