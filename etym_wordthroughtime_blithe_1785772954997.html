- Prehistoric / reconstructed perception — Brightness and mild favor: the ancestral cluster links shining appearance with social gentleness rather than with carelessness.
- Old English blīþe — Positive praise-word for cheerful, kind, friendly disposition; associated with good moral and social warmth in poetry and prose, not with negligence.
- Middle English blithe / blythe — Continues as glad, merry, kindly; pastoral and courtly texts treat blithesomeness as an attractive, untroubled quality of heart.
- Early Modern English — Still largely affirmative "cheerful, happy"; emerging room for "lightly gay" readings that begin to edge toward insufficient seriousness in moral contexts.
- Romantic & 19th-century literary peak — Elevated pure joy (Shelley's blithe Spirit); concurrent Victorian prose can already shade toward unthinking lightness when paired with serious subjects.
- 20th–21st century public perception — Split personality: literary "blithe" can still mean radiant cheer, but everyday educated usage often hears criticism—blithe disregard for risk, privilege, or fact. The word form itself now frequently signals narrative judgment on careless optimism rather than simple happiness.
