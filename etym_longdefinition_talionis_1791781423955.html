Talionis is not an English common noun but the genitive singular of the Latin noun tāliō, “retaliation, punishment in kind.” It occurs in English almost exclusively inside the fixed phrase lex talionis, “the law of retaliation,” where lex is Latin for “law” and talionis is its genitive complement: literally “law of retaliation.” The capital T in the source is a typographic convention for the Latin phrase, not a sign that the word is a proper name.

Grammar of the phrase

  • tāliō, nominative: “retaliation”; genitive tāliōnis; a third-declension noun with stem tāliōn-, and no classical plural.
  • lex, nominative: “law”; genitive legis. Lex talionis is therefore a noun plus genitive phrase, not an adjective plus noun: it is the law of retaliation, not a “retaliatory law.”
  • In English the phrase is usually italicised in scholarly prose and pronounced /leks tæliˈəʊnɪs/. The bare genitive Talionis appears only as part of the citation, or in titles of works on the subject.

What the principle says

The lex talionis is the rule that a wrongdoer should suffer exactly the harm he inflicted: an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, a life for a life. Its canonical formulations are the Babylonian Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BCE), the Roman Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE), and the biblical passages of Exodus 21:23–25, Leviticus 24:19–20 and Deuteronomy 19:21. In each case the rule stands in a legal context concerned with the limits of vengeance, not with its encouragement.

The crucial historical point: a ceiling, not a floor

Modern readers often hear “an eye for an eye” as a licence for cruelty. Historically the formula functions as a restriction. In a society without a central court, an injury set off a blood feud in which the victim's kin could kill indiscriminately; the talion fixed a maximum — one eye for one eye, no more — and often allowed payment in money instead. Rabbinic interpretation (Talmud, Bava Kamma) read the biblical talion as monetary compensation, and Roman law progressively replaced private retaliation with damages and public penalties. The principle is thus a milestone in the history of proportionality.

Roman law and the Twelve Tables

The Twelve Tables contained the clause preserved by Aulus Gellius (Noctes Atticae 20.1.14): “Si membrum rupsit, ni cum eo pacit, talio esto” — “if he has broken a limb, unless he makes peace with him, let there be retaliation.” The conditional is essential: the parties could settle, and the talion applied only if they did not. Later Roman law narrowed the talion and, under Justinian, replaced it in most cases with pecuniary penalties.

Modern legal and philosophical life

  • Retributive justice. Kant's principle that punishment must fit the crime, and Hegel's account of punishment as the negation of the crime, are philosophical descendants of the talion, though both reject literal mirroring.
  • Proportionality in war. The jus in bello requirement that force be proportionate to the military aim is sometimes loosely called a lex talionis; the analogy is suggestive but imprecise, since proportionality in humanitarian law is not measured against the enemy's prior act.
  • Everyday rhetoric. “Lex talionis” is invoked for tit-for-tat politics, for capital-punishment debates and for online feuds; “measure for measure” is its English cousin.

Related English words

English also has the rare noun talion, “retaliation in kind,” borrowed from French; the common noun retaliation is the vernacular equivalent of talionis; and the adjective retaliatory covers the modern sense. The Latin phrase survives because it names a principle compactly and because it carries the authority of Roman law.

Why the source uses it

The sentence lists the Lex Talionis as the first of three witnesses to the persistence of revenge in law, followed by the Code of Hammurabi and the Mosaic demand. The Latin phrase supplies a learned, monumental label for a practice the author wants to present as ancient, official and slightly alarming — a law of exact payback lurking behind modern punishment.
