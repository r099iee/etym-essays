A Norse word in English dress. Thrust is one of the solidly Scandinavian words that entered English in the centuries after the Viking settlements — the same wave that brought take, give, hit, knife, law and leg. Recorded from about 1300 — first in the north and east, the old Danelaw — it took over part of the semantic ground of native scūfan ("shove") and þryccan ("press"), standing beside the new French loan push (from Old French poulser/pousser). Out of that crowded competition English settled into a permanent three-way division that still organises the sense-field: shove is the blunt, often sideways push; push is steady, sustained pressure; thrust is the sudden, directed, point-first shove. The medieval rivalry became a lexical template.

  • Sound and form. The Norse cluster þr- was comfortable in English (thrive, throng, thralldom are all Norse), and the abrupt -st- gives the word a percussive onset that suits its sense — it sounds like its own action, a phonetic luck the language has never lost. The past forms levelled early: weak "thrusted" lost out in the standard language and survives only as a rare, old-fashioned variant, leaving the invariant thrust/thrust/thrust that now seems irregular. The paradox is worth stating plainly: the form is conservative-looking because its forms merged, not because it preserved an ancient strong pattern — an appearance of age produced by convenience.
  • The age of the sword (14th–17th centuries). In the Renaissance of fencing, "to thrust" is the point attack, and the manuals debate the thrust against the blow across real martial culture: the English master George Silver's Paradoxes of Defence (1599) argues at length that the English "push of the pike" and the thrust exceed the Continental cut in reach and speed. "A thrust" becomes the technical word of the fencing school — thrust, parry, riposte — and the language of duels and battles alike. The Authorised Version (1611) uses the plain physical verb with memorable force (Numbers 25:8, "and thrust both of them through"), and the drama of the age keeps the word on the stage and in the street.
  • The figurative turn (16th–17th centuries). The verb turns inward as the body's verb of intrusion: "to thrust oneself forward" is to push in presumptuously; "to thrust upon" is to impose — the same sudden force, now social. The noun acquires the sense of a verbal assault ("a thrust at his reputation"), and "the thrust of an argument" begins its long career as the image of what an argument is driving at — the point of the whole push.
  • Engineering and war (19th–20th centuries). The industrial age needed a word for axial force, and found it ready-made: thrust bearings, propeller thrust, screw thrust, and the lateral thrust of arches and roofs in engineering and architecture. The First and Second World Wars made "thrust" the standard noun for the deep, concentrated offensive push — the point of an army — and postwar journalism kept it for every drive and push in politics and business. After 1945 rocketry took the word to the launch pad: thrust-to-weight ratio, thrust vectoring, maximum thrust — the engineer’s measure of a rocket’s push, now household jargon of spaceflight.
  • Geology (20th century). Structural geology borrowed the word for thrust faults — the great low-angle fractures along which whole rock masses are pushed up and over one another, named in the classic overthrusts of the Scottish Highlands (the Moine Thrust) and the American Rockies (the Lewis Thrust). A violent human verb now names the slow violence of mountain building: the movement is measured in millions of years, the word is the sudden shove of the hand.
  • Modern register. Today THRUST spans the fence, the fencing school, the battlefield, the bearing, the rocket and the report. Its invariant past makes it feel ancient and strong, but that invariance is the very sign of its Norse, vernacular history — a loanword levelled into a shape that looks more native than the natives. In the context sentence of this card, three preterites in a row — "they beat on it, they thrust and pushed at it" — show the word at work in its oldest register: the plain physical push, sudden and repeated, against a door that will not open.
