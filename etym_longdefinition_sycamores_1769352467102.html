sycamores (plural noun): large deciduous trees bearing the name "sycamore" — a name that, confusingly, designates different trees in different regions and eras. In John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, set on the Salinas River in California, the "sycamores" are the American plane trees (Platanus, most likely the western Platanus racemosa, the California sycamore): tall, water-loving trees with broad, maple-like, palmately lobed leaves; trunks whose bark flakes away in patches to reveal a mottled, white, cream, and green surface; and round hanging seed-balls. Primary senses, defined by region and era:
  • American English: the plane tree Platanus occidentalis (the eastern "buttonwood" or American sycamore) and western species such as Platanus racemosa (California sycamore) — towering riparian trees of riverbanks, creeks, and bottomlands, with peeling mottled bark and dense shade; exactly the trees arching over the pool in the Steinbeck passage.
  • British English: Acer pseudoplatanus, the "sycamore maple" or "great maple," a Eurasian maple with large, five-lobed leaves and hanging clusters of two-winged samara fruits, widely planted as an ornamental and timber tree and naturalized in parks, streets, and woods — the tree named in this note's short definition ("a large Eurasian maple with winged fruits").
  • Biblical and historical: Ficus sycomorus, the "sycomore fig" or "mulberry fig," an African and Levantine fig-tree with fig-like fruit and broad, mulberry-like leaves, famous as the tree climbed by Zacchaeus in the Gospel of Luke (19:4) — the original referent of the word and the source of its form.
Secondary and modern uses:
  • Timber and woodworking: American sycamore (plane) wood is hard, dense, pale, and fine-grained, used for furniture, butcher's blocks, flooring, and veneer; sycamore maple wood is prized for musical instruments (violin backs), kitchenware, and turned objects.
  • Ornamental and civic planting: sycamores and their hybrid the London plane (Platanus x acerifolia) are among the world's great urban street trees, valued for dense shade, pollution tolerance, and hard pruning; in American towns, sycamore-lined avenues and sycamore-shaded creeks are common landmarks.
  • Ecological role: as riparian trees, sycamores stabilize riverbanks, overhang and shade pools (cooling the water for fish, as in the passage), and provide habitat in floodplain forests.
  • Nomenclature caution: because the same name names a fig, a maple, and a plane, any precise use of the word must state the species; dictionaries note the regional split, and botanists never rely on the common name alone.
Historical and semantic bridge: Greek sykomoros "fig-mulberry" (Ficus sycomorus) passed through Latin sycomorus and Old French sicamor into Middle English as a biblical word. When northern Europeans could not find the biblical fig, 16th-century British botanists re-applied the name to the broad-leaved maple Acer pseudoplatanus; later English colonists in America re-applied it again to the native plane trees Platanus. The name thus "wandered" across three unrelated tree families by virtue of one shared feature — broad, maple-like leaves — leaving the modern regional split documented above. Register: neutral, descriptive; the plural form in the passage signals a grove of riverbank plane trees seen as scenery.
