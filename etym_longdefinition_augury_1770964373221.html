augury (noun; plural auguries) is a word with two tightly interlocking primary senses that constantly bleed into one another. In its narrowest, most technical, and historically grounded sense it names the ancient practice of divining the future by observing omens — most famously, in the state religion of Republican Rome, the reading of the flight, cries, positions, and feeding behaviour of birds by a college of officially sanctioned priests called augurs. In its broader, far more common modern sense it names the omen or sign itself: the observable event, object, or circumstance that an interpreter, a community, or an individual takes to portend what is to come. Both senses are simultaneously in play in the literary context of this card, where a speaker calls the verses before them "an augury of your ascent," thereby elevating the written word to the status of a divine portent and turning a piece of poetry into an instrument of prophecy. The word is treated throughout this note as a countable noun ("an augury"), while the mass-noun reading ("augury as a discipline or art") is acknowledged where relevant.

The full polysemy of the word can be set out as follows:

  • Sense 1 — The practice of divination (ancient, ritual, institutional). The classical sense: augury as the formalized art of interpreting omens, especially through the observation of birds. In Rome, augury was not a private superstition but a pillar of public religion; no war could be declared, no assembly lawfully convened, and no magistrate legitimately inaugurated unless the omens had been taken and found favourable. The augurs formed a state college, and their technical apparatus — the division of the sky into quarters (templa), the watching for the auspicium, the recording of bird signs — gives the word a dense technical denotation that is often missed.
  • Sense 2 — The sign or omen itself (metonymic shift). By metonymy the word travelled from the act of interpretation to the thing interpreted: an "augury" is the particular event — a bird crossing left, a clap of thunder, a flight of ravens — that carries prophetic meaning. This is the sense used in "an augury of your ascent": the verses themselves are the sign.
  • Sense 3 — Any portent, natural or accidental. Once detached from the birds and the priests, the word broadened to cover any occurrence read as significant: a broken mirror, a comet, an unusual dream, the chance meeting of a black cat. In this general sense augury approaches "omen" almost exactly, and the two are frequently treated as interchangeable in ordinary prose.
  • Sense 4 — A prediction, forecast, or prophecy in general. In looser literary usage an augury can be the spoken or written prediction itself — a prophecy delivered in elevated language, rather than the natural sign on which the prediction is based. This sense drifts into "prophecy" and "prognostication," though it retains a hint of the ancient ritual machinery that those words do not carry.
  • Sense 5 — Metaphoric and modern-extension sense. In contemporary writing, an author may describe a stock-market trend, a poll result, a first chapter, a weather pattern, or a single gesture as "an augury" of what follows. The word here functions as a grave, slightly archaic synonym for "sign," "indicator," or "harbinger," and it deliberately imports an atmosphere of destiny, fate, and cosmic intention into contexts that are otherwise wholly secular.
  • Sense 6 — Register and connotation. Across all senses the word is marked: it is formal, literary, and faintly archaic; it is not the everyday word for a sign. Using "augury" rather than "sign" or "omen" signals that the speaker treats the event as charged with meaning, dignity, or dread — a tone of solemn expectancy that the plainer synonyms lack.

The historical semantic bridge. The chain of senses is a textbook case of metonymy layered on specialization. The PIE root *h₂ewg- "to increase" gave Latin augēre "to make grow, to favour," and the augur was, on the most favoured scholarly reading, "one who increases or favours" — that is, one who secures divine favour and growth for the community. From the priest's office came augurium, the name of the practice; by metonymy the word was then transferred from the rite to the sign that the rite interpreted; and from the sign it was finally generalized to any portent whatsoever, with a modern figurative extension to any significant indicator. Each widening of the word loosened one more link to the birds and the priests, until the technical term of Roman state religion became a general, dignified word for a sign of what is to come.

Boundary comparisons. To see the exact shape of the word, it helps to hold it against its neighbours. An omen is any sign, whether auspicious or terrible, and may be accidental; an augury in the strict sense is the interpreted reading of such a sign by a trained reader. A portent is a sign of momentous, usually dreadful change, and is heavily loaded toward catastrophe; an augury is neutral in valence until read. A prophecy is a direct verbal or written disclosure, often spoken by a god's mouthpiece; an augury is an interpretation of observed signs, not a revelation. Auspice (from avis "bird" + specere "to look") is the ornithological twin of augury — literally "bird-watching" — and survives in "auspicious" and "under the auspices of." A presage is a lighter, purely literary word for a presentiment or foreboding, with no institutional or ritual history behind it.
