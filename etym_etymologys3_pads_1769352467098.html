The story of pads is the story of how a single image — the soft, flat under-layer — has been endlessly re-used by English speakers for five centuries. At the deepest attested level the word family belongs to the Germanic "path" word: Middle Dutch pad and Low German pad meant "path, trodden way," and in Low German the same word also named the sole of the foot, the soft instrument with which one treads. Behind this stands a reconstructed Proto-Germanic *paþaz "trodden track," often connected to a Proto-Indo-European *pent- "to tread, go," though the PIE link is not beyond dispute, since *paþaz has no sure cognates outside Germanic. Some scholars therefore treat the "path" family as Germanic-only, which makes the ultimate pre-Germanic stage genuinely unknown — a point to flag rather than to paper over with a confident reconstruction. When pad enters English in the 1500s (first records c. 1553), it does so as "a soft cushion, wad, or stuffed bag," borrowed from the Low German / Dutch sense of a soft sole or cushion. In the 16th and 17th centuries the word multiplies rapidly: it is a bed-stuffing, a saddle cushion, the soft foot-cover of a horse, and the robber's footpad (a highwayman who roams on foot). The 18th- and 19th-century natural historians then apply pad to the elastic, hairless cushions under an animal's paw — the "foot-pads" of dogs, cats, and bears — and it is precisely this sense, with its neutral, physical register, that Steinbeck draws on in Of Mice and Men when he writes of "the spread pads of dogs from the ranches" on the damp riverbank sand. Meanwhile the industrial and consumer revolutions keep generating new pads, each read through the same template of a flat, soft, protective or absorbent layer:
  • Writing pads and notepads (19th century) — a stack of paper glued at one edge, imagined as a flat cushion of paper.
  • Sports and armor padding — shoulder pads, knee pads, elbow pads, hip pads — soft layers that absorb impact.
  • Aerospace surfaces — launching pads for rockets, landing pads and helipads for aircraft and drones — flat cushioned or prepared bases.
  • Electronic and household objects — mouse pads, touchpads, keypads, ink pads, brake pads, and finally the brand-name iPad of 2010.
  • Slang — a "bachelor pad," "crash pad," "my pad": the home imagined as a soft place to lie down and rest, borrowed by 1960s-70s counterculture.
The verb to pad shows the same logic turned loose and moralized: to pad a jacket is to stuff it with soft material, and to pad a speech, essay, or expense account is to stuff it with extra filler — a dishonest "inflating" sense that carries a clearly negative charge. And to pad along is to walk with a soft, steady footfall, echoing the original sense of treading. From a Low German sole to a dog's footprint, from a notepad to a launching pad, from an honest cushion to a padded expense claim, pads is a compact history of how a language recycles one vivid image across five centuries of material culture.
