- Old English period - ivy is a plain, native word (OE īfig) for the evergreen climber; already in Anglo-Saxon tradition it is the green that outlives the year, twined with holly into the midwinter wreath - a plant of continuity and winter stubbornness rather than of decay.
- Middle Ages - ivy accumulates festive and sacred faces: it is Bacchus's plant and the bush that hung over the alehouse door, while its habit of staying green makes it a Christian emblem of everlasting life; the word names a thing both sacred and tavern-common.
- 17th–18th c. - the Romantic turn begins: ivy-clad ruins, in poetry and landscape painting, become the stock image of picturesque age and noble neglect - the plant that makes abandonment beautiful.
- 19th c. - Victorian flower language fixes ivy as 'fidelity', because it holds fast and stays green; the ivy-mantled tower turns into a byword for nostalgia, while the word itself remains plain, native English in continuous use for a thousand years.
- 20th–21st c. - the name goes transferable: the American Ivy League (1930s) borrows its prestige from ivy-grown old colleges, and poison ivy (an unrelated plant) shows how loosely the name can be applied; today ivy still means the quiet, clenching green that erases masonry - nature taking possession of what man has left.
