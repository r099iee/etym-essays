Chain (the deepest PIE stage is uncertain — stated explicitly). Stage 1 (reconstructed, debated): PIE root not securely established. The Germanic verb is usually traced to a base meaning 'to step widely, stretch, stride', plausibly connected with the PIE root *ster- 'to spread, stretch, extend' (the same family from which English straddle is ultimately formed) or with an extended form *strey-/streydʰ- 'to press, stretch, stride'; but no universally accepted PIE etymon is attested, and some scholars treat the Germanic verb as an independent, possibly expressive formation. Stage 2: Proto-Germanic *strīdaną 'to stride; to step widely; to strive, contend' — reconstructed from West Germanic cognates; the same root produced Old Saxon strīdan 'to strive, contend' and Old High German strītan 'to contend, fight, argue' (ancestor of German streiten 'to quarrel, fight'). Stage 3: Old English strīdan 'to stride, to step widely, to straddle' — attested c. 1000 (in Ælfric's writings and in Old English glosses); a strong verb of class I with past strād. Stage 4: Middle English striden 'to walk with long steps, to straddle' — attested from the early 13th century (e.g. in the Ancrene Wisse and later Middle English texts). Stage 5: Modern English stride (infinitive) with the strong past strode and participle stridden — the past continues Old English strād (> Middle English strod > modern strode), attested in its modern written form from the 16th century onward. Final node: STRODE. Debate note: (1) the PIE etymon is uncertain — reconstruction stops at a probable link to a 'spread/stretch' root, with no attested intermediate; (2) the Germanic root *strīdan- carried two divergent meanings — 'stride' in the English branch and 'strive/contend/fight' on the Continent (cf. German streiten, Old Saxon strīdan) — a prehistoric semantic split of which the English word preserves only the gait; (3) Old English strīdan also meant 'to straddle', a sense now archaic for the verb but surviving in the derived straddle.
