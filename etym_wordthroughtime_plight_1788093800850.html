- [c. 1200: "condition, state"] - Borrowed from Anglo-French plit — at first neutral: any state at all, "in good plight" or "in evil plight".
- [13th–15th centuries: two words in one body] - The native Old English pliht ("danger, peril, pledge") lives alongside the French word; scribes begin to blur the spellings.
- [c. 1550: the collision] - The -gh- spelling wins for the "condition" word, and the old word's "peril" sense bleeds into it; "sorry plight" becomes a stock phrase.
- [17th–19th centuries: the humanitarian word] - Plight is fixed as a miserable condition; Victorian reform writing takes up "the plight of the poor" and "the plight of the working classes".
- [20th–21st centuries: refugee-century usage] - The journalistic and humanitarian staple — "the plight of refugees"; the neutral "state" sense and the marriage-vow "plight one's troth" survive only as archaisms.
