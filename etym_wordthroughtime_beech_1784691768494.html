- Old English & medieval woodland sense - beech is the great mast-tree of the English woods, its small triangular nuts (beech-mast) feeding swine driven to pannage and the wild creatures of the forest; a tree bound up with the rural economy
- Later medieval & early modern working sense - the close-grained wood takes the place of oak for furniture, tool handles, bowls and turnery, and the tree enters the everyday crafts of the countryside
- 17th-19th century domestic sense - seasoned beech becomes the connoisseur's firewood, burning with a clear, steady flame and little smoke, prized for cooking fires, baking ovens and the smoke-houses where herring and bacon were cured; the word carries a cosy, homely glow
- Contemporary sense - beech is the stately amenity tree of parks and beechwoods and a premium timber for furniture and flooring; the connotation is wholesome, old-English and faintly nostalgic
