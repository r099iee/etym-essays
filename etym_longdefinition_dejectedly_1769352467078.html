dejectedly: in a low-spirited, downcast, sad, defeated, or disheartened manner; with visible drooping of the head, shoulders, and gaze that signals a temporary collapse of hope or confidence. As the adverb of the adjective dejected, the word is a classic adverb of manner in English: it does not describe what someone does but how they do it, colouring any verb of walking, speaking, sitting, or responding with the psychological signature of dejection. In the example sentence "He walked away dejectedly after hearing the bad news," the adverb turns a neutral physical action (walking away) into a whole emotional tableau: one can visualise the slumped shoulders, the lowered head, the slow dragging step. The nuance carried by dejectedly is best captured as visible, drooping low-spiritedness — the sadness is not merely felt but performed by the body, a brief, humbled posture of the defeated. Grammar, register, and range
  • Adverb of manner: modifies verbs of motion, posture, speech, and response — "he sighed dejectedly," "she slumped dejectedly into the chair," "they trudged dejectedly home."
  • Register: literary and narrative rather than casual speech; it belongs to written prose, storytelling, and descriptive journalism, and is rare in ordinary conversation, where "sadly" or "miserably" would be used.
  • Derivation: built on the past participle dejected plus the productive English adverb-forming suffix -ly (Old English -līce), the same suffix in sadly, quickly, angrily.
Polysemy & the full semantic family
The adverb is only the tip of a family that shows a striking historical shift from physical to psychological meaning. The members are:
  • Verb deject (15th–17th c.): originally "to cast down, throw down, fell" in a literal, physical sense — a knight dejects his opponent, a storm dejects a tree. From the 16th century the verb turned inward to mean "to cast down in spirits, to depress."
  • Adjective dejected (1580s): literally "cast down," first physical, then almost exclusively emotional — "cast down in spirits, low-spirited, discouraged."
  • Noun dejection: the state of being cast down in spirits; despair, lowness of mood. In modern medicine dejection still carries a technical, physiological twin meaning: "the voiding or casting out of excrement; the bodily waste so ejected," because Latin dēicere also meant "to void, discharge" the bowels. This scatological sense is a genuine modern technical usage of the same root.
  • Adverb dejectedly (17th c. onward): the modern descendant, entirely psychological, stripped of all literal force.
Historical semantic bridge
The entire family descends from Latin dēicere, "to throw down, cast down" — a compound of the prefix dē- ("down, away") and the verb iacere ("to throw, cast"). In Classical Latin the verb was purely physical: a soldier was dēiectus (cast down) from his horse, a building was dēiecta (demolished). The metaphorical bridge to emotion is ancient and intuitive: if the body can be cast down to the ground, so can the spirit. To be "dejected" is to be emotionally thrown to the floor. By the late 16th century English had fully made this transfer, and "cast down" became a stock collocation for depressed spirits. The historical bridge is therefore a classic physical-to-psychological metaphor, of exactly the same type that produced depress ("press down") and downcast ("cast down" in gaze). Modern dejectedly is the fully psychological end-point of that chain: no speaker today would use it of a thrown object, only of a sad person. Metaphoric shifts and modern extensions
  • Physical → emotional: the core metaphoric shift, complete by c. 1600; "cast down in body" becomes "cast down in heart."
  • Medical/technical dejection: physiology retains the literal "casting out" sense — "dejection" as excretion, and the older verb "deject" as "to void."
  • Register narrowing: once a strong, dramatic word, dejectedly is now a quiet, descriptive literary adverb; it describes a passing low mood rather than clinical despair (which would be "despondently" or "hopelessly").
Boundary against near-synonyms
Dejectedly describes visible, drooping, humbled sadness without despair. Despondently is graver, implying the total abandonment of hope; gloomily adds sullen darkness of mood; mournfully adds sorrow for a specific loss; forlornly adds loneliness and abandonment. The dejected person is not hopeless, not angry, and not weeping — merely cast down, temporarily lowered, with the body telling the story the heart cannot.
