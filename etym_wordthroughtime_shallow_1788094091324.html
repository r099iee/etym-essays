- (c. 1400, Middle English schalowe “not deep”) - A practical word for water, pits, and vessels; the opposite of deep; tone strictly physical and neutral.
- (15th–16th c.) - Extends to graves, dishes, soil, and breath; the noun “the shallows” appears as sandbanks and fords where boats must fear grounding — a navigator’s hazard word.
- (16th–17th c.) - The figurative leap: “shallow wit,” “shallow brains,” “shallow love.” Shakespeare makes it a badge of intellectual emptiness and even names a foolish country justice Shallow in Henry IV, Part 2 (1598) and The Merry Wives of Windsor. Tone: moral — shallowness becomes a mild vice.
- (18th–19th c.) - Scientific expansion: shallow seas and shallow deposits in geology; the word becomes standard in hydrography and natural history.
- (20th c.–today) - Technical splitting: shallow-focus earthquakes, shallow-water equations, shallow orthography, shallow copy, shallow parsing, shallow learning versus deep learning. In culture, shallow is the standing accusation against celebrity gossip, reality television, and surface-level politics. Tone: an antonym of genuine, used as a quiet insult.
