- 17th c. — Verb emergence (*predict*): borrowed from Latin praedicere ('to mention beforehand, foretell'), initially confined to scholarly, astrological, or theological contexts denoting prophecy and astronomical forecasting.
- Mid-19th c. — Adjective formation (*predictable*): emerged in mid-Victorian scientific treatises, mechanics, and philosophy to describe natural systems and physical phenomena governed by deterministic Newtonian laws.
- Early to Mid-20th c. — Adverbial coining (*predictably*): formed in modern English to describe actions occurring in demonstrable accordance with statistical forecasts, psychological expectations, or behavioral models.
- 1960s–1980s — Grammatical expansion as an evaluative sentence adverb (disjunct): surged in journalistic, academic, and essayistic prose, typically positioned at the beginning of sentences (e.g. 'Predictably, the talks broke down') to express world-weary non-surprise, ironic commentary, or analytical detachment.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Behavioral science & popular economics: cemented in public consciousness through behavioral economics (such as Dan Ariely’s Predictably Irrational), describing systematic, recurring human departures from idealized rational decision-making.
