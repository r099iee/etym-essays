1. Philological Architecture of Tolkien’s Onomastics. In developing the onomastic landscape of the Shire, J.R.R. Tolkien operated not as an arbitrary inventor of fantasy sounds, but as a professional comparative philologist and Rawlinson and Bosworth Professor of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford University. Tolkien established an elaborate translational fiction for The Lord of the Rings: the actual historical records were presumed to have been composed in Westron (the Common Speech of northwestern Middle-earth), and Tolkien presented himself merely as the modern English translator of the Red Book of Westmarch. Under this framework, hobbit personal names were systematically translated to reflect their societal register, genealogical antiquity, and cultural associations within an English-language analog.

2. The Took Family and Chivalric Frankish/Romance Nomenclature. While common Shire hobbits from working-class or agricultural backgrounds bore simple English names drawn from nature, gemstones, or diminutive nicknames (e.g., Samwise from Old English samwīs "half-wise", Hamfast "home-stayer", Daisy, Rosie), the aristocratic patrician families—most notably the Tooks of Great Smials and the Brandybucks of Buckland—exhibited a markedly different onomastic tradition. The Tooks represented the landed gentry: wealthy, adventurous, eccentric, and holding the hereditary dignity of Thain of the Shire. To convey their ancient dignity and romantic, un-hobbitish taste for wandering, Tolkien assigned them names echoing the heroic nomenclature of the Dark Ages and high medieval romance: Frankish, Gothic, Carolingian, and Middle English chivalric names such as Isengrim, Hildigrim, Isumbras, Ferumbras, Fortinbras, and Bandobras.

3. The Historical and Etymological Elements of Bandobras. The name Bandobras is a two-part philological compound synthesizing ancient Germanic martial roots with medieval Old French chivalric poetry:

  • The First Element (Bando-): The prefix originates in the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰendʰ- ("to bind, tie, join together"), which produced Proto-Germanic *bandiz ("bond, cord, fetter"). In early Germanic warfare, warriors formed sworn martial brotherhoods bound by sacred oaths, known as a "band" or "troop". This term entered Late Latin as bandum (a standard, flag, or banner under which a band assembled) and flourished in Old French as bande ("troop, banner, company"). By utilizing Bando-, Tolkien anchored the name in ideas of military cohesion, banner-leading command, and physical strength.
  • The Second Element (-bras): The terminal element derives from the Classical Latin bracchium ("arm", borrowed from Ancient Greek brachīōn, meaning the upper arm). In Old French, this developed into bras ("arm"). In the 12th-century French chansons de geste (such as the epic Fierabras), the suffix -bras was appended to adjectives to forge heroic warrior epithets celebrating tremendous physical power (e.g., Fierabras, meaning "proud-arm" or "fierce-arm"; Hautbras, "high-arm"). Middle English romances subsequently adopted this naming convention (yielding names like Sir Isumbras, meaning "iron-arm").

4. Thematic and Literary Synthesis. In uniting these two elements, Tolkien created Bandobras: literally meaning "arm of the band," "strong-arm of the troop," or "banner-arm." This compound served as the perfect high-chivalric counterpart to his earthy, rustic English nickname Bullroarer. The contrast is quintessential Tolkien: on one hand, the genealogical tables record the majestic, chivalric name Bandobras, testifying to his noble lineage and historic martial savior status; on the other hand, the fireside storytellers of the Shire celebrate Bullroarer, the four-foot-five giant who rode a horse, clubbed a goblin king, and invented the rustic game of golf. Through this dual naming, Tolkien bridged the grand heroic epics of northern Europe with the cozy, humorous pastoralism of the English countryside.
