EYRIE (older and American spelling aerie, also aery, eyry) is the nest of a bird of prey — an eagle, hawk, falcon, vulture, or large owl — built on a bare cliff ledge, a rocky crag, a high pinnacle, or the topmost fork of a great tree, so elevated, exposed, and inaccessible that the word has widened over time to mean any lofty, isolated, defensible perch. In The Hobbit, Bilbo has just been snatched from the burning pines by the Great Eagles, and he “fell onto the rough platform of an eagle’s eyrie”: the word fixes the scene on a bare, wind-scoured shelf of rock high among the mountain peaks, far above the world, where the whole Company is set down in dazed disbelief. The tone of eyrie in this passage is archaic, majestic, and faintly formidable — an eyrie is not a snug nest but a fastness, a high citadel of the air that can be approached only on the wing, and Tolkien’s adjective “rough” insists that this is no soft, twig-lined cradle but the stark stone platform on which great birds roost and rear their young.

Primary senses.

  • Raptor’s nest — the literal sense: the breeding platform of a bird of prey, built on bare rock or in the crown of a great tree, often reused year after year and added to until it grows enormous.
  • High, isolated dwelling or perch — the extended sense: any lofty, secluded, defensible place, especially a dwelling or retreat built above the surrounding world; “their mountain cabin was an eyrie above the clouds”.
  • Falconry sense: in the traditional falconer’s vocabulary, the eyrie is the place where a hawk breeds; the young taken from it were called eyases, from Old French niais “nestling”.

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Ornithology: in field guides, an eyrie denotes the nest-site of large raptors, frequently a landmark structure on a cliff face, a crag, or a tall tree, and is distinguished from the nests of small birds.
  • Architecture and real-estate metaphor: a penthouse or high office is called “an eyrie” to stress height, isolation, and a commanding view.
  • Military and strategic metaphor: a stronghold placed to be unassailable — “a mountain eyrie” of guerrillas, outlaws, or of power itself.
  • Literary register: eyrie as a symbol of pride, aloofness, and wild sovereignty; the air as the element of the powerful and the free.

Spelling variants and the historical bridge. The word descends from Latin area “level open space; threshing floor”, passed through Old French aire into English in the fifteenth century as aire, eyry, or ayrie, and settled as eyrie in the nineteenth century in a deliberate, faintly antiquarian respelling that tied the word to air and aerial. The “airy” flavour of the modern word is therefore a folk respelling, not etymology: an old word for a dusty threshing floor came to sound as though it belonged to the sky.

Register and collocations. Eyrie collocates with eagle’s, mountain, cliff-top, high, and with adjectives like desolate, windswept, inaccessible, lofty; it takes metaphors of isolation, pride, and power, and carries the quiet medieval glamour of falconry and romance. It differs sharply from nest (any bird’s home, however humble), roost (a sleeping place, not a breeding structure), perch (a bare resting branch), and lair (a ground-level den).
