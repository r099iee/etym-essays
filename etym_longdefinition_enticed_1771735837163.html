enticed: the past participle of entice, meaning attracted or tempted by the offer of pleasure or advantage; drawn or led on by a lure. In the sentence about Gibbon, enticed describes a scholar being drawn toward his subject by an attractive but intellectually compromising external pull — by the lure of his age's causes, such as a revolutionary, religious, or patriotic passion. The verb is carefully paired with driven: driven implies force, while enticed implies seduction, and together they describe the two dishonorable ways in which a historian might take up a subject — pushed from behind or pulled from ahead. Gibbon, the passage insists, was subject to neither: he came to his subject freely, from within. The word's tone in this context is therefore quietly contemptuous of the enticed historian and admiring of Gibbon; it suggests that to be enticed is to surrender one's intellectual independence to something pleasurable or flattering, and that the resulting scholarship is subtly compromised, written to satisfy the passion rather than the truth. The word's senses spread across the moral scale.
  • Sense 1 — primary: to attract or draw in by holding out pleasure or advantage. The standard modern sense. An enticing offer, an enticing prospect, enticed by a high salary — the attraction is tangible and openly offered.
  • Sense 2 — historical: to incite, instigate, provoke. The older French-inherited sense of the word, alive in Middle English from about 1300, meant to stir someone up, to provoke or incite them, often to wrongdoing. This sense survives only in historical and legal contexts; the modern word has shifted decisively toward attraction.
  • Sense 3 — adjectival use. Enticed and, more commonly, enticing work as adjectives: an enticing offer, an enticing aroma, the enticing prospect of travel. Enticing means alluring, seductively attractive; enticed means having been drawn in by such attraction.
  • Sense 4 — noun: enticement. The act of enticing or the thing that entices: enticement to crime, an enticement to desert, the enticements of the city. In older law, enticement named the act of leading a person astray, especially a servant from service.
  • Sense 5 — figurative and intellectual. To be enticed by an idea, a cause, a movement: the enticed scholar who surrenders independence to something flattering, as in the Gibbon passage. The word here describes a seduction of the judgment by pleasure or vanity.
  • Sense 6 — commercial and modern. In advertising and marketing the word is mildly positive and neutral: enticing deals, enticing packaging, an enticing offer. The sharp moral edge of "tempt" and "seduce" has been worn smooth, so that entice now often simply means attract.
The semantic bridge is the image of fire. The word descends from a Latin noun for a firebrand, titio, and the old verb meant literally to set fire to or to kindle; to "stir up" a person's passions came to mean to awaken their desires, and to awaken desire is to attract. The aggressive, instigating sense of the verb faded through late Middle English, and the modern sense — to allure by holding out pleasure or advantage — took its place, while the noun enticement and the adjective enticing registered the same softening. The fire is still just under the surface: to be enticed is to be inflamed toward something. In usage the word is collocated with by (enticed by the offer), into (enticed into the shop, enticed into wrongdoing), and toward (enticed toward a cause); the participle enticed is typically passive and describes a person already drawn in. Its register is polished and slightly literary, comfortable in both narrative prose and advertising, and its distinctive value is that it sits between the gentler allure and the heavier tempt — an attraction that offers a real advantage without yet having corrupted its victim.
