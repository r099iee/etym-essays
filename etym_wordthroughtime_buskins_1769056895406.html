- Mid-16th c. - the word arrives in English (from French) as a working dress-word: a calf-high soft-leather boot worn by hunters and travellers, a plain item of practical clothing recorded from 1560
- 1590s - the theatrical turn: "the buskin" is adopted as the English name for the high soft boot worn by Greek tragic actors, and by metonymy it comes to stand for tragedy itself — a shoemaker's word lifted by the stage into high poetic territory
- 17th–19th c. - "sock and buskin" (comedy and tragedy) hardens into a fixed phrase of criticism, and "to assume the buskin" means to write or act tragedy; the everyday boot-sense survives quietly while the theatrical sense dominates learned usage
- Contemporary - the word recedes to rare, bookish use, replaced in ordinary speech by "boot" or "ankle-boot"; in modern prose it reads as deliberately archaic and theatrical, carrying a fairy-tale, Renaissance-stage glamour and, for the learned ear, the memory of Athens
