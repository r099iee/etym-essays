Meshed is the past tense and past participle of the verb to mesh: to interlace, to catch in a net, to entangle, or — in the most famous modern sense — to engage, as gear teeth or cog wheels lock together and turn as one. The word's concrete root image is the net: a mesh is the open eye between the threads of a net or screen, the little space that both holds and lets pass. Anything meshed is caught up, woven in, or locked together with something else — and that single image of interlacing has powered two very different careers, one in nets and textiles, one in machinery and abstraction. In the dwarves' song — “in twisted wire they meshed the light of moon and sun” — the participle is used at its most concrete and its most magical at once. The dwarven smiths do not merely place jewels beside wire; they net light itself into the wire, interlacing moon and sun into a fabric of twisted metal. The mesh is the open eye of the net through which radiance is both caught and held; the participle makes light behave like thread or prey, captive and interlaced. Tone: high poetic and craftsmanly — magical metallurgy raised to cosmology, sun and moon becoming cloth in dwarven hands. Primary senses of meshed / mesh
  • Caught in, or made like, a net — “finely meshed nylon”; “a net meshed with tiny openings”; “fish meshed in the drift-net.”
  • Interwoven, interlaced, entangled — threads, wires, or filaments meshed together; hair meshed in a comb; vines meshed in a fence.
  • Engaged, of machinery — gear teeth meshed so that one wheel drives another; “the cogs meshed smoothly and the clock began to tick.”
  • Harmonious or compatible, of people, plans, or systems — “our schedules meshed”; “the two departments meshed perfectly.”
Polysemy and secondary modern meanings
  • Engineering: meshing gears, meshing teeth, and “meshing” of chains and sprockets — the mechanical sense developed in the late 18th and 19th centuries as machinery spread; to mesh is to interlock so that motion transmits.
  • Networking and computing: “mesh networks” in which every node links to several others, and “mesh topology” in data transmission — the net-image revived for modern communication, each device a knot in a web.
  • Teamwork and psychology: personalities, skills, or working styles that “mesh” fit together and cooperate; the mechanical sense becomes interpersonal harmony, losing the net but keeping the interlacing.
  • Textiles and screens: “mesh fabric,” “wire mesh,” “mesh screen” — the open-work textile and grille senses, where the mesh is the regular pattern of holes itself.
  • Mathematics and topology: a “mesh” is a subdivision or grid; “mesh size” measures the fineness of screens and sieves.
  • Wrestling and combat slang: to be “meshed” with an opponent is to be locked in grappling engagement — the entanglement sense at its most physical.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge of the senses The whole polysemy hangs from the Germanic noun “mesh,” the open space between the threads of a net. In the ancient world a net was a grid of knots and openings; each opening was a mesh, and to mesh was to weave or catch. When industrial machinery arrived in the 18th–19th centuries, engineers reached for the same image: gear teeth interlock like the threads of a net, each tooth a knot engaging its neighbour. From machinery the image abstracted further — people, plans, and systems “mesh” when they interlock and work as one — and in the computer age the net-image returned as the literal architecture of mesh networks. The bridge is always the same: interlacing of parts into a working whole, whether the parts are thread, wire, steel teeth, or human schedules. How meshed differs from its near-synonyms
  • Woven / interwoven — orderly textile construction with warp and weft; stresses the craft of making fabric, where meshed stresses the open net-structure and the locking of parts.
  • Entangled — caught in a snarl, usually unwanted; where meshed can be deliberate or benign, entangled is disorder and confinement.
  • Enmeshed — caught in a net or trap; the negative, trapping cousin of meshed, which can also mean a clean mechanical engagement.
  • Interlaced — decorative braiding or weaving together; prettier and looser than the functional locking of meshed.
  • Knit — loops formed by needles; a different craft and a tighter, sweater-like texture than open mesh.
  • Locked — fixed and immovable; stresses the prevention of motion, where meshed (of gears) means connected so that motion transmits.
  • Fused — melted or bonded into one; a permanent union, where meshed parts remain distinct but interlaced.
In the card's context The line uses MESHED as the past participle in the song's catalogue of lost craft — stars strung, dragon-fire hung, light meshed in twisted wire. The open-loop geometry of the net — each mesh an eye through which light both passes and is held — is exactly what makes the verb right for trapping moonlight and sunlight in metalwork. Keep the net in mind: a mesh is an opening, and to mesh is to weave, catch, or lock — from fish-nets to clockwork, from light in wire to teams that work as one.
