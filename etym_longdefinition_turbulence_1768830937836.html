turbulence — violent, irregular, unsteady motion, especially of air or other fluids; a state of agitation in which orderly movement breaks down into churning, swirling disorder. In the sentence “A voice deep and harsh issued from the turbulence,” the word is the substance of the summoned cloud itself: the turbulence is what the wizard’s Call has made of the air — a seething, boiling mass of conflicting currents — and the voice does not command it but issues from it, as though the chaos itself were speaking. The literary tone is scientific folded into the supernatural: turbulence is the precise modern word for eddying airflow, the technical term of meteorology and aerodynamics studied since Lord Kelvin and Osborne Reynolds, and Vance’s choice imports all that impersonal physical violence into a scene of magic, so that the demonic voice sounds like the voice of a storm system given throat. It carries none of the moral weight of human disorder; this is the rioting of the elements, a mob made of wind.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. The word descends from Latin turba, “turmoil, disorder,” which itself was borrowed from Greek týrbē, “turmoil, confusion” — and Latin also used turba concretely for “a crowd, a mob, the rabble.” The semantic bridge is fascinating: in Latin the same word named both the churning of confused motion and the churning of a disordered crowd, and from that Latin base came the adjective turbulentus, “storm-tossed, restless,” applied to seas, winds, and rebellious peoples alike. The modern English word thus carries a double inheritance — the physics of churning fluids and the memory of a mob — though the human sense has now drained away, leaving the physics dominant.

Primary senses.
  • Violent, irregular, chaotic motion in a fluid or gas; eddying flow in which a medium abandons its smooth (laminar) calm (the dominant modern, scientific sense).
  • Rough, bumpy air experienced by aircraft — the everyday aviation sense (we hit severe turbulence).
  • Agitation or disturbance in water or air of any scale, from a whirlpool to a storm system.
  • By extension, violent commotion in general — though this older sense is now largely displaced by the scientific one.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings.
  • Meteorology and fluid dynamics: turbulence is the technical term for chaotic, eddying flow; the transition between laminar and turbulent flow is described by the dimensionless Reynolds number (after Osborne Reynolds, 1883). This is the word’s modern home.
  • Aviation: clear-air turbulence, turbulence encountered in flight — the impersonal jolting of air masses.
  • Politics: “political turbulence” — social or political upheaval, unrest, instability (the old crowd-sense revived metaphorically).
  • Finance and economics: “market turbulence,” “economic turbulence” — volatile, disorderly fluctuation in prices and markets.
  • Emotional / psychological: internal turbulence — agitation, unrest, turmoil of feeling.
  • Corporate / organizational jargon: turbulence as disruption and rapid change in a business environment.
  • Cosmology / astrophysics: turbulence in stellar and interstellar gas — the chaotic mixing of cosmic material.
  • Technical adjective set: turbulent, turbulently, turbidity (the cloudiness of a liquid, a related but distinct word from Latin turbidus), and the unrelated but confusingly similar turbine (from Latin turbō, “a whirlwind; a spinning top”).

How turbulence differs from its neighbours.
  • Turmoil — human and interior, the churn of emotion or a crowd’s agitation; turbulence is the impersonal rioting of air, water, or any fluid.
  • Chaos — cosmic formlessness, the absence of all order; turbulence is ordered motion breaking down into churning eddies.
  • Commotion — mere bustle and noise; turbulence is violent, swirling, physical motion without a voice.
  • Uproar — loud public outcry made by voices; turbulence carries no moral or vocal content.
  • Disturbance — mild and external; turbulence is violent and internal to the medium.

In Vance’s dying world the word performs its double office: the turbulence is both the physics of the churning cloud and the mouth of the storm — the chaos itself speaking.
