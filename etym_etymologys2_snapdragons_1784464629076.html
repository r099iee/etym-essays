[ROOT 1 — SNAP] Onomatopoeic Germanic formation (sound-imitative; no securely reconstructable PIE root — the word imitates the sound of a sudden bite or crack) → Middle Low German / Middle Dutch snappen "to snap, to seize, to bite suddenly" (13th–15th c.) → Middle English snappen "to bite suddenly, to snatch" (attested 14th c., e.g. Chaucer) → Modern English snap "to break or bite with a sharp crack; sudden catch or click" → the descriptive snap- element of the plant name → SNAPdragons [ROOT 2 — DRAGON] Proto-Indo-European *derḱ- "to see, to perceive" (the root of Greek dérkomai "to see, to look with a keen eye"; the traditional but debated source of the dragon-word) → Greek δράκων drákōn "serpent, dragon, huge snake" (Homer, 8th c. BCE) — traditionally explained as "the seeing / staring one", the derivation from dérkomai being generally accepted but not fully secure → Latin dracō, dracōnis "serpent, dragon" (classical, e.g. Pliny; also the Vulgate serpent of Revelation) → Old French dragon (from Latin accusative dracōnem) → Middle English dragoun / dragon (c. 1200; the Old English form draca "dragon" was a separate earlier direct loan from Latin) → Modern English dragon → the dragon- element of the plant name → SNAPdragons [COMPOUND] snap + dragon → snapdragon "the flower Antirrhinum, whose two-lipped corolla snaps open like a dragon's mouth" (plant name attested 16th c.) → plural SNAPDRAGONS (in Tolkien, also the shapes of fireworks)
