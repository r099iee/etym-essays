• Freshly cut / fresh-cut: Similar because both mean cut recently; differs because fresh-cut is plain modern English, while new-hewn keeps an archaic, monumental register and implies shaping, not just cutting.
  • Newly quarried: Similar because both describe stone taken from the earth recently; differs because newly quarried names the extraction site and method precisely, while new-hewn adds the dressing (cutting to shape) and is used of timber as well.
  • Rough-hewn: Similar because both use hewn of stone or timber shaped by cutting; differs because rough-hewn emphasizes crudeness and unfinished coarseness, while new-hewn emphasizes freshness and recent workmanship — the objects need not be rough.
  • Dressed (stone): Similar because both denote stone cut to shape and surface; differs because dressed is the professional mason's term for finished facing work, neutral and technical, while new-hewn adds temporal newness and a literary tone.
  • Carved: Similar because both describe shaping by cutting; differs because carving implies artistic or ornamental removal of material (statues, reliefs), while hewing is broad, workmanlike cutting for construction.
  • Chiselled: Similar because both describe cutting and shaping with a bladed tool; differs because chiselled specifies the fine tool and precise finish, while new-hewn points to axe- or adze-scale work and to newness.
  • Wrought / new-wrought: Similar because both are archaic participles meaning shaped or made (work → wrought); differs because wrought covers any making, forging or manufacture, while hewn is specific to cutting with a blade.
  • Felled: Similar because both can describe timber just cut down; differs because felled applies only to trees — the act of dropping them — while hewn covers cutting and shaping stone as well, and new-hewn stresses the whole recent process.
  • Green (timber): Similar because both describe freshly cut wood; differs because green is the carpenter's term for unseasoned, still-wet timber regardless of when it was cut, while new-hewn is about the recency of the cutting itself.
  • Unweathered: Similar because both describe material that time has not yet altered; differs because unweathered is a negative term about the absence of erosion, while new-hewn is positive about the recency of the work.
  • Raw: Similar because both suggest material in a state untouched by time; differs because raw means unprocessed in any way, while new-hewn has already been processed — cut and shaped — only very recently.
  • Newly built / new-made: Similar because both are new + participle compounds stressing recency of construction; differs because built and made cover any method of construction, while hewn restricts the image to cutting and shaping with blades, the oldest building craft.
