The biography of propitious begins with a wingbeat: the PIE root *pet-, "to fly, to rush, to fall," a root whose children everywhere kept motion in the air. In Indic, Sanskrit patati means "he flies, he falls" — the root balancing flight and gravity at once — and patram, "wing, feather, leaf," names the thing that falls from the tree like a bird alighting; modern Hindi preserves the pair in par ("wing, feather") and parnā ("to fall"). Greek flew with it: petomai, "I fly," and pteron, "wing," which modern science has embedded in helicopter and pterodactyl. The Germanic branch gave Old English fether — English feather — a word still visibly winged.
  • The Latin hardening (petere): the branch that matters here grew in Latin, where *pet- hardened into petere: "to fly at, to attack, to strive toward, to seek." Latin filled its legal and political vocabulary from this verb — petitio, a seeking (English petition); appetere, to reach for (English appetite); competere, to strive together (English compete); repetere, to seek again (English repeat); perpetuus, "pursued without interruption" (English perpetual); even impetus, the rush upon a thing, and petulant, the importunate seeker.
  • From striving to favor (propitius): from this striving root Roman religion drew the adjective propitius, "favorable, gracious, well-disposed." The traditional analysis is pro- + petere, "flying or rushing forward": an omen, a deity, or fortune itself that rushes out to meet the worshiper — favor pictured as approach. Some modern scholars prefer a link with prope, "near" (a god who draws close), and the question remains open, but in either reading the word describes a power that approaches in goodwill rather than standing aloof. Propitius appears already in Plautus and Lucretius in ritual and religious contexts: the Romans prayed that the gods be propitii, well-disposed.
  • The Christian turn (propitiate, propitiation): Late Latin and the Church carried propitius into theology. Propitiare, "to make favorable," gave propitiatio, "the appeasing of wrath," and the Vulgate's propitiatorium named the "mercy seat" of the Temple. English took propitius itself through Old French propicieux in the 15th century (Middle English propicious), adjusted the spelling to propitious on the Latin model in the 16th century, and added propitiate, propitiation, and propitiatory — the vocabulary of sacrifice as the act that turns an offended God back toward goodwill. This theological density made the word, for centuries, the property of prayer and scripture.
  • Secularization (18th–19th centuries): from the 18th century propitious broadened from divinity to circumstance: a propitious moment, a propitious wind, a propitious season. The divine agent receded and the word approached "opportune" while keeping its shade of graciousness — a moment that seems to smile. It remained (and remains) formal, dignified, faintly dated in speech.
  • The literary effect: in the passage — "If fortune were propitious, their years of somewhat unwilling exile on the red planet would soon be at an end" — the whole history is present. Fortune is the Roman Fortuna personified, a power with a will; propitious names that will as favor actively flying forward to meet the exiles; and the conditional "if" turns the sentence into a prayer, hopeful and wary at once. The word's three-thousand-year journey from a root meaning "to fly" to a name for fortune's goodwill is complete: what flies toward you is what favors you.
The sound shifts along the way are modest — Latin propitius (pro-PIH-tee-us) became Old French propicieux, Middle English propicious, and modern English propitious (pro-PISH-us), with the cluster simplified and the stress settling on the second syllable — but the semantic shifts are profound: from flight, to pursuit, to prayer, to the favor of gods, and finally to the luck of circumstances. Every stage is attested, and the word still carries its augural scent: a propitious moment is one at which fortune seems, briefly, to be flying forward to meet your hopes.
