Armenian is an English adjective and noun naming the language, people, and culture of Armenia and the Armenian diaspora. It is also the conventional English name of an independent branch of the Indo-European family, spoken by perhaps six to seven million people worldwide. The word is an exonym: Armenians call themselves hay (plural hayer) and their country Hayastan. The English form comes through Latin and Greek from an Old Persian name, and its history is therefore separate from the history of the Armenian self-designation.

Primary sense: the language. Armenian is the official language of the Republic of Armenia and is also spoken in Nagorno-Karabakh, Georgia, Iran, Lebanon, Syria, Turkey, Russia, the United States, France, and many other countries. Modern Armenian has two standardized written forms: Eastern Armenian, based on the dialect of the Ararat plain and used in Armenia and Iran, and Western Armenian, based on the Constantinople dialect and used by much of the diaspora. The two are only partly mutually intelligible. The language has its own alphabet, invented by Mesrop Mashtots around 405 CE, and a continuous literary tradition from the fifth century. It is written left to right with thirty-eight letters in the modern orthography (thirty-nine in some traditional counts).

Secondary senses: people and adjective. As a noun, an Armenian is a member of the Armenian people or a citizen of Armenia; the Armenians are the ethnic group. The adjective covers nationality, language, church, alphabet, cuisine, dance, and history: Armenian literature, the Armenian Apostolic Church, Armenian brandy, Armenian rugs, Armenian Genocide. In the diaspora, Armenian often functions as an identity marker independent of citizenship, so that an American or French citizen may describe themselves as Armenian. The word can also refer to the Armenian diaspora as a social and political network.

Tertiary and technical senses. In linguistics, Armenian is a single branch of Indo-European, with no close living relatives; the Graeco-Armenian hypothesis posits a special relationship with Greek, but it remains debated. The language has a rich system of seven cases, no grammatical gender, and a distinctive consonant shift. In art history, Armenian describes illuminated manuscripts, stone crosses (khachkars), and church architecture. In commerce and craft, Armenian bole is a reddish clay used as a ground in gilding, and Armenian stone is a blue pigment (lapis armenus). Armenian cucumber is a variety of melon eaten as a vegetable. These technical uses are unrelated to the ethnic name except through trade and geography.

Modern usage and semantic bridge. The semantic bridge from a geographical and ethnic label to a language name is typical of ancient ethnonyms. Old Persian Armina named a satrapy; Greek Armenioi named its people; Latin Armenius and Old French armenien transmitted the adjective to English. The endonym hay has a separate and disputed origin, possibly connected with the Hittite name Ḫatti, the kingdom of Hayasa, or a Proto-Indo-European word for "lord". Because the exonym and endonym diverged so long ago, Armenian in English is not felt as a foreign imposition; it is simply the standard name. In modern political discourse, Armenian is inseparable from the memory of the 1915 genocide and from debates over its recognition.

  • Language - an independent Indo-European branch, with Eastern and Western standards.
  • Person - a member of the Armenian people or a citizen of Armenia.
  • Adjective - of Armenia, its people, church, alphabet, or diaspora.
  • Technical - manuscript art, khachkars, Armenian bole and stone, Armenian cucumber.
  • Political - tied to the 1915 genocide, diaspora identity, and Nagorno-Karabakh.
