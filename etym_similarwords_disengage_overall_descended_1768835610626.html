• detach (for disengage): Similar in separating one thing from another; differs because detach is purely physical and neutral, without pledge-imagery, while disengage is the negation of engage - the release from a bond, obligation, or battle-line.
  • release (for disengage): Similar in freeing something held; differs because release implies letting go of what is grasped, while disengage suggests withdrawing from a formal engagement - unfastening, breaking contact, throwing a machine out of gear.
  • disentangle (for disengage): Similar in freeing from a complication; differs because disentangle implies unravelling a snarl or complexity, while disengage is the cleaner act of withdrawing from a fixed engagement.
  • withdraw (for disengage): Similar in removing oneself from a situation; differs because withdraw is the general word for pulling back, while disengage emphasizes undoing an existing bond or engagement.
  • separate (for disengage): Similar in parting connected things; differs because separate is broad and colourless, while disengage carries the formal drama of the broken pledge.
  • coverall (for overall): Similar in being a one-piece protective garment worn over one's clothes; differs because coverall is the American industrial term, while overall (British singular) is the everyday, slightly humble word for the same garment.
  • smock (for overall): Similar in being a loose outer garment; differs because a smock is a light artist's or farmer's over-garment, while the overall is the heavier, protective work garment that covers the whole body.
  • uniform (for overall): Similar in being a garment that covers and identifies its wearer; differs because uniform is institutional and outward-looking, a badge of rank and corps, while the overall is private, utilitarian, and inward - it protects rather than displays.
  • wholly (for overall, adverb): Similar in meaning completely; differs because wholly is a bare adverb of completeness, while overall (adverb) adds the summarizing, final-judgement sense - "taken as a whole".
  • dropped (for descended): Similar in moving downward; differs because dropped is quick and brief, while descended preserves a graduated, orderly, step-by-step movement - from Latin scandere "to climb", the descent is climbing performed in reverse.
  • fell (for descended): Similar in coming down; differs because fell implies gravity and loss of control, while descended is a controlled, graded descent - of stairs, of paths, of a garment settling to the ankles.
  • sank (for descended): Similar in moving down; differs because sank implies submersion in a fluid or yielding medium, while descended is a general downward progress, literal or genealogical.
  • sloped (for descended): Similar in declining in level; differs because sloped describes a static gradient, while descended describes movement along a downward course.
