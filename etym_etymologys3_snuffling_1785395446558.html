Snuffling belongs to one of the most prolific and most ancient word-families in Germanic: the great nasal company of sniff, snuff, snout, snot, snivel, snore, and snort, all built on the imitative base *snu-, which transcribes the wet, congested sound of drawing air through the nose. No Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed for this base; from its very beginnings the group appears onomatopoeic, each branch language re-inventing the noise for itself. The same sound-symbolic pattern rings across the whole Germanic world — Middle Dutch snuffen, Middle Low German snūven, German schnauben and schnaufen, Danish snuse, Swedish snusa — and the modern German verb schnüffeln, "to sniff, to snoop", is a near-twin of English snuffle, having arrived at the same combination of nasal base and frequentative suffix all on its own. In England the base surfaced in snoute, the projecting muzzle of an animal, which gave snout, and in Old English gesnot, the mucus itself, our snot. The entire family is a monument to onomatopoeia: the sound of the nose made speech.

The English line runs straight from Middle English snuffen, "to draw air in through the nose" — the sense behind snuff-taking, when gentlemen inhaled powdered tobacco through the nostrils, and behind the transferred phrase snuff out, to choke out a flame as a candle is smothered. In the sixteenth century English did its favourite trick with nasal verbs: it appended the frequentative suffix -le — the same suffix that turns waddle, crackle, and wrestle into their repeated, drawn-out selves — producing snuffle, a verb that no longer describes a single inhalation but a whole series of small, wet, laborious ones. The result is a word of double life: from the sixteenth century on, snuffle means both "to breathe noisily through a congested nose" (the source of the colloquial the snuffles, the head cold) and "to smell about, to nose out a scent" (the sense of the tracking hound). Both senses grow out of the same physical reality — the wet, repeated, investigative drawing-in of air — and the word has never lost either of them.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries a third, social sense appeared: to speak in a nasal, affected, or whining tone, "a snuffling voice". The word became a weapon of satire, used of preachers and pious talk, of anyone whose nasal delivery suggested sanctimony or complaint, so that snuffling acquired a mocking, faintly contemptuous register it still carries when applied to human speech. At the same time the animal sense deepened its association with hunting by scent: hounds snuffle along the ground as they work a trail, and the wet, laborious, investigative sound became, in hunting literature and in fiction, the very signature of pursuit — the noise of a predator that is patient because it knows it will succeed.

The sound changes in the family are minor but telling. The group's words all begin with the sibilant-nasal cluster sn-, and that cluster is itself the onomatopoeic core: it forces the speaker's own nose to participate in the word, the air passing through the nasal passage as it is pronounced. Within the cluster the final consonants vary — -f in sniff and snuff, -t in snout and snot, -r in snore and snort, -v in snivel — and each variation is a slightly different flavour of the same nasal noise. The frequentative -le of snuffle is the crucial morphological event in the word's history: it converts a single action into a series, and it is exactly this serial, iterative quality that Tolkien exploits in The Hobbit (1937), when the Wargs, having set guards at the foot of the tree, "went snuffling about till they had smelt out every tree that had anyone in it". The present participle keeps the action open and ongoing; the reader hears the wet, patient, predatory sniffing working its way through the night, tree by tree, and the closeness of the noise is precisely what makes Bilbo's hiding feel so hopeless.

  • Chronology: imitative base *snu- (no date, expressive); Middle English snuffen (14th–15th c.); English snuff (inhale); English snuffle (16th c., frequentative); SNUFFLING (present participle; senses 1 and 2 from the 16th c., the social "nasal speech" sense from the 18th c.).
  • Sound and morphology: the sibilant-nasal cluster sn- as the onomatopoeic core; the frequentative -le marking repeated action; parallel formations (Dutch snuffelen, German schnüffeln) showing the same base and suffix arrived at independently.
  • Semantic drift: congested nasal breathing → animal scenting and pursuit → nasal, affected human speech (satirical register); the noun the snuffles as a homely word for the common cold; transferred uses of damp winds and quenched candles.
  • Socio-cultural context: snuff-taking culture of the 17th–18th c.; hunting and hound-lore; religious satire of "snuffling" preachers; modern fiction's use of the word for predatory menace.
  • Literary attestations: Middle English snuffen; the satirists of the 18th c. on "snuffling" voices; Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937), the Wargs' snuffling search from tree to tree.
