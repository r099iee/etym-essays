smouldering is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb to smoulder. Literally, it describes a fire that burns slowly with smoke but no flame — a fire kept alive deep inside a mass of fuel, glowing and smoking without breaking into a visible blaze. In the source context, the dragon Smaug is so consumed by fury that "a thousand years" would turn him to "smouldering stone": the word paints an image of a being reduced to a smoking, slowly burning rock, still hot with hatred inside.

  • Primary sense — slow, flameless combustion: A smouldering fire smokes, glows, and holds heat in its core while its surface stays dark; it can last far longer than a blazing fire and can burst into flame later when fanned. Embers, damp timber, peat, and buried coal all smoulder.
  • Secondary sense — burning from within (of objects): Something "smouldering" is consumed slowly and destructively from inside — a smouldering mattress, a smouldering ruin, smouldering wreckage. This is the sense used when fire crews speak of a blaze that is out of sight but still alive.
  • Tertiary sense — figurative emotion: Of feelings: anger, hatred, resentment, jealousy, or desire that burns fiercely within but is kept below the surface. A "smouldering" grievance is one that has not yet erupted into open conflict; a "smouldering" feud may explode at any time.
  • Romantic sense — suppressed passion: "Smouldering" looks, eyes, and glances express intense, barely contained desire or attraction; the word is a staple of romance and melodrama, implying dangerous, banked-up heat.
  • Political and historical sense: "Smouldering" conflicts, rebellions, and discontents are dormant but not dead — unresolved tensions that burn quietly until a spark sets them alight.
  • Register and connotation: Vividly atmospheric and always suggestive of containment and latent power. Whether of fire or feeling, a smouldering thing is dangerous precisely because it looks quiet but is not extinguished.
  • Grammar and spelling: Smouldering is the British spelling; smoldering (single u) is American. The verb is intransitive (the fire smoulders); the participle acts as a verb form (is smouldering) and as an adjective (smouldering ruins).

The historical bridge: The earliest English sense of the verb smolder was not fire at all but suffocation: it meant "to stifle, choke, smother", and it is a close cousin of the verb smother itself. By the sixteenth century the word had migrated to the fire that is smothered — one that burns without air or flame — and from that literal image of heat trapped and hidden beneath a covering, the figurative senses of suppressed anger, resentment, and desire all naturally grew. A smouldering emotion is, quite literally, a fire being smothered: alive, hot, and waiting.

Sense in context: In the source passage the word is doing double work: Smaug's own internal fury is a smouldering rage, and the image of him turned "to smouldering stone" collapses the literal and figurative senses together — a stone that burns slowly with smoke is the perfect emblem of an immortal, implacable hatred that will never cool and never forgive. Across all its uses, from fire reports to romance novels, smouldering names the state of burning while appearing not to — the smouldering thing is never finished, never safe, and always on the edge of ignition.
