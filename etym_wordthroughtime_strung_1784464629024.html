The verb “string,” with its strong past “strung,” has meant to thread or arrange on a cord since old English — beads, gems, and later pearls threaded into necklaces.
“Strung” in a parallel sense stretched cords tight — stringing a bow for the hunt or war, and later a racket — keeping the word in the language of tension and craft.
“Strung” in the 19th century gave English “highly strung” for a nervous, easily agitated temperament, borrowing the image of an over-tight string.
“Strung” in 20th-century slang “strung out” described someone exhausted or (originally) rendered useless by drugs, while the necklace sense remained — threading light itself in Tolkien’s “On silver necklaces they strung the flowering stars.”
