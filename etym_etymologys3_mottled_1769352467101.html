The history of mottled is unusual because its earliest stage is genuinely uncertain — and honesty about that uncertainty is part of the word's story. The adjective descends from motley, the name of the parti-colored, patchwork garment worn by medieval court fools and jesters, recorded in Middle English from the late 14th century (motle(y)). Motley itself comes from an Anglo-French word of the same meaning, but beyond that the trail goes cold: no secure Indo-European root can be reconstructed. Scholars have proposed connections to Old English mot "speck, mote" (a word meaning a particle of dust, hence "speckled" in its core idea), and to a Frankish or Gaulish base meaning "clod" or "patch" (compare the motte of motte-and-bailey castles, an artificial earth mound), but all of these remain speculative. The safest statement is that of the OED: origin obscure, probably of Anglo-French formation meaning "parti-colored." What is certain is the cultural career of the word in England. In late medieval and Tudor society, motley was the uniform of licensed folly: the jester's coat sewn together from contrasting patches of cloth, a deliberate symbol of the fool's marginal, truth-telling position at court. By Shakespeare's time the phrase "to wear motley" was a fixed idiom meaning to play the jester, and the garment itself was famous enough to generate a new verb: around 1670 English speakers back-formed to mottle, "to mark with spots or blotches like motley cloth," and from that verb the participle adjective mottled appears in the 1670s-1700s meaning "marked with irregular patches of different colors." From its theatrical cradle the word escapes into nature writing. In the 18th and 19th centuries mottled becomes a standard descriptive term of naturalists, painters, and physicians:
  • Natural history: mottled animal coats and plumage, mottled bark and stone, mottled eggs — the workhorse word for organic, weathered, irregular variegation.
  • Painting and landscape: mottled light and shade, the patchy play of light through a canopy.
  • Medicine: mottled or blotched skin, discolored by cold, age, rash, or poor circulation.
The 20th century adds further technical domains: metallurgy uses "mottled cast iron" for iron whose fractured surface shows mottled gray-and-white regions; medicine applies "mottled skin" (livedo reticularis) to the blotchy marbled discoloration of circulatory failure; and zoology formalizes whole species as "mottled" (mottled owl, mottled sculpin, mottled duck), where the word describes the camouflage strategy of the animal. In Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men the word appears at the height of this tradition — "the mottled, white, recumbent limbs" of the sycamores over the Salinas pool — using the naturalist's word for the peeling, patchy bark of the plane tree. The phonetic journey is minimal (motteley to motley to mottle to mottled), because this is a domestic English development, not a borrowed sound shift; the semantic journey is the drama. What began as a fool's costume has become the neutral language of camouflage and cortex — a single image of mixed patches, stitched together once by a medieval tailor and re-woven forever after by nature writers.
