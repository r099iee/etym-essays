Linear derivation chain for DOMINIONS (single root word): 1. PIE *dem- / *domo- "house", nominal *dom / *domos (root glossed "to build"; attested cognates: Sanskrit dama "house", Greek domos "house", Slavic dom "house", Armenian tun). c. 4000-3000 BCE. 2. Proto-Italic *domos "house, household" (reconstructed). 3. Latin domus "house, home, household" (attested from earliest Latin literature, e.g., Plautus, 3rd-2nd century BCE). 4. Latin dominus "master of the house, lord, owner, ruler" (derived from domus with the agentive suffix; attested from the 2nd century BCE). 5. Latin dominium "ownership, property, the master's right; lordship" (classical legal term; Roman jurists use it for full ownership as distinct from mere possession). 6. Old French dominion "rule, lordship, territory" / Medieval Latin dominium (transmitted to English; the same Latin family also produced English domain and demesne as sibling loans, which are NOT ancestors of dominion). 7. Middle English dominion "rule, lordship, sovereignty" (attested c. 1450); the territorial sense develops in the 16th century. 8. English plural DOMINIONS "territories under a single government or ruler" (the present root word, as in Gibbon). Note: Roman dominium originally meant the owner's right, not the land itself; the shift to "the lands ruled" is a 16th-century political extension of the proprietary metaphor. English domain, demesne, and the theological Dominions (from Latin dominationes) are sisters of the same family, not ancestors.
