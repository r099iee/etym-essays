- 14th to 17th Century - The noun barge designated a broad, flat-bottomed vessel for cargo transport or stately royal pageantry along the Thames and European waterways.
- 18th to Mid-19th Century - The verbal form barging emerged among canal and river sailors to describe navigating, hauling, or piloting heavy freight barges through locks and crowded riverports.
- Late 19th Century (c. 1880s) - Nautical slang developed the figurative sense of a vessel colliding heavily with another boat or wharf, which quickly spread to colloquial speech describing clumsy, rough physical movement.
- Early 20th Century (c. 1900s-1920s) - The phrasal verb barge in / barging in crystallized in British and American English (prominently in P.G. Wodehouse, school stories, and wartime slang) to describe rude, unannounced social and physical intrusions.
- Mid-20th to 21st Century - Cemented universally across global English as the standard expressive idiom for entering without knocking, interrupting delicate conversations, or pushing roughly through crowds.
