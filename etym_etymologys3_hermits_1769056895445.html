Hermit is a biography of the 'desert-dweller,' and it begins in Greek. The noun érēmos, 'lonely, desolate, waste,' is one of those ancient Greek words whose deeper Indo-European past is genuinely uncertain — Pokorny linked it to a root *erē- meaning 'loose, separated,' the state of being set apart, but Beekes cautions that the reconstruction is unclear, so the secure history starts with the Greek word itself. In the Greek world érēmos named the empty, uninhabited places — the wilderness beyond the town wall — and from it Greek built a small family: the abstract erēmía, 'solitude, emptiness,' and the agent noun erēmítēs, 'one who lives in the desert.' The biblical turn. The word's enormous career was set by a translation. When the translators of the Septuagint rendered the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, érēmos was the word they chose for midbār, the wilderness of the Exodus — the desert in which Israel wandered and through which, centuries later, the desert fathers would seek God. This textual accident gave the word its religious destiny: the desert was no longer just empty land; it was the appointed place of trial, hunger, and encounter with God.
  • The Septuagint's érēmos fixed the wilderness as a spiritual category in the Christian world: the place of purification, temptation, and revelation.
  • From the third century CE onward, Christian ascetics withdrew from the cities of the Roman East to the Egyptian desert — Anthony the Great and the fathers of the Thebaid — and the Greek word for their situation hardened into a title: erēmítēs, the desert-dweller.
  • The pattern spread from Egypt to Syria, Palestine, and then across Europe, so that the hermit became a standard figure of medieval Christianity — the solitary as specialist in God.
The word's journey west. Late Latin borrowed the word as erēmīta, frequently spelled heremīta under the influence of heremus 'desert' (itself a borrowing of érēmos), and that aspirated spelling would eventually give English its silent h. The word travelled west with monasticism: Old French ermite and hermite, Middle English ermite, and by the sixteenth century English hermit, with the h- deliberately restored to match the Latinate heremita. The dwelling was named alongside the person — Old French ermitage gave English hermitage — and the modern languages all borrowed the same Latin form directly: Italian eremita, Spanish eremita, Portuguese eremita, German Eremit. Semantic drift. The meaning broadened steadily. In the early centuries a hermit was precisely a desert-dweller; as monasticism spread through the forests and mountains of northern Europe, where there was no desert, the word came to mean any solitary who chose a wild place — a cave, a cell on a crag, a hut in a valley. In chivalric romance and saint's legend the hermit became the storybook figure who shelters and counsels the wandering knight, isolation read as training rather than punishment. In modern use the religious force fades and 'hermit' becomes a playful general word for chosen retreat from society — the hermit-crab (1770, the shellfish that borrows a shell), 'to live like a hermit,' 'gone all hermit.' Yet the word never loses its flavour of disciplined solitude, and that double register — austere religious recluse and wise storybook survivor — is exactly what Peter invokes when he reassures the others that hermits have always managed to live in forests.
