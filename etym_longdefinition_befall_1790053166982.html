befall is a verb meaning to happen to someone, especially by chance or fate, typically of something unfortunate, momentous, or ordained — what befalls you comes upon you rather than being chosen by you. Its core construction is impersonal and slightly archaic: disaster befell the city, tragedy befell them, what befell him is unknown. In the source sentence — If such __________ you, it will be due purely to circumstance — befall figures misfortune as falling from above onto the sufferer: such, some calamity previously named, may fall upon you, and if it does its cause will be chance, not desert. The syntax with dative experiencer — befall you, befell him — preserves Old English impersonal pattern where events happen to people.

The word is intransitive in form but takes an oblique object: befall takes a person as its goal, as in sorrow befell us. Past tense is befell, past participle befallen, present participle befalling. Register is literary, formal, biblical, and fateful; everyday speech prefers happen to, hit, strike. One says What happened to you? in conversation but What befell you? in epic, scripture, or fantasy. That archaism is functional: befall summons providence, doom, fortune, and moral order, making chance feel fated even when the sentence insists it is purely circumstance.

  • 1. To happen to, come upon, overtake (central sense, usually adverse): of accidents, disasters, punishments, illnesses that arrive as if falling. Examples: A great famine befell the land; No harm will befall you while I guard; Whatever befalls, we endure. Semantic bridge: from literal to fall upon in Old English befeallan to fall to, fall around — leaves befall the ground, darkness befalls — narrowed to events falling upon experiencers. The falling image survives in collocations like tragedy struck, disaster hit, misfortune fell upon: befall is the lexical fossil of that metaphor.
  • 2. Biblical, epic and fantasy use — fate, providence, doom: And it came to pass, as often translated from befall-patterns, frames events as ordained sequence. Examples: Lest evil befall thee; The curse that befell the house of Atreus; The darkness that befell Middle-earth. Semantic bridge: the impersonal syntax — it befell that — invites a hidden agent: God, gods, fate, circumstance. Even when modern fantasy writes If such befall you from circumstance, the archaic verb lends cosmic weight to randomness, making luck sound like destiny.
  • 3. Neutral or fortunate happening (rarer, archaic): in older use befall could be neutral or even good: joy befell them, good fortune befell him. Example: Such happiness as seldom befalls mortals. Semantic bridge: retention of original neutral to fall to someone's lot — a lot, share, inheritance befalls by casting or dividing. Modern use has darkened by association: because speakers reach for befall mainly for evils worth solemnizing, the verb itself absorbed gloom, a process of semantic pejoration. Dictionaries still list happen neutrally first, then note usually unpleasant.
  • 4. Impersonal and existential construction — it befell that: it befell that they met, as it befell in those days. Example: It befell that the letter arrived too late. Semantic bridge: generalization of experiencer construction to dummy it; what falls, falls in time itself. This use, common in King James Bible and chronicles, makes befall a marker of annalistic time — events recorded because they fell upon the record.
  • 5. Participial-adjectival befallen — stricken, afflicted: the befallen city, frost-befallen crops. Example: The plague-befallen village was quarantined. Semantic bridge: past participle as state resulting from falling-upon; like fallen applied to angels and leaves, befallen applies to communities under calamity. Rare in modern prose but productive in compound coinages that need archaic gravity.

Befall differs from happen by implying descent from above and passivity below; from occur by implying human experiencer rather than neutral event; from overtake by implying sudden arrival rather than pursuit; from strike and hit by being slower, more enveloping, less violent. To say the same facts with befall is to moralize them slightly: what happens merely happens, but what befalls arrives, as if sent. That is why circumstance in the source sentence needs befall — pure chance dressed in the grammar of fate.
