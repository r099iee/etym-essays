Late Medieval Double Life - Spout enters Middle English from the expressive spit/spew family, reinforced by Middle Dutch spuyte, and from the start names both a tube or lip for pouring and the act of liquid gushing through a narrow opening.
Early Modern Hardware & Nature - In Early Modern English spout is established for kettle and ewer spouts, whale blowholes, waterspouts, and downspouts — the concrete "forced passage through a narrow mouth" sense dominating ordinary use.
18th-19th Century Figurative Talk - By the 18th century spout extends figuratively to speech — to "spout" rhetoric or verse is to pour words out as if from a pipe — a comic, dismissive sense alongside the physical ones.
Contemporary Sense - Today spout keeps both the physical jet/tube senses (waterspout, kettle spout) and the figurative "spout opinions," while poetic use can lift it into grand imagery like Tolkien's "spout of flame."
