Derivation chain for BLUNDERING (present participle / gerund of blunder); the card's form is the -ing participle, so the final node is bolded as blundering. [1] (Ultimate origin uncertain) Old Norse blunda "to shut the eyes, doze, have the eyes closed" (attested in Old Norse prose and verse, e.g. the sagas and the prose tradition). The proposed semantic bridge: acting with shut eyes -> acting blindly -> clumsy, stumbling, error-prone action. This is the best-supported link (adopted by the Oxford English Dictionary and modern handbooks), but the Germanic base behind blunda is not securely traced to any PIE root. [2] (Possible reinforcing source, not an attested English form) Continental Scandinavian and Low German forms in the blund-/blond- cluster meaning "cloudy, dim, confused" — contact-zone reinforcements from the North Sea and Baltic trade and settlement area; exact forms are unattested in the English record and their influence must be treated as probable rather than proven. [3] Middle English blundren, blondren "to move blindly, stumble; to confuse, mix up" (first attested c. 1300). [4] Early Modern English blunder (v.) "to act clumsily, to make a stupid mistake" (16th c.) and blunder (n.) "a gross, clumsy mistake" (recorded by the 17th c., fully current in the 18th c.). [5] Modern English blundering "present participle and gerund; also adjective (a blundering fool)" (18th–21st c.). Debated / nuanced note: the standard account links blunder to the Scandinavian shut-eyed verb, and the "blind" idea is the accepted semantic bridge; a minority position derives the word from a native Germanic "mix, confuse, make cloudy" root. Neither line reaches a secure PIE etymon, and the ultimate base must be labelled unknown. Blunderbuss is NOT a stage of this word: it is borrowed from Dutch donderbus "thunder gun" and reshaped by folk etymology toward blunder; it belongs to a different lineage and must not be folded into the chain.
