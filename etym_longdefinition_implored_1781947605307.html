Implored — the preterite and past participle of the verb implore: begged someone earnestly, desperately, and (in the older core of the word) tearfully to do something. In Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit, the friendly sparrows, hearing Peter's sobs, fly to him "in great excitement" and implore him to exert himself — to struggle free of the gooseberry net — converting his passive self-surrender into a last chance at escape. CORE SENSES
  • To beg with great earnestness. The central modern sense: to ask for something, or for someone's action, with intense and often desperate urgency; "she implored him to stay"; "they implored the court for mercy."
  • The older tearful sense. Etymologically "to call upon with tears" (Latin in- + plorare, "to weep"), the classical sense of entreaty accompanied by weeping — the sense Potter's scene enacts, where the sparrows' urgent plea answers Peter's "big tears" and sobs.
  • Transitive constructions. To implore a person (implore someone to do something); to implore a thing (implore pardon, implore aid, implore mercy); "implore + for" (implore for help).
SECONDARY AND REGISTER USES
  • Literary and biblical register. Implore is the formal, elevated verb of petition, at home in prayers ("we implore Thee"), tragedy, and high narrative — where asked and told would be flat, implore carries solemn weight.
  • Past participle as adjective. The participle can shade into adjectival use ("an implored mercy," "implored aid"), the begged-for thing viewed as already sought.
  • Modern weakening of the tear image. In present-day English the weeping component has faded; to implore is to beg very earnestly, with the tears present only in the word's etymological shadow.
WHY THIS WORD IN THIS SCENE Potter chooses a Latinate verb of high emotional intensity rather than plain asked or told: the sparrows' "great excitement" and desperate entreaty raise the stakes, and the elevated word makes the rescue scene feel solemn and charged — a small epic plea in a children's book. FURTHER POLYSEMY AND GRAMMATICAL FACETS
  • Implore with a person or a thing. The verb's object may be a person (to implore someone), a thing desired (to implore mercy, pardon, aid, assistance, forgiveness), or a clause of action (to implore that something be done); the full object-frame set marks it as a verb of communication and desire with formal syntactic habits.
  • Implore in theology and liturgy. In religious language the verb is directed upward: to implore God's blessing, the intercession of a saint, or divine mercy, keeping the ancient Latin sense of invoking a higher power with tears; "implore Thee" survives in formal prayer.
  • Legal and oratorical use. In courtroom and parliamentary rhetoric, to implore is to make an emotional appeal to authority — imploring the court, imploring clemency — where the word carries the urgency of the older "call upon" sense into formal procedure.
  • Direction of the appeal. Unlike beg, which may be upward, downward, or lateral, implore in its strongest use implies an appeal to someone believed able to help — a power-gradient preserved from Latin, where the suppliant calls upon a protector; this nuance explains why the sparrows, who can act, are the ones implored.
  • Intensity register. Implore is situated between entreat (polite, sustained) and beseech (archaic, reverent): it is the modern high-intensity verb of earnest request, too strong for routine asking and weaker than the obsolete supplicate's posture of kneeling humility.
REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS Collocations: implore someone to do something, implore someone's help/mercy/pardon, implore for, implore the court/gods, I implore you. Register is formal and literary; in casual speech begged, asked, or pleaded hold sway.
