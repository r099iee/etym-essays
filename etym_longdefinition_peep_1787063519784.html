peep (verb) — to come partially and briefly into view, or to look at something quickly and secretly, especially from a concealed position or through a narrow opening. In the source sentence the stars begin to peep forth, that is, to appear gradually in the evening sky — the word here carries the gentle sense of something small or distant revealing itself by degrees rather than bursting into sight.

Core meaning: the verb centres on the idea of a small, partial, or momentary disclosure. Whether the subject is a person glancing through a gap or a flower rising through the soil, the common thread is that only a little is seen, only for a little while, and often in a way that is furtive, curious, or delicate.

  • 1. To look through a narrow or small opening (primary modern sense): e.g. she peeped through the curtains. It implies a quick, careful, often secretive glance; it commonly collocates with out, in, at, through.
  • 2. To emerge or appear gradually (secondary modern sense): of the sun, stars, moon, flowers, or a person, to show itself only partly or only gradually, e.g. the moon peeped out from behind a cloud. This is the sense used of the stars in the Hobbit passage, and it is now the dominant literary sense for natural phenomena.
  • 3. Noun — a brief or secret look: let me have a peep. It is often used of glancing at something forbidden, surprising, or merely attractive.
  • 4. Noun — the first light of dawn: at the peep of day (dawn), a fixed collocation dating from the early modern period.
  • 5. Verb and noun — a faint, high-pitched sound: from a separate imitative root, to peep is the thin cry of a young bird, a mouse, or a chick; as a noun, not a peep means no sound at all, and by extension no word or complaint, as in there has not been a peep out of him.
  • 6. Idioms and phrasal verbs: peep out and peep forth (to begin to appear); peep through (to look through a gap); peep at (to glance furtively). The adjective peeping survives in peeping Tom, meaning a voyeur, from the legendary Coventry tailor of the 14th century.

Register and nuance: the visual senses are neutral-to-informal and slightly playful; the sound sense is informal. Unlike see or look, peep always implies brevity and usually secrecy or delicacy, which is exactly why Tolkien uses it of the stars: they do not blaze out all at once but steal quietly into the sky.

Polysemy bridge: the modern word is really two words that have fallen together. The 'look' family and the 'squeak' family were independent in origin but identical in form, so modern English treats them as one — a classic case of homonymic merger rather than gradual semantic drift. The 'gradual emergence' sense grows naturally out of 'to look through a narrow opening', because what one sees through a crack is only a sliver of the thing; hence anything that 'peeps' is seen only in part and only for a moment.
