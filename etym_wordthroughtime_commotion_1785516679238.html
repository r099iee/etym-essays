- [14th century adoption phase] - Entering English in the 14th century, commotion meant violent agitation, shaking, or disturbance, preserving the physical, kinetic force of its Latin source.
- [16th-17th century political upheaval] - The word attached itself to civil disturbance, insurrection, and the tremors of the body politic, describing riots, rebellions, and seditious stirrings.
- [19th-20th century literary agitation] - Victorian and modern writers (Dickens, Conrad) used commotion for street brawls, parliamentary uproar, and mutinous crews, keeping alive its sense of disorder that moves through a crowd.
- [Contemporary everyday sense] - Modern usage has broadened and lightened the word: a commotion is now any noisy, confused disturbance (a market row, children upstairs), the political gravity having faded into general hubbub.
