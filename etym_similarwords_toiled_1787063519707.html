• laboured: similar both mean hard work; differs because labour is the broad, formal word for work of any kind (especially manual), while toil adds weariness and struggle — toil is labour that exhausts.
  • worked: similar the general act of working; differs because work is utterly neutral, while toiled implies prolonged, draining effort beyond the ordinary day.
  • slogged: similar dogged, plodding effort; differs because slog is colloquial and suggests heavy, trudging persistence (slog through mud, slog away), while toil is more dignified and literary.
  • slaved: similar working extremely hard; differs because slave implies oppressive, involuntary labour (slaved away), while toil can be willing and dignified.
  • grubbed: similar toiling over the soil or menial work; differs because grub is crude and underground (grubbing for roots), while toil is general effort, mental or physical.
  • struggled: similar striving with difficulty; differs because struggle is often against an opponent or obstacle and implies resistance, while toil is steady, unheroic endurance.
  • strained: similar exerting oneself; differs because strain focuses on the physical tension of the effort, while toil focuses on the sustained duration of the work.
  • sweated: similar working with visible exhaustion; differs because sweat is concrete and colloquial (sweated over it), while toil keeps a solemn, literary gravity.
  • drudged: similar dull, menial, wearisome work; differs because drudge stresses the dreariness and lowliness of the task, while toil can dignify even great labour.
  • plugged: similar persisting at hard work (plugged away); differs because plug is informal and often steady-but-ordinary, while toiled is weighty and literary.
  • exerted: similar applying effort; differs because exert is formal and momentary (exert oneself), while toil is the full stretch of sustained labour.
  • wrought: similar archaic labour sense (wrought hard); differs because wrought is an archaic strong form now used of making and shaping, while toiled is a living everyday past verb.
