1. Indo-European structural origins of the two elements. The Sanskrit proper noun Subāhu (सुबाहु) is an ancient synthetic bahuvrīhi compound formed by the union of two foundational Proto-Indo-European roots, both of which possess rich, widely documented reflexes across the major Indo-European language families:

  • The prefixal element: *h₁su- (good, well, noble). The initial morpheme su- derives directly from PIE *h₁su- (or *su-), a prestigious adjectival prefix and proclitic meaning "good, well, healthy, noble, or auspicious." In ancient Greek, this root yielded the exceptionally prolific prefix eu- (εὐ-, as seen in English borrowings such as eulogy, euphony, euphemism, and euphoria). In the Celtic branch, it produced Gaulish su- and Old Irish so- / su- (as in so-chenél "of good birth, noble"). In ancient Iranian (Avestan), it produced hu- (as in humata "good thoughts," hūkhta "good words," hvarshta "good deeds"). In Vedic Sanskrit, su- was used abundantly as a prefix indicating excellence, ease, abundance, beauty, or great intensity (e.g., sundara "beautiful," suras "divine").
  • The nominal element: *bʰāǵʰu-s / *bʰeh₂ǵʰu-s (arm, limb, branch). The second constituent bāhu traces back to PIE *bʰāǵʰu-s (or *bʰeh₂ǵʰu-s), which designated "the arm, the forearm, or the front limb of a four-legged animal." This anatomical root was preserved with remarkable fidelity across the Indo-European dialects. In ancient Greek, it yielded pēkhus (πῆχυς, "forearm, cubit," also the rigid arm of a bow or lyre). In Germanic, the root shifted via Grimm's Law (aspirated voiced stop *bʰ becoming voiced stop *b, and palatovelar *ǵʰ becoming velar *g), producing Proto-Germanic *bōguz ("shoulder, upper arm, front leg"). In Old English, this became bōg or bōh ("arm, shoulder of a beast, bough of a tree"), which evolved into modern English bough (the main branch or "arm" of a tree) and archaic/dialectal bow (the shoulder of an animal or the prow/shoulder of a ship). In Avestan, it yielded bāzu- ("arm"). In Sanskrit, it became the standard noun bāhu (बाहु, "the arm, forearm, or strength of limbs").

2. Compound typology and Pāṇinian Bahuvrīhi semantics. In classical Sanskrit grammar as codified by Pāṇini in the Aṣṭādhyāyī (sūtras 2.2.23–28), Subāhu is classified as an attributive compound or bahuvrīhi (specifically a prādi-bahuvrīhi, where the initial member is a prepositional or prefixal particle):

  • A tatpuruṣa compound would signify "a good arm" (neuter/masculine referring strictly to the limb itself).
  • A bahuvrīhi compound, by contrast, shifts its referent outward to an external possessor (anyapadārthe): thus, su-bāhu does not mean "a fine arm," but rather "one who has fine, mighty, or well-formed arms."

This syntactic construction is identical in form and function to the Homeric Greek heroic epithets (such as leukōlenos "white-armed," applied to Hera, or rhododaktulos "rosy-fingered," applied to the dawn). It represents a fundamental poetic strategy of the Indo-European oral epic tradition, where heroes, gods, and formidable adversaries were identified by prominent physical attributes signaling their capacity for combat.

3. Indo-Iranian and Indo-European heroic onomastics. The formation of personal names combining *su- with anatomical or martial features was a hallmark of Proto-Indo-Iranian onomastics. Parallel formations abound in both Vedic Sanskrit and Avestan: compare Sanskrit Su-netra ("fair-eyed"), Su-dās ("giving well"), and Su-ग्रीव (Sugrīva, "having a handsome neck"), with Avestan Hu-bāzu- ("having strong arms"). Furthermore, the exact lexical pairing of PIE *h₁su- and *bʰāǵʰu-s exists as an attested ancient Greek name: Eupēkhus (Εὔπηχυς, "well-armed / having beautiful arms"). This proves that the compound *h₁su-bʰāǵʰu-s was already an established heroic formulation in late Proto-Indo-European poetic speech before the separation of the Greek and Indo-Iranian branches.

4. Literary evolution: from Vedic anatomy to Epic demonology. In the earliest Vedic literature (the Rigveda and Atharvaveda), bāhu appears primarily in somatic, martial, and ritual descriptions, most famously in the Puruṣa Sūkta (RV 10.90.12), where the warrior class (Kshatriyas) is created from the arms (bāhū) of the cosmic Puruṣa, establishing the arm as the eternal biological symbol of martial protection and physical combat.

By the time of the epic composition of the Rāmāyaṇa (c. 5th century BCE), the name Subāhu was strategically assigned to the demonic warrior who attacks Sage Viśvāmitra's altar. The choice carries high narrative poignancy: the demon possesses "mighty arms" (subāhu), which he uses to hurl defilements and wield clubs against peaceful sages; however, his mortal physical arms are completely overwhelmed by the divine arm of Prince Rāma, who draws the cosmic bow Pināka and releases the Āgneyāstra. The physical strength celebrated in the demon's name is thereby subordinated to the spiritual and moral power of divine dharma.

5. Transmission into vernacular traditions and modern scholarship. Throughout the medieval centuries, the story of Subāhu's death at Siddhāśrama was translated and adapted into dozens of vernacular languages across South and Southeast Asia—including the Tamil Kamba Rāmāyaṇam, the Bengali Krittivasi Ramayan, the Awadhi Rāmcaritmānas, and the Javanese Kakawin Ramayana. In every tradition, Subāhu's role remains constant: he is the indispensable initial adversary whose defeat proves the young Rāma's readiness to protect civilization from demonic chaos. In modern Indology, the analysis of Subāhu provides linguists with a pristine textbook example of classical Indo-European bahuvrīhi composition surviving intact across three millennia of written and spoken tradition.
