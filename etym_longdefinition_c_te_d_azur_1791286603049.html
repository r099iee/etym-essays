The Côte d'Azur is the celebrated Mediterranean coastal region of south-eastern France, stretching roughly from Menton at the Italian border westward through Monaco, Nice, Antibes, Cannes and Saint-Tropez toward Hyères and Toulon, renowned for its intense blue sea and sky, mild winters, luxury resorts, yachting harbours and film festivals. In English it is usually glossed as the French Riviera, though French speakers reserve Riviera for the Italian side and use Côte d'Azur for their own shore. The name literally means the Azure (sky-blue) Coast: côte for coast or slope, d' for of, azur for the heraldic and poetic blue of sky and lapis lazuli. To invoke it, as Bertie Wooster does, is to invoke wealth, sunshine, continental tailoring and faintly raffish glamour.

As a proper noun the phrase behaves like a place with strong connotations rather than a mere administrative label. Guidebooks, estate agents and novelists use Côte d'Azur to promise light, leisure and exclusivity: a villa on the Côte d'Azur, wintering on the Côte d'Azur, as we call it on the Côte d'Azur. The definite article is usually retained in French (la Côte d'Azur) and often in English (on the Côte d'Azur), marking it as a defined littoral strip rather than a town. Its prestige derives from 19th-century English aristocratic wintering, Belle Époque grand hotels, interwar artistic colonies and post-war cinema (Cannes), yachting and celebrity culture. Bertie's throwaway reference to what dinner jackets are called on the Côte d'Azur trades precisely on this prestige: he affects Riviera cosmopolitanism to win a domestic argument about which coat Jeeves should pack.

  • Geographical sense: the actual littoral of Alpes-Maritimes and Var — Nice with the Promenade des Anglais, Cannes with the Croisette, Saint-Tropez, Antibes, Menton — prized for limestone hills falling to a tideless blue sea, Mistral-cleared light, palms, pines and Belle Époque architecture.
  • Tourism-luxury sense: shorthand for Riviera high life — grand hotels (Negresco, Carlton), casinos (Monte-Carlo), marinas, beach clubs, the Cannes Film Festival — with connotations of money, fashion, tans and mild scandal.
  • Linguistic-prestige sense: in Wodehouse, as we call it on the Côte d'Azur introduces the French borrowing smoking for dinner jacket; the place-name authenticates the slang as insider continental usage rather than mere affectation.
  • Administrative overlap: the modern region Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur (PACA) institutionalises the tourist brand as government; estate and weather reports use Côte d'Azur for a climatic zone of hot dry summers and soft winters.
  • Colour-brand sense: azur itself evokes heraldic sky-blue; by extension Côte d'Azur sells blueness as commodity, parallel to Côte d'Opale, Costa del Sol and Costa Blanca — coasts named for light and colour to attract visitors.

Stylistically the phrase is always capitalised, often italicised as foreign in older English books, and pronounced in anglicised French: coat-da-ZURE rather than pure French /kotdazyʀ/. Bertie's usage shows its function as social capital: dropping Côte d'Azur into a valet dispute signals that the speaker winters where dinner jackets are smokings and white mess-jackets are de rigueur. The humour is that cosmopolitan authority is invoked for a parochially English question of what to wear to dinner.
