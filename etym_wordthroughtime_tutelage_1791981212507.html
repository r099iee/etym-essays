- c. 1600 — Borrowed as a legal-administrative term for guardianship and protection, close to the modern wardship; a bookish word from the start.
- 18th c. — Extended to the state of being a minor under a guardian, and increasingly to any condition of protected dependence.
- 19th c. — Acquires its dominant educational sense, “instruction, teaching, formative oversight”, especially in biographies of artists and statesmen.
- Late 19th–20th c. — Applied to nations and territories: mandates, protectorates and trusteeships are described as peoples under tutelage, giving the word a colonial and paternalistic colour.
- Contemporary — Chiefly formal and slightly archaic; survives in the fixed phrase under the tutelage of, and is often used with irony about regulators, committees and over-managed institutions.
