Stratagems carries its military ancestry on its sleeve: it descends directly from the ancient Greek word for a general's act of generalship. Its journey — from Greek battlefields, through Latin and French, into English — shows how a technical military term became a general word for cleverness and deception, and how the plural kept the flavour of a whole catalogue of tricks.

1. Two Indo-European roots meet in one Greek word. The story braids two PIE roots. First, PIE *sterh₃- ("to spread, extend, stretch out") underlies Greek stratós, "army": an army is literally the host spread out in its encampment and order of battle, the root being the same one behind Latin sternere (to spread) and English street. Second, PIE *h₂eǵ- ("to drive, lead") underlies Greek ágein, "to lead, drive, guide," the root of Latin agere (to drive, do) and its English descendants such as agent and act.

2. The birth of the general (5th century BCE). Classical Greek combined the two: stratēgós (from stratós + ágein) means literally "army-leader," the military commander or general. The great age of the Athenian stratēgoí (the ten elected generals of Athens) in the fifth century BCE made the word one of the most important in Greek public life. From stratēgós Greek built the verb stratēgeîn ("to be a general, to command") and then the abstract noun stratḗgēma, "an act of generalship."

3. From generalship to trickery. Already in Greek, stratḗgēma narrowed from "any act of a general" to the special sense "a piece of generalship of a deceptive kind": a stratagem, a trick of war. Greek tactical literature — the manuals of Aineias the Tactician, Polybius, and later the collection of Frontinus — made the plural stratēgḗmata a technical genre: catalogues of the ruses, feints, and deceptions by which clever commanders won battles. The word entered Latin as strategema with exactly this meaning, attested in the first century BCE.

4. Into English through French (c. 1490). English received the word through French stratagème in about 1490, in the earliest era of military-scientific translation. For its first two centuries in English it was strictly a war word: a stratagem was an artifice of war, a device for deceiving the enemy. It appears in the military and political prose of the Tudor and Stuart periods, in translations of the classics, and in the language of generalship where cleverness in deception was openly admired.

5. The broadening (18th century). During the eighteenth century the word marched off the battlefield. A stratagem could now be any cunning scheme — in business, in politics, in courtship, in diplomacy. The metaphorical extension was natural: the contest between rivals, whatever its field, is a miniature war, and the clever deceptive plan is its stratagem. The word kept its formal gravity but lost its exclusive military ownership.

6. The literary century (19th century). The nineteenth century made stratagem a favourite of adventure fiction and melodrama. Plots turn on stratagems that are hatched, executed, and foiled; the plural stratagems accumulates a theatrical, slightly archaic flavour, as if every schemer were a general in a private campaign. It is exactly this flavour that Tolkien captures in 1937, when the dwarves discuss the stratagems by which dragons had been slain — a nod to the old genre of tactical catalogues.

7. Modern usage. Today stratagem remains formal and literary: it appears in espionage thrillers, chess commentary, rhetoric about political deception, and historical writing. The plural stratagems still carries the ancient genre's sense of a whole repertoire of tricks. It has never quite become an everyday word — and that is precisely its power: it lends the dignity of generalship to any cunning plan.

8. Summary. From two Indo-European roots — one for spreading, one for leading — Greek built the general; the general's noun built the trick; Latin and French carried the trick into English; and five hundred years of English kept it, slightly grand and slightly wily, for every contest from dragon-slaying to the boardroom.
