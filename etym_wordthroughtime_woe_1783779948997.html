Old English Cry of Lamentation - Woe begins as the bare interjection wā!, the cry of "alas!" torn from the sufferer, heard in the earliest Old English poetry and used to summon, curse, and lament; the noun forms alongside it meaning grief and calamity.
Medieval & Scriptural Weight - By Middle English woe is a firmly established noun for deep, structural misery and, through the Bible's prophetic "woe unto you," acquires a moral and judgmental register — the affliction pronounced upon the wicked by God.
Early Modern Literary Peak - The King James Bible and Milton use woe as the grand word for fated, total suffering ("Death into the World, and all our woe"), giving it an epic and theological grandeur that is now inseparable from the word.
Contemporary Sense - Today woe is archaic, poetic, or scriptural outside fixed phrases (woe is me, woe betide), but remains alive in the everyday adjective woeful ("wretchedly bad") and in phrases like tale of woe, where the old doom-laden colour has softened into mild lament.
