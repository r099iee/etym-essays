- Early Old English `cnotta` - Primarily referred to a physical tie or fastening, also metaphorically to a difficulty or riddle. - Practical utility and early abstract extension.
- Middle English `knotte` - Continued physical and metaphorical uses, with increasing application to natural formations (e.g., in wood) and groups of people. - Broadening of descriptive power.
- Early Modern English `knot` (and `knotted` as participle) - Developed specialized meanings in various crafts (sailing, weaving) and more nuanced figurative senses for complexity, tension, and physical appearance (e.g., muscularity, gnarled wood). - Increased precision and metaphorical depth.
- 17th-18th Century - The 'k' in 'knot' became silent, though spelling remained. The descriptive power of 'knotted' for gnarled, twisted, or tense states became firmly established in literature. - Phonetic shift, solidified descriptive usage.
- Modern English `knotted` - Widely used to describe anything physically or figuratively intricate, gnarled, tense, or full of knots, from tree trunks to emotional states. - Comprehensive and versatile descriptor.
