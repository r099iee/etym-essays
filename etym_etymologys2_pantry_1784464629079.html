Chain: Stage 1: PIE *peh₂- 'to protect, guard, feed' (also written *pā-), c. 4500–2500 BCE; this is the root that underlies the Latin verb pāscō 'to feed, graze, pasture' and, in the standard view, the Latin word for bread. (A minority analysis instead derives pānis from a separate nominal root meaning 'food'; see debate note.) Stage 2: Latin pāscō, pāscere 'to feed, to drive to pasture, to graze' — attested from the 3rd century BCE (Plautus). Stage 3: Latin pānis, pānis (genitive pānis) 'bread, a loaf, food' — attested from the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE (Plautus, Cato), a derivative of the feeding family. Stage 4: Latin pānārius 'of or belonging to bread' — a Latin adjective/noun of the bread-word (attested; also the word for a bread-maker or baker). Stage 5: Old French paneterie 'the room or office where bread was kept, a bread-room' — attested in the 12th century, formed on panetier 'bread-servant, baker' (from pan 'bread' < Latin pānis + -erie). Stage 6: Middle English panetrie / panterie 'a room or store for bread and provisions' — borrowed from Old French c. 1300 (recorded in Middle English sources of the early 14th century). Stage 7: Modern English pantry 'a small room or cupboard for food, tableware, and provisions' — in its modern form and broadened meaning from the 16th century onward. Final node: PANTRY. Debate note: the precise morphology of Latin pānis is disputed — the standard view connects it with pāscō and PIE *peh₂- 'to protect/feed'; a minority view reconstructs an independent PIE noun 'food' or links the family to the root of pastus 'pasture'. All views agree the family centres on 'feed/protect', so the meaning-chain above holds while the exact branching of the Latin stage is debated.
