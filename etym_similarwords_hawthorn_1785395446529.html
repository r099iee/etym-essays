• blackthorn (Prunus spinosa): Similar because it is a thorny, early-flowering hedgerow shrub of the English countryside with white blossom; differs because it is a plum (genus Prunus, Rosaceae) that flowers on bare wood in March–April before the leaves, and bears blue-black sloes rather than red haws.
  • bramble / blackberry (Rubus fruticosus): Similar because it is a wild, thorny hedgerow plant with edible fruit; differs because it is a scrambling, soft-stemmed bush with compound leaves and a compound black berry, growing in thickets rather than standing as a thorny shrub or small tree.
  • holly (Ilex aquifolium): Similar because it is a prickly evergreen hedging plant bearing red berries; differs because it belongs to the holly family (Aquifoliaceae), is evergreen, flowers inconspicuously in spring, and carries berries through winter rather than haws in autumn.
  • briar / brier: Similar because it names a thorny shrub or thicket; differs because it is a vaguer, looser term, often applied to wild roses and prickly growth generally, without hawthorn's precise identity as the hedge-thorn Crataegus.
  • May / may-tree / whitethorn / quickthorn: Similar because these are folk-synonyms for the very same plant; differs because each fixes on one aspect — the May blossom (May), the pale flowers and bark (whitethorn), or the fast living growth of a hedge (quickthorn) — while hawthorn is the general common name.
  • rowan / mountain ash (Sorbus aucuparia): Similar because it is a small rose-family tree with clusters of red berries and toothed leaves; differs because it is thornless, has pinnate leaves and an open graceful crown, and is a tree of woods and crags rather than a hedge shrub.
  • crab apple (Malus sylvestris): Similar because it is a thorny wild rose-family tree with white blossom and small fruit; differs because its fruit is a true apple, it grows as a tree rather than a hedge shrub, and it lacks the dense interwoven hedging habit.
  • dog rose / wild rose (Rosa canina): Similar because it is a rose-family thorny hedge plant with five-petalled flowers and red fruit; differs because it is a scrambling climber with hooked thorns, usually scentless single flowers, and scarlet hips rather than haws.
  • privet (Ligustrum vulgare): Similar because it is a common English hedging plant; differs because it is a smooth, semi-evergreen shrub of the olive family with black berries, no thorns, and none of the spring blossom display.
  • elder (Sambucus nigra): Similar because it is a small native tree with white blossom and dark fruit used in country food and drink; differs because it is a thornless, soft-wooded bush of the elder family with flat cream blossom-heads and blue-black berries.
  • sloe-berry / sloe: Similar because it is the fruit of a thorny hedgerow shrub eaten for its sourness; differs because it is the blue-black drupe of the blackthorn, not the red haw of the hawthorn.
