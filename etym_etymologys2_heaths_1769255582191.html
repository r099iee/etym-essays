1. Proto-Indo-European *kait- "forest, wasteland, uncultivated land" (reconstructed root; Pokorny). Cognates across the Germanic and some other European families suggest a root meaning "uncultivated woodland or waste." Some scholars treat this root as of uncertain — possibly substrate, non-Indo-European — origin (the debate is noted below). 2. Proto-Germanic *haithiz "heath, open uncultivated land, waste" (reconstructed; roughly mid-1st millennium BCE to mid-1st millennium CE) — source of Old English hǣð, Old Saxon hētha, Old High German heida, Old Norse heiðr "heath, moor," Dutch heide, German Heide. 3. Old English hǣð "untilled waste land, heath" (attested from the 9th century) — common in Old English poetry and charters; it yields the derivative hǣþen "heathen," literally "dweller on the heath." 4. Old English plural hǣðas "heaths, tracts of open waste land" — the regular Old English plural of hǣð (attested in Old English). 5. Middle English heth / hethe "waste land, heath" (attested 13th century), plural hethes. 6. Early Modern English heath, plural heaths — spelling stabilized by the 16th–17th centuries. 7. Modern English HEATHS (the plural of heath) — present day. Debated / nuanced note: the Germanic chain from *haithiz onward is solid and fully attested, but the PIE attribution of *kait- is debated in outline. The Germanic (and possibly Baltic) cognates agree on the meaning, yet the root's shape and distribution have led some scholars to suggest a pre-Indo-European substrate origin rather than a normal PIE root. The word's core sense — untilled waste land — has remained constant for over a thousand years of English.
