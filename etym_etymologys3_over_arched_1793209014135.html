The compound over-arched welds together two histories of very different age: a Germanic particle as old as the language itself, and a Romance noun that marched in with medieval masons. Its full story must therefore be told in two strands before the knot is tied.

First strand — over, the ancient particle:

  • A word older than England: reconstructed PIE *uper meaning over, above, and beyond is among the most stable roots in Indo-European, surviving almost unchanged across Sanskrit upari, Greek hyper, Latin super, and Germanic *uberi. Old English ofer carried the whole family of senses — above in place, too much in degree, finished in time — and Middle English over kept them all.
  • The compounding habit: over- is among the most fertile prefixes in English, joining verbs and nouns for a thousand years — overcome, overhang, overlook, overarch — always carrying the sense of something passing above or going beyond its mark.

Second strand — arch, the Roman curve:

  • From bow to building: Latin arcus meant first the archer's bow and then, by the curve they share, the architectural arch — the semicircular ring of wedge-shaped stones that lets masonry defy gravity. Roman engineers spanned rivers and raised aqueducts with it, and the Romance languages inherited both the object and the name.
  • The French crossing: Old French arche arrived in England in the 13th century with Norman masons and Gothic ambitions; Middle English arch soon named every curved span from church chancels to London Bridge. The same French word gave English the storage ark and Noah's vessel — a chest is an arche of planks — but the architectural sense conquered all.
  • Verb from noun: to arch — to curve like an arch, to furnish with arches — grew naturally from the noun, and by the 17th century backs, brows, necks, and rainbows could all arch.

The knot — to overarch and over-arched: the compound verb appears in the 1600s, exactly when English landscape taste began admiring what trees and ruins could do without architects — avenues that roof themselves, grottoes that vault themselves. The 18th-century picturesque made over-arched a stock praise of garden writers, and Victorian novelists laid every approach to every great house beneath over-arching elms. In the 20th century the present participle broke away into abstraction: an overarching theme or narrative spans a subject as an arch spans a road, and the boardroom now overarches where the bower once did. The source sentence keeps the older faith: branches, gravity, dim light — the arch you walk under rather than the agenda you work under. The hyphen itself is the badge of that older faith, holding over and arched visibly apart long after speech has fused them into a single sheltered shadow.
