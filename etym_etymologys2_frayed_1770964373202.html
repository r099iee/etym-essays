PIE *bhrey- / *bhreye- "to rub, scrape, grind, break into pieces" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; the connection to Latin fricāre is debated — see note below) → Latin fricāre "to rub, rub down, chafe, scrub" (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. in agricultural and medical texts) → Vulgar Latin *fricāre "to rub away, wear down by contact" (Late Latin, c. 4th–6th c. CE) → Old French frayer / frier "to rub, rub away, scrape; to clear a path by rubbing" (attested from c. 10th–12th c. CE; also the source of Modern French frayer "to trace a path") → Middle English frayen "to rub, wear away by friction" (borrowed from Anglo-Norman, 14th c. CE) → Modern English fray (verb) "to wear away or unravel at the edges by rubbing" (16th c. CE onwards) → Modern English frayed (past participle / participial adjective) "unraveled or worn at the edge through constant rubbing" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The deep PIE ancestry of Latin fricāre is not universally accepted. The traditional reconstruction (Pokorny) places it under *bhrey- "to rub, break, crush," citing Sanskrit bhrīṇāti "injures, hurts" and Greek *phrik- "bristling, roughness" as cognates, but many modern etymologists (including de Vaan) regard the link as uncertain and note that fricāre may be an independent, possibly sound-symbolic formation. The Romance-to-English portion of the chain (Old French frayer → Middle English frayen → frayed) is securely attested and beyond dispute. Note also the accidental homograph fray "to fight, brawl" (as in "the fray"), which descends separately from Old French affraier / esfreer "to frighten" (from Latin *exfridāre) and is NOT a relative of this word.
