PRIMARY CHAIN (the word pilgrim as a whole): The English word is a single descendant of a Latin compound built from two PIE roots. The linear chain: PIE *h₂eǵ- "to drive, lead" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; a root of driving and leading) with its derived form *h₂eǵ-ro- "field, land (the place where cattle are driven)", combined with the preposition PIE *per- "through, across, beyond" → Latin peregrē "abroad, from abroad, through the fields" (compound of per "through" + ager "field, land"; attested classical, 1st century BCE) → Latin peregrīnus "foreign, of foreign lands; a foreigner, sojourner" (peregrē + the adjectival suffix -īnus; attested 1st century BCE–CE) → Late Latin peregrīnus "one who travels to holy places" (Christian re-reading, 4th century CE onward; cf. Augustine's peregrini, the citizens of the City of God passing through the earthly city) → Old French pelerin / pelegrin "pilgrim, foreign traveler" (9th–12th centuries; with the regular Old French reduction of Latin peregrinus, including the dissimilation of r…r to l…r) → Anglo-Norman / Old French pele(g)rin → Middle English pilegrim, pilgrim "pilgrim" (12th–13th centuries, borrowed via Anglo-Norman) → English pilgrim (modern form; also the extended sense "a traveler or wayfarer," and as Pilgrim, of the 1620 Plymouth colonists). CONSTITUENT ROOT CHAINS (each root in isolation, for completeness): ROOT 1 — PIE *per- "through, across, beyond, forward" (reconstructed) → Latin per "through, across, by" (attested) → Latin peregrē "abroad, through the fields" (per + ager) → Latin peregrīnus "foreign" → Old French pelerin → Middle English pilgrim → pilgrim (the head of the compound, contributing the "through, across" element). ROOT 2 — PIE *h₂eǵ- "to drive, lead" (reconstructed) → Latin agere "to drive, do, act" and ager "field, territory" (genitive agrī; attested) → Latin peregrē "through the fields / abroad" (per + ager) → Latin peregrīnus "foreign" → Old French pelerin → Middle English pilgrim → pilgrim (the base of the compound, contributing the "field, land, territory" element). NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: The chain is secure from Latin onward: peregrīnus is well attested as the Roman legal and social category of the free non-citizen (the peregrini of the empire), and its descent through Old French pelerin to English pilgrim is regular, with the characteristic dissimilation of the first r to l (peregrinus > pelegrinus > pelerin) and the later reshaping of the second syllable. The reconstruction of the two PIE roots, *per- and *h₂eǵ-, is likewise standard and uncontroversial; the only nuance is that *h₂eǵ- in the field-word ager reflects the older sense "the place where cattle are driven," a pastoral logic reconstructed rather than directly attested. Related English cognates of the roots include far, ford, fare, and the prefix per- (from *per-), and acre, agriculture, and action (from *h₂eǵ-).
