The biography of suppositions is the story of an architectural metaphor becoming a pillar of formal logic and then a staple of social conversation.

Stage one — the two PIE elements

The word is built on two Proto-Indo-European building blocks: the prefix *upo- "under, beneath" (which became Latin sub-) and the verbal root *sei- (variously reconstructed) meaning "to put, let, leave, cast", the source of Latin sinere "to let, allow, put". From *apo- ("off, away") plus sinere, Latin contracted ponere "to place, put, set" — one of the most frequent verbs in the language, whose descendants include English position, compose, expose, impose, propose, deposit, and component.

Stage two — Latin supponere and suppositio

Combining sub- "under" with ponere gave supponere "to place under, put beneath" — used concretely of blocks placed under pillars, and of a child or thing fraudulently substituted (the "supposititious child" of legal lore). The noun suppositio meant "a placing under, a substitution". Classical thinkers then performed a decisive translation: suppositio became the Latin calque of the Greek philosophical term hypothesis (from hypo- "under" + thesis "placing", itself from the PIE root *dʰeh₁- "to put"). To "make a supposition" was to lay down a provisional idea beneath a problem as its intellectual foundation.

Stage three — the medieval technical apex

In the High Middle Ages, Scholastic logicians — William of Ockham, John Buridan, Peter of Spain — developed a sophisticated "theory of supposition" (suppositio), a branch of semantics analysing how a general term in a proposition "stands for" or "supposes" particular individuals. At this point the word was a precise technical instrument of university logic, far removed from ordinary speech.

Stage four — Old French and Middle English

Passing into Old French as supposition and into Middle English in the fifteenth century, the word left the lecture hall for general literary use. Renaissance prose uses it of any provisional theory or assumption laid down as a basis for reasoning; the technical "standing-for" sense fades while the "laying down under" image survives.

Stage five — the novelistic irony of Austen

By the nineteenth century supposition had become a standard, slightly formal word for a tentative explanatory guess. Austen places it perfectly in Pride and Prejudice (1813): the Bennet women mount an "attack" of "barefaced questions, ingenious suppositions, and distant surmises" against Mr. Bennet's teasing reserve. "Ingenious suppositions" is double-edged — the guesses are cleverly constructed (hence "ingenious") yet remain mere constructions, unfounded despite their brilliance. The word's old logical gravity now serves comic irony.

Modern state

  • Today supposition is formal and bookish, almost forensic; everyday speech prefers "assumption", "theory", "guess", or "hypothesis".
  • In philosophy and logic the word retains its technical lineage: "supposition theory" still names the Scholastic doctrine of reference.
  • The plural suppositions commonly appears in writing about evidence, investigation, and reasoning — "the report rests on suppositions rather than facts".

Debate note

Two points deserve caution. First, the PIE root behind Latin sinere/ponere is reconstructed only provisionally (variously given as *sei-, *sen-, or with different ablaut grades); the Italic forms themselves are fully attested. Second, the idea that a PIE *stā- ("to stand") root underlies this word is incorrect — "standing" belongs to the Greek hypothesis only through thesis "placing", and the Latin side of the calque rests on ponere "to place", not on any "stand" root.
