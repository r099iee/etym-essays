The verb forsakes represents the third-person singular present indicative form of the native strong Germanic verb forsake (Class VI strong verb: forsake, forsook, forsaken). Its ultimate etymological foundation lies in the Proto-Indo-European root *seh₂g- (or *sag-), conveying the purposeful action "to seek out, track down, investigate, or perceive keenly by scent or inquiry".

From Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Germanic:

  • In the Western Indo-European languages, the root *seh₂g- produced terms related to seeking and legal inquiry: in Latin, it yielded sāgīre ("to perceive keenly", whence sagacious and presage); in Celtic, Old Irish saigid ("he seeks, makes for").
  • In Proto-Germanic, the root evolved into the base noun *sōkiz ("inquiry, dispute, legal case", whence English sake) and the strong Class VI verb *sakaną ("to contend, accuse, dispute, litigate").
  • To create the compound *farsakaną, Germanic prefixed the privative, destructive, and intensive particle *far- (from PIE *pro- / *per-, meaning "away, off, completely, to destruction"). The compound *farsakaną thus carried the literal sense of "to speak against, to dispute away, to contest completely, or to renounce a legal claim".
  • Parallel developments occurred in other Germanic branches: Old High German farsahhan ("to deny, renounce, curse"), Old Saxon farsakan ("to refuse, deny"), and Middle Dutch versaeken (whence Modern Dutch verzaken, "to neglect, fail, renounce").

Old English Period (c. 450–1150 CE):

In Old English, the verb appeared as forsacan (past forsōc, past participle forsacen). In Anglo-Saxon legal texts, poetry, and biblical translations, forsacan occupied a multifaceted role:

  • It functioned legally to denote denying a charge, contesting an oath, or refusing to submit to arbitration.
  • In religious literature (such as King Alfred's translations of Boethius and St. Augustine's Soliloquies), it denoted renouncing pagan gods, forsaking sin, or abandoning worldly greed.
  • In epic poetry (such as Beowulf), it signified refusing battle, breaking faith, or deserting a lord or comrade.

Middle English Evolution and Semantic Broadening (c. 1150–1500 CE):

  • During Middle English, the verb normalized as forsaken. The legalistic sense of verbal dispute gradually receded, while the physical, emotional, and moral sense of "abandoning or walking away from someone" flourished.
  • In Geoffrey Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde and the medieval morality play Everyman (c. 1485), forsaking serves as the central dramatic motif: when Death summons Everyman, all his worldly companions (Fellowship, Kindred, Goods, Beauty, Strength, and Discretion) systematically forsake him, leaving only Good Deeds to accompany his soul.

Early Modern English, Inflectional Shift, and Modern Usage:

In Early Modern English, the verb was prominently featured in the King James Bible (1611) and Shakespearean drama.

  • Inflectional Shift from -eth to -s: During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Northern Middle English third-person singular present inflection -s (forsakes) systematically supplanted the Southern/Midland inflection -eth (forsaketh). By the time Joseph Addison composed his essays in The Spectator (1711–1712), forsakes was the established standard in natural English prose.
  • Phonological Evolution: The root vowel underwent the Great Vowel Shift, transitioning from the Middle English open long /aː/ in forsaken to the modern diphthong /eɪ/ in standard Received Pronunciation /fəˈseɪks/.
  • Today, forsakes remains an exceptionally evocative, poignant verb for the tragic failure of courage, loyalty, or presence when support is most needed.
