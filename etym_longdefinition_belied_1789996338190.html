belied is the past tense and past participle of belie, meaning to contradict, misrepresent, or give a false impression of something — when appearance, words, or manner hide or deny the truth. In the source — Lanarck’s reputation was __________ by his appearance — it means his looks contradicted what people had heard: a fearsome reputation proved false on meeting the unimpressive man.

The central modern sense is innocent contradiction without deliberate lying. A frail appearance may belie great strength; a calm face may belie inner turmoil; shabby clothes may belie wealth; statistics may belie the official story. The subject is usually a sign, surface, or representation — looks, manner, smile, dress, data, appearance — and the object is the reality it masks — strength, age, feelings, reputation, truth. The tone is literary and slightly formal; journalists love it — Her youthful looks belie her 60 years; The sunny headline belied grim figures. No moral accusation attaches: the false impression may be accidental.

An older, stronger sense means to tell lies about someone, to slander or calumniate — now archaic but vital for Shakespeare and the King James Bible. To belie a man was to give false testimony against him, to traduce his character. A second older sense means to disappoint or fail to fulfil — to belie hopes, to belie expectations, to belie a promise — where reality contradicts what was hoped. Both survive in dictionaries and explain the morphology: be- (thoroughly, against) + lie (to deceive).

  • To contradict in appearance (dominant today): to present a misleading surface — His gentleness belied a steely will. No intent to deceive need be present.
  • To misrepresent / falsify (active): to give a false account — The report belied the facts. Closer to distorting, still formal.
  • To tell lies about / slander (archaic): to calumniate — They belied his good name. Found in older literature; use slander, malign today.
  • To disappoint / prove false (archaic-literary): to fail to live up to — The harvest belied our hopes. Now usually expressed with disappoint, fall short of.

The bridge from lying to merely looking false is euphemism and generalization. Originally to belie required a liar; gradually the lie was transferred from the person to the appearance itself, and intent dropped out. Modern readers must therefore distinguish belie from betray: both can reveal truth, but betray usually reveals (his accent betrayed his origin = showed it), while belie usually conceals (his accent belied his origin = hid it). They are near-opposites sharing a root idea of truth versus surface. Grammar is regular (belie / belied / belied / belying; note y → i before -ed), transitive, formal and written rather than conversational. To write that reputation was belied by appearance is to compress a whole irony — fame versus face — into one elegant verb.
