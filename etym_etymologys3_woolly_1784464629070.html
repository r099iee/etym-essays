Woolly is a compound formation entirely native to English and Germanic: the ancient noun wool plus the ordinary adjective-forming suffix -y. To tell its story is to tell the history of the most important textile fiber of prehistoric Europe, and then the quieter history of a very small suffix that turned a noun into an adjective. The noun begins in Proto-Indo-European. The reconstructed form *h2wlh1neh2 "wool" (also written *wlh1neh2) is one of the most widely attested roots in the entire family, a mark of the deep antiquity of sheep-rearing and wool-working in the Indo-European world. Its reflexes cover the family with striking regularity: Sanskrit urnā "wool," whence the ritual woolen threads of the Vedas; Avestan varənā; Latin lāna "wool," whence English lanate "woolly, wool-bearing" and lanolin "wool-fat"; Greek lēnos "wool, fleece"; Lithuanian vilna "wool"; Old Irish olann; and, in Germanic, Proto-Germanic *wullō. The nearly identical meaning everywhere — the fleece of the sheep, the raw material of cloth — shows that the root has meant "wool" from the beginning of the attested record; this is a word that has hardly moved in six thousand years. The form itself is notable: the PIE word begins with the syllabic l (the *l̥ of *wl̥h1neh2), a consonant cluster that is characteristically Indo-European and that each daughter language resolved in its own way — the Slavic and Baltic languages keep the l, Greek and Latin vocalize it, and Germanic turns the whole cluster into wull. The Germanic outcome is a classic example of a regular sound change working on a difficult inherited cluster. Proto-Germanic *wullō continued directly into the West Germanic dialects: Old English wull (also woll), Old High German wolla (modern German Wolle), Dutch wol, and Old Norse ull. In Old English the word appears in the ordinary vocabulary of the rural economy — sheep-shearing, spinning, weaving, the wool trade — and in the legal and charter texts that regulate the countryside. Wool was one of the great commodities of medieval England, the basis of the cloth trade that made the island's medieval prosperity, and the word that named it carried real economic weight. Middle English regularized the spelling as wolle, wole, wool, and modern English wool is the direct descendant. The meaning is extraordinarily stable: wool has meant the soft, curly, greasy fiber of sheep from the earliest record to the present, and the phrase "wool and linen" from the Old Testament, carried through the English Bible, kept the word in the high-register religious vocabulary as well as in everyday speech. The suffix is the second half of the story. English -y derives from Old English -ig, which descends from Proto-Germanic *-ig(a)-, an adjective-forming suffix meaning "having, full of, characterized by." It is one of the most productive suffixes in the language — holy, guilty, sticky, hungry, thirsty, rainy, bony — and it does the ordinary work of turning nouns into adjectives. Old English -ig was already ancient and fully grammatical: the word for "holy" (halig) is literally "having godliness," and the adjective built from wull would have had the expected Old English form. Middle English reduced the unstressed vowel and regularized the spelling to -y, -i, or -ie, and by the early modern period the suffix had become the standard way of making "like X" adjectives. The union of the two native pieces — wool plus -y — is recorded from the sixteenth century: woolly means "made of wool, covered with wool, or resembling wool in being soft, curly, and fleece-like." The formation is not borrowed and not Latin; it is homegrown morphology of the oldest kind, two inherited Germanic pieces joined by the ordinary rules of the language. The adjective's later figurative life is a transfer of texture to thought. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, woolly had extended from the fleece to anything soft and loosely structured, and then — the decisive metaphor — to mental content: "woolly thinking," "woolly ideas," "a woolly argument" are vague, unclear, confused, as if wrapped in obscuring fluff. The same metaphor works in other languages and in other texture-words (fuzzy, fluffy), but woolly has a particular English fortune because of the early and intimate association of wool with cloud-like softness. A secondary technical niche is biological: "woolly" names hairy, fleece-like plant and animal forms — the woolly bear caterpillar, the woolly mammoth, woolly aphids, the woolly apple aphid of horticultural science, woolly willow — where the word is literal again, describing a surface of fine fibers. The noun use in clothing ("woollies," warm woolen garments) completes the range, returning the adjective to the cloth. The register of woolly spans the whole scale: it is the humble word for a lamb's coat, the affectionate word for a hobbit's foot-hair, the contemptuous word for a politician's vague promises, and the descriptive word in a naturalist's field guide. In The Hobbit, Bilbo Baggins's pipe reaches "nearly down to his woolly toes (neatly brushed)": the word carries the literal fleece-like sense, and the parenthetical "neatly brushed" does the comic work of reconciling hobbit animality with hobbit respectability. A philologist who knew the ancient PIE wool-root would have appreciated the joke: the softest, most ancient fiber of European agriculture sprouting from the toes of a fussy little gentlehobbit, tidy enough to pass inspection. The etymology is a straight line from a six-thousand-year-old sheep-farming root to a curly-haired foot in a fantasy novel, with a detour into the fog of "woolly thinking" on the way. "made of or like wool." - Figurative: "woolly thinking" (18th–19th c.): texture metaphor for vagueness. - Biological / technical: woolly bear, woolly mammoth, woolly aphid; nominal "woollies" in clothing. - Tolkien: "woolly toes (neatly brushed)": the literal fleece sense plus comic neatness.
