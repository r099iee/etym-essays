PIE root *per- (or *perh₁-), "to produce, procure, bring forth, make ready" (reconstructed; pre-3000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *parāō, "to make ready, prepare" (reconstructed; 2nd millennium BCE) → Latin parāre, "to make ready, prepare, provide, equip" (attested 3rd c. BCE; source of English prepare, repair, separate, apparatus, apparel) → Latin imperāre (in- "upon, against" + parāre), literally "to impose a command upon," "to order, command, rule" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE; originally the military command of a general, later supreme political rule) → Latin imperium, "command, authority, sovereign power, empire" (from imperāre; attested 2nd c. BCE) → Latin imperiōsus, "commanding, powerful, imperious, possessed of authority" (imperium + -ōsus "full of"; attested 1st c. BCE) → French impérieux, "imperious, haughty, overbearing" (borrowed from Latin imperiōsus, 15th c.) → English imperious, "imperial, commanding; haughty, domineering" (via French impérieux / Latin imperiōsus, 1540s) → imperious + adverbial suffix -ly → imperiously, "in an arrogant, domineering, overbearing manner" (attested late 16th c.) Note on debate: The PIE root is generally given as *per- / *perh₁- "to produce, procure" (the root of Latin parāre and English prepare), and imperāre is standardly analysed as in- + parāre. Older scholarship and some handbooks connected imperium instead to PIE *em- "to take, obtain"; modern consensus (e.g. de Vaan) derives imperō from in- + parō. The exact laryngeal shape of the root (*per-, *perh₁-) is uncertain and varies across reconstructions.
