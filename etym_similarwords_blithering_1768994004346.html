• babbling: Similar in senseless, meaningless talk; differs because it describes actual speech, while blithering has hardened into an intensive meaning "utter, hopeless" that need involve no talk at all.
  • driveling: Similar in foolish, contemptible blather; differs because it suggests weak, slobbering nonsense, while blithering adds a blast of abusive intensity.
  • gibbering: Similar in frantic, incoherent noise; differs because it evokes chattering, monkey-like sounds, while blithering is a contemptuous dismissal of idiocy rather than an imitation of it.
  • gabbling: Similar in rapid, senseless talk; differs because it stresses speed and volume of speech, while blithering now functions chiefly as an intensive adjective of contempt.
  • prattling: Similar in empty, childish chatter; differs because it suggests harmless, trivial small talk, while blithering is abusive and extreme.
  • jabbering: Similar in excited, unintelligible talk; differs because it is fast and agitated, while blithering is the settled, contemptuous verdict of foolishness.
  • blathering: Similar in foolish, wordy talk; differs because it is the direct sibling verb (blather/blether) still describing speech, while blithering has become the intensive adjective.
  • raving: Similar in wild, incoherent speech; differs because it implies frenzy or madness, while blithering implies mere idiocy, folly without passion.
  • utter: Similar in meaning "complete, absolute" as an intensifier; differs because it is neutral, without blithering's note of foolishness and contempt.
  • complete: Similar in meaning "total, entire" as an intensifier; differs because it is a plain, factual word with no abusive charge.
  • absolute: Similar in meaning "unqualified, sheer"; differs because it is formal and neutral, lacking the colloquial sneer of blithering.
  • idiotic: Similar in extreme foolishness; differs because it states the idiocy directly, while blithering both states it and intensifies the noun it modifies.
  • dithering: Similar in sound and in foolishness of behaviour; differs because it is a false friend — from Middle English dideren, "to tremble" — meaning vacillating indecision, unrelated to blithering.
