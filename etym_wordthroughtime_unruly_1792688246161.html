- [Late Middle English (c. 1400–1450)] — Coined as unreuly / unrewely, combining native Germanic affixes with the borrowed French root reule, applied to lawless barons, undisciplined monks, and rebellious subjects — [perception: a grave moral and civic fault denoting resistance to divine and feudal order]
- [Tudor & Elizabethan Era (16th c.)] — Heavily utilized in royal proclamations against vagabonds and "unruly mobs," while Renaissance playwrights (Shakespeare) extended it to tempestuous weather and uncontrollable erotic or psychological passions — [perception: a potent descriptor for both societal rioting and stormy interior emotions]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Neoclassicism] — Contrast-term for the Enlightenment ideals of decorum, geometric balance, and political order; applied pejoratively to unpolished verse, fanaticism, and wild nature — [perception: viewed with distaste by classicists as a failure of reason, proportion, and self-mastery]
- [Romantic Era (late 18th–19th c.)] — Re-evaluated and embraced by Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Byron) as an authentic mark of genius, poetic inspiration, and the uncontrollable natural sublime — [perception: transformed into an evocative badge of creative passion and untamed psychic energy]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Expanded into ubiquitous domestic collocations (unruly hair, unruly classrooms, unruly crowds), while retaining formal legal standing in public disturbance statutes — [perception: a flexible, evocative term ranging from minor cosmetic wildness to dangerous civil disorder]
