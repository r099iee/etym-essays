Evolution of “wielded.” Few verbs travel further — from a king's dominion over a people to a woman's influence over a philosopher — and yet the whole journey is one continuous image: the strong hand governing.

1. The Indo-European root. The chain begins with the PIE root *h₂welh₁-, “to be strong, to rule,” the same root that in Latin produced valere “to be strong, to be worth” and hence our valid, value, and prevail. In the Germanic branch the root gave the verb *waldaną, “to rule, to have power over,” which became the great Germanic word of rule — cognate with Gothic waldan, Old Norse valda, Old High German waltan “to rule, to govern,” and visible still in the name Waldemar and in the modern German Gewalt “power, force.”

2. Old English. Old English inherited this as wealdan, a strong verb of class VII meaning “to rule, to govern, to direct, to control,” a word of kings and lords. In Beowulf the formulaic language of dominion uses wealdan: the king “wields” the realm and the fate of his people; the phrase wyrd geweald names fate's governing power. At this stage the verb is purely about sovereignty and command — nothing yet of swords held in the hand, except that the king's authority was precisely a power over weapons and men.

3. Middle English narrowing. In Middle English, welden / wolden developed alongside the ruling sense a physical sense: to hold and handle a weapon or tool with the hand and use it effectively — to wield a sword, an axe, a staff. The narrowing is natural: the man who rules a realm is the man who can govern the sword in his hand. English, having lost much of the old royal vocabulary, kept wield chiefly in this manual sense, while French loanwords (govern, rule, command) took over the abstract politics of dominion.

4. Early Modern English: the return of power. In the 16th–17th centuries the word expanded again, carrying the martial physicality back into the political realm: to wield the sceptre, to wield power, to wield authority. Shakespeare and the dramatists use it of kingship and of the strong hand of command. The figurative sense preserves the old royal image but now through the weapon: power is something you hold, handle, and master as a warrior masters his blade. It is at this point that “wield influence” becomes possible — influence is a thing one can grasp and direct like a weapon.

5. The 19th century and the present context. By the 19th century the verb was standard in biography and journalism for the exercise of influence, control, and authority. In the present passage the biographer writes of “the influence which Mrs. Taylor wielded over his career”: Harriet Taylor directed the career of John Stuart Mill as a commander directs a campaign. The word flatters her with agency and mastery — her influence was deliberate, effective, and sovereign, not passive or accidental — and it quietly celebrates the intellectual partnership behind On Liberty.

6. The past form. The form wielded is the regular weak past and past participle, built with the Germanic dental suffix (Old English -ede/-ode), whose remote ancestor is the PIE participle suffix *-tó-. The past form frames the act as completed and recorded: she wielded that influence across his career, and the completed act is what the sentence reports.

  • PIE *h₂welh₁- “to be strong, to rule” → PGmc *waldaną “to rule” → OE wealdan “to rule, govern” (Beowulf) → OE wieldan (variant) → ME welden “to govern; to handle a weapon” → ModE wield “to handle effectively; to exercise power” → wield + -ed = wielded.
  • Semantic arc: royal dominion (OE) → manual handling of weapons/tools (ME) → figurative exercise of power, influence, and authority (16th–17th c.) → modern journalism's “wield power / wield influence / wield a pen.”
  • Register: forceful and literary; collocations with power, influence, authority, sceptre, pen, sword; connotation of skilled mastery and command.
