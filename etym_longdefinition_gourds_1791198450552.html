gourds is the plural of gourd: hard-rinded fruits of trailing or climbing plants of the family Cucurbitaceae, especially the bottle-gourd and ornamental squashes, and by extension the dried, hollowed shells of those fruits used as vessels, utensils, or ornaments.

At its core, the word names both nature and artifact. A gourd growing on the vine is a fleshy berry with a hard exocarp when mature; a gourd on the table is a bowl, ladle, flask, rattle, or decoration. This duality explains the source sentence, where gourds stand alongside ladles, spoons, vases, plates, and cups as containers fashioned from natural materials. Early humans did not need pottery to have pottery-shapes: a cut and dried gourd already provides a waterproof bottle, scoop, or dish.

Botanically and practically, the term covers:

  • Bottle-gourd / calabash (Lagenaria siceraria): the Old World gourd, among the earliest domesticated plants worldwide, with elongated, bottle, or dumbbell shapes ideal for flasks, dippers, and floats. When dried, the pulp disappears and the rind becomes wood-like and watertight.
  • Squash, pumpkin, and ornamental gourds (Cucurbita spp.): New World species including field pumpkins, acorn squash, and warty ornamental gourds sold for autumn decoration. Edible when young, hard-shelled and decorative when mature.
  • Sponge / luffa gourd (Luffa spp.): whose fibrous interior becomes a bath sponge when the skin and seeds are removed.
  • Gourd as vessel: any hollowed gourd used as bottle, canteen, ladle, pipe-bowl (calabash pipe), musical resonator (sitar, kora, maraca, shekere), birdhouse, or float for fishing nets.
  • Gourd as slang for head: informal American use, as in "use your gourd," likening the rounded skull to a pumpkin-shell, often humorous rather than insulting.

Further senses and nuances include:

  • Culinary use: young bottle-gourds and bitter gourds are cooked as vegetables in African, Indian, Chinese, and Caribbean cuisines; mature ornamental gourds are generally inedible and bitter from cucurbitacins.
  • Canteen / flask sense: inherited from French gourde meaning pilgrim's water-flask; in hiking and military history a gourd-canteen is a dried gourd fitted with stopper and strap.
  • Musical and ritual objects: gourd rattles in Indigenous American and African ceremonies; water-drums and thumb pianos with gourd resonators; decorated ceremonial bowls.
  • Fishing and maritime use: dried gourds as net-floats and buoys before cork and plastic, and as bailers.
  • Decorative / harvest symbolism: piles of small colorful gourds signify autumn, Thanksgiving, and rural abundance in North America and Europe.
  • Medical-historical echo: cupping-glass once called cucurbitula in Latin from resemblance to a little gourd; dry-cupping instruments retain the shape.
  • Idiomatic extensions: "gourdful" for a gourd's contents; compound forms like gourd-vine, gourd-worm, gourd-dipper, gourd-art.

The semantic bridge uniting all these senses is shape-plus-hardness: a rounded, hollowable, lightweight, waterproof rind. Because the plant freely offers a container-shape, humans named the container after the plant, then extended the name to any similar squash-fruit, then to the head by visual metaphor, and to the flask by functional equivalence. To call something a gourd is thus to evoke natural ingenuity, frugality, rural craft, and pre-industrial making, where art begins where nature leaves off and a fruit becomes furniture.
