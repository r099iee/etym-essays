Noisomeness is an abstract noun designating the condition, quality, or character of being deeply offensive, pestilential, foul-smelling, or injurious to health and well-being. At its most visceral and concrete sensory level, it refers to unbearable, putrid odors and noxious vapors—the stench of rotting organic matter, stagnant cesspools, decomposing carcasses, and unventilated dunghills. At a secondary medical and environmental level, it characterizes unwholesome, disease-bearing atmospheres that sicken those who inhale them. Metaphorically and ethically, it describes moral corruption, revolting wickedness, or vile aesthetic depravity that nauseates the conscience and sensibilities of an observer. The word commands a formidable presence in the lexicon: it does not merely denote unpleasantness, but active, repulsive loathsomeness that forces physical revulsion.

In the source passage from the Preface to John Ruskin’s Modern Painters, the word is deployed with devastating rhetorical brilliance: ‘The envious and incompetent have usually been the leaders of attack, content if, like the foulness of the earth, they may attract to themselves notice by their noisomeness, or, like its insects, exalt themselves by virulence into visibility.’ Ruskin constructs a blistering organic allegory of malicious art criticism. The petty, untalented critic possesses no luminous beauty, technical mastery, or intellectual virtue with which to win public admiration. Unable to shine like gold or soar like an eagle, the critic accepts the role of terrestrial offal: content to be a pile of foul manure or rotting refuse simply because its unbearable stench compels passersby to turn their heads. Noisomeness is thus diagnosed as the desperate substitute for greatness—the intentional weaponization of disgust to achieve notoriety.

Morphology and the Pseudo-Noise Misconception: A crucial lexicographical point regarding noisomeness is its complete lack of etymological connection to acoustic noise. While everyday modern speakers frequently assume that noisome must mean clamorous or loud due to superficial orthographic resemblance, the word is actually an aphetic derivative of Middle English noy (a shortened form of anoy, meaning grief, harm, or vexation, from Old French anoier). By compounding noy with the native Germanic suffix -some (producing or characterized by), English created the adjective noisome (‘injurious, noxious, foul’), which was subsequently nominalized with the abstract suffix -ness. Acoustic noise, by contrast, descends from Latin nausea (seasickness). Thus, etymologically, noisomeness is literally ‘annoy-someness’ in its ancient, intense sense of grievous injury and revulsion.

Exhaustive Exploration of Senses:

  • Olfactory Putrefaction and Malodor (Primary Sensory Sense): The state of exhaling a suffocating, rank, or fetid stench. Characteristic environments include open sewers, slaughterhouse disposal pits, bilge water, and stagnant marshes. The semantic bridge to other senses is immediate: foul odors were historically regarded across human cultures as the primary sensory warning sign of biological danger and decaying death.
  • Pestilential and Unwholesome Harm (Medical and Historical Miasma): The capability of corrupting bodily health through toxic vapors or diseased climates. Throughout Renaissance and Victorian medical treatises, plague pits and swamp mists were condemned for their pestilential noisomeness. Under pre-bacteriological miasma theory, bad smells were not merely symptoms of decay, but the active contagion itself.
  • Moral, Spiritual, and Ethical Abhorrence (Theological and Figurative Sense): Deep spiritual depravity, corrupt venality, or shameless vice that offends divine law and human dignity. In biblical commentaries and seventeenth-century Puritan sermons, sin is frequently depicted as a rotting sore whose moral noisomeness stinks in the nostrils of Heaven.
  • Critical, Stylistic, and Intellectual Degradation (Rhetorical and Critical Sense): As exemplified by Ruskin, the rancid, spiteful hostility of incompetent critics and malicious polemicists whose writings exhibit an offensive, sickening degradation of public discourse.

Register and Connotation: Noisomeness belongs to the highest literary, historical, and philosophical registers of English. It appears prominently in early modern translations of scripture, Jacobean drama, Victorian prose, and formal jurisprudence. It conveys a grave, dignified, yet physically nauseating weight. Unlike common slang terms for bad smells, noisomeness elevates sensory revulsion into a serious philosophical and aesthetic critique, condemning its subject as both physically offensive and spiritually corrupt.
