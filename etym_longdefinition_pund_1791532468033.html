pund is the Old English noun that Modern English spells and pronounces pound. It named two things that began as one: a unit of weight and a unit of money. The word was borrowed from Latin pondus "weight" into Germanic before the Anglo-Saxons settled in Britain, and it is attested in English from the earliest documentary age. Its biography therefore compresses the whole story of Roman commerce, Germanic migration, minting and English law into four letters.

Primary sense: a unit of weight

In Old English pund was above all a measure of mass used in markets, kitchens, monasteries and mints. The Anglo-Saxon pound was not the modern avoirdupois pound: it was probably reckoned at twelve ounces, the unit later formalised in England as the Tower pound. The word could be used of any weighed commodity — grain, wool, wax, iron, silver — and it frequently appears in charters and law codes beside other measures. Because pre-modern weights were local, a pund was less an absolute physical constant than a legal and commercial convention enforced by a lord or a borough.

  • Market weight — the quantity used when selling staple goods; the word implied fair dealing and official standardisation.
  • Mint weight — the amount of silver from which a fixed number of coins was struck, which is why the weight word and the money word are the same.
  • Medical and culinary weight — in recipes and remedy books, pund specified quantities of herbs, honey or flour.
  • Tribute and taxation — royal and ecclesiastical dues were assessed in pounds, making the word part of the vocabulary of government.

Secondary sense: a sum of money

Because early medieval coins were valued by their precious-metal content, pund naturally came to mean a pound's worth of silver, and then the standard monetary unit built on that quantity. The two senses were never fully separable in the medieval mind: to owe a pound was to owe a pound of silver, and the king's coinage was trusted because it contained what it said it contained.

  • The accounting pound — in England one pound came to equal twenty shillings, or 240 pence, a ratio fixed in the Carolingian and later English systems.
  • Pound sterling — the name of the currency, with sterling possibly referring to the "Easterling" silver of German merchants.
  • The sign £ — a stylised capital L for Latin libra "scales, pound", the Latin word that named the Roman weight; English kept the symbol even though it borrowed a different word for the thing.
  • Punds and pence in law — Old English legal texts impose fines in pounds, shillings and pence, showing the monetary sense fully established before the Norman Conquest.

The modern reflex: pound in science and trade

Modern English pound, the direct descendant of pund, has multiplied into a family of precise technical units. Each one preserves the ancient idea of a standard quantity against which other quantities are measured.

  • Avoirdupois pound — 453.59237 grams, the everyday imperial and US customary pound of sixteen ounces.
  • Troy pound — 373.2417 grams, used for precious metals and derived from the medieval troy weight system.
  • Pound-force (lbf) — the force exerted by one avoirdupois pound under standard gravity.
  • Poundal — a now-rare unit of force in the foot-pound-second system.
  • Foot-pound — a unit of energy or torque, still common in engineering.
  • Pounds per square inch (psi) — the pressure unit of tyres, boilers and hydraulics.

Figurative and idiomatic extensions

The weight sense generated a rich figurative life. A "pound of flesh" (Shakespeare, The Merchant of Venice) is a debt exacted with legal cruelty; "in for a penny, in for a pound" urges total commitment once a course is begun; "penny-wise and pound-foolish" contrasts small thrift with large waste. In British retail a "pound shop" is a fixed-price discount store, and "the pound in your pocket" was a famous phrase in the 1967 devaluation debate. These idioms all depend on the pound as a concrete, familiar quantity that can stand for value, obligation or risk.

Homographs that are not related

Three English words look like pund but have different origins and must not be confused with it:

  • To pound "to strike repeatedly" — from Old English punian "to beat, crush", of Germanic origin.
  • A pound "enclosure, pen" — from Old English pyndan "to shut in", related to pund only by accidental spelling.
  • Pound in place names such as Poundbury — usually from the enclosure sense, not the weight.

Semantic bridge

The whole development rests on the balance scale. A thing is placed in one pan, standard weights in the other; the standard weight is a pondus; the silver that balances it is worth a pondus; and the sum of coins that contains that much silver is a pound. Weight becomes value, value becomes currency, and currency becomes idiom. pund preserves the first step of that chain in its oldest English form.
