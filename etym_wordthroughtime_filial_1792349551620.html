- [Late 14th c.] - borrowed from Late Latin / Old French into Middle English by Chaucer and contemporary religious writers, strictly denoting the relation of a child to a parent or the theological relationship of Christ the Son to God the Father - [perception: revered as a holy, solemn virtue of spiritual obedience and familial obligation]
- [16th–17th c.] - secularized during the English Renaissance and Reformation to describe classical filial duty and civic duty toward patriarchal rulers and fatherland - [perception: celebrated as the fundamental bedrock of social hierarchy, domestic stability, and monarchical authority]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Era] - central to sentimental literature and Victorian domestic morality, emphasizing emotional tenderness, filial sacrifice, and devotion to aging parents - [perception: regarded as the ultimate hallmark of a refined moral sensibility and honorable character]
- [1900s–1910s] - adopted as a technical term in Mendelian genetics (William Bateson) to designate successive generations of offspring (F1, F2 filial generations) - [perception: expanded into rigorous biological taxonomy while maintaining its classical moral sense in humanities]
- [Late 20th–21st c.] - extensively utilized in cross-cultural sociology, anthropology, and East Asian studies to translate Confucian xiào (filial piety), as well as in corporate law for corporate subsidiaries (from French filiale) - [perception: recognized globally as an elevated, culturally rich descriptor of generational reverence and ethical duty]
