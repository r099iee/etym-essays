Larch is a survivor of a lost language. The tree's name first appears in the classical Mediterranean as Latin larix (genitive laricis), used by Pliny the Elder for the great timber tree of the Alps, and as Greek lárix (λάριξ), which denotes the larch or its resin. Neither Latin nor Greek shows a plausible internal derivation: the word obeys no Indo-European word-formation rule, which is why scholars agree that larix was borrowed from an unknown pre-Roman language of the Alpine region — one of the shadowy 'substrate' tongues of pre-Indo-European Europe, of which only scattered plant names and place-names survive. There is therefore no PIE ancestry for larch: the tree's name is a fossil of a non-Indo-European speech community, a fragment of a language that died before history wrote it down. The very existence of two distinct substrate names for the tree — Latin larix and the Gaulish base behind French mélèze — suggests that several vanished tongues of the Alps each had their own word for this conspicuous tree, and only chance has preserved this one.

From Latin the name travelled along two channels into the Germanic world. In the Romance channel it passed into Old French as larice or lariçe, crossed the Channel into Middle English in the fourteenth century as lerge, larech, or larch — the French sibilant hardening to -ch in English mouths — and settled into the modern spelling larch by the sixteenth century. In the Germanic channel, Latin larix entered Old High German as lariha, producing German Lärche and Dutch lork, a parallel borrowing independent of the English one; the Italian larice continues the Latin word directly in Romance soil. Each of these paths is a separate branch on the tree, but all descend from the same Alpine fossil. The botanical genus name Larix, fixed by Linnaeus in 1753, preserves the ancient word for science, and the adjective laricine derives from it — so the lost Alpine name lives on in laboratories and herbaria.

The English history of the word is inseparable from the history of British forestry. Though the larch was known to botanists from the sixteenth century, it entered the landscape of the British Isles in earnest in the eighteenth century, when highland estates — above all the Duke of Atholl's plantings in Scotland from the 1730s — planted larch on a large scale for timber. The species proved hardy, fast-growing, and extraordinarily durable in damp conditions, and its wood became prized for shipbuilding, mining props, railway sleepers, and fencing, so that larch acquired a firmly practical, commercial register alongside its botanical one. In North America the same tree, or its close relative, went by the Algonquian name tamarack, and the English word therefore divides the genus by region: larch for the European and Asian trees, tamarack for the American. The Siberian larch, famed for its resistance to rot and insects, carried the name to the edge of the taiga and into the timber trade.

Key stages and attestations:

  • Pre-Roman substrate (unattested) — native Alpine name *larix-; no PIE ancestry; the word's ultimate source is a lost non-Indo-European language.
  • 4th c. BCE — Greek lárix 'the larch, its resin', the earliest literary record; Theophrastus mentions the tree's resin.
  • 1st c. AD — Latin larix, used by Pliny the Elder for Alpine timber; the word is already a loan.
  • 14th c. — Middle English lerge/larech/larch from Old French larice; the -ch spelling begins to win.
  • 16th c. — modern spelling larch fixed.
  • 18th c. — mass planting in British highland estates; the word gains its commercial, timber-register sense.
  • 1753 — Linnaeus fixes Larix as the genus name, fossilizing the Alpine word in science.

Socio-culturally, larch has always carried the paradox of a conifer that is not evergreen. That paradox made it a curiosity to Pliny, a signature of the Scottish highlands in the eighteenth century, a byword for durable timber in the industrial age, and — in Tolkien — the perfect storybook tree: an enormous, bare, alpine conifer that can still be imagined as a Christmas tree. From a lost Alpine language through Greek, Latin, and French into modern English, the word has never changed its reference: a tall deciduous conifer of the mountains, shedding its golden needles and re-greening with the spring.
