- [Middle English blestre (13th c.)] - From Old French, first naming a swelling or pustule on the skin filled with fluid; a plain medical–domestic word describing an ailment, carrying a neutral, uneasy physical register.
- [Extension to materials (17th c. onward)] - Broadens to any raised, bubble-like flaw — blisters in paint, glass, or metal caused by heat or defect; the word gains a faintly technical, craftsman's flavour alongside its bodily sense.
- [Figurative & proverbial use (19th–20th c.)] - Turns figurative: "blistering" for scorching heat or fierce criticism, and the proverb that words cannot "raise a blister"; the hurt stays superficial, skin-deep, stinging yet minor, and the register remains coarse, physical, and unglamorous.
- [Contemporary sense] - Both literal (the fluid bubble of friction, scourge of ill-fitting shoes) and metaphorical (a blistering pace, a blistering attack) stay common; the word is casual, vivid, and always tied to stinging heat or a minor, surface-level hurt.
