• Languedoc: similar as the neighbouring Occitan-speaking south of France with a rich medieval culture of its own; differs in being the western part of the Occitan world, shaped by the counts of Toulouse, while Provence looked east toward Italy and Greece and belonged to a different political line.
  • Occitania: similar as the wider linguistic-cultural zone of the langue d'oc; differs in that Occitania is a modern cultural label for the whole south of France, while Provence is the ancient political-geographic name of the southeast corner.
  • The Midi: similar as the colloquial name for the south of France; differs in covering far more than Provence and in being informal and contemporary rather than historical.
  • Côte d'Azur: similar as the glamorous coastal strip of modern Provence; differs in being a 19th-century tourist coinage limited to the coast, with none of the inland history and no medieval significance.
  • Dauphiné: similar as the region immediately northeast of Provence with a separate medieval identity; differs in being Alpine, tied to the dolphin-counts (dauphin), and outside the Roman "province" tradition.
  • Riviera: similar as the Mediterranean resort zone; differs in running from Marseille toward Genoa and belonging as much to Italy as to France, with no troubadour or Roman-province resonance.
  • Aquitaine: similar as another great medieval region of southern France connected with troubadour culture; differs in being Atlantic-facing, centred on Bordeaux and Poitiers, with a different history of duchy and Plantagenet rule.
  • Marseille: similar as the great port city of Provence, often used to stand for the region; differs in being a single city — and a very particular one, distinct from the hill-country Provence of the poets.
