Part I — The “Up” Element: PIE *upó to English “up”

The first ingredient of upshot is one of the smallest yet most productive words ever to emerge from the Indo-European word-hoard: the particle *upó, reconstructed for Proto-Indo-European around 4000–3000 BCE with the meaning “under, below, and up from under.” A particle of this kind did not name a thing or an action; it plotted position and direction, locating an object or an event in space relative to a speaker. Its peculiar genius was to encode a double viewpoint — “down below, but emerging upward” — so that from a single base the daughter languages could build a whole family of “up-ness” and “over-ness” words: Greek hupó (“under, beneath”), whose combining form hypo- still enters English science through hypothesis, hypodermic, and hypothermia; Latin sub (“under”), the fountainhead of the vast English sub- prefix (subject, subway, submarine, sublime); Vedic Sanskrit úpa (“to, toward, near”), which colours a thousand Indian compounds and survives in modern Hindi upa-; and, in the Germanic branch, the zero-grade *upp that lies behind English up itself.

Sound change and form. The family’s flexibility came from ablaut, the vowel-grading system of Indo-European. The full-grade form *uper (“over, above”) underlies Greek hupér, Latin super, and the English prefix super- and the Latin-derived summit (via summus, literally “highest”); the zero-grade *up- stripped the vowel down and produced the plain particle. In the Germanic branch this zero-grade hardened into *upp, with a geminate consonant that signals a firmly reduced, high-frequency form. From Proto-Germanic *upp come Old English upp and up — recorded in the great manuscripts of the Anglo-Saxon period, used already as adverb (“upward, aloft”), preposition, and adjective — and the whole family of Scandinavian and Continental cognates: Old Norse upp, Danish op, Swedish upp, Dutch op, German auf (from Old High German uf), Gothic iup.

Semantic drift of “up”. The original Indo-European meaning, “from under and up,” retained a trace of its “under” half in Greek and Latin, where hupó and sub settled on “under.” The Germanic branch took the opposite, rising half: *upp and Old English up mean “upward, on top, above.” That split is one of the most instructive accidents of the Indo-European family: the same particle that gives English under-derived words on the one hand and up-words on the other. In English, up then began its astonishing career as the most polyvalent particle in the language: a simple adverb of direction (“he looked up”), a preposition of position (“up the hill”), an intensifier of completed action (“eat up, drink up, use up”), a builder of phrasal verbs with meanings far from the literal (“give up, take up, make up, put up with”), and even a verb — to up and leave, to up the stakes. Its meaning has ballooned from “rising from below” to “any completion, activation, increase, or movement,” and it remains the highest-frequency spatial particle of Modern English, as ordinary and indispensable as the air.

Sociocultural life. Because up was everywhere, it slipped into compounds constantly. When Tudor England’s archery culture needed a name for the final arrow of a match — the shot that rises and closes the contest — the ordinary adverb did the job. The low-register, workaday character of the particle is precisely what gives upshot its authentic, native English feel: it is built entirely of Germanic material, no Latin or French loan, which is why it sounds so plain and so conclusive.

Part II — The “Shot” Element: PIE *skeud- to English “shot”

The second ingredient is the shooting root. PIE *skeud-, “to shoot, hurl, throw, chase” (set by Pokorny), passed into the Germanic branch as the strong verb *skeutaną “to shoot, to hurl,” the ancestor of Old English scēotan “to shoot, dart, fly swiftly.” The Germanic verb is a class-II strong verb with the characteristic ablaut pattern (scēat–scuton–scoten), and its nominal formations produced the noun “shot.” Old English had ġescēot and sceot, “a shot, a shooting, a discharge,” which became Middle English shot, “a shot; the discharge of a missile; a projectile.”

Sound change. The most important phonetic event is the palatalisation of Germanic *k before a front vowel: in Old English, *skeut- became scēotan with the sound now written sh, so that English shoot and shot begin with /ʃ/ where the Continental cousins kept the hard /sk/ or /s/ — German schießen and Schuss, Dutch schieten and schot, Icelandic skjóta, Swedish skjuta, Danish skyde, Frisian sjitte. The Baltic branch preserved the same root with a striking semantic innovation: Lithuanian skaudėti means “to hurt, to ache,” and skaudus “painful,” capturing the “shooting, stinging” quality of pain — the identical ballistic metaphor transferred to bodily sensation. This Baltic development, standard in the handbooks, shows the root’s deep antiquity and its ready metaphoric extension.

Semantic explosion of “shot”. From its medieval base the noun shot exploded in every direction of English life: the discharge of a bow or firearm; the missile itself; a single attempt or chance (“to give it a shot”); a sharp remark (“a parting shot”); a photograph and the act of photographing; a single injection or dose (a shot in the arm); a small measure of strong liquor (a shot of whiskey); the flight of a rocket or spacecraft (blast off, the shot heard round the world); and, in sports, an attempt to score. The word became so elastic that it now means almost any single, discrete, forceful instance of action, while its ballistic core — something fired or thrown — still glimmers beneath every use.

The fusion of 1530. Around 1530 the two halves were fused in the hunting and archery culture of Tudor England: “upshot” was the final arrow fired in a match, the shot that decided the contest. Within forty years the physical image was generalised: by 1570 the word already meant “the final result or outcome of a whole affair,” as if a long sequence of events were an archery round and the last event its decisive arrow. This is the shift that matters — from the concrete trajectory of a single missile to the abstract verdict of a whole process — and it is the classic pattern by which an English compound grows a figurative sense and lets its literal one die.

Modern survival. In modern use the literal archery sense is long extinct, and “upshot” survives only as a summing-up word: “the upshot is that…,” “the upshot of the negotiations.” It carries a faint, pleasant archaism, a whiff of the 1570s, which is why C. S. Lewis’s Nikabrik can deploy it with such grim authority. When he says “I suppose the upshot is that we have to keep it a prisoner for life,” the reader feels the whole argument close like a hand shutting — the final arrow of the debate landing exactly where he has aimed it.
