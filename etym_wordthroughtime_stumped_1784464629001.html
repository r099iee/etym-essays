“Stumped” first described walking stiffly and heavily, as though on wooden legs or the truncated stumps of amputated limbs — a sound-painting verb for a hard, clumping, faintly absurd gait.
“Stumped” leapt to public life in 19th-century American politics: to be “on the stump” meant campaigning, from haranguing crowds while standing on a tree-stump, giving the word an energetic political career.
“Stumped” branched at the same time in British slang — “stump up” meant paying reluctantly, and a baffling question could leave a speaker “stumped,” blocked and speechless as if rooted to a stump.
“Stumped” today is most often heard meaning “baffled or at a loss,” while the walking sense survives in period and comic prose — exactly where Tolkien’s Bilbo clumps off in offended dignity.
