bosom is one of the great survivors of the poetic vocabulary of the body. It is an ordinary Old English noun for the front of the chest, and over a thousand years it has acquired, one layer at a time, a set of figurative uses that are now more important than the literal one: the heart, the inmost or sheltered part of anything, the embrace of a loved one, and, in a handful of fixed phrases, the word for the closest kind of friendship. In the quoted context the word is used attributively and rather unusually, in the sense close, intimate, in constant companionship, which is the adjective use preserved today only in expressions such as bosom friend.

1. The literal and physical senses.

  • The chest, the breast, the front of the torso. The original and still fully ordinary sense, especially of a woman’s breast, where the word is distinctly literary and distinctly decorous compared with the neutral breast or the medical chest.
  • The hollow, dip, or sheltering place. The oldest figurative extension and the one the language built on: the concavity of the chest as a model for any sheltered or enclosed hollow, and in particular for the bosom of the earth, a deep valley or sheltered place between hills. The phrase is biblical, and it survives in the language of Romantic and Georgian poetry as a place of refuge.
  • The lap. By the same physical logic, the front of the body on which a child or a book may rest; the word lap in phrases such as ‘in the lap of the gods’ is a near neighbour, and the two words have been used almost interchangeably in older English.
  • The inner or concave face. A technical use that survives in the vocabulary of crafts, metalwork and heraldry: the concave inner side of a shield is its bosom, and to dash or beat a surface into a hollow is to bosom it. The verb to bosom, now obsolete, meant to embrace, to hold in the arms, and, figuratively, to cherish or keep close to one’s heart.
  • The chest part of a garment, and a pocket in it. In older clothing vocabulary, the bosom of a shirt or coat is the part covering the chest, and a bosom pocket is an inside pocket; both senses are obsolete but perfectly normal in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose.

2. The figurative and figurative-physical senses.

  • The heart, the inner feelings, the conscience. The single most important development: a thing done in the bosom of a person is done in the heart, and a secret kept in the bosom is a secret of the heart. From this come the puritanical phrase bosom sins, the sins of the private life, and the general sense of a person’s deepest feelings.
  • The innermost, sheltered, or central part of anything. The word transfers to a family, a church, a nation, a literature, a landscape or a season: the bosom of the family, the bosom of the Church, the bosom of the earth, the bosom of summer woods. The meaning is always the same picture: the part that is enclosed, protected, and most closely held.
  • Close and constant companionship, as a protection. In the bosom of also means inside the shelter of, as in in the bosom of his family, in the bosom of the Church, in the bosom of plenty, where the sense is of being enclosed and looked after rather than of being emotionally intimate.
  • An embrace, an act of holding to the chest. The concrete source of all the above, surviving in falling into a bosom, in the kiss given on the bosom, and in the verb bosom.
  • The divine embrace, in theology. The bosom of God, the bosom of the Father, and the New Testament’s image of the bosom of Abraham: the word carries real doctrinal weight, since the fourth-century creeds speak of the Son being received into the Father’s bosom, and English religious writing inherits that language wholesale.
  • A secret society’s meeting place. In Freemasonry and similar bodies a bosom is a concealed lodge or meeting chamber, the place where the mysteries are kept, from the idea of an inner and secret place; the word survives in this technical sense in the vocabulary of the craft.
  • An inner circle, a select body. The bosom of a family, of a party or of a patron, meaning the small group nearest the centre and most trusted.

3. The attributive adjective, and the word in the sentence.

  • Bosom as an adjective: close, intimate, dearest. The oldest fixed use, dating from the fifteenth century, is bosom friend, a friend so close that the phrase has become the standard English expression for the highest degree of friendship. The same attributive use appears in bosom companion, in bosom enemy (a rare older phrase for an intimate enemy), and in the sentence quoted, where in any way bosom means in any way close or intimate.
  • Beloved, cherished. Attributively the word can also mean simply dear: a person carried in another’s bosom is a cherished person.
  • The word in the sentence. ‘It wasn’t as if he and I were in any way bosom’ uses the adjectival sense: the narrator is denying that the two of them were close, and the denial is deliberately formal and slightly comic, since bosom is a much grander word than close or intimate. A speaker using it in this way is being arch or amused rather than cold.
  • The survival of the word. The literal sense survives in the language of the body and of blouses and bodices, the figurative sense survives almost entirely in fixed phrases, and the general adjective use survives only in a few compounds. Bosom is now a literary and archaic word in every one of its lives, and the ordinary English words that have taken its work are breast, heart, embrace, inmost part, recess and close friend.
  • Points of conflict. Bosom is easily confused with bosom the heraldic hollow, with boss the raised metalwork, and with bust the older word for the chest. In American usage the word also survives in the language of blouses and shirts as the fabric covering the chest, which is a small thing but a real living sense.
