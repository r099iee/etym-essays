Chain for THEATRICAL (Pre-Greek substrate; NO PIE ancestry): Stage 1 — Pre-Greek *theā "a view, a sight, a spectacle" (the base noun; Robert Beekes: "No IE cognates"; the word is Pre-Greek, as its varying forms théa, théā prove). No PIE root can be reconstructed. Stage 2 — Greek theâsthai "to behold, to view" (the verb built on theā; the sight-word becomes a seeing-verb). Stage 3 — Greek théātron (θέατρον) "a place for viewing; the spectators; the spectacle" (Classical Greek, 5th c. BCE; formed with the instrumental-place suffix *-tron, "a place for doing X" — literally "a seeing-place"). Stage 4 — Latin theatrum "play-house, stage, the audience" (2nd c. BCE, borrowed from Greek théātron with the institution). Stage 5 — Latin theatrālis "of the theatre" (Late Latin adjective; theatrum + -ālis, "pertaining to"). Final node — English THEATRICAL (1550s; borrowed from Latin theatrālis, the English adjective formed on the same Latin stem as theater; the noun theater/theatre had entered English earlier, late 14th c., via Old French théâtre). Note: this is a non-PIE chain. Beekes states plainly that théā has no Indo-European cognates and is a substrate (Pre-Greek) loan, so no Sanskrit, Hindi, Odia, or Germanic branch exists. The English noun theater comes from Latin theatrum via Old French; the adjective theatrical is a direct formation on the Latin stem in the 16th c. Sister words from the same Greek family: amphitheater (Latin amphitheatrum < Greek amphithéātron), theory (Greek theōría "contemplation, a viewing" < theōrós "spectator"), theorem (theṓrēma), thaumaturge (thaûma "wonder" — a thing to look at). No stages are invented; the attested record runs Pre-Greek *theā → Greek theâsthai → théātron → Latin theatrum → theatrālis → English.
