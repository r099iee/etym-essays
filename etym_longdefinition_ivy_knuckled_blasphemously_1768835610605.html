ivy — In its primary sense, ivy names the woody, evergreen climbing vine Hedera helix (and its close relatives in the genus Hedera), native to Europe, western Asia, and northern Africa. It attaches itself to walls, trunks, and rock faces by short adhesive aerial rootlets — not by tendrils and not by twining stems — which lets it scale vertical surfaces and cling to a single host for decades or even centuries. Because it keeps its dark, leathery, five-angled leaves through the winter, it has served, from the classical to the Victorian world, as the emblem of fidelity, constancy, and undying attachment: the ivy that outlives the oak. In the passage being corrected, however, the word is deliberately estranged: the ivy is “black,” it patches the tower “unevenly,” and it cooperates with the “mutilated finger” and the “fists of masonry” to make the whole tower read as a damaged, dying body. Because ivy grips the masonry itself, its presence silently testifies that the tower has gone long unwatched, unpointed, and unmaintained — that time is steadily consuming it. The green badge of constancy is turned here into a black badge of slow, creeping ruin.
  • Botanical primary sense: the evergreen climber Hedera helix, grown wild on trees and walls or cultivated as ground-cover, with numerous variegated ornamental cultivars.
  • Emblematic sense: fidelity, constancy, tenacity — drawn from the evergreen leaf and the unrelenting grip; sacred to Bacchus and the garland of poets in antiquity, still used in funerary and decorative motifs.
  • Gothic-Romantic sense: the companion of ruins — “ivy-clad abbey,” “ivy-mantled tower” — encoding melancholy, decrepitude, and dignified neglect, precisely the atmosphere the passage requires.
  • Modern technical and colloquial extensions: “poison ivy” (Toxicodendron radicans, a dermatitis-causing shrub of a different family); “Ivy League” (the elite north-eastern US universities whose ivied masonry became a brand of old prestige); “English ivy” as an invasive nuisance that strips mortar and harbours damp.
  • Grammatical and phraseological notes: ivy is normally a mass noun, but “ivies” is used of kinds and cultivars; the adjectival forms are “ivied” and “ivy-clad”; stock phrases include “to cling like ivy.”
knuckled — The adjective knuckled derives from knuckle, the bony knob at the base of a finger or toe (anatomically a metacarpophalangeal or interphalangeal joint). As an adjective it means furnished with knuckles or marked by prominent knob-like projections: knotted, jointed, gnarled, bone-like. In the passage the “fists of knuckled masonry” are exactly this — masses of stone that bulge and jut like the clenched knuckles of a fist, so that the fortress wall is given a skeleton and a gesture, and made to grope and fist-clench upward like a body. The word personifies the masonry at the very moment it fossilises it.
  • Primary anatomical sense: the knuckles as the visible, bony joints of the hand; “knuckled” = having knuckles, as in “bare-knuckled” (ungloved, hence brutal and direct).
  • Adjectival, geological extension: any surface or structure marked by knob-like protrusions — knuckled stone, knuckled roots, knuckled vertebrae — where the word imports knottiness, gnarled texture, and an almost skeletal prominence.
  • Verbal idioms: to “knuckle under” (submit, yield — from the gesture of bending the knuckles in submission), to “knuckle down” (apply oneself earnestly — from the game of marbles, where the player sets the knuckles to the ground), to “rap someone’s knuckles” (punish mildly).
  • Register note: “knuckled” is now chiefly literary and concrete; in modern speech it survives mainly in the fixed phrases “bare-knuckled” and “knuckle-duster,” where it carries violence and defiance rather than simple anatomy.
blasphemously — Blasphemously is the adverb of blasphemous: in a manner that speaks evil of, or shows irreverence toward, what is sacred — profanely, sacrilegiously, implously. Etymologically it carries Greek blasphēmos “speaking evil” (blapsis “injury, harm” + phēmē “speech, report”) plus the English adverbial suffix -ly, and the word preserves that structure: to speak blasphemously is to speak in a way that injures sacred repute. In the passage, the tower “pointed blasphemously at heaven”: the mute finger of stone performs a gesture of defiance and profanity, accusing or insulting the heavens with the mere shape of its ruin. The word colours the entire image — the blighted tower is not merely broken but actively hostile to the divine, an upraised, insulting digit.
  • Core religious sense: irreverently, in a manner that curses, mocks, or slanders God or sacred things; also the legal frame of the crime of blasphemy (blasphemous libel).
  • Extended colloquial sense: defiantly, shockingly, or irreverently toward any hallowed institution, value, or custom — “blasphemously expensive,” a “blasphemously bad performance.”
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: “sacrilegiously” implies violation of holy objects or places; “profanely” implies coarse secularity rather than a directed insult; “implously” implies want of piety; “blasphemously” specifically targets speech that injures sacred repute.
Read together, the three words make the tower a living, insulting, decaying body: the ivy is the creeping scab of neglect, the knuckled masonry the jutting bones of a clenched fist, and the blasphemous pointing the open defiance of the ruin against heaven — an image of human vanity and mortal decay flinging itself at the divine.
