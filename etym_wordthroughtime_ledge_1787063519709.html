- 14th c. — Middle English legge "a bar, rod, rail" — a humble builder's word for a laid length of timber; origin uncertain, possibly from the verb "to lay".
- 16th c. — ledge becomes "a horizontal projecting shelf or ridge", both on rock faces and on buildings; the modern geological and architectural senses are born.
- 16th–17th c. — "window-ledge" and "sill" senses appear; the ledge is the utilitarian shelf of every house — the resting place of lamps, pots, and cats.
- 17th–19th c. — nautical and geographical senses: ledges of rock, underwater ledges that wreck ships; the word enters the vocabulary of sailors, surveyors, and geologists.
- 19th–20th c. — mountaineering gives ledge its dramatic colour: the narrow ledge on a cliff face becomes the emblem of peril and of birds' nests; "on a ledge" enters everyday idiom for dangerous positions.
- 20th–21st c. — ledge is everyday domestic ("window ledge", "kitchen ledge") and dramatic landscape ("a rocky ledge above the gorge"); the word bridges the homely sill and the sheer cliff in a single image.
