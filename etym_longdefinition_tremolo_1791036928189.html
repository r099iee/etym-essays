Tremolo is a musical effect consisting of a rapid trembling or wavering of sound, produced either by very quick repetition of the same note, rapid alternation between notes, or rapid periodic variation in loudness, and by extension any tremulous wavering in a singing or speaking voice. The noun is borrowed from Italian musical terminology and is used both technically and descriptively: a vocalist sings with tremolo, a violinist plays tremolo, an organ or amplifier has a tremolo stop or effect.

In strict organology tremolo properly denotes variation in amplitude or reiteration, distinct from vibrato which denotes variation in pitch. In practice musicians, critics and lay listeners often blur them: any rapid quaver is called tremolo. The source sentence — "one spotted the tremolo in the voice" — uses the loose descriptive sense: a perceptible waver betraying age, nerves, emotion or drink.

  • Bowed-string tremolo (primary technical): Very rapid repetition of the same pitch with separate bow strokes, notated with slashes through the stem. Effect is shimmering, agitated, suspenseful. Used from Monteverdi's stile concitato to horror film scores. On mandolin, tremolo picking sustains tone.
  • Vocal tremolo: Rapid fluctuation in a sung or spoken tone. When controlled and narrow it is admired as warmth; when wide, slow or involuntary it signals frailty, fear or inebriation. Opera distinguishes healthy vibrato from wobbly tremolo as a fault. Everyday speech: a tremolo of nerves.
  • Keyboard / wind / electronic tremolo: On organ, a tremulant varies wind pressure; on electric guitar and amplifiers, a tremolo circuit pulses volume (often mislabelled vibrato). Fender's "tremolo arm" actually produces vibrato (pitch bend). Effect pedals labelled tremolo chop the signal rhythmically.
  • Notation and plural: Abbreviated trem., plural tremolos or Italianate tremoli. To play tremolo or con tremolo. Distinct from trill (alternation of adjacent pitches) and mordent.
  • Figurative extension: Any trembling wavering: a tremolo of laughter, of fear. Literary critics borrow the term to describe prose rhythm or emotional quaver. Rarely used outside sound contexts, unlike tremor.

The bridge between trembling body and trembling tone is mimetic: a frightened voice wavers, so a wavered tone signifies fear, ecstasy or pathos even when produced mechanically. Hence Baroque composers wrote measured tremolo to depict battle terror; Romantic tenors cultivated it for dying heroes; country singers use it for sincerity. Listeners decode technique as feeling.

Grammatically tremolo is a countable noun (a tremolo, two tremolos), often attributive (tremolo arm, tremolo picking, tremolo effect). Register is musical-technical but accessible: programme notes, reviews, guitar shops. Pronunciation in RP with initial stress and schwa is standard; Italianate /treˈmoːlo/ is heard only from singers. Confusion with vibrato is endemic and must be learned as a historical accident, not a rule.
