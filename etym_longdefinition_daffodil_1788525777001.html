daffodil (noun) — A bulbous spring-flowering plant belonging to the genus Narcissus (especially the wild species Narcissus pseudonarcissus, the common wild daffodil, or its numerous cultivated hybrids) in the amaryllis family (Amaryllidaceae). Characterized by its linear, strap-like leaves and brilliant yellow or white blossoms featuring a distinctive central trumpet-shaped or cup-shaped corona surrounded by six spreading perianth segments (petals and sepals), the daffodil is universally celebrated as the archetypal herald of early spring, nature's rebirth, resilience, and transient floral beauty.

Primary botanical and horticultural sense. Botanically, the daffodil is a perennial geophyte growing from an underground tunicate bulb. Emerging in late winter and flowering in early spring before deciduous woodland trees leaf out, daffodils thrive in meadows, open woodlands, and domestic gardens. The flower structure is distinctive for its prominent tubular corona (trumpet) projecting from the center of six perianth whorls. The plant produces toxic phenanthridine alkaloids, primarily lycorine and galantamine; while lycorine renders the bulb unpalatable and poisonous to grazing herbivores and pests, galantamine is actively extracted and synthesized in modern pharmacology as a crucial acetylcholinesterase inhibitor used to manage cognitive decline in mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease.

Secondary sense — literary emblem of spring renewal, memory, and pastoral nostalgia. In English literature and European poetry, the daffodil occupies an exalted symbolic status as the joyful conqueror of winter's gloom. In William Shakespeare's The Winter's Tale (Act IV, Scene 4), Autolycus sings of "daffodils, that come before the swallow dares, and take the winds of March with beauty." Most famously, William Wordsworth's 1807 Romantic lyric I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud immortalized the flower: encountering "a host, of golden daffodils; beside the lake, beneath the trees, fluttering and dancing in the breeze," the poet finds an enduring inward wealth of joy ("and then my heart with pleasure fills, and dances with the daffodils").

Tertiary sense — yellow meadow flowers in fairy tales and color terminology. In Oscar Wilde's fairy tale The Nightingale and the Rose, the Rose-tree beneath the sundial compares the rich yellow of its blossoms to natural wonders: "‘My roses are yellow,’ it answered; ‘as yellow as the hair of the mermaiden who sits upon an amber throne, and yellower than the daffodil that blooms in the meadow before the mower comes with his scythe.’" Here, Wilde captures the pristine, intense yellow of wild daffodils while poignantly foreshadowing mortality through the mower's approaching scythe. In fashion, interior design, and paint palettes, "daffodil yellow" designates a bright, cheerful, saturated primary yellow.

Specialized domain usages and polysemy:

  • National emblem of Wales: Known in Welsh as Cenhinen Bedr (literally "Peter's leek"), the daffodil is the traditional national floral emblem of Wales, worn proudly on Saint David's Day (March 1st) to celebrate Welsh heritage and the onset of spring.
  • Symbol of cancer research and hope: Adopted internationally by major cancer charities (including Marie Curie in the UK, the American Cancer Society, and the Canadian Cancer Society) as the official emblem of "Daffodil Day," symbolizing hope, resilience, and a brighter future for patients.
  • Folkloric and vernacular names: Historically known across English countryside dialects by affectionate folk names such as daffadowndilly, daffodilly, Lent lily (due to its flowering season coinciding with Christian Lent), and bell-rose.
  • Horticultural classification: The Royal Horticultural Society (RHS) organizes daffodils into 13 formal divisions based on morphology (e.g., Trumpet, Large-cupped, Small-cupped, Double, Triandrus, Cyclamineus, Jonquilla, and Poeticus daffodils).
  • Mythological intersection with Narcissus: While closely linked to the Greek myth of the youth Narcissus who fell in love with his own reflection, the English word daffodil linguistically derives from asphodel, creating a poetic fusion between the pale Elysian flower of the Underworld and the radiant golden Narcissus of earthly meadows.

Evocative resonance. Daffodil carries an unmatched freshness in English. It evokes the crisp air of early March, vibrant green meadows carpeted in gold, and the triumphant, enduring return of light after winter's long silence across the English-speaking world.
