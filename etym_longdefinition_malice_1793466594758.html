Malice is a noun denoting the conscious, deliberate desire, intention, or ill will to inflict injury, suffering, humiliation, or damage upon another person. Unlike blind rage, hot-headed passion, or sudden panic, malice is characterized by a calculating, cold-blooded psychological orientation: it operates through premeditation, grudge-bearing spite, rancorous envy, and a perverse satisfaction derived from witnessing another’s misfortune. It represents one of the foundational moral, theological, and legal concepts of Western civilization, serving as the critical boundary line that separates accidental or careless harm from culpable wickedness.

In the source passage from Samuel Johnson’s celebrated essay in The Rambler (No. 144, 1751), the term is deployed with biting psychological and moral precision: ‘There is, I fear, so much malice in the hearts of most men, that they are chiefly jealous of that praise which can give the greatest pleasure, and are then most liberal of eulogium when it can no longer be enjoyed.’ Johnson diagnoses an insidious, everyday manifestation of human malice: men begrudge praise to the living because living merit provokes painful feelings of envy and rivalry; yet the moment their competitor dies and can no longer enjoy the honor, their malice relaxes into lavish, self-congratulatory memorial tributes. Here malice is not merely physical violence, but a quiet, poisonous pettiness that suffocates human generosity.

Polysemic Senses and Domain Usages:

  • 1. Psychological and Moral Ill Will (Primary Everyday Sense): An enduring disposition to wish evil or harm upon others; spitefulness, malevolence, and deep-seated rancor. It reflects a desire to undermine a rival’s reputation, happiness, or success simply out of envious resentment.
  • 2. Criminal Law and Culpable Mens Rea (Technical Legal Sense): In Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence, malice aforethought is the requisite mental state (mens rea) that elevates an unlawful killing from manslaughter to murder. It encompasses four distinct mental states: (a) intent to kill, (b) intent to inflict grievous bodily harm, (c) reckless indifference to an unjustifiably high risk to human life (depraved-heart murder), and (d) intent to commit an inherently dangerous felony (felony murder). In this technical context, malice does not necessarily require personal hatred or ill will toward the specific victim, but rather a wicked, depraved, and malignant heart indifferent to social duty.
  • 3. Constitutional Law and Defamation (Civil Libel Sense): Under the landmark United States Supreme Court precedent New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), a public official or public figure seeking damages for defamation must prove that the defamatory statement was made with actual malice—defined specifically as knowledge that the information was false, or reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.
  • 4. Civil Torts and Malicious Prosecution: In general tort law, malice denotes the intentional commission of a wrongful act without legal justification, excuse, or privilege, as seen in claims of malicious prosecution (initiating criminal proceedings against an innocent person without probable cause and with improper motives) or malicious interference with contractual relations.
  • 5. Anthropomorphic and Figurative Attribution (‘The Malice of Inanimate Objects’): A humorous or exasperated rhetorical figure (known philosophically as resistentialism) in which spiteful conscious agency is attributed to lifeless things—such as printers jamming, keys vanishing, or weather turning foul at the worst conceivable moment (‘the sheer malice of the elements’).
  • 6. Cybersecurity and Computer Science: The root of modern computational concepts including malicious software (malware), malicious payloads, and malicious actors—denoting software code or digital agents engineered deliberately to compromise, spy upon, corrupt, or destroy information systems.

Register and Connotations: Malice is a weighty, serious, and ethically severe term. In judicial settings, its presence transforms human actions into severe felonies and tortious liabilities. In literature and moral philosophy, it carries a chilling atmosphere of premeditation, toxic envy, and emotional corruption, contrasting sharply with transient anger or thoughtless negligence.
