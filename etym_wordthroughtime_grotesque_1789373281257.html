- Late 15th to Mid-16th Century - Emerges in Italian art workshops (*la grottesca*) to designate ancient Roman decorative subterranean murals blending human, animal, and plant forms discovered in Nero's buried Domus Aurea.
- Late 16th to Early 17th Century - Enters French (*grotesque*) and Elizabethan English as an artistic and theatrical term denoting fanciful, chimeric ornament, comically deformed figures, or antique scrollwork.
- 18th Century - Broadens into neoclassical aesthetic criticism with a distinctly pejorative connotation, used by Enlightenment critics to condemn deviations from classical symmetry, rational proportion, and good taste.
- 19th Century - Undergoes a glorious romantic rehabilitation through Victor Hugo (preface to *Cromwell*), who declared the grotesque the indispensable counterpoint to the sublime in modern Christian art, while typographers adopted "grotesque" for bold sans-serif typefaces.
- 20th Century to Present - Established as a foundational concept in cultural philosophy and literary theory (Bakhtin, Kayser, Flannery O'Connor, Proust), characterizing the existential, comic, and horrifying alienation of the modern human condition.
