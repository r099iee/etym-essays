Yore is an archaic and literary noun meaning time long past — bygone ages, the distant former days — most familiar today in the fixed phrase of yore. It is not a calendar unit or a measured span; it is a word for the legendary, unreachable past, the "long ago" that stories live in.

Core sense. Yore names the remote past as a whole: "in days of yore," "the heroes of yore," "the dwarves of yore." It compresses an indefinite stretch of former time into a single noun and, unlike neutral terms, always carries elevation: it belongs to ballads, epic verse, fairy-tale openings, and historical romance rather than to ordinary prose.

Register and usage. Yore is almost never used alone in modern English outside deliberately archaic or poetic contexts; it survives overwhelmingly in the collocation of yore (historically "of years [past]", a genitive construction). Phrases such as "the knights of yore," "in days of yore," and "the bards of yore" make the word a badge of legendary time. Its very sound — the long o followed by r — suits a sense of drawn-out, misty antiquity.

How it differs from its neighbours.

  • past — neutral, general, usable of yesterday or of centuries ago; no elevation.
  • olden days / olden times — similar legendary past, but phrase-bound and faintly folksy.
  • antiquity — learned and civilizational, naming the ancient classical or biblical world as a historical period.
  • formerly — flat adverbial, describing any earlier state, not a legendary age.
  • once — can open a fairy tale ("once upon a time") but is thinner and needs context.
  • ago — relative and quantitative, always needs a measure ("long ago," "ten years ago").
  • bygone(s) — "days of yore" resembles "days gone by"; bygones is more casual and often refers to past grievances.

Polysemy: senses and grammatical lives of yore today.

  • Noun (main literary sense): time long past, the legendary former age — "in yore," "of yore."
  • Attributive use (chiefly in verse): yore-constructions such as "yore days" or "yore" as a modifier are rare and poetic, modelling "former" ("yore-time," "yore-ages").
  • Fixed collocation "of yore": the overwhelmingly dominant modern form; the prepositional phrase functions adjectivally or adverbially ("the dwarves of yore," "as of yore," "in days of yore").
  • Archaic adverbial sense: historically yore could stand alone meaning "formerly, long ago"; this survives in consciously archaic prose and verse.

Historical bridge of the senses. Yore is not a separate borrowing but a fossil of English grammar. Old English ġēar "year" formed a genitive plural ġēara, "of years"; used adverbially, "of years" came to mean "years ago, in former times." Middle English contracted this into yore, and as the living case system collapsed the word froze as an independent marker of the distant past. The "of" that modern speakers supply in "of yore" is thus partly a re-analysis of the ancient genitive — the word keeps, in its very bones, the grammar of counting years.

In the card's context. In the dwarves' song — "The dwarves of yore made mighty spells" — yore stamps the craftsmen as figures of a lost golden age: spell-smiths of hollow halls, not the diminished company in Bilbo's parlour. One short noun opens a gulf between then (power, hammers, spells) and now (exile, memory, quest), making pure temporal remoteness carry emotional weight.
