- c. 1st c. CE (Rome): Martial famously used Latin *plagiarius* ("kidnapper / man-stealer") as a stinging metaphor for a rival poet who stole his verses.
- c. 1600–1620: Introduced into English by Ben Jonson (as *plagiary*), describing literary thieves who steal other men's writings and claim them as their own.
- c. 1650–1750: Formed into the verb *plagiarize* / *plagiarizing* during the rise of print capitalism, copyright law (Statute of Anne, 1710), and the legal concept of intellectual property.
- c. 1750–1900: Solidified across academia, journalism, and literature as a grave ethical and professional violation, strictly distinguished from legitimate quotation, parody, or classical imitation (*imitatio*).
- c. 1900–Present: Expanded beyond literature into scientific research, music composition, digital software code, and AI generative text, while remaining a central theme in literary theory (such as Roland Barthes's critique of the institutional copying of language).
