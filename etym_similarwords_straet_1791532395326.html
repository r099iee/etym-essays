• Street: The modern descendant of the same word; differs because it is the living English term, narrowed to an urban road lined with buildings, while straet is the Old English ancestor with a wider sense of "paved road."
  • Road: A wide way for vehicles connecting places; differs because a road need not be urban or lined with buildings, and it comes from a different root (rād, "riding").
  • Highway: A main public road; differs because it is a legal and engineering term for a route between towns, not a residential or commercial street.
  • Lane: A narrow way, often between hedges or in the countryside; differs because it is narrower and less formal than a street, and often rural.
  • Avenue: A broad street, often tree-lined; differs because it implies width and dignity and is a later French borrowing.
  • Boulevard: A wide, often grand urban street; differs because it is a French borrowing with a specifically planned, continental connotation.
  • Thoroughfare: A passage or road open at both ends; differs because it is a functional, legal-sounding term rather than a description of a built-up street.
  • Alley: A narrow passage between buildings; differs because it is secondary and often service-oriented, not a main public street.
  • Causeway: A raised paved road; differs because it is built over wet or low ground, preserving the "paved" element of strāta more literally than street does.
  • Pavement: In British English, the footway beside a road; differs because it names the pedestrian surface, not the whole thoroughfare, and reflects Latin pavimentum.
  • Via: A way or route, used in English mainly in learned or Italian contexts; differs because it is a direct Latin borrowing and not the ordinary word for a street.
  • Stratum: A layer, from the same Latin root; differs because it is a learned geological or anatomical term, a doublet rather than a synonym.
