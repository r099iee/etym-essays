Halted is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb halt — “to come to a stop, to cease moving or progressing.” In the literary context of J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit — Bilbo’s stealth in the goblin-tunnels beneath the Misty Mountains — it describes an abrupt, deliberate suspension of motion: “Bilbo halted and flattened himself against the tunnel-wall.” The nuance is precise. Halted does not merely mean that Bilbo stopped walking; it means that he stopped decisively and at once, the way a soldier freezes at the command “Halt!” — and the word’s military heritage (it entered English as a seventeenth-century borrowing of the German drill-command halt!) is exactly right for the situation. Bilbo is not pausing to think; he is halting as a tactic, killing all sound and motion in a single instant so that he can flatten himself against the wall and let danger pass in the dark. The tone is one of tense, disciplined caution: there is no fluster in halted, no panic, only an instant of absolute stillness achieved at will, the body snapping from movement to immobility like a mechanism disengaged. The verb halt is both intransitive (“to come to a stop”) and transitive (“to cause to stop”: to halt a horse, to halt production), and its participle and preterite halted do the same double duty — a machine that has stopped is halted, and an army that has been commanded to stop is halted. Because the word was born as a command, it carries an institutional, procedural flavour wherever it goes: it is the verb of drills, boards, bulletins, and engineering, not of casual everyday stopping. Core senses and usages:
  • Intransitive: to come to a stop — of a person, animal, army, or procession; the sudden, complete cessation of movement (“the marchers halted at the crossroads”).
  • Transitive: to cause to stop — to bring a horse, vehicle, machine, or process to a stop (“he halted his horse at the riverbank”).
  • Participial adjective — halted as a state: a halted train, halted work, a halted campaign; the emphasis falls on the completeness of the stop.
  • Command / interjection — the bare imperative “Halt!” remains the drill-ground and traffic command, the source of all the other senses.
  • Noun use — “to come to a halt,” “to call a halt,” where the noun denotes the state or act of stopping (a distinct but closely related lexicalization).
  • Figurative and institutional use — halting the spread of disease, halting a negotiation, halting climate change; a clean, bureaucratic, slightly stern form of “stop.”
  • Technical and industrial jargon — computing: a “halt” is the cessation of processor execution (a “halt instruction,” “the system halted”); transport: a “halt” in railway terminology (especially in German-influenced contexts) is a stopping place or small station.
Polysemy and historical bridge: every modern sense flows from the military command. Sixteenth-century German armies used the imperative halt! (from halten, “to hold”) to freeze a marching unit in place; the command was borrowed into French (halte!), Italian (alto!), and English (c. 1659) during the wars of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and from a bare command-word the noun and verb spread into general use. The semantic core is “to hold oneself still,” and the modern participial halted still means stopped held — not merely no longer moving, but arrested in place and held there, which is why the word suits a fugitive flattening himself deliberately against a tunnel-wall. An important trap: Old English had a separate adjective halt (“lame, crippled,” from Proto-Germanic *haltaz), an etymological homograph that must never be confused with the “stop” word — it survives only in archaic and dialectal “halt and maimed” and is unrelated to the modern verb. Among its near-synonyms, stopped is the neutral general term and may imply a gradual slowing; paused suggests a temporary, easily reversed break, usually brief, and lacks any tension; froze is the involuntary cousin — the body stops because fear paralyses it, not because the mind commands it — and carries a connotation of helpless rigidity; stood still describes a state rather than the act of arriving at it; came to a standstill belongs to machines, traffic, and processes; and arrested is formal, legal, or technical. Only halted combines suddenness, deliberateness, and a faint military or procedural discipline — the stopping of a body obeying an inner command — which is precisely the quality of Bilbo’s motionless, wall-hugging stillness in the tunnel.
