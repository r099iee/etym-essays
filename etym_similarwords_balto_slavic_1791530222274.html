• Baltic: the northern half of the grouping, comprising Lithuanian, Latvian and Old Prussian; differs because it excludes the Slavic languages and, in geopolitical use, can mean the Baltic states rather than the language family.
  • Slavic: the southern and eastern half, the largest branch in Europe by speaker numbers; differs because it is one daughter of Balto-Slavic, not the whole node.
  • Slavonic: an older synonym for "Slavic", especially in "Old Church Slavonic"; differs by register and by its strong association with liturgical and scholarly contexts.
  • Balto-Slavonic: an exact synonym of "Balto-Slavic"; differs only by being the older, slightly more formal variant.
  • Slavo-Baltic: a reversed compound found in older scholarship; differs in emphasis, foregrounding Slavic, and is now rare.
  • Proto-Balto-Slavic: the reconstructed common ancestor; differs because it names a reconstructed language, not the family that descends from it.
  • Indo-European: the parent family of which Balto-Slavic is one branch; differs by its far greater scope, covering languages from Irish to Bengali.
  • Satem: the label for the Indo-European group in which palatovelars became sibilants, including Balto-Slavic and Indo-Iranian; differs because it is a phonological classification, not a genealogical branch.
  • Baltic-Finnic: the Uralic languages of Estonia and Finland, geographically Baltic but unrelated; differs because they belong to an entirely different family, a frequent source of confusion.
  • Old Prussian: the extinct West Baltic language, the most conservative attested Baltic member; differs because it is a single language within Baltic, not the branch itself.
  • Common Slavic: the late reconstructed ancestor of the Slavic languages alone; differs because it is post-split and excludes Baltic.
  • Sprachbund: a contact-defined convergence area, offered by some scholars as an alternative to a family-tree account; differs because it is a model of diffusion, not a genealogical grouping.
