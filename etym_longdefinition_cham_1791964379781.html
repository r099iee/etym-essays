Cham, in this passage, is neither a man’s name nor the biblical Ham. It is an archaic English form of Khan, the title of the Mongol emperor — the “Great Khan” of Tartary — used here as a mock-grand epithet for Samuel Johnson: “Johnson, the Great Cham of English literature”.

The phrase belongs to the eighteenth century and is traditionally credited to Tobias Smollett, who is said to have applied it to Johnson in 1759, when the Dictionary (1755) and Rasselas (1759) had made Johnson the most commanding literary figure in Britain. It is an allusion rather than a name: it presupposes a reader who knows that “the Great Cham of Tartary” was a byword for an absolute eastern monarch whose word was law.

What the word denotes

  • A title, not a personal name. Cham renders Khan, the sovereign of the Mongol empire and its successor states — like Caesar or Tsar, a title that could be borne by one ruler after another.
  • The Great Khan. The fuller form Khagan denoted the supreme ruler over subordinate khans. Genghis Khan (d. 1227) and his grandson Kublai Khan (d. 1294) were the two names that fixed the title in the European imagination.
  • Tartary. The European geographical label for inner Asia from the Caspian to the Pacific; “the Great Cham of Tartary” was the standard English phrase for its emperor in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
  • The historical afterlife. After the Mongol dynasty fell in China in 1368 the title survived among the successor khanates of the steppe — Crimea, Kazan, Astrakhan — and remained a synonym for arbitrary power in European political writing.

The Johnsonian epithet

Samuel Johnson (1709–1784) was a bookseller’s son from Lichfield who became, by common consent, the arbiter of English prose: author of the Dictionary of the English Language (1755), of Rasselas (1759), editor of Shakespeare (1765), biographer and moralist in the Lives of the Poets (1779–81), and the dominating presence of the Literary Club from 1764. The epithet catches several things at once:

  • Legislation. A dictionary is a kind of statute book, and Johnson’s was treated as one; to call him a Cham is to say that he gave laws to the language.
  • Oracular manner. Contemporaries described his conversation as a series of decrees, delivered in balanced periods and brooking no appeal. Boswell’s Life (1791) fixed the image of Johnson as a literary monarch.
  • Physical and social incongruity. The joke depends on contrast: a stout, scrofulous, loudly argumentative Englishman, Tory and Anglican, cast as the exotic despot of an Asian empire. It is affectionate as much as satirical.
  • Mock-grandeur. The epithet inflates and deflates in the same gesture. It honours Johnson’s supremacy and quietly mocks the idea that any man should be supreme in letters.

The pejorative potential

Because a khan rules by will rather than by argument, the epithet can also carry a criticism: the literary despot who expects submission, who mistakes authority for truth. Eighteenth-century writers were acutely conscious of the danger of a “dictator of letters”, and the phrase gave them a compact way of naming it. In modern usage the tone depends entirely on context — admiring, ironic, or mildly censorious.

Homographs to keep apart

  • Cham = Ham. Medieval and early modern texts sometimes spell the second son of Noah as Cham; that patriarch has no connection with the Mongol title.
  • Cham = a people and language. The Cham are an Austronesian-speaking people of the former kingdom of Champa in central and southern Vietnam, with their own script and Muslim and Hindu traditions. The name is unrelated.
  • Cham as a place name. Several European towns are called Cham (in Switzerland and Bavaria), from older local river and settlement names. Also unrelated.

Register and use

The word is archaic and bookish, capitalised, pronounced /tʃæm/ to rhyme with ram, and now almost entirely confined to the one phrase. Outside “the Great Cham of literature” it survives only in historical writing about the Mongol empire, where Khan is the normal spelling. Its tone in the Johnsonian epithet is mock-heroic: learned, allusive, faintly irreverent, and flattering precisely because it exaggerates.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage sets Johnson beside Burke: Johnson “sedulously imitated the Ciceronian sentence”, while Burke “attempted to catch the style and flavor of the great orations”. Calling Johnson the Great Cham turns a question of prose technique into a question of sovereignty. He is presented as the monarch of English letters, whose imitation of Cicero was not a private enthusiasm but a decree of style issued from the throne of the language.
