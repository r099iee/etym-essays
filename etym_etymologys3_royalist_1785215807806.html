THE EVOLUTION OF ROYALIST: FROM THE STRAIGHT LINE TO THE PRIDE OF THE CROWN **A root that rules: *h₃reǵ- and the family of order** The ancestry of *royalist* runs back to a single Proto-Indo-European root, *h₃reǵ-, whose core meaning was "to move in a straight line" and hence "to direct, guide, rule." That double meaning is the seed of the whole modern word: the one who rules is, at the oldest layer of the language, the one who keeps things straight. The root's offspring are spread across the Indo-European world with remarkable consistency:
  • In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit rājan-, "king," the source of rājā, still alive in Hindi rājā and rāj "rule, reign."
  • In Celtic it gave Old Irish rí "king" and Welsh rhi "lord."
  • In Germanic it yielded Old English riht and Modern English right — "that which is straight and correct" — with Gothic raihts and German Recht "law" beside it.
  • It was in Latin, however, that the root achieved its most famous dynasty: regō "to rule, guide," the participle rēctus (whence correct, direct, erect, rector), the noun rēgula "rule, straightedge" (whence rule, regular), and the supreme noun rēx, genitive rēgis, "king."
**The Latin royal line: rēx and rēgālis** From rēx Latin formed the adjective rēgālis, "kingly, regal," and here the specifically royal line begins. Everything that follows in the history of *royalist* descends from this single Latin adjective: - Rēgālis passed into Old French, where the characteristic sound changes of Gallo-Romance worked on the Latin medial -g-: the velar weakened to a palatal glide, and rēgālis emerged as roial, then royal, "kingly." The word also reached English separately as regal, preserving the Latin shape, while the French form became the royal of English. - Old French royal was carried into English through Anglo-French after the Norman conquest; Middle English royal is attested from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and modern English royal is its direct descendant. **The partisan suffix: royal + -iste** The decisive morphological step happened on the far side of the Channel: - French, from the adjective royal, formed royaliste, "a supporter of the king," by adding the agent-suffix -iste (from Latin -ista, Greek -istēs, the suffix of partisan and profession). The formation answered a political need: a word for the adherent of the crown, the counterpart of the "republican" and the "parliamentarian." - English took royalist directly from this French formation during the bitterest political conflict of the seventeenth century. The word is attested in English in the 1640s, at the outbreak of the English Civil War, where it named the Cavaliers who fought for King Charles I against the Parliamentarian Roundheads — a moment that fixed the word's romantic and partisan colouring forever. **From party label to temperament: the widening of royalist** In the centuries that followed, the word steadily widened its range: - During the French Revolution it designated partisans of the Bourbon monarchy against the Revolution; in the Restoration it named the supporters of the returning king; in the nineteenth century it was applied to Carlists, legitimists, and every defender of a threatened throne across Europe. - Alongside the political sense, the vocabulary of monarchy — dignity, pride, command, self-possession — fed a figurative use: "royalist" as an adjective of temperament. Carlyle's "royalist volition," applied to Harriet Taylor Mill, is the classic instance: a will that is proud, imperious, and unbending, wearing the lofty self-assurance of a queen while owing nothing to any actual crown. - The figurative transfer works because the political word never lost its emotional charge: a royalist is not merely a constitutional opinion but a loyalty, a pride, and a refusal to bend — precisely the qualities Carlyle sees in his romance heroine. **The sound and the spelling over time** - The -g- of Latin rēgālis became the y-glide of Old French roial and then royal; English spelling preserved the y (royal), while the pronunciation of royal in English kept the disyllabic /ˈrɔɪəl/ inherited through the French diphthong. - Royalist itself, borrowed whole from French royaliste, has kept its spelling and suffix unchanged since the 1640s; only the range of reference has grown. **Why the word means what it means today** The full modern range of royalist — historical partisan of a king, modern supporter of monarchy, and figurative adjective for a proud, sovereign temperament — is a single continuous development from a root that meant "to make straight." Each stage preserved the core idea of authority and order: the straightener became the ruler, the ruler became the king, the king's party became the royalists, and the royalist's loyalty became a model for a will that bows to nothing. Carlyle's "royalist volition" is the last link in that chain — a word for politics pressed into service for psychology, carrying two thousand years of kingship behind a single adjective.
