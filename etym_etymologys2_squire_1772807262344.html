Derivation chain for SQUIRE (the target root word is bold at the final stage): 1. PIE *skeu- "to cover, conceal, wrap; (skin/hide as) covering" (reconstructed; the standard source proposed for Latin scūtum, the shield understood as a portable covering for the body). 2. Latin scūtum "shield" (the long, curved Roman shield; attested from c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE, e.g. Plautus; common in Livy and Caesar). 3. Latin scūtārius "shield-maker, shield-bearer, soldier equipped with a shield" (attested in Late Latin). 4. Medieval Latin scutarius / escutarius "shield-bearer; attendant or armour-bearer of a knight" (attested in medieval Latin documents). 5. Old French escuier / esquier / escuyer "shield-bearer, squire; young man of good birth attending a knight" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries). 6. Middle English squier / squyer "young attendant to a knight, aspirant to knighthood" (borrowed via Anglo-Norman; attested c. 1300; famously the "Squire" of Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, c. 1387–1400). 7. Modern English squire "country gentleman, principal landowner of a district" (the shortened spelling squire, distinct from esquire, emerges in the 16th century; the landed-gentry sense solidifies in the 17th–18th centuries). Debated / nuanced stages: The connection of Latin scūtum to PIE *skeu- "to cover" is widely accepted, but the precise root extension is debated; alternative proposals connect scūtum with a root meaning "to cut / split" (a shield cut from wood or hide). The Romance-to-English portion is fully attested. Note also the parallel form "esquire", which preserves the French initial e- and became the courtesy title.
