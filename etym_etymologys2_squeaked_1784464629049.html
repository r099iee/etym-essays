Stage 1 — No secure Proto-Indo-European root is reconstructable. SQUEAKED belongs to the expressive, imitative (onomatopoeic) stratum of Germanic vocabulary: the s-/sk- onset and the high front vowel encode thin, high, friction-heavy noise. [DEBATED — see note below] → Stage 2 — Middle English squeken / squiken "to make a short, shrill sound; to squeak" (attested from the 14th century CE; sound-symbolic in origin, reinforced by the surrounding sk-/sq- shrill-sound set: squeal, screech, shriek, creak). → Stage 3 — Early Modern English squeak "to make a thin, high-pitched sound; (of a voice) to speak thin and high" (16th century CE onward; verb and noun). → Stage 4 — Modern English squeak "to emit or produce a short, high, thin sound" (ongoing), with the weak past and past participle formed regularly by -ed. → Stage 5 — Modern English SQUEAKED — the card's target form: the weak past tense (and past participle) of squeak, here recording one thin, comic vocal protest by Bilbo. NOTE on the debate: the verb is imitative, so a single linear PIE pedigree cannot be honestly drawn. Parallel formations occur across the Germanic and Scandinavian languages (compare dialect forms in sk- / skr- for shrill sounds), and these may have reinforced the English word, but no strict parentage is claimed. The attested chain from Middle English squeken onward is secure; everything earlier is expressive coinage rather than inherited root. The standard spelling squeak (with -k-) settled in the early modern period.
