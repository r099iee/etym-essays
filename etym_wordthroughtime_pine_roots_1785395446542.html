- A compound of two ancient words — 'pine' (from Latin 'pinus', in English by early Middle English) and 'root' (Old English 'rōt', Norse-derived): the roots of the pine tree. Earliest uses are literal and practical, in woodcraft and timber contexts. Connotation: anchoring, tenacious, the hidden hold of a tree on the ground.
- 16th–19th c.: 'pine-roots' appears in topographical and landscape description as a visible, exposed feature of thin-soiled slopes and conifer woods. Connotation: wild, weathered, earthy — the raw underside of a hillside.
- 19th c. onward: in nature-writing and fiction the plural is drawn on to picture a whole tangled, cable-like root system lying at the surface. Connotation: entanglement, ruggedness, the scene of storms and landslides.
- Modern register: a transparent, literal compound; it carries no transferred senses, but its plural has settled into descriptive prose as shorthand for an exposed, root-laced mountain flank.
