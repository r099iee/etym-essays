The history of betrothed is the history of truth turned into a promise: the same ancient root that gave English tree, true, trust and troth also gave the formal verb for promising marriage, with the prefix be- sealing the pledge. Every stage is well attested and the chain is considered secure by the OED, Etymonline, de Vaan for cognates, and Kroonen for Germanic.

1. PIE *deru- — firmness as worldview. Reconstructed PIE *deru- meant "to be firm, solid, steadfast", yielding words for wood, trees, and reliability across the family: Sanskrit dāru "wood", Greek doru "spear-shaft, wood", drus "oak", Old Church Slavonic drěvo "tree", Old Irish daur "oak", English tree. The semantic logic is that what is woody is firm, and what is firm is true. From the same root came the abstract sense of fidelity: Proto-Germanic *trewwaz "true, faithful, trustworthy", source of Old English trēowe "faithful", Old Norse tryggr "trustworthy", Gothic triggws "faithful".

  • Phonetics: PIE *d regularly yields Proto-Germanic *t by Grimm's Law (*deru- → *trew-); PIE *u diphthongizes to Germanic *eww before suffixes.
  • Semantics: "hard wood" → "firm" → "faithful, conforming to reality". The bridge is moralized physics: a straight oak does not bend, and a true man does not bend.

2. Old English treow and treowth — pledge as object. Old English had both the adjective trēowe "faithful, true" and the nouns trēow "faith, pledge, covenant" and trēowþ (treowth) "truth, fidelity, pledge", formed with the abstract suffix -þ as in warm-th, strong-th. Charters use treow for sworn pledges; poetry uses treowth for loyalty sworn to a lord. Marriage promises were already called wed and treow interchangeably. The prefix be- from Proto-Germanic *bi- "by, around" was highly productive, intensifying verbs: behatan "to promise solemnly", besprecan "to bespeak".

3. Middle English trouthe and bitrothen — the marriage verb. After the Norman Conquest, Old English treowth became Middle English treuthe, trouthe, trothe, famously personified in Langland's Piers Plowman and Chaucer, meaning both factual truth and pledged fidelity. Around 1300 appears bitrothen / betrothen "to promise or pledge in marriage", formed as be- + troth + -en infinitive suffix: literally "to endow with troth". The King Horn and Cursor Mundi use it for formal hand-plighting. Past participle betrothed soon substantivizes: "a betrothed maid" → "his betrothed" as noun for either sex.

  • Phonetics: Old English ēo → Middle English o / ou; intervocalic th voices to th /ð/ in betrothed; prefix vowel reduces to schwa.
  • Semantics: general "pledged with truth" narrows specifically to "pledged in marriage", while plain troth keeps the broader sense in phrases like "plight one's troth".

4. Early Modern to Victorian heyday — banns, joints, settlements. Shakespeare uses betrothed and betroth repeatedly ("Betroth'd to her friend the countie Paris"; Measure for Measure's betrothal law), Tyndale's Bible uses betrothed virgin for Mary, and the Book of Common Prayer formalizes betrothal as espousal de futuro distinct from wedding de praesenti. In law, betrothal created enforceable contracts, breach-of-promise suits, and dowry settlements. Jane Austen's world still treats being betrothed as a semi-public legal state. The French synonyms fiancé / fiancée enter in the 19th century but betrothed remains the native solemn term.

5. Modern retreat — archaism and Wodehouse irony. In the 20th century, with informal engagements and civil marriage, betrothed retreats to formal, religious, journalistic, and comic-archaic registers. Newspapers announce "his betrothed"; churches record "betrothal blessings"; fantasy novels revive it. Wodehouse's use — "Tuppy Glossop, Angela's betrothed" — is typical upper-class comic diction: precise, slightly stuffy, affectionately mocking the very formality of announced engagements among the Drones Club set, where being betrothed means being perpetually, calamitously on the verge of marriage.
