shrivelled belongs to a word whose origins are genuinely obscure, and honesty about that obscurity is part of its history. The verb shrivel first appears in English in the middle of the sixteenth century, with the senses it still has today — "to contract and wrinkle, to dry up and draw together" — and no text from before that date shows an obvious ancestor. The most widely accepted account treats the word as a frequentative formation: English has long created verbs in -el and -le (like wrestle, sparkle, crinkle) to express repeated or gradual action, and shrivel is plausibly such a formation on the stem of the older verb shrink, from Old English scrincan, "to draw together, contract, wither". On this theory, to "shrivel" is to shrink gradually and repeatedly, bit by bit — exactly what the -el suffix encodes.

The Old English verb scrincan itself descends from a reconstructed Proto-Germanic strong verb *skrinkwaną, "to shrink, contract", and its cognates appear across the older Germanic languages — the same root gives us modern shrink and shrunken. But the connection to shrivel is not beyond doubt: the vowels differ, the dates are awkward (a gap of several centuries between scrincan and shrivel), and the shape of the word looks as though it could equally have come from elsewhere. Some philologists therefore prefer a Scandinavian source, pointing to Norwegian skryvla, "to wrinkle, shrivel", or to related Nordic dialect words. The OED, with characteristic caution, labels the origin of shrivel as unknown, listing the shrink-family connection as merely probable.

  • Proto-Germanic (*skrinkwaną): "to shrink, draw together" — the reconstructed ancestor of shrink and, probably, of shrivel.
  • Old English (scrincan, 9th–11th c.): "to draw together, contract, wither, dry up" — the strong verb behind modern shrink.
  • Middle English (15th–16th c.): frequentative -el formations flourish; on the standard theory, shrivel is born as "to shrink little by little".
  • Early Modern English (1560s): shrivelled is recorded meaning "dried, wrinkled, contracted by heat or dryness" — first of plants and flesh.
  • 18th–19th century: the word enters medicine and botany (shrivelled limbs, shrivelled seed) and begins its figurative extension to hopes, spirits, and powers.
  • 19th-century Gothic and 20th-century prose: "shrivelled" becomes a stock adjective of age, decay, and horror — shrivelled crones, mummies, and withered flesh.
  • Modern English: literal and figurative senses coexist, from raisins and drought-cracked earth to "shrivelled dreams" and "shrivelled ambitions".

Phonology and spelling: The modern form shows the typical Early Modern development of the ending and the assimilation of the initial cluster; the vowel i in shri- reflects the short front vowel of the presumed source. British spelling preserves the double l of the inflected forms (shrivelled, shrivelling), while American usage writes shriveled, shriveling — the form on this card is the British one, fitting its source text.

Literary and cultural resonance: The word's power lies in its physical specificity — the image of something full contracting into a small, hard, wrinkled remnant — and English writers have exploited it from the earliest records to the present. In the source text (Tolkien's The Hobbit), the dragon's breath shrivels grass at the door and scorches the hidden dwarves: the participle captures a lethal, instantaneous drying. The same word in horror fiction describes ancient flesh, in medical writing describes dehydration, and in everyday speech describes autumn leaves and forgotten fruit — a small, grim word that has travelled, in barely four hundred years, from probable Germanic roots into the very heart of English description.
