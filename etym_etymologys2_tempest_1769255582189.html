1. Proto-Indo-European *ten- "to stretch, extend, span" (reconstructed root) — the ultimate base, which also yields Latin tendere "to stretch" and English tend, tension. Some scholars posit an intermediate PIE *temp- "to stretch out, span," a labialized variant of *ten-, as the immediate source of Latin tempus; a minority derive tempus from a root *tem- "to cut, measure" (time as measured segment) — the debate is noted below. 2. Latin tempus, temporis "time, season, fitting moment" (attested from the 3rd century BCE) — a noun of the third declension formed on the "stretch/span" idea: time as a stretched-out interval. 3. Latin tempestas, tempestatis "a period of time, a season; weather; bad weather, storm" (attested from the 2nd century BCE, e.g. in Plautus and Cato) — derived from tempus with the abstract noun suffix -tas; originally simply "the weather of a season," it specialized to bad weather and, by the classical period, to "storm, tempest." 4. Late Latin / Vulgar Latin tempesta "storm" — the shortened, specialized form of tempestas (attested in Vulgar Latin sources of the 6th–9th centuries; it is the source of Italian tempesta, Spanish tempestad, French tempête). 5. Old French tempeste "storm, violent wind; tumult" (attested 12th century) — borrowed from Latin tempesta. 6. Anglo-Norman tempeste → Middle English tempeste "violent storm, tempest" (attested c. 1290, e.g. in the South English Legendary). 7. Early Modern English tempest, spelling stabilized by the 16th century; made famous by Shakespeare's The Tempest (1611). 8. Modern English TEMPEST — present day, with the fixed figurative idioms (a tempest in a teapot, tempest-tossed). Debated / nuanced note: the derivation of Latin tempus from PIE *ten- "to stretch" via a labialized *temp- is the standard view (de Vaan, Pokorny), but it is not absolutely certain; the alternative *tem- "to cut, measure" has scholarly support, and the intermediate PIE *temp- is reconstructed rather than attested. The specialization of tempestas from neutral "weather" to "storm" is, by contrast, well documented in Latin texts from the 1st century BCE onward.
