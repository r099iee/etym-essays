ACCLAMATION — single root word; the chain from PIE to the present noun.

1. PIE *kelh₁- “to call, to shout, to summon” (reconstructed root of the raised voice — source of Latin calāre and clāmāre, Greek kaleîn; Pokorny ‘*kel-: to call, shout’).
2. Latin clāmāre “to shout, cry out, call aloud” (classical Latin verb, attested from the 2nd–1st c. BCE; also clāmor, ‘a shout’).
3. Latin acclāmāre “to shout at; to shout approval, to acclaim” (ad-, ‘toward’ + clāmāre; attested in classical and imperial Latin).
4. Latin acclāmātiō “acclamation, shouted approval” (noun from acclāmāre; the institutional shout of senate, soldiers, and circus crowds in Roman political life; attested from the 1st c. CE onward).
5. Old French acclamation “acclamation, shout of approval” (attested from the 14th century; borrowed from Latin acclāmātiō).
6. Middle English / Early Modern English acclamation “acclamation, loud public approval” (attested from the 15th century; borrowed from Old French/Latin).
7. English ACCLAMATION “loud, enthusiastic, collective approval; approval by unanimous voice without a vote” (modern form and senses; verb acclaim from the same root).

Note on debated points: the exact PIE laryngeal shape of the ‘call’ root is written *kelh₁- (some handbooks *kel- or *kely-). It must be kept distinct from several homophonous PIE *kel- roots — notably *kel- ‘to hide, cover’ (Latin celāre, cella — English cell, conceal) and *kel- ‘to drive, set in motion’ (Greek kelēs, ‘racer’), which are separate roots despite the identical surface shape. The Latin side of the chain (calāre, clāmāre, acclāmāre, acclāmātiō) is undisputed and well attested.
