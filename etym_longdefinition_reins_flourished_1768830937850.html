reins (plural noun; singular rein): the long, narrow straps of leather fitted at one end to the bit in a horse's mouth and held at the other end in the rider's hands, by which the animal is guided, checked, stopped, and turned. The word is idiomatic in the plural because the object is conceived as a matched pair, one strap running to each side of the bit, so that the rider steers by tension on either hand: the pair is one instrument of command. Mechanically, the rein is the most direct link between human intention and equine motion — a single pull converts the will into pressure on the sensitive corners of the horse's mouth, and the tautness and angle of the two straps govern speed, direction, and obedience. In the literary context of this card — a mounted duel fought in tight, deliberate movements — the word carries an undertone of mastery and violence held in check. To clutch the reins is to assert control over the horse's power; the word itself, in the rider's hand, is the point where raw force meets trained restraint.
  • to take the reins — to assume command or leadership (of a company, a government, a venture).
  • to hold the reins — to exercise power; 'the reins of government,' 'the reins of power.'
  • to hand over / relinquish the reins — to yield authority to a successor.
  • a free rein — unrestricted liberty of action (the rider loosening the reins to let the horse run).
  • to rein in / rein back — to curb, restrain, or hold something back, from pulling on the reins.
These figurative senses are not modern slang but the word's oldest companions in English. From the fifteenth century onward, ruling has been imagined as holding the reins, and the metaphor hardened into the standard political vocabulary of command; 'the reins of government' is a fixed phrase of statecraft. The semantic bridge is immediate and physical — steering a horse is the most vivid everyday image of control — and the metaphor has branched into management ('taking the reins of a company'), self-discipline ('reining in one's temper,' 'keeping one's emotions in rein'), and diplomacy ('with a free rein to negotiate'). In the technical vocabulary of riding and driving, the word also generates compounds: curb rein (the strap attached to the curb bit), bearing rein (which raises the horse's head), check rein, double reins, and side reins used in schooling; in driving, the pair is 'the reins,' held in both hands over the vehicle. flourished — past tense and past participle of the verb flourish, used here in the sense 'waved something around in a sweeping, showy way.' The context shows the general flourishing his saber at the troops: he sweeps the blade in a wide, theatrical arc, filling the air with it, making the weapon itself a piece of display before any blow is struck. To flourish a weapon is to show it off — to make it visible, to announce readiness with ceremony, to intimidate by display as much as by threat.
  • Sense 1 — brandish / wave showily: to wave or swing something — a sword, a baton, a document — with a sweeping, ornamental motion; the sense on this card.
  • Sense 2 — thrive / prosper: to grow vigorously, to bloom, to be in one's prime; of plants, people, arts, businesses, and fortunes.
  • Sense 3 — ornament (noun): an ornamental stroke in calligraphy or an embellishment in music — a trill, a curved flourish — and, by extension, 'with a flourish,' any showy ceremonial gesture.
  • Sense 4 — display ostentatiously: to present or exhibit in a boastful way, as in 'he flourished the letter in her face.'
  • Sense 5 — announce with a fanfare (older formal style): to greet or signal with waving, as of a banner or kerchief.
The several senses are historically one organism. The word descends from Latin flōrēre, 'to bloom, to be in flower,' built on flōs, 'a flower.' To thrive is to bloom; the verb then acquired an ornamental meaning, because an embellishment in writing or music was conceived as the 'flowering' of the line — the flourish is literally the decorative bloom of a pen-stroke or a melody. The martial sense grew out of the ornamental one: a sword flourished is a sword 'flowering' outward in the hand, at once decorative and deadly, and the showy sweep is the blade's own brief brilliant blooming. Thus the two faces of the word on this card — 'to wave showily' and 'to thrive' — share a single root image: something opening out from within into vivid, visible display.
