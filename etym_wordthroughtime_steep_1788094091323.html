- (Old English, stēap “high, lofty, towering; prominent”) - Used of tall towers, high cliffs, and lofty halls; the tone is grandeur and eminence, with no hint of danger or excess.
- (Middle English, stēpe/steep “abruptly sloping, precipitous”) - The “height” sense narrows to the angle of slopes: hills, cliffs, banks, stairs, roofs. Tone: practical, neutral measurement of ascent and descent.
- (16th–17th c.) - “The steep and thorny way to heaven” (Shakespeare’s Hamlet) fixes the steep road as the image of moral and spiritual difficulty; the King James Bible’s “steep place” (Matthew 8:32, 1611) fixes it as the word for a catastrophic cliff-plunge. Tone: ethical and dramatic.
- (late 19th–20th c.) - New quantitative and informal senses: “steep rise/fall” in figures; “a steep price” = exorbitant; “a steep story” = exaggerated; “steep learning curve” = demanding. Tone: from grandeur to mild complaint about cost and effort.
- (today) - Fully polysemous: the geographical sense is dominant and neutral; the informal senses are mildly negative and partly British; steeple and steeplechase fossilize the old towering meaning. Tone: an everyday word with a split personality — physics by day, grumbling by night.
