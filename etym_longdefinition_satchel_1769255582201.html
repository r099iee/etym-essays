satchel (noun, /ˈsætʃəl/) — In this sentence the satchel is the schoolboy's badge of identity. The verb "slung" tells us how it is worn — one strap crossing the chest, the bag riding the hip or the back as the boy sets off for school — and the whole vignette turns on the word's plain, dependable domesticity. A satchel is a small-to-medium bag, traditionally of leather or stout cloth, closed by a top flap, often buckled, and carried by a single shoulder strap; it is precisely the object that holds books, papers, and the modest provisions of a journey. The tone here is everyday and unpretentious, warm rather than glamorous: the satchel anchors a character's identity as a student or traveler and says, without fuss, "school," "errand," "departure." Because the word is now a little old-fashioned, it also lends the sentence a soft period glow, the sense of a child setting out from a world of slate pencils, inkwells, and knotted string — which is exactly the register the story is reaching for.

Primary sense — a shoulder bag with a flap

  • Form: a bag with a single long strap, worn over one shoulder with the body of the bag resting at the hip or under the arm; classically closed by a top flap secured with a buckle, clasp, or strap.
  • Materials: traditionally leather, canvas, or stout cloth — sturdy enough for books, papers, and daily supplies.
  • Contexts: the school satchel (books and slates), the traveler's satchel (essentials for a journey), the postman's or messenger's satchel (deliveries), and the artist's satchel (sketching materials).

Polysemy and extended uses

  • School satchel: the default modern association — the child's book bag, slung on one shoulder, a fixture of school-day imagery and period literature.
  • Messenger/leather satchel: in the 20th century the sturdy leather shoulder bag became a professional-carrier object — the courier's or paperboy's bag — and later a style object.
  • Designer "satchel bag": in modern fashion retail the word is repurposed for a structured handbag with a top handle and often a flap, sold as retro-chic; the fashion sense trades on the word's schoolroom and heritage associations.
  • Historical baggage sense: in older usage (16th–19th c.) a satchel could be any small bag or wallet for carrying money, documents, or personal goods on the road — the ancestor of the briefcase and the messenger bag.
  • Figurative uses: rare but attested — "to pack one's satchel" as a metaphor for preparing for a departure or new stage of life; the satchel as an emblem of the itinerant scholar, pilgrim, or student.

Boundary comparisons with neighbouring carriers

  • backpack (and cousins rucksack, knapsack): worn on the back with two straps, suggesting hiking, mountaineering, or sport; a satchel uses one shoulder strap and reads urban, studious, or lightly antiquated.
  • briefcase: rigid, leather, hand-carried, the property of the businessman; a satchel is softer, strap-worn, and student-tinted.
  • valise and portmanteau: pieces of hand travel luggage, bulkier, not worn over the shoulder in the schoolboy manner.
  • haversack: a canvas bag carried over one shoulder, but military-outdoor in flavor, for rations and field gear, not books.
  • pouch, wallet, purse: smaller, personal, for money or small items; a satchel is bigger and load-bearing.
  • handbag / shoulder bag: modern, largely feminine, unbuckled or zipped, with no necessary flap; a satchel's flap-and-strap construction is the distinctive feature.

Historical and register notes

  • The word entered English in the Middle Ages as the plain functional name for a small bag carried by travelers, messengers, and laborers; it was purely practical, with no social coloring.
  • In the 18th–19th centuries it became the schoolchild's emblem — the bag for books, slates, and lunch — acquiring its studious, everyday, warm register and its place in scenes of childhood and departure.
  • In the 20th century, as two-strap backpacks and rigid briefcases took over, the satchel turned old-fashioned and gained a soft period glow; today it survives both as literary period flavor and as a revived fashion object.

Connotation summary

  • Warm, unpretentious, lightly antiquated — the word carries a faint storybook glow.
  • Studious and domestic — the bag of the scholar, the schoolchild, and the traveler.
  • Structural identity: single strap, top flap, often buckled — the form itself is the definition.
