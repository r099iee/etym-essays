Single linear derivation chain for hammered: 1. PIE *h₂eḱ- — "sharp, pointed, piercing" (c. 4000 BCE). The root of keenness and piercing, widely attested (Greek akmē "point, edge," Latin acies "cutting edge, blade," Sanskrit aśri "sharp edge"). Its extended form *h₂eḱ-mōn / *h₂eḱ-mn- meant "stone," literally "the sharp thing" — the oldest stone tools were the sharpest things known. 2. PIE *h₂eḱ-mōn / *h₂eḱ-mn- — "stone, sharp stone" (c. 4000 BCE). This "stone" word is the true deep ancestor of the hammer; its cognates include Vedic Sanskrit aśman "stone, thunderbolt, stone of heaven," Greek akmōn "anvil, meteoric stone," and Lithuanian akmuõ "stone." 3. Proto-Germanic *hamaraz — "a hammer; a stone, a crag" (c. 500 BCE–200 CE). Germanic built its hammer-name on the same "stone" root; the retention of the "crag/stone" sense is proven by Old Norse hamarr "cliff, crag, hammer" and German Hammer "hammer," with Old Norse also preserving the meaning "stone" in place-names. The double sense — a stone and a stone-headed striking tool — shows the tool was originally a stone on a handle. 4. Old English hamor — "a hammer" (attested from the 8th c.). West Germanic carried the noun into England; it appears in Anglo-Saxon glosses and later in literature as the weapon of the god Thunor (Thor) and the tool of the smith. 5. Middle English hamer / hammer (noun) and the derived verb hameren / hammeren — "to strike with a hammer, beat, forge" (attested 13th–14th c.). The verb is formed from the noun on the native pattern. 6. Modern English hammered — past participle and past tense of hammer, "struck, beaten, or shaped with a hammer; (slang) drunk; (finance/sport) defeated or hit hard" (attested from the 15th c.; the participle form of the established verb) — the final node, the note's exact word form, highlighted above. NOTE ON COGNATES: Vedic aśman and Greek akmōn are sister words to Proto-Germanic *hamaraz, not ancestors of it — all descend side by side from the PIE "stone" root. The exact pre-Germanic shape behind *hamaraz (a *-m̥-ro- build of *h₂eḱ-) is debated; the connection through the "sharp stone" root is the accepted traditional view (OED, Etymonline, Pokorny), but the precise form is not directly attested.
