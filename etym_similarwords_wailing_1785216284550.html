• lament: Similar because both express grief aloud; differs because lament is articulate and often formal or ritualised — a spoken or written complaint of sorrow — while wailing is the wordless cry forced out as pure sound.
  • keen: Similar because both name a shrill, sustained cry of mourning; differs because keen is the specifically Irish and Scottish ceremonial funeral wail, a grief performed with a set ritual function, while wailing is the general, non-ceremonial cry.
  • howl: Similar because both are long, loud, sustained cries; differs because howl is wilder and more animal than human — a wolf-like baying — while wailing keeps a human, keening pitch and is bound to grief.
  • moan: Similar because both are prolonged cries of suffering; differs because moan is low, indistinct, and quieter — the sound of pain rather than the piercing, high-pitched grief of a wail.
  • whine: Similar because both are high-pitched and sustained; differs because whine is nasal, querulous, and complaining — often peevish — while wailing is deep human grief and carries dignity even in desperation.
  • weep: Similar because both express sorrow; differs because weep means to shed tears, not necessarily aloud, while wailing is specifically the loud, piercing cry without regard to tears.
  • cry: Similar because both mean to shout or sob aloud; differs because cry is the broad, neutral term for any vocal sound of distress, while wailing specifies the high, sustained, mournful register.
  • shriek / screech: Similar because both are piercing high-pitched sounds; differs because a shriek is a sudden short burst of alarm or fear, while a wail is prolonged, sustained, and mournful.
  • scream: Similar because both are loud, high, emotional vocalisations; differs because a scream is sharp, sudden, and often of terror or rage, while a wail rises and falls in grief, more sustained than piercing-sharp.
  • ululate: Similar because both name a high-pitched, wavering cry of mourning; differs because ululate is the technical and ethnographic word for the ceremonial trilled lament of Middle Eastern and African mourning, while wailing is the general English term.
