- Late 14th c. — "coronation" enters English from Old French "coronacion" for the act of crowning a sovereign; from the first it names a formal, public ceremony, heavier and more official than the plain verb "to crown."
- 15th–17th c. — the word is institutionalized as the great state ritual of monarchy — trumpets, oaths, robes, the touch of the crown — and becomes the hinge of a reign, the day costume becomes authority; its register is solemn, sacred and public.
- 18th–19th c. — the ceremony shrinks in frequency even as its weight grows: coronations are rare, carefully recorded set-pieces of national memory, and the word takes on a historical, almost ceremonial flavour of its own — something read about, remembered, prized.
- Contemporary — "coronation" remains reserved for crowning a monarch, but the word has loosened into metaphor: anything that completes or culminates can be called a "coronation" — the crowning moment of a career, a final achievement — while the literal sense stays grand, old-fashioned and full of ritual.
