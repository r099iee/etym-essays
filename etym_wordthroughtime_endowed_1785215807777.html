- 15th century (legal/financial) - “Endowed” enters English from Anglo-Norman, meaning to provide a lasting income or settlement — to give property for permanent support, as to a church or institution.
- 16th–17th century (figurative extension) - Extends from money to qualities: to be “endowed with” intelligence, courage, or grace — a natural gift conferred by nature or fortune, implying permanence and generosity.
- 18th–19th century (dignified formal use) - Flourishes in moral and biographical prose; Mill says he was “endowed with that intense mental energy” which so often accompanies bodily weakness — a compensating gift of nature.
- Contemporary - The double life persists: institutional/financial (an endowed chair, a richly endowed library) and personal/figurative (endowed with patience); still formal and dignified, implying what is given is given to last.
