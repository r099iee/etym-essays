- [c. 1590: arrival] - A Scandinavian loan (Danish dangle), the verb first means "to hang loosely and sway with one's own weight" — a vivid, playful physical word for keys, legs, and trinkets.
- [early 17th century: transitive] - "To dangle something" — to let it hang — and the participle dangling (c. 1600) quickly becomes the word's most familiar shape.
- [19th century: the bait] - The figurative turn: to dangle a reward, promise, or temptation before someone is to hold it out loosely and withdraw it at will — the connotation becomes coaxing and manipulative.
- [19th–20th century: the grammar book] - "Dangling participle" makes the -ing form a schoolmaster's byword for grammatically unanchored prose, giving the word a pedantic, faintly reproachful echo.
- [20th–21st century: the loose end] - Computing and management adopt it: a "dangling reference" points at nothing, a "dangling thread" is left unresolved — the word now names unfinished business as well as pendular motion.
