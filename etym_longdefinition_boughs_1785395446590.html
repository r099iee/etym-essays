BOUGHS is the plural of bough: the large, main structural limbs of a tree that spring directly from the trunk and bear the lesser twigs and foliage - the great arms of an oak, elm, or pine, thick enough to support weight, a man, a hammock, or a rope. In The Hobbit, the Eagles 'had brought up dry boughs for fuel', along with rabbits, hares, and a small sheep: the word is chosen with quiet precision. These are substantial pieces of wood, torn or cut into lengths for a camp-fire on the mountain - not twigs, not sticks, but limbs thick enough to feed a blaze. Bough is the traditional poetic term for a tree's main branches: where branch is merely factual, bough carries weight, age, and shelter, and in verse it is the word for the arms of an oak or elm that can bear a man, a hammock, or a rope. The anatomy behind the word is literal: in its Germanic past bough meant 'arm' or 'shoulder' - the arm of a tree reaching out from the trunk - a metaphor so ancient it has hardened into the dictionary. Tolkien, with his Old English training, keeps this older vocabulary alive, and the word suits the archaic, saga-like atmosphere of the chapter, grounding high adventure in the homely practical detail of gathering fuel. CORE SENSES AND POLYSEMY
  • Primary sense (arboreal): a large limb or branch of a tree springing from the trunk - the main boughs of an oak; a bird nesting in the high boughs.
  • Sense of timber / fuel (as in Tolkien): a bough cut or broken off as a piece of wood - dry boughs gathered for fuel; boughs lopped for firewood.
  • Poetic / archaic sense: the tree-limb as the 'arm' of the tree, the sense that gives the word its literary weight in verse and in writing that cultivates an older diction.
  • Figurative / romantic sense: the bough as a place of shelter, nesting, and repose - under the greenwood bough; the bough that bears the blossom, the cradle, or the fruit.
  • Homophone and spelling note: bough is pronounced exactly like bow (the act of bending and the tied ribbon), but the two words are etymologically unrelated: bow descends from Old English bugan 'to bend', while bough descends from the 'arm' word.
  • Register: bough is poetic and literary; branch is the neutral everyday word; the plural boughs appears chiefly in verse, in nature writing, and in prose seeking an older, more dignified tone.
POLYSEMY - THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE FROM ARM TO TREE-LIMB The whole character of bough rests on one ancient transfer. In the Germanic ancestor of the word, the same form meant 'arm' or 'shoulder' - the limb of a body - and the tree sense is a hardened metaphor: a bough is the arm of a tree, reaching out from the trunk as an arm reaches from a shoulder. The metaphor is so old that it has settled into the dictionary as plain fact, but its physical logic is intact: a bough, like an arm, is a strong, tapering limb that springs from a central body, bends under weight, and bears lesser growth on it. From this core the word developed no further transferred senses of its own; unlike branch, which sprouted a whole family of figurative meanings (a branch of a bank, a branch of knowledge, a branch of a family), bough stayed close to the tree. Its history is instead a history of register: as branch became the neutral everyday word for any limb at any size, bough narrowed to the largest structural arms that spring from the trunk and became the only word of its set with a consistent poetic flavour. A writer who says 'bough' instead of 'branch' is choosing weight, age, shelter, and a faint archaism. DISTINCTIONS AND REGISTER Branch is the neutral, all-purpose term for any limb of a tree at any size; twig is a small slender shoot at the tip; sprig and spray are small cut shoots; stick is any cut piece of wood; log is a length of cut trunk or thick branch; limb is more formal and chiefly American; and bough is restricted to the largest, structural arms that spring from the trunk and bear the lesser twigs and foliage. In the passage the word does the quiet work of period and tone: dry boughs for fuel is the homely, practical detail of a wilderness camp, and the ancient, dignified word keeps that practicality in the register of saga and legend rather than of ordinary household prose.
