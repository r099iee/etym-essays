Latin first, and not quite what it looks like. Everything about adage is decided by the Latin adagium, a word securely attested in classical comedy and prose from the first century BCE. Its range in Latin is wider than English ever allows: an adagium is a proverb, a maxim, a saying, a common observation — and also a remark made in jest, a quip, something said in sport. The form itself resists confident analysis. The ending -ium is the ordinary Latin neuter noun-suffix, found in imperium and consortium, so adagium is a noun of a kind rather than a diminutive of anything. The stem is the difficulty. Nineteenth-century English lexicographers, following continental precedent, derived it from ad + agere ‘to do’, which would give ‘a deed, a thing performed’ and then a motto attached to a deed; the derivation is attractive because it would explain a word about mottoes, and it appears in a great many reference works still. It does not, however, produce the shape of the word, since agere gives -ag- and not -adag-. Modern lexicography connects the word instead with the family of Latin verbs of saying, aiō, ait, aiunt, and behind them a reconstructed PIE *h₂ed- ‘to say, to tell’, whose consonants the word preserves. The competing proposals in the older literature are the price of a Latin word that nobody in antiquity ever explained.

French and the first two centuries in English. French passes the noun on in the thirteenth century as adage, ‘a proverb, a saying, a motto’, and English takes it up in the fourteenth. The Middle English adage is neutral, and often approving: it is the word for a piece of transmitted wisdom, the sort of thing a man of the world could produce on request. Fifteenth- and sixteenth-century writers extend the word in two directions. They apply it to a sentence of Scripture or a doctrinal formula regarded as decisive, so that an adage can be a citation with authority behind it; and they apply it to the words a family places over its arms, so that the adage becomes a function — the device on the shield — and not only a truth. The classical sense of a jest comes across at the same time, and there are seventeenth-century uses in which an adage is a piece of wordplay.

The reversal of the seventeenth century. Then the word turns. The process is worth describing, because it explains almost everything about modern adage. The seventeenth century is the century of the great classical education, of huge collections of proverbs, of Horatian wit, and of a satirical establishment that made mock-learning a genre. In such a culture, a maxim that everyone knows and nobody examines is a target, and the vocabulary of the target settled on this word. An adage became a saying that was worn out; the age which had made the word’s authority became the proof of its emptiness. The shift is not a replacement — the older sense never died — but it did become the default, and lexicographers have been reshuffling the entry ever since, with the contemptuous sense moving steadily upward in the list.

Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: the respectable middle. Alongside the dismissive sense, the eighteenth century kept a genuinely neutral use, and it is the use we hear in the essays of the period: the adage of the Romans, the adages of the ancients, an adage which has never been contradicted by experience. Here the word is not mocking; it is appraising. The nineteenth century, however, finds adage increasingly dusty, a word of a preceding age’s education, and the most common tone in which a Victorian or Edwardian writer uses it is a gentle reminder that the speaker has heard it before. This is the register that survives in the twentieth-century essays: a serviceable, if shopworn, adage.

Twentieth century: the word becomes a criticism. By the twentieth century the centre of gravity has moved decisively. An old adage is a phrase that almost always carries a small sneer, and adage has become interchangeable with platitude and truism. The word is now a piece of argumentative equipment: that is an adage, not a reason is a decisive move in a debate, because it questions the content of an argument by questioning its originality. The twentieth century also extends the noun to the canned wisdom of institutions, so that a broadcaster, a company or a sport can be said to trade in adages. Irony becomes the native register: to call something an adage is to compliment it with the appearance of gravity while announcing that it is empty.

Where it stands now. Present-day adage is a countable noun, almost always singular, and it is doing two jobs at once. It can name a saying with authority — an adage about the value of patience that every village kept — or it can name a stale commonplace, and in most modern sentences it is the second, with the first available for a mock-serious contrast. The word is literary, slightly bookish, and almost never heard in ordinary conversation; it survives most comfortably in debate, in essays about style and language, and in the vocabulary of advertising, where a slogan is an adage in waiting. The quoted context uses the word in its most traditional way, with the modern edge attached: the speaker reaches for a stock comparison between a man and a proverbial cat, and the name he gives to that stock comparison tells the listener in advance that nothing fresh is being said.

  • Phonology. The common British form is /ˈædɪdʟ/ with /dʟ/; a second pronunciation /ˈeɪdɪdʟ/ with a long first vowel is also standard, especially in careful speech, and American usage is divided between the two.
  • Accidence and collocation. Countable only in standard English: an adage, adages. Characteristic modifiers are old, familiar, well-worn, hoary, trite, common, current; characteristic frames are as the adage goes, it is an adage that, and the partitive after the adage.
  • Cognates. The same Latin noun produced adagio in the musical sense through Italian, which is a different word with a different etymology entirely and no connection to speech.
  • The word’s paradox.Adage is one of the few nouns in English whose principal meaning contains its own dismissal. A saying is called an adage because it is old, and it is resented because it is old, and the two judgments have never been entirely separated.
