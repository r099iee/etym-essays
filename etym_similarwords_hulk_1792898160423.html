• hull: Similar because both name the main body or shell of a ship or seed, big and enclosing; differs because hull is the precise structural term for the watertight body, while hulk stresses unwieldy bulk, age, or abandonment.
  • wreck: Similar because both describe ruined ships and by extension ruined things; differs because wreck stresses destruction and disaster, while hulk stresses the surviving mass, still standing and often still usable as shelter.
  • trunk: Similar because in the source sentence the hulk simply is the tree’s trunk grown vast; differs because trunk is neutral botany for any stem, while hulk adds enormity, hollowness, and a ship-like looming presence.
  • stump: Similar because both are what remains after limbs or masts are lost; differs because a stump is short, cut down, and ankle-high, while a hulk towers and encloses, big enough to walk into.
  • carcass: Similar because both name a body emptied of its life or workings, shell without engine; differs because carcass is usually dead meat or scrap, while a hulk may still live, as Tolkien’s leafing tree proves.
  • behemoth: Similar because both evoke something hugely bulky and daunting; differs because behemoth is biblical and often admiring of power, while hulk is dockside and plain, bulk as burden.
  • lumbering mass: Similar because both picture weight that moves clumsily or not at all; differs because lumbering mass is a descriptive phrase stressing motion, while hulk is one noun that already contains the bulk.
  • shell: Similar because a hulk is classically a shell, whether of ship or tree, emptied within; differs because shell is thin and brittle, while hulk is thick-walled, heavy, and enduring.
