- 17th to 18th century (New France & Explorers' Journals) - A novel geographic borrowing from French denoting the boundless, treeless meadows of the North American interior, evoking awe, vastness, and uncharted wilderness among French coureurs des bois and Jesuit missionaries.
- Late 18th to mid-19th century (Pioneer Settlement & Manifest Destiny) - Perceived as a fertile agrarian promised land waiting for the steel plow, romanticized in American literature as a majestic 'sea of grass' yet feared for its brutal blizzards, prairie fires, and profound geographic isolation.
- Late 19th to early 20th century (Industrial Agriculture & Frontier Myth) - Converted largely into the American Grain Belt, transforming the word's emotional resonance from wild sublime grassland to domestic homesteads ('Little House on the Prairie') and pastoral heartland identity.
- Late 20th century to Present (Conservation Biology & Metaphor) - Re-evaluated as one of the most endangered and biologically complex biomes on Earth, while expanding metaphorically into urban sociology ('urban prairie' for blighted open lots) and architectural design ('Prairie School').
