Snarls is the plural noun (and third-person verb form) of snarl in its growling sense: a series of low threatening growls with bared teeth — the warning sounds of dogs, wolves, and cornered creatures — and, by extension, the hostile utterances and twisted expressions that carry the same menace.

In the source sentence the narrator gathers intelligence from hints dropped among the snarls: the prisoner lets fragments of truth slip out between growls of resentment, so that information must be sifted from hostility. The plural matters — not one outburst but a texture of menace through which stray facts surface, like coins glinting in gravel. Reading between snarls is the interrogator's art: patience set against bared teeth.

Core meaning in detail: Each single snarl is one guttural warning — lips retracted, teeth exposed, breath harsh through nose and throat — and snarls in the plural names the repeated volley: a dog's snarls over its food, a captive's snarls between evasions. As a verb form it marks habitual menace (he snarls at every question), while as a noun it can also name the twisted facial expression itself (the snarls of stone gargoyles) and, figuratively, tangled hostile exchanges (their correspondence degenerated into snarls).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Repeated growls with bared teeth (primary sense, from the 1590s): the literal animal volley — the snarls of guard dogs, of wolves around a kill, of a prisoner growling between lies. This is the source-sentence sense: menace as background noise through which hints escape.
  • Hostile utterances (figurative extension): the snarls of the press, partisan snarls across the chamber — angry remarks delivered with audible ferocity. The bridge is acoustic: human anger borrows the growl's harshness, so a bitter exchange is heard as a pack quarrel.
  • Twisted hostile expressions (transferred sense): the snarls on the faces of carved temple dogs, a portrait's snarl caught in marble. The bridge carries the sound to the look that makes it — curled lip, wrinkled nose — freezing a moment of threat into lasting stone.
  • Tangles and knots — the other snarl (homograph warning, distinct origin): snarls in fishing line, snarls of barbed wire, and snarls of traffic belong to snarl meaning entanglement, descended from snare, and share nothing with the growl family but spelling. Context decides: snarls dropped with hints are growls, while snarls in a skein of wool are knots.
  • Heraldic and ornamental snarls (specialised artistic sense): blazoned beasts display snarls as open-jawed threat badges, and architects carve snarling masks over doorways to frighten evil away. The bridge is apotropaic: the warning sound, frozen in art, becomes a guardian's emblem.

Register and connotation: snarls is earthy and vivid — kennels, battlefields, playgrounds, press rooms — always carrying contempt for the snarler mixed with respect for the warning. The plural softens nothing: where a single snarl might be excused as a startled reflex, snarls imply settled hostility, a temper that keeps sounding. Against information, as in the source, snarls are static through which signal must be found, and the narrator's quiet triumph is that he listened anyway.
