- (c. 1400–) - "loop" enters English as a noun: a fold or doubling of a cord — the noose or running knot of sailors, hunters and weavers; a plain craft word with no glamour.
- (1500s–1600s) - "loophole" appears (1590s): a narrow slit in a fortress wall for shooting — "loop" in the sense of a narrow opening; by the 1600s the figurative "way of escape from a rule" is recorded, and the word begins its legal-jargon career.
- (1700s–1800s) - The verb "to loop" is born (19th century): to form a loop, of thread, wire and roads; rivers and railways "loop" across maps — the geographic sense of this card's sentence is now ordinary.
- (1903) - "Loop the loop" enters the language with aviation: the vertical circle of early aeroplanes, and the roller-coaster inversion; the word gains a thrill connotation.
- (1940s–) - Computing adopts "loop": a repeating sequence of instructions (for-loops, while-loops; "infinite loop" the classic bug); the word becomes technical machinery.
- (1950s–70s) - Music: tape loops in electronic composition (musique concrète, Steve Reich's tape pieces), then sample loops in hip-hop and dance — "looping" becomes a studio standard.
- (1970s–) - Management jargon: "in the loop / out of the loop" — informed or excluded; "feedback loop" enters systems thinking and everyday explanation.
- (1990s–today) - "Time loop" becomes a fiction staple (Groundhog Day, 1993); video loops and GIF loops in digital media; the verb "loops" still does its oldest job — a river bending round in a great curve.
