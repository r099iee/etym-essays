Stowed is the past tense and past participle of the verb stow: packed, stored, or put away in a designated space — typically tightly, neatly, and with an eye to the limited room available. The verb means to arrange things into the space they are meant to occupy — cargo into a hold, sails onto a spar, luggage into a compartment, guests into spare rooms — and the participle stowed reports that the arranging has been completed. The word carries a practical, slightly nautical or housekeeperly tone: it is the vocabulary of sailors stowing cargo and of careful householders stowing provisions, and it implies deliberate placement rather than mere disposal. In Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bilbo 'had to find room for them all, and filled all his spare-rooms and made beds on chairs and sofas, before he got them all stowed and went to his own little bed very tired and not altogether happy.' The participle is resultative: the chaotic influx of thirteen dwarves has been turned into temporary order — everyone found a place, tucked into the finite volume of a hobbit-hole — and only then may the exhausted host sleep, 'not altogether happy.' The tone is practical and faintly comic under the strain of guests, the past participle capturing a completed job of domestic logistics.

Polysemy — the range of senses of the base verb. The card form is the inflected participle, but a full definition must cover the verb it is built on:

  • Core: to pack or put away into a designated space — stow the luggage, stow the groceries. Emphasis on placement into limited or fixed volume.
  • Nautical: to arrange cargo, sails, or gear tightly and safely — stow the sails, stow the anchor — the age-of-sail sense from which the general meaning was reinforced; stowage (the space, the act, the charge) is the maritime jargon derived from it.
  • Aviation / travel jargon: stow your tray tables and luggage in safety instructions — the modern institutional use.
  • Stow away and the noun stowaway: to hide oneself aboard a ship, plane, or train to travel without paying — a distinct, well-known phrasal extension in which stow takes the meaning of concealment.
  • Colloquial imperative Stow it!: 'stop it, be quiet' (stow your gab), a nineteenth-century slang use in which stow means 'to put away', i.e. cease — the object is the noise or talk.
  • Figurative / domestic: people can be stowed in rooms or beds (as in the card context), goods stowed away for winter, and even memories or objects stowed in the mind.
  • Resultative participle: stowed as adjective — all safely stowed, fully stowed — describing a state of completed arrangement.

Semantic boundaries. The bridge among the senses is the single idea of deliberate placement into a designated space, from which concealment (stowaway), cessation (stow it), and safety (stow the sails) all develop by extension. Distinguish from near-synonyms: stored is general safekeeping without the tight-fit idea; packed stresses compression into containers or bundles; placed is neutral positioning anywhere; lodged (of people) stresses providing a place to stay and is more formal; housed stresses shelter or accommodation; stashed is informal and implies secrecy; cached is technical or computing register (a hidden store); tucked away is cozier, softer, and implies concealment or snugness; secured stresses making things fast or safe, not merely arranged; and dumped is the careless opposite. Stowed keeps the sense of deliberate, tidy placement into finite space — cargo logic applied even to guests — and the participle form belongs to the completed past of narrative, the job done before the tired host goes to his own little bed, 'not altogether happy.'
