1. The rubbing root. The deep ancestry of morsel lies in a Proto-Indo-European root *mer-, "to rub away, to crush, to harm, to wear down" — the root that also gives English murder and mortify, and ultimately the idea of death and decay. The semantic bridge to biting is physical: to bite is to crush, to wear away by the teeth, and the Latin verb mordere "to bite" was formed on this root, with a common extension *merd- reconstructed for the Latin branch. From mordere Latin produced morsus, the past participle "bitten," and then a noun morsus "a biting, a bite, the act or result of biting" — attested from the 1st century BCE, in Lucretius and others, where it can mean a bite of food, a bite-wound, or, figuratively, the sting of remorse. Already in Latin the "bite" was both a physical act and a sharp little thing.

2. The little bite: Vulgar Latin and the diminutive. The decisive step was the formation of a diminutive. On the noun morsus Latin built morsellum, "a little bite," with the affectionate suffix -ellum, and though this intermediate form is not directly attested, its existence is guaranteed by the uniform outcome in the Romance languages: Italian morsello, Spanish and Portuguese morsello, Occitan and Old French morsel. A diminutive is never semantically neutral: it marks the thing as small, fond, and choice, and morsellum — "a nice little bite" — carried that tenderness from birth. The diminutive is the whole story of the word: morsel is a bite made precious by being tiny.

3. Old French and the Middle English arrival. Old French morsel (11th–12th centuries) means "a small portion of food, a bite," and also "a piece, a fragment" of other things. Anglo-Norman carried it into England, and Middle English morsel is attested from about 1300. From the beginning English uses it warmly and concretely: a morsel of bread, a morsel of meat, the morsel that sustains a traveller or a poor man. In medieval and early modern texts the word lives close to the table — Chaucer's pilgrims eat morsels, the hungry man of the psalm waits for a morsel — and the smallness is always accompanied by worth: a morsel is never garbage; it is the good small bit.

  • c. 1300: Middle English morsel, "a mouthful, a small piece of food," borrowed from Old French.
  • 14th–17th centuries: the sense widens from food to any small, worth-having fragment — a morsel of comfort, a morsel of land — smallness plus value.
  • 16th–17th centuries: "not a morsel" becomes an emphatic idiom for "nothing at all," a use that persists into the present.

4. The literature of the mouthful. In the 18th and 19th centuries morsel becomes a word of narrative prose, fairy tales, and old novels — the thrifty traveller's mouthful, the child's saved crust, the choice titbit at a feast. It is the food-word of the hungry hero and of the housekeeper's careful economy, and it carries the same fond, diminutive register in Dickens, in the Brontës, and in countless adventure stories. C. S. Lewis's use — "He ate a morsel and continued his journey" — is exactly this tradition: the modest, savory, sustaining little bite before the road, a word that makes the reader feel both the hunger and the sufficiency.

5. The figurative widening. The step from "a small portion of food" to "a small portion of anything" is short and old. From the 17th century onward a morsel can be a morsel of truth, of comfort, of news, of gossip — a small, valuable scrap of something non-edible. The semantic rule is constant: smallness plus desirability. A morsel of truth is a rare, real grain inside a mass of falsehood; a morsel of news is a choice item. The word never names refuse or waste — only the good small bit — which is why "not a morsel" works as total negation: even the smallest good thing is wholly absent.

6. The modern word. In contemporary English morsel is slightly archaic and refined. Everyday speech prefers a bite, a snack, or a piece; morsel survives in the warm, literary, faintly old-fashioned register, in cooking and recipe prose (a morsel of cake), in idiom (not a morsel), and in its figurative collocations (a morsel of truth). Its modern French cognate morceau has fared differently — it now simply means "a piece" — but English has kept the diminutive tenderness of "a little bite" for over seven centuries, a small word that has never outgrown its fondness and that still pictures, exactly as it did in medieval narrative, the good small bite that keeps a traveller going.
