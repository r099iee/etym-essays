The word infinitesimal embodies one of the most fascinating journeys in the history of ideas. Coined deliberately in seventeenth-century mathematical Latin to solve the problem of continuous motion, it triggered a furious philosophical firestorm, was banished from mathematical foundations by nineteenth-century purists, and was ultimately vindicated by twentieth-century mathematical logic.

1. Indo-European Roots: The Boundary Stake

The ultimate morphological ancestors of infinitesimal are two Proto-Indo-European elements: the negative prefix *ne- ("not") and the verbal root *dʰeygʷ-, meaning "to stick, pierce, fasten, or drive a stake into the ground" (which also yielded English dike and ditch). In Proto-Italic, this root developed into *fīgnis and subsequently into Classical Latin finis, meaning a boundary post, dividing marker, frontier, or end.

From finis was derived the verb finire ("to limit, bound, terminate") and its participial adjective finitus ("bounded, limited"). In Classical Latin, attaching the negative prefix in- ("not, un-") produced infinitus, signifying that which has no boundaries, borders, or end—limitless, boundless, and infinite. For classical antiquity, infinity was an awe-inspiring, often terrifying concept associated with chaos and the unmeasurable cosmos.

2. The 17th-Century Scientific Crisis: Taming the Continuous

By the early seventeenth century, the birth of modern physics—pioneered by Galileo Galilei, Johannes Kepler, and René Descartes—confronted a formidable mathematical roadblock. Ancient Greek Euclidean geometry was designed for static forms (circles, triangles, spheres). However, the new physics demanded mathematical tools to describe dynamic, accelerating motion: planets orbiting in ellipses with continuously varying velocities, cannonballs tracing parabolic trajectories, and the rates of fluid flow.

Mathematicians sought methods to calculate areas under irregular curves (the problem of quadrature) and slopes of tangents. Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri proposed his "geometry of indivisibles" (1635), imagining surfaces as composed of an infinite number of parallel lines of zero thickness. While practically effective, Cavalieri's indivisibles lacked rigorous logical foundations.

3. Nicholas Mercator's Coinage: The "Infinite-th" Part

The precise word infinitesimus was coined in 1668 by the German-Danish mathematician Nicholas Mercator (Niklaus Kauffman) in his groundbreaking treatise Logarithmotechnia. Mercator sought to compute natural logarithms by calculating the area under a hyperbola. To express the idea of dividing a finite line segment into an infinite number of microscopic parts, he ingeniously combined infinitus with the standard Latin ordinal/fractional suffix -esimus.

In Latin, ordinal numerals were formed with -esimus: vigesimus meant "twentieth," centesimus meant "hundredth," and millesimus meant "thousandth." By analogy, Mercator constructed infinitesimus to signify literally the "infinite-th" part—the fraction $1/\infty$. In his text, he referred to the infinitesima pars as the vanishingly small increment of a geometric curve.

4. Leibniz, Newton, and the Calculus Explosion

Shortly after Mercator's coining, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz embraced the term and made it the cornerstone of his differential calculus. In his seminal 1684 paper *Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis*, Leibniz introduced the differential symbol $dx$, defining it as an infinitesimal difference in the variable $x$. Leibniz treated these infinitesimals as operational quantities that were smaller than any assignable number, yet obeyed standard rules of algebra.

Concurrently, in England, Sir Isaac Newton formulated his method of "fluxions" and "evanescent increments," exploring identical concepts under different terminology. English mathematicians rapidly adopted Mercator's and Leibniz's Latin terminology, anglicizing infinitesimus with the adjectival suffix -al to yield infinitesimal, first attested in English philosophical and scientific transactions between 1705 and 1710.

5. Bishop Berkeley and the 18th-Century Philosophical Tempest

The rapid success of infinitesimal calculus provoked profound intellectual alarm among theologians and classical philosophers. In 1734, the Anglo-Irish philosopher Bishop George Berkeley published his devastating critique titled The Analyst; or, a Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician. Berkeley aimed his attack directly at Newton and Leibniz, arguing that calculus rested on logically incoherent mysteries far more absurd than Christian theology.

Berkeley delivered one of the most famous broadsides in the history of science: "And what are these fluxions? The velocities of evanescent increments. And what are these same evanescent increments? They are neither finite quantities, nor quantities infinitely small, nor yet nothing. May we not call them the ghosts of departed quantities?" Berkeley pointed out that mathematicians treated an infinitesimal as a non-zero quantity when dividing by it, but suddenly treated it as zero when discarding higher-order terms ($dx^2$), creating a fatal logical contradiction.

6. The 19th-Century Ban: The Epsilon-Delta Revolution

Shaken by Berkeley's critique, nineteenth-century European mathematicians set out to rebuild mathematical analysis on an unassailable foundation. Led by Augustin-Louis Cauchy, Bernhard Bolzano, and Karl Weierstrass, they successfully banished literal infinitesimals from the foundations of calculus.

Weierstrass introduced the rigorous epsilon-delta ($\epsilon$-$\delta$) definition of limits: instead of claiming that $dx$ is a tiny, non-zero static quantity, calculus was reinterpreted as a dynamic statement about limits—a quantity can be made arbitrarily close to a target by making the input sufficiently close. For nearly a century, textbook writers insisted that infinitesimals did not exist in formal mathematics, even as physicists, engineers, and everyday speakers continued using the word to describe microscopic quantities.

7. The 20th-Century Triumph: Abraham Robinson's Non-Standard Analysis

The story reached a breathtaking climax in 1960, when logician Abraham Robinson published his revolutionary framework of Non-Standard Analysis. Using the Compactness Theorem of mathematical logic, Robinson proved that the real number system can be rigorously extended into the hyperreal numbers ($\mathbb{R}^*$), which contain mathematically rigorous, logically consistent infinitesimal numbers that are smaller than every positive real number, exactly as Leibniz had envisioned three centuries earlier.

Today, infinitesimal stands triumphant across two realms: in pure mathematics and mathematical logic as a fully validated, rigorous tool; and in natural language as an evocative, universally recognized term for the vanishingly small margins that shape the universe.
