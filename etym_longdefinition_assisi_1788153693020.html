Assisi (/əˈsiːzi/; Italian Assisi, from Latin Asisium) is a small town of about 28,000 people on the western slope of Monte Subasio, overlooking the Umbrian plain above the river Topino, some 24 km east of Perugia. In ordinary use the word does three jobs at once: it names a place (the town and commune), a shrine (the Basilica of San Francesco and the whole Franciscan complex), and — most powerfully — a symbol (the cradle of the Franciscan movement and, since 1986, an international emblem of interfaith peace).

Primary sense — the town. Assisi is a classic Umbrian hill town of reddish-pink stone (pietra rossa), with Roman walls and grid still legible in the centre: the surviving Roman temple of Minerva (now the church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva), the medieval Rocca Maggiore fortress above the town, the Gothic Basilica of Santa Chiara, and the Eremo delle Carceri in the woods of Subasio above it. The town was an Umbrian settlement taken by Rome (the municipium of Asisium), then a Lombard and papal town through the Middle Ages, and part of the Papal States until Italian unification in 1860.

Secondary sense — the Franciscan shrine. Assisi is inseparable from two saints. St Francis (Francesco, baptized Giovanni) was born there c. 1181 and died there in 1226; St Clare (Chiara) was born 1194 and founded the Poor Clares there. The Basilica of San Francesco, begun in 1228 and consecrated in 1253, houses the saint’s tomb and the celebrated fresco cycles by Cimabue, Giotto and their workshops (the Legend of St Francis in the Upper Church), making the town one of the great art-historical monuments of Europe. The whole Franciscan complex — basilica, Sacred Convent and its library — was placed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2000.

Tertiary senses and extensions.

  • Religious metonymy: ‘Assisi’ commonly stands for the Franciscan spirit — poverty, peace, care of creation — as when people speak of ‘the poverty of Assisi’ or ‘the peace of Assisi’.
  • The ‘spirit of Assisi’: since 1986, when Pope John Paul II convened the World Day of Prayer for Peace there with leaders of the world’s religions, the town’s name has been shorthand for interreligious dialogue — the annual Assisi meetings, the ‘Pact of Assisi’, ‘Assisi 2011’.
  • Devotional place-names worldwide: churches, parishes, retreat houses and hospitals named ‘San Francesco d’Assisi’ or simply ‘Assisi’ spread the word across the Catholic world.
  • Everyday Italian idiom:essere come San Francesco (to be simple and good-natured) and the phrase il santo di Assisi (‘the holy man of Assisi’) keep the town-name alive in speech.

Semantic bridge. The later meanings all derive from one historical fact: Francis was born and died in the town, and his order was born there. A place-name became a saint’s cognomen (‘Francis of Assisi’), the cognomen passed into piety, piety built the basilica, the basilica drew pilgrims and art, art brought tourism, and the 20th-century papacy converted the accumulated sanctity into a global symbol of peace. The town-name behaves like a great brand-name of the spirit: concrete place first, symbol second.

Register and tone. In geography the word is neutral; in Catholic and Franciscan writing it is solemn and affectionate; in journalism it means ‘dialogue among religions’ (‘the spirit of Assisi’); and in tourism advertising it means ‘medieval Umbria at its loveliest’. It remains a proper noun throughout, but its symbolic freight makes it usable in metaphor: ‘a little Assisi’, ‘Assisi of the East’, and similar transfers.
