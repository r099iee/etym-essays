- Late 14th c. — Larceny enters English law from Anglo-Norman; it names the felonious taking of another's goods and is prosecuted as a felony, with benefit of clergy available to the literate.
- 16th–18th c. — The grand/petty distinction hardens around the twelve-penny threshold; Blackstone's Commentaries (1765–69) give the offence its classic exposition, and larceny becomes a staple of the assize courts.
- 19th c. — Statutes begin to codify and simplify the old common-law rules, culminating in the Larceny Acts of 1861 and 1916; transportation and hanging give way to imprisonment.
- 1968 — England and Wales abolish larceny by the Theft Act 1968, replacing it with the single offence of theft. The word survives in Scottish and American law and in historical writing.
- Late 20th–21st c. — American states retain grand and petty larceny, often graded by dollar value, while journalism uses "literary larceny" and "petty larceny" figuratively for plagiarism and small-scale corruption.
