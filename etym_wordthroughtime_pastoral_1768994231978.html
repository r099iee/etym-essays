- 15th c.: pastoral enters English from Old French, from Latin pastorālis, “of a shepherd” — the literal sense only: shepherds, flocks, and rural life, with no ideal yet attached.
- 16th–17th c.: the literary revolution — following Theocritus and Virgil, pastoral poetry idealizes shepherd life, and ‘pastoral’ becomes the name of the genre and its golden world: Arcadia, innocent ease, the retreat from the city; at the same time the church sense grows, the pastor being the shepherd of souls.
- 18th c.: the genre expands into painting and music; pastoral now connotes idealized rustic simplicity, a polite nostalgia for a countryside that never quite was.
- 19th c.: the word is increasingly turned on real countrysides, often with condescension (“pastoral charm”); the religious sense stays standard in ministry, and a strain appears between the ideal and the actual working land.
- 20th–21st c.: the ideal is usually invoked with irony — a “pastoral landscape” can be flat working farmland stripped of Arcadia, and the word now signals a broken or exhausted dream as often as it describes a place.
