• Club: Similar because it is a blunt hand weapon swung to strike; differs because it is cruder and often wooden, while mace implies metal head and martial or ceremonial status.
  • Cudgel: Similar because it is a short thick stick for beating; differs because it is rustic and improvised, lacking flanged head and official symbolism.
  • Bludgeon: Similar because it is a heavy striking weapon; differs because it emphasizes brutal violence and criminal use rather than knightly or civic dignity.
  • Truncheon: Similar because it is a short staff of police and parade; differs because it is modern constabulary baton, straight and plain, not flanged war-head.
  • Baton: Similar because it marks command and is carried in hand; differs because it is slender for conducting, relay, or policing, not crushing armor.
  • Morning star: Similar because it is a spiked-ball head on shaft; differs because it often has chain or longer spikes and suggests more savage medieval infantry arm.
  • Flail: Similar because it is a jointed striking weapon with weighted head; differs because it swings on hinge or chain, distinct mechanics from rigid mace.
  • War hammer: Similar because it concentrates force on armored foe with metal head; differs because it has hammer-face and pick, specialized for piercing plate.
  • Sceptre: Similar because it is a ceremonial rod of sovereignty; differs because it is slender and jeweled symbol of kingship, not derived from crushing weapon.
  • Wand of office: Similar because it signals authority in hand; differs because it suggests magic or usher duties, lighter and less coercive than mace.
  • Staff: Similar because civic maces are processional staves; differs because staff is generic support or pastoral emblem, while mace specifies heavy head and enforcement.
  • Gavel: Similar because it is a small hammer symbol of presiding authority; differs because it is struck on sounding block in deliberation, not carried as guard weapon.
  • Bulawa / hetman's mace: Similar because it is the Eastern European ceremonial mace of command; differs because it is a specific national insignia of Cossack and Polish hetmans.
  • Gada: Similar because Indian gada is translated as mace for exercise and myth (Hanuman, Bhima); differs because it is longer training-club tradition, not Western parliamentary emblem.
