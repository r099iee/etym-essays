RAMPARTS — The rampart begins as an act of making ready. The PIE root *perh₃- (also written *perə-) meant 'to produce, to procure, to bring forth' — the gesture of providing, of causing something to be at hand — and it survives in Sanskrit pṛṇāti, 'he grants, bestows', one of the root's most often cited cognates. Latin built from it parāre, 'to make ready, to prepare', a verb of enormous fecundity: it gave English prepare, apparatus (the things made ready), pare (to trim something into readiness), and parent — for Latin parere, 'to give birth', is the same root, the mother as the one who brings forth. It also gave the defensive vocabulary: parāre, 'to ward off', passed through Old French parer into English parry, the swordsman's act of turning aside a thrust.

In the vulgar Latin of the provinces, parāre was reinforced by the prefix in- to make *imparāre, 'to make ready, to defend', which became Old French emparer, 'to take possession of, to fortify' — the gesture of seizing a place and putting it into a state of defence. Then French added re- and produced remparer, 'to fortify again, to re-fence', and from the verb came the noun rempart, the fortification itself: the wall raised in preparation against attack. English borrowed rempart as rampart in the sixteenth century, at first as a strictly military engineering term for the raised embankment of earth thrown up before a wall. The word thus carries inside it the entire logic of defence: a rampart is a prepared place, a brought-forth barrier, the earth and stone of readiness itself.

The sixteenth to eighteenth centuries widened the word from the single embankment to the whole fortified circuit — wall, parapet, and walkway together — making it the standard word of siege warfare and fortress building. From the camp it moved to the page: by the nineteenth century the ramparts of a city, the ramparts of heaven, even the ramparts of an argument, had become the poetic word for any great defensive wall. In Peake's sentence the ramparts of Gormenghast are the physical embodiment of that preparation — the point where the castle has made itself ready against the swarm, and the hovels are held back by the mere presence of prepared stone. The sound history is slight: French rempart, pronounced with nasalised vowels, was Anglicised as rampart, the medial -m- of the French prefix re- settling into the familiar English spelling; the medial consonant cluster simplified and the final -t was kept in spelling but softened in pronunciation. Across the whole family — prepare, apparatus, parent, parry, rampart — the root's original sense of 'bringing forth' can still be heard: a rampart is what a garrison brings forth, from the earth itself, against the enemy.

Literary attestations. The word's greatest English career is patriotic and poetic: the ramparts of the national anthem that holds them as the emblem of liberty, the ramparts of the frontier fortresses that fill the adventure novels of the nineteenth century, the ramparts of heaven and of the soul in devotional prose — the word has been the sound of walls for five centuries of English verse and prose. Its whole history of preparation is audible in it: a rampart is not a wall that has always been there but a wall that was made, brought forth, re-fortified against an enemy. In Peake it carries that accumulated weight: the ramparts of Gormenghast are the last wall between the stronghold and the swarm, and the word's martial dignity — its prepared, brought-forth, re-fortified stone — is what holds the sentence together against the epidemic that presses on it.

HOVELS — The hovel is a word with a lost parentage. It appears in English in the fifteenth century, first as a rough shed for cattle — a roofed shelter for beasts — and its etymology is genuinely obscure. The most probable account connects it with Old English hof, 'enclosure, yard, court, dwelling', a word of Proto-Germanic *hufą, which survives in German Hof, 'court, farmyard', and Dutch hof, 'court, garden': the hovel would then be a diminutive, 'a little court, a little enclosed shelter', formed with the common -el suffix. But the connection is not certain — no unrecorded Old English *hofel is attested, and some scholars prefer a continental Germanic source for the word — and no PIE ancestry for *hufą can be established at all. A speculative link to PIE *keu-, 'to bend, to vault', has been proposed, on the strength of the curved roofs of such shelters, but it is generally rejected, and the honest verdict is that hovel is a Germanic word of obscure internal history.

What is certain is the word's social career, which is a study in degradation. First a cattle-shed: crude, dark, and animal-grade, barely a building at all. Then, by the sixteenth century, the transfer was made to the houses of the poor, and the shed's roughness and darkness came with it: a hovel became a sub-house, barely fit for human habitation, the architecture of poverty. In the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries the word settled into its modern contemptuous register — a small, mean, ramshackle dwelling, the word itself a verdict. Unlike cottage (which retains rural charm) or shack (which can be picturesque), hovel never recovers: it is the chronicler's term when he wants no pity and no romance, the exact antonym of palace. In Peake's sentence the hovels of Gormenghast's shadow 'laid hold on the great walls', and the word's buried history does the work: these are not quaint dwellings but animal-grade shelters pressing against the stronghold, the shed of the poor clamped to the skin of the castle. The phonetic history is slight — hovel from hove + -el shows the weakening of the final vowel and the standard Middle English loss of final -e — but the semantic history is a long slide from the beast's shed to the verdict on the poor.

Literary and social history. The hovel is a word of the industrial age's conscience. As the cities of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries swelled with the poor, the hovel became the standard indictment of housing — the hovels of the slums, the hovels of the Irish peasantry, the hovel where the factory hand 'lived'. Dickens, Gaskell, and the great social novelists used the word as a fixed accusation, and it passed into the language of reform: a man's house might be a cottage, a hut, or a hovel, and the distinction was a moral one. The word's contemptuous force has never softened: no poet has ever found a use for 'a charming hovel', and its very ugliness is its purpose. In Peake's Gormenghast the hovels that swarm around the castle walls are the housing of the mean and the poor, and the word's long history of judgment — from the cattle-shed to the slum — makes the reader feel the castle's cold eye on the swarm before the sentence is half done.

LIMPETS — The limpet's name is a marine borrowing with a strange history of mistaken identity. The chain runs from PIE *lab-, 'to lick', the root attested in Latin lambere, 'to lick', Greek láptein, 'to lap, lick', and Old English lapian, 'to lap'. From lambere, Late Latin formed lampetra, the lamprey — traditionally analysed as lambere 'to lick' plus petra 'rock', the 'rock-licker', because the lamprey, with its round sucker-mouth, appears to lick the rocks to which it fastens. The derivation, though repeated for centuries, is not certain, and the true origin of lampetra is genuinely debated. What matters for our word is the extraordinary transfer that followed. Medieval Latin developed the form lampreda (with the -p-r- metathesis), and both the lamprey and the limpet were borrowed into Old English from the same Medieval Latin stock before 1000 CE: the lamprey came in as lampreda, and the limpet came in as lempedu, the very same word, because to the Anglo-Saxons the small conical shell clamped to the tidal rock was evidently the same clinging creature as the eel-like fish clamped to the river stone. Two creatures were collapsed into one name: the limpet literally carries the lamprey's word.

The Middle English forms lempet and lempid stabilised in the fourteenth century, and from then on the limpet was a settled natural-history term for the small conical mollusc (Patella and its allies) that grips tidal rocks with its powerful muscular foot. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries naturalists fixed the word to the patellid gastropods, and it remained a strictly zoological term in ordinary use. But the figurative career of the word has outgrown the literal. Because the limpet clamps itself to its rock with an adhesion that cannot be pried loose without violence, the word became the emblem of stubborn clinging: a limpet is anything — or anyone — that grips blindly, tenaciously, immovably, and in the twentieth century the naval term limpet mine gave the word its sharpest technical extension, naming a small adhesive explosive that is attached by suction or magnetism to a ship's hull and clings there until it destroys the vessel. In Peake's sentence the innermost hovels 'laid hold on the great walls, clamping themselves thereto like limpets to a rock': the word summons the whole history — the rock-licking lamprey, the clinging shell, the stubborn adhesion — and lets the mean dwellings grip the castle wall with the blind, patient, immovable tenacity of the shore. From the sucker-mouth of a river fish to the hovels under Gormenghast, the limpet has never stopped clinging.

Literary and figurative career. The limpet has become, in modern English, the great emblem of immovable stubbornness. 'To cling like a limpet' is a fixed idiom of the language, applied to everything from persistent lovers to inflexible officials, and the naval limpet mine gave the word a brief, explosive celebrity in the Second World War, when commandos fixed these little clinging bombs to enemy hulls and watched them bite into the steel. In Peake's sentence the hovels 'clamping themselves thereto like limpets to a rock' are made, by one word, into living creatures of the shore — patient, blind, tenacious — and the castle wall becomes the rock that has always borne them. The image is one of the strangest in the passage: architecture clinging like sea-life, the mean and the poor gripping the stone of the stronghold with the adhesion of a shell, and the word's long history — from the rock-licking lamprey to the underwater mine — compressed into a single comparison of a house to a mollusc.
