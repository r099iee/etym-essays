- 13th c. - the noun gutter (Middle English goter) enters English for a water-channel along a roof edge or street - a groove that collects falling water and carries it away.
- 18th c. - the verb appears among chandlers and housekeepers: "to gutter", of a candle, to burn unevenly so the melted wax runs down the side in a channel; a precise, domestic, technical extension of the drainage sense.
- 19th c. - the noun is moralised: "the gutter" becomes the lowest channel of society - gutter press, gutter politics, to live in the gutter; the word takes on a social register of sinking, waste, and decline.
- 19th–20th c. - the candle-sense spreads into literature: a guttering flame, a guttering hope; writers adopt the verb for anything wavering toward extinction, especially the dying of light and of life.
- 20th–21st c. - electric light makes the candle-sense rare and literary; gutter survives in its physical channels (roof, street, bowling lane) and in the moral metaphor of degradation, its original candle-meaning preserved in period prose and poetry.
