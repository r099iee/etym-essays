The name Rhine is first of all a hydronym — a river-name — and river-names are the most conservative words in any language, often surviving every change of population that washes over them. The attested record takes us back to the Gauls: Roman authors report the river as Rhenus, and behind that Latin form lies the Gaulish (Continental Celtic) word Rēnos, 'that which flows'. Rēnos was not originally a proper name at all but a descriptive common noun applied to running water; the Celts, who named rivers by their visible behaviour, gave the great stream the same word they gave to any flowing water. Underlying it is the old Indo-European root *h₃reyH-, 'to flow, run, stream', which is no invented concept but a genuinely attested family: it surfaces in Greek ῥέω (rhéō, 'I flow', whence rheum, rheumatism, rhythm, and the rheo- of rheostat and rheology), in Latin rīvus ('stream', whence derive, rival, rivulet), in Vedic Sanskrit rīyate ('flows') and ríti- ('stream, course'), in Old Irish rían ('sea, way, path' — a flowing), and in the Germanic *rinnaną ('to run, flow', the ancestor of English run) and Old English rīð ('stream, brook', literally 'a runner of water'). The Rhine is thus one member of a pan-Indo-European family of flowing words, but its own identity is that of a place-name borne on Celtic lips. The Roman encounter. When the Romans advanced to the great river, they Latinized the Gaulish name as Rhenus, spelling it with an h under the influence of the Greek form Ῥῆνος, which the Greek geographers had already fixed. Rhenus became a stock boundary-word of the Roman world: the Rhine and the Danube (Rhenus et Danubius) were the two rivers that framed the empire's continental frontier, and Rhenus figures constantly in Caesar, Tacitus, and the imperial geographers. The adjective Rhenānus, 'of the Rhine', produced the Rhenani populi and eventually English Rhenish, the archaic adjective still attached to the white wines of the valley. The river's role as frontier was political, military, and ultimately linguistic: when the empire collapsed, the Romanised provinces west of the Rhine kept their Latin speech, which evolved into French and Walloon, while the Germanic peoples of the right bank kept theirs — exactly the boundary Macaulay draws in the 19th century when he writes that 'from the Atlantic to the vicinity of the Rhine the Latin has, during many centuries, been predominant.' The Germanic borrowing. Meanwhile the Germanic peoples, settling along the river's lower course, borrowed the same Celtic name into their own speech as *Rīnaz — proof that the hydronym predated and outlived its coiners. Old High German Rīn became Middle High German Rīn and modern German Rhein; Old Saxon Rīn became Middle Dutch Rīn and modern Dutch Rijn; Old Norse Rín appears in the Norse sagas. Old English Rīn carried the name into English as early as the Anglo-Saxon period, and Middle English Rine kept it; the modern English spelling Rhine is a re-Latinization, restoring the -h- of Latin Rhenus. This is why the English name pairs an inherited Germanic form (Rīn) with a classicizing spelling — a double history in a single word, and a textbook illustration of how a river-name can pass from one language to another without being replaced. Cultural biography. From the Renaissance onward the Rhine entered English letters as the grand frontier-river of continental history, the artery of the Holy Roman Empire, the highway of wine, timber, and trade that English travellers crossed on the Grand Tour. The 19th century transformed it into the supreme Romantic landscape: the Lorelei of Heine's ballad, the ruined castles of the Mittelrhein, the legends of the Nibelungen (Wagner's Das Rheingold), and the picturesque vistas celebrated in English travel writing. In the 20th century the word became political shorthand again — the Rhineland, the Rhine crisis of 1840, the remilitarisation of 1936 — as the ancient frontier returned to the headlines. Today, in English, Rhine names the busiest waterway in Europe and, in historical prose such as Macaulay's, the invisible wall between the Roman and the non-Roman West: a Celtic word of flowing water that became one of the enduring frontiers of European civilisation.
