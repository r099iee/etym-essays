- [Medieval military origin (14th c.)] - From Old French forrier, a forager, and forager's feed (Latin furare, "to steal"), a foray was a raid for plunder and fodder — medieval knights riding out to seize food from enemy fields, a hostile, violent expedition.
- [Broadening to any swift raid (16th–18th c.)] - The military sense widened: a foray came to be any sudden attack or incursion beyond one's own line — still aggressive, but no longer tied to fodder or plunder.
- [Peaceful extension to excursions (19th c.)] - Foray began its slide toward the benign, describing a brief venture or excursion into unfamiliar territory — still short, purposeful, and slightly risky, but no longer martial.
- [Modern intellectual sense] - Today forays is most naturally the scholar's word for brief excursions into neighbouring fields — a philologist's "forays into the domains of linguistics, psychology, and music." The medieval raid for fodder survives only as a faint trace of boldness and risk, wholly stripped of hostility.
