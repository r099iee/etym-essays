A ravine is a place where the earth has been seized and torn, and its name remembers the violence. The lineage begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *rep-, 'to snatch, tear away, rush', which in Latin produced the famous verb rapere, 'to seize and carry off by force' — the root, through the participle raptus, of English rape and rapid, and through rapina, 'robbery, plunder', of rapine. From rapina Old French developed the noun ravine, whose earliest meanings all concerned violent seizure and violent motion: 'robbery, rapine', and then, applied to water, 'a violent rush of water, a waterfall, an avalanche'. The same stock produced the verb raviner, 'to pillage; to sweep down, to cascade'.

A word borrowed twice. English met the word early but in its greedy sense: from about 1350 Middle English ravin or ravine meant 'booty, plunder, robbery', and the verb raven, 'to devour greedily', survives today in ravenous and ravening. Meanwhile in France the water-sense was hardening: a ravine was the violent flash-flood that tears soil away, and by 1680 French ravin meant a gully. The decisive leap — from the force of water to the scar it leaves — occurred in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, so that the word came to name the result rather than the cause: a long, deep gorge worn by a torrent. English adopted that sense in 1760, though an English dictionary of 1610 had already recorded ravine as 'a raging flood', showing both meanings coexisting for a century.

Semantic engine: metonymy. The semantic engine is metonymy: the torrent's rapina, its plundering seizure of earth and stone, leaves behind a channel that is itself called a ravine — the scar keeps the name of the robbery. Latin rapidus, 'swift, tearing', reinforced the word's association with rushing water, so that rapidity and rapine run together in the ravine's etymology; the same root-family gives English ravish and rapacious, and the violent core of the word is never quite laundered away — a ravine is still felt as a place that has been torn, not merely carved.

Sound and form. English borrowed the word with the French pronunciation and spelling: ravine keeps its final silent -e, now pronounced with a stressed diphthong /iː/ in the modern form (/ɹəˈviːn/), the vowel having followed the French long i through the standard English treatment of loanwords. The plural ravines is ordinary. The doublet ravin (plunder sense, later ravine in spelling) shows how a single French word could split into two English words by time of borrowing.

Socio-cultural life. In the nineteenth century the word entered Romantic and geological vocabulary as an image of sublime, forbidding terrain — the deep, sheer-sided gorge was a favourite scene of awe in landscape poetry and travel writing, darker and more final than a gully, less grand than a canyon. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the plural ravines to cap the escalation from gullies to impassable chasms: 'dark ravines that one could neither jump over nor climb into' — the double impossibility mirrors the word's own double history (plunder and torrent) and its violent root, so that the sheer walls feel torn out of the rock by the very force that named them.
