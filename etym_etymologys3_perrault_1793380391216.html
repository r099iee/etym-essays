The surname Perrault encapsulates a dual etymological and cultural trajectory: linguistically, it represents a classic medieval French patronymic diminutive rooted in biblical and classical onomastics; historically and rhetorically, it became one of European literature's most contested symbols during the dawn of modern cultural consciousness.

The deepest etymological layer of the name originates in Proto-Indo-European *pétros, signifying "a stone, rock, or crag." In Ancient Greek, this developed into the masculine noun πέτρος (pétros, an isolated stone or boulder) alongside the feminine πέτρα (pétra, a solid rock mass or cliff). In the first century CE, the Gospel writers adopted the Greek masculine noun Pétros as a direct translation of the Aramaic name Kēfā (Cephas, "rock"), bestowed by Jesus upon the Apostle Simon (Matthew 16:18: "Thou art Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church"). With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, the Latinized form Petrus spread across Western Europe, becoming one of the most prolific and prestigious male baptismal names of the Middle Ages.

As Latin evolved into Old French between the ninth and twelfth centuries, Petrus underwent systematic Gallo-Romance phonological shifts—including the lenition of intervocalic consonants, the loss of case endings, and diphthongization—yielding the standard Old French name Pierre. Because Pierre was exceedingly common across all social strata in medieval France, speakers frequently attached affectionate hypocoristic suffixes to distinguish individuals. One of the most popular diminutive suffixes was -ot (and its regional variants -aud and -ault, influenced by the Germanic nominal ending *-wald or *-ald). This suffixation generated the diminutive forms Perrot, Perraut, and Perrault, meaning literally "Little Peter" or "Son of Peter." By the sixteenth century, as the French Crown mandated fixed hereditary surnames in parish registers (formalized by the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts in 1539), Perrault became codified as a hereditary bourgeois patronymic, with the silent orthographic l retained in northern French chancery script.

The transformation of this patronymic into an iconic literary and rhetorical proper noun occurred through the career of Charles Perrault (1628–1703) and unfolded across several decisive historical movements:

  • The Grand Siècle and Colbert's Administration: In seventeenth-century Paris, the Perrault family rose to intellectual prominence. Charles Perrault served as chief administrative clerk to Jean-Baptiste Colbert, playing a decisive role in founding the Royal Academy of Sciences and the Academy of Inscriptions. His brother Claude Perrault achieved renown as the royal architect who designed the magnificent Colonnade of the Louvre.
  • The Outbreak of the Querelle (1687): On January 27, 1687, Charles Perrault ignited the celebrated *Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes* by reciting his poem Le Siècle de Louis le Grand before the Académie Française. Perrault boldly asserted that the age of Louis XIV was intellectually, artistically, and morally superior to ancient Greece and Rome, audaciously critiquing Homer's epics as unrefined and primitive.
  • The Battle of the Ancients and Moderns: Perrault expanded his arguments in his four-volume dialogue Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes (1688–1697). Utilizing Cartesian rationalism, Perrault argued that human knowledge is cumulative; therefore, moderns naturally stand upon the shoulders of the ancients and possess superior taste, science, and morality. He was fiercely opposed by Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux and Jean Racine, dividing Europe's intellectual elite.
  • The Fairy Tale Revolution (1697): In 1697, Perrault published Histoires ou contes du temps passé (subtitled Contes de ma mère l'Oye), ostensibly under the name of his son Pierre Darmancour. By transforming rustic oral folk tales into polished, ironic courtly literature, Perrault created the modern fairy-tale genre, providing canonical versions of Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty, Little Red Riding Hood, and Puss in Boots.
  • Neoclassical and Victorian Critical Reception: In England, where the Querelle was mirrored in the "Battle of the Books" (satirized by Jonathan Swift), Perrault was frequently portrayed by defenders of classical education as an arrogant modern iconoclast. By the nineteenth century, John Ruskin invoked Perrault in Modern Painters alongside Zoilus to exemplify critics who commit the insolent error of seeking to degrade the revered monuments of antiquity.
  • Modern Global Legacy: Today, while Perrault's name is inextricably linked with children's literature, Disney adaptations, and folkloric analysis, his legacy in cultural history remains foundational: he was the first major European writer to articulate the doctrine of continuous cultural progress against the dogma of neoclassical imitation.

Phonetically, the French pronunciation /ʃaʁl pɛ.ʁo/ Anglicizes into Received Pronunciation as /pɛˈroʊ/ or /ˈpɛr.oʊ/. Semantically, the name occupies a unique position in Western thought, bridging the whimsical oral fantasy of folklore with the high intellectual vanguard of Enlightenment rationalism.
