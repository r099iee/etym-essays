- Late 17th to early 18th century (French Ancien Régime) - Denoted the morning ceremonial waking and dressing of the French monarch (*le lever* / *la levée du roi*), carrying connotations of court etiquette, aristocratic privilege, and intimate political access.
- 18th century (Colonial Louisiana & British Court) - Bifurcated into two distinct transatlantic tracks: in Britain and America, an official public reception held by a sovereign or head of state (e.g., George Washington's weekly presidential levees); in Louisiana, an embankment thrown up to hold back the Mississippi.
- 19th century (Antebellum & Gilded Age America) - The civil engineering and commercial riverfront sense dominated American usage, evoking steamboats, bustling cotton wharves, river commerce, and precarious flood control against seasonal inundation.
- 20th century to Present (Disaster Preparedness & Popular Culture) - Acquired heightened existential connotations of ecological vulnerability, structural failure, and catastrophe (especially after the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 and Hurricane Katrina in 2005), immortalized in song ('drove my Chevy to the levee') and environmental policy.
