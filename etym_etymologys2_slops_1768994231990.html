SINGLE ROOT CHAIN (liquid-refuse sense — the card's sense): Proto-Indo-European *sleubh- "to slip, slide, glide" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE; a root of motion and loss of grip) → Proto-Germanic *sleupan- "to slip, creep, slide" with o-grade *slaub- (c. 2nd millennium BCE – 5th century CE) → Old English sloppe "dung, slime, mire" (recorded Old English period, 8th–11th c.; o-grade formation) → Middle English sloppe "puddle, muddy place, slush, wet spill" (13th–14th c.) → Modern English slop (n.) "liquid or semi-liquid spillage; watery mess; liquid refuse" (first attested 16th c.), with the plural specialized as "liquid waste, household and sickroom refuse" (16th–17th c.) → slops (the card's root word, plural form fixed by the 17th century as the standard domestic name for such waste). SECONDARY CHAIN (clothing homograph — a distinct but related word): Proto-Indo-European *sleubh- "to slip, glide" (reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *sleupan- / *slaub- "to slip (into a garment), put on loosely" (reconstructed) → Middle Low German slōpe / slop "a loose outer garment, baggy breeches" (attested 14th–15th c.) → English slop (n.) "a loose outer garment; baggy breeches" (borrowed, late 14th–15th c.) → English slops (plural) "ready-made sailor's clothing and breeches sold from naval stores" (17th c.) → the clothing slops, a homograph of the waste-word. NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: The OED labels the waste-word "of obscure origin," and the connection to PIE *sleubh- and Old English sloppe, though widely accepted, is not beyond dispute; some handbooks instead cite a Scandinavian source (cf. Norwegian slop "slime, mire") or an independent echoic formation on the sl- + liquid-vowel template that also produced slush, slosh, and slobber. The clothing sense is securely from Middle Low German, but the ultimate identity of the two words' roots is inferred rather than proven. Neither chain should be read as fully settled; the sound-symbolic sl- frame is the one solid fact.
