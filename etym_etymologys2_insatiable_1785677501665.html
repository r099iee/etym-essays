INSATIABLE is a compound of two Latin elements of independent origin: the negative prefix in- and the verb-family built on satis, "enough." The two chains are traced in isolation below, then joined at the adjective. CHAIN 1 — the negative prefix in-: 1) PIE *ne- / *n̥- "not" (reconstructed; the oldest and most widespread negative in Indo-European, reflected in Sanskrit a-/an-, Greek a-/an-, Latin in-, Germanic un-, Lithuanian ne-) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin in- "not" (negative prefix, attested from the earliest Latin) — the ordinary prefix of negation, as in invictus "unconquered," infelix "unhappy," incertus "uncertain." 3) Latin in- + satiābilis → insatiābilis "not to be satisfied, insatiable" (attested in Classical Latin; Cicero, 1st c. BCE) — the combining point; see CHAIN 2 for the stem. CHAIN 2 — the stem satiāre (the satis family): 1) PIE *seh₂- "to be filled, be sated, satisfy" (reconstructed; the root of Latin satis "enough" and satur "full, sated", and of the Germanic *sadaz "full, sated" that became Old English sæd "full, satisfied" and hence modern English sad, which originally meant "full") — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin satis "enough, sufficient, sufficient quantity" (adverb and noun, attested from the earliest Latin; also sat "enough") — the root of the family's core idea: the point at which desire is filled. 3) Latin satiāre "to satisfy, fill, sate, satiate" (verb, from satis; attested from the 1st c. BCE; the source of English satiate, and of saturāre → English saturate) — the verb "to fill" at the heart of the family. 4) Latin satiābilis "capable of being satisfied, satisfiable" (adjective, formed on satiāre with the suffix -bilis; attested) — the base that the prefix negates. 5) Latin insatiābilis "not to be satisfied, insatiable" (in- + satiābilis; attested in Classical Latin, e.g. Cicero's insatiabilis cupiditas "insatiable desire") — the exact Latin source of the English word. 6) Old French insaciable "insatiable" (adjective, attested from the 12th c.; from Latin insatiabilis) — the likely immediate channel. 7) English insatiable "impossible to satisfy or quench" (borrowed early 15th c., either from Old French insaciable or directly from Latin insatiabilis) — INSATIABLE. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) Both chains are secure and uncontroversial: Latin in- from PIE *ne-/*n̥-, and satiāre from PIE *seh₂- via satis. (2) The Germanic cognate *sadaz "full, sated" (Old English sæd) is well attested and gives the striking fact that English sad originally meant "full, satisfied"; the semantic drift "full, sated" → "solemn" → "unhappy" is documented in Old English. (3) Whether English borrowed insatiable directly from Latin or through Old French is not certain (both channels were open in the 15th century); the French form insaciable is securely attested, so both paths are stated rather than guessed.
