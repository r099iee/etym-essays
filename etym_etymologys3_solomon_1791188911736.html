The odyssey of Solomon from Iron-Age Jerusalem throne-name to Pacific atlas label spans three millennia, two language families, and an imperial fantasy.

  • Semitic root sh-l-m (3rd-2nd millennium BCE): Proto-Semitic and Proto-West-Semitic root *š-l-m means to be whole, intact, safe, at peace, to repay/make complete. It yields Akkadian šalāmu to be healthy, Ugaritic šlm well-being, Arabic salām peace, islām submission/wholeness, Hebrew shalom peace/hello, shalem whole, shillem repay. Theophoric names embed it: Absalom (Avshalom) father-is-peace, Jerusalem (Yerushalayim) foundation-of-peace/wholeness, Solomon (Shelomoh) his-peace / peaceful-one with possessive -o suffix.
  • Biblical Hebrew coinage (10th-6th c. BCE, texts redacted later): 2 Samuel 12:24-25 narrates the naming: Bathsheba bears Shelomoh, "because the LORD loved him," also called Yedidyah beloved-of-Yah. Folk-etymology in Kings links his reign to shalom — peace after David's wars. Historicity of the united monarchy is debated (minimalist vs maximalist archaeology), but the name-form is authentic Iron-Age Hebrew onomastics. Septuagint translators (3rd-2nd c. BCE Alexandria) rendered it Greek Salomōn / Solomōn, with o-vocalism reflecting contemporary Hebrew pronunciation.
  • Greek-Latin-Christian transmission (200 BCE-1000 CE): New Testament Greek Solomōn (Matthew: "even Solomon in all his glory"), Latin Vulgate Salomon, Qur'anic Arabic Sulayman (diminutive of Salman, wise prophet-king who speaks to ants and jinn). Patristic and rabbinic lore magnified Solomon into mage, demon-binder (Testament of Solomon), and lover (Song). Old French Salomon (Chanson, 12th c.) and Middle English Salomon/Solomon carried both sage and sensualist connotations; Wycliffe and Tyndale fixed Solomon spelling with o second vowel under Latin influence.
  • Spanish Pacific fantasy (1567-1568): Viceroy of Peru dispatched Álvaro de Mendaña to find Terra Australis and Ophir — the biblical gold-land of 1 Kings 9-10 whence Solomon imported 420 talents of gold, peacocks, and sandalwood. Sighting gold flecks and coppery skin on Santa Isabel, Mendaña christened the group Islas de Salomón, assuming he had found Ophir. Charts by Ortelius (1570s) and Dalrymple spread Solomon Islands; Pedro de Quiros re-sought them in 1606. No gold mines of Ophir existed — likely San Cristóbal's modest alluvials — but the name endured, unlike many ephemeral saint-names.
  • British-German colonial partition (19th-20th c.): Traders (copra, bêche-de-mer), blackbirders, and missionaries (Anglican Melanesian Mission) wrote lurid Solomons ethnography — headhunting, bride-price, and, as in your source, reports of cannibal feasts where victims were "fattened like pigs." Britain declared a Protectorate (1893-1978) over the southern Solomons; Germany then Australia held the north (Bougainville). WWII Battle of Guadalcanal (1942-43) globalised the name. Phonology anglicised Spanish Salomón to English SOL-o-mon.
  • Postcolonial nationhood (1978-present): Independent Solomon Islands (plural without possessive apostrophe, like Cook Islands) retains the colonial exonym by choice — no indigenous umbrella term covers 70 language groups. Pijin speakers say Solomon Aelan. Socio-cultural perception shifted from biblical gold-fantasy to strategic microstate, aid recipient, and climate-frontline, while King Solomon the sage remains a separate living religious symbol across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

Thus Solomon fuses Semitic wholeness-theology with Iberian imperial imagination: a Jerusalem throne-name sailed 16,000 km to label Melanesian volcanoes, proving how etymology can be hijacked by cartographic wishful thinking and then naturalised by four centuries of use.
