Wagon illustrates a double inheritance: a native Old English word wain and a later Dutch re-borrowing wagon from the same Proto-Germanic source. Old English had wægn — wain — from Proto-Germanic *wagnaz, itself from PIE *wegh- to go, carry. That native line survives in wain, Charles's Wain for the Big Dipper, and wainwright. But Middle English warfare and trade with the Low Countries reintroduced the continental form Middle Dutch wagen as wagon, waggon in the early 1500s for a heavy continental four-wheeled vehicle, and the newcomer displaced wain for ordinary use.

PIE base: *wegh- to go, transport is one of the best-attested motion roots, yielding Latin vehere to carry — vehicle, convey —, Greek okhos chariot, Sanskrit vahana vehicle, Old Irish fén wagon, and Germanic *wagnaz via suffixed *wogh-no- that which carries. Proto-Germanic *wagnaz split into Old Norse vagn, Old High German wagan, Old English wægn, all meaning cart or chariot. The gh → gn development is regular Germanic nasalisation of the PIE labiovelar.

  • Old English wægn / wain (pre-1500): farm cart, war chariot, and constellation. Charles's Wain — the Wagon of Charlemagne — named the Plough as a celestial cart. Wægn-wyrhta — wainwright — was the cart-maker, parallel to shipwright.
  • Dutch re-borrowing (1523 onward): the OED's first wagon is 1523 in a military account of Flanders wagons. Tudor armies hired Dutch wagoners with their sturdy four-wheeled wagens. Spelling wavered wagon / waggon for three centuries; Britain kept waggon for rail use into the 1900s, America standardised wagon.
  • American expansion (1700s–1800s): Conestoga wagon, prairie schooner, chuck wagon, covered wagon made wagon the icon of westward migration — wagon train, wagon master, circle the wagons. The source passage's man is wagon and donkey plays on this burden-bearing sense.
  • Railway and motor age (1840s–1950s): Britain transferred wagon — often waggon — to open railway goods trucks: goods wagon, coal wagon, wagonload. America transferred it to motor cars: station wagon, later shortened to wagon, and to paddy wagon, chuckwagon cuisine.

Sound and spelling: initial w- stable, medial -ag- short /æ/, final -on reduced to /ən/. RP /ˈwæɡən/, American /ˈwæɡən/ identical. Doubled waggon was etymologically motivated by the short vowel but abandoned in the US by Noah Webster's reform and in Britain by the mid-20th century except for rail archaism.

Idioms preserve transport history: on the wagon — teetotal — from water wagons that teetotallers supposedly drank from; off the wagon — relapsed; hitch one's wagon to a star — Emerson's ambition; wagon-burner — dated slang; welcome wagon — neighbourhood greeting. Each keeps the image of a laden vehicle moving, stalling or tipping — the uninterrupted semantic legacy of PIE *wegh- to carry a load along a way.
