- [Late Middle English, c. 1380–1450] — Formed in English via Anglo-Norman from Old French *desfigurer* ("to distort, disguise, alter the face or form"), used in medieval religious allegories and Arthurian romance to describe mystical transformations, hideous disguises, or demonic corruptions of the human form divine — [perception: perceived as an unnatural, monstrous alteration of God's created image]
- [Early Modern & Renaissance, c. 1500–1650] — Applied frequently to physical battlefield injuries, mutilations, and the terrible cosmetic devastation wrought by smallpox, leprosy, and syphilis, as well as the deliberate iconoclastic destruction of Catholic church statues by Protestant reformers — [perception: dreaded as a horrific personal affliction carrying acute social stigma and moral dread]
- [18th & 19th c. Enlightenment & Industrial Revolution] — Transitioned into formal civil jurisprudence and tort law to classify actionable bodily harm, while simultaneously describing the violent machinery trauma in factories and the aesthetic scarring of pristine pastoral landscapes by smokestacks and coal mines ("the disfigurement of nature") — [perception: recognized as both a legally compensable bodily catastrophic injury and a tragic consequence of unchecked industrialization]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Centralized in reconstructive plastic surgery, trauma rehabilitation, disability rights advocacy, and psychological literature, combating social bias against facial differences while maintaining its figurative use in politics and art ("disfigurement of historical truth") — [perception: treated with profound clinical empathy and ethical urgency regarding facial equality, alongside ongoing usage for structural vandalism]
