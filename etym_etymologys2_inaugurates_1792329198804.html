Note on historical debate: The etymology of Latin augur has been one of the most famous debates in classical philology since antiquity. Roman scholars themselves offered two competing explanations: Suetonius and Varro connected augur to avis ("bird") + gerere ("to direct, manage"), literally "one who directs himself by bird signs." Conversely, modern comparative linguists (including Michiel de Vaan, Ernout-Meillet, and Alois Walde) demonstrate that augur is a neuter s-stem noun derived from the PIE verbal root *h₂ewg- ("to increase"), cognate with Latin augēre ("to enlarge") and augustus ("venerable, majestic"). In this analysis, an augur was originally an officiant who possessed or channeled *augos ("divine fullness, sacred increase, blessing"), ensuring that state actions enjoyed divine prosperity. Through centuries of religious practice observing the flights of birds (auspicia), the term converged semantically with ornithomancy, leading to the compound verb inaugurāre.
