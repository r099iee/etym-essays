BY AND LARGE is a compound idiom composed of two independent words — the Germanic adverb/preposition by and the Romance adjective large — joined by the coordinator and. There is no single PIE root governing the compound; each element has its own chain, and the compound has its own chain. CHAIN 1 — ‘by’ element: - PIE *h₂embʰi ‘around, on both sides, about’ (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the reduction to the simple *bi is debated (see note) → Proto-Germanic *bi ‘by, near, about, around’ (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) → Old English bī ‘by, near, at, against, about’ (attested from the 7th–8th c.) → Middle English by / bi ‘by, near, along’ (attested c. 1100–1500) → Modern English by ‘near, beside; past; through the agency of’ (1500–present) — with the nautical sense ‘close to the wind, close-hauled’ developed in the 16th–17th c. and frozen in the compound by and large CHAIN 2 — ‘large’ element: - Latin largus ‘abundant, plentiful, generous, liberal, ample’ (attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — of uncertain origin, possibly Etruscan or Mediterranean substrate; no secure PIE etymon is agreed (see note) → Old French large ‘wide, broad, generous’ (attested from the 11th–12th c.) → Middle English large ‘wide, broad, generous, ample’ (attested from the 12th–13th c.) → Modern English large ‘big, wide, extensive; generous’ (1500–present) — with the nautical sense ‘free of the wind, with the wind abaft the beam’ developed by the 17th c. and frozen in the compound by and large CHAIN 3 — the compound idiom ‘BY AND LARGE’ itself: - English nautical phrase ‘close-hauled (by the wind) and running with the wind free (large)’ — a seaman’s verdict on a ship’s behaviour in all wind conditions (first attested in Samuel Pepys’s diary, 1669) → Figurative extension ‘in all respects, everywhere, taking everything together’ (early 18th c.; attested in Defoe and Swift) → Modern fixed idiom ‘on the whole, generally speaking, everything considered’ (established by the 19th c.) → BY AND LARGE NOTE ON DEBATE: two points are debated. First, the ancestry of English by: the usual reconstruction reduces PIE *h₂embʰi (‘around, on both sides’) to *bi in the Germanic adverb/preposition, but the exact intermediate steps are not directly attested and some scholars treat Proto-Germanic *bi as of uncertain further origin. Second, the origin of Latin largus is genuinely obscure — it is widely regarded as non-Indo-European, possibly Etruscan or of a Mediterranean substrate, and no PIE etymon is securely accepted; the word therefore has no Indo-European chain, and this must be stated rather than invented. The English compound itself is well attested: Pepys’s diary (1669) is the earliest known record of the nautical phrase, and the generalisation to ‘on the whole’ follows in the first half of the eighteenth century.
