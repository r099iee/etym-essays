mummers (noun, plural; singular mummer) names the performers of a distinctive European tradition of masked, often silent, seasonal folk drama. In the literary context of this card, taken from a Chinese classical novel and translated into the rich vocabulary of English festival life, the word conjures a night of lanterns and masked revellers: the "mummers and the coloured lanterns" of Fifteenth Night are the disguised, costumed performers of a popular street celebration. The word's defining core is the combination of disguise (mask or costume that conceals the performer's everyday identity), performance (a play, pantomime, dance, or procession presented to an audience), and seasonal ritual (the mummers' tradition is tightly bound to midwinter, Christmas, Plough Monday, and other festival dates). It is a word of grassroots, participatory entertainment rather than of formal theatre: the mummers are not professionals on a stage but villagers and townspeople who take on masks for a few days of licensed, festive disorder.

The full polysemy and usage range can be set out as follows:

  • Sense 1 — Performers of the folk Mummers' Play (the core historical sense). In English folk tradition the mummers are the masked actors of the seasonal "mummers' play," a ritual drama of death and resurrection — the classic version being the Hero-Combat or St. George play, in which a champion is slain and brought back to life by a quack doctor. The performers travel from house to house or perform in the open, in disguise, often for small payments of food, drink, or money. This is the sense that gives the word its folkloric and anthropological weight.
  • Sense 2 — Any masked or disguised performer (the general historical sense). More broadly, a mummer is any person who performs while masked or disguised — a masquerader, a reveller who joins a costumed procession. In medieval and early modern England, "mumming" covered the seasonal practice of visiting houses in disguise, singing, dancing, and gaming, sometimes shading into rowdy and even menacing behaviour; the word then carried a distinctly plebeian and carnivalesque colour.
  • Sense 3 — Members of a guild or company performing a disguised pageant. In fifteenth- and sixteenth-century records, "mummers" are documented as participants in court and civic entertainments, disguising themselves with painted visors, plumed hats, and gilt garments for the Christmas revels of kings and corporations — the English court's "disguisings," which scholars treat as an ancestor of the masque.
  • Sense 4 — Modern institutional and ceremonial survival. The word lives on in two institutional forms: the Philadelphia Mummers Parade, an elaborate New Year's Day celebration in which thousands of costumed performers march, strut, and perform in fanciful "fancies," comics, and string bands; and the revived or surviving folk mummers' plays of English villages (for example at Christmas and Plough Monday), which folklorists have collected and performed since the antiquarian revivals of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
  • Sense 5 — Figurative and derived uses. By extension, "mummer" may be used (often playfully or disparagingly) of any actor whose performance is wordless, masked, or exaggerated, and "mummery" — the related noun for what mummers do — has acquired a distinct secondary sense of "a ridiculous, pompous, or insincere ceremony or charade," so that "mere mummery" means empty, theatrical pretence.
  • Sense 6 — Register, grammar, and association. The word is now archaic, historical, and folkloric; in ordinary modern speech nobody calls a costumed actor a "mummer." The plural "mummers" (as on this card) is the common form, since the performers normally appear as a troupe. The word is associated with masks, silence, midwinter, and the rough energies of festival inversion, and it carries a whiff of ancient, pre-Christian seasonal custom.

The historical semantic bridge. The word's development is a story of one imitative sound doing a great deal of cultural work. The closed-lip hum "mum" is the sound a person makes when unable or unwilling to speak — hence "mum" meaning "silent" (still alive in "keep mum") and "mumble." Disguised performers who muffled their faces muffled their voices too, and so "mummer" came to mean "one who performs masked and dumb." From the specific medieval practice the word then fanned outward: to the general masquerader, to the court disguiser, to the modern civic parade performer. As the original seasonal practice receded, the word receded with it into folklore and history books, while its satirical derivative "mummery" carried the modern sense of empty ceremony forward into everyday language.

Boundary comparisons. The nearest neighbours need careful separation. An actor works from a script on a formal stage with a clear audience division; a mummer is a masked folk performer with no such professional frame. A busker is a modern street performer working for tips; a mummer is a traditional festival performer with no transactional contract. A harlequin is a specific stylized character of the Italian commedia dell'arte in a diamond-patterned suit; a mummer is a generic disguised performer of the folk tradition. An impersonator imitates a specific real or famous person; a mummer hides behind a mask to play a generic ritual role. And the northern English and Scottish guisers are the direct cousins of the mummers — the same masked, seasonal, door-to-door tradition under a different name.
