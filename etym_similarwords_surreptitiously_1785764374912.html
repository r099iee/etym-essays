• Secretly — Similar because both mean "not openly, so that others do not know." Differs because secretly is the broad, unmarked term for anything not public, while surreptitiously specifies deliberate concealment at the moment of the act, aimed at evading a particular observer.
  • Stealthily — Similar because both describe action done so as to escape notice. Differs because stealthily emphasizes silent, slow, cat-like movement (a predator, a burglar), while surreptitiously also covers verbal, digital, and bureaucratic acts where physical silence is irrelevant.
  • Furtively — Similar because both involve secrecy and evasion of notice. Differs because furtively adds a tone of guilt, nervousness, and sidelong unease (furtive glances, furtive hands), while surreptitiously can be cool, calculated, and methodical without trembling.
  • Clandestinely — Similar because both mean hidden from authority or knowledge. Differs because clandestinely typically describes organized, sustained secret operations (clandestine meetings, networks, missions), while surreptitiously fits small, one-off individual acts as easily as large schemes.
  • Covertly — Similar because both mean "in a hidden, non-public manner." Differs because covertly is the technical term of intelligence, law enforcement, and military institutions for concealed operations and is now dominant in that jargon; surreptitiously is the more literary, general-purpose word for stealthy concealment.
  • Sneakily — Similar because both describe sly, underhanded behavior. Differs because sneakily is informal and often suggests petty, childish, or cowardly trickery, while surreptitiously is formal and neutral about scale — from a schoolboy to a state spy.
  • Underhandedly — Similar because both imply concealment of true intent. Differs because underhandedly emphasizes dishonesty and deceit (cheating, manipulation), whereas surreptitiously emphasizes the act of evasion itself, which may be innocent or merely private rather than deceitful.
  • On the sly — Similar because both describe doing something secretly so others will not know. Differs because on the sly is an informal idiom typically used of minor indulgences or vices (drinking, smoking, flirting), while surreptitiously is formal and spans trivial and grave concealment alike.
