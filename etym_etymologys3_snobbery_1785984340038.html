The history of SNOBBERY is unusual because its earliest stage is wrapped in genuine uncertainty. Unlike most words, which can be traced to a clear Indo-European root, snob appears in English with no secure ancient pedigree. The most commonly repeated theory connects it to Old Norse snápr, “a dullard, an idiot, a charlatan”, a word that itself has no certain further etymology; but the link is speculative and no direct path from Scandinavian into English dialect is attested. Other guesses — that snob is a shortening of the Latin tag s.nob., “sine nobilitate”, supposedly inscribed against the names of boys of humble birth at Eton, or that it comes from dialect verbs of sneering or scolding — are folklore rather than scholarship. Etymologists therefore state plainly that the true origin of snob is unknown, and the story of snobbery properly begins in eighteenth-century English itself.

The social climb of the word.

  • In the eighteenth century, especially at Cambridge, snob was a dialect and university word for “a shoemaker”, “a cobbler”, or “a cobbler’s apprentice” — a tradesman of the humblest sort, emphatically not a gentleman.
  • From the tradesman, the word slid sideways in Cambridge slang to mean “a townsman, a non-university man” — that is, anyone not of the student class — and from there to “one of the lower classes” generally.
  • In the early nineteenth century the meaning turned upward in the social scale: a snob became “a person who vulgarly admires or apes the manners of the upper classes”, someone of lowly origin straining to appear genteel.
  • The turning point was Thackeray’s The Book of Snobs (1848), a series of satirical sketches that made the word famous and fixed its modern, mocking flavour.

Coining the abstract noun. To the noun snob English added the productive suffix -ery — the same suffix seen in roguery, knavery, priggery — which forms nouns denoting the behaviour or practice characteristic of a class of person. The resulting word, snobbery, is attested from the 1840s and means “the behaviour, attitude, or practice of a snob”. The semantic leap embedded in the word is remarkable: it names a contempt for those beneath one that was originally itself a mark of lowly status — the snob, once the cobbler’s boy, becomes the man who despises the cobbler.

Semantic drift in the modern era. In the twentieth century snobbery broadened from social class to every arena of taste and knowledge. The word now attaches to intellectual snobbery, artistic snobbery, musical snobbery, culinary and wine snobbery, and to the reflexive reverse snobbery that despises refinement as pretentious. Sociologists have taken the term up as a mechanism of class distinction and boundary-marking, linking it to the idea of cultural capital: snobbery is one way the privileged signal and police who may belong.

Socio-cultural context. The rise of snobbery tracks the anxieties of an aspirational middle class. In a society of rapid social mobility, where birth no longer fixed a person’s place, snobbery became a way of claiming status by exclusion — of the aristocracy from below, of the working class from above. Its continued vigour reflects the persistent human concern with rank, taste, and belonging, and it remains one of the most precise words English has for the unattractive combination of superiority and anxiety.

Literary life. Thackeray’s title gave the word a permanent satirical home, and it has remained a favourite of novelists, essayists, and critics ever since — a word that names a fault precisely because it lets the speaker place themselves above the snob. In the sentence “His sheer snobbery was evident to all,” the word still does the work Thackeray gave it: it condemns an attitude without needing to specify its content, because everyone recognises the pose of superiority at a glance.
