• Grammatical: Similar because both refer to adherence to the accepted systemic rules of a language; differs because grammatical is an umbrella term encompassing phonology, morphology, syntax, and semantics, whereas syntactic strictly isolates the arrangement and hierarchical ordering of phrases and clauses.
  • Structural: Similar because both emphasize systemic framework, constituent architecture, and organizational patterns over content; differs because structural is a broad macro-concept applicable to buildings, societies, proteins, or abstract models, whereas syntactic specifically denotes formal rule-governed arrangements within symbolic, linguistic, or computational systems.
  • Morphosyntactic: Similar because both address the formal grammatical properties of linguistic expressions; differs because morphosyntactic explicitly spans the boundary where word-internal inflectional markings (like case endings or verb agreements) directly interact with clause-level syntactic relations.
  • Semantic: Similar because both are core components of linguistic analysis and formal logic; differs because semantic focuses exclusively on meaning, truth values, and conceptual interpretation, whereas syntactic concerns the surface form, structural legality, and combinatorial arrangement of symbols regardless of their underlying meaning.
  • Pragmatic: Similar because both describe aspects of linguistic communication; differs because pragmatic addresses how context, social intention, and speaker implicature shape utterance interpretation, whereas syntactic abstracts completely away from social usage to analyze formal grammatical structure.
  • Combinatorial: Similar because both involve the mathematical or formal principles governing how distinct units join together; differs because combinatorial is a mathematical property of permutations and sets, whereas syntactic applies specifically to hierarchical, rule-governed sequences in natural or artificial languages.
