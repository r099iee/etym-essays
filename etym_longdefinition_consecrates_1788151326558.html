Consecrates is the present-tense, third-person singular form of the transitive verb consecrate: to make sacred, to set apart as holy, or to dedicate formally and solemnly to a divine or exalted purpose. At root it is a ritual act: a bishop consecrates a church or another bishop; a priest consecrates the bread and wine of the Eucharist; ground is consecrated for burial. But the word also travels beyond the altar — a life consecrated to a cause, a memory consecrated by poetry — and in Wordsworth’s line, ‘a gift that consecrates the joy’, the verb names the power of art to hallow experience, to lift a moment out of the ordinary stream and make it holy. That remains its essential force in every register: consecrating sets a thing apart, permanently, from common use.

Core senses.

  • Ritual consecration (primary, religious): to perform the ceremony by which a person (a bishop), a place (a church, altar, cemetery), or an object (the Eucharist elements, a chalice, a bell, a flag) is declared sacred and dedicated to God; the noun is consecration, and the thing so treated is consecrated.
  • Solemn dedication (extended): to devote something or oneself wholly and formally to a noble purpose, cause, or person, with a gravity borrowed from religious dedication: ‘consecrate one’s life to the poor’, ‘a monument consecrated to the memory of the fallen’.
  • Figurative hallowing: to make something specially meaningful, sacred, or inviolable in a non-ritual way — the Wordsworthean sense of art or love ‘consecrating’ a joy, a place, or a memory.

Secondary and technical uses.

  • Ecclesiastical law and history: consecration of bishops is the highest ordination act (the consecrating prelates lay hands); the consecration of a church involves anointing and dedication rites, and in Catholic doctrine the words of institution in the Mass — the consecration — convert bread and wine into the body and blood (transubstantiation); Protestant traditions retain ‘consecrated elements’ with varying doctrines.
  • Burial and ground: ‘consecrated ground’ is a churchyard or cemetery formally set apart for Christian burial; historically, exclusion from consecrated ground was a penalty — suicides and excommunicates were buried apart, which made the phrase a heavy social instrument in novels and parish records.
  • Roman imperial usage: Latin consecratio named the deification of emperors after death — the apotheosis decreed by the Senate — so ‘consecrated’ could once mean ‘enrolled among the gods’.
  • Figurative and literary register: ‘consecrate’ is now marked as formal or archaic in ordinary speech; it survives in sermons, dedications, memorials, and high rhetoric, where it outranks ‘dedicate’ by importing sanctity.
  • Antonyms and contrasts:deconsecrate (to undo consecration, secularize a church), desecrate (to violate what is sacred — same root sacer with reversed force), profane, secularize.

Related forms. consecrate (v., and archaic adj. ‘consecrated’), consecrated (adj. and past participle), consecrating, consecration (n.), consecrator/consecratrix (the officiant), consecratory (adj.). The word family is built on Latin consecrāre, ‘to make sacred’, from con- ‘with’ + sacrāre ‘to hallow’, from sacer ‘sacred’ — the same root as sacred, sacrifice, sacrament, and desecrate. In the source sentence, ‘a gift that consecrates the joy’, the verb carries all three cores at once: ritual gravity (thanksgiving), dedication (to the muse), and hallowing (making the joy forever sacred).
