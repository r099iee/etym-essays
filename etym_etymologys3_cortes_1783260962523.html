THE COURT THAT GREW INTO A CONQUEROR The surname Cortes — Macaulay's accentless spelling of Spanish Cortés — is one of the rare proper names that can be traced, with confidence, all the way back to a Proto-Indo-European root. The root is *ǵʰer-, "to enclose, encircle, surround," and from it Indo-European built *ǵʰortós, "an enclosed place": a yard, a fence, an enclosed ground. The reflexes fan out across the family with perfect regularity: Greek khórtos, "enclosed farmyard" (later "fodder, grass"); Latin hortus, "garden, enclosed ground"; and, independently in Germanic, *garda-z, "enclosure, yard," which produced Old English geard, our modern yard, and, through a Norman French detour, garden. The same root, extended, gave gird, girdle, and girth, "to encircle." English gardeners and yard-keepers therefore speak, every day, words that are distant cousins of the conqueror's name — a remarkable fact to hold in mind while following the enclosure of the word into Roman law and Spanish power. FROM YARD TO COURT The decisive development took place in Latin. From hortus, "enclosure," Latin compounded co- (together) + hortus to make cohors, "those shut in together" — first an enclosed yard, then the group that shares it: a band, a retinue, a company of soldiers, and finally the fixed military unit of the tenth, the cohort. The history of Western government is buried in this shift: the cohors of the Roman magistrate — the attendants who accompanied him and waited in his compound — became the model of the ruler's circle, and in the Romance languages the word slid from "retinue" to "court." Vulgar Latin *cōrtem produced Old French cort, whence English court, courteous, courtesy, and courtier; and in Iberia the same Latin word gave Old Spanish corte, "the royal court, the capital," whose plural cortes named the assemblies of the realm — the medieval parliaments of Castile and, later, of a united Spain. THE NAME AND THE CONQUEST In medieval Spain the court-word spawned the adjective cortés, "courteous, courtly, polite," formed with the suffix -és, "of the court," and the surname Cortés, "the courteous one / the man of the court," was born of that adjective (or directly of the plural cortes) in the habitual Spanish way of turning epithets and nicknames into hereditary names. The most famous bearer, Hernán Cortés, was born in 1485 in Medellín, Extremadura; after a false start in law he went to the Indies, and in 1519, at the head of a few hundred men, launched the enterprise that toppled the Aztec Empire. His brilliance lay in strategy and propaganda: he exploited the grievances of peoples under Aztec rule, built alliances (especially with the Tlaxcalans), and when the emperor Moctezuma was killed and Tenochtitlan rose against him, he besieged and destroyed the great island capital in 1521. The conquest was then reported in his own words: the Cartas de Relación, five letters to Charles V that justify the war, describe the land, and present Cortes as the loyal servant who gave Spain a new empire. It is this self-justifying, strategic quality that distinguishes him in Macaulay's prose from Pizarro: Cortes is the conquistador who wrote his own vindication. THE NAME IN ENGLISH PROSE AND MEMORY English historians have spelled the name both with and without the accent; Macaulay's "Cortes" fixed the unaccented form in English historical prose. The British imperial imagination of the 18th and 19th centuries used "Cortes and Pizarro" as the paired negative image of empire by the sword — the realms added to the dominions of Charles the Fifth, their gold drained, their temples thrown down — set against the British colonies that grew mighty by settlement, law, and commerce. The surname has thus followed a trajectory unique among proper names: from the Indo-European enclosure-root *ǵʰer-, through the Latin court and the Spanish cortes, to the conqueror of Mexico, and finally to a byword in English for strategic conquest — an empire won by audacity, cruelty, and self-justification, and a standing contrast to everything Macaulay believed the English method of empire to be.
