foxhounds (plural of foxhound): a breed, or family of breeds, of large, smooth-coated hounds — most notably the English Foxhound and the American Foxhound — bred specifically to hunt foxes by scent, running in disciplined packs and pursuing their quarry with a loud, characteristic "music" (baying).

Primary sense — the hunting breed: A foxhound is a sturdy, deep-chested, powerful hound standing about 22–25 inches at the shoulder, with a short hard coat, strong scenting ability, and remarkable endurance. Foxhounds are pack animals: they hunt socially, following a fox's scent line for hours across open country while the mounted field (riders) follows behind. The English Foxhound was developed in 17th-century England, and the American Foxhound descends from English and French stock imported to colonial Virginia.

Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings:
  • Breed distinction — English vs American Foxhound: The English Foxhound is heavier and slower, bred for the disciplined pace of English mounted hunts; the American Foxhound is lighter, faster, and more agile, bred for long chases over varied American terrain. Both are recognized kennel-club breeds (e.g., by the Kennel Club and the American Kennel Club).
  • Pack-hunting social institution: "Foxhounds" names not just the animals but the institution of the fox-hunting pack kept by a master of foxhounds (MFH) — the organized kennel, hunt servants, and mounted followers that formed the core of British and Irish rural sport from the 18th century. Semantic bridge: the word extended from the dogs themselves to the whole social apparatus that surrounds them.
  • Symbol of wealth and class: In 18th–19th-century literature, keeping "a pack of foxhounds" was the quintessential emblem of landed wealth and leisured aristocracy — as in Elizabeth Bennet's remark in Pride and Prejudice that if she were as rich as Mr. Darcy she "would keep a pack of foxhounds." Semantic bridge: the working hunting dog became a status symbol of the gentry.
  • Contemporary controversy and change: After the Hunting Act 2004 banned traditional fox hunting with dogs in England and Wales, "foxhounds" have become contested symbols — the breed persists for drag hunting, kennel-club showing, and as companions, while the word carries complex associations of tradition, animal welfare debate, and rural identity.
  • Figurative / generic use: By extension, "foxhounds" can name any pack of hounds used to hunt foxes, or — loosely — any relentless pursuit, echoing the phrase "hounded like a fox."

Historical semantic bridge: The compound joins Old English fox (the red wild canine) + hund ("dog", originally the general Germanic word for dog, which narrowed to "hunting dog" after the word dog was borrowed). The term foxhound was coined in 17th-century England precisely when the sport of fox hunting replaced deer and hare hunting among the gentry, so the word's rise parallels the rise of fox hunting as a social institution.
