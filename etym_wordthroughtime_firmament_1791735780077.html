- [Late Antiquity & Biblical Latin (c. 4th-5th c. CE)] - Coined in Jerome's Vulgate ('firmamentum') to translate the Greek 'steréōma' and Hebrew 'rāqīaʿ' - Sacred cosmological concept of a physical, solid crystalline barrier crafted by God to hold back the upper waters.
- [Middle English (13th-15th c.)] - Introduced via Anglo-Norman into English biblical translations (Wycliffe) and medieval poetry (Chaucer) - Reverent theological reality, representing the sphere of the fixed stars in the Ptolemaic geocentric universe.
- [Renaissance & Early Modern (16th-17th c.)] - Immortalized in the 1611 King James Bible and Shakespearean drama ('this majestical roof fretted with golden fire') - Grand poetic and liturgical majesty, bridging ancient cosmology and dramatic wonder.
- [18th & 19th Century (Enlightenment to Victorian)] - As Copernican astronomy and Newtonian physics dissolved the crystalline celestial spheres, 'firmament' shifted from literal astronomical fact into grand poetic metaphor for the visible night sky - Romantic awe, sublimity, and literary archaism.
- [Contemporary Usage] - Primarily used in figurative, elevated prose to denote the sky or metaphorically the highest sphere of eminence in an art, science, or profession ('stars in the literary firmament') - Cultivated eloquence, figurative grandeur, and poetic reverence.
