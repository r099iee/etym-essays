Añjana (proper noun & neuter noun) — In Hindu mythology, Puranic cosmology, and classical Sanskrit literature, Añjana is one of the celebrated Aṣṭa-Diggajas (the eight primordial guardian bull elephants) that support the terrestrial world at the cardinal and intermediate directions under the sovereignty of the guardian deities (*Lokapālas*). Renowned for his colossal, mountain-like stature and glossy, ink-black or deep charcoal complexion, Añjana specifically presides over the Western (or in some Puranic accounts, the Southwestern) quadrant of the universe, paired with his celestial consort, the cow-elephant Añjanāvatī.

Primary mythological sense — the cosmic Diggaja of the cardinal quarters. According to the cosmogony detailed in the Mātaṅga-līlā ("The Elephant Sport") and the Brahma Purāṇa, when the creator god Brahmā sang sacred Sāman hymns over the two halves of the cosmic golden egg (*Hiraṇyagarbha*), sixteen divine elephants emerged from the shell fragments: eight majestic bulls led by Airāvata, and eight female consorts. These eight cosmic bulls became the world-bearers (*Diggajas* or *Diṅnāgas*), tasked with steadying the earth upon their massive brows, shoulders, and tusks. In Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa (the direct source text for the card), the capital city of Ayodhyā is described as teeming with the world's finest royal war-elephants—mighty bull elephants looking like towering mountains, continually in rut, belonging to the pure breeds (*bhadramandra*, *bhadramṛga*, and *mṛgamandra*) descended directly from the primordial cosmic lineages of Añjana and Vāmana.

General lexical and cosmetic sense in Sanskrit — collyrium, kohl, and eye-salve. Outside its mythic personification, añjana is a ubiquitous common noun in Sanskrit denoting eye-salve, collyrium, kohl (*kājal*), or medicinal antimony ointment. Traditionally prepared from soot, burned camphor, lampblack, and clarified butter, or ground from black antimony sulfide (*stibnite*), añjana has been applied for millennia to the rims of the eyelids in South Asia. In Ayurveda, it serves as an indispensable ocular therapeutic (*netra-kriyākalpa*) to cool inflamed eyes, stimulate tear production, remove morbid humors (*doṣas*), and shield the eyes from intense solar glare and tropical dust. In classical aesthetics and court poetry (*kāvya*), the application of añjana to the eyes is celebrated as one of the essential cosmetics (*śṛṅgāra*) enhancing the beauty, contrast, and expressive allure of a woman's gaze.

Metaphorical and philosophical sense — the eye-salve of spiritual wisdom (jñānāñjana). In Indian philosophical traditions, particularly Advaita Vedānta, Tantra, and Vaishnava theology, añjana serves as the foundational metaphor for spiritual illumination. The human mind in its unregenerate state is described as afflicted by the congenital cataract of ignorance (*ajñāna-timira*), which blinds the soul to transcendental reality. The spiritual preceptor (*guru*) acts as the divine physician, applying the eye-salve of wisdom (*jñānāñjana*) with a golden applicator (*śalākā*) to remove the film of illusion and open the spiritual eye. This metaphor is immortalized across India in the celebrated opening verse of the Guru Gītā: "Ajñāna-timirāndhasya jñānāñjana-śalākayā, cakṣur unmīlitaṁ yena tasmai śrī-gurave namaḥ" ("Salutations to that noble Guru who, with the applicator of the eye-salve of knowledge, opens the eyes of one blinded by the darkness of ignorance").

Feminine onomastic derivative — Añjanā, mother of Hanumān. In Hindu sacred genealogy and epic tradition, the feminine proper noun Añjanā denotes the celestial apsaras (originally Puñjikasthalā) who, born on earth as a noble vānara princess and married to the chieftain Kesarī, practiced intense austerities on Mount Añjana. Blessed by the wind god Vāyu and the grace of Lord Śiva, she gave birth to the monkey-god Hanumān. Consequently, one of the most widespread and beloved devotional epithets of Hanumān throughout India is the patronymic/matronymic Āñjaneya ("Son of Añjanā").

Toponymic and geological sense — Mount Añjana (Añjanagiri). In classical Indian geography and Buddhist/Jain cosmography, Añjana designates a legendary mountain of black antimony stone or dark basaltic rock, described as black as an unblemished collyrium cake or a storm cloud laden with rain. It was traditionally identified with peaks in the Kishkindha region (near modern Hampi in Karnataka) or within the Sahyadri/Vindhya systems, regarded as a sacred repository of rare Ayurvedic herbs and antimony ore.

Semantic bridge: anointing, glossy pigment, and the ink-black elephant. Philologically, the common denominator linking all these diverse senses is the Sanskrit verbal root añj, meaning "to anoint, smear, polish, make glossy, decorate, or reveal." An eye-salve is named añjana because it is an unguent smeared onto the eye, producing a dark, lustrous sheen. The cosmic elephant is named Añjana precisely because of his lustrous, coal-black, shiny hide—resembling an elephant anointed with rich black ointment or resembling a glossy raincloud saturated with water. The shift from physical ointment to cosmic elephant and philosophical illumination exemplifies the brilliant metaphorical cohesion of Sanskrit lexicography.

  • In Hindu cosmology and elephant science (Gajaśāstra) — Añjana-diggaja: the colossal western cosmic bull elephant supporting the world, ancestor of royal war-elephants.
  • In Ayurvedic medicine and cosmetics — añjana: herbal and mineral eye-salve (collyrium, kohl) applied to heal eye diseases and beautify the gaze.
  • In Advaita Vedānta and Guru veneration — jñānāñjana-śalākā: the symbolic golden tool used to apply the eye-salve of spiritual enlightenment to dispel ignorance.
  • In sacred epic genealogy — Añjanā-mātā: the mother of Hanumān; source of the name Āñjaneya.
  • In classical Sanskrit poetics (Alaṅkāraśāstra) — the standard poetic comparison for pristine, deep blackness (e.g., night sky, bee wings, rainclouds).

Register and cultural resonance. Añjana operates across multiple elevated registers in South Asian culture, encompassing sacred ritualism, high philosophical verse, classical Ayurvedic medicine, and epic mythology.
