- Late Latin — to beat: battuere means to strike or pound, the soldier's and fuller's verb.
- c. 1300 — English adopts it: Middle English bateren batters doors, enemies, and eggs alike.
- Medieval — the siege engine: battering rams and battering trains become the standard furniture of warfare and chronicle.
- 16th–18th c. — weather and metaphor: storms batter coasts in poetry while pamphleteers batter reputations in prose.
- 19th c. — industry and empire: steam hammers batter iron, and colonial sieges keep the ram in the dispatches (and in boys' adventure books).
- 20th c. — the human cost: battered wives and battered babies (1970s) give the word its grimmest compound, while sport keeps its comic twin (batsmen battering bowling).
- Today — blows everywhere: weather batterings on the news, reputation batterings in politics, and Tolkien's ram still the coziest siege engine in literature.
