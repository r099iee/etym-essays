- The plain old word for faltering speech — Old English stamerian; applied to any halting, repeated or blocked utterance; tinged with associations of fear, guilt or folly.
- The moralising centuries — in 17th–19th-century England “stammering” is an affliction to be pitied, mocked or disciplined; the stammerer is a stock comic or pathetic figure.
- The medical turn — 19th-century speech science makes it a clinical condition (James Hunt, 1861) and opens the stammer/stutter terminology debate.
- Modern revaluation — recognised as a neurodevelopmental fluency disorder (ICD F98.5) affecting about 1% of adults; celebrated figures who stammer (George VI, Darwin, Carroll) have stripped away much of the stigma.
- Metaphorical spread — “stammering” extends to any halting rhythm: a stammering engine, stammering gunfire, a stammering pen.
