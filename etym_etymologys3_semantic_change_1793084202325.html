The compound term semantic change unites a young adjective with an old verb, and its history must therefore be told in two strands that meet only in the modern linguistics classroom.

First element — semantic: the story begins with Ancient Greek sēma, a sign, mark, or token — the blazed mark on a tree, the omen in the sky, the grave-mound that says someone lies here. From it the philosophers formed sēmantikos, significant or indicative, used of symptoms that signify disease and of words that signify things. The adjective slept for two millennia as a rare learned formation until the French philologist Michel Bréal revived it as sémantique in 1883 for the new science of meaning — the study of how signs signify and how signification moves. English borrowed semantics and semantic in the 1890s, and within a generation every introduction to language carried a chapter on semantic change beside its chapters on sound change.

  • Sound change: Greek sēma entered French and English purely as a learned borrowing, so its consonants were never subjected to popular sound laws; the accentual journey from sēmantikós to modern semantic follows the standard anglicisation of Greek adjectives.
  • Semantic drift (of the word itself): fittingly, semantic has itself undergone the process it names — from narrowly linguistic (semantic features, semantic fields) to broadly meaningful (semantic differences in a debate) to computing (the semantic web), widening with every decade.
  • Socio-cultural context: Bréal's coinage answered a nineteenth-century need: after sound laws had been triumphantly mapped, meaning remained the wild frontier, and a science with a Greek name gave scholars licence to chart it.

Second element — change: this is one of the oldest travellers in the lexicon. Latin cambīre, to exchange or barter, was itself borrowed from Celtic *kamb- (bent, crooked — the barter-deal sealed by bending or joining hands), entering Late Latin as everyday commercial vocabulary. Old French changier generalised it from exchanging goods to altering anything — clothes, minds, moons — and Middle English chaungen, arriving with the Normans in the thirteenth century, spread it across the whole language until native wend and shap retreated before it.

  • Sound change: the Norman ch- softened in English mouths, the nasalised vowel levelled to modern change, and the Great Vowel Shift lifted the vowel to its present diphthong — a textbook French loan fully naturalised.
  • Semantic drift: from commercial exchange to universal alteration, then to small coin (spare change), fresh clothing (a change of linen), and modernity itself (the Change, the menopause) — the verb that began by swapping goods now swaps everything.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer chaungeth minds and tales; Shakespeare rings changes on bells and fortunes; the Authorised Version promises that we shall all be changed — three centuries of alteration in one verb.

The compound: semantic change crystallised in early-twentieth-century textbooks as the fixed heading for meaning-drift, paired forever with phonetic change exactly as the source passage pairs them. It names no single event but the weather of all meaning — the slow climate in which expedient feet grew into advantages and jumping salts settled into obviousness.
