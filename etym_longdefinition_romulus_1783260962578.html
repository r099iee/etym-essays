Romulus (prop. n.) — the legendary founder and first king of Rome, traditionally held to have built the city on the Palatine Hill and reigned from 753 BC. With his twin brother Remus — both suckled, in legend, by a she-wolf — he is the central figure of Rome's foundation myth. In this card's context the name serves as the type-case of the "mythical person": "Hengist and Horsa, Vortigern and Rowena, Arthur and Mordred are mythical persons... whose adventures must be classed with those of Hercules and Romulus."

Primary sense (the legendary founder). Son of the god Mars and the Vestal Rhea Silvia, cast out as an infant with Remus, raised by the shepherd Faustulus, Romulus is credited with founding the city (traditionally 21 April 753 BC), populating it with outlaws and refugees, supplying women by the rape of the Sabine women, and instituting the Senate and the early Roman state. The name is inseparable from the foundation narrative and from the founding act of fratricide — the murder of Remus — that Roman tradition itself pondered as the city's original sin.

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings:

  • Eponym of Rome and "the Roman" — the name is the adjectival/diminutive of Rōma (with the Latin suffix -ulus), literally "he of Rome" or "little Roman"; to the ancients, Romulus and Rōma were locked together as city and eponym, and the name became a badge of Romanness. Semantic bridge: the founder's name explains the city's name (or, sceptically, the city's name was back-formed into a founder).
  • The apotheosised god Quirinus — Roman tradition held that Romulus was carried up to heaven in a storm and became the god Quirinus; the name thus also names a deity of the Roman state cult. Semantic bridge: the euhemeristic story — the founder as a divinised hero — mirrors the Greek apotheosis of Heracles.
  • Romulus Augustulus and the fall of the West — the last Western Roman emperor, deposed in 476 AD, was a boy nicknamed Romulus Augustulus ("little Augustus"); the coincidence of names supplied historians with the ironic symmetry of an empire that began with a Romulus and ended with one. Semantic bridge: the name's reuse makes it a bookend of imperial history, from mythic foundation to historical fall.
  • Historiographical type of the mythic founder — modern historians use "Romulus" as the exemplar of the aetiological founder-figure: a name invented or retrojected to explain a city's origin. In Macaulay's sentence the name does exactly this work, standing alongside Hercules as the standard against which British legendary heroes are judged mythical.
  • Astronomical and scientific naming — "Romulus" (with "Remus") names a moon of the asteroid 87 Sylvia; the pairing preserves the myth in modern astronomical nomenclature. Semantic bridge: the discoverers consciously borrowed the inseparable twin-names.
  • Popular culture — "Romulus and Remus" and the she-wolf (the Lupa Capitolina) are global symbols of Rome; the names recur in games, films, and literature whenever the foundation myth is invoked.

Etymology in brief and sense development. The name is formed on Rōma, itself of debated — probably Etruscan — origin, with the Latin suffix -ulus, giving "the Roman one." The Roman antiquarians offered folk-etymologies (from Greek rhōmē "strength," or from an old name of the Tiber), all now rejected. What matters for meaning is the symbolic load: the founder's name is the city's name, and the word thus carried from the start the full weight of Roman identity. Over time it evolved from personal name to myth, from myth to civic emblem, and from emblem to historiographical term for the mythic founder as such — precisely the sense in which Macaulay deploys it when he classes the British legend with "those of Hercules and Romulus."
