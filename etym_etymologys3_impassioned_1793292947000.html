The root of impassioned is a wound. Its deepest ancestor is the PIE root *peh₁-, to hurt or harm, a grim stem whose descendants include Greek pema, suffering and calamity, and Latin pati, to suffer, endure, and bear. From pati Latin built a whole family of endurance: patient as the one who bears, passive as the one acted upon, compatible as those who suffer together. The moral world of Rome measured virtue by what a person could bear without breaking, and the verb of bearing became the noun of holiness.

Late Latin passio narrows this endurance to its most sacred instance: the suffering of Christ in the final days, and by extension the sufferings of martyrs who imitated him. Capitalised as the Passion, the word centres the Christian calendar, naming the season, the plays, the music, and the relics of the holy hurt. Its grammar is telling: passio is built with the suffix of completed states, so it names not the act of suffering but the condition of having suffered, the hallowed aftermath that art and liturgy contemplate. Nothing yet suggests romance or rhetoric.

Old French passion carries the sacred sense into the vernacular and then lets it overflow. By the 12th century the troubadours and romancers, writing of love with the vocabulary of devotion, call the lover's torment a passion: to love hopelessly is to suffer exquisitely, and the Crucifixion's language ennobles the bedroom's ache. Middle English passioun inherits both altars at once, so that one text mourns Christ's passion on the cross while the next sighs over a knight's passion for his lady, and preachers warn that earthly passions ape the heavenly one. Phonetics glide smoothly throughout: the Latin double ss softens in French, the diphthong simplifies in English, and stress settles on the first syllable of the modern noun.

The adjective impassioned itself is an early modern English creation, first clearly seen in the 16th and 17th centuries, built by prefixing im-, in or into, to passion and adding the participial -ed, on the model of impassioned verse and impassioned clay. Where French said passionné, English made its own participle, and the new word meant endowed with passion or plunged into feeling. Shakespeare's age applies it to music, poetry, and lovers with equal ease, and Milton's devils debate with impassioned rhetoric, proving that even Hell speaks well when it burns.

Semantic and social landmarks:

  • Late antiquity: passio hallows suffering in martyrologies and liturgies, the Church's central noun of merit.
  • Courtly love: troubadours transfer the vocabulary of the Cross to the chamber, and passion becomes love's delicious wound.
  • Reformation pulpit: preachers contrast holy and worldly passions, urging hearers to school feeling by faith.
  • Restoration stage: critics praise impassioned acting and verse, feeling successfully counterfeited as art.
  • Romantic agitation: reformers and patriots grow impassioned over causes, and earnestness becomes political virtue.
  • Modern scholarship: universities prize the impassioned lecture but distrust impassioned allegiance, honouring fire in delivery and fearing it in judgement.

Literary attestations mark every turn. The Authorised Version gives us passions of the flesh to be mortified; Restoration drama advertises impassion'd heroes on playbills; 18th-century sermons plead with impassioned earnestness for charity; Romantic novels breed impassioned heroines whose letters burn the page; and 20th-century oratory, from courtrooms to civil-rights marches, makes the impassioned plea the set-piece of public persuasion. The source sentence belongs to this last tradition by negation: the scholar of orality and literacy refuses the crusader's heat and asks for reflection instead.

Today impassioned keeps its double reputation intact. Juries are moved by impassioned pleas, charities thrive on impassioned appeals, and lovers still write impassioned letters, while editors warn that impassioned allegiances make bad scholarship and worse policy. From PIE hurt through Latin endurance to modern earnestness, the word has travelled from the wound to the warmth, and its whole history explains why we both crave and distrust it: passion proves we care, but only reason proves we are right.
