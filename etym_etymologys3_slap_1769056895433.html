The word slap begins as a sound before it begins as a meaning. Its earliest identifiable ancestor is an imitative Germanic base *slapp-, a form shaped to echo the flat, sharp noise made when the palm of the hand meets a surface; such sound-symbolic words are coined over and over in the Germanic languages, and slap is one of the survivors. No Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed for it—the family is best treated as sound-symbolic rather than inherited, the word's whole "ancestry" being the human ear's imitation of impact. In the Middle Ages the base was current on the continent: Middle Low German slappe and Middle Dutch slappe meant simply "a blow, a cuff," and it was from these continental forms, at the start of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, that English took the word into Middle English as slappe or sclappe—a noun meaning "a blow with the open hand." The borrowing is a good example of how the English of the period enriched itself from the Low German and Dutch speech of the North Sea trade. 1. The sound-symbolic birth
  • Imitative base *slapp- (Germanic): the flat, sharp report of an open hand meeting a surface. Related imitative clusters in English—clap, flap, lap, smack, whack, thwack, slab—show the same pattern of a s- or stop-onset plus a final -p/-k burst; these are siblings by formation, not cognates by inheritance.
  • Middle Low German slappe / Middle Dutch slappe (14th century) — "a blow, a cuff, a slap." The continental forms supplied the word to English.
  • Middle English slappe / sclappe (early 14th–15th centuries) — noun, "a blow with the open hand." The borrowing entered with trade and was in place well before the end of the medieval period.
2. The explosion of senses in early modern English
  • Noun to verb: the verb to slap, "to strike with the open hand," is recorded from about 1600, formed from the noun. From the verb grew the figurative idioms: a slap in the face (an insult or humiliation), a slap on the wrist (a mild or token punishment), slap down (to crush an argument or ambition).
  • The adverbial shift (1670s): slap came to be used as an adverb meaning "suddenly, directly, full upon"—as in running slap into a wall, or sitting slap in the middle of something. The semantic mechanism is transparent: because a slap makes immediate, complete, face-to-face contact with its target, the word came to mean exactly that kind of contact in space, with nothing in between. This is the sense used in this passage ("slap up against the gate").
  • Compounds: slap-bang (1786), which adds the still more explosive bang; slapdash (1670s), originally "with a slap and a dash," i.e. in one hasty stroke; slapstick (18th century), the Harlequin's two-lath stick, and by extension the whole physical comic genre; slap-happy (1936), "dazed from blows," later "cheerfully foolish."
  • German parallel: Schlappe, from the same imitative base, moved from "a slap, a blow" to the abstract "defeat, setback"—the blow that knocks a person or party down—showing how the same sound-word specialized in two directions in two languages.
3. Sound shifts and form
  • English /slæp/ shows the short vowel and final unvoiced plosive typical of the imitative family; the spelling with s- preserved the Low German onset, and the word has never undergone the Great Vowel Shift because its vowel remained short.
  • The adverbial use is grammatically notable: a concrete noun and verb developed a pure adverb sense with no change of form—a conversion rare enough that dictionaries treat "slap" the adverb as a distinct part of speech from the 1670s onward.
4. Socio-cultural and literary life
  • The word lives at the junction of slang and standard: slapstick made it a word of theatre and then of film comedy; slap bass carried it into jazz and rock; the British adverbial phrases (slap in the middle, slap up against) keep it as a marker of colloquial, emphatic English—exactly the register Edmund uses to express his indignant disbelief.
  • Modern slang extends the same logic: "slap" as a compliment ("the track slaps") still means something that lands with flat, instant, total impact—the ancient palm-print of the original blow, five centuries after the word crossed the North Sea.
