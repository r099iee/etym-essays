In the Cao Xueqin passage about Aroma and Bao-yu, remonstrate names a particular kind of speech: not scolding for dominance, but earnest moral protest born of devotion. Aroma finds Bao-yu's character 'strange and incomprehensible,' yet her simple loyalty repeatedly drives her to remonstrate with him—to set reasons before him, to point out why his behavior is wrong or dangerous. The verb is weighted with care and futility: she speaks because she must, and he 'took not the least notice,' so the remonstration becomes a measure of her attachment and of his resistance to correction. Tone is serious, affectionate, and slightly formal; the word lifts her objections above mere nagging into the register of principled appeal. At its core, to remonstrate is to make a forcefully reproachful protest—to argue earnestly against a course of action, a decision, or a person's behavior by setting out the reasons against it. The word's literal Latin anatomy is revealing: re- 'back, in return' + monstrāre 'to show,' hence 'to show back,' to hold the grounds of complaint up against the thing complained of. Remonstrating is therefore not commanding or punishing but demonstrating—pointing out to someone why they are wrong, in the hope that they will see it.
  • Core sense: to protest or object earnestly and with argument (intransitive: remonstrate with someone about / against / over something; formal: remonstrate that...). The protest is reasoned, interpersonal, and directed at a person in the hope of reform, not at a crowd in hope of publicity.
  • Historical-political sense: remonstrance (the noun) came to mean a formal written statement of grievances addressed to a sovereign or authority—famously the Grand Remonstrance presented to Charles I in 1641—so remonstrate carries a parliamentary and constitutional echo of petitioning power by argument.
  • Religious-historical nuance: the Remonstrants were the seventeenth-century Dutch Arminian theologians who 'remonstrated' (formally set forth their five articles of protest) against Calvinist doctrine; the word's register of formal doctrinal protest lingers in remonstrance.
  • Grammatical behavior: remonstrate is usually intransitive with with (remonstrate with a friend) plus about / against / over the matter; transitive use (to remonstrate that...) is formal. Related forms: remonstrance (formal protest, petition), remonstration, remonstrator, remonstrative.
  • Register and collocation: literary, legal-historical, and educated standard—more at home in novels, petitions, and parliamentary prose than in casual speech. Collocates include strongly, gently, vainly, bitterly; with a friend, with the authorities. The word implies that reasons are shown back to the hearer, who is expected to acknowledge them; when they are not, the remonstrance has failed, as it does with Bao-yu.
Synonym boundaries sharpen the sense. Protest can be public, political, or collective; remonstrate is typically interpersonal and reasoned. Object stresses disagreement with a proposal more than moral re-showing of fault. Reprove and rebuke are more openly hierarchical and punitive—the voice of authority correcting an inferior—whereas remonstrate often implies the speaker lacks coercive power and relies on argument and feeling. Admonish sits close but leans toward warning and counsel; remonstrate emphasizes demonstration of grounds for complaint (the monstr- of 'showing'). Expostulate is a near twin in formality, slightly more rhetorical and pleading. In this literary context, remonstrate is the devoted servant's or companion's protest: forceful in sincerity, weak in effect.
