The English abstract noun bravado entered the language during the vibrant lexical explosion of the Elizabethan era (c. 1580s–1590s), a period marked by intense cultural, military, and linguistic cross-pollination between England and continental Europe. Borrowed primarily from French bravade and influenced by Spanish bravada and Italian bravata, the word underwent a distinct morphological modification in English, where it was outfitted with a pseudo-Spanish suffix -ado to evoke the swaggering, swashbuckling atmosphere of Mediterranean martial culture and Renaissance fencing halls.

Ancient Roots and the Paradoxical Evolution of "Bravo":

  • The Barbarian Heritage (*bárbaros to bravus): The ultimate etymological progenitor of bravado is the ancient Greek onomatopoeic adjective βάρβαρος (bárbaros), coined to imitate the unintelligible, babbling speech ("bar-bar") of foreigners outside the Hellenic world. This passed into Classical Latin as barbarus ("foreign, uncivilized, savage, wild").
    • Phonological Lenition in Vulgar Latin: As the Western Roman Empire disintegrated, Vulgar Latin speakers across northern Italy and southern Gaul contracted and lenited barbarus, likely through intermediate forms like *barvus to *bravus or brabus ("wild, savage, unruly, fierce").
    • Semantic Conflation with Latin Prāvus: Many historical lexicographers (such as Joan Coromines and Alberto Nocentini) note that this transition was heavily influenced or crossed with Classical Latin prāvus ("crooked, perverted, depraved, vicious"), giving Late Latin bravus an initial meaning of dangerous, wild, fierce, and unruly (retained in Spanish toro bravo, a wild fighting bull).
  • The Semantic Shift from Ferocity to Gallantry: In medieval Provençal and Italian, the meaning of bravo underwent a profound chivalric bifurcation:
    • Noble Valence: Under the influence of courtly chivalry, "fierce in battle" shifted into "daring, valiant, noble, excellent" (the source of the French praise brave and the theatrical acclaim bravo!).
    • Sinister Valence: In Italian cities, bravo simultaneously maintained its dangerous edge, specializing into a noun denoting a swaggering mercenary, hired ruffian, cutthroat, or assassin—immortalized in Alessandro Manzoni’s historical novel I Promessi Sposi as Don Rodrigo's armed thugs.

The Coining of Bravata and Elizabethan Anglicization:

  • Romance Suffixation (bravata / bravada / bravade): From the adjective bravo, Renaissance Italian coined the action noun bravata ("a boast, defiance, swaggering challenge, blustering threat"), which Spanish mirrored as bravada and French adopted as bravade. These terms captured the combative posturing of professional mercenaries during the Italian Wars (1494–1559), where soldiers sought to terrify opponents before sword blows were struck.
  • The Elizabethan Fascination with -ado Suffixes: When the word crossed into Elizabethan English in the late 1580s, English writers demonstrated an extraordinary stylistic penchant for appending the Romance suffix -ado to loanwords, associating the sonorous terminal vowel with Spanish imperial arrogance, grandiloquence, and martial danger (witnessed in Elizabethan coinages like bastinado, renegado, desperado, camisado, and tornado). Thus, French bravade and Spanish bravada were reshaped into bravado.
  • Literary Naturalization from Shakespeare to Modernity: By the 1590s, the word was thoroughly naturalized in the English theater, appearing in Shakespeare’s Love’s Labour’s Lost and throughout the works of Ben Jonson, Thomas Dekker, and John Dryden. Initially used to denote an explicit verbal threat or swaggering military challenge, its meaning steadily deepened over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries into an internal psychological critique: a hollow display of courage adopted precisely by those who lack the quiet moral fortitude of genuine bravery.
