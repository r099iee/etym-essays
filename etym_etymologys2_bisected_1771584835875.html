BISECTED — two Latin sources fused in a modern coinage; full attested chain from each source, converging at the English verb. Root source 1 (the "bi-" half, "two"): • PIE *dwóh₁ / *dwó "two" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; attested across the whole Indo-European family in Greek dúo, Sanskrit dvā́, Latin duo, Gothic twai, etc.) → Proto-Italic *duō (reconstructed) → Latin duo "two" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE); adverbial accusative bis "twice, doubly" (classical form of an older *duis, attested from Plautus onward; duis survives in the compound "duisilvae" and is given by ancient grammarians such as Priscian as the older form of bis). Root source 2 (the "-sect" half, "cut"): • PIE *sek- "to cut" (reconstructed; the root is securely attested across the family in Latin secāre "to cut," Greek hékhein/hékein? no — Greek forms are disputed; Old Norse sǫg "saw" (a cutting tool), English "saw," German Säge, Lithuanian įsekti "to cut in"; the root is among the most solidly reconstructed PIE verbal roots). → Proto-Italic *sek-ā- (reconstructed) → Latin secāre "to cut" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE); past participle sectus "cut" (attested; also the adjective "cut off, separate"). → Latin secāre gives the participial stem sect- which is used in dozens of Latin and New Latin compounds (sector, section, intersect, dissect, trisect, vivisect). Convergence: → New Latin bisectus / bisectāre "to cut in two" (scholarly coinage of the 17th century; formed from Latin bis "twice" + sectus "cut" in the mathematical Latin of the early modern period; the formation follows the established pattern of intersect/dissect). → English bisect "to divide into two equal parts" (verb, borrowed 1640s, first in geometry — dividing angles and lines; OED first records c. 1647). → English bisected "divided into two parts" (past participle and participial adjective; used from the 17th century, extended beyond geometry from the 19th century). → Modern English BISECTED. Note: there is no intermediate unattested or disputed stage in this chain — both Latin branches are fully attested, and the only "coinage" step is the well-documented New Latin formation of the 17th century.
