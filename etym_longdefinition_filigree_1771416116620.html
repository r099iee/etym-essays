Filigree is the art of the delicate in metal: fine threads of gold or silver twisted, curled, and soldered into open, lace-like tracery, often accented with tiny beads. In this passage the word is a jewel of costume description—Xi-feng’s “chignon was enclosed in a circlet of gold filigree and clustered pearls.” The detail is not incidental. Filigree is gold that imitates embroidery: it converts the heaviest of precious metals into something that looks weightless, lacy, even floral, and it frames Xi-feng’s hair like a halo of manufactured light. The narrator uses it to signal status (only the very rich could afford such labour-intensive work) and temperament (the intricate, interlaced pattern mirrors Xi-feng’s own complex social artistry—delicate to the eye, unyielding beneath). As a material fact and as a symbol, filigree is the perfect ornament for a woman whose entire life is a web of carefully woven relations and transactions. The word must be distinguished from its metalworking cousins. Engraving cuts an image into a solid surface; repoussé pushes metal outward from behind into raised relief; damascening inlays contrasting wire or gold into etched steel; granulation decorates a surface with tiny fused spheres; tracery names the open stonework of gothic windows and, by metaphor, any open pattern; while lace is fibre, not metal. Filigree is distinct in being constructed from wire itself, soldered strand by strand into an openwork structure, so that the finished piece is largely empty space—a web, a cage, a woven thing of metal. It is therefore the ornament of lightness and intricacy par excellence, the goldsmith’s answer to embroidery. In register, filigree belongs to connoisseurship: jewellery catalogues, antique dealing, art history, and literary prose that wishes to convey fine craftsmanship. It collocates with gold, silver, fine, delicate, intricate, and wire (“gold filigree,” “a filigree of silver”), and it is frequently used metaphorically for any fine network of lines or relationships (“a filigree of veins,” “the filigree of a bureaucracy”). In the novel it is a word of crafted splendour: it lends Xi-feng’s entrance a technical, jewelled precision, the same precision with which the narrator herself is constructing the portrait of one of fiction’s most artfully self-made women.
  • Primary sense (metalwork): Ornamental work of fine gold or silver wire formed into delicate tracery; a technique of twisting, curling, and soldering wire into openwork, often studded with small beads or granules.
  • Extended sense (materials and structures): Any fine openwork pattern of wire, metal, or even stone that mimics the jeweller’s technique (“a filigree of iron”).
  • Figurative sense: Any extremely fine, intricate network of lines, veins, frost, or relationships (“a filigree of veins,” “the filigree of court intrigue”).
  • Register: Technical in jewellery and metalworking, literary and aesthetic in prose, metaphorical in journalism and description.
Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings: (1) In jewellery and silversmithing, filigree is the precise technique of wire-based openwork, distinguished from engraving, chasing, and repoussé; (2) in architecture and design it names any delicate open pattern imitating wirework, including iron gates and architectural screens; (3) in figurative language it describes anything of exquisite fine interlacing—frost on a window, leaf veins, snowflake arms, the traceries of bureaucracy or of a plot; (4) historically, the French form filigrane also named the watermark in paper, the thread-like design pressed into the sheet, and this paper sense survives in connoisseurship and philately. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: the word is a compound of Latin filum “thread” and granum “grain”: medieval Italian goldsmiths coined filigrana (filo “wire” + grana “grain, bead”) for wirework studded with grains of gold; passing through French filigrane into English in the seventeenth century, the term extended from the specific technique to any delicate openwork, and finally to any metaphorically “woven” fineness. In the passage it frames Xi-feng—herself an interlaced web of arts and transactions—in a circlet that is literally a small woven web of gold.
