The word giddy traces one of the most remarkable semantic journeys in the English language — from the sacred to the insane, from the insane to the foolish, from the foolish to the dizzy, and from the dizzy to the delightful. Its root, PIE *ǵʰeu- "to pour, to libate," belongs to the deep ritual vocabulary of the Indo-European peoples, encoding the fundamental religious act of pouring out a liquid offering to the gods. In the reconstructed world of the Proto-Indo-Europeans, the libation was the quintessential act of worship: honey, milk, melted butter (ghee), or the blood of a sacrificed animal was poured onto the earth, into a fire, or into running water, accompanied by spoken invocations, hymns, and prayers. The poured substance was the material medium through which the worshipper made contact with the divine; and the divine, in response, might "pour" its presence into the worshipper. This conceptual symmetry — humans pour offerings upward; gods pour inspiration, or madness, downward — is embedded in the very structure of the root and its descendants. In the Germanic branch the semantic development is spectacular. The verbal adjective *ǵʰu-tó-m "that which is poured," the offered substance, was reinterpreted as a noun meaning "the one to whom the offering is poured, the invoked deity" — giving Proto-Germanic *gudą "god." This is not a unique shift: in many religious traditions the recipient of an act is named by the act itself. From *gudą the Germanic languages formed an adjective *gudīgaz by adding the productive suffix *-īgaz, the ancestor of Modern English -y, producing a word that transparently meant "god-y" — having the quality of a god, influenced by a god, under the power of a god. In the pre-Christian Germanic world, where the boundary between inspiration and possession was thin, such a person was both holy and terrifying: an ecstatic prophet or a raving lunatic, one whose mind had been taken over by the divine. Old English inherited this as gydig (West Saxon) and gidig (Anglian), meaning "possessed, insane, mad, frenzied." This is the only recorded meaning in the Anglo-Saxon corpus, and it carries no hint of dizziness: a gydig man was either a visionary overtaken by a god or a dangerous madman. The word thus entered English with a weight that was simultaneously sacred and terrifying — the state of being out of one's own command, seized by a power from outside. The great semantic drift unfolded in Middle and Early Modern English. In the 13th–15th centuries gidi broadened from "possessed/insane" to "foolish, rash, thoughtless" — the frivolous, inconstant person who acts on impulse and cannot be trusted with serious matters; Chaucer's gidi characters are silly and light, not clinically mad. Then, in Early Modern English (1500–1700), the word acquired its modern physiological meaning, "dizzy; having a whirling or reeling sensation." Shakespeare uses it in both old and new senses: "the giddy round of Fortune's wheel" (fickle, inconstant) and "the giddy footing of the hatches" (dizzying deck of a ship). The semantic bridge is the idea of loss of control: a god-possessed person loses command of the mind, a dizzy person loses command of the balance and of spatial orientation. In both, the self is momentarily displaced from its normal mastery of the body — a connection the language made centuries before neurology would give it a name. The final stage of the journey is the most surprising. In the 18th and 19th centuries the dark associations of the word faded and the positive emotional sense flowered: to be "giddy with joy," "giddy with excitement," "giddy with delight" came to mean a pleasurable lightheadedness, the vertigo of happiness rather than of terror. The archaic sense of divine madness survives only in fossilised literary use and in the word's etymology, but it is precisely this buried depth that a careful writer like Tolkien can activate. When he writes "At the best of times heights made Bilbo giddy," the word carries both the modern physiological meaning — the hobbit's dizzying fear of heights — and a faint, resonant echo of its oldest sense: the vertiginous, self-dissolving panic that is the secular descendant of divine possession. Bilbo's giddiness is not merely physical but existential; the abyss threatens to unmake his hobbit-sense of being grounded in the world. A single four-letter word thus compresses a thousand years of religious, moral, and physiological history.
