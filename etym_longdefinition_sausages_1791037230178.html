Sausages is the plural of sausage: short cylindrical cases — traditionally cleaned animal intestine, now often collagen or synthetic — stuffed with seasoned minced meat, typically pork, beef, or a mixture, with salt, pepper, herbs, breadcrumbs or rusk, eaten fried, grilled, baked or boiled. In British English the word evokes the breakfast banger, the toad-in-the-hole, sausage rolls and the butcher's counter; in wider use it covers frankfurters, bratwurst, chorizo, salami and hundreds of regional types. The source context — "the matter of the sausages and ham" — refers to a dish served at breakfast, a comic emblem of English plenty.

The prototype is savoury and meaty, but the category has widened to include chicken, venison, blood (black pudding), fish, vegetarian and vegan sausages that mimic shape and seasoning without meat. Form rather than filling increasingly defines the word: anything sausage-shaped in a skin is called a sausage.

  • Food prototype: Seasoned minced pork in natural or artificial casing, linked or in lengths. Pork sausages, Cumberland sausages, Lincolnshire sausages, cocktail sausages. Cooked to browned, savoury, juicy. Core of full English breakfast with bacon/ham, eggs, beans.
  • Regional types: Banger (British everyday, so called from bursting), frankfurter / wiener / hot dog (German-American), bratwurst, chorizo (paprika pork), salami / pepperoni (cured), merguez, Chipolata. Each varies meat, grind, spice, cure, size.
  • Dachshund (sausage dog): Colloquial British: a sausage dog is a dachshund, from long cylindrical body. He walks two sausages in the park. Affectionate, never formal.
  • Not a sausage / not a sausage (British slang): Nothing at all. There wasn't a sausage left, he knew not a sausage about it. From rhyming or comic understatement; equivalent to not a bean, not a sausage.
  • Sausage fingers / sausage factory: Sausage fingers are short, stubby, swollen fingers. Sausage factory (often pejorative) is a place producing uniform products — pop songs, graduates — like sausages extruded. Sausage party / sausage fest (vulgar US) is a gathering with too many men.
  • Shape metaphor: Anything sausage-shaped: sausage cushion (bolster), sausage balloon, airship sausage. Technical: in statistics a sausage plot; in climbing a sausage (rolled rope).

The semantic bridge from meat to metaphor is visual and cultural: length, plumpness, uniformity, ordinariness. A sausage is humble, filling, slightly comic — never luxurious like fillet or exotic like chorizo until qualified. Hence insults and jokes stick easily, while praise requires adjectives: proper sausages, decent sausages.

Grammatically sausages is a regular countable plural (one sausage, two sausages), often mass in catering (sausage and ham, sausage for breakfast). Collocates with bangers, mash, sizzle, fry, pork, Cumberland, veggie. Register is neutral-everyday for food, informal-humorous for idioms. Unlike French saucisson or German Wurst with artisanal prestige, English sausages remains homely, school-dinner and Wodehousian — the food of Bertie Wooster's world, threatened, stolen, overeaten, and always funny.
