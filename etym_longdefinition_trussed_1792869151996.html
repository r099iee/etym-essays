Trussed is the past participle of truss, to tie, bind, or pack up tightly: drawn together with cords or straps until the bundle holds fast and nothing straggles.

In the source sentence breakfast is over and the packs are all trussed up again, and any walker knows the small ritual: blankets rolled, straps hauled, knots tested with a boot-toe, the whole camp condensed into two neat burdens after the sprawl of the meal. The up matters, completing the job, and the all tells of habit, travellers who have trussed and untrussed their lives a hundred mornings running, with the day turning fine and hot around them.

Core meaning in detail: To truss is to master looseness: the fowl whose legs are skewered to its body, the prisoner lashed to a chair, the hay compressed into a truss, the hernia held by a surgical truss, all share one action of binding scattered parts into a governed whole. The participle trussed reports the job done, the bundle competent, ready to be carried, cooked, lifted, or borne.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Packs and burdens trussed up (primary source sense): luggage strapped, bedrolls bound, parcels corded for the road. The bridge is the noun truss, a bundle or pack: to truss a pack is to make it a proper truss, compact against jolting miles.
  • The trussed fowl (cookery): the chicken or turkey with legs and wings skewered close to the body for even roasting. The bridge runs from pack to bird: the cook binds the carcass exactly as the carrier binds the bundle, neatness serving the spit as it serves the shoulder.
  • The trussed captive (restraint): prisoners, Guy Fawkes effigies, and comedy villains lashed hand and foot. The bridge is darker but identical in handiwork: cord mastering a sprawling body, whether blanket-roll, bird, or burglar.
  • The surgical truss (medicine): the padded belt holding a hernia in place, worn for centuries before modern repair. The bridge is support by binding: the same strapped pressure that steadies a pack steadies the body, and the device keeps the old noun alive in chemists' windows.
  • The engineer's truss (architecture): strictly the noun, the braced framework of a bridge or roof, triangulated timber or steel. The bridge, if the pun is forgiven, is structural binding: members tied into triangles that cannot rack, the packer's knot writ large in iron over a river.

Register and connotation: trussed is workmanlike and faintly archaic, smelling of canvas, straw, twine, and kitchen string. Soldiers truss kit, cooks truss birds, nurses once trussed patients' limbs with bandages, and each use praises competence: nothing dangling, nothing to catch in doors. Applied to people it can sting, suggesting helplessness or ceremony overdone, a lord mayor trussed in robes, a victim trussed like a turkey, but applied to packs, as in the source, it is pure approval, the morning's first small victory before the hot miles.
