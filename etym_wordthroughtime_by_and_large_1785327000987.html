- [Mid-17th century: nautical jargon] - In the language of sail, “by” means close-hauled to the wind and “large” means running with the wind free; a vessel that sails “by and large” performs creditably in every condition of the wind, and the phrase begins as a seaman’s all-round verdict on a ship.
- [18th–19th century: transfer ashore] - The idiom sails out of the navy into general speech, taking on the meaning “in every respect, comprehensively” — a way of summing up a situation without carving out exceptions, its nautical precision gradually dissolving.
- [Later 19th century: hedged generalization] - It settles into its modern sense, “on the whole, everything considered,” and becomes the polite formula by which a speaker asserts a broad truth while quietly leaving the door open for anomalies — the characteristic move of cautious essayists and scholars.
- [Contemporary sense] - A fully frozen, irreversible binomial (never “large and by”), equally at home in casual conversation and in academic prose, where it licenses a responsible generalization: the claim holds for the great run of cases, exceptions aside, with only a faintly salty flavour left to recall the forecastle.
