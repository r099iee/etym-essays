coveted — The biography of covet begins in heat. Its ultimate root, PIE *kwep-, meant "to boil, to seethe, to smoke" — the agitation of water coming to the boil, of steam rising, of something stirred from within. Different branches of Indo-European poured this heat into different vessels. In Sanskrit the root produced kúpyati, "he is angry," and kopa, "anger, wrath" — the boil turned to rage; the word passed into Hindi as kop, the learned inherited word for anger, so that in India the same root that burns in covetousness still means fury. In Latin, however, the heat was channeled toward longing: cupere meant "to desire, to long for," the desire that seethes in the chest. From cupere Latin made cupīdō, "desire" personified, which gave the West the god Cupid — the passion of the root given wings and a bow — and cupiditās, "greed, inordinate longing," the moralized form of the same heat. From cupiditās the Vulgar Latin of the late empire built the verb *cupidietāre, which became Old French covoitier, "to desire inordinately," and from it Middle English coveten, by the thirteenth century, gave English covet — a word that entered the language already wearing the weight of the Decalogue, for the covetous man was the tenth commandment's man, the sinner who wants what is not his. Meanwhile, in a separate room of the same house, the root's smoking sense hardened into Latin vapor, "steam, exhalation," the visible breath of heat, which English took as vapour. The ancient unity is striking: the same PIE root yields the anger of Sanskrit kopa, the desire of Latin cupere, the arrow of Cupid, the greed of covetousness, and the steam of vapour — one thermal image, inner heat rising, dispersed through the languages. When Peake writes that the carvers so coveted the honour, he reaches for exactly this heat: the longing that boils beneath the disciplined surface of the castle's craft, the ambition that smokes in the chests of men whose one remaining passion is their art. The word's long social history only deepens the image. From the thirteenth century covet wore the pulpit's thunder — covetousness ranked among the seven capital sins, preached against from every medieval pulpit — and the adjective coveted, which begins to gather its modern career in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, slowly loosened its sin while keeping its ardour: to covet a prize, a role, or an honour is a fierce, simmering ambition, the moral edge dulled but the heat unchanged. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries coveted became a staple of prizes, markets, and careers — the coveted award, the coveted spot — desire still burning bright, with the old pulpit condemnation reduced to a faint undertone that the word's very form still carries. battlements — The word battlement belongs to the family of the blow. Its ultimate root is the PIE *bhau-, "to strike, to beat," a root of physical violence whose clearest Latin reflex is battuere, "to beat, pound" — a verb whose own immediate origin is debated (it may be a Celtic loan), but which was certainly a word of the workshop and the battlefield, the beating of metal and the pounding of blows. From battuere, Late Latin built battalia, "fighting exercise, combat," the arena of the blow; Old French took it as bataille, "battle," the great word of medieval war, from which English battle descends. But the Old French also formed a verbal adjective of fortification: bataillier, "to fight, to fortify," and from it bataillement, the fortification built for fighting — the parapet of a castle wall, crenellated so that defenders could strike between the gaps. Middle English received it as batelment in the fourteenth century, at the height of the castle age, and English battlement has kept its military soul ever since: the alternating merlons and crenels are literally architecture for combat, the notch in the parapet being the shooting gap. The word's cultural career, however, has drifted from the practical to the picturesque. Throughout the medieval and early modern periods battlements were functional military architecture — the archer's station along tower and curtain wall. From the late eighteenth century the Gothic Revival recast them as the romantic signature of the castle: the notched skyline became an ornament of memory, reproduced decoratively on Victorian villas, railway stations, and follies, its defence long forgotten but its silhouette universally read as "castle." In the passage, the battlements along which the carvers move at the full moon are exactly this moonlit skyline — the walkway of honour, the prize they covet, the word quietly uniting its military ancestry with the romance of the notched wall against the night sky.
