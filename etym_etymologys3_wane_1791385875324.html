The verb wane is a bedrock Germanic term with an unbroken lineage stretching back more than five millennia to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₁weh₂- (traditionally reconstructed in older scholarship as *eue- or *wā-), which carried the fundamental concrete meaning "to leave, abandon, give out, be empty, or lack."

From this ancient root, Proto-Indo-European formed an adjectival participle with the dental/nasal suffix *-nó-, yielding *h₁uh₂-nó-s ("deficient, empty, lacking"). In the Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, this developed systematically through regular sound changes—most notably the loss of the initial laryngeal and the development of Germanic short *a—into the Proto-Germanic adjective *wana-, signifying "lacking, wanting, deficient, or missing."

  • The Proto-Germanic Verb and Sister Dialects: From the adjective *wana-, speakers of Proto-Germanic formed a class II weak factitive/inchoative verb, reconstructed by Guus Kroonen as *wanōną (or *wanēnan), meaning literally "to become lacking, to make less, to diminish in quantity or size." The verb was shared across every primary branch of the Germanic diaspora:
    • In Old Saxon, it appeared as wanōn ("to diminish, decrease").
    • In Old High German, it was recorded as wanōn and wanēn ("to lessen, fail, fade away").
    • In Old Norse, it took the form vana ("to decrease, impair, cause to lack"), alongside the neuter noun vanr ("lacking, wanting").
    • In Gothic, the root is preserved in the derived noun wanains ("diminution, waning") and the adjective wans ("lacking, in want of").
  • Indo-European Cognate Networks: The root *h₁weh₂- was exceptionally productive across the wider Indo-European language family, generating cognates that illustrate how "emptiness" branched into distinct conceptual domains:
    • In Classical Latin, the same suffixed form *h₁uh₂-nó-s yielded the adjective vānus ("empty, void, hollow, unsubstantial"), which entered English through French borrowings such as vain, vanity, vanish, and evanescent.
    • In Latin verbal morphology, the extended root *h₁w-eh₂-k- produced vacāre ("to be empty, to be free from duty"), giving English vacant, vacation, vacuum, and evacuate.
    • In Sanskrit, the zero-grade derivative emerged as ūná- (ऊन), meaning "deficient, wanting, less than, incomplete," used in mathematical and astronomical texts for quantities lacking a fractional part.
  • Old English Development (*Wanian*): In Anglo-Saxon England, the verb was recorded as wanian (and West Saxon dialectal wonian). It was an extraordinarily versatile term in the Old English literary canon. In the epic poem Beowulf and the poetic translation of Boethius’s Consolation of Philosophy, wanian was used both intransitively (to describe the diminishing of physical strength, the receding of floodwaters, and the passing of earthly kingdoms) and transitively (to diminish, curtail, or injure someone's rights or wealth). Crucially, Old English speakers already employed se wanienda mōna to designate the "waning moon," establishing an astronomical colocation that has survived intact for over twelve hundred years.
  • The Alliterative Formula *Wax and Wane*: In both Old and Middle English, wanian was paired in rhythmic, alliterative opposition with weaxan ("to grow, wax", from PIE *h₂ewg-, "to increase"). The twin formula waxen and wanen became a staple of Middle English alliterative verse, symbolizing the transience of sublunary life under Boethian philosophy: as the moon waxes and wanes, so too must all earthly fortune, health, and imperial majesty rise only to decline.
  • Phonetic and Orthographic Evolution: In the transition from Old to Middle English (c. 1150–1400), the infinitive ending -ian was leveled to -en (producing Middle English wanen). The stem vowel underwent open-syllable lengthening in early Middle English, shifting short /a/ to long open /aː/. During the Great Vowel Shift of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, this long vowel raised and diphthongized through /æː/ and /eː/ to modern Received Pronunciation /eɪ/, producing modern wane. The silent final -e was preserved in standard orthography to indicate the lengthened preceding vowel.
  • Surviving Morphological Relatives in English: Beyond the verb and noun wane, the Proto-Germanic adjective *wana- survives in modern English inside the compound word wanton (from Middle English wantowen, Old English wan-togen, literally "lacking discipline, ill-brought-up", from wan- [deficient] + togen [trained/pulled]). It also directly gave rise to the North Germanic borrowing want (from Old Norse vanta, "to lack"), demonstrating how closely "waning," "wanting," and "vanity" are tethered in the deep history of human speech.

From an ancient Indo-European depiction of hollow abandonment, wane evolved into the quintessential English verb of natural, gradual diminishing—forever mirror-linked to the celestial breathing of the moon.
