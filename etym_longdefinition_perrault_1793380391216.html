Perrault refers primarily to Charles Perrault (1628–1703), the celebrated French author, academician, and high-ranking civil servant under King Louis XIV. While universally beloved in modern popular culture as the founding father of the European literary fairy tale (*Contes de ma mère l'Oye* / *Tales of Mother Goose*), in classical literary history, aesthetics, and rhetoric his surname became famous as the vanguard and chief instigator of the "Moderns" in the celebrated intellectual conflict known as the *Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes* (the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns).

In the source context from John Ruskin's Modern Painters (1843), Ruskin links Perrault directly with Zoilus, Nero, and Caligula. Ruskin examines the corrupt tendencies of critical reviews that unjustly lower living genius while exalting perished power, or conversely display insolent ignorance by trying to degrade the dead. Ruskin's reference to Perrault targets Charles Perrault's aggressive campaign in the late seventeenth century to diminish the towering monuments of ancient Greece and Rome—deprecating Homer, Plato, and Virgil as crude, primitive, and obsolete—in order to flatter the contemporary court culture of Louis XIV. To Ruskin, Perrault epitomized the critic who lacks the humility to revere timeless ancient majesty, using clever modern standards to dismiss foundational genius.

Core historical identity and the Quarrel of the Ancients and Moderns: Born into an elite Parisian bourgeois family, Perrault trained as a lawyer and served as the chief administrative assistant to Jean-Baptiste Colbert, the powerful Controller-General of Finances. In 1671, Perrault was elected to the Académie Française, where he became an influential cultural arbiter. On January 27, 1687, during a celebratory session of the Académie, Perrault read his provocative poem Le Siècle de Louis le Grand ("The Century of Louis the Great"). In this manifesto, he audaciously declared that seventeenth-century France had surpassed antiquity in the sciences, arts, philosophy, and moral refinement, explicitly arguing that Homer and the ancient poets were overrated, morally flawed, and aesthetically inferior to modern French masters.

This reading ignited the fierce Querelle, pitting Perrault and the Moderns (including Fontenelle) against the champions of the Ancients, led by Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux, Jean Racine, and later in England by Sir William Temple and Jonathan Swift. Perrault defended his modernist thesis across four monumental volumes of dialogues entitled Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes (1688–1697), systematically applying Cartesian rationalism to argue for continuous human intellectual and cultural progress.

All senses, primary and extended usages:

  • The leader of the Moderns in European literary history (critical and aesthetic sense): Charles Perrault as the historical polemicist who challenged the unquestioned authority of classical Greco-Roman antiquity, asserting that art and civilization progress alongside scientific discovery and moral refinement.
  • The father of the literary fairy tale (folkloric and cultural sense): Author of the 1697 masterpiece Histoires ou contes du temps passé, which codified classic folklore tales into enduring high literary art—including "Cinderella" (Cendrillon), "Sleeping Beauty" (La Belle au bois dormant), "Little Red Riding Hood" (Le Petit Chaperon rouge), "Puss in Boots" (Le Maître chat ou le Chat botté), and "Bluebeard" (Barbe bleue).
  • Antonomasia for the iconoclastic modernizer or anti-classical critic (literary rhetoric): Used by neoclassical, Victorian, and conservative critics (like Swift and Ruskin) to designate a critic who dismisses timeless classical models out of fashionable arrogance, cultural chauvinism, or progressive bias.
  • The Perrault architectural and scientific family (historical sense): Reference to the broader Perrault intellectual dynasty, notably Charles's older brother Claude Perrault (1613–1688), the renowned physician, anatomist, and architect who designed the monumental Colonnade of the Louvre and translated Vitruvius into French.
  • Metaphor for progressive cultural optimism: In philosophy and historiography, the "Perrault position" denotes the confident conviction that the modern era represents the summit of human enlightenment, viewing historical tradition not as an immutable sacred canon, but as a stepping stone to be surpassed.

Register and cultural resonance: Perrault operates on two distinct cultural planes. In nursery folklore and global media, it is associated with enchanted nostalgia and fairy-tale archetypes; in literary scholarship, philosophy of history, and art criticism, it carries a sophisticated, formal register, denoting the dawn of modern cultural self-confidence and the perennial debate between classical veneration and progressive modernization.
