- 8th to 10th Century (Old English) - Coined as *gōd-spel* as an Anglo-Saxon loan-translation (calque) of Ecclesiastical Latin *evangelium*; vowel shortened to *godspel* before consonant cluster, encouraging early homophonic association with *God* rather than *good*.
- 11th to 14th Century (Middle English) - Solidified in religious prose (John Wycliffe's Bible, *Cursor Mundi*, Chaucer) as the supreme ecclesiastical authority, denoting the four New Testament Gospels, liturgical readings, and the teachings of Christ.
- 16th to 17th Century (Reformation & Early Modern English) - William Tyndale and the King James Bible cement *gospel* as the central pillar of English vernacular faith; begins extending into idiomatic secular phrases like "gospel truth," denoting indisputable, sworn facts.
- 18th to 19th Century (Enlightenment & Victorian Era) - Broadened to encompass secular social philosophies and moral crusades, as seen in Thomas Carlyle's writings and Andrew Carnegie's *The Gospel of Wealth*.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Flourishes in African American musical culture, establishing "gospel music" as a globally recognized genre blending spirituals, blues, and jubilant vocal improvisation.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Embraced in literary criticism and philosophical theory (as in Roland Barthes and Jean Stafford) to characterize profound, transformative literary works that demand lifelong spiritual re-reading.
