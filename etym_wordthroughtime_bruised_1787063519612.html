- Old English (c. 9th–11th Century): [Smashing & Crushing to Pieces] - Expressed through Old English *brȳsan*, meaning to crush, pound, pulverize, or smash violently into fragments in mortar or battlefield combat.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century): [Convergence with Old French & Somatic Focus] - Merged with Anglo-Norman *bruisier* / *briser* ("to break, smash"). Gradually shifted from total destruction toward contusions: injuring flesh or bone through blunt trauma without breaking the skin.
- 16th to 17th Century: [Scriptural Metaphor & Emotional Wounding] - Gained profound spiritual and psychological resonance across Early Modern English (e.g., King James Bible: *"A bruised reed shall he not break"*), representing delicate, suffering, or humbled human souls.
- 18th to 19th Century: [Botanical, Culinary & Material Precision] - Standardized across agronomy, horticulture, and culinary literature to denote dropped or damaged produce, as well as indented timber and metals.
- 20th Century to Present: [Psychological & Macroeconomic Resilience] - Universally applied in modern psychology (*"bruised ego"*) and economic/political journalism to describe institutions, markets, and careers that survive major crises with noticeable impairment.
