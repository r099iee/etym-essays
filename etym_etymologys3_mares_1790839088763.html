The word mares (singular mare) is an ancient Germanic zoological term that traces back to a specialized regional Proto-Indo-European root *marko- or *márkos, meaning 'horse.' Unlike the widespread PIE root *h₁éḱwos (which yielded Latin equus, Greek hippos, Sanskrit aśva, and Old English eoh), *marko- was predominantly concentrated in the north-western Indo-European dialect areas, specifically Celtic (Old Irish marc, Welsh march) and Germanic.

In Proto-Germanic, the masculine noun *marhaz ('steed, horse') was paired with the feminine derivation *marhijō ('female horse'). While the masculine *marhaz eventually faded from everyday English (surviving only in fossilized compounds like marshal, literally 'horse-servant,' from Frankish *marhskalk), the feminine *marhijō survived with vibrant vigor as the universal designation for the female horse across all Germanic languages (Old High German meriha / German Mähre, Old Norse merr, Old Frisian merie).

  • Old English Phonetic Evolution (*Mīere* / *Mere*): In Anglo-Saxon England, Proto-Germanic *marhijō underwent regular West Germanic i-mutation (i-umlaut) and palatal diphthongization, developing into Old English mīere (West Saxon) and mere (Anglian). Anglo-Saxon legal codes (such as the laws of King Ine and Alfred the Great) frequently cataloged mīeran as valuable agricultural and breeding assets, assigning specific fines for their theft or injury.
  • Middle English Leveling: With the loss of grammatical gender and the reduction of unstressed vowels in Middle English, Old English mere / mīere leveled into the standard form mare (pronounced /maːrə/). In medieval literature and folklore, mares were celebrated for their maternal devotion and draft endurance. Geoffrey Chaucer features the word extensively in The Canterbury Tales, notably in The Reeve's Tale, where the loose horse races into the fen among the wild mares.
  • Etymological Disambiguation from 'Nightmare': A crucial etymological point is the distinction between the equine mare and the folklore demon in nightmare. The second element of nightmare traces to Old English mara (from PIE *mer- 'to rub, crush, or harm'), denoting a mythical female wraith that suffocates sleeping humans. The two words are entirely distinct etymological roots that fell together through homophony in Middle English, prompting centuries of evocative folk associations between horses and nocturnal terrors.
  • Modern Agrarian & Literary Prominence: By the 18th and 19th centuries, the plural form mares was firmly fixed in bloodstock breeding registries and literary prose across the world. In the Russian pastoral world depicted by Leo Tolstoy in Kholstomer, the question 'Have you let out the mares, eh?' captures the timeless rhythm of horse-breeding estates, where releasing the broodmares into the morning pasture constitutes the primary daily duty of herdsmen. Across four millennia, mares has stood as an unbroken symbol of equine life and matriarchal harmony.
