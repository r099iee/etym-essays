• amanuensis: Similar because an amanuensis is a literary assistant who writes at another person’s dictation, exactly Tiro’s daily work; differs because it is a role-noun of Latin origin, not a name, and it implies dictation rather than the whole range of confidential service.
  • secretary: Similar because a secretary handles correspondence and confidential papers for a principal; differs because the modern word is a broad office category, neutral about status, whereas Tiro’s position was that of an enslaved, then freed, intimate of one household.
  • scribe: Similar because a scribe is a professional writer and copyist; differs because scribes reproduce existing texts, often in workshops or scriptoria, rather than living speech dictated by a single patron.
  • copyist: Similar because copyists preserve and multiply written works, as Tiro did with Cicero’s speeches; differs because copying is purely textual and usually anonymous, with no personal attendance or editorial judgement.
  • stenographer: Similar because shorthand is Tiro’s technical legacy and the modern stenographer does what he did; differs because the modern term names a trained profession using machine or phonetic systems, not a household dependent.
  • notary: Similar because the Roman notarius was a shorthand writer who took down proceedings, the descendants of Tiro’s craft; differs because a modern notary authenticates documents and has no dictation role.
  • freedman: Similar because it is Tiro’s actual legal status, the very word used in the source; differs because it names a status category, not a person or an office, and could describe any manumitted slave.
  • tyro: Similar because it descends from the same Latin noun tiro and even shares the spelling in older English; differs because it means a beginner or novice, not the historical secretary, and carries no reference to Cicero.
