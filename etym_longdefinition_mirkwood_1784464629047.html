Mirkwood is a proper place-name in the legendarium of J.R.R. Tolkien: the vast, darkened forest of northern Middle-earth, through which the dwarves' road east passes on the way to the Lonely Mountain. As an English word it is transparent — mirk (dark, gloomy) + wood (forest) — and it was coined as the English form of the Old Norse legendary forest-name Myrkviðr, "dark forest." In the map-talk of The Hobbit, Thorin names it as a known landmark on the road east, paired with the Withered Heath where the great dragons bred: geography of dread spoken almost casually by travellers who have not yet had to cross it. Tone: toponymic, archaic-northern, ominous without melodrama; the name itself is a warning label. Bilbo's later ordeal — spiders, enchantment, pathlessness — will fill the label; here it is still a point on the map. Within the wider history of Middle-earth the name has an internal biography. The forest was long called Greenwood the Great (in the invented language of the elves, Eryn Galen); after a shadow and a necromancer came to its southern edge in the Third Age, it was renamed Mirkwood — a name meaning literally "dark forest," the change itself a piece of history in a single compound. Tolkien, a professor of Anglo-Saxon and a student of Old Norse, was deliberately using a real legendary place-name: Myrkviðr appears in Old Norse heroic poetry and sagas as the dark woodland dividing realms. Mirkwood is thus both a fictional toponym and a philological quotation. Primary senses and uses
  • In Tolkien's The Hobbit: the great dark forest east of the Misty Mountains and north of the Great River, crossed by the company on the road to the Lonely Mountain; domain of the Wood-elves, haunted by spiders and a nameless darkness.
  • In the later legendarium (The Lord of the Rings, Unfinished Tales): the same forest, renamed after the shadow fell upon it; the abode of the Necromancer at Dol Guldur; after the fall of that shadow it is renamed once more, Eryn Lasgalen, "Wood of Greenleaves."
  • As a transparent English compound: mirk (dark, gloomy — the older northern spelling of murk) + wood (forest), so that the name reads at once as "the dark wood."
  • As a philological coinage: Tolkien's Englishing of Old Norse Myrkviðr, the legendary dark forest of Germanic heroic literature.
Polysemy and secondary uses
  • As a generic term in fantasy and gaming, "Mirkwood" has been recycled as a type-name for any shadowed, perilous forest; several role-playing games and fantasy works use the name or near-variants (Murkwood, Darkwood) as atmospheric place-names.
  • As an English adjective and noun, "mirk" (or "murk") itself means darkness or gloom, and "murky" means dark, obscure, or morally murky — the word-family from which the place-name draws its element.
  • As a pattern of English place-naming, -wood compounds (Blackwood, Redwood, Greenwood, Hollywood) show that attaching a descriptive element to "wood" is an ancient and productive English habit; Mirkwood is a literary instance of that habit.
The semantic history is thus twofold: each element of the compound has a long Germanic past (darkness; forest), while the compound itself is a modern literary coinage on an Old Norse model. The name's power lies in its transparency — every English-speaking reader instantly understands "dark wood" — combined with its archaic, northern ring, which makes the plain phrase feel old and dread-full. That is exactly why the name works as geography of fear long before the reader ever enters the trees.
