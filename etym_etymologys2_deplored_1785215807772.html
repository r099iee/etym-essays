Chain for deplored:

  • Proto-Indo-European *pleu- "to flow, run, swim, float" (reconstructed; traditional etymology of plorare — see note; cognates: Sanskrit plavate "he floats," Greek pleo "I sail," Latin pluere "to rain," Old English flowan "to flow").
  • Latin plorare "to cry out, weep aloud" (attested from the 2nd century BCE; origin uncertain — possibly onomatopoeic, per de Vaan).
  • Latin deplorare "to bewail, weep bitterly over" — de- ("down, thoroughly") + plorare (attested 1st century BCE; Cicero uses it of mourning the dead).
  • Old French / Middle French deplorer "to lament, mourn" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries; also desplorer).
  • Middle English deplore "to lament, mourn" (borrowed from French, first recorded c. 1550–1560).
  • Modern English deplore "to lament, mourn; (modern) to condemn, disapprove strongly" → regular past participle deplored (used by J. S. Mill in the dedication of On Liberty, 1859: "the beloved and deplored memory").

Note: Classical Latin plorare has no certain etymology. The traditional account — followed here — derives it from PIE *pleu-, "to flow," the tears of weeping conceived as flowing out; de Vaan treats plorare as of uncertain origin, possibly onomatopoeic. The de- prefix is intensifying ("thoroughly") or directional ("down").
