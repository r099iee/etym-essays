- 13th c. (ME sege): “seat, chair, throne; rank, station” — the everyday sense of sitting and of high place; neutral, domestic and courtly.
- By c. 1300: the new military sense — “the sitting of an army before a town” — borrowed ready-made from Anglo-French siegecraft; within a century the battle sense dominates the dictionaries of usage.
- 14th–16th c.: the age of great sieges in English experience (Hundred Years War, Wars of the Roses) makes “siege” a household word of war; still spelled sege/syege.
- 16th–17th c.: modern spelling “siege” fixed; Shakespeare’s sieges (Angiers, Harfleur) and the new tactical literature anchor the word in military English.
- 18th–19th c.: figurative extension — “siege of illness”, romantic “lay siege to a heart”, and the legal-political “state of siege” (from French état de siège).
- 20th c.: “under siege” becomes a universal metaphor for beleaguered institutions; “siege mentality” enters politics and psychology.
- 21st c.: applied to modern urban warfare (Sarajevo, Mariupol) and to any person or body under sustained attack; the ancient “seat, throne” sense survives only in archaic literature (Malory’s Siege Perilous).
