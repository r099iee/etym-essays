The history of momentous is really the history of moment, because the adjective was coined in English in the seventeenth century and inherits all of the noun's accumulated senses. Latin movēre, “to move”, belongs to one of the great Indo-European verbal families; its root, reconstructed as *meuH-, “to push, set in motion”, also underlies Sanskrit mīvati “pushes” and, through other formations, a wide scatter of words for movement and removal. From movēre Latin built the noun mōmentum, whose suffix -men-tum is the normal instrument-and-result formation seen in monumentum and documentum.

Weight and the balance. The crucial development is semantic. Mōmentum did not mean “movement” in the abstract but the movement of something — in particular the swing of a balance when a weight is placed in the pan. From “the movement of the scales” it came to mean “the weight that moves them”, and hence “weight, influence, importance”. Cicero uses momentum in exactly this way when he writes of the momentum of a person's authority or of a crisis: the word denotes the pull a thing exerts on the outcome. The same image lies behind the English phrase to weigh in, and behind the modern physical sense of momentum, which was fixed only in the seventeenth century by Galileo's and Newton's mechanics.

From weight to time. A second line of development produced the temporal sense. The pointer of a balance or the marker of a water clock moves through small increments, and mōmentum came to denote the smallest division of time, the “instant”. Medieval and Old French moment carries both senses, and Middle English borrowed the word around the fourteenth century with both intact: a moment is a point in time, and a matter of moment is a matter of importance. English has never quite lost the second, though it now survives mainly in set phrases.

The adjective. Momentous is first recorded in the 1650s, formed from moment plus the Latin-derived suffix -ous, on the model of Latin adjectives in -ōsus meaning “full of”. The coinage belongs to the great seventeenth-century expansion of English abstract vocabulary, when writers naturalised hundreds of Latin adjectives to supply the needs of scientific, theological and political prose. Its early sense is exactly “of moment”: full of weight, consequence, importance. The word was not formed from momentum, but it inherits the same metaphor.

Era by era.

  • 1650–1700: a learned adjective, close to weighty and considerable, used of arguments, reasons and affairs of state. It competes with momentany and momentary, which mean “brief” and are already distinct.
  • 1700–1800: the word moves into historiography and public rhetoric. Gibbonian and parliamentary prose use it of wars, treaties and revolutions, and it acquires the public, slightly solemn tone it retains.
  • 1800–1900: the age of the historical novel and of imperial self-description makes momentous a favourite: “a momentous epoch”, “momentous consequences”, “the momentous year”. The word becomes a marker of Victorian seriousness.
  • 1900–2000: journalism and broadcasting widen its range and thin its force. Anything from a summit to a centenary becomes a “momentous occasion”, and the adjective begins to be used with self-conscious irony.
  • Contemporary: still the default elevated word for turning points, but now often a signal of hype. Corpus evidence shows it clustering around occasion, event, decision and day, with historic as its nearest rival in newspaper prose.

The momentary trap. Because English keeps both momentous (“weighty”) and momentary (“brief”), the pair illustrates how a single Latin noun could split into opposite temporal and evaluative senses. The confusion is old — eighteenth-century writers occasionally use momentous where we would write momentary — and it is worth a moment's attention, since the two words now sit at opposite ends of the scale of consequence.

Why the word fits this passage. The source is describing the years in which Cicero grew up, when the Roman Republic was convulsed by the rivalry of Marius and Sulla. Momentous does double duty there: it registers the scale of the events, and it quietly advances the book's thesis that great oratory is produced by societies in crisis. The adjective is not decoration; it is the argument in miniature.
