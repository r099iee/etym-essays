Note on linguistic morphology, folk-etymology, and substrate analysis: The name Ikṣvāku presents a celebrated case in Indian grammatical and etymological traditions. Traditional Sanskrit grammarians (following the *Uṇādi-sūtras*) and Purāṇic commentators advanced two classical explanations: (1) an association with ikṣu (इक्षु, "sugarcane") plus the primary agentive suffix -āku, suggesting a sovereign who brought sweetness, agricultural abundance, and protective juice to his subjects, or who was miraculously generated from a stalk of sugarcane; (2) a legendary folk-etymology connecting the name to Sanskrit kṣavathu ("a sneeze"), narrating in the *Padma Purāṇa* and *Viṣṇu Purāṇa* that King Manu Vaivasvata sneezed during a sacred ancestral sacrifice and produced his firstborn son Ikṣvāku. Modern historical linguists (such as Michael Witzel, F.B.J. Kuiper, and Thomas Burrow) observe that the morpheme -āku is an archaic nominal and animal-name suffix found in early Indo-Aryan (compare kṛkavāku "cock, rooster", taṇḍulāku "grain-eating bird", madhūka), suggesting that Ikṣvāku may have originated as an archaic plant-totem or agricultural title associated with the wild bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*), which bears the identical Sanskrit name ikṣvāku in classical Āyurvedic medical treatises.
