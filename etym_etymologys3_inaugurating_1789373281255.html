The participle inaugurating traces an extraordinary etymological, religious, and socio-political evolution spanning Proto-Indo-European concepts of vital growth, archaic Roman state divination through ornithomancy, Renaissance humanistic rediscoveries of classical ritual, and modern historiographical descriptions of epochal artistic breakthroughs.

  • Proto-Indo-European Roots of Divine Increase: At its deepest linguistic stratum, the word derives from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₂ewg- (or *aug-), signifying "to increase, enlarge, augment, or strengthen." This primordial root yielded an exceptionally rich family of cognates across the Indo-European languages, including Sanskrit ojas ("strength, power, vitality"), Greek auxein ("to increase, augment"), Gothic aukan ("to grow, increase"), Old English ēacian ("to increase", source of English eke), and Latin augēre ("to increase, enlarge"). From Latin augēre arose not only augment and auction (originally an increasing of bids), but also the exalted honorific augustus ("venerable, magnificent, consecrated by divine power", bestowed upon Octavian as the first Roman Emperor) and the specialized sacerdotal noun augur.
  • Archaic Roman Augury and the Sacerdotal College: In ancient Roman religion, an augur was a priest belonging to the prestigious College of Augurs (Collegium Augurum), whose sacred duty was not to predict the distant future, but to determine whether the gods—specifically Jupiter—approved or disapproved of a proposed public action, military campaign, or election. The traditional folk etymology proposed by ancient Roman grammarians derived augur from avis ("bird") + gerere ("to conduct, carry"), reflecting the augurs' practice of observing the flight, feeding habits, and cries of birds (ornithomancy or auspicia). Modern historical philologists (including Ernout-Meillet and Michiel de Vaan) establish that augur is more accurately formed from the neuter substantive *augos ("divine increase, sacred efficacy") with the agentive suffix -ur, signifying "one who channels or interprets divine increase." From this noun was derived the denominative compound verb inaugurāre (prefix in- "into, upon" + augurāre "to take auguries"), meaning literally "to consult the bird omens before initiating an undertaking" and specifically "to consecrate a sacred precinct (templum) or install a magistrate, king, or priest under divine approval." No Roman king, consul, or public assembly could act legitimately without first being properly "inaugurated" by the flight of the sacred birds.
  • Late Latin Survival and Humanist Renaissance Revival: Following the Christianization of the Roman Empire under Constantine and Theodosius in the fourth century CE, the pagan institution of augury was outlawed, and the verb inaugurāre largely faded into ecclesiastical dormancy or was condemned as demonic superstition by church fathers like Saint Augustine. However, with the Renaissance revival of Classical Latin literature and Roman constitutional jurisprudence in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, humanists resurrected inaugurare to denote the grand, solemn installation of civil monarchs, university chancellors, and civic magistrates. The word entered the English language around 1600 as the verb inaugurate (derived from the past participle stem inaugurātus), accompanied by its active present participle inaugurating. In 1606, early English legal and historical texts record the term applied to the formal coronation and investiture of kings and bishops with sacred anointing and crowns.
  • Metaphorical Secularization in the 18th and 19th Centuries: During the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution, inaugurating underwent a momentous semantic broadening, transitioning from aristocratic and liturgical ceremonies into the civic and technological spheres:
    • Civic and Architectural Dedications: By the late eighteenth century, the word was standardly employed to describe the formal opening of public structures—bridges, civic halls, universities, and canals—where the cutting of ribbons and dedicatory speeches replaced pagan prayers.
    • Historiographical Epochs: In the nineteenth century, Victorian historians such as Thomas Babington Macaulay and Thomas Carlyle utilized inaugurating metaphorically to denote watershed moments that opened new intellectual or political epochs. The storming of the Bastille was described as inaugurating democratic revolutions; James Watt's steam engine was characterized as inaugurating the industrial age; and Charles Lyell's geology was credited with inaugurating modern uniformitarian science.
  • Modern Aesthetic and Critical Usage: By the twentieth century, inaugurating became an indispensable critical term in literature, art, and philosophy. In literary criticism, as illustrated in the prompt context comparing Marcel Proust's retrospective summation of nineteenth-century realism with his pioneering modernist innovations, inaugurating denotes the creative act of carving out new aesthetic territory. Proust did not merely inherit the novelistic techniques of Balzac, Flaubert, and the Goncourts; by pioneering involuntary memory, non-linear psychological duration, and poetic subjectivity, he was inaugurating twentieth-century fiction.
