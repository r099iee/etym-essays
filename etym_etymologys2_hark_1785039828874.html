Linear derivation chain for HARK (single line of descent, well attested throughout): 1) Proto-Indo-European *kew- "to perceive, pay attention, catch sound" — reconstructed; with an s-extended variant *kous- "to hear"; Greek koein "to mark, perceive, hear" is a poetic survival of the bare perception sense. 2) Proto-Germanic *hauzjaną "to hear" — built on the root; its Old English reflex hieran / hyran "to hear, obey" gives modern English hear. 3) Proto-West Germanic *harkēn / *harkōn (also reconstructed *harkijaną) "to listen hard" — an intensive, frequentative formation on the hear-stem; exact cognates: Old Frisian harkia "to listen," Middle Dutch horken "to listen," Old High German horechōn "to listen," ancestor of German horchen "to listen, eavesdrop." 4) Old English *heorcian "to hearken, listen" — c. 1200; with the longer, suffixed twin heorcnian "to give ear, listen" attested in Old English. 5) Middle English herken / harken "to listen, give ear" — 12th-14th c.; used both as an ordinary verb of attention and as the urgent imperative of the hunting field. 6) Early Modern English hark "to listen attentively" — the short form settles into poetic, dramatic, and rhetorical use; hark back appears in 1817, first of hounds retracing a lost scent, then of returning to an earlier topic. 7) Modern English HARK "listen! (archaic, poetic, dramatic)." Note: the relationship between hark and its doublet hearken is one of shortening and re-formation on the same intensive Germanic stem; both are native English words, and no stage here is seriously debated.
