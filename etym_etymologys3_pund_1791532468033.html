The history of pund begins not in England but in the Roman marketplace. Latin pondus (genitive ponderis) meant "weight, mass, a weight", and it was the everyday noun of the Roman balance. It derives from pendere "to hang, to weigh", whose original image is a pair of scales hanging from a beam: the thing weighed is, quite literally, suspended. The deeper root is usually reconstructed as PIE *(s)pen- "to draw, stretch, spin", the same root that gives English span and spindle, because a weight hanging from a cord stretches it.

From Latin to Germanic

The word did not pass into English through the Norman Conquest or through the Church. It was borrowed much earlier, while the ancestors of the Anglo-Saxons still lived on the Continent and traded with Roman soldiers and merchants along the frontier. The borrowing is reconstructed as Proto-Germanic *pundą, and it is one of a small group of Latin commercial words (alongside win "wine", ciese "cheese" and cuppe "cup") that entered Germanic before the migrations. This is why the word looks and behaves like a native English word even though its ancestry is Roman.

  • Vowel change — Latin short /o/ regularly appears as Germanic /u/, so pondus yields *pund-.
  • Loss of inflection — the Latin nominative ending -us was dropped, and the stem was fitted to the Germanic neuter a-stem pattern.
  • Stress — Germanic fixed the accent on the first syllable, so the word became a blunt, monosyllabic item, quite unlike the trochaic Latin original.
  • Semantic stability — unusually for a loanword, the meaning did not drift: it still meant a standard weight, and then the money based on that weight.

Old English attestations

In Old English pund is thoroughly documented. It appears in the laws of the later Anglo-Saxon kings, in charters recording grants and rents, in the glosses where monks translated Latin schoolbooks, and in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Ælfric's Glossary and the Old English Heptateuch use it for Latin pondus, libra and talentum — a revealing habit, because it shows that English speakers mapped the whole Roman and biblical system of weights onto their single inherited term. The word was neuter, and its plural was the same as the singular, pund, a grammatical fact that survives in Modern English expressions such as "three pound" in informal British usage.

Metrology: Tower, mercantile and avoirdupois pounds

The medieval English pound was not one thing but several competing standards. The Tower pound, used by the royal mint, was based on twelve ounces and seems to descend from the Anglo-Saxon unit. The mercantile pound of fifteen ounces was used for goods sold by merchants. From the fourteenth century the avoirdupois pound of sixteen ounces spread through international trade and eventually became the standard for all ordinary goods, while troy weight kept the older twelve-ounce ratio for gold and silver.

  • Tower pound — c. 350 grams; used for coinage until Henry VIII's reforms.
  • Avoirdupois pound — 453.59237 grams; standardised in the nineteenth century and still used in the United States.
  • Troy pound — 373.2417 grams; twelve troy ounces, used for precious metals.
  • Metric pound — a colloquial 500 grams in several European countries; not an English legal unit.

The monetary pound

The monetary sense was already implicit in the weight sense, because coins were struck from a fixed weight of silver. In the Carolingian reform of the eighth century, one pound of silver was divided into 240 deniers, and the English system inherited that arithmetic: 240 pence to the pound, twelve pence to the shilling, twenty shillings to the pound. The symbol £ is not from pund at all but from Latin libra, the Roman pound, which shows how completely the Latin and Germanic systems were fused in medieval accounting.

Sound changes from Old to Modern English

The formal history of the word is a textbook sequence of English sound changes. Old English pund had a short /u/. In Middle English, /u/ before /nd/ underwent diphthongisation, giving spellings such as pound and pownd; the new diphthong was then caught up in the Great Vowel Shift, which raised and fronted the first element and produced the modern /aʊ/. The final -d has remained stable throughout, giving the word a compact, percussive shape that suits its role as a unit of account.

Early modern and modern afterlives

Shakespeare made the word proverbial in the "pound of flesh" of The Merchant of Venice, where the literal weight and the legal bond are deliberately confused. The Authorised Version and the Book of Common Prayer keep the monetary and weight senses side by side. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the pound sterling became the currency of a global empire, and the word acquired an almost patriotic weight: "the pound" could mean British credit, British power and British probity all at once. The twentieth century brought devaluation in 1949 and 1967, decimalisation in 1971, and the slow retreat of imperial weights in favour of metric measures, but the word survived in idiom, in engineering (psi, foot-pounds) and in the names of shops and prizes.

Why the word matters

pund is a small case study in the making of English: a Roman commercial term, borrowed on the Continent, naturalised in Old English, reshaped by native sound laws, and finally split into a weight, a currency and a set of metaphors. It also shows that borrowing is not a superficial process. When a language takes a word for a standard measure, it takes a whole way of quantifying the world.
