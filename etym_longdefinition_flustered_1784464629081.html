FLUSTERED is the past participle and adjective of the verb fluster: made nervous, agitated, or confused — typically by hurry, surprise, social pressure, or too many demands at once — so that composure and clear action momentarily fail. The state is hot, scattered, and temporary rather than deep trauma: a flustered person blushes, fumbles, loses the thread of what they were saying, and makes small mistakes. In the card's context — "Yesterday he had been too flustered to do anything of the kind" — Bilbo, after the chaos of the unexpected party and Thorin's company, had been too agitated to act with presence of mind (to read the contract, refuse properly, or keep his dignity). The tone is domestic-comic and mildly sympathetic: hobbit nerves under dwarvish invasion. Grammatically the form is an adjectival past participle — it names a condition resulting from agitation, not a completed narrative action.

Primary sense — the state of nervous confusion: The core meaning is a temporary loss of composure produced by pressure: being flustered is being put off balance by hurry, surprise, embarrassment, or an overload of simultaneous demands. The features that distinguish it are (1) it is caused by circumstance rather than temperament, (2) it is short-lived, (3) it combines cognitive confusion (forgetting, muddling) with bodily agitation (blushing, fluttering, fumbling), and (4) it carries a mildly comic or sympathetic register — we laugh at a flustered person affectionately. Unlike confused, which can be purely mental, flustered always implies visible, hot, ruffled agitation.

Secondary senses of the verb and noun: The transitive verb fluster means 'to agitate, confuse, or upset someone so that they lose composure' ("the question flustered the speaker"), and the reflexive/plain intransitive means 'to become agitated or confused'. The noun fluster names the state itself: 'a state of nervous agitation' ("she was in a fluster", "it put him in a fluster"). An archaic and dialectal sense of the verb, attested from the 15th to the 19th centuries, meant 'to excite with drink; to make slightly tipsy' — the original heat of the word was closely tied to strong ale — and this drink-sense explains the old phrase "flustered with drink". This archaic sense is historically important because it shows that the modern 'nervous agitation' meaning grew out of a 'heated, excited, disordered' meaning.

Register and distinctions: Modern flustered is conversational, colloquial, and mildly comic — a homely word of daily nerves rather than clinical or tragic language. It is distinguished from its neighbours as follows: confused is purely cognitive disorientation; agitated is broader arousal, physical and emotional, without the social or temporal cause; embarrassed is shame- or mortification-focused; panicked is a stronger, fear-driven state; frazzled stresses exhaustion and burnout rather than the momentary flutter; rattled implies being knocked off balance by a shock; ruffled suggests a slight disturbance of calm composure. Flustered uniquely blends social and temporal pressure with ruffled competence — the blushing haste of a person caught off guard.

  • Sense 1 — adjective/past participle: made nervous, agitated, or confused by hurry, surprise, or pressure (the card's sense).
  • Sense 2 — transitive verb: to agitate or confuse someone ("the question flustered her").
  • Sense 3 — intransitive/reflexive: to become agitated ("don't fluster").
  • Sense 4 — noun: a state of nervous agitation ("in a fluster").
  • Sense 5 — archaic/dialectal: to excite with drink, to make tipsy ("flustered with ale").
