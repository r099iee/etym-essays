Leaping carries the English word leap back to the Proto-Germanic strong verb *hlaupaną, 'to leap, to jump, to run', and no further with any security: the PIE stage is uncertain, because the verb has no certain cognates outside Germanic. Proposed links with Baltic and Slavic forms command no general assent, and most handbooks treat *hlaupaną as a Germanic-isolate base of possibly expressive origin, so the honest etymology begins at the Germanic doorstep and declines to guess at what stood behind it. What is certain is the shape the verb took across the family — a balance between running and leaping that each branch resolved differently — and it is precisely that balance that makes leap's history so instructive. THE GERMANIC BALANCE BETWEEN RUN AND LEAP Old Norse hlaupa meant both to leap and to run; its descendant re-entered English as lope, borrowed through Middle English lopen, so that English has two words from one ancestor, leap by inheritance and lope by later Norse loan. In the modern Scandinavian languages the verb simply means 'to run': Icelandic hlaupa, Norwegian løpe, Swedish löpa, Danish løbe. The Continental West Germanic branch shifted the same way: Old High German hloufan / loufan became German laufen, 'to walk, to run', and Old Saxon hlōpan became Dutch lopen, 'to walk, to run'. So across most of its range the Germanic 'leap' descended into the everyday word for walking and running, while English alone preserved the original leap-sense. The old class VII past, formed with reduplication (*hehlaup), is a reminder of the verb's antiquity within the Germanic strong-verb system; English preserved it as hlēop in Old English, and the modern past leapt and the participle leapt continue the inherited strong pattern (alongside the newer weak form leaped). OLD ENGLISH AND MIDDLE ENGLISH Old English inherited the verb as hlēapan, a class VII strong verb whose past was hlēop, and used it of jumping, springing, running, and even dancing, in prose and poetry alike: the heroes of Beowulf and the Guthlac legends leap and bound, and the compound oferhlēapan means 'to leap over'. The verb sat at the energetic, dignified end of Old English vocabulary, the word of warriors and dancers. In Middle English the initial h- was lost — the same drift that turned hlaford into lord and hring into ring — leaving lepen, and by early modern English the form had settled as leap. The noun leap appears early (a leap, a spring, a bound), and from it English built the fixed phrases that give the word its modern reach. THE LEAP INTO TIME AND LANGUAGE The noun and verb bred a remarkable crop of idioms and institutional terms. Leap year — a year with an extra day, which 'leaps' a weekday ahead of the calendar each year — is attested in English from late Middle English, and with it the leap-year tradition that women might propose marriage, 'leaping' the convention. Leapfrog, the children's game of vaulting over a stooping playmate, is recorded from the 16th century and has spawned the military term leapfrog for units advancing alternately past each other. A leap in the dark, 'a hazardous venture', and by leaps and bounds, 'with dramatic progress', both use the noun's image of a long, risky, or impressive bound; leap to conclusions, leap at an opportunity, leap to one's feet, and leap of the imagination all map the physical spring onto thought, desire, and sudden action. The 20th century added the quantum leap, borrowing the physicist's term for the discontinuous jump of an electron between energy states as a metaphor for any dramatic advance, and the leap second, the calendar's latest leap-idiom. THE PARTICIPLE AND ITS LITERARY LIFE The present participle leaping, formed with the -ing suffix of continuous action and attested from late Middle English as lepynge, is the form the word takes when motion is to be shown as unbroken. It names the leaping fish, the leaping flame, the leaping dancer, and the leaping salmon, and it is the form Tolkien chose for the moment of maximum agony in The Hobbit: the burning Warg is 'leaping to and fro yelping horribly', and the participle's continuity locks the frantic bounding to the shrill pain in one unending loop. It is a small masterpiece of word-choice, because the same verb that carries ballet and salmon and legend into the air here carries nothing but fire, panic, and helpless convulsion — the dignity of hlēapan's Beowulfian heroes collapsed into the to-and-fro of a burning beast, which is precisely the fall from menace to misery that the whole scene dramatises. KEY ATTESTATIONS AND TURNING POINTS
  • Proto-Germanic — *hlaupaną 'to leap, jump, run', class VII strong verb, past *hehlaup; PIE stage uncertain.
  • Old Norse — hlaupa 'to leap, to run'; re-entered English as lope (loanword); modern Scandinavian descendants mean 'to run'.
  • Old English — hlēapan 'to jump, spring, run, dance', past hlēop; used of warriors in Beowulf and Guthlac.
  • Old High German / Old Saxon — hloufan / hlōpan, whence German laufen and Dutch lopen 'to walk, to run'.
  • Middle English — lepen after loss of initial h-; the noun leap and leap year appear.
  • 16th–20th century — leapfrog, a leap in the dark, by leaps and bounds, leap of faith, quantum leap, leap second; the participle leaping fixed in continuous motion.
