Contented (adjective) describes a person or animal who feels quiet, peaceful satisfaction with what they have and where they are — free from envy, restless desire, or complaint. It is the past-participle form of the now less common verb to content ("to satisfy, to make someone happy with what they have"), and it differs subtly from the shorter adjective content: content is usually used after a verb (predicative: "she was content"), whereas contented is used both predicatively and, very commonly, before a noun (attributive: "a contented smile", "a contented cow"). In the passage from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the dwarves, having eaten well in Lake-town, are "well contented" — their bellies full, their immediate needs met — and they "quickly grew fat and strong again". The word here carries its full old-fashioned force: not merely full, but peacefully satisfied with their lot.

Primary meaning. To be contented is to be in a state of tranquil satisfaction: one's wishes, desires and expectations are all within reach of one's present condition, so there is nothing left to grasp for. The emphasis falls on inner calm rather than on outward joy. A contented person may be poor but is not disturbed by poverty; a contented reader needs no new books; a contented cat needs only the sun. This is why the word so often collocates with well ("well contented"), perfectly, and quietly — all adverbs that underline moderation and ease.

  • Attributive use (the hallmark). Unlike content, the form contented sits freely before nouns: a contented sigh, a contented smile, contented labour, contented citizens. This attributive power is exactly what makes the -ed form useful: it packages the state of mind into a quality of a person, animal, or atmosphere.
  • Predicative use. "They were well contented", "I am contented" — used after be, feel, or seem, it is interchangeable with content, though slightly weightier and more formal.
  • Applied to animals and scenes. In the nineteenth century especially, contented became the stock adjective of pastoral and rustic contentment: contented cows, contented bees, contented peasants. The word thus carries a whiff of simple, wholesome, undemanding life.
  • Derived forms. The noun contentment (and rarer contentedness) names the state; the adverb contentedly (from contented + -ly) describes the manner: "he ate contentedly". The opposite is discontented, and the noun discontent.

Secondary meanings and semantic bridges. Unlike many English words, contented has not drifted into new senses; its history is one of deepening rather than splitting. The semantic engine is Latin contentus, the past participle of continēre "to hold together, contain": a contented person is literally "contained" — their desires are held within limits, not spilling outward. From this ancient metaphor come all later uses: the satisfied diner, the philosophically resigned sage, the well-fed animal. The bridge is therefore conceptual, not accidental: contentment has always been understood as self-containment, the opposite of greed, ambition and envy. In Stoic and Christian moral tradition this is a virtue; in modern psychology it is the "satisfied life" or hedonic well-being, prized as a stable, low-arousal positive state. The only noticeable register shift is modern: contented is now a mild, warm, homely word — we rarely call a great man contented, but a child, an old pet, or a Sunday afternoon can be perfectly so.

Grammar and usage. Contented is a regular adjective (comparative more contented, superlative most contented), formed from the participle and fully adjectival: it can be modified by very, perfectly, well. It is more emphatic and literary than content and is the only one of the two used before nouns. Note the family: content (noun "the thing inside", as in "the content of a book"), content (adjective "satisfied"), content (verb "to satisfy"), and contented (adjective from the verb's participle) — four words sharing one spelling history, an instructive case of a single Latin participle branching into several English words.

In the reading context. Tolkien writes that the dwarves were "well contented" and "quickly grew fat and strong again". The adjective works on two levels: literally, their hunger and hardship were over; figuratively, it evokes the old ideal of contentment after danger and loss. It is a small, warm word doing quiet character work — restoring the dwarves to well-being before the story moves on.
