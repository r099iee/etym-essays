In this landscape description from The Hobbit, the mountain mass appears dark and drear, its brown sides patched with sunlight, while behind its shoulders the tips of the snow-peaks gleamed. The verb is chosen for a very particular quality of light: to gleam is to give out or reflect a bright but soft radiance, often at a distance, so that a small, clear point of brilliance is seen against surrounding darkness or gloom. Here the gleam belongs to the uppermost summits only — the tips — tiny dashes of luminous white high above the dark bulk, so that the whole sentence is built on contrast: the drear, brown, heavy foreground against those far, cold, brilliant heights. The nuance is one of arrested beauty breaking through dread; the gleam is seen, not felt, and its remoteness makes it the more striking, a glimpse of another, purer world beyond the shadowed mountain. The word carries a slightly literary and even archaic gravity, and this dignity suits the awe of the travellers' first sight of the ranges.

Polysemy & secondary senses. Modern English gleam has a small but rich semantic cluster built on the single notion of soft, subdued light:

  • Intransitive verb — core sense: to shine with a soft, moderate, sometimes intermittent radiance ('the lighthouse gleamed through the fog'); the light is steady enough to be located but soft enough not to dazzle.
  • Intransitive verb — reflected light: to give back light from a smooth or polished surface — polished metal, water, wet stone ('the coins gleamed in the dim light').
  • Figurative of the eyes or face: to betray an emotion by a sudden brightness of the eye ('her eyes gleamed with amusement'); the semantic bridge is the age-old association of bright eyes with alertness, joy, or mischief.
  • Archaic transitive use: 'to gleam' meaning to cast light upon something, rare in modern English but attested in older poetic diction.
  • Noun (a/the gleam): a brief or subdued flash or beam of light; the abstract noun names the light itself rather than the act ('a gleam of hope', 'a gleam in his eye').
  • Idiomatic and metaphoric extension: 'a gleam of hope', 'a gleam of humour', 'a gleam of recognition' — any faint sign of something desirable appearing against darkness or despair; the bridge is the perception of a small, remote brightness as a token of better things.
  • Technical and domain uses: in optics and colour science 'gleam' can designate a soft specular highlight without hard sparkle, distinguished from 'glint' (a single brief flash) and 'glare' (harsh light); in literary criticism the word names a particular mode of descriptive light-writing practised by the Romantics and their successors.

Register and connotation. The word is slightly literary but perfectly standard. It suggests purity, remoteness, and delicacy: a gleam is light that is clean, small, and often distant, which is why it suits distant snow-peaks, far windows, and faint hopes. Because it sits between the harshness of 'glitter' and the weakness of 'glimmer', it carries an elevated, almost Romantic tone, prized by English Romantic and Victorian poets for exactly that balance. In speech it is heard less often than 'shine' or 'glow', and its mildly bookish flavour makes it a natural choice for descriptive and adventure prose like Tolkien's.

Family boundaries. The shine-verb family of English is unusually rich, and each member occupies its own niche: glimmer is weak, unsteady, fading light on the point of going out; glitter is hard, fractured, many-pointed sparkle like metal or spangles; glisten is light lying on a wet or oily surface; glint is a single brief reflected flash; shimmer trembles, as on water or heat-haze; glow is steady warm light with no visible source; shine is the plain general word. Gleam is steadier than a glimmer, softer than a glitter, brighter and cleaner than a glisten — light that is small, pure, and seen from afar, which is why the language also speaks of a gleam of hope. On the distant snow-tips, 'gleamed' holds all of this at once: remoteness, purity, beauty breaking through dread.
