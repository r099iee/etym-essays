quickening is the present participle and verbal noun of quicken, and its meanings fan out across two great families because the adjective quick itself split in two: the older sense “living, alive” (as in “the quick and the dead”, “cut to the quick”) and the newer, everyday sense “fast, rapid”. Quickening therefore means both “being made alive / coming to life” and “growing more rapid”, and the two senses have lived side by side for centuries.

Family 1 — coming to life, being made alive. The historically primary sense: the action of giving or receiving life; revival, animation, enlivenment. In religious diction it is prominent: St. Paul's “the quickening Spirit” (1 Corinthians 15:45) is the Spirit that makes alive, and hymns and prayers speak of the “quickening of faith” or of the heart. In nature description it is the surge of life in spring — the quickening of the world after frost exactly as in the source passage, where a breeze “gently moved / With quickening virtue” (Wordsworth, The Prelude), i.e. with life-giving, vivifying power.

Family 1, special sense — obstetrics and law. From the 16th century “quickening” denotes the first perceptible movements of the fetus in the womb, felt by the mother roughly between the 16th and 20th weeks of pregnancy. In early modern theology and medicine this was the moment the child was held to be alive (variously, to receive its soul), and it carried legal weight: early modern common law and then statute — the English Lord Ellenborough's Act of 1803 and many 19th-century U.S. state laws — distinguished the offence of abortion before and after quickening. The term is therefore a historical milepost in the development of abortion law and is still glossed in medical and legal history.

Family 2 — acceleration, growing more rapid. The modern primary sense: the process of becoming faster or more active — “the quickening of the market”, “a quickening of pace”, “the quickening pulse of the city”. Here quick carries its modern meaning “fast”, and the transfer is a bare synecdoche: the speed of life is the sign of its intensity. The bridge between the two families is the ancient association of life with motion, so that “alive” overflowed into “vigorous, energetic, rapid” — the same bridge visible in “lively”, “animated”, and “liveliness = speed”.

Figurative and other uses. (a) Enlivening of mind, spirit, or affections: “the quickening of the heart at the sound of her voice”; (b) the quickening of the pulse — excitement, arousal, alarm; (c) 19th-century medical writing still used “the quickening” absolutely for fetal movement; (d) cultural proper use: “the Quickening” in fantasy fiction (e.g. the Highlander franchise's mystical transfer of life-force) and church/named services of renewal. Note also the adjective use — “a quickening moment”, “quickening virtue” — and the fact that in modern speech the word is normally the noun or gerund of acceleration unless context points to religion, poetry, or medical history.

  • Making alive: animation, revival, enlivening of body, nature, or spirit.
  • Acceleration: growing faster or more active (trade, pace, tempo, pulse).
  • Obstetric/historical: first felt fetal movements; pre/post-quickening legal line of early abortion law.
  • Figurative: any invigorating or accelerating force — mental, emotional, economic, natural.
  • Register: neutral in the acceleration sense; literary/religious in the life-giving sense.
