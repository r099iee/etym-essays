Stout is one of English's most warmly approving adjectives of substance. In its dominant modern sense it describes a person — and by extension animals, objects, and even abstractions — that is thick, solid, and strongly built: broad rather than tall, weighty rather than slight, giving an impression of durable power that borders on corpulence without ever implying weakness.

In the source sentence — Old Maggot is really a stout fellow, if you leave his mushrooms alone — Tolkien reaches for the older, fuller sense of the word, in which physical solidity and moral backbone are one thing. Maggot is thickset and formidable, yes, but above all he is brave, blunt, and not to be trifled with: a stout fellow is someone who will stand his ground, thresh uninvited guests with his stick, stare down a Black Rider at his own door, and then offer beer to friends. The sentence is half warning and half tribute, and stout carries both halves at once.

Core meaning in detail: Applied to people, stout sits on the kinder side of the vocabulary of weight. Where fat judges and obese diagnoses, stout almost compliments: a stout gentleman is broad-chested and thick-limbed, the build of farmers, sailors, and sergeants-major. Clothiers long sold stout sizes and stoutwear as the dignified term for larger men's and women's clothing. Applied to things, it means strongly made and dependable — stout boots, a stout rope, stout walls, a stout defence — and applied to actions it means vigorous and unyielding: stout resistance, stout denial, a stout performance.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Thick-bodied, corpulent, stocky (primary modern sense, of people): broad and heavily built, as in a stout old farmer. This is the sense dictionaries list first today, though it was a late arrival historically, growing out of the older sense of strength.
  • Strong, sturdy, robust (of objects and structures): stout shoes, stout timber, a stout staff. The bridge is the body metaphor: a shoe or wall described as stout is imagined as having the thick limbs and broad chest of a strong man.
  • Brave, resolute, valiant (older sense, still literary — Tolkien's sense): a stout fellow, stout hearts, stout resistance against the enemy. This was the word's FIRST English meaning, borrowed from French chivalry: stoutness of body and stoutness of heart were the same virtue in a knight.
  • Vigorous, forceful, uncompromising (of actions and denials): a stout denial, stout opposition, stout efforts. The bridge runs from brave warriors to brave statements: a denial that refuses to budge is as immovable as a thickset man with his feet planted.
  • Strong dark beer (noun, short for stout porter, from the 1700s): Guinness and its kin — a pint of stout. The bridge is strength of brew: porters asked for the strongest vat were served the stout (strong) porter, and the adjective quietly became a noun.
  • Obstinate, stubbornly unyielding (shade of the brave sense): stout in his prejudices, a stout refusal. The bridge is the short step from admirable firmness to irritating inflexibility — the same oak is either sturdy or knotty depending on whether you are sheltering under it or trying to move it.

Register and connotation: stout is warm, old-fashioned, and faintly admiring — the vocabulary of affectionate obituaries, inn signs, and Tolkien himself. To call boots stout is practical praise; to call a man stout is to say he can be leaned on; to call resistance stout is to honour it. Only the drink sense is neutral, ordered daily in pubs without a thought for knights or farmers. The word has no real sting: even applied to weight, it remains the kindest word in that drawer, which is why euphemism adopted it for clothing sizes and why it survives there still.
