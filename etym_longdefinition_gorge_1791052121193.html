gorge most commonly means to eat greedily and in large quantities, often to the point of discomfort, and as a noun means the throat or gullet, and by extension a deep narrow ravine or passage between mountains.

As a verb, to gorge (especially reflexive to gorge oneself / to gorge on something) denotes cramming the throat, devouring food with animal excess, without restraint or manners. It implies loss of measured control, eating past satiety. As in the source — Savages gorge themselves or fast — it contrasts binge with abstinence, excess with foresight. Modern use retains this disapproval: gorging is not feasting (joyful) or dining (civilised) but stuffing. By metaphor it extends to any greedy consumption: to gorge on television, on praise, on novels.

As a noun, gorge preserves older senses still active:

  • 1. Throat, gullet, crop: Now literary or technical. To stick in one's gorge means to rankle, to be hard to swallow morally. To make one's gorge rise means to nauseate. Falconry: a hawk's gorge is its cropful. This is the etymological core.
  • 2. Act of gorging / surfeit: A gorge can mean a gluttonous meal. Less common today but seen in gorge-purge cycles in eating-disorder literature.
  • 3. Deep narrow valley, ravine: In physical geography, a gorge is a steep-sided narrow valley cut by a river, narrower and rockier than a valley, often with a stream at the bottom. Examples: Olduvai Gorge, Columbia River Gorge. This sense dominates in travel and geology.
  • 4. Narrow passage / throat-like constriction: In fortification, the gorge of a bastion is the open rear entrance; in architecture, the gorge or gorgerin is the neck of a column; in anatomy obsolete for pharynx. All preserve throat-image.
  • 5. Obstruction, mass: An ice gorge, log gorge: a mass blocking a narrow passage. Here the passage sense meets the stuffing sense — a throat choked full.

Connotations and register: The verb is informal-neutral to slightly condemnatory; the ravine noun is neutral technical; the throat noun is poetic/archaic. To gorge suggests animality, urgency, lack of refinement, and subsequent sickness or shame. It differs from devour (which stresses speed and violence but can be admiring), from gobble (which stresses sound and haste, more comic), from feast (positive abundance). In psychology, gorge appears in binge-eating discourse. In ecology, gorge-feeding describes predators that eat hugely at a kill then fast, exactly the savage pattern described in the context.

Polysemy bridge: All senses flow from THROAT as narrow devouring tube. Throat-full → act of filling it → landscape shaped like a throat → blockage in such a passage. The moral judgement (greedy) comes from observing the throat overfilled. Gorgeous, surprisingly, shares the root via Old French gorge (bosom, fine clothing covering bosom) → splendid dress → beautiful, showing how the same neck/chest word branched positively elsewhere.
