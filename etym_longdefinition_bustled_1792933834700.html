Bustled is the past tense of bustle: to hurry about with a show of busy energy, all flutter, clatter, and importance. It is hurrying made audible and visible — skirts swishing, doors opening and shutting, voices calling down passages — motion that announces itself and expects to be noticed. A person who bustles is never merely moving; they are performing busyness, and the room rearranges itself around them.

In the source sentence Farmer Maggot bustled in and out — fetching beer, calling to his wife, eyeing three strange hobbits at his table while dogs, lanterns, and mushrooms crowd the scene. Tolkien's verb does the whole staging in a word: no calm entrance, no stately hosting, but the short-legged Farmer's energetic shuttling between kitchen and parlour, hospitality as happy commotion. The bustle tells us Maggot's house is alive, his welcome genuine, and his attention divided three ways at once — exactly the kindly chaos a frightened traveller hopes to find.

Core meaning in detail: To bustle is to move with energetic, noisy fussiness — brisk short journeys, sharp turns, hands full, mind fuller. It differs from mere hurrying in its sound and display: bustling is public, rustling, interruptive. One bustles about (general activity), bustles in and out (repeated entries, as Maggot does), or bustles with something (a station bustling with travellers). The past tense bustled freezes one such flurry: she bustled him into the taxi — fuss as a means of locomotion, the guest propelled by sheer hospitality.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hurried about busily and noisily (primary sense, from the 1500s — Tolkien's sense): Maggot bustled in and out, the landlady bustled about with towels. The living core — domestic energy made audible — from which all the rest descends.
  • Fussed over someone; hurried them along (transitive): they bustled the children off to bed, nurses bustled him into a wheelchair. The bridge turns the bustler's own motion outward: the energy spills onto others, who are swept, settled, and served before they can object.
  • Was full of lively activity (of places): the market bustled, the harbour bustled with sailors. The bridge generalises the person into the place: a square where a hundred people bustle is itself said to bustle, the crowd becoming one great bustling organism.
  • Made a fuss; showed officious importance: committees bustled for a week and achieved nothing. The bridge shades energy into emptiness: bustle without result is the universal emblem of self-important inefficiency, and the verb carries the satire ready-made.
  • A padded frame worn to puff out a skirt (noun bustle, Victorian fashion, 1780s): crinolines and bustles. The bridge is disputed but felt: the projecting pad juts and flounces behind its wearer, rustling and demanding room — dress that bustles even when its wearer stands still. Scholars note this noun may be a separate word of unknown origin that merely collided with the verb.
  • A noisy commotion or flurry (noun): the bustle of the city, the hustle and bustle of the holidays. The bridge nominalises the verb's sound-world: the abstract noise of many people bustling at once, now the fixed partner of hustle in the great modern doublet.

Register and connotation: bustled is warm and slightly comic — the vocabulary of kindly landladies, efficient nurses, Christmas kitchens, and Tolkien farmers. It praises energy while gently mocking fuss: to say someone bustled is to like them and to smile at them simultaneously. Only officious committees hear the edge of the word. Paired with hustle, it names the beloved noise of human activity itself — the sound of a world getting things done, loudly.
