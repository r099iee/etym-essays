- Late 14th c. — Anglo-Norman legal tenure & scholastic disputation: entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman French from Latin pertinens, initially functioning as a technical legal term designating lands, privileges, and rights that appertained or belonged to an estate (preserved in Scottish land law as 'parts and pertinents').
- 15th–16th c. — Scholastic dialectic & theological discourse: expanded into philosophical and ecclesiastical debate to describe biblical citations, patristic authorities, and logical arguments that bore directly upon a doctrinal question.
- 17th c. — Asymmetric divergence from *impertinent*: as its negative counterpart impertinent began its historical semantic shift toward insolence, rudeness, and social impudence, pertinent remained purely anchored in intellectual, logical, and judicial relevance.
- 18th–19th c. — Victorian jurisprudence, parliamentary debate & forensic inquiry: firmly established in common law courts and parliamentary procedure as the definitive evidentiary standard for questions and testimony bearing directly upon the issue at trial.
- 20th–21st c. — Academic literature, clinical diagnostics & research methodology: standard vocabulary across scholarly citations ('pertinent studies') and medical diagnostic practice (identifying 'pertinent positives' and 'pertinent negatives' in clinical diagnosis).
