The ancestry of preoccupation is a chain of seizing. Latin capere, “to take, seize”, compounds with ob- to give occupāre, “to seize, take possession of” — the verb that gives English occupy, occupant and occupation. Add the prefix prae-, “before”, and the seizure happens first: praeoccupāre is to occupy the ground ahead of someone else, and in rhetorical theory praeoccupātiō is the figure of meeting an objection before it is made. The military and forensic image is present at the very beginning; nothing in the Latin word yet suggests daydreaming.

The noun reaches English in the sixteenth century, first as a literal and legal-sounding term for prior possession or prior occupation. Its modern life begins when the metaphor is applied to attention.

  • Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE): praeoccupāre “seize beforehand”; the noun praeoccupātiō belongs to rhetoric and to the language of prior claim.
  • French (16th–17th c.): préoccuper and préoccupation acquire the sense of anxious concern, of being claimed by a worry before one can attend to anything else.
  • English (1550s): “prior occupation”; through the seventeenth century the word also means prepossession or bias, a mind already taken by an opinion.
  • English (19th c.): the psychological sense settles: the state of being absorbed, abstracted, with the attention elsewhere.

Phonology and form. The word keeps the Latin prefix in its French shape, pré- becoming English pre-, and takes the ordinary English suffix -ation on the Latin stem. Pronunciation is /ˌpriː.ɒk.jəˈpeɪ.ʃən/, with secondary stress on the first syllable and the main stress on the penult; the vowel of oc- is reduced in running speech. The adjective preoccupied is far commoner than the noun in speech, and its meaning has narrowed to “distracted, lost in thought”.

Semantic drift. Three shifts deserve notice. First, from space to mind: ground seized before an enemy becomes attention claimed before other claims. Second, from act to state: the event of being occupied beforehand becomes the enduring condition of being engrossed. Third, from state to object: by metonymy, the preoccupation becomes the thing that preoccupies — “his preoccupation with money” can mean either the habit or its object, and the source uses this object-like sense of a whole culture’s fixation on Cicero.

Socio-cultural context. The word flourishes in periods that worry about attention. Puritan moralists used it for the mind possessed by worldly cares; Victorian psychologists used it for the withdrawal of attention from the senses; twentieth-century psychiatry used it for intrusive concerns in anxiety and obsessive states. In each case the underlying image is the same: the mind is a place, and something has got there first.

Literary and public life. The word is a staple of the novel of consciousness, where a character’s preoccupation is revealed by what she fails to notice, and of political commentary, where “the preoccupations of the age” names the themes a society cannot stop discussing. It is rarely a term of praise: even intellectual preoccupation implies a narrowing of vision, a subject that has crowded out the rest of the world.

Modern connotation. Today the noun is formal, slightly diagnostic and often mildly critical. It sits between interest and obsession: stronger and more exclusive than the first, less pathological than the second. To call Cicero a preoccupation of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance is to describe a long, collective absorption — admiring, imitative, sometimes pedantic — that shaped what Europeans thought eloquence and education were for.
