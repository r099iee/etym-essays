Proclamation is a noun meaning an official, formal, public announcement or declaration, particularly one issued by a head of state, a government, a municipality, or some other constituted authority and intended to be made known to the whole community. It is the noun of the verb to proclaim ("to make known publicly, to declare with authority"), and its core sense can be caught in two pictures: the royal herald crying the king's words in the market square, and the modern government announcing a public holiday or a state of emergency. In law and government the proclamation is a weight-bearing word — it can announce the accession of a monarch, the opening of parliament, an amnesty, a period of martial law, or a national day of mourning — and in its strongest historical forms it carried legal force.

Primary sense: the official declaration. In legal and administrative English a proclamation is a formal public statement made by an authority with that authority's weight behind it. In the British tradition the royal proclamation is a specific instrument of state: the accession of a new sovereign is announced by royal proclamation, and sheriffs and town criers "cried" proclamations through the streets so that all whom they might concern should have notice of the law announced. In the United States, a presidential proclamation is a formal document by which the president declares facts or occasions, and the Emancipation Proclamation of 1 January 1863 is the most famous American proclamation ever issued; corporations, universities, clubs, and societies issue proclamations of their own to mark anniversaries and honour individuals. What unites all these uses is publicity and formality: a proclamation is never private, never casual, and never unsigned.

Secondary senses and their semantic bridge. Traced from its Latin root (proclamare, "to cry out forth", from clamare, "to shout"), the word passes step by step from the sound of shouting to the document of state. The modern uses spread along that bridge:

  • The act itself — the proclamation of the independence of the thirteen colonies is the act of making a decision public, whether or not a single sheet of paper exists.
  • The extended political declaration — revolutionary and national proclamations (the Proclamation of the Irish Republic, Easter 1916) invest the word with the authority of a people speaking, not of a crown alone.
  • The solemn institutional statement — the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) is "proclaimed" in its own opening words ("THE GENERAL ASSEMBLY proclaims this Universal Declaration..."), showing how international bodies borrowed the term.
  • The rhetorical and playful use — in ordinary conversation "I hereby issue a proclamation" is conscious hyperbole: the word lends comic grandeur to small announcements (no more meetings, dessert before dinner), and the humour works precisely because the real proclamation is so solemn.

Boundaries and relatives. A proclamation differs from a law or decree in that it announces rather than primarily commands, though royal proclamations could carry the force of statute — Henry VIII's statute of 1539 briefly made them law, and English judges in the Case of Proclamations (1610) ruled that the king could not create new offences by proclamation alone. It differs from an edict (a ruler's command, colder and more legislative), from a declaration (broader: any formal statement, official or personal), from an announcement (neutral and small), from a manifesto (a party's or movement's programmatic statement, appealing to the public rather than commanding it), and from a bulletin or communiqué (news-like, without ceremony). The register is formal and public: one issues a proclamation; one does not whisper one. In Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince, the Mayor — finding a dead swallow at the foot of the ruined statue — declares that "we must really issue a proclamation that birds are not to be allowed to die here", and the Town Clerk dutifully makes a note of the suggestion: the comic mismatch between the grand word and the absurd decree is exactly what the word's dignity buys the joke.
