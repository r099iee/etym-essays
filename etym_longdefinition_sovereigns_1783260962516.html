In Macaulay's sentence "the long struggle between our __________ and their parliaments," the plural noun sovereigns designates the Stuart kings — the rulers of England from James I to James II — considered as the supreme authorities of the realm in their contest with the legislature. The "long struggle" is the constitutional conflict of the seventeenth century, from the Petition of Right and the Civil War to the Exclusion Crisis and finally the Revolution of 1688, over whether final authority lay with the crown or with parliament. Sovereigns is the word chosen because it names them not by rank (kings, queens) but by the claim they advanced: each Stuart monarch asserted himself sovereign, a supreme ruler whose prerogative stood above the law, and it is precisely that claim that Parliament resisted. The tone is constitutional, formal, and even-handed — Macaulay weighs "our sovereigns and their parliaments" in one balance — and the plural gathers the whole dynasty into a single long contest that his revolution was to terminate.

At its core, sovereign (singular) is a noun and adjective denoting supreme authority: a monarch, king, or queen as the supreme ruler of a state, or the quality of being supreme and above all others. Its root, Latin super "above," tells the whole story: the sovereign is the one "above" everyone else, the authority that acknowledges no higher power. In the plural, sovereigns simply collects such rulers — the kings and queens who hold supreme power.

  • Monarchical sense (the primary meaning): a king, queen, or other supreme ruler — "the sovereign," "our sovereign lord the King," "a reigning sovereign." This is the sense of Macaulay's plural "our sovereigns."
  • Political and legal sense: the supreme authority in a state, the person or body in whom final power resides; "sovereign power," "sovereign authority," "sovereign will," and the principle of sovereign immunity (the state cannot be sued without its consent).
  • Constitutional theory (17th c. and after): the notion of sovereignty detached from persons — the "sovereignty of Parliament," the "sovereignty of the people," the sovereign state as a nation acknowledging no higher power.
  • Numismatic sense: the English gold coin of twenty shillings, first struck under Henry VII in 1489, depicting the monarch enthroned, which long served as the great standard coin of the British Empire.
  • Adjectival and figurative senses: "supreme, utmost" — "a sovereign remedy" (an infallible cure), "with sovereign contempt," "sovereign excellence"; also "possessing independent authority" as in "sovereign state."

The word must be distinguished from its neighbours in the vocabulary of rule. A monarch is etymologically "one who rules alone" (Greek mónos "alone" + árchein "to rule") and stresses the singleness of supreme rule, without necessarily the territorial or legal nuance of sovereignty. A king or queen is a specific rank of hereditary ruler; an emperor rules an empire and claims a higher, imperial dignity. Ruler is the plain, generic term for anyone who governs, from a chief to a president, and carries no majesty. Potentate is a dignified and slightly exotic word for a powerful ruler, often foreign ("the Eastern potentates"), with a touch of oriental colour. Sovereign is the precise legal and political term: it denotes the supreme authority in a state, the person or body in whom final power resides — and in modern usage it has detached from persons entirely, so that a sovereign state is one that acknowledges no higher power. It is this absoluteness, the claim to stand above every other authority, that distinguishes sovereign from all its synonyms and makes it the right word for the Stuart pretension.

Collocationally the noun is royal and constitutional: the sovereign, sovereigns and parliaments, a reigning sovereign, sovereign power, sovereign authority, sovereign will, sovereign state, sovereign immunity (the legal principle that the state cannot be sued without its consent), and the liturgical our sovereign lord the King. There is also a famous monetary sense: the sovereign is the English gold coin of twenty shillings, first struck under Henry VII in 1489 and depicting the monarch enthroned — a "sovereign" of gold that long served as the great standard coin of the British Empire. The register of the word is formal, legal, and historical; its plural in constitutional prose like Macaulay's carries the full weight of a national argument about where final authority truly lay.

Polysemy & Modern Usage. In numismatics and statecraft, sovereign refers to British 22-carat gold coins (£1) or to independent autonomous nations. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: Vulgar Latin *superānus "supreme" (from super "above") via Old French soverain expanded from monarchic rulers (the one "above" the realm) to nation-state autonomy (the state acknowledging no power "above" it) and to gold currency minted with royal portraits — the image of the ruler standing above all others projected onto coin and commonwealth alike.
