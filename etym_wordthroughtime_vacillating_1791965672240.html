- 1590s — vacillate appears in English as a verb of physical unsteadiness, “to sway, totter”; the participle is first used of things that visibly rock or stagger.
- 17th c. — the participle becomes a character adjective: “a vacillating mind”, “a vacillating purpose”. The metaphor of the body that cannot stand straight hardens into a moral verdict on irresolution.
- 18th c. — political writers adopt it for ministries and alliances that reverse course; the word acquires its enduring association with statecraft and its contemptuous tone.
- 19th c. — a stock term in parliamentary abuse and in biographies of the indecisive; vacillation becomes a standard item in the vocabulary of character criticism.
- 20th–21st c. — confined largely to formal commentary, journalism and biography; indecisive is the neutral everyday substitute, and vacillating survives as the more damning, slightly old-fashioned alternative.
