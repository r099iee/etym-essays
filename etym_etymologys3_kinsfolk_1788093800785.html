PART I — KIN, FROM PIE TO ENGLISH.

The story of kin begins with the Indo-European root *ǵenh₁-, "to give birth, beget", one of the best-attested roots in the family: Sanskrit jánati "gives birth", Greek gígnomai "I am born, become", Latin gignō "beget" with its derivative genus "birth, stock, kind, race". The Germanic branch took a specialist collective form, Proto-Germanic *kunją, "family, clan, lineage" — the human group produced by one begetting line — and from it Old English cynn, attested from the earliest records with the senses "family, race, kind, sort, nature, sex, rank". In Beowulf and the homilies cynn is at once kin-group and category; the word articulates the deepest unit of Anglo-Saxon society, the kin-based clan that underpinned feud, wergild, inheritance and oath-loyalty.

  • Kinship and kingship. Old English cyning "king" is in origin "scion of the kin" (*kun-ing-az, "descendant of the noble lineage"): kingship was conceived as kinship writ large, the king as the representative head of the people's family line. Compounds such as cynn-rǣden "kin-arrangement" and cynn-ēadig "blessed in kin" show the word's social weight.
  • The drift to "kind". Because cynn also meant "sort, nature", the uninflected noun slid into an adjective — first "natural, of one's own kind" (13th c.), then, by the 14th century, "well-disposed, benevolent": behaving as kin behave. Modern "kind" and "kindness" are therefore direct semantic descendants of the kinship noun, and the same breeding-ground produced "kindred" (Middle English kynrede, kin + -rede "condition, state") and "akin".
  • Sound changes. PIE *ǵenh₁- → Germanic *kunja- (o-grade) → Old English cyn(n) /kyn(n)/, spelled with y. Middle English shortened the vowel and unrounded [y] to [ɪ] in the south-east ("kyn" → "kin"), the same change that produced "kith" from cȳþþu and "kill" from cyllan; the modern pronunciation /kɪn/ is the end of that chain.

PART II — FOLK, FROM HOST TO PEOPLE.

Proto-Germanic *fulką meant "people, host" and is attested across the whole branch: Old English folc, Old Saxon folk, Old High German folc, Old Norse fólk. Its ultimate origin is genuinely debated: the traditional derivation links it to the PIE root *pleh₁- "to fill" — a folk as "the full complement, the complete body of fighting men" — while modern handbooks mark the etymology uncertain. No stage beyond *fulką can be added without guessing, and none is added here; the honest chain stops where the record stops.

  • The Old English word. Folc is among the highest-frequency words in the Old English corpus: Beowulf's folc-cyning "people-king", folc-scearu "people's portion", folc-bealu "national calamity"; Alfred's prose uses folc for the nation and its army alike. The Old Norse cognate fólk leans toward "host, war-band" — the war-band as the filled, complete company — and the English word carries the same dual profile: "people, nation" and "troop, army" side by side.
  • The medieval narrowing. By the 14th century "folk" increasingly meant the common people as distinct from lords and clergy — the folk versus the gentry — a shift tracked in Piers Plowman and the popular ballads; the compound pattern "old folk", "country folk", "townsfolk", "kinsfolk" grows out of this collective habit of mind, in which a group is named by its bond to a person or place.
  • The modern re-inflation. The 19th-century antiquarian movement, following the German Volk tradition of the Brothers Grimm, recharged "folk" as an ethnological modifier — folk-tale, folk-song, folk-lore (the word "folklore" was coined by W. J. Thoms in 1846) — and American English made the plural intimate: "my folks" = my parents and family (19th c.), the same inclusiveness that produced the southern doublet "kinfolk".

PART III — THE COMPOUND AND ITS FATE.

The two elements welded together in Middle English as the genitive compound kynnesfolk, "the folk of the kin", recorded from the late 14th century; the modern spelling "kinsfolk" (16th c.) preserves the -s-. For three centuries it was the ordinary dignified word for relatives — biblical translation, chivalric romance, funeral and wedding prose — and then, as the 19th century gave the plain words "family" and "relatives" the field, it retreated into literature, dialect and solemn address. Tolkien's "O my kinsfolk!" (The Hobbit, 1937) is the perfect terminus: a word that began as everyday Anglo-Saxon kinship terminology ends as the formal battle-cry of a legendary king, its archaic colour a deliberately preserved literary resource — a king calling his whole people to him by the oldest word for blood and belonging that English owns.
