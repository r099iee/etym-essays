ADVENT (noun) — at its plainest core Advent means the arrival or coming of a notable person, event, era, or institution, especially one long anticipated and of great significance. The word carries an aura of importance and destiny: what "comes" in an advent is not a mere arrival but a momentous arrival that changes the state of things. Its original and still primary home is the Christian season of Advent, the four weeks of preparation before Christmas commemorating the coming of Christ; from that liturgical sense the word has spread to any era-changing arrival — the advent of printing, of railways, of television, of the internet, of a reformer in the legislature. The capital-letter season and the lower-case metaphor are two halves of one word, and the metaphor never quite forgets the season. The sense in the Mill context In this sentence the Liberal leader and future Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone is quoted as having said of John Stuart Mill's brief parliamentary career (1865–1868): "For the sake of the House of Commons at large, I rejoiced in his advent and deplored his disappearance. He did us all good." The word is chosen with a politician's precision. Mill's entry into Parliament is elevated from a routine election to a landmark event — the arrival of a man whose presence raised the moral and intellectual tone of the whole assembly. The pairing of "advent" with "disappearance" implicitly casts his leaving as a kind of eclipse or loss, and the contrast makes the word's grandeur audible: what Gladstone welcomed was not the arrival of a new member but the coming of a public good. Primary senses
  • The Christian season (capitalized, Advent): the liturgical season of preparation for Christmas, beginning on the Sunday nearest 30 November and covering the four Sundays before Christmas; the season of waiting and anticipation for the coming (advent) of Christ.
  • The coming of Christ (theological): the Incarnation, and by extension the expected Second Coming; the word's oldest English senses are both theological.
  • A momentous arrival (general, lower-case): the coming into being, use, or prominence of a person, movement, invention, or era — "the advent of the steam engine," "the advent of democracy," "the advent of the digital age."
  • The arrival of a public figure: a grand or solemn coming — of a king, a reformer, a new leader — the sense Gladstone uses of Mill.
Polysemy: secondary, technical, and metaphoric senses
  • Adventism and Adventist: from the same word come Adventism, the 19th-century millenarian movement awaiting the imminent Second Coming, and Adventist, its follower (as in Seventh-day Adventists) — the theological sense hardened into the name of a church.
  • Modern consumer culture: the Advent calendar (counting the days of the season, now often holding chocolate or gifts) and the Advent wreath have made the liturgical word familiar to millions who never enter a church; "advent" is also used in marketing for new product seasons and launches.
  • Technical and scientific prose: "the advent of X" is a standard formula in history of technology and science for the introduction of an innovation — the advent of printing, of vaccination, of the computer — where the word implies that the innovation changed an era.
  • Related but distinct words (cognates, not synonyms): adventitious ("coming from outside, accidental," from Latin adventicius) and adventure (from Latin advenīre via "that which is to come, a chance event") share the same Latin verb venīre but have diverged into different meanings; the family of "coming" also includes convene, convention, event (literally "a coming out"), and avenue (a way of coming).
  • Metaphoric use: by extension, the advent of anything long awaited and transformative — of spring, of peace, of an age — where the word lends the coming the aura of destiny.
How to use the word precisely Arrival is the neutral, everyday word for reaching a place or stage and carries no hint of grandeur; a train arrives, a parcel arrives. Coming is vaguer and more intimate, suited to persons one expects, while advent is grand and impersonal, reserved for epochs, movements, inventions, and public figures. Emergence stresses gradual appearance from concealment; onset names the beginning of something unwelcome or neutral (the onset of winter, of illness); inception is a cold, technical word for the start of an enterprise; debut implies performance and public exposure. Advent alone implies a destined or providential quality — the word one uses of a reformer entering the legislature, of printing transforming literacy, of a new age arriving. That is exactly its force in Gladstone's tribute, where Mill's arrival is treated as something like the coming of a season.
