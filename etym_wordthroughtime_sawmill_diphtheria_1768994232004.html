- Sawmill, a plain compound of 'saw' and 'mill,' enters English as water- and horse-powered machinery for sawing timber, the earliest mills turning logs into boards for shipwrights and builders.
- Through the 17th–18th c. sawmills spread with the timber trades and the colonies, and the word becomes the name of an industry rather than a single machine.
- In the 19th-century frontier economy the sawmill is the town's engine — the building where forest becomes lumber, the centre of 'sawmill towns,' 'sawmill hands' and raw industrial labour.
- In modern usage the word keeps its matter-of-fact register, named by its process (the sawing) rather than its product, and it is remembered as the vocabulary of work itself.
- Diphtheria arrives later and differently: coined in the 1850s by Bretonneau from the Greek for 'leather,' after the grey leathery membrane that forms in the throat, it names the most dreaded child-killer of the age.
- Through the late 19th–20th c. the word passes from terror (the pesthouse, the quarantine, the sudden choking of children) to hope, as antitoxin and then the vaccine make the disease rare — so that in modern texts 'diphtheria' is largely a historical word, kept alive by the old fear.
