PIE *seh₂k- "to sanctify, to make solemnly binding; the sacred" (reconstructed; Pokorny *sāk-; de Vaan: sacer < *sak-ro-) → Latin sacer, sacra, sacrum "sacred, holy, consecrated; also accursed" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; the word names both poles of the ancient taboo) → Latin sacrāre "to make sacred, to consecrate, to dedicate" (attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Latin dēsecrāre (dē- "away, down, reversal" + sacrāre) "to unhallow, to make profane, to undo a consecration" (attested in post-classical Latin) → Latin dēsecrātiō, dēsecrātiōnis "a making unholy, profanation" (late and ecclesiastical Latin) → English DESECRATION (17th c., borrowed through ecclesiastical Latin and learned French; the verb desecrate follows shortly after) Note (debated): The two-sidedness of sacer — "holy" and "accursed" — is a well-attested feature of the Roman taboo (the homo sacer was a criminal given over to the gods for destruction). The positive counterpart consecration (Latin cōnsecrātiō) descends from the same root with the prefix con-. A debated possible sister is Hittite šāklāi- "custom, rite," but the connection is not universally accepted.
