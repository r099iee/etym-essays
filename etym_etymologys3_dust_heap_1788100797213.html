Introduction. Dust-heap is a compound of two of the oldest, plainest words in English, and its history is that of two ancient senses colliding in the nineteenth century. First the two words must be traced separately — dust from Old English dūst, the powder of the earth and of the mortal body; heap from Old English hēap, the pile and the crowd — and then their compound, which rose with the dust-yards of London and fell, in the twentieth century, into the dust-heap of history.

1. Dust: Old English dūst and its debated pedigree

Old English dūst meant "dust, fine dry particles", and also "earth, ground" and "the mortal body", with the old liturgy of Genesis 3:19 behind it — "dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" — so that from the earliest records dust carries both the physical powder and the funereal sense of humanity returned to earth. The word is confined to the Germanic branch (West Germanic): Old Frisian dust, Middle Dutch dust, Middle Low German dust, "dust, powder", pointing to a reconstructed West Germanic *dustą. Beside it stood a parallel form *dunstą, "vapour, haze, dust" (Old English dūnst, Old High German tunst, modern German Dunst, "vapour, fume, haze"), and beyond Germanic the word has no certain cognates: the Oxford English Dictionary records the origin as uncertain. An Indo-European connection is often proposed to the root family of Sanskrit dhūli ("dust") and dhūma ("smoke"), Latin fūmus, and Greek thūmos — a base *dʰeu-, "to rise in a cloud (of dust, smoke, vapour)" — but the exact formation is debated, and handbooks mark it a hypothesis, not a fact.

The vowel history is a neat English story of its own: Old English long ū was shortened before the consonant cluster -st in Middle English, so dūst became ME dust [dust], which the Great Vowel Shift did not diphthongise as it did house (< Old English hūs) and mouse (< mūs) — hence modern dust rhymes with must, not with house. Semantically, dust spread in every direction from the powder: the dust of the road and of the battlefield ("to bite the dust" is a 19th-century American racing idiom), gold-dust, sawdust, coal-dust; the verb grew a double life — "to dust" in the old sense "to sprinkle or powder" (dusting crops, dusting a cake with sugar) and, from the 19th century, "to dust" in the household sense "to wipe the dust away"; and the dustman of the 18th century was the collector of the household ash-and-refuse called "dust", whose dust-yards are the bridge from the word to its compound.

2. Heap: Old English hēap, the pile and the crowd

Old English hēap (masculine) meant two things at once: "a pile, a mass" and "a crowd, troop, multitude" — a heap of stones and a heap of men were the same word, and the second sense survives faintly in older poetic English (a "heap" of warriors) while the first has triumphed. The word continues Proto-Germanic *haupaz, with cognates throughout the branch (Old Saxon hōp, Old Frisian hāp, Middle Dutch hôop > Dutch hoop, Old High German houf > German Haufen, "heap, pile, crowd"). Beyond Germanic the etymology is uncertain: the traditional comparison is with the Baltic forms Lithuanian kaũpas ("heap, pile"), Latvian kaûps, and Old Prussian kaupis ("hill"), which suggest an old European base of the *keup- type meaning roughly "rounded mound, hummock, heap" — but the Oxford English Dictionary notes that no Indo-European etymology for the Germanic word is generally accepted, and the Greek comparisons sometimes added (κύφος, "a bend, a hump") are speculative. The modern word kept both of its Old English halves in changed proportion: the crowd sense faded into archaism, while heap grew the colloquial intensifier ("heaps of fun", "a heap of trouble"), the technical heap of programming (the data structure and the memory pool of a running program, both named for the piles they manage), and the verb "to heap" (to heap up, to heap gifts on someone).

3. The compound: dust-heap and the London dust-yards (18th–19th centuries)

The collocation of "dust" and "heap" is natural enough to have occurred any time after the words existed; but the compound dust-heap became an institution with the London dust-trade of the 18th and 19th centuries. In the absence of municipal waste collection, a house's "dust" meant the combined ashes of its fires — cinders, rags, bones, and kitchen refuse — collected by the dustman's cart and piled into great mounds on the edges of the city. The dust-yards employed dust-girls and dust-workers who sifted the mounds for salable cinders (re-sold for burning), bones (for glue), rags (for paper), and the occasional rumoured treasure; some dust-contractors grew rich, whether through honest salvage or, as contemporaries joked, through the valuables that fell among the ashes. The word's literary apotheosis came in Dickens's Our Mutual Friend (1865), whose plot is built on the "dust-heaps" of the miser — mountains of rubbish concealing a fortune — and on the dustman Mr. Boffin, who inherits the mounds. In the same decades the word crossed into ordinary vocabulary as the place where anything worthless ends.

4. Wilde and the literary dust-heap (1888)

It is this Victorian dust-heap that receives the ending of Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince: the dead Swallow is thrown onto "a dust-heap", and the leaden heart that would not melt in the foundry is cast after it. The compound does the story's final judgement in a phrase: beauty, love, and sacrifice reduced to the mound of ashes and refuse — the funereal sense of dust ("dust to dust") meeting the plain domestic heap — while God, in the tale's last lines, takes the bird into Paradise and the Prince's soul into his golden city. The dust-heap is the story's earth; evermore is its heaven.

5. The dust-heap of history (20th–21st centuries)

In the twentieth century the literal dust-yards closed with the coming of municipal incineration and landfill, and the compound made its great figurative leap: "the dust-heap of history" joined "the dustbin of history" — the phrase long attributed to Trotsky (1917), who told his opponents they belonged in the history-bin — as the standard image for oblivion: failed regimes, superseded ideas, and dead fashions are "consigned to the dust-heap of history". Today the word does double duty: in journalism and literary prose it is a lively, slightly antique metaphor with a Dickensian glow, and in the story that keeps the mound visible it is, simply and terribly, the corner of the world where the dead swallow lies.
