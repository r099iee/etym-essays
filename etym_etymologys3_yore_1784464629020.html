1. A root for the year: PIE *yeh₁r- and the Germanic *jērą

The story of yore is the story of the word year seen through a grammatical accident. Proto-Indo-European reconstructed the word for "year, season" as *yeh₁r- (also written *yer- or *yōr-), with a plausible but unprovable connection to a root meaning "to go, to travel" — the year as the going-round, the completed circuit of the seasons. The reconstruction's laryngeal and vowel grades are debated, but the family is one of the most stable in Indo-European: Latin annus, Greek hóros ("year, season, hour"), and the Germanic forms all continue it in one way or another.

The Germanic branch made the noun *jērą, "year," which under the West Germanic sound changes became Old English ġēar — ordinary "year," the direct ancestor of the modern word. Cognates appear across Germanic: Old High German jār (modern German Jahr), Old Saxon jār, Old Norse ár, Gothic jēr. All mean the same thing: the cycle of the year.

2. The grammatical accident: the genitive plural ġēara

The decisive step for yore was purely grammatical. Old English, like other older Indo-European languages, had a living case system. The noun ġēar "year" formed a genitive plural ġēara, meaning "of years." Used adverbially — a pattern older English inherited from its case grammar — "of years" came to mean "years ago, in former times," the sense we would render today as "formerly" or "long since." This adverbial genitive appears throughout Old English prose and verse, where geara (also spelled geāra, gearra) means "long ago, of old, in times past."

  • The adverbial genitive is the direct ancestor of yore: ġēara "of years, years ago" → yore.
  • The meaning at birth was relational and quantitative: many years had passed since the events in question.
  • No separate word was invented; yore is year itself, frozen in an ancient case-form.

3. Middle English and the freeze: from grammar to lexical fossil

In Middle English the living case system of Old English collapsed, and the genitive construction was no longer productive. But the frozen adverbial form did not die; it was re-analysed as an independent word. Middle English records yore, yoor, yare, and related spellings meaning "long ago, formerly," used adverbially ("yore ago," "of yore") and, increasingly, as a noun for the distant past. The spelling settled as yore, and the word's meaning drifted from "years ago" to "the time long past" — from a relative measurement of time to an absolute name for legendary antiquity.

Crucially, modern speakers usually meet the word inside the phrase of yore. This is partly a re-inheritance of the old genitive sense ("of years [past]") and partly a parallel with other "of"-phrases of old time ("of old"). The word thus preserves, in its everyday collocation, the very grammar that created it.

4. Register retreat: from ordinary word to literary badge

The social history of yore is one of steady retreat into elevation:

  • In medieval English, yore could be used in plain narrative to mean "long ago" — ordinary, unselfconscious.
  • By the Renaissance it had grown archaic and poetic, withdrawing from everyday prose into verse, chronicle, and elevated diction.
  • In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the ballad revival and the Romantics seized on it for legendary time — the golden days of heroes, minstrels, and knights — and it became a marker of the mythic past: "in days of yore," "the bards of yore."
  • In the twentieth century, fantasy and period verse (including Tolkien) cemented yore as the word that opens a gulf between legendary then and diminished now.

5. Literary attestations and the dwarves' song

Yore appears throughout the ballad and romance tradition — in Walter Scott's historical verse and novels, in Longfellow's and Tennyson's archaizing poems, and in the many imitations of medieval style. In Tolkien's dwarves' song — "The dwarves of yore made mighty spells" — the word does exactly what its etymology predicts: it compresses uncounted years into a single archaic noun, so that the craftsmen of the song are not a people of any particular century but figures of legendary, unreachable former time. The sense of loss, of a golden age, is built into the word's very shape: yore is year's ancient case-form that outlived its grammar — a fossil that still tells time.
