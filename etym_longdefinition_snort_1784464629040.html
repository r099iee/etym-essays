Snort is a verb meaning to force air suddenly and explosively through the nose, producing a short, harsh, often involuntary or semi-voluntary sound; as a noun it names that sound itself. The nasal blast can express contempt, disbelief, derision, anger, suppressed or explosive laughter, or a mere physical clearing of the nostrils — and it belongs as naturally to animals (above all horses) as to humans. The word sits on the boundary between speech and noise: it is one of English's most onomatopoeic items, a word whose sound is its meaning, and its blunt physicality has kept it at the rude, comic, or emphatic end of the vocabulary. In the sentence "This is what he heard, Gloin speaking: 'Humph!' (or some snort more or less like that)," the narrator refuses to treat the dwarf's reaction as articulate language. Gloin does not offer a polished sentence of refusal; he produces a nasal blast of skepticism — half interjection, half animal noise. The noun snort captures the sound's status between body and speech, and the comedy lies precisely in that: Bilbo's world of polite hosting collides with dwarf bluntness that can be measured only in snorts. Tone: comic, physical, slightly rude; the word keeps the scene grounded in temper and flesh rather than courtly rhetoric. Primary senses of snort (verb and noun)
  • Verb, physical — to force air noisily and explosively through the nostrils, of humans, horses, and other animals; to breathe with such a sound.
  • Verb, expressive — to make this sound as a sign of contempt, scorn, derision, disbelief, or amused dismissal ("he snorted at the suggestion"; "she snorted with laughter").
  • Noun — the sound itself; a single short, harsh nasal explosion ("a snort of contempt").
  • Noun and verb, equestrian — the loud blowing exhalation of a horse, often expressing alarm, greeting, excitement, or irritation.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern senses
  • Drug slang — to "snort" a powdered drug is to inhale it sharply through the nose, and "a snort" is a dose so taken (20th century onward). Historical bridge: the same sharp nasal inhalation; the nose replaces the mouth as the route of intake, and the short, explosive quality of the action is preserved.
  • Laughter — "a snort of laughter" is a laugh forced through the nose, usually suppressed or ungraceful; the word names the physical failure to contain amusement in the mouth.
  • Machinery and vehicles — "snort" is applied colloquially to loud, nasal, or throbbing engine and instrument noises, by extension from the bodily sound.
  • Nautical — the "snort" (or snorkel) is the air tube that lets a submerged submarine draw air; the nose-image is transferred to machinery, a 20th-century technical coinage.
  • Figurative — "to snort at" something is to dismiss it with contempt; the physical gesture has become a fixed idiom of rejection.
Register and collocation Snort collocates with of-laughter, of-derision, of-contempt; with horses, nostrils, and contemptuous contexts. It sits at the rude end of the sound-vocabulary scale, alongside huff, grunt, and sniff, but is louder and more explosive than any of them. In the present card it is used as a noun to describe Gloin's interjection, and its body-noise status is exactly the joke: the dwarves express themselves through the snort, not through the polished sentence.
