Cosmography is the story of two very different words that met halfway in the workshops of Hellenistic learning, and the two halves of the word have utterly different histories — one a mystery without an agreed ancient root, the other a scratch-mark that can be followed back to the oldest layers of the Indo-European family.

The second element first: -graphy, the scratch that became writing. The suffix -graphy descends from Greek graphḗ, "writing, description," built on the verb gráphō, "to scratch, carve, draw, write." This verb goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *gerbh-, "to scratch, carve, cut" — the key to understanding what "writing" meant to the early Greeks: to write was literally to scratch marks into a surface, be it clay, wax, stone, or bark. The Germanic branch preserves that primal sense beautifully in English carve (Old English ceorfan), German kerben "to notch," Old Norse karfa, and possibly crab, the clawed "scratching" creature. In Greek the root blossomed into the whole vocabulary of graphic signs: grammar (the art of letters), diagram, paragraph, photograph, biography, autograph — and, by attaching the suffix -graphy to the name of any subject, a host of "descriptive sciences": geography, oceanography, bibliography, and cosmography itself. To call a science a "-graphy" is to assert that it can be written down, described, mapped onto a page.

The first element: kósmos, order made into a world. Greek kósmos originally meant "order, arrangement, adornment" — the sense visible in cosmetic, the art of adornment. Its extraordinary career began when the Presocratic philosophers, above all the Pythagoreans, declared that the universe is not chaos but an ordered whole, and so appropriated the everyday word "order" as the name of the universe itself: kósmos, "the ordered world." It was a conceptual leap of the first order — the claim that the cosmos is beautiful because it is lawful. Scholars today, however, must be candid: the ultimate ancestry of kósmos is not known. It has no agreed Indo-European etymology; the standard handbooks (Beekes among them) class it as of uncertain, probably Pre-Greek origin, and earlier attempts to attach it to Indo-European roots of "order" or "praise" are rejected or unproven. The honest chain therefore begins at attested Greek and goes no further back.

The meeting: kosmographía. The two elements joined in Hellenistic Greek as kosmographía, "description of the world," attested in the first century BCE in Strabo, who used the word for the geographer's total picture of the inhabited earth and its heavens. Rome took the word over as cosmographia — the elder Pliny and the geographic writers of the first century CE used it of the encyclopaedic description of the world — and the medieval West inherited it through Latin and Old French. In the Christian middle ages, cosmography became part of the encyclopaedic tradition: the universe was a divinely ordered whole, and the cosmographer's duty was to set that order down — the globe surrounded by the spheres, the four elements, the climes of the earth. The medieval mappa mundi, with Jerusalem at the centre and the rivers of Paradise flowing from the east, is the pictorial cosmography of that age.

The golden age of the Renaissance. The word's great age was the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, when cosmography crowned European learning. Sebastian Münster's Cosmographia (1544) was the best-selling book of its century, a compendium of the whole known world; Gerardus Mercator, the father of the modern atlas, published his Atlas in 1595 as a cosmographical collection of maps of the world, the heavens, and the created whole; in England, Peter Heylyn's Cosmographie (1652) carried the tradition into the early modern period. The cosmographer was the master of the world-picture: at once astronomer, geographer, historian, and cartographer, able to place every land and every star in a single ordered scheme — the very encyclopaedic figure C. S. Lewis imagines when a prince in Narnia is taught Cosmography beside Rhetoric and Heraldry.

The unravelling. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the unified science came apart. Astronomy became a branch of Newtonian mathematics, geography an empirical science of measurement, and the two no longer needed a common master-discipline; cosmography's unified scope unravelled and the word grew antiquarian, fading from practical use. It survives today in three registers: the history of science (where it names the early-modern genre it was); reverent and poetic uses (a grand name for the whole spectacle of the created universe); and metaphor, where any complete model of reality — "a cosmography of the mind" — can borrow the word. The two ancient roots live on separately too: kósmos in cosmos, cosmology, and cosmopolitan, and *gerbh- in every "-graphy" from geography to photography — the scratch that became writing, and the order that became a world, still joined in the one word that describes them both.
