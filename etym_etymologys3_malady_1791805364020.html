The word entered English in the late thirteenth century as a direct borrowing of Old French maladie, the noun of state formed from malade "sick". French had created it in the twelfth century, and from the beginning it covered both the condition of being ill and a particular illness. Middle English spellings — maladie, maladye, malade — show the word still felt French; the change of final -ie to -y in the fourteenth century is the same anglicisation seen in melody, remedy and company, and it fixed the modern shape of the headword.

The Latin ancestry and its problems

Dictionaries derive French malade from Latin male habitus, literally "badly held" or "in a bad condition", from male "badly" and habitus "condition, disposition" (the past participle of habēre, "to hold"). The phrase is plausible as a Late Latin description of a body in poor condition, and the semantic fit is excellent. The phonology, however, is not perfectly regular: the loss of the internal syllable and the change of intervocalic -b- to -d- require analogical or dialectal explanation, and the Vulgar Latin etymon is reconstructed rather than attested. Some reference works therefore mark the derivation as "probably" or note that its details are uncertain. The ultimate Indo-European root *gʰrebʰ- "to seize, grasp" (source of habēre) is secure; the precise Romance pathway is the debatable part, and no classical Latin noun *maladia exists to bridge the gap.

  • Secure: Latin habēre → habitus; Old French malade (12th c.) → maladie → English malady.
  • Uncertain: the Vulgar Latin form and the -b- to -d- development, which is not a regular sound change.

Medieval senses: body, soul and humour

In Middle English a malady could be any harmful condition — a wound, a fever, a chronic disease, or, in religious prose, the sickness of sin. Preachers used the medical metaphor systematically: sin is a malady, penance the cure, the priest the physician. This was not mere ornament. It reflected the medieval conviction that body and soul were governed by the same balance, and it explains why the word could move so easily between physical and moral senses for three centuries without strain.

Early modern narrowing

With the rise of clinical anatomy and the new nosology of the seventeenth century, the word settled into the bodily sense while keeping an air of generality. It became the natural choice for illnesses that were grave, prolonged or imperfectly understood. George Cheyne's The English Malady (1733) gave the language a lasting phrase for melancholy and nervous disorder, and its title shows how the word could carry a national diagnosis as well as an individual one.

The royal malady

Eighteenth-century writers use malady of the illnesses of kings with particular frequency, partly out of decorum. "The King's malady" at Metz in 1744, and later "the royal malady" of George III, avoid naming a disease that was politically explosive. Modern decline and figurative survival

  • Nineteenth-century medicine largely abandoned malady for disease, disorder and later condition; the older word survived in belles-lettres and in translations from French.
  • Figurative use expanded: "the malady of the age", "social maladies", "a malady of the body politic" — the medical frame now diagnoses institutions rather than bodies.
  • In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word is marked as literary or archaic; using it in speech signals formality, period flavour or gentle irony.
  • It has produced almost no derivatives in English — no working verb, no adjective in general use — which is one reason it feels like a borrowed relic rather than a productive root.

What the history explains

The word's long double life — bodily disease and moral sickness — accounts for its peculiar tone today: it can be used of a cancer and of corruption in the same breath, and it always implies something the sufferer endures rather than something he merely contracts. That is exactly the sense required for a king struck down on campaign, and exactly why the word outlived its rivals in literary prose long after medicine had discarded it.
