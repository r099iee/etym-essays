DEBARRED is the past tense and past participle of the transitive verb debar, meaning "to exclude, shut out, prohibit, or prevent (a person) from doing something, entering a place, or enjoying a right or privilege," usually by authority or by an insurmountable barrier. In the sentence before us — from a biographical study of John Stuart Mill — the word describes the philosopher himself: "although the philosopher himself, through his belief in the equality of the sexes, was debarred from thinking so." Mill, the sentence argues, could not believe that his wife's mind belonged to a fundamentally different class or order from his own, because his own conviction of the equality of the sexes made that belief impossible for him. The nuance is one of prohibition that is almost physical: a mental action is blocked, shut out, excluded, as if a bar had been dropped across the doorway of a thought. The barrier here is internal — Mill's own principle functioning as a bar across the entrance of an idea — and the passive construction, "was debarred," emphasizes that the exclusion happened to him by the logic of his own convictions, not by any external rule.

  • Sense 1 — physical exclusion: "to bar the way to, to prevent entry to a place" (debarred from the premises, debarred from entering the building). This is the most concrete use and the one closest to the word's origin in the image of a bar across a door.
  • Sense 2 — legal and institutional exclusion: "to exclude by authority from a right, office, privilege, membership, or body" — debarred from holding public office, debarred from practising a profession, debarred from the sacraments, debarred from a competition. This is the classic, still current sense.
  • Sense 3 — principled or internal prevention (the Mill sense): "to make it impossible for a person to think or believe something," the barrier being a conviction, principle, scruple, or impossibility rather than an external rule — the mental bar that shuts out a thought.
  • Sense 4 — technical administrative sense (debarment): in public procurement, professional regulation, and international trade, "debarment" is the formal administrative exclusion of a contractor, vendor, or practitioner from doing business with an agency or organization — a systematic modern extension of the medieval legal sense, with its own regulatory jargon (a debarred list, debarment procedures, suspension and debarment).
  • Historical bridge: the word is formed of de- ("away, from, off") + bar ("to close with a bar, to shut out"), from the picture of a wooden bar dropped across a door or gate. The barrier was physical first (a beam across a threshold), then institutional (the bar of the court, the rule of exclusion), then internalized (a principle acting as a bar across the door of a thought). The semantic route is transparent and mechanical: to debar is to treat a person as though a bar had been laid across the entrance to a right, a privilege, or an idea.
  • Register and connotation: formal, legal-administrative, sober; the word carries the authority of courts and statutes. In everyday speech it tends to be replaced by barred, excluded, or prevented; debarred is reserved for contexts of formal or principled exclusion, where the gravity of the word matches the gravity of the exclusion.
  • Differentiating terms: barred (simpler, more physical), excluded (merely left out), prohibited (the rule that forbids), disqualified (loss of an eligibility once held), prevented (blocked by a cause or obstacle, with less authority), precluded (rendered impossible, often logically), banned (often by decree or law, closer to prohibition).

Derivatives and collocations. The word appears with the constructions debar (someone) from (something) and debar (someone) from (doing something), and in the passive be debarred from (something). Common collocations include debarred from holding office, debarred from practising, debarred from entering, debarred by statute, debarred by regulation. The derived noun debarment names the act or condition of exclusion and is standard in legal and administrative English; the agent noun debarring (rare) and the adjective forms are formed regularly. The word's root bar also underlies unrelated homonyms (the counter of a tavern, a musical measure), but in debarred the operative image is always the barrier.
