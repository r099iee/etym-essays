Sulla is a Roman cognomen used here as a proper noun for one man: Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix (138–78 BCE), general, consul, and dictator of Rome from 82 to 79 BCE. In the source sentence — “A great struggle was going on between two generals, Marius and Sulla, for the control of Rome” — the name stands for the aristocratic commander who marched on Rome, defeated the Marians, and then used unlimited power to remake the constitution before astonishing contemporaries by resigning it.

The name and the family. Sulla is a cognomen of the patrician gens Cornelia, one of the greatest clans of the Republic, which also produced the Lentuli, the Scipiones, the Cethegi and the Dolabellae. A cognomen in this position marks a branch of the clan rather than an individual, but by the late Republic the branch had become identified with one spectacular career. Sulla added the agnomen Felix, “the fortunate” or “the blessed”, partly in earnest and partly as a claim to divine favour: he attributed his successes to Fortuna, and his private devotion to the goddess was notorious.

The career.

  • Jugurtha: as quaestor under Marius in Numidia (107 BCE) he was credited with obtaining the capture of Jugurtha by negotiation with the Moorish king Bocchus, a coup that made his name and planted the seed of his rivalry with Marius.
  • Germany and the courts: he served with distinction against the Cimbri and Teutones, and later governed Cilicia, where he installed a client king on the Armenian throne.
  • The Social War and the consulship: his successes against the Italian allies won him the consulship of 88 BCE, and with it the command against Mithridates of Pontus.
  • March on Rome (88 BCE): when the tribune Sulpicius transferred the Mithridatic command to Marius by law, Sulla led his legions into the city — an unprecedented act that broke the taboo on armed entry — and had his opponents declared public enemies.
  • The East (87–84 BCE): he campaigned in Greece and Asia, sacked Athens in 86 BCE, and made peace with Mithridates at Dardanus in 85 BCE, while Rome fell to the Marians and his supporters were massacred.
  • Civil war and dictatorship (83–82 BCE): he returned with a veteran army, was joined by Pompey and Crassus, and destroyed the Marian forces at the Colline Gate. He was appointed dictator legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae, a dictatorship without the traditional six-month limit.
  • Proscriptions: the first great proscription, published as lists of the condemned, killed thousands of senators and equestrians and confiscated their property; the names of the dead were posted in the Forum and rewards paid for their heads.
  • Reform and abdication: he enlarged the Senate, restricted the tribunate's legislative power, reorganised the courts and settled veterans on confiscated land; then in 79 BCE he laid down the dictatorship, retired to Campania, and died of disease in 78 BCE.

Why the name matters. Sulla is the hinge between the old Republic and the age of warlords. He demonstrated that a general with loyal legions could take the city itself, that proscription could be a instrument of policy, and that a constitution could be rewritten by force. Caesar learned the lesson and was more thorough; Augustus learned the cost and was more patient. Sulla's abdication, which astonished antiquity, made his career a standing puzzle: was he a reformer who retired when the work was done, or a man who had exhausted himself in blood and could no longer bear the city? The question has shaped every biography since Plutarch.

Reputation and later use. In Latin literature Sulla quickly becomes a byword for cruelty and for the misuse of victory; the phrase tempora Sullana, “Sullan times”, means an age of proscription and terror. English political writing from the seventeenth century onwards uses the name the same way, and “a new Sulla” is a standard phrase for a dictatorial strongman. The adjective Sullan is used of his legislation, his veterans, his colonies and his constitution.

An unrelated homograph. The same spelling names a Mediterranean fodder legume, Sulla coronaria (formerly Hedysarum coronarium), known in English as sulla or French honeysuckle. The botanical name is Italian in origin and has no connection with the Roman family.
