ivy — The history of the English word ivy is the history of what a single climbing plant could mean to successive civilisations. Linguistically, the most striking fact is negative: unlike almost every other tree and vine name in English, ivy has no secure Indo-European pedigree. Old English īfig goes back to a reconstructed Proto-West Germanic *ibah-, and the handbooks are largely agreed that this stem belongs to the pre-Germanic substrate — a botanical name that the Indo-European newcomers took over from the earlier peoples of western Europe along with the plant itself, much as they kept river names and mountain names. That substrate origin is itself a kind of history: it tells us that ivy was distinctive enough, and common enough, to be worth naming long before English existed, and that the name has travelled down through the millennia without any intermediate chain of meaning-shifts.
  • Old English period: īfig appears in the glossaries of the late 7th–8th centuries; it was a plain, practical plant name for the evergreen that thatched cottage walls and was gathered for garlands.
  • Classical inheritance: Latin hedera had made ivy the badge of Bacchus and of poetic laureation — poets wore ivy as well as bay — and this association of ivy with inspiration and with fidelity (its evergreen constancy) filtered into medieval European learning and heraldic and funerary art.
  • Medieval and early modern English: ivi / ivy is a stable, transparent word from c. 1300 on; proverbs such as “ivy is the friend of the oak, and its killer” were already in circulation, showing that the double nature of the plant — tenacious companion and slow destroyer — was noticed early.
  • The Gothic-Romantic revolution: the decisive shift came in the 18th–19th centuries, when the picturesque and Gothic revivals made the ivy-clad ruin the stock image of melancholy. The word acquired a colouring of dignified decay; to be “ivy-mantled” was to be beautiful, ancient, and abandoned.
  • Modern double life: in the 20th century the word split. Horticulturally, English ivy is often an invasive nuisance that strips mortar and drives out native plants. At the same time “Ivy League” (the 1930s nickname of the old north-eastern US universities) borrowed the ivied masonry of old campuses as a brand of elite prestige. The word now swings between vandal and aristocrat.
In the corrected passage the Gothic-Romantic layer is everything: the ivy is black, uneven, patched — the sign of a tower that no one loves and no one repairs. The reader is expected to bring the whole symbolic history of fidelity and ruin to the single word, so that the “black patches” quietly accuse.
  • Botanical detail: ivy flowers only in the autumn, when most other climbers are dormant, and its berries ripen in winter — a plant whose whole life-cycle is out of step with the ordinary year, which deepens its association with the ghostly and the deathless in the passage.
  • Attested compounds and proverbs: “ivy-bush,” “ivy-man,” “ivy-tod,” and the proverb “ivy is the friend of the oak” (with its grim coda, “but the oak dies”) show how deeply the word is woven into English folk observation of the plant’s slow strangling of its host.
  • Etymological resonance: the very opaqueness of the word’s origin (no secure PIE root) fits its use here — ivy is one of the oldest named things in the English landscape, a word from the deep past, appropriate to a tower that is all ruin and age.

What is clear is the morphology: the English word is the diminutive *knukilaz, “little bone, little knob,” which enters Old English as cnucel (Ælfric’s Glossary, c. 1000), then settles as Middle English knokel in the 13th century and modern knuckle by the 16th.
  • Sense-development: from “little bone/knob” the word narrowed to the specific visible joints of the hand; from there it seeded a set of violent and practical idioms — “to knuckle under,” “to knuckle down,” “a knuckle-duster,” “bare-knuckled.”
  • The adjective knuckled (16th c.) keeps the noun’s concrete image: anything “knuckled” is knobby, jointed, articulated like a fist.
  • Literary use: in the passage, “fists of knuckled masonry” performs a deliberate metaphor — the wall is given bones and a hand; the fortress clenches. The word’s anatomical concreteness is what makes the personification work: it is not that the masonry is rough, but that it is skeletal and gestural.
The cultural history of knuckle runs from anatomy to violence to submission: the same joint that touches the earth in the game of marbles, that raps in reproof, and that clenches in a fist is here lent to stone, so that the tower’s decay reads as a clenched, defiant hand.
  • The gesture in grammar: the phrase “fists of knuckled masonry” is built so that the word knuckled does double duty — it describes the physical roughness of the stone and simultaneously sets up the comparison of the tower to a hand, so the adjective is both literal and the seed of the personification.
  • Family of kin words: the same Germanic “knob, knot, bone” family gives knoll, knur, knurled, knot, and knap, all of which share the core sense of a rounded protuberance; knuckled is the one that keeps the jointed, articulated anatomy of the hand.
  • Sound symbolism: the initial cluster kn-, once actually pronounced with the k (it was lost in English pronunciation only in the 17th–18th centuries), itself suggests a hard, knobbly impact — the word sounds like the contact of bone against stone, which suits a passage about fists and masonry.
  • Attested compounds: the noun has generated “knuckle-bone,” “knuckle-duster,” “bare-knuckled,” and the verbs “knuckle under” and “knuckle down”; each keeps the physical image of the joint while bending it toward submission, violence, or effort.
What the passage gains is a wall that is not merely rough but skeletal — a fortress reduced to the bones of a fist, clenching upward in the last gesture of a decaying body. blasphemously — English blasphemy enters through the Latin and Greek of the Christian tradition, but the compound is pagan Greek in origin: blasphēmos, “speaking evil,” from blapsis “an injury, a harm” and phēmē “speech, report.” The earliest attested sense is secular — to slander a person or god — and only gradually, through the Septuagint and the New Testament, did the word become the technical name for speaking against the true God. That long religious career shaped the English adverb blasphemously, which has never quite lost its liturgical gravity.
  • Greek background: phēmē is an ordinary word for what is said or reported (compare “fame,” “euphemism”), and blasphēmein in classical prose means simply to speak injuriously, to revile.
  • Biblical fix: the Septuagint and Vulgate (4th c. CE) stabilise the religious sense; ecclesiastical Latin blasphemāre passes into Old French as blasfemer/blasmer and into Middle English as blasfemen (13th c.).
  • The adjective and adverb: blasphemous is attested in Middle English by the 14th century; blasphemously follows in the 15th–16th centuries, with Shakespeare confirming the modern shape in the 1590s.
  • Extension beyond religion: from the 18th century onward the word loosens to mean “shockingly irreverent toward anything held sacred” — institutions, taste, customs — so that today we can say “blasphemously expensive” without the shadow of the stake.
In the passage the religious register is fully live: the tower “pointed blasphemously at heaven.” The mute stone finger does what no human tongue dared — it insults the divine itself — and the adverb carries the entire history of forbidden speech, from Greek slander to Christian sacrilege, into a single gesture of defiant ruin.
  • Modern secular widening: since the 18th century the adverb has escaped the church and now applies to anything held sacred in a loose, social sense — “blasphemously bad taste,” “blasphemously expensive” — so that even secular readers feel the shock of disrespect it carries.
  • Etymological transparency: the Greek compound blasphēmos literally analyses as “injury” + “speech”; the English adverb inherits that grammar, so a tower pointing blasphemously is a speech-act made of stone — an utterance that injures the reputation of heaven by its very posture.
  • Degree of intensity: among the irreverence words, blasphemously is the strongest — profanely merely secularises, sacrilegiously violates holy things, implously neglects piety, but blasphemously attacks the sacred itself with speech or gesture, which is precisely the defiance of the pointing ruin.
In the final image, then, the three words fuse into one anatomy of decay: ivy supplies the creeping black scab of neglect, knuckled the skeletal clenched fist of the stone, and blasphemously the open insult hurled at heaven — the tower is a dying body that dies cursing.
