Proto-Germanic *harmô (reconstructed; Germanic, 1st millennium BCE) — “weasel” (the Germanic line is one of the two sources that feed the word; cf. Old English hearma “shrew, weasel” and Old High German harmo “weasel, ermine”) → Old High German harmo (attested 8th–9th c.) — “weasel, ermine” (in parallel) Latin armēnius “Armenian”, from the toponym Armenia “Armenia”; in the phrase mūs armēnius “Armenian mouse” (attested in Late Latin and Medieval Latin sources) — the stoat, so named because the finest white pelts were believed to come from Armenia → Old French hermine, ermine (attested from the 12th c.) — “stoat; stoat fur; ermine” — the French form shows contamination between Latin armēnius and Germanic harmo (the Germanic initial h- was absorbed into the Latin-derived word); the relative weight of the two sources is debated → Anglo-Norman / Middle English ermine, ermin, ermyn (borrowed from Old French, attested from c. 1200) — “the fur of the stoat, especially as trimming for royal and noble robes” → Modern English ermine (present-day) — “the stoat in its white winter coat, or its fur; a heraldic fur; the royal or judicial dignity symbolized by the fur” NOTE (debated/nuanced): Scholarship is divided between the toponymic theory (from armēnius, “Armenian”, because the pelts were traded from Armenia) and the Germanic theory (from *harmô, “weasel”); the Old French hermine almost certainly blends both, and the “Armenian mouse” phrase may itself be a learned folk-etymology superimposed on a native Germanic word. No Proto-Indo-European root is posited with confidence.
