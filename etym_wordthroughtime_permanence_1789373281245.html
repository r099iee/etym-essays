- Late 14th to 15th Century - Emerges in Middle English legal charters and theological translations from Anglo-Norman and Latin; denotes perpetual duration, unbreakable covenants, and the eternal, immutable nature of God.
- 16th to 17th Century - Broadens into natural philosophy and material descriptions; characterizes durable architectural masonry, lasting institutional statutes, and physical substances that resist corrosion or decay.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Elevated into an indispensable philosophical category by rationalist and empiricist metaphysicians (Kant's permanence of substance), signifying the constant physical substratum underlying all sensory flux.
- 19th Century - Flourishes in industrial chemistry, photography, and patent law; designates archival paper permanence, lightfast photographic pigments, and permanent legal easements.
- 20th to 21st Century - Expands in two radically divergent directions: in commercial consumer culture, it denotes cosmetic hair treatments ("perm"); in continental philosophy, narrative theory, and Proustian criticism, it undergoes profound deconstruction as Gaston Bachelard and modernist authors diagnose the "false permanence" of biographical identity.
