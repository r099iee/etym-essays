Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*h₂wē- / *h₂weh₁- (or *wer- 'to lift, raise')
"to blow (of wind); or to lift, suspend, elevate"
Note: PIE root associated with atmospheric movement and breath, cognate with Greek aēmi ('to blow') and English wind and weather.
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th–4th c. BCE)
ἀήρ (āḗr)
"lower atmosphere, mist, vapor, dense air surrounding the earth"
Note: Distinct in Homeric Greek from αἰθήρ (aithḗr), the pure, radiant upper ether of the gods.
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE–4th c. CE)
āēr (accusative: āerem)
"air, atmosphere, breath, weather"
↓
Old French (c. 10th–13th c.)
air
"air, breeze, climate; (metaphorically) visible appearance, countenance, bearing, mien"
Note: Developed the secondary sense of outward appearance or personal demeanor from the 'atmosphere' or aura surrounding a person.
↓
Italian / Middle French (Musical Specialization) (c. 14th–16th c.)
aria (Italian) / air (French)
"musical melody, lyrical tune, song, vocal phrase"
Note: Sound waves traveling through air gave rise to 'air' as a melody; French court etiquette also gave rise to 'se donner des airs' ('to give oneself airs').
↓
Middle to Early Modern English (c. 1380–1650)
air (singular) → airs (plural: tunes; pretentious manners)
"melodic tunes; affected postures, haughty demeanor"
↓
Modern English (present)
