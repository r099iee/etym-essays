In Macaulay's panoramic comparison of empires, dominions names the aggregate of sovereign territories, realms, and colonial possessions that together made up a monarch's political universe — specifically, in the sentence in which it appears, the “dominions of Charles V,” the vast Habsburg inheritance to which Cortes and Pizarro added the conquered realms of Mexico and Peru. The word is the plural of dominion, and its tone is deliberately legal and imperious: it derives from Latin dominium, the right of a master (dominus) over his house and property, and it still carries that original scent of lordship and possession. Macaulay's point is comparative and ironic. The Spanish crown gained enormous realms in a few years, added by the swords of adventurers; but the wealth so acquired did not build durable power. Against these “dominions” won by conquest, Macaulay sets the slow, organic growth of the British colonial settlements — “far mightier and wealthier” by trade, law, and settlement than the conquered realms. The word thus carries, in a single noun, the whole argument of the passage: conquest adds territory, but only institutions create empire.

Sense 1 — Sovereign territory or territories; a realm or realms under one rule (the sense on this card). In historical and formal prose, “the dominions of X” is the aggregate of all lands under X's sovereignty — “the dominions of Charles V,” “the dominions of the Ottoman sultan.” The plural, as here, stresses the composite and additive nature of the holdings: many lands, many peoples, one lord. It is the standard word of early modern diplomatic and imperial English for the territories of a crown.

Sense 2 — Lordship, sovereignty, or dominion as an abstract power. The singular dominion also names the abstract right or exercise of rule itself: “man's dominion over nature,” “dominion over the beasts” (Genesis 1:26, “have dominion over the fish of the sea”). This is the oldest English sense, “lordship, mastery, rule,” and it coexists with the territorial sense throughout the word's history; in political philosophy (e.g. Locke's Two Treatises) “dominion” is the technical term for the right of property and rule.

Sense 3 — The Dominions (capital D), the self-governing British colonies (late 19th–20th century). From the 1860s to the mid-twentieth century, “the Dominions” was a constitutional term for the self-governing parts of the British Empire — Canada (from 1867), Australia (from 1901), New Zealand, South Africa (from 1910), and Newfoundland until 1949. A Dominion was a community with responsible government and its own parliament within the empire, owing allegiance to the Crown but not ruled by Westminster; the status was formalized in the Balfour Declaration of 1926 and the Statute of Westminster of 1931, and the term fell out of use as the Dominions became fully independent.

Sense 4 — Related and extended uses.

  • Dominions in theology: in Christian doctrine, “Dominions” (Latin Dominationes) is one of the nine choirs of angels, the order that carries out the commands of God — a heavenly lordship that preserves the word's root meaning of rule.
  • The Dominion of New England: in American colonial history, “the Dominion of New England” (1686–1689) was the short-lived royal amalgamation of the northern colonies — one of James II's own errors of the very sort Macaulay is about to recount.
  • Dominion as a natural-law term: in political philosophy, “dominion” names the right of property and rule, the foundation of government, and “right of dominion” is the technical phrase for sovereignty.
  • Common modern sense: today dominion survives chiefly in historical, legal, and formal prose, in Christian hymnody and theology, in the phrase “dominion over,” and in the historical name of the British Dominions; the everyday register has largely been taken over by its doublet domain (“a field of authority; an internet address”).

Semantic architecture. The whole range grows from the Latin house. PIE *dṓm/*dem- “house; to build” gave Latin domus “house, household,” and from domus came dominus, “master of the house,” then dominium, “lordship, right of ownership,” which in Roman law named the master's full power over his property. When the word entered English through Old French, it carried both the abstract sense (rule, mastery) and the concrete one (the territory ruled); the plural, dominions, naturally named the many lands under one lord — exactly Macaulay's Charles V, and exactly the colonial word the British Empire later claimed. Graded against its competitors: territory is the neutral geographical word; realm is the romantic, feudal word for a single kingdom; possession is the strictly colonial and legal term for distant holdings; and domain is the modern doublet that has taken over the everyday register.
