duck (ducked)
- c. 1300 Middle English douken/duken — "to dive, stoop, dip"; the bird "duck" takes its name from the verb, the bird that ducks for food.
- 16th-17th c. — the sense narrows from plunging to dodging: to lower the head and body to pass under a low obstruction or avoid a blow; avoidance and compression fuse.
- Contemporary — figurative evasion: "duck out," "duck a question"; the animal origin stays audible in "duck and weave," while the noun gains British endearment and the cricketer's "duck" (a score of nought).

ratty
- 1812 — "of or like a rat": the rodent transferred to a quality.
- 1840s — "infested with rats": the animal becomes an infestation.
- 1867 — "shabby, worn": the vermin-sense slips to general squalor, gnawed and soiled.
- 1880s — "angry, irritable" (chiefly British, of a person): the worn thing becomes a snappish temper, as if bitten.

heady
- 14th c. — "headstrong, impetuous, hasty": the word's first sense, now archaic, surviving in the noun "headiness."
- c. 1570 — "going to the head, intoxicating": applied to wine, perfume, aroma — a force that acts on the senses like alcohol or a drug.
- Contemporary — "heady days," "heady heights," "heady success": exhilarating overload, at once glamorous and dizzying; in the passage, the vocabulary of elevation turned to nausea over a stinking reek.

sally
- 16th c. — the military sortie: a sudden rush-out of a besieged garrison against the besieger, launched from the sally port.
- 17th c. — extended to speech: a "sally of wit," a sudden pointed verbal attack.
- Contemporary — deliberately archaic and mock-heroic: "sally forth" survives in literary diction for an old-fashioned, purposeful breaking-out, its siege-origin still audible.
