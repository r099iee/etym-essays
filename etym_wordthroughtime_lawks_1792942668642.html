- Medieval — the oath itself: by the Lord and good Lord swear surprise solemnly, with real blasphemy risk attached.
- 17th c. — mincing begins: pulpits and parlours demand softened oaths, so Lord frays at the edges into law, lor, and lud in rapid speech.
- 18th c. — lawks is born: the clipped law takes the exclamatory -s and the full cry lawks-a-mussy (Lord have mercy) trots through farce and ballad.
- Victorian era — below stairs and on stage: Dickens's servants, music-hall comics, and provincial novels make lawks the signature gasp of the good-hearted rustic.
- Early 20th c. — Tolkien files it: the Shire's hobbits inherit exactly this servant-and-farm diction, so Merry's Lawks! dates him as rural, affectionate, and unspoiled.
- Today — pure period colour: nobody exclaims it unselfconsciously now; it survives as deliberate archaism, quoted warmth from a less guarded age of surprise.
