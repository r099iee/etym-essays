The history of Gypsies is a history of mistaken geography becoming social identity. The word descends from Egyptian, not from any Romani self-designation. When Roma communities reached the Byzantine world and then western Europe in the late Middle Ages, they were often called Aigyptioi, Egyptians, or similar names, either because they claimed to come from Egypt or because observers placed their homeland there. The label stuck, and English Gypsy is a worn-down form of Middle English egypcien / gypcian. The plural Gypsies then became the ordinary way of speaking about the people as a group.

From Egypt to Europe. The Greek name for Egypt, Aigyptos, is probably derived from the Egyptian phrase ḥwt-kꜣ-ptḥ, "mansion of the ka of Ptah", the name of a temple at Memphis. Through Greek Aigyptios and Latin Aegyptius, the word reached Old French as gyptien and Middle English as egypcien. By the late Middle Ages it could mean an Egyptian person, an Egyptian artifact, or a Roma person. The semantic narrowing to Roma was complete in English by the sixteenth century, when statutes refer to "Egyptians" and then to "Gypsies". The earliest English records include a Kent parish account of 1533 mentioning gypcous, and the OED dates Gypsy to the late 1500s, with an early literary example in Geoffrey Fenton's translation of 1574. The spelling gipsy is the older British form; gypsy became standard in the nineteenth century.

The legend of Little Egypt. The Egyptian origin was not merely a scholarly error; it was a story that Roma groups themselves sometimes used. In the Balkans and Byzantium there were regions called "Little Egypt", and itinerant groups could claim Egyptian descent to obtain safe conduct or alms. The claim was useful in a society that granted privileges to pilgrims and penitents. Once the story was fixed in law and literature, it became difficult to dislodge. By the nineteenth century, philologists had demonstrated the Indian origin of Romani, but the old name remained in popular use.

Semantic drift and stereotype. The word's evaluation changed dramatically. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries Gypsies was a legal and ethnographic category, associated with vagrancy, fortune-telling, and petty theft; the Tudor statutes treated "Egyptians" as a problem of public order. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Romantic literature gave the figure a new glamour: free, mysterious, musical, outside ordinary society. Pushkin's The Gypsies (1827), Prosper Mérimée's Carmen (1845), Victor Hugo's Notre-Dame de Paris (1831), and George Borrow's Lavengro (1851) all contributed to this double image of romance and menace. In the twentieth century, racial science, fascism, and the Porajmos turned the stereotype into a weapon. After 1945, Gypsy persisted in official names and in popular culture, but Roma activism increasingly rejected it.

Sound and spelling. The formal development is a classic case of deglutination and shortening: egypcien lost its initial vowel and the affricate was simplified, producing gypcian, then gipsy or gypsy. The plural Gypsies follows the regular English pattern, with -ies after a consonant. The British spelling gipsy reflects the older convention; the y spelling was reinforced by the association with "gypsy" as a picturesque word. The pronunciation /ˈdʒɪpsiz/ has remained stable.

Socio-cultural and legal context. The word cannot be separated from the history of anti-Roma legislation. The Egyptians Act of 1530 and later statutes in England, Scotland, and continental Europe criminalized itinerancy and sometimes prescribed transportation or death. In the Romanian principalities, Roma were enslaved for centuries. Under National Socialism, Roma were classified as "asocial" or "racial enemies" and murdered in camps. These histories explain why Gypsies now carries a heavy burden. Modern British usage sometimes combines Gypsy, Roma and Traveller in service provision, but many Roma prefer Roma alone. In academic writing, Romani is the language and Roma the people.

  • Source - Middle English egypcien / gypcian "Egyptian".
  • Cause - the false belief that the Roma came from Egypt, reinforced by "Little Egypt" legends.
  • Early English - 1530s parish and legal records; literary Gypsy by 1574.
  • Semantic shift - neutral demonym to romantic stereotype to pejorative label.
  • Modern preference - Roma or Romani; Gypsies only in historical or artistic contexts.
