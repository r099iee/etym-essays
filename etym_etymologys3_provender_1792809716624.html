Every sack of provender begins with an open hand. Its deepest ancestor is the Indo-European root *ghabh-, to give and to receive — the two-way gesture of the hand extended, which Latin narrowed into habēre, to have, to hold, to keep. What the hand holds forth it furnishes: prefix prae- (before, in front — from PIE *per-, forth, forward) to habēre and Rome gets praebēre, to furnish, supply, or offer — literally to hold a thing out before someone. From its gerundive, praebenda (things that must be furnished), the whole later history hangs: the allowance, the due, the ration that authority owes each mouth.

The Church’s allowance (early medieval): The first great employer of praebenda was the cathedral. As chapters of canons organised across Europe from the 700s onward, each canon drew his fixed praebenda — a share of the chapter’s lands, food, lodging, and coin — and the word entered administration as the standard term for a clergyman’s maintenance. The system fed the praying clerks of every diocese from Durham to Sicily; the allowance was measured, recorded, litigated, and bequeathed. To hold a praebenda was to hold a living — and the plural machinery of prebends, prebendaries, and prebendal stalls still stands in English cathedrals today, the direct, learned, unbroken heir of the Latin word.

  • Habēre to praebēre: the prefix aims possession outward — to have becomes to furnish, holding turns into giving.
  • Praebēre to praebenda: the gerundive converts the act into the due — furnishing hardens into the allowance that must be furnished.
  • One mother, two children: the Church carries praebenda straight into English as prebend (stipend), while the French stable-yard reshapes it into provende (ration, fodder).

The French stable-yard (12th–13th c.): Meanwhile, on the manorial estates of northern France, praebenda had gone to work in humbler quarters. Old French provende — also spelled provendre — named the fixed allowance of oats and hay dealt to each horse, and by extension the stores of any household, garrison, or travelling train. Sound-shifts did their quiet carpentry: the prae- softened toward pro-, the b voiced toward v, and the word came out of the stable smelling of hay. When English grooms and quartermasters borrowed it in the 1300s — first as provendre, soon settled as provender — they borrowed the manger with it: the word’s first two centuries in England are overwhelmingly equine and military, measured in bushels and nose-bags.

From manger to table to mind (15th–19th c.): Soldiers eat from the same wagons as their horses, so the ration widened naturally: Tudor muster-rolls issue provender to men as well as mounts, and by Stuart times travellers praise inns for good provender — the full table, not just the full nose-bag. The widening completed itself in the Victorian study, where essayists and preachers — raised on the Authorised Version’s agricultural parables — began laying in intellectual and spiritual provender: books as fodder, sermons as feed, the library as a barn stocked against winter ignorance. The metaphor was irresistible because the logic was identical: minds, like horses, must be fed beforehand by a provident master.

The Shire’s pantry: Modern use has let the quartermaster retire and kept the joke: provender survives wherever abundance is affectionate — farms laying in winter feed, walkers praising pub provender, parents surveying the wreckage of the refrigerator. Tolkien, who knew the cathedral twin prebend as well as any Oxford man, chose the stable-yard twin with a connoisseur’s timing: Bringing up young hobbits took a lot of provender treats toddlers as livestock and parents as quartermasters, the whole heroic labour of the nursery compressed into the oldest supply-word in the language. From an extended Roman hand to a cathedral stall to a French nose-bag to a hobbit pantry, the word has never stopped meaning what its root enacted: something held forth, in advance, so that no mouth — horse, canon, soldier, scholar, or young hobbit at second breakfast — goes unfurnished.
