The history of the word hysterical presents an extraordinary journey across ancient gynaecological mythology, 19th-century psychiatric revolutions, feminist critique, and 20th-century comedic slang.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Ancient Greek Medicine:

  • The etymological foundation traces to the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *ud-tero- (or *ūdero-), meaning "belly, abdomen, or womb" (which also gave Sanskrit udara and Latin uterus).
  • In Ancient Greek, this developed into hystéra (ὑστέρα), the standard anatomical word for the uterus. In the medical treatises of the Hippocratic Corpus (5th–4th centuries BCE) and Plato's Timaeus, physicians posited the theory of the "wandering womb" (hysteria passio). It was believed that if a woman remained unmarried or childless, the womb became dry and light, migrating upward through the body in search of moisture, compressing the diaphragm and lungs, and causing acute suffocation, convulsions, and mental distress.
  • To describe this condition, the Greeks formed the adjective hysterikós (ὑστερικός), literally "suffering in or pertaining to the womb."

2. Transmission into Latin and Renaissance Medicine:

  • The term entered Late Latin as hystericus, primarily in medical commentaries. In the 16th and 17th centuries, Renaissance physicians began translating Greek medical treatises into vernacular European languages.
  • The adjective hysterical first entered recorded English in the 1610s as a technical diagnosis for fits, faints, and suffocation (often termed "the suffocation of the mother" or "fits of the mother," where *mother* was the archaic English word for uterus). Edward Jorden's landmark 1603 treatise, A Briefe Discourse of a Disease Called the Suffocation of the Mother, sought to prove that demonic possession trials were often misunderstood cases of natural *hysterical* illness.

3. The 19th-Century Psychiatric and Psychoanalytic Era:

  • During the 19th century, at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris, the famed neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot conducted extensive clinical studies on hysteria. Charcot demonstrated that hysteria was not caused by an anatomical uterine disorder (even demonstrating its occurrence in male patients), but rather represented a functional neurological affliction of the central nervous system.
  • Charcot's pupil, Sigmund Freud, alongside Josef Breuer, published Studies on Hysteria (1895). Freud formulated the foundational theory of psychoanalysis: that hysterical symptoms (such as psychogenic paralysis or aphonia) were somatic conversions—physical manifestations of repressed psychological trauma and unresolved emotional conflicts.

4. Popularization, Gender Critique, and the Slang Shift:

  • As psychoanalytic concepts saturated popular culture in the early 20th century, hysterical detached from clinical bedsides and became a widespread colloquial term for any display of frenzied panic, wild weeping, or loss of composure. Late 20th-century feminist scholars critiqued the historic use of "hysterical" as a gendered weapon used to delegitimize women’s legitimate anger and intellectual dissent.
  • Concurrently, in mid-20th-century informal American and British English (c. 1930s–1950s), the adjective took a dramatic comedic leap. Because an extreme fit of laughter resembles a convulsive emotional seizure, people began referring to uproarious jokes, shows, and comedians as "hysterical." Today, the word comfortably balances two very different registers: serious emotional breakdown and uproarious comedic brilliance.
