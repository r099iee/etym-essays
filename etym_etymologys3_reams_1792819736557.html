The reams piled beside every printer and parodied in every family letter carry one of English’s most travelled trade words — a bundle that journeyed from Arab paper mills to French warehouses to English counting-houses, gathering sheets as it went.

Arabic bales: The story starts with medieval Arabic rizma, meaning a bale, bundle, or packet — the ordinary warehouse word of merchants handling cloth, grain, and, increasingly, paper. Arab papermaking, learned from Central Asian craftsmen and industrialised in cities like Baghdad and Damascus, made paper a bulk commodity centuries before Europe caught up, and bulk commodities need bundle-words. The verb behind it, razama, meant to gather into a bundle — to truss goods for the caravan. When Mediterranean traders bought paper in quantity, the bundle-word travelled with the bundles.

French mediation: Old French took the Arabic term as rayme or raime, already narrowed to a bundle of paper — proof that the word arrived specifically through the paper trade rather than general commerce. French stationers and printers were Europe’s great paper consumers, and their unit became standard: so many quires to the bundle, so many sheets to the quire. The form shows the usual French softening of the foreign consonants into something a Paris clerk could pronounce.

  • Caravan to counting-house: the bundle-word follows the paper road from the Levant to the Champagne fairs and Paris shops.
  • Narrowing to paper: a general bale-word becomes specifically a paper measure, because paper was the commodity bought in such bundles.
  • Quires within: the ream standardises as twenty quires, each of twenty-four or twenty-five sheets.

English standardisation: Middle English borrowed the French term as reme in the fourteenth century, just as England’s own paper trade was beginning, and the printing press then made the ream indispensable: every edition began as so many reams in the warehouse. The trade distinguished short reams of 480 sheets (twenty quires of twenty-four) from long or printer’s reams of 500 or even 516 sheets, the surplus allowing for spoilage and proofing. Offices, schools, and governments bought paper by the ream for five centuries, and the wrapped white bundle became a visual icon of bureaucracy.

Figurative flood: From the nineteenth century the physical bundle overflowed into metaphor: reams of verse, of evidence, of good advice. Dora Baggins and her half-century of counsel are the perfect emblem — the affectionate excess of words that no one could bear to throw away and no one could quite finish reading. Digital documents have emptied the stationery cupboard, but the metaphor outlives the measure: nobody buys reams any more, yet everybody still drowns in them.

Paperless paradox: The photocopier kept the physical ream alive long after typewriters died — offices into the 2000s still spoke of being out of reams — and then screens abolished the bundle while multiplying the words. Programmers now joke about reams of logs and reams of generated code, text no printer will ever see. The measure has thus completed its journey from merchant’s bale to pure metaphor: nobody weighs the paper, but everybody recognises the weight.
