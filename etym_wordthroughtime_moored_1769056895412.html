moored
- c. 1500 - the verb moor surfaces in English as moren / mouren in unmistakably nautical writing, a word of the water-trade born on the North Sea (with Middle Low German mōren and Middle Dutch māren behind it): to make a vessel fast with cables or ropes to a fixed point.
- c. 1490-16th century - the noun mooring appears ("a place of anchorage, a fastening"), and the verb becomes the plain, workaday word of harbours and creeks, alongside "mooring rope, buoy, post."
- 16th century onward - the word drifts inland and inward: to moor a person, a life, or an idea is to fix it, hold it steady, make it resistant to drift - "moored to the past," "a life moored in habit" - the quiet metaphorical engine of the word's longevity.
- Contemporary - still the general nautical verb for securing a vessel, technical but never arcane, and the only word of its set at home on the water that still permits attachment to a natural object like a tree; the past form moored carries the calm, competent finality of a voyage ended and made safe.
