CONTENTEDLY is an adverb meaning in a manner of quiet, satisfied ease; peacefully pleased with one's present state, without restlessness or craving. To do something contentedly is to do it from a settled sufficiency, wanting nothing more. The word is transparently formed from the adjective contented, which is the past participle of the verb to content, "to satisfy, to make content," built in turn upon the noun and adjective content, "satisfied, pleased," a borrowing from Old French content (attested from the tenth century) that entered Middle English around 1400. Behind Old French content stands Latin contentus, the past participle of continere, "to hold together, to contain," a compound of con- ("together") and tenere ("to hold"). The etymology is precisely revealing: a contented person is one whose desires are held within bounds, contained inside the vessel of the self, nothing spilling over and nothing wanting. Contentment, in the history of the word, is literally the containment of appetite. In the supplied context, from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bilbo Baggins is described as feeling that he could sleep contentedly, his stomach "full and comfortable again," though he would "have liked a loaf and butter better than bits of meat toasted on sticks." The adverb carries the whole emotional register of the moment: deep, bodily satisfaction and safety after the perils of the Misty Mountains, an ease that asks nothing more. Yet the following "though" introduces the characteristic Tolkien comic counterpoint — a contentment that is real but not absolute, a full stomach that still wistfully imagines the finer thing. The tone is gentle, inward, and warm, the word of a creature at peace with itself after danger. The polysemy of the word family is small but worth mapping exactly. The core adverbial sense — in a satisfied, easy manner — is the modern centre of the word. The related adjective contented names an abiding state (a contented person, a contented sigh, a contented cat) and differs from the simple content, which since the sixteenth century has tended to name a settled disposition rather than a passing mood. In psychological and therapeutic usage, to do something contentedly implies a state of acceptance and low stress, the opposite of restlessness, anxiety, or craving; this is not a new sense but a modern, clinical refraction of the same containment metaphor. In colloquial and domestic English the adverb attaches to homely actions — eating contentedly, dozing contentedly, purring contentedly — and carries a faintly self-sufficient, even self-contained, warmth. In rhetorical and ironic contexts contentedly can shade toward complacency when the satisfaction is unexamined or enjoyed at another's expense (one can watch contentedly while others labour), though unlike complacently it never by itself carries the accusation of self-approval; the boundary is drawn precisely by the speaker's attitude to the satisfaction. Senses at a glance:
  • Core adverbial sense: in a quiet, peaceful, satisfied manner, with desire held within bounds. Historical bridge: Latin contentus, "held in check," the containment metaphor.
  • State sense (via adjective contented): marked by abiding, settled satisfaction rather than a momentary pleasure. Bridge: the participle of content (v.), "made satisfied."
  • Psychological / wellbeing register: acting in acceptance and low-arousal contentment, free of craving and anxiety. Bridge: clinical refraction of the same containment metaphor.
  • Domestic / colloquial register: homely satisfactions — sleep, food, warmth, company — done without fuss. Bridge: the word's old association with hearth, sufficiency, and security.
  • Rhetorical / ironic shading: near-complacency when satisfaction is unexamined. Bridge: the Roman moral ideal of the self-contained man (contentus) hardening into smugness.
Register ranges from neutral to slightly literary; the adverb is far less common than the adjective, and in modern prose it is prized precisely for its quietness and its suggestion of settled, unpretentious ease. It collocates with verbs of resting and enjoying — sleep, sigh, purr, eat, smile — and with prepositions of comfort (by the hearth, after a meal, in old age). It is the word of the nursery and the fireside rather than of the court or the battlefield; Bilbo's contentment is the hobbit's characteristic inward virtue, the peace of one who has enough and wants no more, and the irony that Tolkien folds into it is that even the most perfectly contained desire can still long, in the next breath, for the loaf and butter.
