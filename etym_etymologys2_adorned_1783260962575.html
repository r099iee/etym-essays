PIE root *h₂er- / *ar-, "to fit together, to join" (cf. Greek ἁρμονία "fitting, harmony", Latin ars "art", artus "joint", arma "fittings, weapons") ↓ Latin ōrnāre, "to equip, fit out, furnish, decorate" — the denominative verb built on this root (exact formation debated; see note); attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE (Plautus, Cato) ↓ Latin adōrnāre, "to equip, furnish, adorn" — compound of ad- "to, toward" + ōrnāre "to decorate"; attested classical, e.g. Cicero ↓ Latin adōrnātus, "adorned, furnished" — past participle of adōrnāre (also adōrnāmentum "ornament", the source of English ornament) ↓ Old French adorner, "to adorn, prepare, furnish" — inherited from Latin adōrnāre through Vulgar Latin; attested from the 9th–11th centuries ↓ Middle English adornen / adournen, "to adorn, deck, embellish" — entered English c. 1300 (verb adorn recorded from c. 1300) ↓ adorned (past tense and past participle of adorn) — the target word, "decorated or beautified with ornaments" ◄ TARGET DEBATED / NUANCED: The ultimate root of Latin ōrnāre is uncertain. The traditional derivation connects it with the PIE root *h₂er- "to fit together, to join" (the family of Latin ars "art, skill", artus "joint", Greek harmonia "harmony"), interpreting ōrnāre as a denominative "to fit out, equip". Some authorities (e.g. Michiel de Vaan) treat ōrnāre as of obscure or uncertain origin, and an alternative minority view links it with the root of Latin ōrīrī "to rise". The prefix-addition stage (adōrnāre), the Romance and English descent, and the semantics are all securely attested regardless of which base root is accepted.
