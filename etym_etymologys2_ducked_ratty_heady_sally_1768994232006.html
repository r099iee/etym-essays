DUCKED chain:
1. PIE *dʰeu- "to fly about, shake, scatter" — the family is not fully secured; a purely imitative origin is also proposed, and some scholars cite a related *dʰewb- "deep, hollow". Cf. Sanskrit dhū- (धू) "to shake, agitate", dhūma (धूम) "smoke" (the thing that shakes and drifts).
2. Proto-Germanic *dūkaną / *dūkijaną "to duck, dive, stoop".
3. Old English *dūcan "to duck, dive, stoop" — inferred from the bird-name dūce "duck, diver" (the bird that ducks for food).
4. Middle English douken / duken — c. 1300.
5. English duck (verb).
6. English ducked — the preterite/participial form of the note's word.
Note on nuance: cognates kept the water sense (Old High German tuhhan → German tauchen, Middle Dutch dūken → Dutch duiken); English uniquely extended the word from immersion to avoidance. Old Norse dýfa "to dip, immerse" is possibly related but uncertain.

RATTY chain:
1. Stage debated / unknown: the ultimate origin of Germanic "rat" is disputed — either native under PIE *reid- "to scratch, tear, gnaw" (connecting Latin rōdere "to gnaw", whence English rodent, erode, corrode) or a substrate / Wanderwort carried by trade. Stated explicitly as uncertain.
2. Proto-Germanic *rattaz "rat" (the animal-word; possibly the "gnawer" formation).
3. Old English ræt "rat".
4. Middle English ratte.
5. English rat.
6. English rat + -y (adjective suffix from OE -ig) → ratty — 1812 "of or like a rat"; senses in sequence: 1840s "infested with rats", 1867 "shabby, worn", 1880s British "irritable".
Note: the Romance words for "rat" (French rat, Italian ratto, Spanish rata) are borrowings back from Germanic, not independent cognates.

HEADY chain:
1. PIE *kaput- "head" — cf. Latin caput (→ capital, captain, chief, chef, chapter), Sanskrit kapāla "skull, cranium" (→ Hindi kapāl), Tocharian kap. Greek κεφαλή (kephalē) is from a different root (*ghebhel-) and is a sister word, not a cognate.
2. Proto-Germanic *haubudą "head" — from *kaput- by Grimm's Law (k→h, p→b).
3. Old English hēafod "head".
4. Middle English hed / heved.
5. English head.
6. English head + -y (from OE -ig) → Middle English hedi → heady — 14th c. "headstrong, impetuous"; c. 1570 "intoxicating, going to the head".

SALLY chain:
1. PIE *sel- "to spring, jump, leap" — cf. Greek ἅλλεσθαι (hállesthai) "to leap, spring" (s→h), Vedic Sanskrit sarati (सरति) "to run, flow" (root sṛ-).
2. Latin salīre "to leap, spring".
3. Vulgar Latin → Old French saillir "to leap, rush, gush forth".
4. Old French saillie (feminine past participle) "a rushing forth, a sortie".
5. Middle French saillie.
6. English sally — noun 1540s "a sortie"; verb 1550s "to rush out, set out suddenly".
Note: Germanic lost the root *sel- (Germanic "leap" is *hlaupaną: English leap, German laufen), so the English descendants — sally, assault, exult, resilient, insult, result, salient — are all Latin/French loans. Balto-Slavic and Celtic show no secure *sel- reflex.
