The Conquistadores (singular conquistador; anglicized also as conquistadors) were the Spanish soldiers, captains, and adventurers who, under licence from the Castilian Crown, conquered, claimed, and settled large parts of the Americas and the Philippines between the 1490s and the late 1500s — most famously Hernán Cortés in Mexico (1519–1521) and Francisco Pizarro in Peru (1532–1533), but also Balboa, Velázquez, Narváez, de Soto, Coronado, Valdivia, Quesada, and dozens of lesser captains. The Spanish plural conquistadores preserves the original ending -es; English often strips the final -e- to conquistadors. In the source sentence “When the Conquistadores arrived in Mexico…” the word denotes precisely these men as military-technological newcomers encountering Aztec hoe agriculture.

Historically the conquistadores were not a regular national army but self-financing bands (huestes) bound by contract (capitulación), recruited among hidalgos, Extremadurans, Andalusians, artisans, priests, African and Indigenous allies, and acting for God, king, and booty — gold, silver, land, titles, and souls. Their advantages — steel swords and armour, firearms and artillery, horses, war dogs, sailing ships, writing, and, decisively, divisions among Indigenous polities and Old World epidemics — allowed hundreds to topple empires of millions. Their regime installed the encomienda and repartimiento, founded Spanish-American cities (Veracruz, Lima, Santiago), imposed Catholic evangelization, and inaugurated the Columbian Exchange, the transatlantic slave trade’s expansion, and massive demographic collapse. Modern historiography therefore treats “conquistador” as both agent and structure: individual daring and cruelty embedded in Crown law, papal donation, Reconquista ideology, and early capitalism.

By extension conquistadores has broader senses. It can mean Portuguese conquistadores in Brazil and Asia, or any early-modern conquerors by analogy. Adjectivally (“conquistador spirit”) and metaphorically (“corporate conquistadores,” “conquistadores of space”) it denotes ruthless pioneering acquisitiveness. In Spanish the word remains the ordinary plural for conquerors of any era (“los conquistadores romanos”), while in English it is almost exclusively tied to Spanish America and carries a morally ambivalent charge — heroic explorer versus brutal invader — that shifts with historiography, from 19th-century romantic admiration to Black Legend condemnation to contemporary decolonial critique.

  • Spanish conquerors of America (noun, plural, primary): the 16th-century Castilian expeditions. “Cortés and his conquistadores.” The source sense.
  • Individual conqueror type (noun, singular conquistador): “He was a ruthless conquistador.” Abstracts one man as archetype of conquest.
  • Portuguese / other conquerors (noun, extended): “conquistadores of Brazil.” Shares overseas conquest schema; differs in nationality and legal regime.
  • Conquerors in general in Spanish (noun, generic): “Alejandro y sus conquistadores.” In Spanish any conquerors; in English this generic use is rare.
  • Metaphorical pioneers (noun, figurative): “the conquistadores of Silicon Valley.” Shares bold seizure of new domains; differs because conquest is economic or cultural, not military-territorial.
  • Attributive / titular (adjective-like): “conquistador helmet (morion),” “Conquistadores exhibition.” Uses the history as styling or theme.
  • Anglicized spelling variant (noun, orthographic): conquistadors vs. conquistadores. Same referent; former is assimilated English plural, latter preserves Spanish morphology and often a more learned tone.

Grammar: Conquistadores is a masculine plural in Spanish (el conquistador / los conquistadores; feminine conquistadora(s) for women, rarely used historically). English capitalizes it inconsistently — lower-case for the type, capitalized for the historical group. Collocations include “Spanish conquistadores,” “conquistadores and missionaries,” and “age of the conquistadores.” Register in English is historical-literary; in Spanish it is ordinary vocabulary. Choosing the Spanish plural -es rather than -s signals foreign colour and often respect for the source language, which is why textbooks about Mexico prefer “conquistadores” when Aztecs are named alongside.
