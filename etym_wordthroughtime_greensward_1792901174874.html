- Old English — the parts live apart: grēne names the colour of growing things and sweard names skin or rind, including the grassy skin of the earth.
- Middle English — sward narrows toward turf: the rind sense fades and the grassy-surface sense takes over, while green stays the colour of life.
- 16th–17th c. — the compound greensward appears: poets join the two to name smooth lawns and pastoral ground, and the word takes on its literary polish.
- 18th–19th c. — the age of landscape: landscape gardeners lay out real greenswards at great houses while poets from Gray to Tennyson make the word the very sound of English summer.
- 20th c. — fantasy inherits it: Tolkien and his heirs plant greenswards before elven halls and village inns, keeping the word alive wherever an imagined England needs its green carpet.
- Today — bookish but undead: lawn rules speech, yet greensward endures in nature writing, garden history, and fantasy, instantly dyeing any scene green.
