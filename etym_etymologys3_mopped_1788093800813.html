1. Latin mappa — napkin, cloth, and map. The ancestor usually proposed for the English cloth-verb is Latin mappa 'a napkin, a cloth,' attested from the classical period (it appears in Plautus and in imperial writers; the emperor would signal the start of the games by dropping a mappa). The Latin word's own origin is uncertain — the standard suggestion is a borrowing from a Semitic or Punic source (compare Hebrew mappāh, 'cloth,' and the curious afterlife by which modern Hebrew mapa means 'map') — so the pre-Latin chain cannot be reconstructed with confidence; an honest etymology begins at Latin. The same word famously produced English map: in late medieval fashion the mappa mundi ('cloth of the world') was a chart drawn on cloth, and 'map' was clipped from the phrase in the sixteenth century. 'Mop' and 'map,' cleaner and chart, are thus etymological cousins — the same 'cloth' that wiped floors once drew the world, a bridge every dictionary of the language enjoys pointing out and everyday usage has entirely forgotten.

2. From napkin to floor-cloth: a fifteenth-century obscurity. The intermediate history between Latin mappa and English 'mop' is documented only loosely. Middle English has the forms mappe (a piece of cloth), and mop, moppe appear in fifteenth-century texts meaning 'a bundle of yarn or rags used for cleaning'; the word seems to have travelled through the cloth-trade vocabulary of France and the Low Countries (compare Dutch dialectal mop, 'a tuft, wisp of cloth'), and the OED and later dictionaries carefully mark the precise route as uncertain or unknown — 'perhaps from Latin mappa,' 'perhaps influenced by Low German or Dutch' — and honest lexicography must say so explicitly. What is certain: by 1500 English had both the noun (the cleaning bundle) and the verb (to clean or wet-wipe with it, or with any absorbent cloth), and by Shakespeare's time 'to mop one's brow' — wiping sweat — was established idiom. Domestic prose of the period shows the verb in full everyday service, exactly as in the flashcard's scene.

3. The two 'mopped's diverge. Grammar and lexicography meet in an instructive split. The grimace-verb mop ('to make a wry face'), attested from the late sixteenth century and of disputed origin — possibly imitative of a pout, possibly from Dutch moppen 'to make a face' — produced the identical past tense 'mopped,' and Elizabethan writers revelled in the rhyming pair 'mopped and mowed' for monkey-grimacing (Shakespeare's Caliban complains of tormentors who come 'like apes that mow and chatter,' The Tempest). While the cloth-verb thrived, the grimace-verb died out during the eighteenth century; yet it left its trace in the fossil 'mop and mow' that historical dictionaries still print and historical novelists still mine.

4. The weak ending. 'Mopped' is built with the ordinary English weak preterite marker -ed, which descends from the Old English endings -ode/-ede and ultimately from the Proto-Germanic dental suffix *-d- (itself from the PIE root *dʰeh₁- 'to put, place' — the past as 'laid down'). A fifteenth-century verb simply took the standard machinery: mop + -ed. The doubled -p- is a Renaissance spelling-rule reflex, conserving the short vowel before the vowel-initial suffix, and the spelling has been stable since the seventeenth century; the doubling is the reason 'mopped' keeps its short /ɒ/ in RP /mɒpt/.

5. Semantic expansion: from floors to battlefields to reserves. The verb's core — clean with a mop; wipe with cloth — never shifted, but its phrasal extension mop up carried it into new domains. First literal ('mopped up the spilt wine'); then, in twentieth-century military language, 'to clear the remaining enemy': 'mopping-up operations' filled the dispatches of the First and Second World Wars; then economic journalism borrowed it for 'to absorb surplus money,' as central banks mop up excess liquidity (monetary-policy language from the mid-twentieth century onward). The internal logic is constant: the last residue — spilt milk, a defiant pocket of troops, excess cash — is 'mopped up' like a stain. The noun, meanwhile, ran an eccentric side-career: 'mop-headed' people (from the eighteenth century), 'mop-top' (the Beatles' haircut, a 1960s coinage), 'mopboard' (the American baseboard), and the English mop-fair, the hiring fair at which servants once stood with a mop as the badge of their trade.

6. Today. MOPPED is one of the most ordinary words in English — domestic, industrial, nautical, military, financial — with no register colour of its own. Its only curiosities are etymological (the napkin that begot both 'map' and 'mop'), morphological (the doubled -p- preserving /ɒ/), and literary (the quaint 'mopped and mowed'). The flashcard's June scene — a sweating traveller borrowing a silk handkerchief at Elrond's house — sits squarely in the verb's oldest attested use: face-wiping. No word in the language is cleaner at heart, and none has a more surprising paper trail.
