- Irish background (long before English) - smiodar, 'a fragment,' takes the little-diminutive -in to become smidirin, 'a small fragment'
- 1829 - smithereens first appears in English print - a colourful Irish borrowing meaning tiny shards, often in stage-Irish voices
- 1830s-19th c. - the word spreads through novels, humour, and the popular press - Dickens uses it in 1836; the spelling 'smither-' (as if from 'smith') becomes standard by folk-etymology
- Early 20th c. - explosives give the word its glory years - wars, comics, and cinema blow things 'to smithereens,' making it the playful standard for total fragmentation
- Mid-late 20th c. - figurative use booms - plans, reputations, and deals are 'smashed to smithereens' in headlines and sports commentary
- 21st c. - smithereens remains the vivid, informal, faintly funny word for utter shattering - still plural, still Irish at heart, and still the first word writers reach for when fragments must fly
