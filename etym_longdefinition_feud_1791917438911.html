Feud is a noun (and, since the sixteenth century, a verb) denoting a state of lasting enmity between two families, clans, factions, or groups, maintained by reciprocal acts of aggression and typically inherited by the next generation. Its defining features are duration, reciprocity, and collective liability: the quarrel outlives the original insult, each injury demands a return, and the whole kin group is drawn in. In the source context — “the ordeal, however unjust, would end a feud that might otherwise embroil the tribe for generations” — the word names exactly this: a self-perpetuating cycle of vengeance that threatens to consume a whole people across time.

Core sense

  • A blood feud. The classic form: a killing or injury by one family against another initiates an obligation of revenge, which produces a counter-obligation, and so on indefinitely. Anthropologists call this the blood feud or vendetta, and it is found in honour cultures from the Mediterranean to the Caucasus to the Scottish Highlands.
  • A lasting group quarrel. Less extreme but still structured: two lineages, political camps, or business families locked in open hostility over years or decades, with each side interpreting every new event through the old grievance.
  • The underlying state, not a single fight. A feud can be dormant for years and flare again; what makes it a feud is the standing obligation of hostility, not continuous violence. This is why the word collocates with long-running, bitter, old, and ancient.

Legal and historical dimension

In early Germanic law a feud was not simply lawlessness; it was a recognised institution. A wronged kindred could declare enmity, and the ensuing violence was regulated by rules about who might be attacked, when hostilities must be suspended, and what compensation would end the matter. The Old High German Fehde and the Icelandic saga vocabulary of hefnd (revenge) describe a world in which private war was a legal remedy, and in which kings and the Church struggled for centuries to replace it with courts, fines, and the king's peace.

  • Composition and wergild. The alternative to feud was payment: a fixed compensation for injury or death, negotiated or adjudicated. The whole early history of law, as the source passage argues, is the slow substitution of composition for revenge.
  • Peace legislation. The Peace and Truce of God in tenth- and eleventh-century France, the imperial Landfrieden from 1103 onward, and the English king's peace all aimed to outlaw private warfare among subjects.
  • Formal enmity. Medieval and early-modern practice recognised a declaration of feud, a truce, and a formal reconciliation; the feud had a grammar of its own, which is why historians treat it as an institution rather than mere disorder.
  • Survival in custom. Honour-based societies in the Balkans, Corsica, Sardinia, Albania (the kanun), and the Caucasus maintained blood-feud rules into the twentieth century, often with elaborate provisions for mediation and the giving of one's word.

Modern and figurative senses

  • Political feud. A sustained public quarrel between politicians, parties, or factions — “a bitter feud over the leadership” — in which each side seeks advantage and neither forgets an injury.
  • Celebrity and media feud. A running public antagonism between performers, athletes, or public figures, often conducted through interviews and social media. The word lends the dispute epic, tribal weight, which is precisely why publicists use it.
  • Family feud. A quarrel between relatives, especially over inheritance or honour. The phrase was fixed in popular culture by the American television game show Family Feud (1976–), which turned the phrase into light entertainment.
  • Institutional and corporate feud. Rivalry between departments, executives, or companies, conducted through leaks, briefings, and blocking manoeuvres; “the feud between the two founders” is a staple of business journalism.
  • Sporting feud. A personal rivalry between competitors that goes beyond ordinary competition, with bad blood on both sides.

The verb

To feud is to carry on a feud, almost always intransitively and usually in the continuous form: “the two families have feuded for generations”; “feuding neighbours”; “the leaders feuded over the succession”. The verb foregrounds ongoing process rather than a single quarrel, and it typically takes with (the opponent) or over (the object). It is slightly journalistic and can sound melodramatic when applied to minor disagreements.

A different word: feud as “fief”

English also has a homograph feud meaning a piece of land held under the feudal system — a fief, or the tenure by which such land is held. That word descends from Medieval Latin feodum (also feudum), which passed into Old French as fief and into English legal writing as feud and fee. It is unrelated to the blood feud: the one comes from a Germanic root for hostility, the other from a medieval Latin term of land tenure whose own origin is disputed. The overlap is purely accidental, and modern historians usually prefer fief or fee to avoid confusion. The adjective feudal and the nineteenth-century noun feudalism belong to this second word, never to the blood feud.

Connotation, intensity, and collocations

  • Stronger and more structured than quarrel, more personal and reciprocal than conflict, more durable than dispute.
  • Implies honour, memory, and obligation: a feud is inherited, and the participants are bound by loyalty to their side.
  • Common collocations: blood feud, bitter feud, long-running feud, ancient feud, family feud, personal feud, political feud, feud with, feud over, feuding families, feuding factions, end a feud, settle a feud.
  • Register: neutral to literary. In journalism it is often a deliberately dramatic framing; in history and anthropology it is a technical term.
  • It can be used lightly (“a feud over the office coffee machine”), and the mock-heroic effect depends on the word's weight.
