The biography of effused begins with one of the most ancient and most sacred words of the Indo-European world: the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰew-, 'to pour'. Pouring was not an everyday act for the ancestors; it was the mechanism of offering. In Vedic religion the priests poured clarified butter into the sacred fire in the rite called havana, and the root lives on in Sanskrit juhóti 'he pours (a libation)' and huta 'offered'. In Greece the same root produced khéō 'I pour' and the khoē, the drink-offering poured out to the gods and the dead. A libation was, quite literally, an effusion — a pouring out of something valuable in order to give it away. From its very first appearance, then, the concept of pouring carried the weight of sacrifice, generosity, and the release of vital substance. The root reached Latin through the extended form *ǵʰewd-, producing fundō, fundere, 'to pour, shed, melt, cast'. The Romans expanded the image in two directions. On one side they poured molten metal into moulds, and fundere became the technical verb for casting bronze and iron — the origin of our words 'foundry', 'found' (as in casting metal), 'fuse', and 'fusion'. On the other side they poured liquids of every kind — rivers, blood, tears, grain — and here the basic meaning of 'shed' or 'spill' flourished. To this modular verb Latin added directional prefixes to map every possible vector of pouring: infundere 'to pour in', refundere 'to pour back', diffundere 'to pour apart' (whence diffuse), transfundere 'to pour across' (whence transfuse), confundere 'to pour together' (whence confuse), and profundere 'to pour forth' (whence profuse). And combining ex- 'out' with fundere gave effundere, 'to pour out', with its past participle effūsus, 'poured out, spread abroad'. In Latin literature effundere and effūsus developed rich figurative lives. Rivers and seas effunduntur — they overflow their bounds. Armies effunduntur from the city gates. Blood is effused in battle. A spendthrift effundit his patrimony — pours out his inheritance. And an orator or poet effundit words, pouring out speech without restraint. The idea of excess is already present: effūsus can mean 'unrestrained, prodigal, lavish', and the noun effūsiō meant both a literal outpouring and a figure of lavish spending or extravagant joy. The participle carried its full range into Late Latin and into the learned vocabulary of Europe. English borrowed the family directly from Latin, without passing through French. The adjective 'effuse' (spread out, diffuse, with extended senses in conchology describing a shell whose lip spreads outward) came first, in the early sixteenth century, straight from Latin effūsus. The verb 'to effuse', 'to pour out', was formed from the same Latin material and is recorded from the seventeenth century; the past participle effused followed naturally. In the same period English took the noun 'effusion' from Latin effūsiō, and it is in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries that effusion becomes most interesting: it came to mean a literary work written in an overflowing, emotional style — a 'poetical effusion', usually slightly disparaging — and in medicine it became the technical name for fluid escaping into a body cavity, a sense that survives in modern clinical language as pleural or pericardial effusion. The modern career of the verb is a study in elegant fossilization. Everyday English has largely abandoned 'effuse' in favour of 'pour out', 'give off', 'spread', or 'emanate'. But the word survives where generosity of outpouring matters: in poetry and elevated prose describing light, fragrance, warmth, and feeling; in scientific writing describing the outward spread of gases, vapours, or radiation; and, in a deliberately old-fashioned key, in descriptions of people effusing enthusiasm or praise. Its sound — the long, open initial vowel flowing into the soft /zj/ of 'effused' — enacts the meaning: a word that feels liquid in the mouth. The prompt sentence, in which the refreshing winds are effused for the refreshment of all life, sits at the meeting point of the word's two great traditions: the sacred pouring-out of ancient ritual and the modern, generous spreading of vital energy through an atmosphere. That is why effused sounds both ancient and warm — it carries three thousand years of libation in a single verb.
