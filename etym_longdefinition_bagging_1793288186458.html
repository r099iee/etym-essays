Bagging is the gerund and present participle of the verb bag, and it names two linked pictures: the action of putting things into bags, sacks, or sacks of paper and cloth, and the resulting state of hanging loose, full, and slightly sagging like a filled bag itself. Hops are bagged after picking and drying; trousers bag at the knees after long wear; and in the source sentence the threefold reckoning of picking, drying, and bagging prices the whole harvest routine of the hop-ground from bine to pocket.

In the source sentence the word carries its straightforward agricultural and commercial sense: after the hops are picked from the bines and dried in the oast-house, they must be pressed into large sacks or pockets for sale and transport, and that packing stage costs its share of the famous fifty shillings. Cobbett itemises it because every stage of the hop economy was taxed, tolled, and laboured over, and bagging was the moment the airy crop became dense taxable merchandise. The modern reader who knows only shopping bags should picture instead the tall hop-pockets, coarse jute sacks as tall as a man, trodden full by the packer's feet.

Core meaning in detail: As an action noun bagging covers the whole routine of furnishing, filling, and closing bags: providing sacks, pouring in the produce, shaking it down, stitching the mouth, and stacking for the carrier. As a descriptive participle it covers the loose physics of flexible containers: cloth that bellies, knees that pouch, eyes that swell beneath. Both senses share the image of a flexible skin stretched by contents, whether the contents are hops, groceries, or tired flesh, and both remember that bags are defined by hanging.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Packing produce or goods into bags or sacks for storage, sale, or transport (primary commercial sense; the source sense): the bagging of hops, grain, coal, or cement. The bridge is direct composition: bag plus -ing names the trade operation, and agricultural writers from Cobbett onward price it alongside reaping, threshing, and carting.
  • Forming a loose sagging pouch or swelling, hanging full and wrinkled (descriptive sense, from 18th c.): bagging trousers, bagging eyes, sails bagging with wind. The bridge runs from container to shape: what holds like a bag comes to look like a bag, and tailors, sailors, and doctors all borrow the image for cloth, canvas, and skin that bellies under load.
  • Capturing, securing, or killing game and by extension any prize (sporting and slang, 19th c.): bagging a brace of pheasants, bagging a bargain, bagging the best seat. The bridge runs through the game-bag: birds put into the bag become birds secured, and the hunter's boast spreads to shoppers, anglers, and football scorers who bag goals.
  • Taking or stealing something for oneself, pocketing it (colloquial, 20th c.): he bagged the last biscuit. The bridge narrows the capture sense toward the cheeky and domestic: to put something in one's own bag is to claim it, and playground ethics made the verb the standard term for calling dibs.
  • Criticising or mocking someone, putting them in the bag rhetorically (slang, chiefly Australian and New Zealand, 20th c.): to bag someone out. The bridge is rougher: the victim is stuffed into words as game into canvas, shaken and displayed, and the particle out marks the public exposure.
  • The material forming bag-like enclosures in industry (technical): bagging for tea, for cement, for vacuum cleaners, and bagging cloth itself. The bridge returns to the noun: the textile and paper of sacks, graded by strength, becomes a commodity with its own merchants and specifications.
  • Gathering wild produce into a bag while foraging or harvesting (countryside sense): bagging mushrooms, bagging windfalls. The bridge joins harvest and container in a single stroll: the bag carried outward empty returns home full, and the verb celebrates the filling.

Register and connotation: bagging is workmanlike and earthy in its harvest sense, the vocabulary of oasts, mills, and warehouses, but playful and competitive in its sporting and slang senses, where to bag something is to win it with a grin. The sagging sense is gently comic or sympathetic: bagging clothes suggest comfort over fashion, and bagging eyes invite rest. Across all uses the word keeps a soft, bulging, slightly lumpy affection that crisper verbs like packing or capturing never quite manage.

Collocations and grammar: commercial frames run the bagging of plus produce, and picking, drying, and bagging as the canonical harvest trio; descriptive frames run bagging at plus the joint (bagging at the knees, at the elbows); sporting frames run bag a brace of plus game and bag the winner for races and prizes.
