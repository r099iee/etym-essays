Towed — the past tense and past participle of the verb "to tow": pulled or drawn along behind another moving vessel, vehicle, or engine, usually by a rope, cable, chain, or bar. In the Tolkien context, the raft is "towed away round the high shoulder of rock into the little bay of Lake-town": that is, drawn along the water by a rope to a boat that pulls it, out of the river current and into the sheltered bay.

To tow something is to haul it along behind you while you move — the towed thing does not propel itself. The word is used wherever one moving machine or vessel supplies the power for another: a tugboat tows a ship; a breakdown truck tows a broken-down car; a tractor tows a trailer or a plough; an aeroplane tows a glider into the air; a launch tows a dinghy. What all these share is the image of the towline: the rope or coupling that ties the follower to the leader, so that the leader's motion becomes the follower's. That image, and the verb itself, are thousands of years old.

  • Primary sense — to draw behind on water: "to tow" a boat, ship, barge, or raft by a line attached to a moving craft. In the age of sail this was essential: sailing ships could not manoeuvre against wind and tide inside harbours and rivers, so rowing boats and then steam tugs took them in tow. "Towed into harbour", "towed by a tug", "the barge was towed up the canal".
  • Second sense — to draw behind on land: the canal age transferred the technique to dry land: barges were "towed" by horses along towpaths, and the verb generalised to any vehicle drawing another behind it: "the car was towed to a garage", "the caravan was towed behind the Land Rover".
  • Aviation sense: an aircraft may "tow" a glider or a target banner; "aero-towing" launches sailplanes, the towed craft being the passive follower.
  • Figurative senses: "to take someone in tow" (to take charge of and guide someone, as a tug guides a ship); "towed along" of events (carried along helplessly). The idiom preserves the nautical image of the towline.
  • Noun and adjective uses of the same stem: the noun "tow" (the rope), "a tow", "in tow", "a tow-rope", "a tow-truck", "towage" (the service or fee of towing), "towing", "a tow-bar".
  • Distinct homograph: the noun "tow" meaning "hemp or flax fibre" (and "tow-coloured" hair) is a different word of Old English origin, unconnected with pulling; the past forms "towed" and "towing" belong only to the pulling word.

Semantic history in brief: the Old English verb togian meant "to drag, pull, haul" — a plain word of effort. Maritime life specialised it to drawing a vessel by a rope; the canal and road ages carried that specialised sense onto land; and the motor age made "towed" the standard word for a vehicle drawn behind another. The form is completely regular and unremarkable — but the history behind it is one of rope, water, horses, and engines.
