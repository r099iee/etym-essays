- 14th to 17th Century - Flourishes in Middle English and Northern English dialects as *didder*; strictly denotes physical shivering, teeth chattering, or trembling with cold or ague.
- Late 18th Century (c. 1770s) - Undergoes intervocalic consonant softening from [d] to [ð], establishing *dither*; spreads from regional dialect into vernacular English to describe shaking, quivering, or being in a nervous flutter.
- Early to Mid-19th Century - Crosses into literary English as a psychological metaphor for mental vacillation, irresolution, and flustered hesitation; colloquial idioms like "all of a dither" become widespread in Victorian parlance.
- Early 20th Century - Solidifies in political and literary critique as a derogatory term for weak-willed vacillation, bureaucratic paralysis, or symbolist aesthetic hesitation.
- 1940s to 1950s - Adopted by military and control-systems engineers to describe deliberate small-amplitude mechanical vibrations applied to gears to overcome static friction.
- Late 20th Century to Present - Becomes a vital technical term in digital signal processing (DSP), computer graphics, and digital audio mastering, where "dithering" designates the deliberate addition of controlled noise to eliminate quantization distortion and banding.
