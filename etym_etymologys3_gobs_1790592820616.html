The history of gobs is the history of a mouth that became a measure. Unlike learned Latinate words with clean paper trails, gob belongs to the demotic, bodily stratum where Celtic speech, northern dialect, sailors talk, and school slang all churn together. Its etymology is therefore partly reconstructed, partly debated, and essentially oral for centuries before it surfaces confidently in print.

The most widely accepted account begins in Celtic. Old Irish has gop meaning beak or bill, extended to mouth, with congeners in Scottish Gaelic gob and Welsh gop. This points to a Proto-Celtic *gobb- for pointed mouth-part, itself plausibly from a broader Indo-European root for jaw and mouth such as *gebh-, seen in Germanic words for jaw and gabble, or from an imitative complex *gob- / *gab- denoting movement of lips and mouth, as in gab, gobble, gulp. Celticists note the expressive doubling of -bb- typical of nursery and body words, which suggests sound-symbolism reinforced inheritance: the mouth makes a gobbling noise when it takes a gob.

How this Celtic mouth-word entered English is a contact story:

  • Early medieval coexistence: Irish missionaries, Scottish settlers, and Norse-Gaelic contacts carried Gaelic gob into northern English and Scots dialects, where English already had native words like mouth, muzzle, and chops. Gob survived as a vivid, slightly rude outsider synonym.
  • Middle English blending with French: Old French had gobe meaning a mouthful, lump, or poisoned bait, from gober to swallow greedily. Whether this is cognate, borrowed from Celtic, or independently onomatopoeic is debated, but by the 14th century Middle English gobbe, gobet clearly means a lump, fragment, or mouthful of food, as in Chaucerian and culinary texts. A small gobbet of meat is what you gulp in one go. This French-mediated lump sense reinforced the Celtic mouth sense, fusing them: the mouth defines the lump.
  • Early Modern vulgarization: By the 1560s-1600s, gob as mouth is recorded as low slang: hold your gob, shut your gob. It is the organ of eating, spitting, shouting, and kissing, hence coarse and comic. Simultaneously gob as clot, as in gob of spit, gob of phlegm, becomes standard in medical and everyday talk for expectorated matter.

From mouth and lump to sailor and lot, the modern extensions unfold in industrial and imperial England. In mining districts, gob, goaf, or gobbin means waste rock stuffed back into the worked-out cavity, the mined-out mouth filled with rubbish. Miners speak of gobbing up a pit. This industrial sense keeps the idea of shapeless stuffing. In naval ports, particularly in Britain and then America, a sailor becomes a gob, originally a gob-shite in American service slang around World War I, because Jack Tar lives by his mouth: shouting, eating, swearing, singing. The reanalysis from organ to person is typical slang metonymy. By World War II, American newspapers casually refer to gobs on leave, and the plural now means men, not mouthfuls.

The Wodehouse sense, large amounts, emerges from the lump branch in the late 19th century and flowers in the 1910s-1930s. To have gobs of money, gobs of pluck, gobs of cheek, extends the physical image of ladles of porridge or mouthfuls of pudding to abstract qualities. Writers of light fiction, sports pages, and music-hall songs love it because it sounds lavish without being precise, generous without being genteel. P. G. Wodehouse, who soaked up Edwardian public-school, club, and theatrical slang and then exported it to America, uses it as part of his Bertie Wooster idiom: stuff in gobs is moral and nervous energy imagined as pudding. The doubling gobs and gobs in American use further intensifies the sense.

Phonetically, the word barely changes across a millennium: gob remains gob, short vowel, voiced final stop, highly stable because its expressiveness depends on abrupt closure, like blob, knob, lob, sob. Pluralisation to gobs with voiced /z/ follows regular English morphophonology. Semantically, however, it drifts from anatomy to quantity via food, a classic embodiment path: body part to body action to measured portion to indefinite abundance. Modern dictionaries therefore list gob under two headwords that are historically one, and gobs under slang quantifier that presupposes both. The debate about French versus Celtic priority does not alter this trajectory; both sources point to the same mouth-lump nexus, showing how convergent sound-symbolism can repeatedly create gobs wherever mouths gulp.
