- [Late Middle English (14th c.)] - Emergence as the standard southern English 3rd-person singular present indicative inflection ('-eth') of the newly borrowed Norman verb 'moven' (Chaucer, Wycliffe Bible) - Standard literary and vernacular present tense describing physical movement, emotional affection, and judicial motivation.
- [Early Modern English / Tudor Period (16th c.)] - Retained in solemn, religious, liturgical, and legal registers (Tyndale Bible, Book of Common Prayer) while everyday spoken London English increasingly adopted northern '-s' ('moves') - Sacred solemnity, ceremonial gravity, and divine majesty.
- [Jacobean Era (1611)] - Immortalized in the Authorized King James Bible (e.g., Genesis 1:21: 'every living creature that moveth') and Shakespeare's sonnets and high tragedies - Peak of sacred scriptural authority and elevated poetic grace.
- [18th & 19th Century] - Completely superseded in ordinary spoken and written English by 'moves'; restricted exclusively to liturgical prayer, hymnody, deliberate biblical parody, or historicist poetry (Milton, Keats, Tennyson) - Archaic reverence, religious solemnity, and nostalgic romanticism.
- [Contemporary Usage] - Preserved almost exclusively in biblical quotations, liturgical recitations, historical reenactments, and deliberate stylizations evoking King James cadence or mock-antique gravity - Scriptural grandeur, archaic liturgical resonance, and rhetorical elevation.
