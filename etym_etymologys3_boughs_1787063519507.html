The historical evolution of the word bough (plural boughs) illuminates an ancient, intuitive somatic metaphor deeply rooted in the Proto-Indo-European worldview, wherein the human and animal anatomy provided the primary conceptual vocabulary for describing the physical landscape and plant kingdom.

The term descends directly from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European noun *bʰā́ǵʰus, signifying an "arm," "forearm," or the "foreleg/shoulder" of a quadruped. This root generated widespread cognates across virtually every major branch of the Indo-European linguistic family:

  • Indo-Iranian: Sanskrit bāhú- (बाहु, "arm, forearm, foreleg") and Avestan bāzuš ("arm"), which also gave rise to Persian bāzū ("arm, muscle").
  • Hellenic: Ancient Greek pē̂khus (πῆχυς, "forearm, cubit").
  • Germanic: Proto-Germanic *bōguz ("shoulder, upper arm, foreleg; large tree branch").

According to Grimm's Law of regular Germanic consonant shifts, the Proto-Indo-European voiced aspirated stop *bʰ regularly de-aspirated to the voiced bilabial stop *b, while the velar stop *ǵʰ shifted to the velar spirant *g. In the Proto-Germanic dialects of Northern Europe, speakers forged an organic metaphorical extension: just as a powerful shoulder or arm branches outward from the human torso or animal flank, so too does a massive, thick limb branch outward from the central trunk of a tree.

This dual semantic capacity—referring both to animal anatomy and arboreal limbs—was preserved across early Germanic daughter languages. In Old Norse, bógr meant the "shoulder of an animal" as well as the "bow of a ship" (the curved shoulder of a vessel cutting through water). In continental Germanic languages, the anatomical and maritime senses became dominant: Middle Dutch boech (modern Dutch boeg, "bow of a ship") and Old High German buog (modern German Bug, "shoulder of an animal; bow/prow of a vessel, nose of an aircraft"). In German, the arboreal sense was supplanted by Ast and Zweig.

In Anglo-Saxon Britain, however, Old English bōg or bōh (dative singular bōge, nominative/accusative plural bōgas) maintained both senses throughout the Old English period. Old English medical and legal texts used bōg to designate the human shoulder or an animal forequarter (especially in hunting laws detailing butcher cuts), while pastoral and poetic texts used it for the massive limbs of oaks, yews, and ash trees. In Old English poetry, such as the Dream of the Rood, the cross is conceptualized through living arboreal imagery.

During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), the anatomical sense of bough gradually receded as French loanwords (such as arm, shoulder, and joint) consolidated their medical domains, leaving bough (variously spelled bowe, bogh, bowgh) dedicated almost exclusively to large tree branches. The spelling with -gh- represents the historical preservation of the Old English voiced/voiceless velar fricative [ɣ] or [x]. During the Great Vowel Shift and early modern phonological reductions (15th–17th centuries), this velar fricative became vocalized, and the preceding long vowel underwent diphthongization, shifting the pronunciation from Middle English /boːx/ to the modern Received Pronunciation /baʊ/ (plural /baʊz/).

In Renaissance and modern English literature, boughs attained supreme poetic status. William Shakespeare immortalized the word in Sonnet 73 ("Bare ruin'd choirs, where late the sweet birds sang" upon yellow leaves and shaking boughs) and in The Tempest ("Where the bee sucks, there suck I; In a cowslip's bell I lie; There I couch when owls do cry. On the bat's back I do fly After summer merrily. Merrily, merrily shall I live now Under the blossom that hangs on the bough"). It remains a cornerstone of evocative, elevated nature writing.
