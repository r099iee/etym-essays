Shun is a strong, evocative Anglo-Saxon verb meaning to deliberately, persistently, and consciously avoid, ignore, turn away from, or ostracize someone or something out of fear, caution, moral revulsion, religious principle, or social disapproval. Unlike neutral words for physical evasion, shun conveys purposeful intentionality, ethical demarcation, or communal sanction.

The principal senses, contextual domains, and polysemic dimensions of shun include:

  • Interpersonal and Social Ostracism: To systematically refuse social interaction, communication, or association with an individual. When an entire group or community shuns a member, they treat the person as if they do not exist—refusing to speak, shake hands, eat at the same table, or acknowledge their physical presence. This can manifest informally in schoolyards and social cliques, or formally as severe social banishment.
  • Religious Excommunication and Sectarian Discipline: In strict confessional traditions (notably among the Anabaptist Amish, traditional Mennonites, Hutterites, and Jehovah's Witnesses), shunning (known in German as Meidung) is a formal ecclesiastical penalty imposed upon unrepentant sinners or apostates. Members of the religious body are forbidden from dining, conducting business, or maintaining domestic intimacy with the shunned person until repentance and restoration occur.
  • Moral, Ethical, and Philosophical Abstention: To deliberately reject or abstain from practices, vices, worldly temptations, or corrupting influences. A moralist may shun dishonesty; a recovering addict shuns alcohol; an environmentalist shuns wasteful consumption; an ascetic philosopher shuns superficial fame and luxury in pursuit of tranquility.
  • Psychological and Interpersonal Defense: In modern clinical and developmental psychology, shunning (frequently categorized alongside the "silent treatment") is recognized as a potent form of non-physical psychological aggression or relational ostracism. It operates by exploiting the human dread of social abandonment and emotional isolation.
  • Physical or Environmental Avoidance: In literary and naturalistic contexts, shun is applied to animals or sensitive individuals instinctively retreating from perilous conditions, bright light, or predators (e.g., nocturnal creatures that shun the daylight; wild animals that shun human settlements).

Polysemy and Semantic Bridges:

The semantic bridge connecting the ancient Old English sense of visceral bodily terror (shrinking back in horror) to contemporary social ostracism is the physical gesture of "turning away." When an ancient warrior shrank back in horror from a dragon or corpse, the reaction was involuntary panic. Over centuries, the bodily act of turning away was internalized and intellectualized: rather than recoiling from physical terror, one turned away from moral disgrace, heretical theology, or disgraced individuals. Thus, what began as an involuntary physical flinch evolved into a calculated, solemn instrument of social and spiritual exclusion.
