Tails up is a familiar British colloquial exhortation meaning cheer up, keep your spirits and courage high, do not let yourself droop or despair. Said buoyantly as encouragement, it treats low morale as a temporary droop that can be lifted by will and bonhomie, much like a hound lifting its tail when it catches the scent and runs keenly.

Literally the phrase pictures a hunting dog, horse or other animal moving with tail erect - the outward sign of health, energy and eagerness. By metaphor it maps that animal semaphore onto human mood: tails up equals confident, optimistic, on the front foot; tails down equals dejected, cowed, out of sorts. As an imperative (Tails up, Aunt Dahlia!) it functions as phatic encouragement, a clubman's buck up meant to jolly the listener out of gloom without addressing the cause. As a descriptive predicate (to keep one's tails up) it means to maintain morale under pressure and stay resilient.

The tone is hearty, schoolboyish and deliberately superficial - upper-class Edwardian slang of the playing field transferred to the drawing room. It assumes troubles are best met with breeziness, and in Wodehouse it often characterizes Bertie Wooster's well-meaning but inadequate optimism when confronted with aunts, debts and engagements that require Jeevesian strategy rather than slogans. The capital T reflects sentence-initial position, not a proper noun, and the plural tails follows sporting usage for the set of hounds.

  • Imperative encouragement Cheer up! (primary, contextual): Tails up, Aunt Dahlia equals pull yourself together, look on the bright side. Bridge: direct transfer from kennel observation (hound with high tail equals keen) to human pep-talk.
  • Descriptive state in high spirits, confident (extended sense): To be or play or go with tails up means to be confident and aggressive, for example a team with tails up after two wins. Bridge: generalization from momentary order to durable condition and form.
  • Literal animal posture (literal base): A dog, horse or deer physically carrying its tail erect. Bridge: the source image that grounds the metaphor; still the dominant sense in veterinary and hunting writing and kennel reports.
  • Hunting and equestrian jargon (technical nuance): Hounds running with tails up have scent and energy; a horse with tail up is fresh and mettlesome. Bridge: specialized field-sports reading of tail carriage as performance indicator, later borrowed by school and military slang.

In modern use the idiom is dated but instantly intelligible in Britain, surviving alongside chin up, buck up, keep smiling and keep your pecker up. It has no formal, legal or scientific sense, but remains useful as period colour and as a compact lesson in English's habit of moralizing animal bodies: we lift tails, chins and heads to lift hearts, and Wodehouse exploits the bathos when breezy advice meets real financial worry. Learners should hear both the kennel image and the clubman's pat on the back, recognizing that the phrase promises courage by posture rather than by plan.
