surmises — the plural noun and third-person singular verb form of surmise: suppositions, conjectures, or guesses that something may be true when there is no firm evidence to confirm it. As a noun, a surmise is a cautious inference projected beyond the known facts; as a verb, to surmise is to suppose that something is so on slight grounds. The word carries a distinctive literary tint — refined, tentative, and slightly sceptical: a surmise is an idea one entertains without fully trusting it. In Austen's phrase "distant surmises", the guesses are kept at a polite, speculative arm's length.

Core senses

  • Noun — a conjecture without proof: "His surmises about the secret were later proven correct." A surmise is more considered than a random guess yet stops short of a reasoned inference.
  • Verb — to suppose without evidence: "She surmised that they had already left." The subject forms an opinion on clues too thin to settle the question.
  • Plural emphasis: surmises typically denotes a cluster of tentative ideas rather than a single settled theory — conjecture offered piecemeal, as in "distant surmises".

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Historical legal sense: in Anglo-French and Middle English law, a surmise was a formal accusation, charge, or allegation laid upon an accused person before the court — an unproven claim formally lodged. The modern "guess" meaning grew out of this: to surmise is to lay an unproven idea upon a matter, just as a charge was once laid upon a defendant.
  • Suspicion nuance: in some contexts surmise shades into suspicion or doubt of wrongdoing, though usually weaker than outright distrust.
  • Literary / formal register: surmise is now elevated, slightly dated, and rare in everyday speech; "guess", "hunch", "conjecture", and "speculation" have displaced it. It survives strongly in prose and poetry for its precision and elegance, and it is a favourite of novelists describing characters who speculate quietly — "she spoke in surmises", "his surmises grew wilder".
  • Connotation of restraint: because a surmise is offered without insisting on its truth, the word implies intellectual modesty; the speaker holds the idea lightly, aware it may be wrong. This restraint distinguishes it from the confidence of an assumption and the boldness of a theory.
  • Modern technical uses: in statistics and forensic or machine-learning analysis, "surmise" occasionally appears as a technical synonym for a low-confidence inference or model hypothesis, though "conjecture" is the standard term in mathematics and logic.

Historical semantic bridge

Old French surmettre ("to lay upon, accuse, allege") is literally "to send/put over" (sur- "over" + mettre "to put", from Latin mittere "to send"). Its feminine past participle surmise became a noun for the thing "laid upon" someone — a charge or suspicion. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the accusation sense softened into guesswork: what is "laid upon" a matter without proof is a conjecture. The bridge is the shared image of projecting something onto a surface — a charge onto a person, an idea onto a set of facts.
