PIE *plā- 'flat, spread out, broad', with extended form *plāk- 'to be flat; smooth; hence agreeable, to please' (reconstructed) → Latin plācēre 'to please, be acceptable, give satisfaction' (classical Latin, attested 1st c. BCE) → Latin placitum 'that which pleases; decree, judgement, ordinance' (neuter participle of placēre; classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) → Medieval Latin placitum 'a legal suit, action, plea; judicial assembly' (the 'plea' sense hardening in legal Latin, 5th–10th c. CE) → Old French plait / plaid 'lawsuit, agreement, discussion; plea' (11th c.) and the verb plaidier 'to go to law, argue a case, plead' → Anglo-Norman pleder / plaider 'to plead' → Middle English pleden / plede 'to plead, argue a case in court' (attested 13th c.) → Modern English plead 'to make an earnest request or formal legal statement' (legal sense retained, personal-entreaty sense widening from the 15th c.) → present participle pleading + adverbial -ly → pleadingly (adverb, attested from the 16th c.).

Note on the family: the same Latin plācēre base yields English please, pleasant, and pleasure (via Old French plaisir), and placid, placate, complacent, and placebo via other derivatives. The 'please' and 'plea' lines are semantic sisters under one Latin roof: what is pleasing is what is accepted, and a legal plea is what is 'laid before' or presented for acceptance by authority.
