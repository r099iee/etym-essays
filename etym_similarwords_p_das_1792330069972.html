• metrical foot: Similar because both relate poetry to the metaphor of the human or animal foot; differs because a Western metrical foot (like an iamb or dactyl) is a tiny recurring pulse of 2 or 3 syllables within a line, whereas a Sanskrit pāda is an entire quarter-verse line (typically 8, 11, or 12 syllables).
  • hemistich: Similar because both represent structural subdivisions of a verse; differs because a hemistich is an entire half-line (comprising two pādas or sixteen syllables in a śloka), whereas a pāda is a single quarter.
  • quarter-verse: Similar because this is the exact literal English translation of pāda; differs because quarter-verse is a descriptive calque lacking the rich anatomical, devotional, and chapter-division connotations of the Sanskrit original.
  • stich: Similar because both refer to an individual line of poetry; differs because a stich in classical Greek metrics is a complete standalone line, whereas a pāda is strictly understood as one of four co-dependent limbs of an organic stanza.
  • caraṇa: Similar because both literally mean "foot" in Sanskrit and are used synonymously in prosody for a verse quarter; differs because caraṇa frequently carries dynamic connotations of wandering, moral conduct, or an entire Vedic recensional school, whereas pāda is the standard technical term in metrical grammar.
  • canto: Similar because both denote structural subdivisions of a long literary work; differs because a canto is an extensive narrative book or major chapter (equivalent to a sarga), whereas when pāda designates a chapter (as in the Yogasūtras), it refers to one of exactly four foundational quarters of an entire treatise.
  • quadrant: Similar because both describe one-fourth of a geometrical or conceptual whole; differs because quadrant is a mathematical and astronomical term derived from Latin quadrans, whereas pāda retains an organic bodily metaphor.
