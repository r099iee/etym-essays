ROOT WORD 1 — disembark 1. Egyptian bꜣjr / bꜣir "a seagoing vessel" (attested in Egyptian; c. 2nd millennium BCE) — the probable, though debated, ultimate source. 2. Ancient Greek bâris (βᾶρις) "a round-hulled barge, a Nile boat" (Herodotus, 5th c. BCE) — Greek borrowed the Egyptian boat-name. 3. Late Latin / Vulgar Latin barca "a small boat, barge" (attested in Latin glosses; 4th–6th c. CE) — probably from the Greek or from a parallel western Mediterranean source; the form barga "boat" also appears (cf. Gaulish *barga), and the relationship of barca and barga is debated. 4. Old French barque "a boat, a bark" (12th c.) — and the derived verb embarquer "to put aboard a boat" (en- + barque, 12th c.). 5. English embark "to go aboard a ship" (1540s, via French embarquer). 6. English disembark "to go ashore, to leave a ship" — dis- + embark, first recorded c. 1590 in naval and logbook contexts. 7. English DISEMBARK — the form on this card. NOTE: the whole family is a loanword chain with NO reconstructible PIE ancestry; it is a Mediterranean nautical word, and it would be an error to force it under any PIE "carry" root. ROOT WORD 2 — aboard 1. PIE *bʰer-dʰ- "to cut, split" (a debated reconstruction; many modern handbooks instead label the family "of uncertain origin, a North-West-European innovation"; c. 4000 BCE). Note: NOT from PIE *bʰer- "to carry" (the root of English bear) — that is a common error and is rejected here. 2. Proto-Germanic *burdą "board, plank, table" (c. 200 CE), built on the "cut/split" idea — a board as a split-off plank. 3. Old English bord "board, plank, table; the side and deck of a ship; a shield" (attested 8th–11th c.). The ship's side/deck is its "boards," the planked bulwark. 4. Old English phrase on bord "on board (a ship)" — to be on the planked platform of the vessel. 5. Middle English aborde / aboord "on board" (the preposition on reduced to a-, giving a- + bord; attested 14th c.). 6. English ABOARD "on or into a ship" (14th c.), later extended to any conveyance (train, plane, bus) from the 19th c. NOTE: the PIE ancestry of *burdą is contested — older handbooks invoked *bʰer-dʰ- "to cut," modern ones call it an origin-uncertain North-West-European form; neither path reaches into Sanskrit, Greek, or Italic. ROOT WORD 3 — lighter 1. PIE *h₁lengʷʰ- "light (in weight), quick, agile" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of "lightness." 2. Proto-Germanic *linhtaz "light (not heavy)" (c. 200 CE). 3. Old English lēoht "light, not heavy" (attested 8th–11th c.); and the derived verb līhtan "to make light, to relieve, to lighten." 4. Middle English lighten "to make lighter, to relieve" (11th–15th c.). 5. English lighten + agent suffix -er → lighter (noun) "one who or that which lightens" — specifically, from the 1480s, "a barge that lightens a deep-draft ship of part of its load." 6. English LIGHTER "a flat-bottomed transfer barge" (1480s), and by metaphorical extension the pocket flame device "a lighter" (early 20th c., named for making light). 7. The same lighter is also the comparative adjective "more light" — formally identical, semantically distinct; the context disambiguates. NOTE: the reconstruction *h₁lengʷʰ- with the laryngeal is standard; the root also produced Latin levis "light" (whence lever, levity, alleviate) and Sanskrit laghú- "light, quick" (whence Hindi halkā "light," via Prakrit). ROOT WORD 4 — chattels 1. PIE *kap-ut- "head" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of "head," in Italic and Germanic. 2. Latin caput "head" (gen. capitis; classical, 1st c. BCE) — the head as the chief or principal thing. 3. Latin capitalis "of the head; chief, principal" — extended to money: Late Latin capitale "principal sum, wealth, property" (4th–6th c. CE), the "chief" goods. 4. Old French chatel (Parisian palatalized ca- → cha-) "property, goods, wealth" (10th–12th c.). 5. Middle English chatel "property, goods" (13th–14th c.), usually in the plural. 6. English chattel (plural CHATTELS) "movable personal property" (14th c.), the legal term; and the DOUBLET cattle (from Anglo-Norman catel, which kept the hard ca-) "livestock, property" — two English forms from one Old French word. NOTE: the Indo-Iranian and Hellenic reflexes of the "head" root are disputed or different (Greek kephalē is of uncertain origin; Sanskrit has no secure *kap-ut- reflex), so no Indic or Greek node is forced here. ROOT WORD 5 — bight 1. PIE *bʰewgʰ- "to bend, to curve" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of bending. 2. Proto-Germanic *buhtiz "a bend, a curve, a bay" (c. 200 CE), a derived noun from the bend-root. 3. Old English byht "a bend, a corner, a curve; a bay" (attested 8th–11th c., e.g. in place-names and prose). 4. Middle English bight "a bend, a bay, a corner" (11th–15th c.). 5. English BIGHT "a wide shallow curve in a coastline; the loop of a rope" (14th c. onward). NOTE: the homophone root PIE *bʰewg- "to flee" (whence Latin fugere, English fugitive, refuge) is a DIFFERENT root — only accidental similarity of spelling; the two must not be merged. The bend-root also produced English bow "to bend," bough "a bent arm/branch," and elbow (OE elnboga "forearm-bend").
