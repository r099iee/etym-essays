- Ancient Greece — the battle-wall: phálanx names the locked-shield infantry block, the pride of hoplite warfare and the engine of Macedonian conquest.
- Rome and after — the historian's word: Polybius and Livy analyse the phalanx versus the legion; the formation dies but the word survives in military treatises.
- 1550s — English adopts it: Renaissance soldiers and scholars revive phalanx for pike-blocks that echo the ancient formation.
- 17th c. — anatomy borrows it: physicians name the finger and toe bones phalanges, little ranks of bone-soldiers.
- 19th c. — utopians march in it: Fourier's socialist communes call themselves phalanxes, battle-order turned to brotherhood.
- Today — metaphor reigns: phalanxes of lawyers, cameras, and riot police fill the newspapers, while Tolkien's swans show the word's undimmed poetic power.
