The biography of curtly is a story of the blade. At the head of the family stands the Proto-Indo-European root *sker-, "to cut," one of the most productive roots in the entire Indo-European language family, and one equipped with an s-mobile — it appears sometimes as *sker- and sometimes, with the leading s- dropped, as *ker- or *kert-, without any difference of meaning. Its Indo-Iranian branch kept the literal sense: Sanskrit kṛntáti, "he cuts, cuts off," and from Sanskrit kṛttá, "cut," descends Hindi kāṭnā, "to cut," with its Odia counterpart kāṭibā — the same blade still active in the modern vernaculars of India. Greek keírō, "I cut, shear, clip," served the wool-clipping and hair-cutting of the ancient world, while the Celtic branch produced Old Irish scaraim, "I separate, cut off." The Germanic branch gave *skurtaz, "cut off, short," from which come Old English sceort, our short, German kurz, and the family of garments that are literally "cut" — shirt and, via Old Norse skyrta, skirt. In Italy the root yielded Latin curtus, "cut short, shortened, docked, maimed" — a word used of a docked tail, a stunted plant, a maimed limb, and, tellingly, of speech, for the Romans already applied curtus to words and replies clipped of their length. From curtus came the verb curto, "to shorten," and it is from this family that the English adjective arrived. The OED records curt entering English around 1620, borrowed directly from Latin or adopted through Middle French court, "short" — the same French word that gave English curtail in the sixteenth century, via courtault, "short-tailed," later reshaped by folk-etymology against the English word tail. The adverbial form curtly followed in the seventeenth century, at first meaning simply "in brief terms." The history of the word since then is the history of the root's edge asserting itself. Latin curtus had meant not merely short but docked, cut off, damaged — the word of the clipped and the maimed — and that harsh edge slowly coloured the English adjective and adverb. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, curt and curtly hardened from neutral brevity into deliberate brevity: speech deliberately clipped of politeness and padding, brevity that carries impatience and dismissal. Modern English thus possesses a remarkable doublet: short and curt, both descended from the same cutting root, yet entirely different in tone — short is the neutral measure of length, while curt carries the blade. (A caution against false friends: curtsy and courtesy come from a different Latin root, cohors, "courtyard" — the behaviour proper to the court — and are unrelated to curt, a fact that hides an irony, since a curt reply is precisely the opposite of courtesy.) Today curtly fuses brevity with dismissiveness: it is never merely brief, but pointedly, rudely brief — a sentence snapped off, trimmed to the stump, communicating that the speaker will not waste words on the obvious. That is the Bellman's manner in the passage: his "rather curtly" agrees in two words and then moves at once to action, the ancient blade of *sker- still cutting the discussion short as the storm approaches. The word's social history is complete — from the Roman word for a docked tail, to a neutral seventeenth-century adverb of brevity, to a modern sign of impatience and dismissal — and yet the original cutting never leaves it: to speak curtly is still, after four thousand years of the root's life, to cut one's words to the stump.
