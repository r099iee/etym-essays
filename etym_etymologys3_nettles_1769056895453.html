The story of nettle begins, not with pain, but with rope. The plant's stems and leaves are packed with tough bast fibres that, once dried, soaked, and combed, yield a flax-like thread strong enough for cordage and coarse cloth. Precisely because it was a plant for binding, the first Germanic speakers who named it reached for the Indo-European root *ned-, "to twist, to bind together," and built a word meaning, in effect, "the little binder" — *ned-tlō, using the old instrumental suffix *-tlō that elsewhere forms tools and purpose-nouns. The plant was named for its utility, not for its sting: for thousands of years the nettle was harvested and woven, and the historical irony is that the same fibrous stems humans reached for are also the ones that stung them. From Proto-Indo-European *ned- the root travelled into every major daughter family, and its fortunes split. In Italic it yielded Latin nōdus, "knot" — literally "the tied thing" — along with nassa, a woven wicker fish-trap; from nōdus English later borrowed node, nodule, and noose (through Old French nous). In Germanic it produced both *natilōn, the nettle plant, and *natją, the fishing net, which survives in English net, netting, and network. In Celtic and Indo-Iranian a parallel variant *nedh- (with an extra consonant) gave Old Irish nascaid, "to bind," and Vedic Sanskrit nahyáti, "binds, ties," with its participle naddhá, "bound." Everywhere the semantics stayed with tying: knots, nets, bonds, fetters, cords. Then the northern plant took over the story. Proto-Germanic *natilōn became Old English netele, Middle English netel, and finally nettle, while identical siblings were born right across the Germanic world: Old Saxon netila, Middle Dutch netele (Dutch netel), Old High German nezzila (beside nazza; German Nessel), and Old Norse nǫtra, the ancestor of Danish nælde and Swedish nässla. The word's sound shape changed with the great Germanic and English sound-shifts — the trisyllable netele reduced to the disyllable netel, and the final unstressed -e was eventually lost — but the referent never moved: from the earliest records to the present, the word has named the same stinging herb. The semantic drift happened later and entirely on English soil. Around the 16th century the noun grew a verb: to nettle came to mean "to irritate or provoke," because the plant's chemical sting was the perfect image for a sharp, personal provocation — to be "nettled" is to smart with annoyance as if stung. The adjective nettlesome ("irritating, prickly") and the participial nettled both descend from this figurative transfer, and in the same period the word began attaching itself to medicine: nettle-rash became the everyday name for the skin eruption that medical Latin called urticaria, from urtica, "nettle," itself from urere, "to burn." Literary and proverbial uses completed the word's career. The proverb "to grasp the nettle" — seize a difficulty boldly, since a nettle gripped firmly stings far less than one brushed lightly — turns the plant's sting into a figure of courage and is recorded in English from the 15th century. Writers from Chaucer's age onward used "nettled" for the quick smart of wounded vanity, and the plant itself appears in Shakespeare's Henry IV, Part 1 ("out of this nettle, danger, we pluck this flower, safety") as the emblem of rough things that can be turned to good. The modern age has brought a striking reversal. Where the Victorian town imagined the nettle as a nuisance weed, the twenty-first century has rediscovered its uses: nettle soup and nettle tea, herbal remedies for hay fever and joint pain, sustainable nettle fibre for cloth (the practice of the Bronze Age brought up to date), and nettle water in the garden. The word now carries both its oldest meaning — the useful, binding fibre-plant of the Germanic forest — and its newest one — the fashionable, wholesome ingredient of foraging culture — with the sharp, stinging verb sense still alive in between. Very few words keep a thousand years of history so compactly in a single syllable: the plant that stings and the plant that binds were, from the very beginning, one and the same, and nettle has never needed to choose between them.
