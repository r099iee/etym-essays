- 1300s–1500s - Emerged in Middle English to designate forged iron and steel chest defenses of knights during the transition from chainmail shirts to full articulated plate harness.
- 1500s–1650s - Adopted in Early Modern English Biblical translations (Tyndale, Geneva Bible, King James Bible) for the High Priest's sacred Hoshen and spiritual armor ("breastplate of faith", "breastplate of righteousness"), imbuing the word with profound moral and religious resonance.
- 1700s–1800s - Shifted in military terminology to the distinctive polished steel cuirasses of heavy shock cavalry (cuirassiers) and extended technically into equestrian saddlery tack and comparative zoology (plastrons of turtles).
- 1900s–Present - Primarily utilized in historical, archaeological, fantasy, theological, and figurative contexts denoting protective psychological, institutional, or moral barriers.
