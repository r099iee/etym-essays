- [19th-century origin] - 'To trail one's coat' comes from the literal custom of dragging a coat along the ground behind one as a dare, inviting passers-by to tread on it and so start a fight — a challenge expressed by an object rather than a word.
- [Victorian and Edwardian idiom] - The phrase becomes a stock expression for ostentatiously seeking a quarrel, with an insolent, theatrical and often lighthearted flavour rather than genuine malice; it is a public, performative dare issued to any comer.
- [Modern figurative use] - The literal gesture is forgotten and the idiom is now almost always figurative: to bait opponents or provoke controversy on purpose, with more sport and ego than real hostility — the speaker trailing their coat 'about minor controversies' rather than matters of substance.
