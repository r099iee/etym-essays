The historical evolution of thoroughfares represents a splendid showcase of Germanic morphological compounding. It demonstrates how two foundational Old English elements—one expressing spatial penetration (through) and the other denoting motion and journeying (fare)—synthesized into an essential concept of municipal jurisprudence, civil engineering, and urban life.

1. Linguistic Roots of the First Element: *Thorough* (*Through*)

The initial element traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *terh₂- (zero-grade *tr̥h₂-), signifying "to cross over, pass through, overcome, or pierce." This ancient root generated diverse progeny across the Indo-European languages, including Sanskrit tirás ("through, across"), Latin trans ("across, beyond") and intra- ("within"), Celtic *trei, and Old Irish tré ("through").

In Proto-Germanic, it shifted via Grimm's Law to *þurx ("through"). In Old English, it appeared as the preposition and adverb þurh (often vocalized with an epenthetic vowel as þuruh). In Old and Middle English, an accentual split occurred:

  • When unstressed, the vowel was reduced, yielding the preposition through.
  • When bearing full stress, the epenthetic vowel was retained and lengthened, yielding thorough (meaning complete, exhaustive, passing completely from start to finish).

2. Linguistic Roots of the Second Element: *Fare*

The second constituent stems from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *per- (o-grade *por-), meaning "to lead across, traverse, pass, or go on a journey." This root produced Ancient Greek poros (πόρος, "ford, passageway, journey," source of pore), Latin portus ("harbor, port"), and Slavic parom ("ferry").

In Proto-Germanic, it gave rise to the strong verb *faraną ("to travel, go, migrate") and the related feminine noun *farō ("journey, passage, course"). In Old English, faran was one of the primary verbs of motion (cognate with modern German fahren, "to drive, ride"). The noun faru denoted a journey, expedition, or course of travel (surviving today in compounds like wayfarer, farewell, fieldfare, and fare as passenger payment for transit).

3. Middle English Synthesis and the Genesis of the Compound

During the Middle English period (circa late 14th century), the compound thurgh-fare (also spelled thurghe-fare or thorowfare) was forged. In its earliest recorded attestations in late 14th-century texts (including Geoffrey Chaucer’s The Knight's Tale, c. 1386), the word was employed metaphorically and abstractly:

In The Knight's Tale, Egeus consoles Theseus with the solemn philosophical reflection on human mortality: "This world nys but a thurghfare ful of wo, / And we been pilgrymes, passynge to and fro" (lines 2847–2848). Here, Chaucer uses thurghfare to denote an open, temporary passageway—a place through which mortals merely travel on their spiritual pilgrimage, never finding a permanent dwelling.

4. Legal Codification and Urban Infrastructure

By the 15th and 16th centuries, the abstract meaning of "unimpeded passage" shifted metonymically to the physical road itself. In Tudor and Elizabethan England, the expansion of commercial carriage transit and turnpike routes necessitated clear legal definitions of highways. A thoroughfare was formally codified in English common law as a public road or street that was open at both ends, connecting two established highways without obstruction.

This legal status distinguished public thoroughfares from private ways, dead ends, and easements. Municipal authorities erected signs stating "NO THOROUGHFARE" to notify travelers that a passage was a cul-de-sac or private property where no common right of transit existed. In 18th-century London, as urban density exploded, thoroughfares became the commercial lifeblood of the metropolis, bustling with stagecoaches, hawkers, and post-chaises.

5. 19th-Century Urbanism, Haussmannization, and Modern Metaphor

The Industrial Revolution fundamentally altered the scale and character of thoroughfares. Medieval cities possessed narrow, winding streets inadequate for mass traffic and sanitation. In the mid-19th century, Emperor Napoleon III and Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann dramatically remodeled Paris by slicing broad, straight grands boulevards through crowded quarters, establishing the modern concept of urban arterial thoroughfares.

Victorian essayists and novelists (such as Charles Dickens in Bleak House and Edgar Allan Poe in The Man of the Crowd) immortalized the frantic energy of metropolitan thoroughfares, portraying them as surging rivers of humanity. In the 20th and 21st centuries, the plural thoroughfares transitioned seamlessly into modern infrastructure discourse—applied to multi-lane urban highways, trans-oceanic shipping corridors, and metaphorical information highways that carry global internet traffic.
