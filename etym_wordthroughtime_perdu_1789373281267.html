- 11th to 14th Century - Emerges in Old French as the past participle of *perdre* (from Latin *perdere*); signifies being ruined, damned, killed in battle, or spiritually forfeit in early chivalric romances and Catholic homilies.
- 15th to 16th Century - Develops specialized military applications across European battlefields; denotes scouts posted in hazardous positions (*sentinelles perdues*) and the vanguard squad assigned to storm fortifications (*enfants perdus*).
- 17th Century (Elizabethan and Stuart England) - Borrowed directly into English military and literary idiom; writers such as William Shakespeare (*King Lear*) and Ben Jonson employ *perdu* for a desperate sentinel or someone lying in stealthy concealment.
- 18th to 19th Century - Flourishes across Victorian English literature (Sir Walter Scott, Charles Dickens, William Thackeray) as the set phrase *to lie perdu* (to remain in hiding or incognito); in French civil life, it establishes everyday institutional usages such as *objets perdus*.
- 1913 to 1927 - Marcel Proust publishes *À la recherche du temps perdu*, forever canonizing *perdu* in world philosophy, psychology, and modernist literature as the supreme symbol of temporal transience and the quest for memory.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Retains global literary recognition as the defining adjective of Proustian aesthetics, while continuing in French as the primary everyday word for lost objects, disoriented travelers, and unusable architectural spaces.
