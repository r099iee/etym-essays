Core sense in context. In the reading sentence — the dwarves beating on the secret door in the Mountain — thrust is the past tense of the verb "to thrust": they pushed at it, they thrust and pushed. The word is unusually neat because its past form is identical to its present: thrust, thrust, thrust — an invariant form that makes the verb feel old and rugged, though the history behind that invariance is oddly modern (see etymology).

The verb.

  • To push suddenly and forcibly: to drive something forward with a sharp, direct effort — "he thrust his hands into his pockets", "she thrust the letter at him". The emphasis is on abruptness and direction: a thrust is a push with a point and a purpose, not a gradual pressure.
  • To stab or pierce: to drive a pointed weapon into someone or something with a forward motion — a sword-thrust, to thrust a dagger home, to thrust someone through. In fencing, the "thrust" is the straight attack with the point (as opposed to the cut with the edge).
  • To press or force in / upon: "to thrust oneself forward" = to push one's way in, to intrude presumptuously; "to thrust something upon someone" = to impose it, to force it on them — the figurative pressure of the same physical motion.
  • To project: "to thrust out" = to jut, to protrude (a cliff thrusting into the sea) — the static result of having been pushed outward; the past participle does this duty: "a head thrust forward", "the dock thrust into the harbour".

The noun.

  • A violent push: the physical act or its force — "with one thrust he forced the door open", a knife-thrust.
  • A lunge or stab: the fencing attack with the point — "a thrust and a parry"; the literary sword-fight's vocabulary.
  • A military attack: a concentrated offensive push — "the enemy's thrust towards the river", "a deep thrust into enemy territory". In warfare the word describes an advance that drives fast and deep — the weapon metaphor: the point of the attack.
  • The essential direction or point: "the thrust of the argument is that..." — the main line, the driving force of a case, a report or a policy. The idea is the pressure that pushes the whole forward: what the argument is driving at.
  • Engineering and science: the propulsive force that drives a rocket, a jet or a propeller forward (thrust-to-weight ratio, thrust vectoring, maximum thrust); in machines, the axial force that presses along a shaft (thrust bearing, thrust washer, end thrust).
  • Geology: a thrust fault is a fracture along which one rock mass is pushed up and over another — the great overthrusts of mountain building (the Moine Thrust, the Lewis Thrust), named with the human verb of pushing.
  • Architecture and structures: the outward (lateral) force of an arch or roof is its "thrust", which the abutments and walls must resist — the visible physics of masonry.

The semantic bridges. Every sense is the same physical event — force applied suddenly and point-first — carried through metaphor from the body to the weapon, the battlefield, the courtroom, the engine and the earth's crust. The invariant past (thrust/thrust/thrust) is a historical accident: the word came into English from Old Norse þrysta, a weak verb, and English levelled its forms (“thrusted” survives only as a rare, old-fashioned variant); the result — a verb that refuses to change — now reads as ancient strength, though it is really the product of simple levelling, not of ancient pattern. In the context sentence the form does double duty: "they thrust and pushed" is past tense, exactly parallel to "they beat" and "they implored" — three preterites in a row, one of them invariant.
