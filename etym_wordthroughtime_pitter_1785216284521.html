- [16th c., within the old formula] - Appears inside 'pit-a-pat' / 'pitapat', an onomatopoeic adverb for a quick trembling beat, chiefly of the heart; as yet hardly a free-standing noun.
- [18th–19th c., the pair crystallizes] - 'Pitter-patter' becomes the standard reduplicative pairing for rain, small feet, and pulses, and 'pitter' gains a semi-independent life as the pair's lighter, more delicate member.
- [19th–20th c., the heart's whisper] - Increasingly used of the racing heart, 'pitter' connotes something tiny, rapid, and tremulous — a small creature's chest, fear made childlike rather than dreadful.
- [Contemporary sense] - Lives almost wholly in the company of 'patter' as the softer half of the double-beat: smaller, faster, more delicate — almost a whisper of a beat, at once anxious and tender.
