- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed into English from Old French *refuge* (from Latin *refugium*), originally designating spiritual harbor in God or the legal privilege of ecclesiastical sanctuary within church walls.
- 16th to 17th Century (Early Modern English) - Expanded beyond church altars to describe military bastions, wilderness hiding places, and fortified strongholds during the English Civil War and continental religious conflicts.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Augustan Age) - Acquired celebrated moral, political, and rhetorical weight, notably in Samuel Johnson's famous 1775 aphorism calling patriotism "the last refuge of a scoundrel," establishing refuge as an excuse or disguise for moral failure.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely deployed in philanthropic urban reform (night refuges for the homeless, animal refuges) and satirical literature, preeminently in Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888) defining hard work as "the refuge of people who have nothing whatever to do."
- 20th to 21st Century - Formally codified in international humanitarian jurisprudence (the 1951 Geneva Refugee Convention); integrated into evolutionary biology (glacial refugia) and urban transport engineering (pedestrian refuges).
