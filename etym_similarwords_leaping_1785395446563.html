• Jumping: Similar because both mean propelling oneself off the ground; differs because jumping is the neutral, generic term with no implication of distance, height, or grace, whereas leaping implies a longer, higher, more dramatic trajectory.
  • Hopping: Similar because both involve leaving the ground in small or repeated springs; differs because hopping is small, quick, and done on one or both feet, the movement of a bird or a child with a stone in its shoe, covering little ground.
  • Bouncing: Similar because both describe an up-and-down, rebounding motion; differs because bouncing emphasises elastic rebound from a surface, like a ball or a car on a rough road, not the single directed flight of a leap.
  • Springing: Similar because both denote a sudden forceful bound into the air; differs because springing suggests a controlled muscular release, a deliberate coiling and uncoiling, often of a pouncing or attacking animal, rather than a long dramatic trajectory at speed.
  • Vaulting: Similar because both mean clearing a height or distance; differs because vaulting implies clearing an obstacle with the aid of hands, a pole, or a support, whereas leaping uses only the body's own momentum.
  • Pouncing: Similar because both involve a sudden airborne movement; differs because pouncing is a downward strike onto prey or a target, an attack ending in a catch, while leaping is a general flight through the air.
  • Bounding: Similar because both describe energetic, long-strided, airborne progress; differs because bounding emphasises the springy, bouncing rhythm of repeated long steps, whereas leaping centres on a single powerful vault.
  • Capering: Similar because both are playful, energetic airborne movement; differs because capering is frolicsome, skittish, and purposeless — a lamb or a fool capers — whereas leaping can be athletic, urgent, or as desperate as the burning Warg's to-and-fro.
