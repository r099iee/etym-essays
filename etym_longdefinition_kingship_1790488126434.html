kingship means the state, office, power, and art of being a king — the dignity a king holds, the territory he rules, the period he reigns, and the ideal of how he ought to rule. In the source sentence — my contemplation of existent kingship was a very distant one — young Ruskin, bred on Homer and Scott to think kings work harder than anyone, looks at the actual thrones of 1820s Europe and finds them remote, irrelevant, almost theoretical. He has an idea of true kingship (labouring, selfless, paternal) and sees little of it in existing courts. The word thus carries both fact (there are kings) and judgment (they fail at kingship).

The richness of kingship lies in this double pull between institution and ideal. Lawyers speak of succession to kingship, historians of the nature of early-medieval kingship, theologians of Christ's kingship. In each case kingship is more than kings: it is the system, aura, and obligation surrounding them. To abolish kingship is to abolish monarchy itself; to fail at kingship is to be a bad king while still a king.

  • Office and rank sense (core legal sense): the position of king, kingly dignity; e.g. to assume kingship, to claim kingship, succession to kingship. Here kingship parallels presidency, chairmanship, bishopric: a post with rights and revenues. The bridge from person to office is suffix -ship (state of being), as in friendship, lordship.
  • Reign and rule sense: the period or exercise of rule; e.g. during his kingship, a long and peaceful kingship. Less common than reign but more solemn, stressing legitimacy rather than duration. The bridge is metonymy: office stands for its time.
  • Dominion and kingdom sense (archaic, biblical): the realm ruled; e.g. to enlarge his kingship, the kingship of Israel. The King James Bible uses kingship for territory as well as office. The bridge is container for contained: rule stands for land ruled.
  • Ideal-art sense (Ruskin's and Carlyle's sense): true kingliness, the duty to govern hard and give much; e.g. to understand kingship, to fail at kingship, the ethics of kingship. Ruskin contrasts Homeric kingship (kings do most and get least) with modern kingship (superior persons govern less and get more). Carlyle's lectures On Heroes make kingship the highest heroism. The bridge is moralisation: office becomes vocation.
  • Theological sense: divine kingship: Christ as King, God as King; e.g. the kingship of Christ, universal kingship. Feast of Christ the King celebrates it. Here kingship is cosmic authority, merciful and absolute. The bridge is analogy: earthly rule images heavenly rule.
  • Anthropological sense: sacral kingship: the theory that early kings were priest-magicians embodying fertility; e.g. Frazer's Golden Bough on kingship and sacrifice. Scholars debate Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and African kingship as ritual burdens where the king must die for the land. The bridge is comparative: all monarchies share sacred residue.

Connotation shifts with politics. To royalists, kingship glows — continuity, fatherhood, splendour. To republicans, it grates — privilege, mystification, waste. Ruskin holds both: he reveres ideal kingship and pities existent kingship as distant and decayed. Modern writers often prefer monarchy for the system and kingship for the mystique, keeping the old word for poetry and theory.

To master kingship is to hold office, time, land, ideal, heaven, and ritual in one noun — exactly why a child could contemplate it distantly yet judge it severely.
