Single root word: scowled (from the English verb scowl). Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *skeu- (also cited as *keu- / *sku-), "to cover, conceal, hide, enclose" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Vedic Sanskrit skunāti "he covers," Ancient Greek skytos "hide, leather," Latin cutis "skin" and scutum "shield." 2. Proto-Germanic derivative, probably *skūhaną or *skul- (exact form uncertain), "to hide, lurk, look out from under cover; to squint" — reconstructed Proto-Germanic era, c. 500 BCE–c. 200 CE. NOTE: this intermediate stage is not securely attested; its shape is inferred from the later Scandinavian forms and is debated. 3. Scandinavian verb: Old Norse–area skula, Middle Danish skule, Norwegian skula, "to cast down the eyes, look askance, squint, lower the brows" — attested in medieval and early-modern Scandinavian, 13th–16th c.; modern Danish and Norwegian skule retain the sense. 4. Middle English scowlen (also scoulen, skoulen), "to look with the brows drawn together, to frown sullenly, to lower the eyes" — c. 1300, borrowed into northern and East-Midland English through Anglo-Norse contact (see also scowre, skulken/skulk). 5. Modern English scowl (verb), "to contract the brows in anger or displeasure; to frown threateningly" — attested from the 16th c. onward, used by Shakespeare ("she scowl'd and frowned") in dramatic register. 6. English scowl + regular past-tense / past-participle suffix -ed —> scowled, "frowned in an angry or bad-tempered way" — the inflected form is attested from the earliest use of the verb. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The OED classes scowl as "of Scandinavian origin," and the deep Indo-European link to *skeu- "to cover" (via the idea of looking out from beneath a lowered/shadowing brow) is plausible but not certain. Some lexicographers hold that the Scandinavian source verb itself is of obscure origin and cannot be traced securely to PIE. The related English verb skulk (from Old Norse skulka) and the noun sky (from *skeu-) support the same cover-based family but do not settle the question. Where a stage is unknown, it is stated explicitly above rather than invented.
