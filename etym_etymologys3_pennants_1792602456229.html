1. Dual Indo-European lineages: flight (*peth₂-) and hanging suspension (*(s)pen-). The etymological genesis of pennants represents a fascinating linguistic hybridization—the convergence and fusion of two separate Indo-European lexical roots that entered the English maritime and heraldic lexicon via Anglo-Norman French. The first root is *peth₂- / *pet-n-, denoting "to fly, to rush forward," which gave rise to names for wings and feathers across the Indo-European dialects, including Sanskrit patra- ("wing, feather, leaf"), Ancient Greek pterón ("wing"), and Latin penna ("feather, wing"). The second root is *(s)pen-d- / *(s)pen-, meaning "to draw, to stretch, to spin thread, to hang," which produced Greek pénesthai ("to toil at the loom"), Latin pendēre ("to hang down, be suspended"), and Germanic derivatives like English spin and span. These two independent conceptual streams—the fluttering feather of flight and the suspended hanging cord—eventually fused in the English naval crucible.

2. The chivalric branch: Latin penna to medieval pennon. In Classical Latin, penna (and its variant pinna) denoted a feather or wing, but quickly expanded into military architecture to describe the battlements, crenellations, or "wings" of a fortress wall (whence English pinnacle). In late antiquity and the early Middle Ages, as Roman cavalry adopted small feather-like streamers attached to spearheads to gauge wind direction, the term developed in Old French into penon (first recorded in the 11th-century Chanson de Roland). In medieval chivalric warfare, the pennon was a small, pointed or swallow-tailed flag borne immediately beneath the lance head of a bachelor knight. It bore the personal heraldic arms of the knight and served as a tactical rally point in the chaos of battle. Geoffrey Chaucer incorporated the word into Middle English in The Knight's Tale (c. 1385): "And by his baner born is his penoun / Of gold ful riche." In castle architecture, larger versions of these heraldic streamers were mounted upon stone turrets and watchtowers to signal the lord's presence and proclaim feudal sovereignty.

3. The maritime branch: Latin pendēre to nautical pendant. Simultaneously, the Latin verb pendēre ("to hang") developed through Old French into the present participle pendant ("hanging down, suspended"). In early English seafaring and dockyard rigging (15th and 16th centuries), a pendant became an essential piece of standing and running rigging: a short length of sturdy rope or wire cable spliced to a masthead or yardarm with an eye or pulley block at its end, used to hoist heavy cargo, secure braces, or anchor a vessel. Seafarers also applied pendant to any long, tapering streamer of bunting that "hung down" in trailing folds from the highest mast truck when the wind subsided.

4. The 17th-century naval blend: the birth of pennant. During the late Tudor and early Stuart periods (c. 1600–1630s), as England expanded its fighting fleet into a permanent professional Royal Navy, the two separate nautical words—pennon (the triangular, feather-like flag) and pendant (the hanging tackle or streamer)—inevitably clashed and assimilated in the vernacular speech of English sailors. Seafarers blended the nasal body of pennon with the excrescent dental suffix -t of pendant, giving birth to the hybrid phonetic form pennant. For more than two centuries, British Admiralty records maintained an idiosyncratic orthographic duality: naval orders conservatively insisted on writing the word as pendant, while naval officers, sailors, and civilian dockworkers universally pronounced it /'penənt/ and increasingly spelled it phonetically as pennant in informal ship logs.

5. The commissioning pennant and naval sovereignty. In the 17th and 18th centuries, the pennant became the supreme emblem of sovereign naval authority. Legend recounts that during the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654), Dutch Admiral Maarten Tromp lashed a broom to his masthead to boast that he would sweep the English from the seas; in response, English Admiral Robert Blake hoisted a long, thin whip (a streaming coach-whip pennant) to signify that he would whip the Dutch fleet off the ocean. While apocryphal, this legend cemented the commissioning pennant as the sacred mark of a naval warship, distinguishing an armed state vessel under a commissioned officer from a private merchantman—a tradition vigorously maintained by modern navies worldwide today.

6. The American sporting revolution: baseball and the pennant race. In the late 19th century, the word embarked on an extraordinary cultural migration across the Atlantic. In post-Civil War America, as organized professional baseball coalesced into the National League (founded 1876) and American League (founded 1901), winning clubs were awarded a large, tapered triangular felt flag known as the pennant to fly from their centerfield flagpoles. By the 1880s, American sportswriters had popularized the phrases "winning the pennant" and the "pennant race" to describe the dramatic, season-long campaign to capture the league championship and advance to the World Series. Today, pennants occupies a rich, multi-layered register: from the fluttering streamers atop medieval fortress towers and modern warship mastheads to the colorful felt flags waving in packed baseball stadiums and the triangular patterns on financial stock charts.
