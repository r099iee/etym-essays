Echoic origin: rustle is a sound-imitative (onomatopoeic) word: it has no recoverable PIE ancestor because it was coined by echoing the sound it names. Middle English rustelen is attested from the 14th century meaning "to make a soft crackling or whispering sound" (of leaves, grass, silk, paper, wings). The OED and Etymonline class it as imitative, with formal cousins across Germanic that are independent but parallel echoic creations: Middle Low German ruschen, Middle Dutch ruuschen, German rauschen ("to rustle, to rush, to whisper"), Swedish forms in rusk-, and related words. These are not proof of a shared Proto-Germanic verb with a defined meaning so much as evidence that Germanic speakers heard the same sound and built the same word from it — the classic signature of sound symbolism. An exact earlier etymology (a Germanic root with a frequentative suffix) is sometimes reconstructed, but the safest statement is that the word is echoic and appears in English by the 14th century.

Phonology: the form is stable: Middle English rustelen (a frequentative formation in -elen, i.e. -le, giving a repeated-action colouring) → Modern English rustle /ˈrʌsl̩/. The cluster -stl- simplifies in pronunciation to /sl/, giving the familiar disyllable with a syllabic final l. The vowel has stayed short and back. The word's sound — a dry, repeated fricative cluster — is itself the imitation of dry matter rubbing together, so the form and meaning have been mutually reinforcing for seven centuries.

Semantic history:

  • 14th century: rustelen = "to make a soft crackling sound" (leaves, dry matter, silk, paper). The noun "rustle" follows for the sound itself.
  • 16th-17th centuries: a second strand emerges: "to rustle" = "to move about briskly, busily, with commotion" — from the sound of energetic movement to the movement itself. The word begins to describe human bustle as well as leaves.
  • 18th-19th centuries: in American English "to rustle" develops "to gather, prepare, or secure quickly and resourcefully" — "to rustle up a meal," "to rustle in" (to get busy). This is the bridge to the theft sense: energetic, improvised gathering.
  • c. 1880s, American West: "to rustle" = "to steal cattle or horses, to round up and drive off livestock." The "rustler" — originally just a busy, energetic person (a rustler of work) — becomes specifically a cattle thief, romanticised in dime novels, westerns, and film as a daring outlaw of the range. The semantic jump is from "to move energetically and gather things" to "to gather other people's animals quietly and illegally."
  • 20th-21st centuries: the sound-sense remains primary and universal ("the rustle of leaves," "a rustle of interest in the crowd"); "rustle up" remains a homely colloquialism; "cattle rustling" survives as a crime in rural jurisdictions and as a genre staple in western media.

Two words or one? Dictionaries treat the sound-verb and the theft-verb as the same word with diverged senses, though some modern reference works give the theft sense its own entry (rustle v.2) because the connection is no longer felt by speakers. Historically they are one: the busier you are, the more you rustle — and a person who "rustles up" cattle for himself may end up rustling someone else's. The two senses coexist in one sound.

Cultural profile: "rustle" is unusual in pairing one of the gentlest words in English — the soft dry murmur of leaves and silk — with one of the boldest images of the American frontier, the cattle thief on horseback. In the Tolkien context, "by a trick of the echoes of the tunnel hear the rustle of the whispering voices of the others just outside," the word carries its oldest sense: the faint, dry, almost inaudible sound of moving and speaking, heard through stone — an echoic word doing its primal job of giving voice to the almost-soundless.
