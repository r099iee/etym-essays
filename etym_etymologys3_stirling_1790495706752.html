Stirling names a rock older than speech and a town older than record, and its language belongs to the tangled Brittonic-Gaelic-English succession in the Forth valley. The crag was fortified in prehistory; Romans built the Gask frontier road to its north and a fort at Camelon; Picts held the carse; Northumbrians pressed from the south; Gaels pressed from the west. Yet the name first enters writing only with David I's charters of the 1120s-1140s: Strivelin (royal castle, mint, and burgh), capella de Strivelin, vicecomes de Strivelin. The forms show three syllables with middle v (Strive-lin), later reduced to two (Stir-ling). No contemporary gloss explains it, and unlike transparent burgh-names such as Edinburgh or Roxburgh it contains no recognisable burgh element.

Scholars therefore divide honestly. The Brittonic party notes Old Welsh ystrev- (to strive, contend) and Cornish strevia (to strive), proposing *ystrevelin — place of contention, battlefield — attractively describing the Forth crossing but suspiciously convenient, and without exact Welsh parallel for the -elin ending. The Gaelic party notes sruth (stream), srib? (stride?), linn (pool, as in Linlithgow), proposing srib-linn or sruth-linn — pool in the river, crossing-place — phonologically plausible (sr- to str- by English ears, -linn to -lin) and topographically apt for the meandering Forth below the rock, but again without a Gaelic record of that exact compound. A third view treats the second element as the common Brittonic place-suffix *-lin (lake, pool, as in Dublin dark pool) attached to an unknown river-word *striv-. All authorities — Watson, Nicolaisen, Taylor — conclude with an obelus: origin uncertain, probably Celtic, not Old English, not Norse. The present essay follows that verdict rather than inventing a PIE root, as the protocol requires.

  • Phonetic path from Strivelin to Stirling (12th-16th c.): Strivelin /strɪvəlɪn/ loses middle v by lenition before l (Strilin, 14th c.), metathesises vowel and r in Scots speech (Stirling for Striling), and fixes spelling with -ing on analogy with English place-names in -ing (Reading, Stirling). The change of v to nothing and of e to i is regular Scots reduction in unstressed syllables. Southern Englishmen write Sterling (whence confusion with the coinage, which comes from Old English steorra, star, plus -ling, and is unrelated).
  • Royal and burghal fixation (12th-14th c.): David I makes Stirling castle, mint, and burgh; William the Lion and the Alexanders hold court there; Wallace and Bruce make it the prize of wars. The name migrates from rock to castle to town to shire: the constable of Stirling, the shire of Stirling, the bridge of Stirling. Charters stabilise the spelling while never glossing it.
  • Scott's Snowdoun overlay (1810): The Lady of the Lake renames the castle Snowdoun — James Fitz-James, the Knight of Snowdoun — borrowing a word that belongs properly to Arthurian romance (Snowdon in Wales) and to older Scots prophecy (When the cock crows at Snowdoun). Scott's note claims manuscript authority; antiquaries have never found it. The invention succeeds poetically: for a century tourists ask for Snowdoun and guides point to Stirling.
  • Empty-castle to heritage city (1830s-present): Ruskin's boy finds Stirling without its knight; Victorian railways bring crowds to find the same absence picturesquely; the army keeps the castle as barracks until 1964; Historic Environment Scotland restores James V's palace and rehangs the Stirling Heads; the city regains city-status in 2002. The opaque name now denotes centrality itself: Stirling, the gateway.

Pronunciation today is RP /ˈstɜːlɪŋ/ (Scots /ˈstɪrlɪŋ/ with trilled r). The spelling with ir records the metathesis; the final g is pronounced /ŋ/ as in all -ing places. No native sound law beyond Scots reduction applies because the pre-12th-century form is unknown; to trace it to PIE would be guesswork.
