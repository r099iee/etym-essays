- Late 14th–15th Century - Entered Middle English via medical treatises from Latin *strictura*, denoting physical compression, tight bandaging, or the binding of wounds.
- 16th–17th Century - Specialised in anatomical and surgical pathology to signify an abnormal narrowing, constriction, or blockage of a bodily duct, canal, or vessel (such as the urethra or esophagus).
- 17th Century - Shifted metaphorically into philosophical, moral, and legal discourses to mean a restrictive limitation, binding condition, or regulatory curb imposed on human conduct or thought.
- 18th Century (Augustan Age) - Semantic bifurcation into literary criticism; came to mean adverse critical remarks, severe strictures/commentary, or sharp fault-finding analyses of a text or character (e.g. "passing strictures upon modern manners").
- 19th–20th Century - Co-existed stably across dual domains: in literary/essayistic prose as sharp critique ("literary strictures"), and in socio-economic discourse as structural limitations ("budgetary strictures").
- 21st Century - Continues in high-register literary criticism and editorial commentary as intellectual restraint or severe critique, alongside its unchanged precise medical-surgical application.
