Covetous descends from one of the oldest moral vocabularies in Indo-European: the desire to have. Its ultimate root is PIE *kup- to boil with desire, to yearn, which also yields Sanskrit kup- to be angry or agitated, and Latin cupere to desire, wish, long for. Latin already moralized the field: cupidus could be neutrally eager (cupidus discendi, eager to learn) or blameworthily greedy, and cupiditas became the theological term for inordinate desire, set against caritas love.

Late and Vulgar Latin created a frequentative/intensive *cupidietare or *cupiditare, to keep desiring, to hanker after, unattested in classical texts but demanded by Romance outcomes. Old French regularized this as coveitier / covoitier (modern convoiter), with the characteristic Gallo-Romance voicing and diphthongization of stressed vowels. From the verb came the adjective coveitous: full of coveting, grasping. Anglo-Norman carried both verb (coveten) and adjective (coveitous) into England after 1066.

Middle English coveitous / covetous (c.1300, Cursor Mundi, Rolle, Chaucer) belongs to the Seven Deadly Sins system. Avaritia in Latin becomes coveitise in English, and the covetous man is a stock figure: the miser, the usurer, the hoarder. The form fluctuated — covetise, covetous, coveitous — until Caxton and later the Authorized Version fixed covetous with -ous on the model of plenteous, beauteous. The u spelling reflects the French ou compressed to English u.

Phonetically the journey shows: Latin cu- /ku/ → Old French co-/cov- /kov/, loss of intervocalic d in cupid- → cove-, and English stress shift to the first syllable COV-et-ous /ˈkʌvɪtəs/. The t that looks like part of -ous actually belongs to the verbal stem covet- plus adjectival -ous (covet + -ous), unlike cupidus + -osus directly.

Semantically the crucial narrowing happened in English Bible translation. The Tenth Commandment — Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, wife, ox, ass — made covet mean not any desire but illicit, envious desire for what belongs to another. Covetous therefore never means merely desirous or ambitious; it means graspingly, enviously desirous, with hands already imagined closing around the thing. Dickens's catalogue — squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous — exploits exactly this: covetous as the summary moral adjective after a string of grasping participles describing Scrooge. It is the sin made character.

Later usage distinctions:

  • Theological vs legal: divines use covetousness for avarice generally; lawyers use covet narrowly for intent to acquire another's property.
  • Covetous vs greedy vs avaricious: greedy wants to consume (food, praise), avaricious wants to hoard (money), covetous wants what is another's (neighbour's goods). Covetous uniquely contains envy.
  • Modern softening: advertising copy now says coveted award, most-coveted handbag almost positively, bleaching the sin into desirability. But covetous as a person-descriptor resists bleaching and remains condemnatory; no one calls a philanthropist covetous.

Literary attestations are dense: Chaucer's Pardoner on covetise, Langland's Covetousness in Piers Plowman, Shakespeare's covetous Jew and covetous knights, Bunyan's Mr. Hold-the-World, and above all Dickens's Scrooge as the covetous old sinner. Each era re-dresses the same figure: medieval hoarder of coin, early modern encloser of commons, Victorian miser icing his office.

Debate note: the exact Vulgar Latin reconstruction (*cupidietare vs *cupiditare vs *convoitire with prefix) is debated among Romanists, but all agree the base is Latin cupidus from cupere from PIE *kup-. The English -ous re-formation on covet is not disputed.
