CONSULS: AN EVOLUTION ESSAY THE LATIN NAME AND ITS MEANING The story of "consul" begins with a Latin verb, "consulere", "to deliberate, take counsel, consult". The Romans themselves understood the connection: both Cicero and Livy derive the name "consul" from "consulere" - the consul was the man who consulted the Senate and the people, who took counsel for the commonwealth. The word is formed with the prefix "con-" ("together") and a base whose Indo-European origin is debated: the most favoured proposal links it to PIE *sel- "to take, seize, grasp", so that "consulere" would literally mean "to take (counsel) together", while an alternative school connects it with "salus" "health, well-being", giving "to take care for". Neither etymology is certain, and this deep-rooted uncertainty is itself part of the word's history: like so many political terms, "consul" was a coinage of civic life whose pre-historic past is only dimly visible. THE OFFICE AND THE REPUBLIC The consulship was the supreme magistracy of the Roman Republic. According to the traditional chronology, the office was created in 509 BCE, in the same year as the expulsion of the kings, and it was designed as a republican substitute for monarchy: two consuls were elected each year, each holding the highest civil and military command (imperium), each able to veto the other, each limited to a single year. The dual, annual, accountable character of the office was the very definition of republican government - power shared, limited, and returned to the people. The consuls presided over the Senate, commanded the armies in major wars, and gave their names to the year ("in the consulship of Gaius and Marcus"). Latin literature is full of the word: Plautus and Ennius already use it in the third and second centuries BCE, and the republican political vocabulary - "consulship" ("consulatus"), "proconsul" (a former consul governing a province), "consular" - grew around it. Gibbon's phrase "the active emulation of the consuls" points to a real feature of the system: because the prize was so high and the term so short, the competition for the consulship was the driving engine of aristocratic ambition and, with it, of Roman military expansion. THE END OF THE REPUBLIC AND THE IMPERIAL AGE With the fall of the Republic the consulship changed character but not name. Under the emperors the office became an honorific held for a few months rather than a full year, and the emperor himself often held it; the "consuls" remained the nominal heads of the state, giving their names to the year and lending their prestige to the regime, but real power had migrated to the princeps. Yet the word kept its republican echo: the consulship remained the supreme dignity of the Roman political order, and writers like Tacitus and Suetonius still describe its workings. The institution survived in the western empire into late antiquity and in the eastern (Byzantine) empire until the sixth century, when it was finally abandoned - but the name did not die with the office. THE MEDIEVAL REVIVAL The eleventh and twelfth centuries produced a remarkable second life for the word. As the cities of Italy and southern France threw off feudal overlordship and established self-governing communes, they revived the vocabulary of Roman republican self-government, and their chief magistrates were called "consuls" (Italian "console", Old French "conseil"). Pisa, Genoa, Lucca, Arles, and dozens of other communes were governed by bodies of consuls, and the word thereby reacquired its ancient meaning of "chief civic magistrate" - not by direct continuity with Rome, but by deliberate cultural imitation of the Roman Republic. This medieval civic sense is the bridge to the modern meaning: the merchants and city-states of the Mediterranean, trading overseas, appointed resident officials to manage their commercial communities in foreign ports, and these officials, like the magistrates at home, were called "consuls". THE MODERN DIPLOMATIC SENSE From the medieval Mediterranean trading world the modern diplomatic "consul" was born. From the sixteenth century onward the great powers of Europe stationed consuls in foreign ports and capitals to protect their merchants, oversee trade, and assist their nationals; the institution spread worldwide in the nineteenth century with the expansion of commerce and empire. Today a consul is an official of a state's foreign service, ranking below an ambassador, who handles commercial affairs, visas, and the welfare of citizens abroad, working out of a "consulate" with "consular" officers. The word has thus completed a double journey: from Roman republican magistrate to medieval commune-magistrate to modern commercial diplomat, with the two most recent stages both drawing, consciously and unconsciously, on the ancient republican prestige of the title. THE WORD IN ENGLISH AND IN GIBBON English borrowed "consul" in the fourteenth century, using it first for the Roman magistrate in translations of historical and biblical literature, and then for the modern diplomatic official from the sixteenth century onward. By the time Gibbon wrote The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire in 1776, the word had two fully established senses: the historical Roman chief magistrate and the modern commercial diplomat. In the source passage the meaning is unambiguously the first - the "consuls" are the republican executives whose competitive ambition, combined with the Senate's policy and the people's martial enthusiasm, produced Rome's principal conquests. The plural is used generically of the successive pairs of annual magistrates across the centuries of the Republic, and "active emulation" captures the competitive drive that the office institutionalised.
  • 509 BCE (traditional): the consulship is founded with the Republic.
  • 3rd-2nd c. BCE: Latin "consul" is attested (Plautus, Ennius); the name derives from "consulere".
  • Late Republic: the consulship is the apex of the cursus honorum and the engine of ambition.
  • Under the emperors: the office becomes an honorific; real power moves to the princeps.
  • 11th-12th c.: Italian and Provencal communes revive the title "consul" for their magistrates.
  • Mediterranean trade: resident officials abroad are called "consuls".
  • 16th c.: the modern diplomatic "consul" appears in English usage.
  • Modern: "consul" is a commercial diplomat; "consulate" and "consular" are standard.
CONCLUSION The history of "consuls" is the history of a political word that survived the fall of its own world. Born from a Latin verb meaning "to deliberate together", the name named the two magistrates who embodied republican self-government; it became a symbol of civic freedom when the Italian communes revived it in the Middle Ages; and it was adapted again to name the officials who managed commerce and community abroad. In Gibbon's sentence the word carries the full weight of republican Rome - the paired executives, the competition for power, the machinery of conquest - and it shows how a single title could travel from the Roman Forum to the medieval commune to the modern consulate without losing the core idea of deliberating and governing together.
