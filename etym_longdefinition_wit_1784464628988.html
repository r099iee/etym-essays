WIT (noun). Primary sense: the capacity for intelligent, quick, and often amusing mental activity; mental sharpness or inventive intelligence; the ability to perceive and express in an unexpectedly clever or comical way. By extension it also names a person who possesses such brilliance ("a great wit"), and in the plural (wits) it denotes the mental faculties themselves, composure, or soundness of mind. Polysemy — all senses:
  • Mental faculties and sanity (plural "wits") — the five wits (the senses); "to be out of one's wits", "to scare the wits out of someone", "to keep one's wits about one", "at one's wits' end", "to collect one's wits". Here the plural denotes the whole apparatus of perception and reason, treated as something that can be scattered, collected, lost, or regained. This is the oldest living sense and the one active in Tolkien's line about collecting one's wits after a fright.
  • Intelligence and understanding (singular) — "a man of wit", "mother wit" (native shrewdness); the plain intellectual faculty as such. Partly archaic, retained in phrases like "wit and wisdom" and in the fossil verb "to wit".
  • Clever humour, verbal brilliance — "a quick wit", "a witty remark", "witticism". This is the dominant modern sense: the ability to make brilliant, surprising, pointed connections, especially in speech.
  • A person of wit — "the wits of the age"; in Restoration comedy the "wit" is the verbally dextrous, brilliant character who wins by repartee.
  • "To wit" (verb, archaic and legal) — "that is to say; namely", from the infinitive of Old English witan "to know"; survives in formal and legal English ("the defendant, to wit, John Smith").
  • The five wits (medieval psychology) — a technical medieval sense distinguishing the five outward senses from the inner faculties (common sense, imagination, fantasy, estimation, memory); the source of the idiom "the five wits" and of treating wits as collectible parts of the self.
Compounds and derivatives: witty, wittiness, witticism, witless, witlessly, half-wit, dimwit, nitwit, quick-witted, slow-witted, unwitting, outwit, witless. Related by the same root but through different routes: wise, wisdom, witness, wist (obsolete "I know"), and (through the Frankish verb "to show the way") the verb guide. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges: In Old English witt (from witan "to know", from the PIE root *weid- "to see; to know") meant the knowing faculty itself — the mind as the organ of knowledge. Three bridges produced the modern split. (1) By the later medieval period the plural "wits" had become the standard word for the senses and mental faculties, so that losing one's wits meant losing one's mental grip; the phrase "at one's wits' end" is fixed in English by the psalm translations. (2) In the 16th–17th centuries "wit" narrowed to mean readiness and cleverness of mind, and then, under Restoration and Augustan influence, to verbal dexterity and comic brilliance — the age of Dryden, the coffee-house wits, and the Spectator's essays on true and false wit. (3) The plural preserved the older faculty sense in idiom, so that today the word splits between "punchline" (singular) and "composure" (plural). Tolkien's Bilbo, sitting down to "collect my wits", uses the plural, faculty sense: he does not want a joke, he wants his scattered mental powers gathered back.
