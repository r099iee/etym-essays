• Pinned an ear: Similar because both involve rotating and angling an ear backward against the horse's head; differs because pinning an ear (or pinning both ears) is an intense, overt display of aggression, hostility, or deep anger indicating an imminent bite or kick, whereas "dropped an ear" is a subtle, mild, cautious, or auditory-monitoring posture.
  • Flattened an ear: Similar because both involve pressing the ear close to the neck or skull; differs because flattening implies deliberate tension and hostility across the facial muscles, while dropping an ear can occur through simple muscular relaxation, selective listening, or mild wariness.
  • Cocked an ear: Similar because both denote an asymmetrical, intentional adjustment of a single ear to gather auditory information; differs because cocking an ear implies raising, angling, or pricking it upward with keen curiosity and alert focus, whereas dropping an ear involves lowering or tilting it downward and backward.
  • Swiveled an ear: Similar because both describe the independent 180-degree rotation of an equine ear; differs because swiveling is a purely mechanical, neutral description of rotation across any axis, while dropping an ear specifically denotes a downward, resting, or cautious orientation.
  • Drooped an ear: Similar because both describe an ear hanging downward; differs because drooping conveys limpness, exhaustion, sickness, or deep sleepiness where the ear lacks muscle tone entirely, whereas "dropped an ear" is an active, responsive behavioral reaction to an immediate event or sound.
  • Pricked an ear: Similar because both are expressive equine ear movements; differs because pricking an ear means thrusting both ears stiffly upright and forward in acute alarm, keen interest, or excitement—the exact opposite spatial direction of dropping an ear.
  • Turned an ear: Similar because both indicate reorienting the ear canal toward a sound; differs because turning an ear is a general acoustic orientation (also widely used for human listening), whereas dropping an ear carries specific equine ethological connotations of mood and herd dynamics.
  • Tilted an ear: Similar because both involve an angled ear posture; differs because tilting is a gentle, neutral geometric deviation, whereas dropping an ear carries emotional undertones of wariness, irritation, or calculated indifference.
