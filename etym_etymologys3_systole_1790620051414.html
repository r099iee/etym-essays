Systole enters English as a learned medical borrowing of Greek systolē, itself a noun of action from systellein, “to draw together,” compounded of syn-, “together,” and stellein, “to put, send, draw.” The deep root is PIE *stel-, “to put, stand, set,” a widespread root whose reflexes also supply English stall, still, stool, and Greek stēlē, “standing stone.” The semantic kernel throughout is placing things so that they stand, and by extension drawing them into place — hence contraction.

In Hippocratic writings and decisively in Galen (2nd c. CE), systolē and diastolē became the canonical pair for the pulse: diastole the dilation of heart and arteries, systole their drawing-together. Galen’s physiology, transmitted through Late Latin medical compendia, preserved both the cardiac sense and a parallel grammatical sense in which systole meant shortening a naturally long syllable — the same image of compression transferred from flesh to speech.

  • Greek medicine to Late Latin: Galenic texts Latinized as systole keep both the cardiological and prosodic senses intact; medieval manuscripts copy the pair systole / diastole as standard pulse vocabulary.
  • Renaissance anatomy (16th c.): Vesalius, Harvey’s predecessors, and then Harvey himself (1628) reanimate the terms during the discovery of circulation; English physicians write of the heart’s “systole and diastole” while debating whether arteries actively contract or are merely filled.
  • Early Modern English borrowing: first English attestations in medical dictionaries and anatomy translations treat systole as an unanglicized technical noun, pronounced with a long final -e marking its Greek origin, and almost never appearing without diastole.
  • Phonetic path: Greek upsilon and spelling y pass through Latin into English as /ɪ/; stress remains initial; the final -ē becomes English /iː/ rather than silencing, preserving the learned shape and signalling foreign technical status.
  • 19th-century instrumentation: the sphygmograph, sphygmomanometer, and later the ECG give systole a number — systolic pressure — shifting it from a felt pulse to a measured peak on a trace.
  • 20th-century cardiology: atrial versus ventricular systole, systole as an electromechanical interval (QRS to end of T), systolic heart failure as failure to eject; the adjective systolic becomes far commoner in speech than the noun.
  • Literary migration: Coleridge, Yeats, and later critics seize the diastole–systole pair as the perfect figure for imagination’s expansion and contraction, which is exactly the use in your context: two modes of reading as the heartbeat of reading itself.

The remarkable stability of systole — same paired meaning for two millennia — reflects the stability of its referent: every human heart contracts. What changed is precision and metaphorical range, from Galen’s finger on the wrist to echocardiographic ejection fractions and to poetics in which attention itself is said to beat.
