Beady (/ˈbiː.di/) is an adjective meaning small, round, bright, and shiny like a bead, and above all — in its most frequent modern use — describing eyes that are small, dark, and glittering, often with a sharp, watchful, or greedy quality. In the reading context the old thrush watches with beady eyes, meaning tiny, bright, alert eyes fixed on the ground below.

Primary senses

  • Of eyes: small, round, and bright — beady eyes is the dominant collocation. Applied to people it suggests cunning, suspicion, greed, or intense watchfulness (the shrewd merchant, the miser, the spy); applied to birds and small animals it is simply accurate description of their small bright glossy eyes. From it derives beady-eyed, sharp-eyed and covetous.
  • Of objects: resembling beads — small, globular, shiny, like drops of liquid — beads of sweat, beady drops of dew. The literal sense covered in or made of beads is also attested and historically earlier.

Secondary and extended meanings

  • Adorned with beads: literally decorated or sewn with beads — a beady bag, beady embroidery, beady trimming. This literal sense is now rare and mostly historical or craft vocabulary.
  • Moisture and drops: forming into bead-shaped droplets — beady perspiration, beady condensation running down a cold glass, beady droplets of rain on a spider's web.
  • Of liquids: standing in little round globules without spreading — as mercury or oil beads on a surface; the wax beaded and cooled.
  • Figurative alertness: beady alone can carry the sense of penetrating observation — his beady gaze swept the room — the watchfulness idea detached from the literal shape of the eye.

The historical bridge

Beady is formed in English from the noun bead plus the adjectival suffix -y, meaning having the nature, shape, or appearance of beads. The noun bead itself has one of the most famous semantic journeys in the language: Old English gebed meant prayer; in the Middle Ages each prayer of a rosary was counted on a small ball, so the ball itself came to be called a bead (one prayer equals one bead); and by the 14th century bead meant simply a small round perforated object, its religious meaning fading. Once bead meant small round object, the adjective beady (bead-like, full of beads) followed in the 16th century. The transfer to eyes — beady eyes — crystallised in the late 19th century and now dominates the word's entire life.

Register and nuance

  • Of eyes, beady is usually faintly negative or wryly comic — it implies cunning, greed, or a piercing, appraising stare — though for birds and animals it is merely exact.
  • Of drops and moisture it is neutral and slightly technical.
  • Of beaded decoration it is old-fashioned and today rare; a fashion writer is more likely to say beaded.
