The evolution of “prismatic”: from the saw to the rainbow. The word prismatic is a child of geometry and light, and its father is a saw. Greek πρίειν (priein) meant “to saw,” and from it the Greeks formed πρίσμα (prisma), “something sawn” — the word Euclid chose, in the Elements (c. 300 BCE), for a solid with parallel, identical faces, because such a block looked as though it had been sawn cleanly from a larger mass.
  • The uncertain Greek root. The deeper ancestry of priein is not securely established. Older dictionaries proposed a PIE root *per-/*prei- “to strike,” but no consensus exists, and no certain cognates beyond Greek have been demonstrated — so an honest derivation chain must stop at Greek rather than be forced into a PIE core. This is a word whose visible history begins with a carpenter.
  • From geometry to optics (3rd century BCE – 17th century). Latin took the word as prisma, a purely mathematical term; English took it in 1570 in Henry Billingsley’s translation of Euclid, still purely geometric. Then came Newton. In his optical experiments of 1666 he passed sunlight through a triangular glass prism and produced the visible spectrum; the device became the emblem of colour analysis. From the noun, New Latin formed prismaticus — with the adjective-forming suffix -ic — and French prismatique and English prismatic (with the older variant prismatical) followed, meaning first “of or like a prism,” and, by the early 18th century — Newton’s Opticks of 1704 — “displaying prismatic colours,” the rainbow band of separated hues.
  • The semantic leap. Where the Greek noun meant simply “a sawn object,” the modern adjective means “colour that has been sawn apart” — white light dissected into its constituent rays. That geometric violence — the clean cut, the separated bands, the cold precision — is what Vance exploits in the Excellent Prismatic Spray, a spell whose name promises not soft radiance but refracted, sliced, disciplined colour. It is one of the few words in English in which a carpenter’s tool, a geometer’s solid, and a physicist’s rainbow all remain faintly visible at once.
  • Generalisation (19th–21st centuries). In the 19th century the rainbow sense detached from the instrument: anything vividly many-coloured could be “prismatic,” and the figurative sense of brilliant, dissecting analysis grew from the optical metaphor of seeing through a prism. Today the geometric and optical senses remain technical, while the common sense is simply “rainbow-hued, kaleidoscopic, iridescent.”
Literary attestations run from Newton’s Opticks through Romantic descriptions of light on water — the “prismatic” sparkle of spray and dewdrops — to modern fantasy, where the word still carries its scientific precision into the realm of magic. The history of prismatic is thus a history of cutting: a saw, a solid, a beam of light, and finally a splash of every colour at once.

The evolution of “mantle”: a cloak that became the earth. The biography of mantle begins in obscurity. Latin mantellum (also mantelum), “a cloak, a covering,” is attested from the late Republic, and its origin is unknown: no secure PIE root has been reconstructed, and scholars have proposed — without proof — an Etruscan or a Celtic origin (a Gaulish *mantlon has been suggested), making the word possibly a Mediterranean or continental loan rather than a native Indo-European noun. The chain therefore cannot be pushed back to a PIE stage with any confidence.
  • The garment (1st century BCE – medieval). The Latin word passed two ways into English. Along the Germanic route it entered the Germanic stock as *mantilaz (a loan from Latin mantellum) and so reached Old English as mentel before the Norman Conquest; along the Romance route Old French mantel reinforced it, and the Middle English mantel/mantle (12th–13th centuries) blends both sources. The sense is the ancient cloak: a broad, loose sleeveless garment over the shoulders, the everyday outer wear of the medieval world and the ceremonial robe of kings, clergy, and heralds. From this second use grew the idiom of office: to wear the mantle of a king was to hold his authority, and “to take up the mantle” of a role — the phrase modern users know best — is a direct inheritance of the medieval robe of dignity.
  • The enveloping layer (17th–19th centuries). Science and metaphor then widened the word from cloth to world. Geologists in the 19th century named the earth’s great rock shell beneath the crust the “mantle,” on the image of the core as a body wrapped in a cloak; the gas-lighting industry gave the word to the incandescent mesh that glows over a flame (the “mantle” of a lamp); ornithologists applied it to the feathery covering on a bird’s back and wings; poets speak of a “mantle of snow,” a “mantle of night,” a “mantle of silence.” The bridging idea in every case is the same: a covering that wraps, protects, conceals, or dignifies what lies beneath.
  • The verb. As a verb, mantle means “to cover or envelop as with a mantle,” and, of liquids and faces, “to foam, sparkle, or suffuse with colour” — blood mantles the cheek, wine mantles in the glass — an extension of the covering image to the surface that blushes or glows.
  • Modern double life. Contemporary English keeps both senses at full strength: the actual garment (now rare but literary) and the enveloping metaphor (“a mantle of leadership,” “a mantle of silence”), with the geological term the most exact and most widely taught. The word’s ancient origin, obscure as it is, hardly matters to its modern power: a mantle is anything that wraps, and English uses it for cloth, rock, light, and power alike.
In the card’s passage — Phandaal’s Mantle of Stealth — the word does what it has always done: a covering that conceals the wearer, robe and magic fused. From an unknown Latin cloak to the core of the earth, the career of mantle is the career of covering itself.
