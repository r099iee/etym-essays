1. The name before the region. The word Provence began as an administrative abstract. Latin prōvincia first meant the sphere of duty assigned to a Roman magistrate abroad, then, by natural extension, the conquered territory under his command. When Rome subdued the Ligurian tribes of the southeast of Gaul in 125–121 BCE and organised the region as Gallia Narbonensis, the area was so thoroughly a Roman possession that it was simply called la Provincia — the Province — the first Roman province beyond the Alps. Pliny the Elder remarks that the name had become the region's ordinary designation. No imperial decree made Provence "Provence"; the word made itself by sheer referential gravity, and it is the only region of France whose modern name is the Latin word for province.

2. From Roman province to medieval county. With the decline of Roman power the administration vanished but the name did not. The area of cities such as Arles, Aix, and Marseille, ruled successively by Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Franks, and the counts of Burgundy and Provence, kept the old designation as its plain geographic label. From the tenth century Provence was a real political unit again — a county, later joined to the crown of Aragon and to Anjou, with its own counts, its own coinage, and its own identity. The ninth-century Latin documents already write Provincia for the political unit; the Old Occitan Provença and Old French Provence follow in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. The word by then has a double life: a place and a political jurisdiction, with the common noun "province" hived off separately in the learned languages.

3. The troubadour century: the name becomes a byword for love. The sense that matters most for Pater's sentence was made in the twelfth century, not by administrators but by poets. The court of Poitiers and the castles of Provence produced the troubadour lyric — the first European poetry of romantic love as an art: Guilhem de Peitieus (1071–1126), Marcabru, Raimbaut d'Aurenga, and above all Arnaut Daniel, whom Dante places among the masters of the sweet new style. Their subject, fin'amor, refined or courtly love, proposed that desire could be disciplined into a school of manners and song — love as a way of life at once ethical and aesthetic. Because the poets wrote in the language of the south, Occitan (often then called Provençal), the region's name became the byword for the entire culture: "the poetry of Provence" meant the sweetest music Europe had yet devised. Dante and Petrarch read the Provençal poets as their masters; their own Italian poetry is unthinkable without this school.

4. Union with France and the long provincial sleep. In 1481 Provence passed to the French crown on the death of Count Charles of Maine, and the region became a French province proper — the common noun catching up with the proper name. Over the following centuries the word receded from the centre of European culture: the troubadours were half-remembered, their language declined into patois, and Provence became a picturesque but provincial corner of France, from which court and capital looked wearily away. Yet the memory never quite died. Renaissance and Romantic scholars collected the Provençal manuscripts; Petrarch's reverence for the south kept the association alive; and the 18th and 19th centuries rediscovered the troubadours as the founders of modern lyric poetry and of "romantic love" — the very doctrines of which Pater speaks.

5. Mistral, the Félibrige, and the invention of modern Provence. The nineteenth century gave the name its second career. Frédéric Mistral's epic poem Mirèio (1859), written in the Provençal language, won him the Nobel Prize (1904) and, with the movement he founded, the Félibrige, restored Provençal as a literary language and Provence as a self-conscious cultural identity. Painters — Cézanne at Aix, Van Gogh at Arles and Saint-Rémy — fixed the region's light on canvas; writers from Daudet to Pagnol made its speech and manners famous; the railway and the steamship turned it into the Riviera of fashionable travel. "Provence" was re-invented as the image of a sun-drenched, unspoiled, sensuous France: olive oil, lavender, bouillabaisse, the Mistral wind, the cicadas.

6. Today: the byword multiplied. In the twenty-first century the name works on at least four levels at once — the ancient province beneath the modern region; the medieval county and its troubadour poetry; the Romantic-Provençal revival; and the global lifestyle brand of Mediterranean ease. Pater's sentence rides on the second and third of these: when he says that in the poetry of Provence "the rude strength of the middle age turns to sweetness", he is using the name as the old criticism used it — as shorthand for everything the medieval south meant to the history of feeling. That a single word could hold two millennia of sovereignty, poetry, revival, and tourism is itself a small monument to the region's history — and to the staying power of a Roman tax term.
