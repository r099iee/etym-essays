1. The deep root: dragging and drawing — Draught belongs to the Indo-European root *dʰregʰ-, "to draw, drag, run", from which Germanic built its verb for drawing and pulling. The same root lies behind English draw, drag, dray, and draught itself, and it is distantly related to the Latin verbs trahere "to drag" (whence tractor, train, trail) and to drachma, the handful of coins that was literally "drawn" from the palm. The family is one of the most productive in the language, and draught is one of its oldest members.

2. Germanic formation — In Proto-Germanic the verb *draganą, "to draw, drag, carry", produced an abstract noun with the -tuz/-þuz suffix, *drahtuz, "a drawing, a pulling, a thing drawn". Old English inherited two forms of this noun, dræht and dreaht, both meaning "a drawing, a pull, a draught". The noun is thus the deverbal partner of the verb draw: to draw is the action, a draught is the result or instance of it — a pull, a haul, a breath drawn in, a thing drawn out.

3. The branching of senses in Middle English — Middle English draught retained the concrete senses: a pull, a drawing of a sword, a haul of a net, a drawing of breath. From these the word multiplied through the ordinary logic of "drawing". A current of air is air "drawn" through an opening (recorded from the 16th century). A swallow is liquor "drawn" into the mouth. The depth a ship draws is its draught (16th century). A preliminary sketch is "drawn out" first (17th century). And the board game in which pieces are "drawn" or moved forward gives us draughts (the game) and draughtsmen (the pieces) — the game is recorded under this name by the 16th-17th centuries. Each new sense is a new instance of the same ancient act of drawing.

4. The great spelling split — In the 18th and 19th centuries English developed two spellings for the same word: draught, retaining the old gh spelling that reflects the /x/ sound once pronounced in dreaht, and draft, a simplified spelling matching the modern pronunciation. The two coexisted in Britain, with draft favoured for the "preliminary document" and "bank draft" senses and draught for air, liquid, depth, and the game. In America, Noah Webster's reforms fixed draft for all senses, and draught became a British, or literary, form. Today the British still write draught beer, draught horse, and draughts the game, while Americans write draft everywhere.

5. Sound history — The pronunciation history is neat. Old English dreaht/dræht had a final /x/ sound (spelt h), which in Middle English appears as gh; by early modern English that /x/ had been lost, leaving a lengthened vowel, so that draught is pronounced exactly like draft (/drɑːft/ in RP, /dræft/ in American English). The spelling draught is thus a historical fossil: it preserves the gh that the pronunciation no longer has, a small monument to English sound change.

  • c. 4500-2500 BCE: PIE *dʰregʰ- "to draw, drag".
  • c. 500 BCE - 100 CE: Proto-Germanic *draganą "to draw"; *drahtuz "a drawing, a pull".
  • c. 700-1000 CE: Old English dræht / dreaht "a drawing, a draught".
  • 14th-15th c.: Middle English draught with the senses pull, haul, drawing.
  • 16th c.: Senses of air-current, drink, and ship's depth appear.
  • 16th-17th c.: The game draughts (checkers) named for moving the pieces; the "draft" spelling emerges for documents.
  • 19th c.: American English fixes draft for all senses; British English keeps draught for air, liquid, depth, and the game.
  • 20th-21st c.: Draughts (air currents) used by Tolkien in The Hobbit; the word remains fully alive in British English.

6. Summary — Draughts is a 2,500-year-old word for "things drawn": first pulls and hauls, then the air drawn through a hall, the beer drawn from a cask, the water drawn under a ship's keel, and the pieces drawn across a board. Tolkien's torches fluttering "in the draughts" of the mountain use the oldest and most physical of those senses, and the word's long history of "drawing" runs quietly beneath his sentence.
