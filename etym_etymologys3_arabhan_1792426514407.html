1. Indo-European foundations of the two compound roots. The name Śarabhan (representing the stem of the epic hermit Śarabhaṅga) is a classical Sanskrit compound constructed from two ancient Indo-European morphological elements: the zoomorphic base *śarabha- and the anatomical noun *aṅga-.

  • The root of *śarabha-* — Traces to Proto-Indo-European *ḱer- / *ḱerh₂-, fundamentally meaning "horn, head, or horned beast." This versatile root generated the primary words for horned beasts, stags, and hard head structures across Eurasia: Latin cervus ("stag, deer") and cornū ("horn"), Greek kéras (κέρας, "horn"), Old English heorot (Modern English hart, "male deer"), and Sanskrit śṛṅga ("horn"). In the Indo-Iranian branch, it produced Proto-Indo-Iranian *ćarabʰás. In the Atharvaveda, the śarabha was conceptualized as a formidable, eight-legged mountain creature inhabiting the snowy peaks, swifter and stronger than a lion, embodying untamed biological vigor.
  • The root of *aṅga-* — Traces to Proto-Indo-European *h₂eng- / *ang-, meaning "to bend, to curve; a joint, corner, or limb." It gave Latin angulus ("angle, corner"), Greek ankṓn (ἀγκών, "bend of the arm, elbow"), Old High German ancha ("shin, leg joint"), and Sanskrit áṅga ("a limb, member, or constituent division of the body"). In Vedic philosophy, aṅga designated both physical bodily limbs and the supplementary branches of sacred science (the Vedāṅgas).

2. Compounding morphology and semantic interpretation. When combined according to the rules of Sanskrit composition (samāsa), śarabha and aṅga coalesce into Śarabhaṅga. Philologists recognize two primary grammatical interpretations:

  • Possessive Bahuvrīhi compound — "One whose limbs possess the strength, agility, or endurance of a Śarabha." In epic heroic and ascetic onomastics, sages and warriors were frequently named after animals of immense fortitude (such as Rṣyaśṛṅga, "deer-horned," or Vyāghrapāda, "tiger-footed"). For an ascetic, possessing the limbs of a Śarabha symbolized the superhuman physical and mental endurance required to sustain centuries of severe penance (*tapas*) in the wilderness.
  • Ablative or Karmadhāraya compound — Alternatively, ancient scholiasts suggested "one whose bodily limbs were broken or shed like a Śarabha," foreshadowing the sage's ultimate spiritual act: the voluntary casting off and dissolution of his mortal limbs into the sacred fire.

3. Textual, phonological, and orthographic genesis of *Śarabhan-*. The form Śarabhan arises directly from the nuances of Sanskrit phonology and manuscript transliteration. In Sanskrit, when a nasal consonant immediately precedes the voiced velar plosive g, the dental nasal n or anusvāra obligatorily assimilates to the homorganic velar nasal ṅ (Pāṇini 8.4.58: anusvārasya yayi parasavarṇaḥ). In Devanāgarī, this is written as शरभङ्ग (Śarabhaṅga). However, in early Western transliteration systems, 19th-century European printings, and critical apparatuses (such as Schlegel, Gorresio, and older British editions), the velar nasal was frequently represented with an over-macron or tilde (n̄ or ñ), typeset as Śarabhan̄ga. In morphological segmentation and indexical parsing, the form was separated into the stem element Śarabhan- and the terminal consonant group, yielding the headword representation Śarabhan.

4. The ascetic climax in the Araṇyakāṇḍa. Within the narrative texture of the Rāmāyaṇa, the sage Śarabhaṅga occupies a profound theological threshold. Having mastered all bodily desires through centuries of austerity, he is visited by Indra, who offers him the ultimate reward of ascending to the realm of Brahmā. In an extraordinary act of spiritual discernment, the sage declines immediate salvation because he knows that Rāma—the divine Supreme Lord walking in human form—is approaching his hermitage. The sage values the direct physical vision (darśana) of the divine avatāra above all heavenly realms.

5. Agni-praveśa and the metaphysics of voluntary departure. Upon welcoming Rāma and directing him toward the hermitage of Sutīkṣṇa, Śarabhaṅga enacts the ancient rite of *Agni-praveśa* (entering the sacred fire). In ancient Indian law and philosophy, when a forest hermit reaches the end of his spiritual quest, he is permitted the noble departure (*Mahāprasthāna*). Śarabhaṅga prepares the sacrificial altar, recites sacred mantras, and calmly enters the flame. The fire acts not as an instrument of destruction, but as a divine crucible of transmutation: it consumes his aged, decrepit mortal shell and releases his radiant, youthful spiritual body, which ascends to the highest heavens. Śarabhaṅga thus stands as an immortal monument to ascetic transcendence, bodily mastery, and supreme devotion.
