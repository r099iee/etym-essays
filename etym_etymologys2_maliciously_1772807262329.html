MALICIOUSLY is the adverbial form of English malicious, built on the noun malice, a loan from French. The chain runs: - PIE *mel- "bad, wrong" — a reconstructed base sometimes proposed for the 'bad' word-family; the reconstruction is debated (see note) → - Latin malus "bad, evil, wicked; unfortunate" (attested Classical Latin; the further PIE etymology is uncertain) → - Latin malitia "badness, wickedness, ill will, spite" (noun of quality from malus + -itia; attested Classical Latin, common in moral and rhetorical texts) → - Old French malice "ill will, wickedness, crafty evil" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries) → - Middle English malice "malice, ill will" (attested from the 13th century) → - English malice "ill will, the desire to harm" (noun) → - English malicious "characterized by malice; done with intent to harm" (adjective; via Old French malicieux < Latin malitiosus "full of malice", attested in English from the 15th century) → - English maliciously (adverb; adjective + suffix -ly; attested from the 15th century) → - MALICIOUSLY "in a manner characterized by malice; with intent to do harm". Notes on debated points: - The deepest PIE shape behind Latin malus is debated. de Vaan and others treat the further etymology of malus as uncertain; a connection to a PIE root *mel- "to be mistaken, to fail" (or a 'bad' root) has been proposed but is not secure. Treat the *mel- stage as a speculative reconstruction, not an attested fact; the secure path for English is Latin malus → malitia → French malice → English malicious(ly). - The related Latin adverb male "badly" and adjectives maleficus / malignus "doing evil" fed the English mal- prefix words (malefactor, malformed, malfunction, malevolent, malediction, malignant) — a separate but parallel family from the same Latin base.
