ledge is a noun meaning a narrow, horizontal shelf or ridge that projects from a surface — most commonly a shelf of rock on a cliff face, or a projection on the side of a building. In the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — the travellers follow a track that "wandered on to the top of the southern ridge and brought them at last to a still narrower ledge, which turned north across the face of the Mountain": the ledge is a thin walkway of rock cut into the sheer mountain-side, perilous and thrilling, the mountain's own doorstep for those who dare it.

PRIMARY SENSE — A NARROW ROCK SHELF

  • A horizontal shelf or ridge of rock projecting from a cliff, mountain-side, or excavation: "the climbers bivouacked on a narrow ledge", "a bird nested on a high rock ledge".
  • It is typically flat enough to stand on but narrow — a ribbon of floor along a vertical face; the word never loses this thin, precarious quality.
  • In geology, a ledge is a layer or stratum of rock that juts out; miners call an outcrop of ore a ledge, and American prospectors "struck a ledge" (a vein) of gold-bearing quartz.
  • In the source text the ledge is the route itself: a "still narrower ledge" turning north across the face of the Mountain — a dizzying path between the cliff and the void, exactly the word's romantic peril.

SECONDARY SENSE — A PROJECTION ON A BUILDING

  • A horizontal projection or shelf on a wall of a house or other structure: "the window ledge", "she set the pot on the ledge", "the cat sat on the kitchen ledge".
  • In everyday British and American English, ledge and sill overlap: a window ledge (or window-sill) is the flat strip below the window that holds plants, cups, cats, and elbows.
  • Broader architectural use: any projecting horizontal moulding or shelf, including ledges for statues, candles, or tools; the word also names flat projecting members at the base or top of a wall (cf. cornice, coping).
  • This domestic sense shares the essential image with the rock sense: a flat shelf that juts out from a vertical surface — the household shelf is a tame rock ledge.

TERTIARY SENSES — NAUTICAL, UNDERWATER, AND FIGURATIVE

  • Underwater: a ledge is a submerged ridge or reef of rock near the surface, dangerous to shipping — "the ship struck a ledge"; sailors' ledges, off-lying ledges, and "a rocky ledge that breaks the waves" are stock terms of coastal navigation.
  • Nautical building: ledges are the small transverse beams between the larger deck-beams of a ship; the word belongs to the technical vocabulary of the shipwright.
  • Figurative: "on a ledge" or "on the ledge" names a precarious emotional or social position — someone teetering on the edge; in crisis journalism a "ledge" can also mean the edge itself of a rooftop or bridge.
  • In masonry, a ledge is also a ledge-stone, a course of stone projecting to support a wall above.

REGISTER AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE

  • Register: ledge is everyday and concrete; it has no formal register of its own but slides easily between domestic chat (window ledge) and dramatic narrative (a mountain ledge above the gorge).
  • The semantic bridge among all senses is the horizontal projection: whatever the material — rock, timber, stone, metal — a ledge is a thin floor that sticks out from a vertical face, offering footing, a resting place, or (underwater) a hazard.
  • Because of the source text and mountaineering prose generally, ledge in many readers' minds is inseparable from peril: the ledge is where climbers rest, where goats and birds shelter, and where one wrong step ends — a word that keeps its edge.

GRAMMAR AND FORMS

  • Plural: ledges; it is a countable concrete noun, rarely abstract.
  • Compound and phrase uses: window ledge, window-sill (overlap), ledge-stone, rock ledge, cliff ledge, off-lying ledge, "ledge and brace" (carpentry: a ledged door is one braced with a cross-bar, from the same word-family).
  • The related adjective ledged ("having a ledge") exists but is rare; the noun ledger is historically connected to the same family via "to lie/lay" and the laid-open book, though the words have drifted apart in meaning.
  • In the source text ledge is used in its most dramatic concrete sense: a still narrower ledge, turning north across the face of the Mountain — the last step of a track that disappears into the rock.

SUMMARY

  • ledge = a narrow horizontal shelf or ridge projecting from a rock face, a building, or the seabed — a thin floor of stone, wood, or metal that sticks out from a vertical surface.
  • In the source text it is the perilous ribbon of rock carrying the travellers across the face of the Mountain, the word's cliff-side heart made visible.
