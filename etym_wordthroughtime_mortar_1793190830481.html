- Roman world — the kitchen grinder: Latin mortarium names the pounding bowl found in every villa kitchen, grinding grain, herbs, and pigments.
- Old English — the word lands: mortere enters the vocabulary with monks and leeches, the vessel of medicine and cookery.
- Middle Ages — the paste borrows the bowl: builders grinding lime mixtures name the mixture for the vessel, and bricklayer's mortar is born.
- 17th c. — the cannon takes the name: squat siege pieces resemble the bowl in profile, and armies adopt the kitchen word for thunder.
- 19th c. — folklore fixes the image: collectors of Russian tales show Baba Yaga riding her iron mortar across illustrated pages, and the vessel becomes a vehicle.
- Today — three trades at once: pharmacists, masons, and mortar crews each own the word fully, while witches keep the flying bowl aloft in storybooks.
