- Mid-to-Late 18th c. (c. 1760s–1780s) — Emerged in Scottish and Northern British English via nominal inversion of the phrasal verb to set out; initially criticized by English linguistic purists as a northern provincialism.
- Early 19th c. (Romantic Era) — Rapidly embraced by prominent English writers (such as Sir Walter Scott, William Hazlitt, and Samuel Taylor Coleridge), successfully entering standard British literary prose.
- Mid-to-Late 19th c. (Victorian Era) — Fully naturalized across academic treatises, legal arguments, and parliamentary rhetoric, becoming ubiquitous in the opening transitional phrase "at the outset".
- 20th c. — Established as an indispensable structural signpost in scholarly method, scientific papers, and corporate strategic planning.
- 21st c. — Universal standard English idiom across international academic discourse, investigative journalism, and everyday professional correspondence.
