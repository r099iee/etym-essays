Assimilated is the past tense and past participle of the verb assimilate, and in modern English it is also a fully independent adjective. Its governing image is making alike: something is taken into a body, a group, a system or a pattern and rendered so similar to it that the joining seam disappears. Because the metaphor of ingestion works equally well for food, people, sounds, ideas and data, the word has branched into several distinct technical vocabularies while keeping a single semantic core.

Primary sense: absorbed into a community

The dominant modern use is sociological. An immigrant, a minority or an individual is said to be assimilated when they have adopted the language, customs, dress, values and social habits of the host society to the point of being indistinguishable from it. The word is stronger than integrated: integration implies participation while preserving distinctiveness, whereas assimilation implies the effacement of the original difference. It is usually construed with into (“assimilated into the mainstream”) and less often with to (“assimilated to local custom”), a prepositional contrast that preserves the Latin sense of ad- “towards.”

  • Cultural assimilation — the adoption of a dominant culture’s language, religion, cuisine and public manners; historically the official goal of immigrant-receiving states from the 1890s to the 1960s.
  • Linguistic assimilation — the replacement of a heritage language by the majority language, typically within three generations; the speaker becomes monolingual in the host tongue.
  • Structural assimilation — in sociology, the entry of a minority into the primary groups of the majority: clubs, friendships, marriages, neighbourhoods. It is distinguished from mere acculturation, which can occur without social mixing.
  • Assimilated Jew, assimilated Indian, assimilated middle class — adjectival uses in which the participle marks a person as having traded a marked identity for unmarked belonging; the tone is neutral in older scholarship and often critical today.

Physiological and biological senses

The oldest English sense is physiological: the body assimilates food when it converts nutrients into its own tissue. Here the Latin metaphor is almost literal — the digested substance is made similar to the body that consumes it. From this came the wider biological use:

  • Nutrient assimilation — absorption through the gut wall and incorporation into cells; the opposite process is excretion or dissimilation.
  • Carbon assimilation — the fixation of atmospheric carbon dioxide into organic compounds during photosynthesis, the basis of the biosphere’s carbon cycle.
  • Nitrogen assimilation — the conversion of inorganic nitrogen into amino acids and proteins in plants and microbes.
  • Assimilation in metabolism — the constructive, energy-storing half of metabolism (anabolism), as opposed to catabolism, the breaking-down half.

Phonetics: sounds that become alike

In phonology, one sound is assimilated to another when it acquires one or more of its neighbour’s features, usually to ease articulation. The change may be regressive (the following sound influences the preceding one), progressive (the preceding sound influences the following one), or reciprocal. Familiar English examples include input pronounced with /m/ before /p/, ten bikes with /m/ before /b/, and the loss of the final /d/ in handbag. The term entered philology in the nineteenth century, when the Neogrammarians systematised sound change, and it remains basic to historical linguistics, where assimilation explains many otherwise irregular correspondences.

Cognitive and psychological sense

Jean Piaget used assimilation for the process by which a child fits new experience into an existing mental schema, as opposed to accommodation, in which the schema itself is changed. An assimilated fact is therefore one that has been made to fit what we already believe — which is why the word can carry a faint warning of cognitive conservatism, of evidence being swallowed by theory rather than correcting it.

Technical and contemporary senses

  • Data assimilation — in meteorology and oceanography, the statistical blending of observations with a numerical model to produce the best estimate of the current state of the atmosphere or ocean; the model “assimilates” the data.
  • Assimilated intelligence — in security and intelligence work, information that has been evaluated, cross-checked and absorbed into the accepted picture, as distinct from raw reporting.
  • Assimilated (Star Trek) — since the Borg of Star Trek: The Next Generation (1989 onward), “resistance is futile; you will be assimilated” has made the word a pop-culture byword for forced absorption into a collective. This is now one of the most recognisable uses in everyday speech.
  • Figurative absorption — a scandal is “assimilated” into the news cycle, a building is “assimilated” into the streetscape, a style is “assimilated” by the mainstream: in each case the alien thing is naturalised until it no longer reads as alien.

Grammar and collocations

The verb is both transitive (“to assimilate immigrants”) and intransitive (“they assimilated quickly”). The participle functions adjectivally with degree adverbs — fully, largely, partially, readily, easily assimilated — and in compounds such as well-assimilated and newly assimilated. The derived noun assimilation and the agent noun assimilationist (often pejorative: one who demands that minorities abandon their distinctiveness) belong to the same cluster.

Semantic bridges

Every sense descends from the single Latin image of making a thing similis, “like.” Food is made like the body; a foreigner is made like the nation; a sound is made like its neighbour; a new fact is made like an old schema; a data point is made like a model state. The shift from the physiological to the social sense occurred in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when assimilation began to be applied to populations as earlier generations had applied it to nutriment. The critical, negative colouring is a twentieth-century development, arising when the melting-pot ideal was re-examined by minority writers who saw assimilation as loss rather than gain.
