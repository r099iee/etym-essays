The name Avignon is older than Roman France, older than the papacy, older even than the Gauls who first gave it its permanent form. It belongs to a stratum of European naming so ancient that linguists cannot fully date it: the layer of river-names built on the water-word *ab-, "water, river, marsh," which survives in Welsh afon, Irish abhainn, and the repeated English river-name Avon. To say "Avignon" is, at the deepest level, to say "the town at the water." The substrate name: water and the Cavares.
  • Before the legions arrived, the left bank of the Rhône, near its junction with the Durance, was held by the Cavares, a Celto-Ligurian people. Their chief settlement stood on a marshy reach of the river, where a floodplain met a ford, and they called it Avennio — built on the ancient European hydronymic root *av-, "water," plus the Gaulish place-suffix -iō. The name means, in effect, "the town at the water," or more precisely "the town of the river-marsh."
  • This root is one of the great water-words of western Europe. The same element gives the English river-name Avon (which simply means "the river," so that several different Avons exist across England and Scotland), the Roman Abona of Wiltshire, the French river Aven in Brittany, and the Aveyron. In Avignon it surfaces directly in the town-name itself.
  • Whether the root is pre-Indo-European substrate or early Celtic is debated; what is secure is its meaning. Ancient place-names of running water are among the most conservative names in Europe, surviving conquest after conquest, and Avennio proved extraordinarily durable.
Roman Avignon: from river settlement to civitas.
  • Roman administration Latinized the Gaulish name as Avenniō (genitive Avenniōnis) and absorbed the town into the province of Gallia Narbonensis. The Latin form kept the old hydronym intact, hiding inside a third-declension Latin noun.
  • Avennio flourished as a river port on the Rhône, one of the great highways of Roman trade, and eventually became a civitas, a self-governing Roman city with its own magistrates. Its position at the Rhône-Durance junction made it a natural crossing and market.
  • The -iōnem suffix of the name is the same Gaulish place-forming element seen in Vesontio (modern Besançon) and Cabillonum (Chalon-sur-Saône); the root, not the suffix, carried the water meaning.
The medieval transformation: Provence and the papacy.
  • With the collapse of the Western Empire, the town passed into the world of the Romance-speaking south of France. Provençal and Old Occitan, the speech of the Midi, reshaped the Latin Avenniōnem into Avinhon, and Old French further smoothed it into Avignon. The name crossed every linguistic boundary with the same element intact.
  • Between 1309 and 1377 the city entered European history at the highest possible level. When the French Pope Clement V chose not to go to Rome, he settled his court at Avignon, then part of the lands of the counts of Provence and effectively a papal possession. Seven French popes followed him, building the immense Palais des Papes that still dominates the skyline and holding the largest, richest court in Christendom.
  • In church history this Avignon Papacy is often called the "Babylonian captivity of the Church," a phrase borrowed from the biblical exile of the Jews, because the papacy had left its true seat at Rome. In English, therefore, the word "Avignon" carried, from the fourteenth century on, a double resonance: splendour and exile, the wealth of the papal court and the scandal of an absent Rome.
  • The folk tradition fixed the city in European song with the round "Sur le pont d'Avignon" — "on the bridge of Avignon" — though the famous dance is, ironically, traditionally performed under the bridge (sous), not on it. The bridge itself, the Pont Saint-Bénézet, was begun in the twelfth century by a shepherd saint and repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt by the Rhône's floods, until only four arches were left standing.
The nineteenth century: Avignon in English letters.
  • After the French Revolution Avignon was annexed to France (1791), ending its centuries as a papal enclave, and faded in English awareness to a quiet provincial city — until the middle of the nineteenth century, when it entered English biography at the most personal level.
  • It was at Avignon that Harriet Taylor Mill died on November 3, 1858, after the seven years of happy companionship her husband records in his Autobiography. She was buried in the St. Véran cemetery, and John Stuart Mill bought a house overlooking her grave, spending the remaining years of his life near it and returning each year on the anniversary of her death.
  • For Victorian readers, the sentence that anchors this card — "was buried at Avignon" — thus carried a melancholy, romantic glamour entirely unlike the papal history of the name: Avignon became the emblem of Mill's grief, the place where a great philosopher chose to end his days gazing on his wife's grave. The same association recurs in the later life of the Mills' stepdaughter Helen Taylor, who also lived at Avignon, and in Mill's own grave, dug beside his wife's in the same cemetery.
The contemporary city.
  • Today Avignon is a tourist byword: the bridge of the nursery rhyme, the Palais des Papes, the medieval walls, and the annual Festival d'Avignon, one of the great theatre festivals of Europe, which fills the city every July.
  • The name thus carries a remarkable accumulation of meanings in the English imagination — the water-town of the Cavares, the Roman civitas, the seat of the exiled popes, the subject of a children's song, the resting place of Harriet Taylor Mill, and a modern stage for the performing arts. It is a single name stretched over more than two thousand years of European history.
Conclusion. Avignon is a proper noun whose etymology unlocks an entire chronology: from the prehistoric water-words of western Europe, through the Gaulish settlement Avennio and the Roman civitas, to the Provençal and French Avignon of the papal court and the song, and finally to the Avignon of Victorian grief and modern festival. Its meaning has never been a definition — it has always been a place and a story.
