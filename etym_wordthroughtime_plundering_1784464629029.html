“To plunder” entered English in the 17th century from wartime — the military act of stripping a household or town of its movable goods, goods carried off as the spoils of war.
“Plunder” through the age of piracy and colonial warfare was the standard word for seizing loot — pirates plundered ships, armies plundered cities — a brutal, matter-of-fact vocabulary of conquest.
“Plunder” in the 19th–20th centuries broadened to any forcible theft and to the figurative stripping of resources — plundering nature, plundering the earth — keeping the image of taking everything movable.
“Plundering” today still carries its predatory, army-loot flavour — which is why dragons are the perfect plunderers: creatures whose nature is to strip households and halls.
