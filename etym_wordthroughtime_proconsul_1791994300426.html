- 4th c. BCE (326 BCE) — Instituted during the Second Samnite War as the Latin phrase prō consule ("on behalf of a consul") to extend a general's military command beyond his one-year term without violating constitutional checks.
- 1st c. BCE — Established as the definitive office of Roman provincial administration and warlord hegemony under the late Republic, granting vast armies and revenues to Caesar, Pompey, and Cicero.
- 1st–3rd c. CE — Reorganized under the Principate as the specific designation for senatorial provincial governors administering peaceful Roman territories.
- 16th–17th c. — Entered Middle and Early Modern English via classical historiography and translations of Livy and Plutarch, used strictly in antiquarian Roman contexts.
- 19th c. (Victorian era) — Adopted into imperial political journalism as a proud accolade for British imperial administrators (e.g. Curzon, Cromer, Milner) exercising autocratic rule across the British Empire.
- 20th–21st c. — Applied in international relations and geopolitical analysis to modern military and diplomatic leaders governing occupied territories (MacArthur, Bremer) or powerful multinational corporate envoys.
