Adornments are the plural of adornment: the things with which something is adorned — ornaments, decorations, and embellishments added to beautify a person, a building, an object, or even a piece of writing. In the source passage the word describes the old splendour of the dwarves' kingdom: "all the old adornments" — the carved pillars, gilded work, jewels, and hangings of the great halls — have "long mouldered or destroyed," blasted by the monster. The sentence is a small elegy for a civilisation's decoration, and the word carries exactly that weight: adornments are not necessities but the sumptuous additions that make a place or person beautiful and worthy of memory.

Core sense — decorative objects. An adornment is a concrete ornament: a piece of jewellery (a necklace, ring, or brooch), a carving, a gilded boss, an inlaid gem, a hanging, an embroidery, a finial. Houses are adorned with carvings and mouldings; temples with statues and gilt; garments with lace and beads; the person with jewels and fine attire. The word is a count noun — one may count the adornments of a room or of a bride — and it overlaps closely with ornament.

  • Personal adornment. In fashion and anthropology, "personal adornment" is the standard term for everything humans wear to decorate the body — jewellery, paint, tattoos, plumes, rings — a usage that makes adornments technical as well as everyday.
  • Architectural and decorative arts. In art history and heritage writing, the adornments of a building are its mouldings, statues, reliefs, and painted or gilded flourishes — precisely the sense in the source passage, where a dragon-blasted hall has lost its adornments to time and ruin.
  • Abstract and literary use. Adornments can be non-physical: "the adornments of style" are the rhetorical flourishes of prose; a biography may be "free of fictional adornments," meaning added, embellishing detail. Here the word means decoration in general, often with a mild suggestion that the added matter is optional or even superfluous.
  • The singular and the act. Adornment also names the act of adorning (the adornment of the bride took two hours), alongside the concrete thing. Both senses descend together from the verb adorn, "to make beautiful, to decorate," via Old French adorner from Latin adornare, "to fit out, equip, decorate."
  • Register and connotation. Adornments are usually refined, precious, or ceremonial rather than merely functional; the word often appears in descriptions of luxury, ritual, and ruin, where the loss of adornments is felt as a loss of beauty and dignity.

Semantic bridge. Latin ornare meant to fit out and equip — to supply a thing with what it needs — and this practical sense later widened to "to decorate." The prefix ad- ("to, toward") plus the suffix -ment (making a concrete result) yields literally "things added for the fitting-out": the adornments are, at root, the additions that complete a thing's beauty.
