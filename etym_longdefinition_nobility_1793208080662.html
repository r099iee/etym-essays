Nobility names, in its primary and overwhelmingly dominant sense, the hereditary aristocratic estate of a society: the dukes, marquesses, earls, viscounts, and barons of Britain, the princes and counts of the Continent, the families whose rank, titles, and often lands descend by blood and whose collective body stands directly below royalty and above gentry and commons. To speak of the nobility with the definite article is almost always to mean this order as a political actor — voting in an upper house, officered into regiments, taxed or exempted, loyal or disaffected — and the source sentence uses exactly this collective political sense: in 1797 the nobility, gentry, and clergy rushed as three estates to sanction the system of repression then beginning.

The word second great life is moral rather than heraldic: nobility as elevation of character — generosity, courage, honour, disinterestedness — the quality of a noble mind whether or not its bearer owns an acre or a title. This ethical sense is not a modern softening but nearly as old as the social one, because the aristocratic ethic always claimed that high birth entailed high conduct, noblesse oblige. Thus a peasant may act with nobility, a speech may possess nobility of sentiment, a building nobility of proportion; the word measures stature of soul by the borrowed yardstick of rank. The bridge between the two lives is that ancient claim — that the well-born are, or ought to be, the well-made — and centuries of satire and sermon have played on the gap between inherited title and earned virtue.

Core meaning in detail: As a social estate, the nobility in Britain comprises the peerage — those holding hereditary (and now life) titles entitling the bearer, until recent reform, to sit in the House of Lords — together with, in looser journalistic use, their families and the untitled great landowners adjoining them. On the Continent the sense widens: the French noblesse, the German Adel, the Russian dvorianstvo, each a legally defined order with privileges of taxation, office, and honour, each abolished or hollowed by revolution and reform yet surviving as a social fact. Historically the estate justified itself by the sword — the nobility were those who fought — then by the land, then by sheer prescription; economists and radicals from the 1790s onward attacked it as unproductive, and the source sentence fury at a nobility bankrolling repression is a textbook instance. As a moral quality, nobility denotes greatness of mind made visible: the refusal of meanness, the bearing of suffering without complaint, generosity to enemies — Aristotle megalopsychia in English dress.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The hereditary aristocratic order collectively (primary social sense): the nobility as an estate of the realm — peers and great titled families acting as a body. The bridge from the Latin root is direct: nobilitas was Roman rank and renown made hereditary, and every European aristocracy consciously copied the Roman vocabulary of honour.
  • Noble rank or status of an individual: elevation to, or possession of, aristocratic dignity — raised to the nobility, born into the nobility. The bridge narrows the collective to the personal: the estate abstracted into the rank its members enjoy, the way royalty slides between the institution and the persons.
  • Nobility of character — moral elevation (great ethical sense): magnanimity, honour, generosity of spirit — the nobility of his sacrifice moved even his enemies. The bridge is the aristocratic ideology itself: high birth was supposed to produce high conduct, so the name of the rank became the name of the virtue, and the virtue long outlived the rank power.
  • Nobility of style, form, or expression (aesthetic sense): grandeur and dignity in art, architecture, music, or prose — the nobility of the cathedral proportions, phrases of Virgilian nobility. The bridge carries moral elevation into aesthetics: as a soul may be noble, so may a façade or a sentence that lifts the beholder rather than flattering him.
  • The ancient Roman nobilitas specifically (historical technical sense): in Roman history the nobiles were descendants of consuls, a quasi-hereditary governing class distinct from the wider patriciate. The bridge is etymological survival: English historians use nobility of Rome because the Romans used nobilitas of themselves, and the modern peerage inherited the Latin word through French.
  • The quality of eminence in a field (extended figurative use): the nobility of science, the intellectual nobility — those who lead an art or discipline. The bridge generalizes rank beyond blood: wherever hierarchy and honour appear, the vocabulary of the old estate is borrowed to dignify the new hierarchy, sometimes sincerely, sometimes ironically.

Register and connotation: nobility is formal, weighty, and faintly archaic in its social sense — leader-columns and history books rather than conversation, where aristocracy, upper class, or posh do the work. The moral sense, by contrast, remains fully alive and warmly approving: to credit someone with nobility is among the highest compliments English offers, precisely because it detaches virtue from birth and awards the coronet to character. Radicals wield the social sense as accusation and the moral sense as reproach — the ignoble nobility is a favourite nineteenth-century paradox — while conservatives defend the estate as the ballast of the constitution. Few words thus carry their own critique so openly: nobility honours what it names and indicts it in the same breath.
