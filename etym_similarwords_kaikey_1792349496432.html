• consort: Similar because both refer to the royal spouse of a monarch; differs because consort is a general functional title denoting any reigning sovereign's marital partner without literary or mythical specificity, whereas Kaikeyī is a specific historical/epic proper noun carrying profound narrative, tragic, and ethical connotations.
  • Kausalyā: Similar because both are queens of King Daśaratha and mothers of royal princes in the Rāmāyaṇa; differs because Kausalyā is the chief senior queen (Mahiṣī), characterized by quiet endurance, maternal grief, and selfless devotion to her firstborn son Rāma, whereas Kaikeyī is the dynamic, beautiful, favored junior wife whose political assertiveness disrupts the succession.
  • Sumitrā: Similar because both are co-wives of King Daśaratha in the Ayodhyā royal household; differs because Sumitrā represents consummate wisdom, spiritual poise, and voluntary self-effacement as the mother of the devoted twins Lakṣmaṇa and Śatrughna, whereas Kaikeyī becomes the epic's primary agent of crisis.
  • Mantharā: Similar because both are central instigators of Rāma's exile; differs because Mantharā is the hunchbacked domestic servant whose malicious rhetoric, class resentment, and whispered paranoia plant the seeds of discord, whereas Kaikeyī is the sovereign queen who executes those suggestions through royal authority and legally binding boons.
  • tragic antagonist: Similar because both act against the epic protagonist; differs because a tragic antagonist is an abstract dramaturgical archetype, whereas Kaikeyī is a nuanced epic individual whose love for Rāma was initially genuine and immense before being poisoned by maternal panic.
  • matriarch: Similar because both denote high-ranking female familial authorities; differs because matriarch suggests overarching venerable leadership and family unity, whereas Kaikeyī's pivotal action fractures the dynastic family and breaks the king's heart.
  • femme fatale: Similar because both use beauty, royal favor, and emotional leverage over a male ruler to alter political affairs; differs because femme fatale denotes a seductive, self-interested villainess driven by personal greed or sexual power, whereas Kaikeyī's motivation is rooted in dynastic maternal protection and ancestral boons, complicated by profound subsequent remorse.
