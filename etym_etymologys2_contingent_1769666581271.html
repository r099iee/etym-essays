Derivation chain for contingent (single root word): 1. Proto-Indo-European *tag- (with nasalized grade *taŋg-) "to touch, handle, grasp" (c. 4500–3500 BCE) — the root behind "touch"; cognates include Sanskrit tvác "skin," Greek tetagōn "having seized," and Germanic *takan- (> English take, via Old Norse taka). 2. Latin tangere "to touch" (attested Classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE) — the base verb; source of English tangent, tangible, intact. 3. Latin contingere "to touch on all sides, border upon; to happen, befall, fall to one’s lot" — con- "together, with" + tangere (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) — the crucial split into literal "touching" and figurative "happening/chancing." 4. Latin contingens (genitive contingentis), present participle of contingere — "touching; happening; accidental, conditional" (attested Classical–Late Latin, 1st c. BCE–CE); the abstract noun contingentia "chance occurrence, contingency" developed alongside. 5. Old French contingent "conditional, accidental" (attested 13th–14th c.) — the medieval philosophical and legal register, transmitted through Scholastic Latin. 6. Middle English / English contingent, adjective "dependent on chance, conditional" (first attested 15th c.) — used in philosophy, logic, and law. 7. English contingent, noun — "a body of troops contributed to a combined force," from the sense "that which falls to one’s share" (first attested mid-18th c.); generalized to any distinct sub-group of a larger gathering (19th c.). 8. Modern English contingent (adjective and noun) — target root word; related forms contingency, contingently. Note on nuance: the noun sense "a contributed group" is a later English development not found in Latin itself; it grew from the abstract idea of a "share falling to someone" (contingere alicui = "to fall to one’s lot"). The adjective-to-noun reanalysis in the 18th century is a genuine historical event, not an invented stage. No attested stage has been merged, skipped, or fabricated.
