The history of cannoned is the history of a reed that became a gun that became a way of moving. It begins not in England but in the marshes of the ancient Near East, where Semitic speakers named the tall hollow reed qanu in Akkadian and qaneh in Hebrew. The word travelled west with trade in reeds, canes, baskets and measuring rods, entering Greek as kanna and Latin as canna, still meaning reed, cane, small tube or pipe. In Latin it already shows the semantic bridge that will later explode: a hollow cylinder, whether botanical or manufactured, whether a flute, a cane, or a conduit.

Medieval Italian took Latin canna and formed the augmentative cannone, literally a big tube. By the early 14th century gunpowder artillery had arrived in Europe, and the great bronze tubes that hurled stone balls were the biggest tubes anyone had seen. They were naturally called cannoni. French borrowed it as canon and Middle English as canon / cannon by the 1400s, first for the weapon itself and then by metonymy for the ball it fired. Phonetic development was simple: Latin /kanna/ with geminate /nn/ survived into Italian, was simplified slightly in French, and stabilized in English with initial /k/ and stressed /ae/.

Once the noun was entrenched, the verb followed. Seventeenth-century English could say to cannon a town, meaning to batter it with cannon, a regular denominal verb like to gun or to bomb later. Eighteenth-century billiards, immensely fashionable in Britain and France, supplied the second verbal birth. In the game then called carambole or cannon-game, the stroke in which the striker’s ball hits two others successively was called a cannon, and to make that stroke was to cannon. Sporting manuals from the 1700s and 1800s speak constantly of cannoning, and the verb acquired its distinctive rebound sense: not merely to hit, but to hit and glance onward.

  • Reed to tube: Semitic reed-name becomes Greco-Latin word for any hollow cylinder, preserving shape as the core.
  • Tube to gun: Italian augmentative narrows to gunpowder artillery, shifting from botanical to military technology in the 1300s.
  • Gun to projectile to trajectory: English metonymy moves from weapon to ball to flight, allowing ball-like motion to be described as cannoning.
  • Bombardment to rebound: verb shifts from firing at to bouncing off, especially via billiards, turning destruction into elastic motion.
  • Sport to farce: 20th-century comic literature generalizes the rebound to human bodies blundering into each other, as in Wodehouse and Waugh.

Socio-cultural context explains why the comic sense triumphed. Victorian and Edwardian Britain loved billiards, loved military metaphor, and loved farce. A word that combined all three — loud, fast, masculine, slightly absurd — was ideal for describing crowded corridors, football charges, motor accidents, and sharks hitting surfboards. P. G. Wodehouse, writing in the 1920s-1930s, uses cannoned precisely for this upper-class slapstick world where bodies are always colliding at the beach, on the golf course, or in the Drones Club.

Modern usage retains that lightly archaic, British-comic flavour. Americans more often say caromed or crashed; Britons still say cannoned into. The -ed form marks completed punctual action and almost always selects a preposition: into for penetration, off for rebound, against for blunt stop. Its survival reminds us how a humble reed-name, via imperial trade, gunpowder revolution, and parlour sport, ended as a favourite verb for describing sharks, schoolboys, and shopping trolleys colliding in English prose.
