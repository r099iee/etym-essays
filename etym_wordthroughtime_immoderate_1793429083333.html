- Late 14th c. — Philosophical and theological borrowing: Borrowed from Latin immoderātus during the rise of scholastic humanism, signifying passions, appetites, or worldly desires that defy Christian virtue and moral temperance.
- 15th–16th c. — Medical humours and bodily regimen: Adopted in Renaissance medical treatises to describe lifestyle factors (immoderate diet, immoderate sleep, immoderate venery) that corrupt the bodily humours and induce disease.
- 17th–18th c. — Enlightenment critique of unreason and physical toil: Used by classical satirists like Jonathan Swift and moral philosophers to condemn dangerous irrationality, fanatical religious zeal, and lethal physical strain (‘immoderate labour’).
- 19th c. — Victorian social and moral critique: Applied to industrial exploitation (immoderate hours of factory work) as well as the emotional excesses of Romantic poetry and the perils of alcoholism.
- Modern day — Formal and evaluative discourse: Retained as a dignified, formal adjective in editorial prose, legal analysis, and ethical discussions, denoting political partisanship, financial greed, or self-destructive habits that lack restraint.
