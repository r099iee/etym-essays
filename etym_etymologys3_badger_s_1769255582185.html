The uncertain birth of the name.

Few English animal names have a more genuinely obscure pedigree than badger, and that obscurity is itself worth attention, because the older English word for the animal was not badger at all but brock — a loan from Celtic (compare Welsh broch and Irish broc) that survived in dialect for centuries. When the new word badger appears in the record in the 1520s, it arrives suddenly and without a clearly documented intermediate stage, and scholars still disagree about what produced it. The two leading theories frame the animal in two different ways. The badge theory sees the badger’s striking black-and-white striped face as a natural “badge” or mark, so that badger is literally “the bearer of a badge”: badge (from Anglo-Norman bage, from Medieval Latin bagia, ultimately of uncertain, possibly Germanic, ancestry) plus the agentive suffix -er. The lair theory instead derives the name from Old French bauge, “a lair or den,” making the badger “the animal of the burrow.” Both words were possible, both would have produced exactly the spelling we have, and neither is attested in a clear chain — so the handbooks print badger with a cautious “origin uncertain.”

From noun to possessive.

The form at issue here, badger’s, is the possessive singular, and its history is transparent even where the noun’s is not. English has had a possessive -es since Old English, where stānes meant “of a stone”; through Middle English the ending became —es, —is, and finally the apostrophe-s of the modern language. By the 17th century the written apostrophe had been inserted to mark the genitive, and the spoken form — a final /z/ after the r — has never changed. “Badger’s face” is thus an exact grammatical descendant of the oldest English possessive pattern, even though the noun it attaches to is itself only five hundred years old.

The cultural biography of the animal.

How the word felt changed as fast as the animal’s fortunes. In the 16th and 17th centuries the badger was a wild, hunted creature of the countryside, pursued for its fur and its bristles (long used in brushes) and tormented in the brutal sport of badger-baiting, in which dogs were set on the cornered animal. It is directly from that baiting that the English verb to badger was born in the 18th century: to worry, pester, or hound someone relentlessly, as the dogs harry the badger. The verb is now far more frequent in ordinary speech than the noun’s possessive, and it tints every later use of the word with a sense of dogged, relentless persistence. Meanwhile the animal itself was revalued in folklore as wise, old, and stubbornly independent — a creature that minds its own business and will not be shifted — and that is the image C. S. Lewis draws on when he makes the badger Trufflehunter the voice of old-fashioned loyalty and common sense among the Old Narnians.

Badger’s in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

In modern English the possessive surfaces in a range of settled contexts: the badger’s sett (its burrow), badger’s hair and badger’s bristles in the brush trade, the badger’s image as mascot of the University of Wisconsin and of countless scout and sports clubs, and the celebrated badger in the children’s classic The Wind in the Willows, where Badger is the gruff, dependable elder of the riverbank. At the same time the real animal has passed from vermin to protected species: badger culling, badger sett protection, and the badger’s role in bovine tuberculosis policy have made the word a fixture of environmental and agricultural headlines. When Lewis writes “but a badger’s,” then, the bare possessive compresses this whole history into one astonished instant — the old folk beast of the setts, the baited victim of the baiting-rings, the tenacious image behind the verb, and the speaking, intelligent friend who looks back at Caspian in the firelight. The apostrophe is the same one English has used for a thousand years; the wonder it frames is entirely the author’s own.
