- late 14th c. — the small sharp bite: nippen names pinching and biting little, a barnyard and kitchen word for what small teeth and claws do.
- 16th c. — the weather learns to bite: nipping frost and nipping winds enter poetry and proverb, and the cold becomes an animal with teeth.
- 17th c. — the gardener's fingers: nipping out buds and shoots turns the pinch into cultivation, a constructive rather than hostile act.
- 19th c. — brisk British movement: to nip out or nip along becomes the idiom of the quick errand, and the street, not the farmyard, owns the word.
- early 20th c. — slang sips: a nip of spirits makes nipping the tippler's verb, small sharp mouthfuls against winter.
- Today — playful and bracing: puppies nip, winds nip, neighbors nip to the shops — the word stays small, quick, and faintly cheerful wherever it lands.
