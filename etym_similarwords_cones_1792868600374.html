• Pinecones: Similar because they are the classic fallen cones of the pine; differs because pinecones names one genus only, while cones gathers pine, fir, spruce, and larch together.
  • Fir cones: Similar because both are conifer seed-cases gathered as kindling; differs because fir cones disintegrate scale by scale on the tree, so whole fallen fir cones are rarer finds than pinecones.
  • Strobili: Similar because a strobilus is botanically a cone; differs because strobili is the technical term of textbooks and keys, while cones is the fireside and schoolroom word.
  • Seed pods: Similar because both are dry cases built to hold and scatter seed; differs because pods belong to flowering plants, splitting along seams, while cones are gymnosperm scale-clusters that open and close with the weather.
  • Catkins: Similar because male conifer cones are soft pollen-shedding catkin-like bodies; differs because true catkins belong to willow, birch, and hazel, and no catkin ever hardens into a woody seed-cone.
  • Conkers: Similar because both are glossy autumn seeds children pocket from the woods; differs because a conker is the single seed of the horse chestnut, while a cone is a whole case of many winged seeds.
  • Acorns: Similar because both litter autumn woods and feed fireside foraging; differs because an acorn is the single nut of the oak in its cup, while a cone is a scaled architecture housing dozens of seeds.
  • Kindling: Similar because gathered cones serve as kindling, catching flame to start the fire; differs because kindling is the functional role, any small dry fuel, while cones names the particular resin-rich fuel doing the job.
