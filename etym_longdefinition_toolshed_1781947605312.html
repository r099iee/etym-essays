In The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the toolshed is a place of refuge that instantly becomes a trap. Fleeing from Mr. McGregor, Peter rushes into the shed and jumps into a watering-can — which, the narrator notes with deadpan irony, was full of water. The word itself is deliberately humble and concrete: a toolshed is a small outbuilding for storing garden implements, and Potter places this plain man-made structure inside the wild, dangerous garden to heighten the comedy of the collision between a rabbit and human domesticity. Its tone is matter-of-fact, practical, and faintly reassuring — a word that names a place where a child might take shelter — which is exactly what makes the dousing in the watering-can so satisfyingly ironic.

Primary sense. A toolshed is a small building or shed used for storing garden implements and tools — spades, rakes, hoes, trowels, watering-cans, and the like. It is typically a modest wooden or brick structure standing at the edge of a garden, allotment, or farmyard. The compound is head-final: a shed for tools, with the first element specifying purpose. Its defining feature is the storage of implements, not the performance of work — which is what separates it from a workshop.

Polysemy and extended senses:

  • Garden storage: the core sense, from the 19th century; collocations include wooden toolshed, garden toolshed, the toolshed door, at the bottom of the garden.
  • Workshop-adjacent use: loosely, any small outbuilding where tools are kept and minor repairs are done; the boundary with workshop is a matter of activity rather than size.
  • Software engineering metaphor (modern): a "toolshed" or "tool shed" is a repository or collection of small utility scripts, programs, or command-line tools kept for reuse — a digital storehouse parallel to the physical one.
  • British comic overtone (20th c.): the shed became a mildly comic symbol of male domestic retreat (the "man shed," the garden shed as a private den), so "toolshed" carries a faint humorous, self-deprecating register in modern British usage.
  • Community "Men's Sheds" movement: since the 2000s, community workshops for older men are called "Men's Sheds" or "tool sheds," reviving the word as a social-project name.
  • Organizational metaphor: "the toolshed" as the storehouse of an organization's instruments, methods, or capabilities (rare, but parallel to "toolbox" and "toolkit").

Register and spelling. The register is informal-domestic and rural, the everyday vocabulary of gardeners and children's literature; it sits naturally alongside potting shed, wheelbarrow, and watering-can — the whole material world of the Edwardian English garden. Spelling has moved from tool shed (open) and tool-shed (hyphenated, the Edwardian norm) to the closed compound toolshed (modern standard). In Potter the word simply marks the modest human structure in whose practical interior one small rabbit is about to get very wet.
