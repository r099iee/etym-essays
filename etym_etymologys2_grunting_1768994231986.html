GRUNTING — the present participle of English grunt. Note: no Proto-Indo-European root is reconstructible; the grunt-words form an onomatopoeic family, independently created wherever speakers met pigs, and the similar forms across Germanic and Italic are parallel sound-imitations, not inherited from a common PIE ancestor. 1. Proto-Germanic *grunnōjaną "to grunt" — an expressive, onomatopoeic verb (c. 500 BCE–500 CE); sisters include Old High German grunnizōn "to grunt," Old Saxon grunnian, Dutch gronzen, and Scandinavian grynte / grymta. 2. Old English grunian "to grunt, to utter a low guttural noise" (attested from the 8th–9th c. CE). 3. Old English grunnettan "to keep grunting" — a frequentative formation in -ettan, "to do repeatedly" (attested; this is the form that produced the modern verb). 4. Middle English grunten "to grunt" (attested from the 14th c. CE). 5. English to grunt "to make a short, low, guttural throat-sound" (modern verb), and its present participle English grunting — the rootWord on this card: GRUNTING. Sister branch (Italic): Latin grunnīre "to grunt" (of pigs) — an independent Italic imitation — gave grunnītus "a grunting," and the Romance verbs Italian grugnire, Spanish gruñir, Portuguese grunhir; French grogner drifted toward human grumbling. Latin also gave the gurnard, the "grunting" fish (via Old French gournart, by metathesis of grun- to gurn-, c. 1300), and English later named the grunt fish (1713) for the same noise. Debate note: because the word is onomatopoeic, any claim of a single inherited PIE root (e.g. a reconstruction *ghreu-) is rejected by the handbooks; the family resemblance is acoustic, not genetic — languages re-create the noise independently.
