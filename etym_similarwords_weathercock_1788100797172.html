• Weathervane: Similar because it is the general term for the pivoted wind-direction instrument, and 'weathercock' is technically a rooster-shaped weathervane; differs because 'weathervane' is the neutral, modern, all-shapes term, while 'weathercock' names the traditional cock form and carries the figurative register of fickleness.
  • Vane: Similar because a vane is the swinging indicator itself in any shape, and 'weathercock' is one species of vane; differs because 'vane' (from Old English fana, 'flag, banner') is a bare technical syllable naming the whole class, with none of the barnyard charm or the moral censure.
  • Windsock: Similar because a windsock also indicates wind direction and is used at airfields; differs because a windsock is a fabric cone, not a rigid pivoted pointer, gives approximate rather than exact direction, and has no figurative or fickle-person sense.
  • Anemometer: Similar because it also measures the wind; differs because an anemometer measures wind speed (and is a precision instrument), whereas a weathercock shows direction and never measures anything.
  • Turncoat: Similar because both figuratively name a person who abandons one side for another — each swings with prevailing power; differs because 'turncoat' is purely a traitor or party-deserter with a sharp moral charge, while 'weathercock' is a milder taunt for the merely changeable and opinionated.
  • Trimmer: Similar because a trimmer is a 17th-century political time-server who 'trims' his sails between factions, the human counterpart of the vane; differs because 'trimmer' implies opportunistic calculation between two parties, while 'weathercock' implies mere inconstancy blown by the latest opinion.
  • Chameleon: Similar because both words metaphorically describe people who adapt to their surroundings; differs because the chameleon image is about blending in and taking the colour of the environment, while the weathercock image is about swinging publicly with the wind — adaptation to opinion rather than to surroundings.
  • Flip-flopper: Similar because both are modern American slang for a politician who reverses positions; differs because 'flip-flopper' is colloquial, recent and partisan, while 'weathercock' is literary, four centuries old, and carries a wry, mocking elegance.
