Stumblest is the archaic second-person singular present of the verb stumble: thou stumblest means you stumble, addressed to a single familiar thou in the grammar of the King James Bible, Shakespeare, and early wonder tales. The -est ending is the whole story of its form; the stumble stem carries all of its rich meaning, from tripping hooves to faltering souls.

In the source sentence a rider berates his horse: Why stumblest thou, sorry nag — why do you trip and falter under me, wretched beast. The verb here is at its most literal: a horse catching its foot, breaking gait, jolting its rider. But the surrounding tale gives the literal trip a moral shadow, for wonder-tale horses that stumble are very often wiser than their riders, faltering for a reason — enchanted ground, a taboo broken, a doom ahead — and the rider's anger marks him, not the beast, as the one who errs.

Core meaning in detail: To stumble is to strike the foot against an obstacle so that the body lurches, nearly falls, or breaks its rhythm — the half-fall, the caught trip, the recovered totter. From this bodily picture the word radiates outward into every kind of faltering: the tongue stumbles over unfamiliar names, the memory stumbles over dates, the conscience stumbles into fault, the reader stumbles upon a discovery. The archaic stumblest can bear every one of these senses wherever a thou subject governs it, though in tales of riders and horses the hoof-trip leads.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To trip or miss one's footing (primary literal sense): the foot catches, the gait breaks, the body pitches forward — of people on rough ground and supremely of horses, whose stumbling unseats riders and loses races. The bridge is the word's very core: all other senses are metaphors of this lurch.
  • To falter in speech or progress (figurative): to stumble over a word, through a recitation, through a difficult passage. The bridge is rhythmic: speech and narrative are journeys of their own, and hesitation is a trip of the tongue exactly as a stone causes a trip of the foot.
  • To err morally or spiritually (biblical and literary sense): thou stumblest in a sermon means thou fallest into sin; the stumbling-block is the classic scriptural image of an occasion of sin. The bridge runs through the prophets and epistles, where the lurching body figures the lurching soul, and a stone in the path becomes a temptation in the life.
  • To happen or light upon — to stumble on or upon (idiomatic): to find by accident what was never sought. The bridge is the trip revalued: the same interrupted step that spills the walker delivers the treasure to the seeker, so that clumsiness becomes providence.
  • To stagger under a burden or blow (extended physical sense): boxers stumble, empires stumble, markets stumble. The bridge generalizes the failing support: whatever should hold upright — legs, economies, arguments — can lurch, and stumble names the lurch in them all.
  • Second-person archaic address (grammatical sense of the -est): stumblest marks its subject as thou and its time as Early Modern English. The bridge is pure morphology: as lovest is to love, so stumblest is to stumble — intimacy and antiquity in a single syllable.

Register and connotation: Stumblest is doubly marked: humble in meaning, lofty in form. Nobody trips in a grander tense than thou stumblest, and the comedy of the source sentence — high biblical grammar spent on a scolded horse — is part of the tale's humour. Elsewhere the form is solemn: psalms, prayers, and prophecies ask why the wicked stumble not and why the righteous stumblest thou. The word always slows the modern reader down, forcing the mouth around the clustered consonants — a small philological stumble of its own.
