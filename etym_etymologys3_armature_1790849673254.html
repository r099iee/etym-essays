The noun armature demonstrates a remarkable historical transformation from ancient military weaponry and defensive plate armor into high-technology electrical machinery, plastic arts engineering, and structuralist literary analysis.

The ultimate origin of armature is the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₂er-, signifying "to fit together, join, connect, or adapt." This root was exceptionally productive across Indo-European, generating words related to fitting joints, harmonious ordering, and crafted implements, including Sanskrit aram ("fittingly"), Ancient Greek harmos (ἁρμός, "joint, shoulder" > English harmony), Greek arthron (ἄρθρον, "joint" > English arthritis), Latin artus ("joint, limb"), and Latin ars ("skill, craft, art").

In the Italic branch, the root gave rise to the Proto-Italic neuter plural noun *armā-, which in classical Latin became arma (neuter plural, meaning "tools, implements, defensive equipment, weapons of war"). From arma, Latin formed the denominal verb armare ("to furnish with weapons, equip, outfit"). In the first century BCE, during the late Roman Republic, Latin writers formed the abstract feminine noun armatura by attaching the suffix of action and resulting apparatus -tura to the past participial stem armat-:

  • In classical Latin (Cicero, Caesar, Tacitus), armatura carried three related military meanings: (1) defensive armor and equipment; (2) armed soldiers or troops categorized by their equipment (e.g., levis armatura, "light-armed troops"); and (3) systematic weapon training and tactical drills taught to gladiators and legionaries.

Following the decline of the Roman Empire, armatura passed into Old French and Middle French as armature (alongside armure, which became English armor), denoting protective battle gear and structural iron reinforcement used in medieval masonry and window glazing.

English borrowed armature in the early seventeenth century (c. 1600s), initially using it in the direct sense of defensive armor and martial equipment. However, during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the term experienced a series of specialized technical adaptations:

  • Biology (1750s): Naturalists applied armature to the offensive and defensive appendages of animals and plants—such as claws, beaks, shells, and thorns.
  • Fine Arts (1830s): Sculptors borrowed the term from French studios to describe the internal wooden, lead, or iron framework that supports heavy modeling clay or plaster during the creation of a sculpture.
  • Electromagnetism (1830s–1880s): Early electrical pioneers (such as Michael Faraday) used armature to designate the piece of soft iron placed across the poles of a permanent magnet (the "keeper") to preserve magnetic power. When dynamos and electric motors were invented, the term was applied to the revolving iron core wound with copper coils that cuts through the magnetic field to generate electricity.
  • Structuralism and Semiotics (1950s–1970s): Structural anthropologists (Claude Lévi-Strauss) and semioticians (Roland Barthes) adopted armature as a metaphor for the rigid, foundational framework of grammar, syntax, and narrative relations that supports meaning in language and myth.
