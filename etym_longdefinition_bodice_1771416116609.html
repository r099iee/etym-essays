Core definition. Bodice names the close-fitting upper part of a woman's dress — the section that encases the torso from the shoulders or neckline down to the waist, generally excluding the sleeves. It is a sartorial, formal word, carrying the vocabulary of the dressmaker's workshop: a bodice is by definition shaped and structured to the body, whether by seams and darts or by boning and stiffening, and it is integral to the dress that hangs below it rather than a garment in its own right. Historically the word has a remarkable origin, being a plural that was reborn as a singular: it comes from the Tudor tailor's phrase "a pair of bodies," where "bodies" meant the torso-coverings of a woman's gown, and the plural was eventually reinterpreted as a single garment called a bodice. In the literary context. In Dream of the Red Chamber the word appears in the dress of Wang Xi-feng: "Her dress had a fitted bodice and was made of dark red silk damask with a pattern of flowers and butterflies in raised gold thread." The qualifier "fitted" is decisive: a bodice is by definition shaped to the body, and a "fitted" one declares precision tailoring — seams, darts, and cut engineered to follow the wearer's form. Paired with dark red silk damask and raised gold thread, the bodice becomes the architectural core of a costume that is simultaneously textile and armour of rank: structured, expensive, immovable in its elegance. The word silently emphasizes Xi-feng's physical presence — the bodily authority the garment both reveals and encloses — and it carries the register of formal, historical dress that the translator intends for a great noble lady of eighteenth-century China. Exhaustive sense inventory. Though its central meaning is stable, bodice has developed a small cluster of senses and uses across fashion history and popular culture:
  • Core — the fitted upper part of a woman's dress (since the 1560s–70s): the portion of a gown above the waist, excluding sleeves; the dominant modern sense.
  • Historical — the stiffened, laced upper garment of Tudor and Stuart dress: originally "a pair of bodies," the two-part, frequently boned and laced torso garment that could be worn as the bodice of a dress or over it as a separate stiffened piece; the sense from which the word itself derives.
  • In modern fashion — the structured, corseted-looking torso section of a gown or bodysuit: a bodice in contemporary dressmaking is still the shaped torso of a one-piece garment, now often with boning, cups, or a sweetheart neckline; it survives in bridal, ball, and formal wear.
  • Popular-culture extension — "bodice-ripper": a colloquial genre term for a historical romance novel, especially one set in the Regency or Victorian era, so called from the trope of tearing a heroine's bodice in passionate scenes; the phrase transfers the garment to the plot it signals and is now used affectionately or dismissively of the whole genre.
  • Occasional figurative use — the "torso" of a garment or object: in historical and craft writing the word may designate any close-fitting upper covering, and by loose extension the body-covering portion of armour or costume, though these uses are rare and consciously archaic.
The semantic bridge. The historical bridge between the senses is the garment's own structure. Because the Tudor bodice was laced as two halves, tailors called it "a pair of bodies"; because the plural felt increasingly like the name of a single garment, it was reinterpreted as singular and respelled bodice (final -ice fixed by the eighteenth century). The word then slid easily from "the stiffened torso garment" to "the upper part of any dress," and from the sewing-room to the novels that dramatized the garment's ripping. The genre term bodice-ripper shows a garment noun generating a literary label — a metonymy in which the article of clothing stands for the kind of story in which it figures. Against near-synonyms. Against its near-synonyms, bodice holds a precise niche. A corset is an undergarment of stiffened cloth or boning worn beneath the dress to mould the figure; a bodice is the visible outer garment, even when (historically) it was itself stiffened. Blouse and shirt name loose, separate upper garments, being garments in their own right rather than parts of a one-piece dress; a bodice is integral to the dress that hangs below it. Top is the modern catch-all, informal and unstyled, carrying none of the historical tailoring the bodice implies. The American waist once named exactly this region of a dress but stressed the waistline, whereas the bodice covers the whole torso down to that line. A bustier is a modern, often strapless and revealing garment of the same region, erotic rather than dignified in tone. A camisole is a loose undergarment. And a stomacher is the decorative triangular panel worn over the front of a bodice in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — an accessory to the bodice rather than a rival name for it. It is this combination of structure, integration with the dress, and historical depth that distinguishes the bodice from all of them.
