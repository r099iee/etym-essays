• Dismissed: Similar because both mean removed from a position; differs because dismissed is neutral, broad, and often innocent (redundancy, end of contract), while cashiered always involves formal disgrace and the stripping of rank.
  • Discharged: Similar because both release a person from service; differs because discharge can be honourable, routine, or medical, whereas cashiering is always punitive and shameful.
  • Sacked: Similar because both mean fired; differs because sacked is informal, civilian, and usually financial, lacking the ceremony of dishonour inherent in cashiering.
  • Fired: Similar because both remove a person from employment; differs because fired is blunt, contemporary, and value-neutral, while cashiered is archaic, formal, and marks moral failure.
  • Ousted: Similar because both force a person out of a position; differs because ousted focuses on removal from power or influence, while cashiered emphasises the ritual, moral destruction of standing.
  • Expelled: Similar because both eject a person from a group; differs because expelled is used of schools, parliaments, and organisations, while cashiered is specifically military and hierarchical.
  • Deposed: Similar because both strip a person of authority; differs because deposed refers to a ruler or leader overthrown from sovereignty, while cashiered refers to an officer or official dismissed by their own institution.
  • Drummed out: Similar because both describe military dismissal in disgrace; differs because 'drummed out' names the ceremonial removal from the ranks (a soldier marched out to the beat of a drum), while cashiered is the general formal term for the same shame.
  • Cast aside: Similar because both discard a person or thing as worthless; differs because cast aside is a general metaphor with no institutional, ritual, or ceremonial weight.
  • Divested: Similar because both strip a person of rank or office; differs because divested is neutral and legal (to divest of authority), lacking the disgrace and punishment of cashiering.
