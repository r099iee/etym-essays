The lineage of obstinacy begins with one of the most productive roots in all of Proto-Indo-European: *steh₂-, "to stand, make stand, set firm," whose zero grade *sth₂- lies beneath a staggering number of European and Asian words. The core image is verticality and immobility — a thing planted, fixed, immovable. From this single root the Italic branch produced Latin stāre "to stand" and its many compounds; the Hellenic branch produced Greek hístēmi, stásis, sýstēma; the Indo-Iranian branch produced Sanskrit sthā- and Avestan hištaiti; the Germanic branch produced English stand and German stehen; and the Balto-Slavic branch produced Lithuanian stóti and Russian stojátʹ. Every one of these keeps the root's essential meaning of standing, and it is this immobility — standing planted and refusing to move — that gives obstinacy its psychological content.

The specifically Roman development turned the root toward the vocabulary of will. To the simple stem stāre, Latin added the prefix ob-, "towards, against, in the way of," and built the denominative verb obstināre, "to stand firm against, to persist." The -in- element (*stināre, also seen in dēstināre "to fix, appoint" and praedestināre) is a nasal-present formation meaning "to make stand," so that obstināre literally suggests "planting oneself in the way." In early Latin the word could be honourable: obstinātus meant "resolute, steadfast," the quality of a soldier or a loyal ally, and obstināta virtus is "unconquerable resolution." But even in classical usage the seeds of censure are present, and in the moralising Latin of late antiquity and of Christian theology the meaning hardened: obstinātiō became the stiff-necked refusal to yield to right, reason, or grace — stubbornness against God — one of the marks of the reprobate soul. The abstract noun obstinātia, formed with the same -tia suffix as audācia from audāx, was the immediate ancestor of the French obstinacie and of the English obstinacy attested from the 1380s.

Form and transmission. The word reached English by the normal Anglo-French route: Late Latin obstinātia → Old French obstinacie → Middle English obstinacie (Chaucer's age, c. 1380s) → Modern English obstinacy, the -cie/-cy spelling showing the French abstract suffix anglicised. The adjective obstinate came separately, from Latin obstinātus via Old French obstiné, in the fourteenth century; the rare noun obstination (from obstinātiō) is a scholarly doublet of the fifteenth century. English thus possesses obstinacy, obstinate, and obstination as three loan-layers from one Latin cluster.

Semantic drift: from virtue to vice. The moral career of the word is a steady darkening. Latin obstinātus could still be praised; the English obstinate and obstinacy, from their first appearance, tilt toward censure — stubbornness against reason rather than steadfastness for it. The seventeenth-century essayists and divines (Burton, Browne, the sermon tradition) fixed the word in the vocabulary of moral failure, and eighteenth- and nineteenth-century historiography made it a political accusation. It is precisely this developed, censorious sense that Macaulay exploits: "imprudence and obstinacy broke the ties which bound the North American colonies to the parent state." The two nouns pair folly of judgement with hardness of will; obstinacy is the vice of rulers who will not bend, and the etymology — planting oneself in the way — still audibly underlies the image of a ministry standing immovably across the road to compromise.

Modern career. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries obstinacy has receded from speech, displaced by plain stubbornness and by the political intransigence, but it survives in careful formal prose precisely because it is weighty, formal, and morally decisive. Its sister words — stand, state, status, constant, obstacle, destiny, system, stasis, assist, resist, exist — are a roll-call of the standing-root's extraordinary productivity across four thousand years and a dozen languages. Obstinacy remains the word for the moment when standing becomes refusal.
