The noun Muse descends from one of the most prolific roots of the Indo-European lexicon: the verb root *men-, "to think, to remember, to have in mind." In the Hellenic branch this root was extended with a feminine suffix (-tyā) to form *mont-yā, "the remembering one," which became the Classical Greek noun Moûsa (Μοῦσα) — the Muse. The ancient Greeks themselves felt the connection: the Muses were the daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne, Memory herself, because in an oral culture the poet's song was literally a feat of remembrance — the poem the Muse dictated was memory made audible. Every invocation of the Muse at the start of an epic poem (the Iliad, the Odyssey, and the hymns that followed them for a thousand years) is thus a ritual act of remembering.

The root *men- and its family

The root *men- produced a vast constellation of sister words across the family:
  • Greek: mnēmē ("memory," whence mnemonic and the goddess Mnemosyne), mántis ("seer," one who reflects on divine things, whence mantis and the word mantle), mēnis (the brooding wrath with which the Iliad opens), and the verb mnaomai ("remember, be mindful of").
  • Italic and Latin: mēns ("mind"), meminisse ("to remember," whence memory, memorable, memorial), monēre ("to remind, warn," whence admonish, monitor, monument), mentālis ("of the mind," whence mental, mention, mentation).
  • Germanic: *gamundiz ("remembrance"): Old English gemynd gave English mind; Gothic gamunds glossed "remembrance"; Old Norse minni meant "memory"; Old High German minna, a "loving remembrance," became Middle High German Minne, the secular courtly love of the medieval lyric.
  • Indo-Iranian and beyond: Sanskrit mánas ("mind, spirit," whence the English psychology term manas), Avestan manah-; the same root appears in Old Irish menme ("mind") and, via *mn̥-to-, in the family of mania and of mantra.
  • Hindi connection: Hindi man (मन, "mind") and the Sanskrit-derived words for thought all continue this same *men- — which is why the mnemonic's "man + us" (inspiration lives in our man, our mind, whispered by the Muse) is etymologically sound.

From Greek to English

From Greek the word passed to Latin as Mūsa, then through Vulgar Latin into Old French Muse, which gave English Muse in the late fourteenth century — Chaucer already uses it in his invocations. The sequence is: PIE *men- → Greek *mont-yā → Moûsa → Latin Mūsa → Old French Muse → Middle English Muse → Modern English Muse. Modern scholars such as Beekes note that the -s- cluster in Moûsa is not entirely regular for a derivation from *men-, and a Pre-Greek substratum origin has been proposed; but the traditional and still-standard account — "the remembrancer," built on *men- — remains the derivation adopted by the lexicographical mainstream, and the ancient Greek belief in the Muses as daughters of Memory is its cultural confirmation.

The verb: a different, obscure line

The English verb muse is etymologically a different word. It descends from Old French muser, "to loiter, idle, gape, be idle; to ponder," whose base is muse, "muzzle, snout" — the image of an animal standing with its muzzle raised, vacant and dreaming. The Old French word (attested from the twelfth century) is usually traced to Gallo-Romance *mūsa, itself of obscure origin, possibly Germanic (compare Middle Dutch muys, "snout") or perhaps onomatopoeic. From "idle waiting with the nose in the air" the sense slid to "lost in thought," and Middle English borrowed musen in the late fourteenth century with the meaning "to ponder, reflect, be absorbed." Because the spelling collided with the noun Muse, English speakers have conflated the two for over six centuries: to be "mused" is to be under a goddess's spell; to "muse" is to be lost in one's own thoughts — and it is arguably that very conflation that gave the verb its dreamy, inspired colour.

Cultural career

The Muse was a ritual invocation in Homer and Hesiod; the muse became the courtly patroness of Renaissance art and the standing figure of the epic invocation in Milton ("Sing Heav'nly Muse"); and by the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word secularized into the artist's inner or external inspiration — the "muse" one marries or loses. In modern usage the lowercase muse is any person or thing that inspires creative work, while the capitalized Muse survives only in classical and archaic literary contexts. The verb has remained quietly alive in literary prose for six centuries. The word is thus a double fossil: a goddess who began as a memory-figure, and a homonymic verb born from a muzzle.
