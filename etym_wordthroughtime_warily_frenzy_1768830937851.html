warily:
- Native prudence (Old English to 16th c.) - the adjective 'wary' grows out of the old word for being aware and on one's guard; early use is plain and moral - the wary man does not walk into traps
- The adverb settles (16th-17th c.) - 'warily' becomes a fixed feature of prose for careful, prudent action: the register of statecraft, commerce, and good counsel, caution without alarm
- Watchful narrative caution (19th-20th c.) - the word drifts into fiction and its colour changes: warily now describes the vigilance of hunters, soldiers, and detectives - alert senses rather than mere prudence
- Contemporary - the adverb of studied composure: not timidity but heightened perception, the stance of a fighter who has read the danger coming and is already moving to the safe side of it

frenzy:
- A clinical condition (14th c.) - 'frenzy' names an actual medical disease: an inflammation of the brain producing delirium, mania, and hallucination; the word is strictly the doctor's
- Widening to behavior (16th-17th c.) - the word moves from the sickness to any fit of wild excitement, agitation, or rage; fury becomes frenzy when it begins to look like temporary madness
- Romantic intensity (18th-19th c.) - poets seize on the feverish metaphor: frenzy is a mind on fire, passion as combustion; the clinical origin still colors its literary use
- Contemporary - a strong word for rage as disease: anger so intense it has burned past the reach of reason, a blaze that no discipline can cool
