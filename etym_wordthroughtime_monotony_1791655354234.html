- Early 18th Century (c. 1700–1720): Classical acoustic and musical defect - Coined in English to describe chanting, reciting, or speaking upon an unvarying musical pitch without vocal inflection.
- Mid to Late 18th Century: Extension to literary style, landscape, and art - Applied by Augustan critics (Pope, Johnson) to poems with unvarying meter, flat landscapes lacking scenic variety, and uninspired prose.
- 19th Century (Industrial Revolution): Socio-psychological affliction and factory alienation - Emerged as the central critique of mechanization, describing the soul-crushing routine of assembly lines and urban working-class life.
- Early to Mid-20th Century: Existential dread and modernist alienation - Explored by modern philosophers, novelists (Kafka, Camus, Woolf), and historians (Durant) as a primary catalyst for neurosis, rebellion, and war.
- Late 20th to 21st Century: Universal psychological and clinical term - Widely used to denote corporate cubicle drudgery, algorithmic feed fatigue, and neurological speech flattening (aprosody).
