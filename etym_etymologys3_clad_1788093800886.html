The oldest layer: cloth. The family of clad begins not with a verb but with a noun: the reconstructed Proto-Germanic *klaiþą, "cloth, garment", the ancestor of English cloth, German Kleid ("garment"), and the Germanic verb family of clothing. The word is attested across the Germanic languages — Old English clāþ ("cloth, woven fabric, garment"), Old Saxon and Old High German kleid — but its deeper history is closed to us: the dictionaries record the ultimate origin of *klaiþą as unknown, with no secure Indo-European etymology. What is certain is its Germanic substance: it is a word for the woven thing that the body wears, and from it Old English built the verb clāþian (attested from the ninth century), "to clothe, to cover with garments". At this stage the entire family is plain and everyday: the vocabulary of dressing, not of ceremony.

Middle English: the participle contracts. In Middle English the verb clothe (with its participle clothede) began, in rapid speech, to shed its interior: the participle contracted to cladde and then clad by the fourteenth century — a natural clipping exactly parallel to lit from lighted or dreamt from dreamed. Both forms, clothed and clad, then coexisted for the next six centuries, with clad carrying a progressively more formal, poetic and archaic colour while clothed stayed neutral. Because clad is not a new formation but a contraction, its phonetic history is the familiar Middle English story of unstressed vowel loss and consonant simplification — the same pressures that produced lord (from hlāfweard, "loaf-ward") and lady (from hlæfdige).

  • 14th–15th centuries: the form stabilises. Middle English verse and prose use clad freely; it is in this period the natural past of clothe in many dialects, and the participial adjective works at full strength: a knight "clad in armour", a monk "clad in grey". The word belongs to the high-style stratum of romance where armour and vestments both shine.
  • 16th–18th centuries: the poetic twin. As clothed became the ordinary prose form, clad took on the mantle of literature: biblical translation and elevated prose prefer it for dignity (the Authorised Version uses both, deploying clothed most often but reserving the occasional clad for poetry of texture), and the later eighteenth century hands it to the nascent Romantic vocabulary of the picturesque — mountains clad in mist, valleys clad in forest.
  • 1850s: ironclad. In 1852–1853 the word re-enters history as technology: the first armoured warships were called iron-clad (the French La Gloire, 1859, and the British Warrior, 1860, fixed the type), and ironclad swept instantly into metaphor — an "ironclad" promise or rule is one that cannot be breached. The whole family of compounds — steel-clad, armour-clad, snow-clad, ivy-clad, tree-clad — flowers in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, each one dressing a surface in its material.
  • 20th century: the builders borrow it back. In the mid-twentieth century, architecture and construction re-imported the word as cladding: the outer skin of a building — timber cladding, stone cladding, rain-screen panels — and the verb "to clad a facade". The semantic circle is complete: from the garment that clothes the body, to the surface that clothes a building, to the armour that clothes a ship, the participle has never stopped meaning "fitted with a covering" — and every new application recharges its old literal force.
  • 1937: Tolkien and the mail-clad host. In The Hobbit, the sentence on this card is a miniature of the word's literary power: "Each one of his folk was clad in a hauberk of steel mail that hung to his knees." The archaic participle and the archaic loan-word cooperate — the clipped form and the Frankish armour both pull the prose backwards into a heroic register while the syntax remains perfectly modern. Philologists like Tolkien knew precisely why "clad" beats "clothed" here: it is the older form, the shorter word, and the one that sounds like steel put on.

Grammar and present state. In modern English, clad is fully standard as the past and past participle of clothe in literary and technical use; it forms compounds freely (ironclad, mail-clad, snow-clad, glass-clad); and as cladding it has become a technical noun of the building trades. Its register has never been vulgar and its imagery never weakened: from Old English weaving-looms to medieval mail, from Victorian ironclads to architects' facades, clad has spent a thousand years doing exactly one thing — fitting a surface with its covering.
