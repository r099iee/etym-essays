- 8th century, Old High German - wazzar names the substance, the element and the biblical waters; it is one of the four elements in monastic cosmology and a staple of baptismal language.
- 12th–14th century, Middle High German - wazzer also means a river, stream or lake, and courtly poetry uses flowing water as an image of mutability and of love's passing.
- 16th century, Reformation - Luther's Bible fixes Wasser in the creation narrative, the Flood and the baptism of Christ; the word gains a strong theological charge of judgment and renewal.
- 17th–18th century, early science - water is studied as an element and then as a compound; the German chemical vocabulary coins Wasserstoff for hydrogen, making the word a building block of nomenclature.
- 19th century, industry and medicine - spas, hydropathy and urban water supply turn Wasser into a public utility and a health cure; Wassermann becomes the ordinary name of Aquarius.
- early 20th century, war and infrastructure - Wasser marsch! becomes the firefighters' command, and dams, canals and Abwasser systems make the word central to engineering and hygiene.
- late 20th century, environmentalism - Wasserverschmutzung, Grundwasser and Wasserknappheit make the word a political keyword of ecology and development.
- 21st century - bottled water, water rights and climate-driven drought turn Wasser into a contested commodity; the old element word now carries the anxiety of scarcity.
