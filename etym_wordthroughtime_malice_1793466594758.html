- 14th c. — Theological Iniquity and Mortal Sin: Borrowed from Old French into Middle English; denotes general wickedness, active sinfulness, and the moral corruption of the soul in contrast to divine charity.
- 15th to 16th c. — Codification of Common Law Murder: English jurists adopt the Anglo-French formula malice prepense (later malice aforethought) as the indispensable mental element dividing capital murder from manslaughter.
- 17th to 18th c. — Augustan Moral Psychology: In Enlightenment essays (Swift, Pope, Johnson), malice becomes the defining vice of polite society—a covert, envious resentment that quietly rejoices in the downfall of neighbors and living genius.
- 19th c. — Civil Liability and Victorian Tort Law: Expands across legal doctrine into civil wrongs; courts distinguish between 'express malice' (actual spite) and 'malice in law' (intentional wrongdoing without legal excuse).
- Mid-20th c. — Constitutional Shield for Free Speech: The US Supreme Court in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) establishes the 'actual malice' doctrine, transforming the word into the ultimate legal benchmark for defamation against public figures.
- 21st c. — Digital Warfare and Information Security: Shapes digital terminology via malware and malicious cyber actors, framing unauthorized software intrusions as modern iterations of deliberate hostile intent.
