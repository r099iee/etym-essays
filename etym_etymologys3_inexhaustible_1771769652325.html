The biography of INEXHAUSTIBLE begins with a simple, ancient human action: drawing water. The word's deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eus-, "to draw (water), to scoop," the root of the verb that any riverside people would have used for lifting water from a well or stream. From this root Latin formed haurire, "to draw (water), to drink in, to drain," a verb attested from the earliest period of the language and developed in classical literature: Vergil's Aeneid uses it of drawing water and of drinking in the light and air; the image of scooping and draining is alive in the word throughout the classical period. Already in Latin, haurire carried the two poles that define the whole later history: drawing in (drinking, absorbing) and drawing out (emptying, draining). Both senses would matter. From haurire, Latin built the compound exhaurire with the prefix ex-, "out": exhaurire means "to draw out, to empty, to drain dry." Where haurire could be neutral — drawing water for use — exhaurire is the verb of depletion: Livy uses it of emptying the treasury, Cicero of draining resources, and the word comes to mean "to use up entirely," exactly the modern sense of exhaust. The past participle exhaustus, "drained, emptied," then supplied the stem on which English built. The Latin word family thus completed, in miniature, the whole conceptual journey from the well to the empty purse: first you draw the water, then you draw it out, then it is dry. English received the verb "exhaust" in the mid-sixteenth century, around 1540, borrowed from the Latin past participle exhaustus. The earliest English uses are exactly the classical ones: to exhaust is to drain a supply — a treasury, a fund, the resources of a kingdom. In the course of the seventeenth century the word widened and deepened in two directions at once. First, it entered the vocabulary of physical science and engineering: to exhaust a vessel is to pump the air or liquid out of it, and the "air-pump" experiments of the Royal Society in the 1660s, which created vacuums by exhausting the air, made the verb a working term of the new experimental philosophy — Robert Boyle's exhaustions of air established the word in the language of mechanics. Second, it extended to human energies: to exhaust a person is to drain their strength, and the sense "to weary out" appears and becomes standard. The negative adjective appeared in the early seventeenth century, formed transparently in English by prefixing the privative in-, "not," to the positive adjective "exhaustible," which had been coined in the 1600s. Inexhaustible is first attested in the 1610s, and the earliest uses set the two great registers that have governed the word ever since. The practical register applied it to resources and supplies that cannot be drained: an inexhaustible mine, an inexhaustible spring, an inexhaustible fund. The human register applied it to qualities of character and mind: inexhaustible energy, inexhaustible patience, and — crucially for this card — inexhaustible curiosity and wonder. The seventeenth-century divines and moralists were fond of the word precisely because it fused the physical image of the un-drainable spring with the moral praise of the unfailing soul: an inexhaustible sense of wonder is a spring that every new fact feeds rather than drains, and the metaphor has held for four centuries. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries consolidated the word's place in the language of intellectual biography and of natural description. The encyclopedists, the naturalists, and the historians — the very world of Edward Gibbon, whose biography this card's sentence describes — used "inexhaustible" of the scholar's curiosity and the mine's riches with equal ease. In the nineteenth century the word entered the technical vocabulary of economics and engineering, where it still lives: an "exhaustible" resource is one that human use can deplete, an "inexhaustible" resource — solar radiation, tidal energy — is one that cannot be used up, and the distinction is now a working term in the theory of sustainable development. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the word completed its drift toward loose, admiring hyperbole in everyday speech: an "inexhaustible supply" of jokes, "inexhaustible charm," "inexhaustible topics of conversation" — usages where the spring image is rhetorical rather than literal. But even in that loose modern use, the ancient image holds: we still say that a person has inexhaustible energy as we might say their well never runs dry, and the word remains, as it was for the biographer of Gibbon, the natural way to praise a mind whose curiosity was a spring no reading could empty.
