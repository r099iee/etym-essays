Zeus (Ancient Greek: Ζεύς, romanized: Zeús, [zděu̯s] or [zdéu̯s]) is the paramount deity in ancient Greek religion and mythology, revered as the king of the gods, the ruler of Mount Olympus, and the god of the sky, lightning, thunder, law, order, and justice. He is the youngest son of the Titans Cronus and Rhea, and the husband of Hera, though he is famously known for his numerous extramarital affairs and the vast progeny that resulted from them, including many gods, demigods, and heroes such as Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Hermes, Persephone, Dionysus, Heracles, Helen of Troy, and the Muses.

As the supreme ruler, Zeus wields the thunderbolt as his primary weapon and symbol of power, capable of unleashing devastating storms and striking down those who defy his will or violate sacred oaths. His other iconic symbols include the eagle, which often accompanied him or served as his messenger, and the oak tree, particularly the sacred oak of Dodona, where his oldest oracle was located. Zeus's dominion extends over the cosmos, ensuring the balance of power among the gods and maintaining the cosmic order. He is often depicted as a majestic, bearded man, typically holding a scepter and a thunderbolt, embodying strength, authority, and paternal wisdom, though his capricious nature and frequent infidelities also highlight a more complex, often flawed, divine character.

Beyond his role as a sky god, Zeus was also deeply associated with various aspects of human society and morality. He was known as Zeus Xenios, the protector of guests and hosts, upholding the sacred laws of hospitality (xenia). As Zeus Horkios, he was the guardian of oaths, punishing perjurers with severe retribution. His epithet Zeus Eleutherios marked him as the god of freedom, particularly in the context of political liberty and liberation from tyranny. Furthermore, as Zeus Polieus, he was the protector of cities and states, overseeing civic life and justice. These diverse epithets underscore his pervasive influence on Greek religious, social, and political life, making him not just a cosmic force but also a moral arbiter and a foundational figure for societal norms.

In modern usage, the name 'Zeus' or 'Zeus-like' can be used metaphorically to describe a powerful, authoritative, or dominant figure, often one who commands respect or fear, or who has a commanding presence. For instance, a CEO with absolute control over a company might be described as 'Zeus-like' in their authority. The term can also appear in scientific contexts, particularly in astronomy or physics, where projects or phenomena of immense scale or power might be given names referencing the king of the gods. For example, 'Project Zeus' could refer to a large-scale engineering endeavor or a powerful particle accelerator. In popular culture, Zeus remains a prominent archetype, frequently appearing in literature, film, video games, and art as the quintessential powerful patriarch or a symbol of divine might. His stories, particularly his numerous romantic escapades and the resulting divine and heroic lineages, continue to be retold and reinterpreted, reflecting enduring human fascination with power, desire, and the complexities of divine authority.

The worship of Zeus was central to ancient Greek religion, with major sanctuaries at Olympia (site of the Olympic Games, held in his honor) and Dodona. Sacrifices, prayers, and festivals were dedicated to him across the Greek world. His cult was not monolithic but varied regionally, with different epithets and local traditions emphasizing specific aspects of his divine portfolio. Despite the rise of philosophical thought and later, Christianity, the figure of Zeus remained a potent cultural symbol, influencing Western thought and artistic expression for millennia. His mythos provided a framework for understanding the cosmos, human nature, and the often-unpredictable forces that govern both the natural world and human destiny. The intricate web of relationships among the Olympian gods, with Zeus at its apex, served as a rich source of narrative and moral inquiry for ancient Greeks, and continues to do so for modern audiences exploring classical mythology.
