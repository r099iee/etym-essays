- Old English — waste in any direction: mōr means marsh, fen, barren hill, any land the plough cannot take, low or high, wet or dry.
- Middle English — the high waste pulls away: low wet ground becomes marsh and fen, while more climbs uphill and settles on the heathered heights of the north.
- Early modern — law fixes the waste: manorial records treat moors as common grazing and turbary, unenclosed and unencloseable, the parish's rough edge.
- Romantic age — the sublime moves in: poets and novelists from the Brontës to Scott make the moors the landscape of passion, ghosts, and freedom, beautiful because they are merciless.
- Victorian — sport privatises the wild: grouse moors are burned, keepered, and shot over by gentlemen; the waste becomes an asset, managed for birds.
- Today — heritage and hazard at once: national parks, peat-carbon stores, and reservoir catchments, still the place English weather goes to practise, and still, in stories like the hobbits', the near distance where marvels may be glimpsed.
