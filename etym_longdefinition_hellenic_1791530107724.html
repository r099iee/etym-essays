Hellenic is the formal adjective for "Greek", especially in reference to ancient Greece, to the Greek people as a whole, and to the branch of the Indo-European family that includes Greek. It is the word used in the official name of the modern state (the Hellenic Republic), in the classification of the Greek dialects, and in the scholarly and cultural vocabulary of classical reception. It is also the adjective preferred by many Greek institutions abroad, from the Hellenic Parliament to the Hellenic Bank, because it carries the prestige of Hellas rather than the Latin-derived exonym "Greek".

Primary sense: the people and culture. "Hellenic" means "of the Hellenes", the name the Greeks gave themselves. It covers the civilization of the Greek city-states, their language, religion, art, philosophy and literature, and, by extension, the modern Greek nation and its diaspora. In cultural history it often implies the classical ideal: balance, proportion, civic freedom, the polis, the Olympic games, tragedy and philosophy. The phrase "the Hellenic world" can mean the ancient Greek world, the Byzantine Greek world, or the modern Greek-speaking world, depending on context.

Linguistic sense. In comparative philology the Hellenic branch is one of the major branches of Indo-European. It includes:

  • Ancient Greek, attested from Mycenaean Linear B (c. 1450-1200 BCE) through the classical and Hellenistic periods to late antiquity.
  • Modern Greek, the standard national language, descended through Koine and Byzantine Greek.
  • Tsakonian, a highly divergent descendant of Doric still spoken in the Peloponnese.
  • Pontic and Cappadocian Greek, varieties shaped by the Black Sea and Anatolian diasporas.
  • Griko and Calabrian Greek, endangered varieties of southern Italy.

The ancient dialects (Ionic, Attic, Doric, Aeolic, Arcado-Cypriot) and the later Koine are all "Hellenic" in this technical sense. The branch is usually treated as a single daughter of Indo-European, though its internal chronology and its relationship to ancient Macedonian remain debated.

Institutional sense. Since the Greek constitution of 1975 the official name of the state is the Hellenic Republic (Ellinikí Dimokratía). The same adjective appears in the Hellenic Parliament, the Hellenic Navy, the Hellenic Statistical Authority, the Hellenic Open University, the Hellenic Broadcasting Corporation and many banks, foundations and cultural bodies. In these names "Hellenic" is formal, national and slightly archaic in flavour; it signals continuity with ancient Hellas rather than everyday Greekness.

Religious and philosophical sense. Hellenic polytheism (sometimes Hellenismos) is the modern reconstruction or revival of the worship of the Olympian and other Greek gods. Hellenic philosophy covers the schools from the Presocratics to the Neoplatonists. In these uses the word distinguishes pagan or philosophical antiquity from Christian or modern Greek culture.

Critical distinctions. "Hellenic" is not the same as "Hellenistic" or "Hellenism":

  • Hellenic: Greek in the classical, national or linguistic sense.
  • Hellenistic: the period and culture from Alexander the Great to the Roman conquest (roughly 323-31 BCE), characterised by the spread of Greek language and institutions across the Near East.
  • Hellenism: the adoption or study of Greek culture, or, in Jewish history, the Greek cultural movement opposed by the Maccabees.
  • Hellenist: a student of Greek culture, or, in antiquity, a non-Greek speaker of Greek, especially a Greek-speaking Jew.
  • Hellenization: the process of making something Greek.

Modern connotations. In English "Hellenic" is elevated and slightly academic; "Greek" is the everyday word. Saying "Hellenic art" rather than "Greek art" can signal scholarship, nostalgia or a claim about continuity. In Greek nationalist discourse the adjective can carry the weight of the classical inheritance and the Orthodox tradition; abroad it is common in the names of community organisations, festivals and university departments. It rarely appears in casual speech, and when it does it often sounds formal or deliberately grand.

Semantic bridges. The word's unity lies in a single ethnonym: the Hellenes. From a Thessalian tribal name it widened to all Greeks, then to their language family, then to the modern state, then to a cultural ideal. Each extension is a claim about continuity, and the persistent pairing with "Hellenistic" shows how carefully the classical and post-classical phases must be distinguished.
