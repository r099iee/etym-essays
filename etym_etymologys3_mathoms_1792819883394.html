The history of mathoms is really two histories braided together: thirteen centuries of real language change from a reconstructed Indo-European root to an Old English treasure-word, and then a twentieth-century resurrection in which J. R. R. Tolkien, Oxford philologist turned storyteller, lifted the dead word out of Beowulf and set it down in the Shire with a comic wink.

Exchange becomes treasure: the deepest reconstructible ancestor is the widespread PIE root *mey-, meaning to change or exchange, the root behind Latin munus and communis as well as Germanic gift-words. The semantic logic is the gift economy itself: in societies without money, wealth moves by exchange, and the thing exchanged, the present that binds giver and receiver, takes its name from the act of changing hands. Germanic speakers built on this root a noun *maiþmaz, literally something like that which is exchanged, which settled into the settled sense of gift, present, precious thing.

The Germanic gift-hoard: the root is richly attested across the early Germanic languages, which is why etymologists trust it despite its age. Gothic has maiþms for gift or alms in Wulfila's fourth-century Bible; Old Saxon and Old High German show matching forms; Old Norse has meiðmar for precious things. Everywhere the sense is high and serious: treasure, war-gear, rings. A chieftain proves his greatness by giving mathoms, and a hero proves his worth by receiving them.

  • Old English glory (8th–11th c.): māþum rings through Beowulf dozens of times — māþum-sweord for treasure-sword, māþum-gifu for treasure-giving — always gleaming, always grave. The scop never smiles when he says it; mathoms are what kings stake their honour on.
  • Death by conquest (11th–14th c.): after Hastings, French words for precious things — jewel, treasure, present, gift itself reinforced from Norse — push the native word out of daily speech until it survives only in manuscripts, glossaries, and the memory of antiquarians.
  • Philological afterlife (19th–early 20th c.): editors of Beowulf gloss māþum carefully, and students of Tolkien's generation learn it as a set text. The word waits in the dictionary like a sword in a barrow.

Tolkien's resurrection: Tolkien, who as Rawlinson and Bosworth Professor of Anglo-Saxon knew Beowulf line by line, needed a Shire word for the well-known village phenomenon of the present that will not die. Reviving mathom was a characteristically philological joke: take the loftiest word for heaped gold and apply it to duplicate sugar-spoons. The bathos is the point. Hobbits, a peaceable, unheroic people, treat their bric-a-brac with the reverence Beowulf's court gave to swords, and the reader feels both the comedy and the continuity.

  • 1937 — The Hobbit: mathoms enter print as circulating birthday presents of forgotten use, defined in passing with the ease of a word every Hobbit already knows.
  • 1954–55 — The Lord of the Rings: the prologue on the Shire institutionalises them with the Mathom-house at Michel Delving, the Shire's affectionate parody of a museum, where historic mathoms retire from circulation.
  • Sound and spelling: Tolkien normalises the Old English spelling to modern eyes, simplifying māþum to mathom and adding the ordinary English plural -s, so the ancient word sits naturally beside presents and parcels on the page.

After Tolkien: lexicographers of invented languages record mathom as one of the most successful Hobbit-words, and fans carry it back into real life for attic boxes, convention freebies, and role-playing loot. Its career thus mirrors its meaning: a thing of forgotten use, rescued from the manuscript heap because someone could not bear to throw it away, now circulating happily all around the district of English.
