• DEROGATORY: Similar because both mean tending to lower a person's or thing's standing; differs because derogatory focuses on the act of damaging reputation or respect ("derogatory remarks about a colleague") and is the broadest word for reputational harm, while pejorative is narrower and more specifically linguistic — it describes words, senses, and affixes that inherently carry a negative charge, and it is the technical term of lexicography.
  • DISPARAGING: Similar because both mean belittling or lowering in worth; differs because disparaging is used of speech that discredits or seeks to reduce esteem (the tone of the speaker), while pejorative is more often a fixed property of a word or sense itself — a dictionary can label a word pejorative, but "disparaging" describes the act or manner of speaking.
  • BELITTLING: Similar because both involve making something seem small or unimportant; differs because belittling is a verb-like action of minimizing a person or achievement, while pejorative is a classifying term for words or remarks whose function is disparagement — the critic's remarks in the card's sentence are pejorative precisely because they belittle the novel's themes.
  • DEMEANING: Similar because both lower a person's dignity or worth; differs because demeaning stresses the effect on the object's dignity or status (humiliating, degrading to the person), while pejorative describes the quality of the language itself and can apply to words about objects and concepts, not only persons.
  • DEPRECATORY: Similar because both express disapproval; differs because deprecatory has two senses — pleading or apologetic (as in "self-deprecating") and, in older and rarer use, "expressing disapproval" — while pejorative always carries the full disparaging charge and is the standard term in linguistics; the two overlap only in the disapproval sense.
  • CONTEMPTUOUS: Similar because both can express scorn and low estimation; differs because contemptuous describes an attitude of deep disdain held by a person toward its object, while pejorative describes language whose function is to disparage — one can feel contempt without using pejorative words, and a pejorative term can work without the speaker feeling strong contempt.
  • INSULTING: Similar because both give offense; differs because insulting is about causing personal hurt or offense through remarks, while pejorative is about the evaluative downgrade encoded in the word — a dictionary entry labelled "pejorative" is not necessarily being hurled as an insult at anyone.
  • ABUSIVE: Similar because both are harsh and hostile; differs because abusive implies sustained, violent, or injurious mistreatment (abusive language, an abusive critic), while pejorative is a cooler, precise label for the negative evaluative charge of a term — more taxonomic than emotional.
  • SCORNFUL / DISDAINFUL: Similar because both convey contempt; differs because scornful and disdainful describe the proud, haughty attitude of a person expressing contempt, while pejorative describes the property of the word or remark itself — a pejorative term is the instrument a scornful person might use.
  • UNCOMPLIMENTARY: Similar because both mean not praising; differs because uncomplimentary is a weak, everyday word meaning simply "not flattering," with no implication of active disparagement, while pejorative implies the active lowering of status that the card's sentence describes.
  • NEGATIVE: Similar because both can describe unfavorable language; differs because negative is a broad, neutral adjective covering any unfavorable evaluation, while pejorative is the precise term for language whose function is to depreciate — all pejorative remarks are negative, but not all negative remarks are pejorative.
  • OPPROBRIOUS: Similar because both mean expressing strong censure or disgrace; differs because opprobrious is rare, heavy, and archaic-sounding, expressing reproach mixed with disgrace (opprobrious epithets), while pejorative is the standard modern technical term for disparaging language, lighter in register and routine in dictionaries.
