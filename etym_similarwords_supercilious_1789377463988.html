• Haughty: Similar because both describe arrogant pride and a sense of superiority over others; differs because haughty (derived from Old French *haut*, 'high') emphasizes overt, flaunted pride in one's birth, wealth, or station, whereas supercilious specifically highlights the chilly, eyebrow-raising, patronizing facial disdain and intellectual aloofness.
  • Disdainful: Similar because both involve treating others as unworthy of notice or respect; differs because disdainful focuses primarily on the intense feeling of scorn and contempt toward something deemed beneath one's dignity, whereas supercilious describes the polished, patronizing manner and outward demeanor.
  • Imperious: Similar because both convey an overbearing sense of superiority; differs because imperious describes a commanding, tyrannical, and authoritarian manner that demands absolute obedience (like an emperor), whereas supercilious is aloof, indifferent, and disdainfully dismissive rather than actively demanding.
  • Arrogant: Similar because both involve an inflated estimation of one's own importance; differs because arrogant is a broad, universal term denoting aggressive, boastful, and presumptuous overconfidence, whereas supercilious is refined, subtle, cold, and expressed through polished condescension.
  • Cavalier: Similar because both treat others or serious matters with an offhand lack of respect; differs because cavalier suggests a blithe, careless, and nonchalant disregard for rules or feelings, whereas supercilious is actively judgmental, icy, and deliberate in asserting hierarchy.
  • Patronizing: Similar because both involve treating another person as an inferior; differs because patronizing (or condescending) often adopts a false veneer of kindness, gentleness, or helpfulness (talking down to someone as if they were a child), whereas supercilious is openly cold, detached, and aloof.
  • Contemptuous: Similar because both view another as utterly beneath respect; differs because contemptuous expresses a profound, bitter, and often passionate disgust toward vile or worthless behavior, whereas supercilious is an airy, aristocratic manner of looking down one's nose.
