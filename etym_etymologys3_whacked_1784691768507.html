Whacked is the past form of whack, a word of the purest echoic pedigree: it was made, not inherited. There is no Proto-Indo-European root behind it, and no serious dictionary pretends otherwise; the honest history of the word is the history of a sound turned into a vocabulary. 1. The echoic family and the problem of the deep root The first member of the family to appear in English was thwack, recorded from the early sixteenth century in the sense "to strike smartly with a stick or flat object." Whack itself is first recorded about 1712, and scholars have plausibly derived it from thwack by the loss of the initial consonant cluster, so that a word which once began with the four consonants thw- shrank to a brisk w-. Alternatively it may have been coined independently on the same sound pattern, for English has long delighted in a whole family of such verbs — whap, whop, whack, thwack — in which a w or thw plus a short vowel and a hard stop imitates the sharp, ringing crack of a blow. The sound is the meaning: the crisp onset and the abrupt final consonant reproduce exactly the percussive snap of a branch striking a surface. Because the word is expressive vocabulary, no deeper reconstruction is possible; this is a coinage, not an inheritance, and the honest account says so. 2. From a single blow to a small system of senses From this simple acoustic nucleus the word flowered into a remarkable little system of senses, each with a traceable semantic bridge. The noun whack ("a blow") gave rise in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to the sense "a share": a whack of the loot, a fair whack — the image being of a portion cut off by a decisive blow, each person's allotted lump. From a different direction came the American idiom out of whack, "out of order," first recorded in the late nineteenth century of machinery that had been knocked out of line by a blow: a machine literally whacked out of alignment. In the twentieth century the past participle went its own way. British, Australian, and New Zealand speakers made whacked mean "exhausted, done in," the image being of a blow that has knocked the strength out of a person; American slang produced whacked out, "crazy or drugged," and the murder sense — to whack someone is to knock them off, out of the picture — and whacker, in British and Antipodean colloquial use, came to mean something remarkably large. 3. The senses in summary
  • Physical core (c. 1712–present): a sharp, resounding blow; to strike such a blow.
  • Share, portion (18th–19th centuries–present): a fair whack, top whack.
  • Attempt (19th century–present): have a whack at it.
  • Out of whack (late 19th century, American): out of order, misaligned.
  • Whacked = exhausted (20th century, British, Australian, New Zealand).
  • Whacked out = crazy or drugged; to whack = to kill (20th century, American slang).
  • Whacker = something remarkably large (20th century, British and Antipodean).
4. Socio-cultural and literary life The word has always been vernacular, and its modern senses are a miniature history of colloquial English: the share-and-booty idiom of the eighteenth-century underground, the machine-age American idiom of things knocked out of line, the working man's exhaustion of the twentieth-century Commonwealth, and the violent slang of American crime fiction. In The Hobbit, Chapter 2, Tolkien uses it in its oldest, most physical sense: "Tom whacked at them both with a branch." The choice is acoustic and comic at once. Against the heavy, dull "thumping" of the wrestling trolls, the bright, ringing "whacked" of the rescuing troll's stick strikes a note of crisp futility — a sharp little sound in a scene of gross, thudding violence, and its briskness makes the failure of the rescue ("and that of course only made them madder than ever") all the funnier. A word coined to imitate the crack of a stick thus ends its career carrying tiredness, madness, murder and comedy in one brisk syllable.
