Dais is a biography of the 'table' that became a throne-platform. The story opens in the vast Indo-European family of *deyḱ- 'to point out, show,' one of the most productive roots in the whole language group. In Latin it became dīcō 'to say' — for to speak is to point out in words — producing dictate, diction, verdict, and judge (Latin iūdex, literally 'law-speaker'). In Germanic it produced the 'sign' words, *taiknam, whence English teach (Old English tǣcan, originally 'to show') and token, and German Zeichen. In Indic it gave Sanskrit diś- 'to point,' with diśā 'direction' and deśá 'region' — the ultimate source of Hindi deś 'country' and the name Bangladesh. And in Greek the root took a different, physical turn: the zero-grade *dik- produced the verb dikeîn 'to throw,' the idea being that to throw is to aim at a point you have shown. From 'thrown thing' to 'dais'. The chain that leads to English is a long but legible series of metonymies. Greek δίσκος, diskos, was the quoit, the disc that is hurled — 'that which is thrown.' The Romans borrowed it as discus, and the borrowed word quietly expanded its range: in a Roman household the discus was the round plate or platter, and by the Latin of the Middle Ages it had come to mean the table itself, the wooden board laid on trestles for the meal. Old French took this as deis 'high table,' and in the architecture of the feudal hall the deis acquired a fixed home: a raised platform at the upper end of the room where the lord and his household sat at the high table, elevated above the hall's common diners.
  • The transfer 'thrown disc → plate' is metonymy by shape and material: a round plate is the descendant of a round discus.
  • The transfer 'plate → table' is metonymy by containment: the board on which plates stand took the plate's name.
  • The transfer 'high table → platform' is metonymy by contiguity: the block that carried the table took the table's name, so that 'dais' now names the whole raised structure, not the furniture on it.
By a second, equally natural metonymy, the name passed from the table to the platform that carried it — and when the word crossed the Channel in the Anglo-Norman form deis, English adopted the platform sense wholesale. Middle English deis became modern dais, with the -ai- spelling reflecting the French digraph and no trace of the original 'thrown disc' remaining in sight. Sibling words. English is remarkable for keeping three modern words from the same Latin discus: dish (from the early Old English loan disc, 'plate'), desk (from Medieval Latin desca, a writing-table, via Italian desco), and dais (via French). The same Latin word also gave disc/disk (the modern loan), discus (the sport), diskette, and the family of 'disc-shaped' technology words. The Romance languages kept the plain forms: French disque, Italian disco, Spanish disco — and modern 'disco' music is, etymologically, music for dancing to discs (records). The French drift. Meanwhile French developed the word independently: modern French 'dais' has shifted from the platform to the canopy that covers a throne or altar, so the English and French words, once siblings, now point at different objects — English keeps the block, French the covering. In English the dais retains its medieval gravity: the raised block at the head of the hall, the vertical lift that made rank a matter of height. Its literary career runs from the medieval chronicles of feasts and councils through the stately set-pieces of Victorian historical fiction to the Narnian scene where a child suddenly recognises the geography of his own lost coronation.
