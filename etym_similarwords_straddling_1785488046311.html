• Bridging: Similar because both describe connecting or spanning two sides; differs because bridging builds a link between two fixed, separate banks while keeping them distinct, with no sense of a body bestriding the gap.
  • Spanning: Similar because both mean extending across something; differs because spanning stresses width and extent, as of an arch over a river, rather than a person keeping a foothold in both domains.
  • Bestriding: Similar because both mean standing astride something; differs because bestriding is grander and more conquering (Caesar “bestrides the narrow world”), implying domination from above rather than equal partnership.
  • Balancing: Similar because both hold two things in equilibrium; differs because balancing stresses counterweight and the constant danger of tipping over — wobblier and less confident than effortless straddling.
  • Combining: Similar because both join two things; differs because combining merges the two into one, while straddling keeps both sides distinct and in place.
  • Sitting on the fence: Similar because both mean occupying both sides of a division; differs because sitting on the fence is idiomatic and evasive, implying refusal to commit, while straddling can be admiring and athletic.
  • Hedging: Similar because both can mean avoiding commitment; differs because hedging is strategic caution and qualification, while straddling is the posture of standing in both camps at once.
  • Having a foot in both camps: Similar because both describe belonging to two domains at once; differs because the idiom stresses membership in two groups, while straddling stresses the balancing posture itself.
