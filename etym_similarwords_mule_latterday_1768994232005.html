• horse: Similar in being an equine quadruped ridden or worked; differs because the horse is a purebred, fertile species conferring prestige, speed, and equestrian dignity, while the mule is a sterile cross bred for cheap, patient labor.
  • donkey / ass: Similar in being the mule's parent species and fellow beast of burden; differs because the ass names the animal itself and leans toward stupidity and stubbornness ("asinine"), while mule stresses obstinacy fused with laboring power.
  • hinny: Similar in being a horse-family cross; differs because the hinny is the smaller, slighter reverse cross of stallion × jenny, while mule names the substantial jack × mare cross on which freight and agriculture relied.
  • jackass: Similar in naming a donkey used in breeding; differs because the jackass is the male donkey itself and doubles as a coarse insult, an energy entirely absent from the pragmatic, occupational mule.
  • burro: Similar in being a humble work-ass of the American Southwest; differs because the burro is the small Spanish-derived donkey, rustic and diminutive, while the mule is the larger, deliberately bred draft machinery of frontier transport.
  • jenny: Similar in naming a female donkey in the mule-breeding cross; differs because the jenny is the mother of the mule (or, with a stallion, the mother of a hinny) — a parent species, not the hybrid itself.
  • beast of burden / pack animal: Similar in naming animals used to carry loads; differs because these are generic categories covering oxen, camels, llamas, and asses, while mule names a specific hybrid species with its own proverbial character.
  • smuggler / courier: Similar in the slang sense of "mule" as a drug carrier; differs because a courier is a legitimate professional and a smuggler is defined by illegality, while the slang mule is a hybrid metaphor — a person used as a living cargo vehicle, inert and disposable.
  • latter: Similar in naming the second of two or the later member of a pair; differs because latter is strictly comparative and pronoun-like, while latterday is an attributive adjective binding recency to an ending.
  • late: Similar to latter in meaning near the end or recently occurring; differs because late also carries the funereal sense of "the late Mr." (deceased) and can mean merely "recently," which latter escapes.
  • recent: Similar to latterday in pointing to the near past; differs because recent is a neutral report of time, while latterday binds recency to an ending — new and already exhausted, an ironic reincarnation at the close of a longer story.
  • modern: Similar to latterday in implying currency; differs because modern often carries approval and describes contemporary standards, while latterday is ironic and terminal.
  • contemporary: Similar in denoting shared time; differs because contemporary implies simultaneity without any historical endpoint, while latterday implies a concluding phase.
  • up-to-date: Similar to latterday in signaling the present age; differs because up-to-date implies fashion, technology, and approval, while latterday implies exhausted recency.
