Pursuance means "the carrying out or execution of a plan, principle, or course of action; the act of following something through to its proper end." In the passage from which this card is drawn, the word appears in its fixed legal-and-moral formula, "in pursuance of": Dai-yu's parents "had brought their daughter up to believe that good health was founded on careful habits, and in pursuance of this principle, had always insisted that after a meal one should allow a certain interval to elapse before taking tea." The word captures a life governed by method. It is not mere obedience and not mere habit - it is the deliberate, continuing execution of a rule one has adopted as one's own. When Dai-yu, newly orphaned and far from home, waits before taking tea in the unfamiliar household of the Jia mansion, she is carrying her parents' teaching forward in time; the word suggests that she is not being slow or timid but is acting in pursuance of a principle, keeping faith with her dead parents through a small daily discipline. The nuance is one of integrity and continuity: a life "in pursuance of" a principle is a life that follows it like a path, step by step, even when the one who laid the path is gone. Core senses.
  • In pursuance of (a plan, principle, request, policy, duty). The overwhelmingly dominant modern use: the fixed prepositional formula meaning "in the carrying out of, in accordance with, in the course of executing." "In pursuance of the new policy, all employees must attend the training."
  • The act of pursuing (obsolete as a bare noun). Historically pursuance meant simply "the act of pursuing or following up," whether a legal suit, a claim, or a course of action. This bare sense has been almost entirely absorbed by the fixed phrase; used alone today the word feels archaic and legalistic.
  • The following through of a course already adopted. As a process noun, pursuance names the continuation of something begun - the step-by-step execution that is the point of the word's root, "to follow."
The word's shape and its family. Pursuance is built on the verb to pursue with the noun suffix -ance, and both descend from Latin prosequi, "to follow forward." The family is enormous and reveals the root at work everywhere: pursue "to follow with intent; to carry on," pursuit "the act of chasing a goal or quarry," pursuant to "in accordance with" (the modern administrative formula), prosecute "to follow out a charge in court" (a learned doublet of the same root, from the Latin participle prosecūtus), and beyond them sequence, consequence, society, social, execute, and acolyte - all following from the same Indo-European verb "to follow." The -ance suffix (Latin -antia, via French) forms nouns of action, so pursuance is literally "the action of following." Register and the historical bridges.
  • The legal-administrative formula. From the late sixteenth century, and especially from the seventeenth, "in pursuance of" crystallized as the standing phrase of legal deeds, wills, statutes, and official correspondence - the formula by which an action is justified as the carrying out of an earlier instruction. It survives today in contracts and formal documents.
  • From "following" to "carrying out": the semantic bridge. The core image is Latin sequi, "to follow." To pursue is to follow forward; to carry something out in pursuance of a rule is to follow that rule, step by step, to its end. The moral warmth of the word - integrity, fidelity, continuity - is precisely the loyalty of the follower, the person who keeps walking the path laid down by another.
  • Pursuance vs pursuit: the split. The two nouns, born of the same verb, have diverged. Pursuit is active, energetic, forward and often competitive - the pursuit of happiness, the pursuit of a quarry. Pursuance is procedural and calm - one is in pursuance of a rule, a request, a policy, not "pursuing" it. The phrase has hardened into a formula, and the formula has kept the word alive long after its bare senses faded.
  • Modern survival. Ordinary speech prefers in accordance with and pursuant to; pursuance survives in legal and official prose, felt as stiff and archaic but prized precisely for its gravity and its sense of faithful execution.
Why the word fits the passage. The word's whole meaning is a chain of fidelity: follow, carry out, keep faith. Dai-yu's parents brought her up on a principle; in pursuance of that principle she continues to obey its small rule, even in a strange household, even after their death. The formula says that her waiting before tea is not timidity or oddity but obedience carried forward - a daughter still following her parents' teaching, step by step, like a path. That is what pursuance contributes to the sentence that the blunter words execution, performance, or implementation would lose: the quiet, continuing fidelity of the follower.
