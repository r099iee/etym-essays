RAGGED — an essay. Ragged is a word of the Viking Age that came to English wearing animal hair and ended up describing the shirts of the poor. Its history begins in Old Norse rǫgg — "shaggy tuft, coarse hair, tuft of wool" — the word a Norse speaker used for the rough, matted, tufted coat of a beast, the coarse hair of a dog or sheep, the shaggy surface of fur. The base is Germanic (a Proto-Germanic *ragw- "tuft, shagginess"), but beyond that the record is silent: no secure Indo-European root has been found for it, and the proposed link to the family of English "rough" (Proto-Germanic *rūhaz, from PIE *h₁rewk- "to dig up, tear out") remains speculative. Honesty requires the chain to begin, not at a reconstructed root, but at the attested Norse word: this is a word whose pre-history is genuinely lost, and the correction protocol's rule against inventing missing links applies here with full force.

The transfer from hair to cloth happens in Middle English. Around the late thirteenth century English borrowed the Norse word as ragge, meaning "a torn scrap or shred of cloth" — the shift is intelligible the moment you picture it: a frayed, tattered garment, its edge worn into loose threads and points, looks at a glance exactly like a shaggy, tufted hide, the same broken, rough outline. The noun rag (a torn piece of cloth) is the direct heir of this borrowing, and on it English built the adjective ragged(e) in the fourteenth century with its ordinary participial suffix: raggede, literally "furnished with rags," meaning "rough, shaggy, tufted; worn into tatters, torn and uneven." For the first centuries of its life the adjective still pointed both ways — at shaggy, rough surfaces of all kinds and at torn cloth — and the double meaning explains the word's later versatility. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries ragged settled firmly onto fabric and dress: the ragged coat, the ragged sleeve, the garment whose edges have worn into jagged points, holes, and loose threads, the unmended shirt of the poor.

From the eighteenth century ragged became a social diagnosis. The ragged poor — ragged children, ragged beggars, the ragged regiment — were the visible bottom of the social order, and the adjective hardened into the badge of destitution, the unmended garment worn by the body that has failed. Novelists of the nineteenth century exploited the word's flatness: to report that a child wears "a thin and ragged linen shirt" is to record poverty as one records the weather, and the absence of pity in the grammar is exactly what makes the detail unbearable. In the same centuries the word extended its broken-edge image far beyond cloth — ragged rocks and ragged coastlines, ragged breath and ragged coughs, a ragged line of battle, a ragged crowd, ragged timing, ragged edges of a plan — because the core image of the word is not "cloth" but "a broken, uneven, irregular edge," and any surface, sound, or process that loses its clean outline is ragged. The old Norse shagginess survives in all of it: the tufted, uneven surface of the beast's coat is the same raggedness that marks the torn sleeve, the broken rock, and the gasping breath.

  • Old Norse rǫgg "shaggy tuft, coarse hair" (Viking Age, 10th–13th c.); no secure PIE root — the pre-history is lost and stated as such.
  • Middle English ragge "scrap of torn cloth" (late 13th c.), borrowed from Norse with the shift from "tuft of hair" to "scrap of cloth" — the same rough outline unites them.
  • Middle English ragged(e) "shaggy; torn into tatters" (14th c.), formed from ragge with the participial suffix -ed.
  • 15th–16th c.: the sense narrows onto fabric and dress — the ragged garment; modern spelling ragged.
  • 18th–19th c.: a social diagnosis — the ragged poor, the unmended shirt of the destitute; the flat, pityless report of the ragged linen shirt.
  • Modern: the broken-edge image spreads — ragged rocks, ragged breath, ragged lines, ragged timing, ragged edges — while the literal torn garment remains the root sense.
