LINEAR CHAIN FOR "lumbered" (verb, past tense of "to lumber", "to move heavily and clumsily")

NOTE ON ORIGIN: Unlike most English verbs, "to lumber" has NO securely attested PIE ancestor. The verb is recorded only from the Middle English period, and its deeper origin is debated (see the note at the end of this chain). What follows is the honest attested record.

Stage 1 — Middle English: lomeren (c. 14th century) — "to move heavily, clumsily, or with difficulty". First attested in Middle English usage; origin uncertain. Some scholars suggest an echoic or sound-symbolic formation (the heavy thudding rhythm of the walk itself), others a Scandinavian connection (compare dialectal Swedish lomra "to move heavily"), but neither is proven. It is NOT a reflex of any reconstructed PIE root.

Stage 2 — English: lumber (verb, 16th century onward) — "to move in a slow, heavy, awkward, noisy way". This modern verb is the direct continuation of Middle English lomeren, reinforced by semantic pressure from the noun lumber ("disused bulky household goods", 16th century), which itself is often traced to Lombard (the Italian bankers whose London pawnshops stored pawned household goods; see debate note).

Stage 3 — English: lumbered (past tense / past participle) — the regular weak-verb past form of lumber, in continuous use since the 16th century. LUMBERED ◄ TARGET

NOTE ON THE DEBATE / NUANCE: The ultimate origin of this word family is genuinely uncertain and is openly debated in the dictionaries. (1) The verb lomeren has no known Indo-European pedigree; OED marks the origin as uncertain, proposing possible echoic formation or an unverified Scandinavian loan. (2) The noun lumber ("clutter, pawned goods") is traditionally derived from Lombard via the pawnbroking quarter of London (Lombard Street), with the sense "goods stored in a pawnbroker's (Lombard's) warehouse"; this is the OED's preferred but not certain connection. (3) The North American sense of "lumber" = "felled timber, sawn wood" (17th century) is a separate development, widely believed to have arisen from the idea of bulky goods being stored/piled — possibly influenced by the verb sense or by a Canadian French source — and is itself debated. Because no PIE stage exists, this chain deliberately begins at the Middle English record rather than inventing a false root.
