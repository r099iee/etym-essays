walleyed

1. Primary sense (of the eye). Walleyed describes an eye whose iris and pupil turn visibly outward, away from the nose, so that the two eyes do not look in the same direction; the clinical term is exotropia, and walleyed is its blunt, centuries-old everyday name. In older and still-current usage the word also describes a pale, whitish, or bluish-opaque iris — the "wall-eye" of a horse or a dog — an eye that looks glassy, clouded, or blank because of a light or discoloured lens. The two ideas, the outward-turned and the whitish-opaque, are historically one: an eye that is pale and white reads as strangely staring and sideways-glancing, so that "wall-eyed" came to mean "looking askance," "diverging," and finally "turned outward."

2. The historical sense: a "beam" in the eye. The first element of the word is not "wall" in the sense of a building's wall, though folk-understanding often hears it so. English took the term from Old Norse, where vagl meant a roost or a pole, and by extension any projecting beam — and then, in the compound vagl-eygr, "having a beam in the eye," the small pale speck, mote, or cloud in the iris that looked like a little beam or flaw lodged in the gaze. The image is ancient: in the Bible's language of the mote and the beam (Matthew 7:3–5), a tiny speck and a great timber are the two measures of another's fault as against one's own, and walleyed preserves that old metaphor in miniature, a whitish fleck read as a "beam" in the eye. The veterinary "wall-eye" is thus not a white wall but a pale "beam-eye," an eye that contains a little wooden flaw.

3. Applied to animals and the fish. Through the medieval and early-modern periods the word was chiefly a veterinary one: a "wall-eyed" horse had a pale, bluish or whitish iris and often a wandering, sidelong glance, and the same term was used of dogs and other beasts. That same luminous, sideways-glancing eye gave the great freshwater game fish its name: the walleye (Sander vitreus, still sometimes called the walleyed pike) has large, milky, light-catching eyes that look out from the sides, and the fish is prized in North American angling — a modern technical name that keeps the old adjective alive as a noun.

4. Extended and metaphoric uses. From the physical eye the word stretches to the whole stare: a walleyed look can be a glassy, startled, or dazed stare, the look of someone whose gaze does not quite meet yours. In this literary context — "He was slightly walleyed and he was watching the kid earnestly" — the word is a precise, almost clinical piece of portraiture: the man's eyes are misaligned, turned outward so that his gaze never quite settles on its object, and the small asymmetry shades his whole character as slightly "off," hard to read. Yet the choice is careful — "slightly walleyed," not "wild-eyed" or "bug-eyed" — so the defect reads as a small human imperfection, a quirk, rather than a mark of menace or madness. We are invited to distrust the eye but believe the intention.

  • Exotropia: the modern clinical name for an outward-turned eye; walleyed is the lay synonym.
  • Wall-eye / walleye: a pale or opaque iris; also the fish Sander vitreus named for such eyes.
  • Glass eye / blue eye: equine and veterinary names for the pale iris of a wall-eyed animal.

5. The second word: earnest / earnestly. Earnestly is the adverb of earnest, "serious, sincere, zealous, given to genuine intention rather than show." To do something earnestly is to do it with the whole self — gravely, from conviction, without irony, lightness, or calculation. The word carries a strong moral temperature: earnestness in the Victorian period became a cultural ideal, the quality of taking duty, faith, work, and friendship with complete seriousness, famously satirised in Oscar Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest (1895), where the very title puns on the moral virtue and on the hero's assumed name "Ernest."

6. The noun and its phrases. Earnest also survives as a noun. "In earnest" means "in reality, seriously, not in jest"; to be "in earnest" is to mean what one says and to be ready to act on it. A quite separate and homonymous word yields "earnest money" — a token payment given as a pledge or deposit to make a bargain real and binding — which comes not from the Germanic root but from Latin arrha "pledge, deposit" (ultimately of Semitic origin); the two English words are homophones with different histories, a classic lexicographer's trap. Both senses, however, share the core idea of something real, weighty, and meant, which is what makes the pair "slightly walleyed" and "earnestly" so effective: the crooked eye and the straight intention are set against each other, so that walleyed becomes a note of strange physicality and earnestly the sign of the man's true, unwavering purpose.

  • seriously: the everyday synonym of earnestly; earnestly adds moral weight and heartfelt urgency.
  • sincerely: stresses truthfulness of intent; earnestly stresses the intensity and gravity with which the intent is held.
  • wholeheartedly: earnestness with warm, unreserved emotional investment; earnestly may be calm and controlled where wholeheartedly implies abandon.
