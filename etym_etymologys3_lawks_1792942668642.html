The smallest word in this batch carries the longest social history, because Lawks is not really one word at all but a whole Reformation settlement in a syllable. Its story starts with bread. Proto-Germanic *hlaibaz, loaf or bread — whose deeper Indo-European connections remain debated, with Greek klibanus, oven, sometimes proposed and often doubted — joined *wardaz, guardian or keeper (from PIE *wer-, to watch or guard, the root behind warden, guard, and wary), to make *hlaib-wardaz, the bread-keeper: the head of the household in whose hands the loaf was broken and bestowed. Old English wears the compound openly as hlāfweard, still two ideas — loaf plus warden — before centuries of kitchen-and-hall wear rub it down: hlāfweard to hlāford by the eleventh century, then laverd, loverd, and at last lord, the master of men, of manors, and, capitalised, of heaven. That final sacred promotion is what makes the oath possible and dangerous at once.

From bread-keeper to startled gasp:

  • Old and Middle English — the loaf becomes the master: hlāfweard sheds syllables as unstressed vowels weaken and the f voices and vanishes; by Chaucer lord commands manors and prayers alike. Swearing by the Lord is frequent, weighty, and — after the Third Commandment preaching of the Reformers — increasingly actionable as profanity.
  • 17th c. — the age of minced oaths: Puritan discipline, blasphemy statutes, and plain parlour respectability force sacred names through the mincing-machine: God becomes gog, golly, and gosh; Christ becomes crikey and crumbs; Lord in rapid affectionate speech wears to law, lor, and lud. Court depositions and plays catch all three spellings of the worn-down Lord in witnesses' mouths.
  • 18th c. — the exclamatory -s arrives: clipped law takes the same sibilant tail heard in ye gods and odds bodkins, yielding lawks, while fuller phrases run alongside: lawks-a-day, lawks-a-mussy (Lord have mercy), and lawks-a-mercy-me. Farces, ballads, and trial reports print them as established vulgar interjections, needing no gloss for their audiences.
  • Victorian era — typecasting below stairs: Dickens, Surtees, and a hundred provincial novelists assign lawks to servants, ostlers, housekeepers, and comic rustics — honest tongues whose surprise must read as innocent rather than knowing. Music-hall comedians boom it; elocution satires mock it; children learn from it exactly which speakers are meant to be laughed with rather than at.
  • Sound and spelling: phonetically the path is erosion plus emphasis: the long vowel of lord broadens and shortens in unstressed oath-position to the law- vowel, the final -d drops before the added sibilant, and sentence-initial stress restores the capital in print. Variant spellings — lawk, lawks, lorks, lor — record regional vowels rather than different words, and the capital in Lawks here simply marks an utterance beginning a speech.
  • Tolkien's Shire — the rustic signature: Tolkien, a philologist who collected dialect exclamations the way others collect stamps, gives Lawks! to Merry at a moment of peering discovery because no other syllable says unspoiled country surprise so economically. The hobbits' whole sociolect leans on servant-and-farm diction against the high Elvish and kingly registers elsewhere, and this one gasp places Merry — for a heartbeat — among gardeners and grooms rather than princes.

So Merry's shortest line in the batch is also its most historical: a PIE loaf-word worn through bread-keeper, feudal master, and heaven's Lord down to a single surprised syllable in a hobbit's mouth — astonishment with ten centuries of crust still on it.
