indemnities is the plural noun form of indemnity, denoting payments, compensations, or guarantees made to cover loss, damage, or injury, and in international politics sums exacted by a victor from the vanquished. The singular oscillates between protection against loss and payment for loss; the plural overwhelmingly denotes concrete sums paid or exacted, especially war reparations.

Understanding indemnities requires holding both legal poles together: to indemnify is both to hold harmless before the fact and to make whole after the fact. An indemnity clause protects a contractor; war indemnities compensate, or punish, after conquest.

  • Compensation for loss or damage: The core modern sense. Indemnities to farmers after flood, to workers after accident, to homeowners after fire mean money that restores, however imperfectly, what was lost. Bridge: from Latin indemnitas, security from loss, to the sum that secures it. Often collocates with pay, receive, claim, award indemnities.
  • War reparations and tribute: The sense used in the source sentence. A nation victorious in war enslaves the enemy with indemnities: instead of extermination it imposes debts, crippling budgets, seizing customs, forcing labour to pay. Examples include Napoleonic indemnities, the 5-billion-franc indemnity after 1871, Boxer Protocol indemnities. Bridge: compensation logic perverted into punishment and control; the loser pays for the cost of being defeated.
  • Legal protection and exemption: Though plural favours payments, indemnities can denote guarantees: indemnities granted to officials for acts done in emergency, Acts of Indemnity in British history pardoning those who broke the letter of the law. Bridge: the older sense of indemnity as holding harmless. More common in singular, but plural occurs for multiple guarantees.
  • Insurance and commercial guarantees: In business, indemnity insurance, professional indemnity, indemnity bond promise to cover specified losses. To seek indemnities is to seek such assurances. Bridge: contractual extension of Roman cautio damni infecti, security against threatened damage, into modern risk management.
  • Moral and historical irony: Because indemnities sound just, making victims whole, their use for victors justice carries irony critics exploit: the word masks extortion as justice. Abolition history sharpens this: British and French states paid indemnities to slave-owners, not to the enslaved, compensating property-loss while ignoring human loss. Contemporary debates over reparations consciously echo this reversal.
  • Grammar and usage: Regular count plural in -ies: one indemnity, several indemnities. Pronounced with voiced ending. Typically takes verbs impose, exact, demand, levy, pay, receive. Register is formal, legal, diplomatic, journalistic; everyday speech prefers compensation, damages, payout, cover. Note American legal distinction: indemnity as hold-harmless versus damages as court-awarded compensation, though indemnities in treaties blur this.

In short, indemnities names the price of loss translated into money and law: sometimes restitution, sometimes ransom, always a reminder that modern power prefers debt to destruction because debt enslaves longer.
