• autonomy: Similar because both denote freedom from external domination and the capacity for self-regulation; differs because autonomy (from Greek *autos* [self] + *nomos* [law]) can describe sub-national regions, institutions, or individuals possessing limited self-governance within a larger overarching state, whereas sovereignty denotes absolute, supreme, non-subordinate jurisdictional supremacy.
  • independence: Similar because both signify that a nation or entity is not subject to foreign colonial rule; differs because independence is a negative condition describing the state of not being dependent or ruled by others, whereas sovereignty is a positive legal and constitutional power to exercise ultimate authority and enforce laws internally.
  • supremacy: Similar because both convey the highest degree of power and authority; differs because supremacy emphasizes hierarchical dominance over competitors (as in judicial supremacy or naval supremacy), whereas sovereignty represents an institutionalized, recognized constitutional mandate of complete political self-determination.
  • dominion: Similar because both involve the exercise of lawful governing control over a territory; differs because dominion emphasizes ownership, territorial possession, and subjugation (historically applied to British semi-autonomous dominions), whereas sovereignty is the ultimate, undivided fountain of authority itself.
  • hegemony: Similar because both involve sweeping political power on the world stage; differs because hegemony denotes indirect predominance, geopolitical leadership, or cultural dominance exerted by one powerful state over neighboring states without formal legal annexation, whereas sovereignty is formal, de jure internal and external legal legitimacy.
  • suzerainty: Similar because both describe forms of political overlordship; differs because suzerainty refers to a relationship where a dominant state controls the foreign policy of a tributary vassal state while permitting domestic autonomy, whereas sovereignty excludes any such subordinate vassalage.
  • imperium: Similar because both denote absolute executive and military commanding authority; differs because imperium is rooted specifically in ancient Roman constitutional law denoting the supreme command held by magistrates and emperors, carrying heavily militaristic and imperial overtones.
  • jurisdiction: Similar because both relate to lawful governing power; differs because jurisdiction is the specific geographic or legal scope within which a court or official has the right to pronounce and enforce the law (*juris* + *dictio*), whereas sovereignty is the sovereign origin from which all jurisdiction flows.
  • self-determination: Similar because both emphasize a people's right to govern themselves; differs because self-determination is primarily a moral, political, and human-rights principle whereby a community chooses its political status, whereas sovereignty is the realized institutional reality of that authority.
  • rule: Similar because both involve the exercise of political management over subjects; differs because rule simply describes the active exercise of power or administration, which can be tyrannical, provisional, or delegated, whereas sovereignty is the constitutional legitimacy that underpins lawful authority.
