The biography of belated is the biography of an apology: it begins as slowness, hardens into nightfall, and softens into the sheepish smile on a late birthday card. Its deepest proposed root is PIE *lēd-, to be weary or to linger — the tiredness that makes feet slow — which Germanic shaped into *lataz, slow and sluggish, and Old English inherited as læt. In Anglo-Saxon mouths læt was as much moral as temporal: the læta servant was the negligent one, slow because unwilling, and lateness carried a whiff of sin that twenty centuries have never quite washed away.

Phonetically the word barely moved for a thousand years, which is why its story is semantic rather than sonic. Old English læt became Middle English late by the routine lengthening of vowels in open syllables; the final -e fell silent, leaving the modern spelling with its deceptive -e. The magic lies in the prefix be-, Old English be- or bi-, meaning about, around, or thoroughly — the same intensifier as in beloved (thoroughly loved), besieged (thoroughly besieged), and bewildered (thoroughly in the wild). Be- + late + participial -ed yields literally be-lated: made late, thoroughly delayed, with lateness accomplished upon you. Shakespeare, who loved the prefix's completeness, reached for belated to mean night-caught — travellers whom lateness had fully happened to.

From sluggishness to sheepishness:

  • Old English (9th–11th c.): læt means slow, sluggish, and negligent in charters and homilies; Ælfric scolds læte Christians who drag their feet to duty.
  • Middle English (c. 1200 – 1400): late gains its twin modern senses — deceased (the late king) via lately dead, and after-time (coming late); early bilated forms describe hindered or benighted travellers.
  • Shakespearean (c. 1600): belated is fixed in the benighted sense — travellers overtaken by darkness — in plays and in the era's pastoral verse, where nightfall is always catching somebody.
  • Restoration to Georgian (late 17th – 18th c.): sermon-writers extend it to the soul: belated repentance, belated wisdom, the sinner caught by death as the traveller by night; letter-writers apologise for belated answers.
  • Victorian (19th c.): the commercial greeting trade and cheap postage make belated birthday and belated congratulations standard formulae; lateness becomes an occasion for wit rather than shame.
  • 20th – 21st c.: institutions adopt it for dignified regret — belated pardons for wartime injustices, belated honours for overlooked heroes — while Tolkien's belated cup of tea (1937) shows the word at its most lovable: delay measured in teacups.

Literary witnesses crowd every stage. Shakespeare's belated wanderers haunt the edges of the plays; Milton's Eve in Paradise Lost works under evenings that belate the labourer home; Gray's Elegy tolls its curfew over a world of belated peasants plodding homeward. The King James Bible, preferring tarry and linger, never uses it — which left the field to poets, and poets made it twilight-coloured for two centuries. The modern apology-card sense looks trivial beside Milton, but it preserves the old structure perfectly: a timetable missed, a darkness (however small) endured, and a rueful confession that time beat intention. Belated survived because every human life keeps missing timetables, and because English, alone among the Germanic languages in this formation, coined a single long word that turns being late into a minor art of regret.
