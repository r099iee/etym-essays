battering-rams is a compound of two separate words — battering and ram — whose independent histories reach back through Latin and Old English, and whose pairing in English is a vivid translation of a much older siegecraft vocabulary.

Root 1: batter — beating into submission

The verb batter 'to strike repeatedly, to beat hard' enters Middle English in the 14th century as bateren, from Old French batre 'to beat, strike', with the frequentative suffix -er giving the sense 'to beat repeatedly'. Old French batre descends from Latin battuere 'to beat, strike', first recorded in classical Latin sources of the 1st century BCE, where it is used chiefly of striking and fighting. Roman writers themselves thought it a foreign — probably Celtic or Gaulish — loan, most likely imitative of the sound of blows. The same Latin verb gave French battre and, in English, battle, battery (originally 'the action of battering', later a unit of cannon), and abate (via 'to beat down').

Root 2: ram — the beast that butts

The word ram, the male sheep, is native English: Old English ramm (before the 10th century), from Proto-Germanic *rammaz 'a male sheep'. Its ultimate origin is uncertain — it may be connected to a verb of butting or to a root meaning 'strong' — but there is no doubt that the siege engine is named for the animal: a ram attacks by lowering its head and slamming its skull and horns against an obstacle, which is exactly the motion of the beam. The Greeks already used krios 'ram' for the machine, and the Romans followed with aries, also 'ram'; when the ancient sources described the device as 'a ram', every later European language copied the metaphor. The Middle English term was ram or goderam ('good ram'), and the English verb to ram ('to force into place by striking, to slam') was born directly from the engine's action.

The compound and its sense-history

  • c. 14th century: English writers describe siege engines as rams or as things that batter walls; the two ideas circulate side by side.
  • 16th-17th centuries: the explicit compound battering-ram becomes the standard English name, translating Latin aries and matching the contemporary understanding of the machine as a ramming beam used in battering.
  • 18th-19th centuries: with gunpowder making the engine obsolete in real war, the phrase becomes historical vocabulary — yet remains vivid in historical writing, art, and schoolroom accounts of antiquity.
  • 19th-20th centuries: the figurative sense is born — a 'battering-ram' is anything used to smash down opposition, whether a legal ruling, a propaganda campaign, or a protest march; singular and plural are both common.
  • Today: the phrase lives in historical and metaphorical registers, its concrete military meaning preserved by archaeology, film, and fantasy literature — precisely the register in which Tolkien, describing a blow 'like the crash of battering-rams made of forest oaks and swung by giants', sets his epic simile.

Cultural memory

The battering-ram remains a symbol of irresistible, grinding force; the image of the butting ram-beast fused with the battering beam made it one of the most physically expressive pieces of military vocabulary, and it now survives chiefly where writers want to make a crash feel massive, ancient, and unstoppable.
