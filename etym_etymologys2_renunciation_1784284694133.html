Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable root to RENUNCIATION: 1. Proto-Indo-European *neu- (also *neṷ-/*nū-), "to shout, call, make a sound," a verbal root of vocal utterance (Pokorny's *neu-; Watkins), the standard source of the Latin family of words for announcing. No attested form survives in any single daughter language at this depth; the root is reconstructed from the Latin evidence and a few cognates (e.g. Greek neúō, "to nod, beckon," which complicates the picture — see the note below). 2. Latin nūntius, "messenger, message, announcement" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE), an agent noun built on *neu- with the suffix -tius, "one who conveys a report or tidings." 3. Latin nūntiāre, "to announce, report, give tidings" (1st c. BCE), a denominative verb formed on nūntius. 4. Latin renūntiāre, "to bring back word, report back; to declare the result of an election; to renounce, disclaim, give up" (Cicero and later, 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE), from re-, "back," + nūntiāre — literally "to send word back," hence to make a formal declaration, and by extension to declare a right or claim surrendered. 5. Latin renūntiātiō (renuntiatio), "a report, announcement, declaration; a renunciation" (classical legal and rhetorical usage), an abstract noun in -iō from the past-participial stem renūntiāt-. 6. Old French renonciation (13th c.; also renoncement), a legal-religious noun meaning "renunciation," borrowed from Latin renūntiātiō with the regular French sound changes (Latin -tiō > Old French -cion/-tion). 7. Middle English renunciation, renunciacioun (c. 1380–1400), borrowed from Old French in legal and religious contexts; Chaucer and the Wycliffite tradition use the verb renounce and the noun. 8. Modern English RENUNCIATION (15th c. onward), the standard formal noun, unchanged in spelling and sense since the early modern period. Note on the debated stage: the derivation of Latin nūntius from PIE *neu- "to shout, call" is the standard account (Watkins, Pokorny) but is not universally secure. Because Greek neúō means "to nod, beckon" (with the noun neûma, "a nod"), a rival reconstruction derives nūntius from a PIE *neu- "to nod, make a sign," i.e. the messenger as "one who communicates by sign or nod." Either way the root concerns communication; the later stages — renūntiāre, renūntiātiō, Old French renonciation, English renunciation — are uncontested and traceable in dated texts.
