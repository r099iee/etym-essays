Pulleys is the regular plural of pulley, whose singular carries the whole etymological puzzle. The word appears in Middle English in the early 1300s as puli, pully, pulley for a small wheel with a grooved rim over which a rope passes to lift weights. All dictionaries agree the immediate donor is Old French poulie — pulley, roller, reel — attested in the 13th century for cranes, wells and ships. Beyond French, the trail divides, and both branches must be reported honestly.

Leading theory — Greek pivot: the most widely repeated derivation takes Old French poulie from a Vulgar or Medieval Latin *polea, *poledia, itself from Latin polus — end of an axis, pole — borrowed from Greek polos — pivot, axis, pole of the heavens, turning point. Greek polos is securely from PIE *kwel- to turn, revolve — wheel, pole, collar — which also yields English wheel by a different route. The semantic path pivot → small pivoted wheel → rope-wheel is natural, and Medieval Greek diminutives like *polidion little pivot could have passed into shipboard Latin in Marseille or Naples. The weakness is attestation: *polea is reconstructed, not found in surviving Latin texts, so the chain has a hypothesised link.

  • Alternative — colt / windlass: Old French also had poulain — colt, foal — used for a crane or windlass, perhaps because the beam reared like a colt. Some etymologists derive poulie as a shortened or feminine form of poulain in the sense of lifting device. Phonology is workable but the semantic leap is less direct than pivot → wheel.
  • Alternative — pull: popular folk etymology connects pulley to English pull, since one pulls a pulley rope. Chronology rules this out as origin — French poulie predates the English verb-noun association — though pull undoubtedly reinforced the spelling and pronunciation once borrowed.
  • Middle English establishment (1300s–1500s): dockyards, mines and building sites adopted pulley alongside block, tackle, windlass. Chaucerian and Gowerian texts use pulleys for wells and ships. Plural pulleys is regular with y → ies.
  • Scientific formalisation (1600s–1800s): Galileo, Stevin and Newton classify the pulley among the six simple machines. Fixed pulley, movable pulley, compound pulley, block and tackle enter textbooks. Pronunciation settles to /ˈpʊli/ with plural /ˈpʊliz/.

Cultural career: from well-heads andpiazza to sailing rigging — every Age of Sail ship carried dozens of blocks — to Victorian factories, elevators and window sashes, to modern rock-climbing, theatre fly-systems and gym cable machines, the pulley has stayed technologically current while its name stayed frozen. The source passage rightly places ropes, levers, and pulleys at the dawn of mechanics, before sledge, roller, wheel and cart: the author intuits the simple-machine sequence taught by Archimedes onwards.

Modern extensions keep the wheel-and-rope image: pulley clothesline, pulley window, belt pulley are literal; pulley system of patronage, pulley of influence are metaphorical mechanisms that hoist or drag. Computer and automotive senses — timing-belt pulley, idler pulley — denote toothed wheels without rope, showing the wheel sense outliving the rope sense. That persistence of a 13th-century French pivot-word in 21st-century engine bays is the quiet triumph of pulleys.
