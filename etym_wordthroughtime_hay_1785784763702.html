- Pre-OE / Proto-Germanic *haują — Perceived as "that which is hewn": cut plant mass, not yet a botanical species name but a product of striking down grass.
- Old English hēg / hīeg — Ordinary rural English for dried fodder; wealth-marker on estates; associated with mowing seasons and animal survival through winter.
- Middle English hay / hei — Spelling stabilizes; remains core farm vocabulary after Norman Conquest; hay meadows and tithes embed the word in legal-economic speech.
- Early Modern English hay — Full proverbial life: "make hay while the sun shines" frames the word as metaphor for timely action; haystack becomes image of bulk and concealment.
- 18th–19th c. hay — Pastoral literature and enclosure-era agriculture reinforce hay as emblem of countryside labor; technical husbandry manuals specify curing and stacking.
- Late 19th–early 20th c. hay — American slang "hit the hay"; "hayseed" as rustic epithet; "haywire" shifts from farm material to "out of order"; "hay fever" medicalizes seasonal discomfort linked to mowing time.
- Modern English hay — Standard term for sun-dried forage vs. silage/straw; still idiomatic and nostalgic; industrial baling changes practice but not the core word’s identity.
