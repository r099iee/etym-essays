CONSULS is the plural of CONSUL. In the source passage from Edward Gibbon's The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the word names the two annually elected chief magistrates of the Roman Republic: Rome's principal conquests, Gibbon argues, were achieved "by the policy of the senate, the active emulation of the consuls, and the martial enthusiasm of the people". Here "the consuls" are not generic officials but the specific paired executives of the republican constitution - the apex of the cursus honorum, the ladder of Roman public office - whose short tenure and mutual rivalry (Gibbon's "active emulation") drove campaigns and ambitions. PRIMARY SENSE (the Roman magistracy)
  • The two annually elected chief magistrates: In the Roman Republic the consulship was the highest ordinary office. Two consuls were elected each year to serve jointly for a single term of one year, holding supreme civil and military command (imperium), presiding over the Senate and the assemblies, and wielding the power of veto against each other - a deliberate design to prevent one man from becoming too powerful.
  • Symbol of the Republic's executive power: The consuls gave their names to the year (Roman dating used "in the consulship of X and Y"), and their office symbolised the republican principle that power is shared, annual, and accountable. The dual consulship is the institutional opposite of monarchy and dictatorship.
  • The competition for office ("active emulation"): Because only two men held the office each year and the term was short, ambitious aristocrats competed fiercely for the consulship, and a consul who won a major war could go on to celebrate a triumph. Gibbon's phrase "the active emulation of the consuls" captures this engine of Roman expansion.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • Modern diplomatic sense (the living sense today): In modern international usage a "consul" is an official appointed by a state to represent its commercial interests and protect its citizens in a foreign city. The word is preserved in "consulate" (the consul's office) and "consular" (adjective). The semantic bridge: medieval Italian and Mediterranean trading states reused the ancient Roman name for their chief magistrates and for the officials who governed merchant communities abroad, and from this civic use the modern diplomatic meaning developed.
  • Medieval civic use: In the Middle Ages "consul" (Italian "console", Old French "conseil") named the chief magistrates of the self-governing communes of Italy and southern France - cities like Pisa, Genoa, and Arles were ruled by consuls in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. This usage kept the republican colouring of the ancient word alive.
  • Honorary and historical-technical uses: In scholarship "consul" anchors a technical vocabulary: "consulship", "proconsul" (a former consul governing a province), "consular" (of or relating to a consul), "the consular armies", "consular dating". These are precise historical terms of Roman institutional history.
  • Figurative and generalising use: Occasionally "consul" is used metaphorically for any presiding or governing figure in a deliberative body, and the phrase "consular rank" survives loosely in descriptions of civic dignity; but the word's figurative life is much smaller than that of "triumph" or "Augustus", because it names a specific office rather than a general idea.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The word comes from Latin "consul", derived from the verb "consulere" "to deliberate, consult, take counsel together". The consul was, etymologically, "the one who deliberates together" - the magistrate who consulted the Senate and the people. Two semantic bridges carry the word forward. First, the office itself: because the consulship was the supreme executive, the word became the standard term for the Republic's chief magistracy, and through Roman history writing it entered all the European languages. Second, the medieval bridge to the modern sense: when Italian communes in the eleventh and twelfth centuries revived republican self-government, they called their magistrates "consuls" in conscious imitation of Rome, and when merchants overseas needed resident officials to manage their communities, those officials were also called "consuls" - hence the modern diplomatic term. Both modern uses (historical and diplomatic) descend from the same Latin core meaning of "deliberating and governing together".
