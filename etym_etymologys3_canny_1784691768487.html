Canny is a word of the Indo-European root *ǵneh₃-, 'to know', one of the great roots of the family — a root whose reflexes span nearly the whole Indo-European world. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit jānāti 'he knows', and through the vernaculars the modern Hindi jānnā 'to know', which the note's mnemonic rightly invokes: the Hindi word and the English can really do share the same ancient ancestor. In Greek the same root gives gignōskō 'I know', and from its nominal forms come gnōsis 'knowledge' and the modern English gnostic, agnostic, diagnosis, prognosis, and recognize. In Italic it produced Latin (g)nōscō 'I get to know', whence cognizance, cognition, incognito, and connoisseur. In Balto-Slavic it yielded Lithuanian žinoti and Russian znat'. And in Germanic it produced two distinct descendants: the ordinary verb for 'know', Old English cnāwan, the ancestor of modern know; and a special preterite-present verb *kunnaną, 'to be mentally able, to have learned', whose first- and third-person singular present form was *kann, giving Old English cann/can, modern can, German können, and Gothic kunnan. The preterite-present structure is the key to the whole Germanic story. Such verbs — the same class that produced may, shall, must, and ought — are historically past-tense forms that acquired present meaning; *kunnaną meant literally 'I have come to know', and hence 'I know how, I am able'. Old English inherited this verb as cunnan, 'to know, to know how, to be able', recorded from the eighth century, with the present singular can and the past participle cūþ 'known'. The participle survives in English today in uncouth, which originally meant 'unknown, unfamiliar' before sliding into 'strange, uncouth, rude', and in the reborrowed couth; and the same verb supplied the present participle cunnende, which yielded cunning, 'knowing, skilful', first recorded in the fourteenth century with the respectable sense 'learned, skilful' before it too slid, by the sixteenth century, toward 'crafty, deceitful'. The old 'know' sense of the verb survived longest in Scotland and the north of England, where the descendant verb, Scots ken (from Old Norse kenna, itself from the same Germanic root), kept the meaning 'to know' into the modern period. It was in this northern, Scots-speaking world that the adjective canny was formed — from can, the 'know how' stem, with the common adjectival suffix -y (Old English -ig) — and it is first recorded in the 1630s meaning 'knowing, wise'. From that single root sense the word flowered in two directions. In one direction, knowledge became practical competence: 'careful, skilful, clever' and, by the eighteenth century, 'frugal, thrifty, economical' — the canny housewife and the canny businessman, people who know how to manage. In the other direction, knowledge became caution: by the early nineteenth century, and partly under the influence of Sir Walter Scott, whose novels made the northern word fashionable throughout Britain, canny acquired its most familiar modern sense of 'cautious, wary, shrewd' — the quality of a man who never risks more than he must. But the word's distinctive warmth belongs to its Scots superstitious register, and it is exactly this register that Thorin exploits in The Hobbit. In Scots, canny could mean 'safe', 'lucky', 'of good omen', and 'safe to deal with', a sense that grows directly out of the older belief that a knowing person — one who knows the ways of the world and the local spirits — is a good and fortunate person to have around. Scott's own glossary to The Heart of Mid-Lothian (1818) defines the word with full precision: 'skilful, prudent, lucky; in a superstitious sense, good-conditioned, and safe to deal with; trustworthy; quiet.' When Thorin sends Bilbo toward the mysterious light in the dark with the instruction to discover 'if all is perfectly safe and canny', he is not being redundant: 'safe' covers the physical risk, and 'canny' covers the other, older kind of danger — whether the place bodes ill, whether it is lucky or unlucky, whether it is 'safe to deal with' in the superstitious sense. The pairing is a quiet stroke of characterisation, marking the exiled dwarf as a speaker of the old northern speech and giving the adventure a layer of folk-omens and good-and-bad luck. The negative uncanny completes the picture, and its history is the history of canny in reverse. Recorded from the 1590s with the sense 'mischievous' (in a Scottish text), uncanny meant, as the simple negation of canny, 'not safe to deal with, unreliable, untrustworthy' — the word is literally 'un-canny' = 'not canny'. In the nineteenth century, as the superstitious senses of canny came to the fore in Scots folklore writing, uncanny was drawn into the supernatural vocabulary, meaning 'suggestive of something unnatural, weird, eerie'. The modern English uncanny, famous from Freud's essay Das Unheimliche (1919) and now a standard word for a disturbing, almost supernatural strangeness, thus carries the whole northern history of canny folded into its single negative. Modern distribution and register. Canny remains a living word in two distinct registers. In general English it is a somewhat literary, approving adjective for shrewd, long-sighted prudence, often with a suggestion of northern hard-headedness ('a canny investor', 'canny about money'). In the north of England and Scotland it is still a warm, colloquial word with a wider range than the standard language: in Northumberland and Durham 'canny' can mean pleasant, nice, good, or 'quite considerable' (as in 'a canny bit'), and it is woven into local identity — the affectionate nickname of Newcastle, the 'Canny Toon'. The derived forms cannily ('gently, carefully, shrewdly') and canniness (the noun) are standard English, and uncanny has long since outgrown its dialectal origins to become a word of international currency in psychology and literary criticism. The word is thus a small museum of its own history: a PIE 'know' root, a Germanic 'know how' verb, a northern 'knowing' adjective, a Scottish 'lucky and safe' charm-word, and a modern synonym for both prudence and the eerie — all alive in the same language at once. Milestones in brief: - c. 3000 BCE (reconstructed): PIE *ǵneh₃- 'to know'; reflexes: Sanskrit jānāti, Greek gignōskō, Latin nōscō, Hindi jānnā. - Proto-Germanic *kunnaną 'to know, be able' (preterite-present). - 8th century: Old English cunnan; present can. - 1630s: canny 'knowing, wise' first recorded (Scots). - 18th century: 'careful, skilful, clever, frugal, thrifty'. - Early 19th century: 'cautious, wary, shrewd', popularised by Scott; Scots 'safe, lucky, of good omen' documented in his glossary (1818). - 1590s–19th century: uncanny slides from 'mischievous, not safe to deal with' to 'eerie'. - 1937: Thorin's 'perfectly safe and canny' in The Hobbit.
