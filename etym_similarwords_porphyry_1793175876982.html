• Granite: Similar because both are hard speckled igneous rocks used for monuments; differs because granite is the common grey building stone of kerbs and kitchens, while porphyry is the rare purple imperial stone of emperors.
  • Marble: Similar because both are costly classical ornamental stones, often named together in palace descriptions; differs because marble is a soft metamorphic limestone that carves easily, while porphyry is a flint-hard igneous rock that defies the chisel.
  • Basalt: Similar because both are dark fine-grained igneous rocks of ancient monuments; differs because basalt is black volcanic commonplace (roads, statues), while porphyry is purple, crystalline, and imperial.
  • Alabaster: Similar because both are luxury stones of tombs and temples; differs because alabaster is soft, pale, and translucent, carving like soap, while porphyry is hard, dark, and opaque, yielding only to emery.
  • Jasper: Similar because both are prized opaque coloured stones, red and purple; differs because jasper is a chalcedony quartz of small seals and inlay, while porphyry is a monumental building stone of columns and sarcophagi.
  • Obsidian: Similar because both are igneous rocks treasured since antiquity for beauty and hardness; differs because obsidian is black volcanic glass that flakes to blades, while porphyry is crystalline and massive, built to be built with.
  • Diorite: Similar because both are speckled plutonic rocks, and diorite porphyry is literally a porphyry; differs because diorite names the grey salt-and-pepper composition, while porphyry names the imperial purple stone or the two-crystal texture.
  • Serpentine: Similar because both are coloured ornamental stones of imperial Roman interiors; differs because serpentine is soft green mottled marble-like rock, while porphyry is hard purple-red igneous majesty.
  • Tyrian purple: Similar because both share the same Greek colour-root and imperial symbolism; differs because Tyrian purple is the murex-shell dye of cloaks and robes, while porphyry is the stone that wears the same purple in architecture.
