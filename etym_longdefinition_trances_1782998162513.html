TRANCES is the plural of trance, meaning 'half-conscious states of deep absorption, meditation, or ecstasy', and in this card it opens Book First of William Wordsworth's The Prelude (1805): 'Trances of thought and mountings of the mind / Come fast upon me: it is shaken off.' The capital T, at the start of the verse, gives the word an almost heraldic prominence. A trance here is not a stupefied or drugged daze but a state of profound, involuntary mental absorption — the mind fully turned inward, detached from the immediate sensory world, suspended in deep thought until it seems to pass out of ordinary time. The word is plural and paired: 'trances of thought' recur again and again, and they arrive, like the mountings, 'fast upon' the poet — suddenly, involuntarily, as visitations.
  • Primary sense — altered absorption: a trance is a state in which the mind is deeply absorbed and partially or wholly detached from external stimuli; it can be spontaneous, hypnotic, or mystical, and it carries an undertone of the uncanny or the sacred ('to fall into a trance').
  • Religious / mystical sense: an ecstatic state of communion or revelation — the trances of saints and seers, the prophetic 'visions and trances' of Acts 10:10 and 22:17, where the Greek ékstasis is translated 'trance'.
  • Hypnotic / mesmeric sense: since the nineteenth century, the state artificially induced by a hypnotist ('hypnotic trance', 'mesmeric trance'), the subject responsive to suggestion and anaesthetized to pain.
  • Medical / pathological sense: a stupor, swoon, or suspension of the senses; historically the word could mean a faint, a fit, or the passage of a dying soul, and it retains that old fear in phrases like 'death trance' (apparent death, catalepsy).
  • Musical sense: since the 1990s, 'trance' names an electronic dance-music genre built on hypnotic, repetitive drones and beats, designed to induce a trance-like state in dancers.
  • Figurative sense: any state of rapt, oblivious concentration ('lost in a trance of reading', 'a trance of grief'), the everyday residue of the mystical meaning.
The etymology is surprising and dark. Trance descends from Latin transīre, 'to go across', via Old French transir 'to pass away, die, be chilled' and the Old French noun transe, 'the passage of life into death, the death-agony, a swoon'. A word that began as 'I go across' became, within a few centuries, the name of the moment of dying — and only later softened from dread to rapture. Wordsworth's use, then, sits at the end of a long semantic migration: the ancient fear of the body passing away has become the mind's absorption, the mind passing out of ordinary time. Discriminating the family:
  • Reverie: a pleasant, passive, often idle daydream, milder and entirely secular — one drifts into a reverie, but one falls into or is caught in a trance.
  • Daydream: a conscious, wish-fulfilling fantasy, escapist and self-directed, without the involuntary depth of a trance.
  • Meditation: a deliberate, disciplined practice of focused awareness; a trance is undergone, not willed.
  • Ecstasy: an intense state of overwhelming joy or exaltation; it emphasizes rapture, while a trance emphasizes absorption and detachment from the senses.
  • Swoon: a faint or loss of consciousness — the older, near-synonymous sense of trance, now mostly archaic.
In Wordsworth's lines the trances and mountings together describe the rhythm of Romantic inspiration: the stilling of the senses, then the upward leap of the spirit — the two experiences that allow the burthen of the 'unnatural self' to be shaken off. The tone is meditative and elevated, and the word's long history (swoon, death-passage, rapture) makes the phrase feel ancient even as it describes a private, modern moment.
