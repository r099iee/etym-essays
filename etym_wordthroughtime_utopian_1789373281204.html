- [1510s–1590s] - Coined in Neo-Latin by Sir Thomas More (1516) and subsequently Anglicized as a proper noun and adjective denoting the fictional island commonwealth of Utopia - [connotation: erudite humanist satire, philosophical thought-experiment, earnest model of Christian-civic virtue]
- [1600s–1780s] - Broadens across English political philosophy to describe any speculative commonwealth, ideal constitution, or imaginary republic (such as Francis Bacon's New Atlantis or James Harrington's Oceana) - [connotation: speculative political theory, intellectual curiosity, constructive constitutional modeling]
- [1790s–1880s] - Under the impact of the French Revolution and the rise of early socialist movements (Henri de Saint-Simon, Charles Fourier, Robert Owen), acquires a distinct socioeconomic ideology (Utopian Socialism) - [connotation: radical communal experimentation, benevolent social reorganization, later contrasted sharply by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels with scientific socialism]
- [Late 19th–Early 20th c.] - Undergoes pejorative semantic drift among realpolitik statesmen, classical economists, and pragmatic politicians who use it as an epithet to dismiss social reforms - [connotation: political naivety, dangerous unworldliness, idle pipe-dream, blindness to human fallibility and economic laws]
- [Mid-20th c.–Present] - Solidified as a dual-edged term in political theory, literary criticism, and everyday discourse: celebrated as an indispensable imaginative horizon for human aspiration and ethical critique, while cautionary when rigid utopian blueprints engender dystopian authoritarian coercion - [connotation: noble visionary striving versus totalitarian hubris]
