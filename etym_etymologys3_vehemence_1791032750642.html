Vehemence rides on one of the oldest Indo-European ideas: thinking. PIE *men-, to think, to remember, to await, ramifies into English mind, mental, memory, remind, mantra, mentor and Latin mens, mentis, mind, and meminisse, to remember. Latin prefixed to this mental stem a puzzling particle ve-, found also in vesanus (mad, literally out-of-mind), vegrandis (diminutive), and vehiculum disputes aside. Roman grammarians Aulus Gellius and Festus already argued about it: does vehemens mean possessing much mind (intensive) or carried out of mind (privative)? The dominant ancient reading — a mind rushing out of itself, quod vi mentis feratur — captures the psychology perfectly: passion as intellect swept away by its own force.

Classical vehemens is an adjective for persons, winds, fevers, orations, and desires that rush impetuously. Cicero praises vehement orators and fears vehement emotions; Livy describes vehement charges; Ovid, vehement love. The abstract noun vehementia names the quality: eagerness, violence, force. Phonologically the word is straightforward: whements in early Latin becomes Classical /weheːmens/ with rhotacism in the genitive, spelled vehemens, vehementis. Medieval Latin keeps it as a rhetorical term for forceful style.

  • French mediation: Old French borrowed vehemence (13th century) for legal and theological force, pronounced with loss of /h/ and nasalisation. Anglo-French carried it to England around 1400, first in Wycliffite and Lollard prose for violence of wind and fervour of prayer. Early spellings vehemence, vehemencie show French suffix -ence replacing Latin -entia.
  • Semantic drift: rushing motion → violent force → impetuous feeling → forceful expression. The physical sense (vehement wind) recedes after 1700; the rhetorical-emotional sense dominates. Eighteenth-century elocution manuals list vehemence as a fault when excessive and a virtue when seasonable — the paradox of passion in polite discourse.
  • Socio-cultural context: Reformation polemic thrives on vehemence — Latimer, Knox, and Puritan divines commend holy vehemence against lukewarmness. Restoration and Augustan politeness then stigmatise enthusiasm, so vehemence becomes suspect, needing the qualifier righteous or just. Victorian muscular Christianity and imperial oratory rehabilitate it: Gladstone's vehemence is manly earnestness. Wodehouse, writing after the Great War, gently deflates it: Tuppy Glossop's rising vehemence about newts and shark-flatfish exposes how Edwardian honour language survives as comic bluster.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucerian-era vehemence of wind; Shakespeare's vehement oaths; Milton's vehement zeal; Swift and Pope praised or blamed for vehemence of satire; Austen notes vehemence of attachment; Dickens gives minor officials sudden vehemence; Wodehouse makes rising vehemence a running joke for Tuppy, Bertie, and aunts whose dignity outruns their cause.

Phonetically, modern RP /ˈviː.ə.məns/ shows the Great Vowel Shift raising Latin e to /iː/, reduction of medial vowels to schwa, and anglicised stress on the first syllable, unlike French véhémence /ve.e.mɑ̃s/ with final stress. The spelling h is etymological and silent, a Latin fossil. The persistence of that silent h signals the word's learned register: unlike heat or fire, vehemence announces its classical pedigree, which is why Wodehouse can use it for mock-heroic inflation — a grand Latin noun for a silly argument about fish.
