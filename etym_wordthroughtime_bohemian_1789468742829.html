14th–15th c. - Entered Middle English as an ethnic and geographic demonym denoting a native of Bohemia (*Čechy*), the historical Central European kingdom; associated in Europe with the Hussite religious wars and independent monarchy.
16th–17th c. - Renowned across Europe for world-class craftsmanship in glassmaking, engraving, and crystal cutting (*Bohemian glass/crystal*); French popular misconception mistakenly assumed Romani travelers (Gypsies) originated from Bohemia, leading French *bohémien* to mean 'Romani wanderer'.
1840s–1850s - French novelist Henri Murger published *Scènes de la vie de bohème*, romanticizing the impoverished, nomadic, anti-bourgeois lifestyle of young artists, writers, and students in the Latin Quarter of Paris.
Late 19th c. - English adopted the romanticized French sense (championed by Thackeray and Victorian writers); Bohemianism became an internationally celebrated literary and artistic identity.
20th–21st c. - Retains its geographic and artisanal prestige (Bohemian crystal/glassware) while flourishing globally in fashion (*boho-chic*), interior design, and cultural commentary to denote relaxed, eclectic, nonconformist, and creative lifestyles.
