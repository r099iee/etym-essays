Fleisch (German neuter noun, pronounced /flaɪ̯ʃ/) is the ordinary German word for both flesh and meat. It is a mass noun with no plural in normal usage; the genitive is Fleisches or Fleischs, and the archaic dative Fleische survives in fixed phrases. Its semantic field is wider than that of English flesh, because German uses a single word where English separates the living tissue (flesh) from the edible product (meat). The word is capitalized as a noun, as all German nouns are, and it forms the first element of a very large compound family.

Primary sense: meat as food. In everyday modern German, Fleisch is the default, unmarked term for the muscle tissue of slaughtered animals sold and eaten as food. It is the word on butchers' signs, in recipes, in supermarket labelling and in dietary discussion. The type of meat is normally specified by a modifier:

  • Species and cuts: Rindfleisch (beef), Schweinefleisch (pork), Kalbfleisch (veal), Lammfleisch (lamb), Schaffleisch (mutton), Geflügelfleisch (poultry), Wildfleisch (game), Bärenfleisch (bear meat).
  • Butchery and commerce: Hackfleisch (minced meat), Dörrfleisch (dried meat), Fleischwaren (processed meat products), Fleischtheke (meat counter), Fleischbeschau (meat inspection), Fleischwolf (mincing machine), Fleischskandal (meat scandal).
  • Preparation and cooking: Fleischgericht (meat dish), Fleischsuppe (meat broth), Fleischsalat (meat salad), Sonntagsbraten (Sunday roast). In grilling usage a Fleisch can be a single slab or cut, as opposed to sausages.

Secondary sense: living flesh and the body. Fleisch also denotes the soft living tissue of a human or animal body, and by extension the body as opposed to the mind or spirit. This is the sense inherited from the Germanic core of the word, and it remains strong in religious, literary and medical registers:

  • Theological and incarnational: das Wort ward Fleisch ("the Word became flesh", John 1:14); Fleischwerdung (incarnation); das Fleisch in the Pauline opposition of flesh and spirit.
  • Kinship and shared humanity: Fleisch und Blut ("flesh and blood"); ein Mensch aus Fleisch und Blut ("a human being of flesh and blood"); mein eigenes Fleisch und Blut ("my own flesh and blood").
  • Moral and ascetic: Fleischeslust ("carnal lust"), fleischlich ("carnal, fleshly"), and the proverb der Geist ist willig, aber das Fleisch ist schwach ("the spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak", Matthew 26:41).
  • Medical and pathological: wildes Fleisch ("proud flesh", granulation tissue), Fleischwunde (a wound involving flesh), Zahnfleisch (gums, literally "tooth flesh").

Tertiary and technical senses. The word extends from animal tissue to other soft, edible or structural masses. Fruchtfleisch is the pulp of fruit, the soft part around a stone or pip; the simplex Fleisch can carry the same botanical sense in context. In anatomy and butchery, Muskelfleisch ("muscle meat") distinguishes muscle from fat, bone and connective tissue. In ecology, Fleischfresser ("meat-eater") and fleischfressend ("carnivorous") classify animals by diet. Fleischfaser ("grain of the meat"), Fleischreifung ("meat ageing") and Laborfleisch ("cultured meat") belong to food science and the modern meat industry. The compound Fleischfarbe and the adjective fleischfarben describe a pale pinkish colour, literally "flesh-coloured".

Figurative and idiomatic uses. German idiom exploits the concreteness of flesh in several directions:

  • sich ins eigene Fleisch schneiden — literally "to cut into one's own flesh", to harm oneself by one's own action, roughly "to cut off one's nose to spite one's face".
  • weder Fisch noch Fleisch — "neither fish nor fowl", said of something insipid, half-hearted or impossible to classify.
  • ein Stachel im Fleisch or ein Pfahl im Fleische — "a thorn in the flesh", a persistent source of irritation, ultimately Pauline.
  • in Fleisch und Blut übergehen — "to become second nature", literally to pass into flesh and blood.
  • Sitzfleisch — literally "sitting flesh", the ability to sit still and persevere; vom Fleisch fallen — to lose weight dramatically, literally to fall off the flesh.
  • den Weg allen Fleisches gehen — "to go the way of all flesh", a solemn euphemism for dying.

The semantic bridge. The double sense is not accidental. Proto-West Germanic *flaiski denoted flesh and meat without distinguishing the living tissue from the food. As meat became the prized and commercially central product of animal bodies, the culinary sense rose to dominance in German, while the anatomical sense was retained in religious language, kinship idioms and medicine. English took the opposite route: flesh, the exact cognate of Fleisch, stayed anatomical, while meat (Old English mete, "food") narrowed from "food in general" to "animal flesh". The result is a classic false-friend pattern: German Fleisch is etymologically flesh but is usually translated meat.

Modern and contested usage. In the twenty-first century Fleisch has become a keyword in debates about climate, health and animal welfare. Compounds such as Fleischersatz ("meat substitute"), Fleischsteuer ("meat tax"), Fleischkonsum ("meat consumption"), Fleischindustrie ("meat industry") and Laborfleisch ("cultured meat") carry ethical and environmental charge. The word itself remains neutral in form, but its use now often signals a position in the argument over what should be eaten. In advertising and packaging, Fleisch is legally protected as a designation for animal tissue, which is why plant-based products are labelled with analogues such as Fleischalternative rather than Fleisch.

Register and grammar. Fleisch is standard-register and stylistically unmarked in its food sense. In its bodily sense it can be neutral (medicine), solemn (religion, funerals) or mildly archaic (idiom). The noun is strong and neuter, takes no plural, and is capitalized. Kinds of meat are counted with Fleischsorten ("meat types"), not with a plural of Fleisch. The diminutive is not lexicalized. Regional synonyms for the butcher's trade, Fleischer (north and east) and Metzger or Schlachter (south and west), belong to the same word family and show how central butchery has been to German everyday culture.
