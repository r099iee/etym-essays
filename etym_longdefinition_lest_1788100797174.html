LEST is a subordinating conjunction expressing negative purpose or apprehension: "for fear that", "so that … not", "in order that … not". It introduces a clause describing something unwanted that the main clause seeks to prevent — "He whispered, lest anyone should hear." Because the clause after lest normally takes a subjunctive verb (or should + infinitive in British English), the word instantly signals formal or literary register. It is one of the few surviving English conjunctions that requires a special verbal mood, and that subjunctive requirement is what gives the word its old-fashioned gravity.

  • Negative purpose (the primary function). "Take an umbrella lest it rain" means: take an umbrella so that it does not rain, or for fear that it may rain. The clause after lest states the feared outcome; the main clause states the precaution. Wilde's sentence is a textbook instance: the Councillor adds his qualification "fearing lest people should think him unpractical" — that is, for fear that people would think him impractical. Note the exact parallelism: the matrix verb fearing makes the apprehension explicit, while lest alone can carry the same fear without any such verb.
  • The subjunctive requirement. In formal style lest takes the present subjunctive ("lest he forget", "lest the truth be lost", "lest there be any doubt"); older English also used the past subjunctive ("lest he were discovered"). British usage equally allows lest + should ("lest it should rain"). The subjunctive is what makes the construction feel bookish today, since the English subjunctive is otherwise in retreat; lest is thus the last native bastion of a mood that Latin and Greek mark with dedicated particles (nē, mḗ).
  • Register and domain. Lest is rare in spontaneous speech; it flourishes in formal prose, cautionary academic sentences, religious language, legal drafting and public warning. It is a marker of careful, deliberately archaic-flavoured style, and it is strongly idiomatic in fixed phrases.
  • Fixed phrases. The most famous is "lest we forget", from Rudyard Kipling's poem "Recessional" (1897), now a fixed memorial formula across the Commonwealth, engraved on war memorials and recited on Remembrance Day. Equally current: "lest there be any doubt", "lest it be thought that …", "lest the reader …", "lest we grow complacent".
  • Overlaps and near-synonyms. Modern colloquial speech prefers "in case", "so that … doesn't", "for fear that"; lest differs from all of these in combining apprehension with negative purpose and in demanding the subjunctive or should. It is often replaceable by "or" with a negative outcome ("hurry, lest you miss it" ≈ "hurry, or you'll miss it"), but only lest marks the clause as feared in advance.
  • Grammatical history. The word is the contracted survivor of an Old English comparative phrase, þȳ lǣs þe, literally "by the less that" — "by that much less, so that …". Its modern shape, lest, is the husk of that phrase, and its semantics still carry the original logic: an action is taken to make the feared thing happen less. This is a classic grammaticalisation — a full clause shrinking to a one-syllable conjunction, the earliest documented path of this kind in English negative-purpose marking.
  • Semantic bridge. From a comparative adverb phrase ("by so much less [that …]") to a purpose conjunction is a textbook semantic narrowing: the meaning moved from "to a lesser degree" to "so that [the bad thing] does not happen", and the word froze into an invariant conjunction, losing its inflectional and comparative freedom.

In context. In Wilde's The Happy Prince, the Councillor "added, fearing lest people should think him unpractical, which he really was not" — the subjunctive-style construction mirrors the caution of a man who monitors other people's opinions of him, which is precisely the satire: the same councillor who dismisses the statue's utility is desperately anxious about his own reputation. The lest-clause is the story's little mirror of vanity.
