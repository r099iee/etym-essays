BLOTCHED is the past-participial adjective meaning marked with blotches, irregular patches of colour, texture, or discoloration larger and less defined than spots and less uniform than stains. The smooth skin was blotched with pink and grey describes the mottled post-mortem lividity of a stranded porpoise, where blood settles, capillaries break, and pale flesh shows through grey epidermis in uneven islands. To be blotched is to have lost evenness: a blotched complexion flushes red and white, blotched paper cockles with damp, a blotched copy is marred by ink.

The base noun blotch names both the mark and, in older medicine and horticulture, the swelling or eruption that makes it: a blotch on the skin could be a pimple, boil, or scab as well as a flat discoloration. The verb to blotch means to cover with such marks, usually passively or accidentally: cheeks blotched with tears, leaves blotched with mildew, pages blotched with mould. The -ed form stresses resulting state rather than action, which is why natural-history prose favours it: the observer records pattern, not agency.

  • Primary sense — mottled with irregular colour patches (participial adjective): covered in uneven spots of contrasting tone. Examples: skin blotched pink and grey, face blotched red, sky blotched with cloud. The semantic bridge from blot plus botch is visual-tactile: blot supplies the stain schema of ink spreading on paper, botch supplies the swelling schema of a botched sore, and blotch fuses them into a raised or sunken discoloured patch. Larger than a speck, smaller than a zone, ragged at the edges.
  • Secondary sense — diseased, blemished, or spoiled in patches: leaves blotched by leaf-miners, fruit blotched with scab, paint blotched by damp. Here the bridge is pathological: the colour patch signals underlying decay, fungus, virus, or faulty workmanship. Horticulturists speak of blotch as a symptom class alongside spot, streak, and mosaic; printers speak of blotched impressions where ink pools. The porpoise sentence hovers between neutral mottling and decay, because death mottles and rots at once.
  • Tertiary senses — figurative and technical extensions: a reputation blotched by scandal maps skin blemish onto honour; a blotched performance maps ink failure onto art. In dermatology blotched erythema, blotched pigmentation, and blotchy are diagnostic descriptors distinguishing livedo, melasma, and allergy from uniform rash. In art criticism blotched handling condemns uneven impasto. Each extension keeps the core complaint: an expected even field broken by intrusive islands.
  • Usage, register, and literary nuance: blotched is plain, slightly clinical, and faintly repellent, stronger than mottled which can be decorative, weaker than gangrenous which is fatal. Pink and grey as a pair evokes cold flesh, cigarette ash, and Channel light; combined with smooth skin it makes the porpoise touchable and pitiable. Collocations include blotched with, blotched by, blotched face, blotchy skin, blotch mine. To write blotched rather than spotted is to insist on irregularity, size, and unease.

Mastering BLOTCHED is thus mastering unevenness made visible: ink, blood, mildew, and shame all spreading past their outlines, leaving the smooth world interrupted.
