ply is a verb of restless activity. Its oldest surviving senses cluster around bending and applying: to bend something, to apply oneself to a task, to wield a tool, to work at a trade. From that cluster English developed two large modern branches — ply a trade / ply one’s business (to carry on an occupation busily and steadily) and ply someone with something (to keep offering it, often until they yield) — plus a transport sense, to ply a route, used of ferries, coaches and taxis. A separate noun ply, meaning a layer, thickness or strand, is a homograph borrowed along a parallel route from the same Latin verb.

In the source passage the verb is used in the first of these branches: “men ply their useful or useless business as if no Louis lay in danger.” The collocation is normally ply a trade or ply one’s calling; Carlyle substitutes “business” and so treats the whole commercial life of a city as a craft that is practised, hour after hour, with the same dogged repetition. The effect is half-admiring and half-ironic: the city works on, oblivious, while its king is in danger. There is no implication that the business is servile; ply implies energy and continuity, not drudgery.

Core verbal senses

  • To work at, practise or exercise (a trade, craft, calling, or, by extension, a business). “He plies his trade as a cooper”, “she plies her craft in a converted mill”. This is the sense in the source and the one that keeps the word alive in formal and literary English.
  • To use or wield (a tool, weapon or instrument) vigorously and repeatedly. “Ply the axe”, “ply the needle”, “ply the oar”. The image is of brisk, sustained action rather than a single stroke, which is why the word suits labour that is ongoing.
  • To keep supplying, offering or pressing something on a person. “Ply him with drink”, “ply her with questions”, “the crowd plied the negotiators with advice”. The construction is ply someone with something; the implication is insistence, sometimes hospitable, sometimes coercive.
  • To travel regularly along or over (a route, strait, sea or street). “Ferries ply the Channel”, “taxis plying the airport road”. The subject is a vessel or vehicle, not a person, and the sense is scheduled, commercial repetition.
  • To wait for custom in a public place, chiefly of taxis and boats: “to ply for hire”. This is a regulatory and legal phrase, common in licensing statutes and newspaper reports.

Archaic, technical and figurative senses

  • Nautical: to beat to windward, to tack. A ship plies when it works its way against the wind by a series of zigzags. The usage is recorded from the sixteenth century and survives in sailing manuals and historical fiction.
  • Obsolete: to bend, fold or twist. This is the earliest recorded sense in English and the source of all the others; it survives only in the noun and in related words such as pliable and pliant.
  • Figurative: to work away at an argument, a theme or a person’s resistance. “He plied the same point for an hour.” The metaphor is of repeated strokes on a stubborn surface.
  • Noun: a layer, thickness or strand. “Three-ply wool”, “a two-ply tyre”, “plywood”. Each ply is one sheet or strand laid over another, which preserves the original sense of folding or laying together.
  • Noun, archaic: a fold or pleat in cloth, and by extension a strand in a rope or a twist in yarn.

Register, connotation and collocation

  • Register: ply a trade is formal and slightly archaic, at home in biography and obituary; ply with questions is standard modern journalism; ply for hire is bureaucratic; three-ply is commercial and technical.
  • Connotation: diligence, persistence, steady-handed skill. With with, a hint of siege tactics — the person plied is being worn down by kindness or curiosity.
  • Typical collocations: ply a trade, ply one’s craft, ply one’s business, ply the oars, ply the needle, ply with drink, ply with questions, ply the route, ply for hire, ply the streets, two-ply, multi-ply.
  • False friends: apply, reply, imply, supply, multiply and complicate all contain the Latin verb plicāre but are independent borrowings, not derivatives of English ply.

Why Carlyle’s choice is exact

Ply is the verb of people who keep their hands moving. Applied to “business”, it strips commerce of any glamour of speculation and presents it as a craft: something done daily, with tools, by habit. That is precisely the contrast the passage needs — the ordinary, repetitive, useful-or-useless work of a city continuing while history hangs on a king’s life.
