SHANTIES is the plural of English "shanty," and the word is a textbook case of two etymologically unrelated words that have merged in spelling. The hut sense and the song sense have separate chains, traced in isolation below, then joined at the modern form. CHAIN 1 — the "crude hut" sense (the older English borrowing): 1. (Origin debated; see note) Old French chantier "a wooden support, a log-yard, a building site, a workplace" (attested from the 12th century; from Latin canthērius "a gelding; a rafter, a beam, a frame," itself of uncertain further origin, traditionally connected with Greek kanthḗlios "a pack-ass, a beast of burden," because a beam or frame was called a "horse") — the source of the modern French word. 2. Canadian French chantier "a lumber camp, a lumberjack's hut or camp" (attested in 18th–19th-century Canadian French lumbering; the semantic shift from "building yard, workplace" to "lumber camp and its hut" happened in the North American logging trade) — the immediate source of the English word. 3. English shanty "a crude hut, a lumberjack's cabin, a rough dwelling" (attested from the 1820s in North American English; borrowed from Canadian French chantier with adaptation of the final syllable; earliest citations concern lumber camps) — the hut sense. 4. English shantytown "a settlement of crude huts" (derived from "shanty" + "town," attested from the later 19th century) — the extended compound of the hut sense. CHAIN 2 — the "sea song" sense: 1. Proto-Indo-European *kan- "to sing" (reconstructed; projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; cognates: Latin canere "to sing," cantāre "to sing, chant" and carmen "song," Old Irish canid "sings," Welsh canu "to sing," and English hen via chanticleer "the crowing cock") — the Indo-European root of singing. 2. Latin canere "to sing" (attested from the earliest Latin; the verb of song and incantation, cf. carmen "song, charm") — the root verb. 3. Latin cantāre "to sing, to chant, to repeat in song" (attested; the frequentative of canere, "to keep singing") — the extended verb that produced the Romance family of "sing." 4. Old French chanter "to sing" (attested from the 9th–10th centuries; the regular Romance continuation of Latin cantāre, with the characteristic shift of c to ch in Gallo-Romance) — the direct source of the English word. 5. English chantey / chanty "a sailor's work song" (attested from the 1850s–1860s in American nautical writing; borrowed from the French imperative/noun use of chanter "to sing," as the sailors' song; earliest forms show chanty and chantey) — the song sense in its original spelling. 6. English shanty "a sea song" (the spelling shanty for the song sense takes over from chantey in the later 19th century, under the influence of the identical hut-word; by the early 20th century "sea shanty" is the standard form) — the merged spelling. 7. NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: (1) The derivation of French chantier is genuinely debated at its deepest level: the traditional chain runs Latin canthērius "a gelding; (by metaphor) a beam, rafter, wooden horse" ← Greek kanthḗlios "a pack-ass," but both the Greek source and the "beam = horse" metaphor are contested, and some authorities prefer a native Latin formation of uncertain origin; the French "building yard, lumber camp" senses are, however, securely attested. (2) The Irish derivation sometimes offered for "shanty" (from Irish sean-tigh "old house") is a folk etymology without documentary support and is rejected by the OED. (3) The song sense enters English as chantey/chanty, and only later adopts the "shanty" spelling by merger with the hut word; the exact date and mechanism of the spelling merger are not precisely documented, but the coexistence of both spellings in 19th-century nautical writing is well attested. (4) For the song sense, the PIE root *kan- "to sing" is standard and secure, with well-attested Latin, Celtic, and Germanic cognates.
