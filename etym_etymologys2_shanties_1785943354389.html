First sense: sea songs sung by sailors

PIE (c. 4000–3000 BCE)
*kan-
"to sing, resound"

↓
Latin (3rd–1st c. BCE)
canere
"to sing"

↓
Latin (frequentative)
cantāre
"to sing, sing over and over"

↓
Old French (9th–12th c.)
chanter
"to sing"

↓
French (imperative plural)
chantez!
"(you all) sing!"

↓
English (1860s)
chanty / shanty
"a sailors' work song"

↓
English (plural)
shanties
"sailors' sea songs"

Second sense: crude huts

Greek (c. 5th–4th c. BCE)
kanthēlios
"a pack-ass, beast of burden"

↓
Latin (1st c. BCE)
cantherius
"gelding, pack-horse; beam, rafter, frame"

↓
French (13th c.)
chantier
"timber yard, workshop, gantry"

↓
Canadian French (18th–19th c.)
chantier
"a lumberjack's camp, loggers' hut"

↓
English (U.S., 1820s)
shanty
"a crude hut, cabin"

↓
English (plural)
shanties
"crude huts, cabins"

Note: The two English words "shanty" are distinct. The sea-song word was first spelled "chanty" and is usually derived from the French imperative chantez! "sing!" (of chanter); its spelling was later absorbed into the hut word's form, and the exact song-source (imperative vs. infinitive) remains debated. The hut word comes from Canadian French chantier "loggers' camp."
