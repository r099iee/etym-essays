Latin origins. The word goes back to Latin castrum, “fort, fortified place”, and its plural castra, “camp”, the standard term for a Roman military encampment. The diminutive castellum, “small fort, stronghold”, is the form that travelled: Caesar uses it of forts and redoubts, and it is the source of a whole family of words — castellan, castellany, the place-name elements Chester, -caster and -cester, and, through French, château and châtelain.

An uncertain root. Latin castrum is traditionally connected with a PIE root *kes- “to cut (off)”, the fort being a place cut off or marked out. The semantic link is weak, and the etymology is not settled. Some scholars treat castrum as an isolated Italic word, possibly a loan; the derivation from *kes- should be presented as the conventional hypothesis rather than as established fact. What is certain is the Latin form and its diminutive, not the prehistoric root.

Old French developments. Vulgar Latin castellum became Old French chastel, attested around 1100 and familiar from the Song of Roland and the romances, where it means a castle, a fortified town or a noble stronghold. The initial c palatalised before a to the affricate /tʃ/, spelled ch; the s before a consonant weakened and was eventually lost, and its loss lengthened the preceding vowel, which is why modern château has a long â and a circumflex. Middle French spellings such as chasteau preserve the transitional stage; the circumflex was adopted in the eighteenth century as an orthographic convention for the lost s, not as a mark of vowel quality in origin.

English double borrowing. Old English borrowed castellum as castel, and Norman French reinforced it after 1066; this is the ancestor of modern castle. English borrowed the word a second time, directly from French, in the seventeenth century, as chateau. The two are doublets with a shared Latin ancestor but different histories: castle underwent the native English sound changes and became the ordinary word for a fortified building, while chateau kept its French shape and its French connotations.

Semantic drift in French.

  • Fortress: the medieval château fort is primarily military, with walls, towers and a keep.
  • Seigneurial residence: from the fourteenth century the castle becomes the seat of a lordship, and comfort and display begin to rival defence.
  • Renaissance palace: the Loire châteaux of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — Chambord, Chenonceau, Azay-le-Rideau — are pleasure houses with staircases and gardens rather than fortresses.
  • Classical palace: Vaux-le-Vicomte and Versailles turn the château into an instrument of absolutist display, which is the sense Carlyle needs.
  • Wine estate: from the eighteenth and especially the nineteenth century, Bordeaux properties adopt château as a designation, and the word becomes a brand as much as a building.

English usage. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries chateau is a traveller’s word for French buildings; in the nineteenth it acquires the wine sense through the Bordeaux trade, and in the twentieth it becomes a tourism and property word. Carlyle’s “his own Chateau of Versailles” belongs to the older, architectural use: the palace as the king’s house, with the French word lending dignity and a touch of irony.

Modern notes. English dictionaries accept both chateau and château; the accented form is more formal and more clearly French. The plural is châteaux in French and either châteaux or chateaus in English. In French the word is far broader than in English: château d’eau is a water tower, château de cartes a house of cards, château de sable a sandcastle. English has narrowed the loan to the grand, the old and the French.
