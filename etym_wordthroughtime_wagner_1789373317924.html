- 13th to 15th Century (Middle High German) - Established as an occupational family name (*wagener*), denoting a craftsman who built or drove four-wheeled carts, wagons, and wooden wheels.
- 16th to 18th Century - Remained a ubiquitous, mundane German surname without aesthetic, musical, or philosophical associations.
- Mid-19th Century (1850s–1880s) - Richard Wagner transforms the surname into an international aesthetic storm; his revolutionary music dramas (*Tristan und Isolde*, *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, *Parsifal*) revolutionize harmony and theatrical staging.
- Late 19th Century (French Symbolism & Fin-de-Siècle) - French intellectuals launch the *Revue wagnérienne*; Baudelaire, Mallarmé, and Villiers de l'Isle-Adam embrace Wagner as the high priest of artistic synesthesia.
- Early 20th Century (Modernist Literature & Proust) - Marcel Proust, James Joyce, Thomas Mann, and T. S. Eliot adopt Wagnerian polyphony and leitmotifs as the foundational structural method for twentieth-century modernism.
- Mid-20th Century (Post-War Ideological Reckoning) - The name undergoes severe critical examination due to Wagner's anti-Semitic essays and the Nazi regime's propaganda co-optation of his operas.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Celebrated as the ultimate benchmark of monumental artistic scope and orchestral architecture, his leitmotif system becoming the universal structural language of modern cinematic scoring.
