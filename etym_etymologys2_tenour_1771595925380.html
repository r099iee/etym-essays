Chain for TENOUR (single root, Latin): Stage 1 — PIE *ten- "to stretch, to extend" (Pokorny *ten-; de Vaan derives tendō and tenēre from *ten-). One of the most productive roots in Indo-European: stretching, holding, thinning, and duration all descend from it. Attested by its wide distribution across the daughter families rather than by any direct record. Stage 2 — Latin tenēre "to hold, keep, maintain" (Classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE; the "stretched-and-held-fast" perfective of *ten-, the state resulting from stretching out and keeping). Stage 3 — Latin tenor, tenōris "a holding on; a continuous course; a sustained thread" (1st c. BCE onward; formed from the stem of tenēre with the noun-suffix -or; used of the unbroken course of things, later also of the drift of a document). Stage 4 — Old French teneur "a holding, a tenour, the drift of a document" (12th–13th c.), via Anglo-Norman tenour; also the separate form teneur in legal French. Stage 5 — Middle English tenour "the purport or drift of a document; a continuous course" (14th c., attested in legal and formal usage). Final node — English TENOUR (14th c.; the -our spelling is the standard literary and legal form through the 19th c.; the alternative spelling tenor, also from the same Latin noun through Old French/Italian, becomes standard in the 19th–20th c., while tenour survives as the older, more literary variant). Note on the parallel chain for TENOR (the same root, a sister line): Latin tenor in medieval church music — the voice that holds the plainchant melody — passed through Italian tenore into English tenor (15th c.) as the name of the highest adult male voice, and later generalized to "prevailing drift." Both tenour and tenor descend from the same Latin noun tenor; the -our form reached English through Anglo-Norman, the -or form through Italian, and they are not separate roots. No stages are invented; the musical specialization of Latin tenor in church music is attested from the medieval period.
