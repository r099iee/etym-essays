SUMPTUOUSNESS is the abstract noun meaning "the quality of being expensive-looking, luxurious, or lavish" — the property of a thing, a room, a feast, a garment or a spectacle of costing a great deal and looking it. In the passage from David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng (The Story of the Stone), the word stands at the architectural climax of Dai-yu's first tour of the mansion: "The whole formed an architectural unit of greater sumptuousness and magnificence than anything Dai-yu had yet seen that day, from which she concluded that this must be the main inner hall of the whole mansion." The tone is one of hushed, cumulative awe. The noun gathers up everything the young visitor has been absorbing room by room — carved beams, lacquered columns, antique bronzes, shimmering silks — and announces that here, in the main inner hall, the display of wealth reaches its peak. Sumptuousness is expense that has become a total environment: not one precious object but a coordinated wealth laid over every surface, so that the visitor moves inside the family fortune rather than merely looking at it. For Dai-yu the effect is overwhelming and diagnostic: it is the material proof that she has entered the absolute heart of the Jia empire, a place where magnificence is not an ornament of daily life but its very standard. The word therefore performs two duties at once — it records a quality of the building and, at the very same moment, measures the outsider's initiation into the house, her slow dawning recognition that this is what wealth, at its summit, actually feels like to walk through.

The senses of SUMPTUOUSNESS, primary and secondary, are these:
  • Material costliness: the quality of objects or surroundings that are expensive and visibly so — costly materials, rich fabrics, gilded and inlaid surfaces.
  • Lavishness of expenditure: the quality of spending great sums, of a feast or entertainment prepared with unstinting outlay; the related adjective "sumptuous" describes the feast itself.
  • Magnificence of effect: the grandeur that cost produces — the sumptuousness of a palace, a ceremony, a banquet, an interior.
  • Enveloping luxury (the modern, almost environmental sense): wealth laid over every surface so that the observer moves inside it — the sense that governs the Hong lou meng sentence.
  • Moral or economic shade (historical): excess and self-indulgence, the display that moralists mistrusted; the word is connected etymologically to "sumptuary" and "sumptuary laws," the laws that once limited private luxury, so "sumptuousness" can carry a whisper of reproach as well as admiration.
  • By extension to non-material things: a "sumptuous" style of prose, a "sumptuous" colour scheme, a "sumptuous" imagination — richness of effect transposed from money to aesthetics.

The semantic bridge from the ancient to the modern sense runs through the idea of spending. Etymologically "sumptuous" descends from Latin sumptus, "expense, cost, outlay," so the whole family means, at bottom, "costly," "expensive," "characterised by outlay." Over the centuries the word slid from the neutral economic meaning (costly) to the admiring aesthetic one (magnificent): what costs a great deal came to be admired for looking it, and the admiration slowly swallowed the accounting. The moral shading has never quite died — because expense invites questions of excess, "sumptuousness" retains a faint hint of reproach even in admiring use, and it is this double tone — admiration plus a whisper of excess — that the architectural sentence exploits when Dai-yu concludes, from the very peak of sumptuousness, that she has reached the main inner hall of the whole mansion.

Register boundaries against the near-synonyms sharpen the word:
  • "Luxury" is the broadest, pointing at comfort, pleasure and self-indulgence rather than at cost; a luxury hotel is comfortable, but the word does not insist on solemn, old-fashioned costliness the way sumptuousness does.
  • "Lavishness" stresses reckless abundance and open-handed spending — great quantity poured out — where sumptuousness stresses refinement in the very act of spending; the lavish can be gaudy, the sumptuous never is.
  • "Opulence" is the nearest competitor, for it too means wealth on display; but opulence has a showy, almost theatrical energy, whereas sumptuousness is heavier, graver, more old-fashioned — the wealth of a great house rather than of a spectacle.
  • "Magnificence" emphasises the scale and splendour of the effect, while sumptuousness stresses the costly materials and appointments that produce the effect.
  • "Richness" is plainer and broader, covering colour, texture and density of effect in art, music and prose as well as money; sumptuousness is confined to costly grandeur.

In short, SUMPTUOUSNESS is the noun for wealth that has become atmosphere: not the price tag of a single object but the coordinated costliness of an entire environment, admired and faintly reproved at once.
