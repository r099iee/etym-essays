PIE *per- 'to lead, pass over, go across' (a widely attested root of 'crossing' words, zero-grade *pr̥-) → PIE nominal *pértus / *por-tu- 'crossing, passage' (a noun built on the root; the family includes Greek poros 'ford, passage, strait' and Latin portus 'harbour, port', both from the same root) → Proto-Germanic *furdu- 'a ford, a place for wading across a stream' (Grimm's Law shifted initial PIE *p to Germanic *f; the u-grade of the root supplies the vowel) → Old English ford 'a shallow place where a stream may be crossed' (attested from the 8th–9th c., and fixed in the earliest place-names, e.g. Oxenaford 'ford of the oxen', the origin of Oxford) → Middle English ford (12th–15th c., form and meaning unchanged) → modern English FORD (noun; the verb 'to ford, to wade across' develops from the noun in the 16th–17th c.). Debated/nuanced stages: Old Norse fjǫrðr (source of English fjord and firth) descends from the closely related variant *ferþu- of the same root rather than directly from *furdu-; whether all the Germanic 'crossing-and-inlet' words form one family or two parallel derivatives is a minor scholarly question. The core chain from PIE *per- to English ford itself is secure and undisputed. Note also the ancient semantic relationship with Latin portus 'harbour': both a ford and a harbour are places of passage between land and water, which is why the same PIE root gave the English river-crossing and the Latin seaport.
