bindle is a noun of American English, a hobo's word for a bundle of bedding and personal possessions wrapped in a cloth or blanket and carried over the shoulder, typically on the end of a stick. It belongs to the slang of itinerant workers, tramps, and migratory labourers of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and it is a word with strong literary after-life, inseparable from the image of the Depression-era road.

PRIMARY SENSE — the hobo's bundle

  • A bindle — a roll of bedding, spare clothes, cooking utensils, and other personal effects, tied up in a cloth or blanket and slung over the shoulder, often carried on a stick propped on the shoulder. The hobo's bindle is the sum total of his worldly goods — everything he owns in one cloth roll.
  • Bindle and bindle-stiff — a "bindle-stiff" (or "bindlestiff") is the man who carries one: a migrant worker, tramp, or hobo who travels from job to job, particularly through the Western United States. "Bindle-stiff" compounds the bundle with the older slang stiff ("an itinerant worker," as in "farm stiff").
  • Context use: "George unslung his bindle and dropped it gently on the bank" — the bindle is the portable home of the itinerant labourer, set down as a man sets down his entire domestic life.

SECONDARY SENSE — drug slang (20th century)

In American drug-user slang, "bindle" came to mean a small folded paper packet holding a dose of powdered drugs — especially heroin or cocaine — the folded paper itself being a little "bundle" of powder. This sense extends the core idea of a wrapped, tied-up packet:

  • A bindle of heroin — a single dose wrapped in a folded paper.
  • The etymology is the same: a small thing bound up in paper, exactly like the hobo's small blanket-bound packet of goods.

TERTIARY AND MODERN USES

  • Literary and historical marker: since the mid-twentieth century, "bindle" has been read chiefly in fiction of the Great Depression and the road — Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men (1937) being the canonical text — where the bindle is the emblem of the wandering labourer's rootlessness, poverty, and stubborn independence.
  • Nostalgic hobo lore: in the revived folklore of American hobos (the code of the road, hobo conventions, hobo museum culture) the bindle is the iconic prop, and the bindlestiff is the archetypal figure.
  • Computing / technical jargon: the word has been borrowed as the name of a small, portable, self-contained bundle of data or software — a "bindle" as a light, portable container — echoing the original meaning of a small wrapped package carried from place to place.
  • Geographical and dialectal echoes: "bindle" also appears in English dialect for a bundle or small package, and the related "bindlestiff" has passed from slang into general American English as a historical term for the hobo labourer.

HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE

The bridge runs straight through the idea of binding. The word is a dialectal alteration of bundle, reshaped under the influence of the verb bind — the thing you bind together. Its deep etymology is the Indo-European root *bhendh-, "to bind, tie," the same root that gives English bind and band and German Bündel "bundle." The hobo's bindle is literally "the bound-up thing," and every later sense — the folded drug packet, the portable data container — merely re-binds the same idea of things tied together into one portable parcel.
