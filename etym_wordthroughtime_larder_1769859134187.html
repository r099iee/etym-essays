- 14th–15th c. 'A storeroom for meat' — taken into English from Anglo-French lardier, a cool room set aside for cured pork fat and lard, among the most prized preserved provisions; wholly practical and domestic in register.
- 16th–18th c. 'A cold room for raw provisions in great houses' — widened to hold meats, fish, dairy, and perishables, becoming a standard feature of manor houses and country homes where cooling relied on north-facing masonry rather than ice; signalled household prosperity and careful management.
- 19th c. 'The food store of a country kitchen' — essential before refrigeration, the larder was the housekeeper's domain, carrying associations of abundance, self-sufficiency, and cozy domestic order.
- Contemporary 'A pantry or cupboard for food; figuratively, an abundant supply' — with modern refrigeration the original necessity has faded, so the word now carries a warm, nostalgic or rustic charm and survives figuratively ("a well-stocked larder") for ample reserves.
