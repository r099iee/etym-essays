To SHRIVEL is to contract, wrinkle, and shrink, drawing up into hard, creased folds, typically because of intense heat, dryness, or loss of moisture — and, by extension, to lose vitality, power, or substance, so that a thing or a person is diminished as if dried and crumpled. The verb is both intransitive ("the leaves shrivelled in the sun") and transitive ("the heat shrivelled the leaves"), and its participle forms (shrivelled or shriveled, shrivelling or shriveling) are freely adjectival: "shrivelled fruit", "a shrivelled old man", "shrivelled hopes". The core physical image is unmistakable: something plump, moist, and whole puckers, hardens, and folds in on itself, its surface creasing into a tight network of lines, until what was soft and full becomes small, hard, and dry. The word is applied above all to plants — leaves, roots, fruit, flowers — and to skin, but its force transfers easily to anything that wastes away: a shrivelled economy, a shrivelled reputation, a soul shrivelled by meanness. In the goblins' chant — "Shrivel and scorch!" — from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the verb is a bare imperative, an order of total destruction: fire is commanded not merely to burn but to make things pucker, wither, and collapse. Where scorch works on the surface, searing and blackening the outer layer, shrivel works on the whole body, robbing it of moisture and form; the pairing suggests the complete annihilation of living things — first their covering is seared, then their very substance crinkles, hardens, and dies. The tone is at once childish and apocalyptic, a jeering rhyme that reduces life to crisp, crumpled husks, and the word's homely, kitchen-and-garden associations make the cruelty feel domestic and therefore worse: the goblins chant a curse with the vocabulary of a cook or a gardener describing a shrivelled apple or a shrivelled leaf. The full polysemy of the word:
  • Physical contraction by heat or dryness (the core sense): "the paper shrivelled in the flame", "the grapes shrivelled on the vine", "her hands were shrivelled with age". The earliest and dominant sense, describing the puckering and folding of leaves, fruit, and skin as moisture leaves them.
  • Physical contraction by cold (rare but attested): "his skin shrivelled at the touch of the ice" — the same folding response to a different extreme.
  • Transitive causative: to make something shrivel — "the drought shrivelled the crops", "time shrivels all things". The verb with an object names the agent of the wasting.
  • Loss of vitality, power, or substance (figurative): "his confidence shrivelled under criticism", "the company's ambitions shrivelled in the recession", "a shrivelled spirit". The body-image transfers to the inner life: what loses its moisture loses its life.
  • Shrivel up (phrasal): emphatic or resultative form — "the flowers shrivelled up and died", "she shrivelled up with embarrassment". The added particle stresses the completion of the process.
  • Shrivelled as adjective: the settled participial form meaning dried, wrinkled, shrunken — "shrivelled leaves", "a shrivelled old face". Highly productive in descriptive writing.
  • Modern figurative of failure or decline: "his career shrivelled", "the dream shrivelled to nothing" — the word names a quiet, drying-out kind of failure rather than a sudden collapse.
The boundaries among its near-synonyms are precise:
  • Wither: similar in describing the drying and decline of a plant; differs because wither implies a slow, gradual drooping from age or drought, while shrivel is quicker and more violent, a physical contortion into folds usually driven by an extreme external force such as flame.
  • Wilt: similar in naming loss of plant rigidity; differs because wilt is the limp drooping of turgor that can often be reversed with water, while shrivel is permanent deformation into creased, hardened folds.
  • Shrink: similar in the idea of reducing in size; differs because shrink is the general, often reversible word (wool shrinks in hot water), while shrivel is an involuntary, damaging, wrinkled contraction.
  • Contract: similar in drawing together and becoming smaller; differs because contract is neutral and can be voluntary (a muscle contracts), while shrivel is always involuntary and destructive.
  • Scorch: similar as a partner in destruction by fire; differs because scorch blackens and sears the surface but leaves the shape intact, while shrivel deforms the whole object, folding it inward.
  • Wizen: similar in naming dry wrinkledness; differs because wizen (as in wizened) names the lean, dry wrinkledness of age, a state, while shrivel is an active process of contracting under stress.
  • Wrinkle: similar in the creasing of a surface; differs because wrinkle is superficial and can be gentle or cosmetic (a wrinkled brow), while shrivel involves the whole shrinking and hardening of the body or object.
  • Dehydrate: similar in the loss of moisture; differs because dehydrate is clinical and technical (of the body's water), while shrivel describes the visible, folded result of that loss.
Collocations are firmly physical: "shrivel up", "shrivel and die", "shrivelled leaves", "shrivelled fruit", "shrivelled skin", "shrivelled with age", "shrivelled in the heat", "shrivel under the sun", "shrivel and scorch" (the goblins' pairing). Register: standard to literary English, with a marked bodily, naturalistic force; the word belongs to the kitchen, the garden, and the skin, and its figurative uses always retain the memory of something soft and moist drying into something hard and small. The verb rhymes naturally with words of destruction and drying, which is why it has been a favourite of curses, threats, and apocalyptic description from its first appearances in English.
