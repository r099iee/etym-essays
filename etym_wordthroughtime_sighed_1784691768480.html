- Old English "sīcan" - itself an imitative word whose long sibilant beginning mirrors the soft hissing outflow of breath, "sigh" was always the sound of exhalation — the most intimate and least theatrical of the expressions of emotion.
- Middle English to 16th century - the verb was reshaped from its own past stem into "sīken/sighen", the -gh spelling recording a palatal fricative pronounced with the breath of the sigh itself; by the 16th century the fricative was lost in speech and the vowel drifted, leaving a spelling that now rhymes with "buy" though it once rhymed with "see".
- Early modern to modern - from human grief the word spread to the natural world: winds sigh, leaves sigh, waves sigh, so that "sighed" can move from a human breast to a riverside tree without strain, keeping its melancholy breath in every use.
