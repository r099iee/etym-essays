- Lovable and pleasing (14th-15th c.) - Enters Middle English with the older, affective sense: delightful, worthy of love, charming in itself - the sense of 'easy to love' that still glimmers beneath the modern word.
- Kindly disposed (16th-17th c.) - The emphasis slides from being loved to being loving: amiable now means good-natured, warmly disposed, friendly by temperament rather than effort, the social sense that would come to dominate.
- The polite compliment (18th-19th c.) - A staple of polite society vocabulary at its sunniest; Austen makes it a settled verdict on Bingley's character, while its twin amicable splits away to describe goodwill between parties rather than within a person.
- Modern usage - Still natural but slightly old-fashioned, warmer and more personal than bureaucratic 'friendly'; rarely applied to things, it quietly says of a person that they are easy to love.
