Kunstprosa is a young compound of very old parts: Kunst art, skill and Prosa straightforward speech. Its evolution must therefore tell three stories — Kunst, Prosa, and their marriage as a critical term around 1900.

Kunst descends from Proto-Indo-European *gneh- to know, recognize. Germanic *kunn- to know, be able gives Old High German kunnan to know, kunst knowledge, skill, mastery, Middle High German kunst courtly accomplishment. Luther still uses Kunst for knowledge and craft, not only fine art. Only in the 18th c. under Winckelmann, Lessing, Kant, and Schiller does Kunst narrow to fine art with aesthetic autonomy — the very idea that prose could be Kunst depends on this modern elevation.

  • PIE *gneh- “to know”: yields Greek gnosis, Latin noscere, notitia, notice, English know, knowledge, can, cunning, German kennen, können, Kunst. Knowing-how underlies art as skill.
  • Proto-Germanic *kunn- + -sti-: abstract noun *kunsti- knowledge, skill, mastery. Cognate with English cunning (originally skill, not slyness).
  • Old High German kunst (9th c.), Middle High German kunst (12th–14th c.): learned skill, courtly arts, magic arts (swarziu kunst necromancy). Walther von der Vogelweide praises kunst of singing.
  • Early New High German Kunst (1500s–1700s): craft guilds (Handwerk und Kunst), Dürer's art theory, then aesthetic separation: Kant Kritik der Urteilskraft (1790) defines fine art as purposiveness without purpose.

Prosa comes from Latin prosa oratio straightforward speech, from prorsa turned forward, direct, from provertere to turn forward, from PIE *wert- to turn (same root as subvert, verse). Quintilian contrasts prosa with versus (turned lines, verse): prose goes straight ahead, verse repeatedly turns back. Borrowed into Old High German as prosa in monastic rhetoric (Notker, 11th c.), then Middle High German, always denoting unversified learned writing. French prose reinforces the form.

  • PIE *wert- “to turn”: same root as subvert; here forward-turning gives directness.
  • Latin prosa (1st c. BCE onwards, Quintilian, Seneca): prosa oratio direct, straightforward discourse, as opposed to verse bound by metre.
  • Old French prose (12th c.), German Prosa (11th c. onwards): learned borrowing for non-verse writing, chronicles, sermons, later novels and essays.

The compound Kunstprosa itself is a modern coinage of German Stilgeschichte. The Brothers Grimm Deutsches Wörterbuch (1850s–1860s) barely knows it; it spreads c. 1890–1930 through philologists Eduard Norden (Die antike Kunstprosa, 1898, the founding history of Greek and Latin artistic prose rhythm), Wilhelm Hörmann, Karl Vossler, and Leo Spitzer. Norden's two-volume masterpiece traces clausulae and period-building from Gorgias to Augustine, giving art prose a continuous ancient pedigree and a technical vocabulary — exactly the taxonomy your book says English teachers lack (hypotactic, nominal, isocolon, hypotaxis, parataxis). Thomas Mann's 1920s–1940s self-conscious artistry makes living Kunstprosa world-famous; critics call Der Zauberberg and Doktor Faustus peaks of modern Kunstprosa.

Post-war Germanistik institutionalizes the term: dissertations on Kunstprosa des Barock, des Expressionismus, handbooks distinguishing Kunstprosa vs Gebrauchsprosa. Adorno and later Sebald debates ask whether mandarin Kunstprosa is complicit or resistant — the same charge your passage stages as executive scorn for pesky ornament. In the 1970s–1990s Anglo-American stylistics borrows the word precisely because English belles lettres had died and creative nonfiction sounded thin; saying Kunstprosa confers German seriousness.

Phonetics: German /ˈkʊnstpʁoːza/ with short u, consonant cluster -nst-, uvular r, long oː, voiced s as /z/, final schwa. Anglicized borrowing keeps spelling but often says /ˈkʊnstprəʊzə/. The user-given /ˈkʊnstpʁoːza/ preserves the German uvular fricative, signalling scholarly pronunciation. Compound stress remains initial, distinguishing it from phrasal Kunst Prosa.

Today Kunstprosa stands at the centre of the book's plea: an information society needs an economics of attention, and attention is shaped by rhythm, period, and figure — by art prose. What executives dismiss as peripheral is in fact infrastructure. The ancient straight road of prosa, once artfully paved by Kunst, becomes the highway of data.
