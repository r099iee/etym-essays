ROOT 1: FAG
Old Norse / North Sea Germanic (pre-14th c.)
*fagg- / *flak-
"to droop, flap, hang loosely"
↓
Late Middle English (c. 14th–15th c.)
fagge
"a loose end of cloth; tuft, flap"
↓
Early Modern English (1580s)
fag
"frayed cloth end; unraveled rope"
"(later) weary drudge, cigarette"

ROOT 2: END
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*h₂entí
"in front, facing, opposite; end, forehead"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*andijaz
"front, end, boundary, limit"
↓
Old English (c. 8th–9th c.)
ende
"end, boundary, extremity, death"
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.)
ende
"conclusion, termination, edge"
↓
Modern English (16th c.)
end
"final point, terminal part"

COMPOUND FORMATION
Early Modern English (c. 1580s–1600s)
fagge-ende / fag-end
"frayed end of a piece of cloth"
↓
Modern English (17th c.–present)
fag-end
"remnant, dregs, cigarette stub"
Note: The etymological origin of the element *fag* is historically nuanced and debated. Linguists connect Late Middle English *fagge* with dialectal northern words for loose tufts of wool, sheep ticks, and fluttering flaps, likely related to the Germanic root behind *flag* (to droop or flap). The element *end* has an impeccable Indo-European pedigree tracing to *h₂entí*. Their lexical compounding is securely attested in English textile records from the late 16th century.
