No other Romance language carries its ancestry so openly in its name. Romanian and România descend from Latin Romanus, "Roman", the adjective of Roma. The name of Rome itself is of uncertain origin: ancient tradition made it eponymous with Romulus, while modern scholars have proposed an Etruscan source, a link with Latin ruma "teat" (the she-wolf's suckling of the twins), or a Greek loan meaning "strength". What matters for Romanian is not the ultimate origin of Roma but the extraordinary persistence of Romanus as a self-description long after the western empire had gone.

Roman citizenship and the spread of the name. In the empire Romanus was first a legal status and then, after the Constitutio Antoniniana of 212 CE extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants, an increasingly general identity. In the Greek-speaking east the same idea was carried by Rhōmaios (Ῥωμαῖος), which survived as the Byzantine self-designation and, in various forms, in modern Greek. In the Latin-speaking Balkans the word became the ordinary name for the Latin-descended, Orthodox Christian population, and it is this usage that surfaces in the earliest Romanian texts.

From Dacia to the first texts. Roman Dacia was conquered in 106 CE and given up by the army in the 270s, and the question of what happened to its Latin-speaking population is one of the great debates of European ethnogenesis. The continuity theory stresses archaeological and linguistic evidence for a persistent Daco-Roman population; the migration theory stresses the absence of early documentation and the possibility that Romance speakers moved north of the Danube later. Romanian linguists have also reconstructed a Proto-Romanian stage, from which Aromanian, Megleno-Romanian, Istro-Romanian and Daco-Romanian descend, and dated its breakup to roughly the tenth to twelfth centuries. The first surviving Romanian document, Neacșu's letter of 1521, is a short warning about an Ottoman threat, but it already shows the characteristic morphology, the Latin-derived lexicon and the Cyrillic script of the chancery.

The social double life of rumân. Old Romanian rumân (also spelled român) could mean not only "Romanian" but also "serf, bondsman, dependent peasant". The social sense, now archaic, is a striking reminder that an ethnic name can slide into a status term: the ordinary countryman was both the bearer of the Roman name and the labourer bound to a lord's estate. The modern national meaning was reinforced in the nineteenth century, when writers and philologists standardised român and the state adopted România; the older social sense faded, leaving the ethnic and national meaning dominant.

Exonyms and the politics of naming. Outsiders did not call the Romanians by their own name. Germanic Walhaz, originally "foreigner" and specifically "Celtic or Romance speaker", produced German Wale, Walachen, Slavic Vlasi, Hungarian oláh and English Vlach. These terms survive for Balkan Romanian-speaking groups and in place-names, but they are external labels and can carry a dismissive or archaic tone. The choice between "Romanian", "Rumanian" and "Roumanian" in English reflects nineteenth-century French and German mediation: Roumanian follows French roumain, Rumanian was a common older English spelling, and Romanian eventually prevailed because it visibly connects the people to Rome.

Language reform and the re-Latinisation of the nineteenth century. The modern literary language was deliberately reshaped. The Transylvanian School, led by Samuil Micu and Gheorghe Șincai, argued for the Roman origin of the Romanians and promoted a Latin-oriented orthography. In the 1830s and 1860s the principalities moved from Cyrillic to the Latin alphabet, and writers borrowed heavily from French and Italian to modernise the vocabulary: a edita, a adopta, a realiza, deșert (in the sense of "dessert"), and thousands of other learned words entered the language. This re-Latinisation increased the Romance appearance of Romanian and made it more intelligible to Western Europeans, while also creating doublets alongside inherited words.

Orthographic politics. The spelling of the central vowel has been a political football. The 1953 reform replaced â with î in most positions but preserved român; the 1993 reform restored â in many words, including România and român. These changes were debated in parliaments, schools and newspapers, because orthography is never only a technical matter: it is a statement about Latin ancestry, national identity and the authority of the academy.

Modern attestations and global spread. Romanian literature offers a rich record of the word's national charge, from the poetry of Mihai Eminescu to the fiction of Ion Creangă, Liviu Rebreanu and Mircea Eliade, and in the twentieth century to Eugène Ionesco and Emil Cioran, who wrote in French, and Herta Müller, who wrote in German about a Romanian-German world. Since 1989 and especially since EU accession in 2007, several million Romanians have worked abroad, so "Romanian" is now a commonplace of European labour markets, schools and newspapers, and the language is sustained online by a large diaspora.

  • Phonetic drift: Latin ā > /ɨ/; intervocalic l > r; stressed e, o diphthongise; final -us is lost.
  • Semantic drift: "Roman" > "Orthodox Romance speaker" > "Romanian"; a parallel archaic sense "serf" shows how ethnicity can be re-read as status.
  • Sociolinguistic drift: Cyrillic to Latin script, Slavonic to Romanian liturgy, French fashion to English influence.
  • Political drift: the name has been used to argue continuity, to resist Hungarian and Soviet claims, and to anchor Romania in the Romance and EU fold.

In short, "Romanian" is less a label than a thesis: it asserts that Rome survived on the Danube, and every reform of spelling, vocabulary and script has been a way of restating or contesting that claim.
