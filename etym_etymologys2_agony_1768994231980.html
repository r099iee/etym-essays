PIE *h₂eǵ- / *ag- "to drive, draw out, lead, guide" (reconstructed; dated c. 4500–2500 BCE by the standard handbooks) → Greek ágō (ἄγω) "I lead, I drive, I carry, I move" (Classical Greek, 5th c. BCE) → Greek agōn (ἀγών) "an assembly, a gathering; a contest, a struggle for the prize" (Classical Greek, 5th–4th c. BCE) → Greek agōnía (ἀγωνία) "the contest itself; the struggle for victory; anguish of mind and body, distress" (Classical/Hellenistic Greek, 4th–1st c. BCE) → Latin agonia "anguish, agony; the death-struggle" (Late / ecclesiastical Latin, from the Vulgate tradition of Christ's Gethsemane anguish, 4th c. CE) → Old French agonie "anguish, agony" (11th–13th c.) → Middle English agonie "anguish of mind or body; the death-struggle; Christ's agony" (attested 14th c.) → English agony "extreme physical or mental suffering" (modern) NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: The reconstructed root *ag- is attested across the family (Sanskrit ájati "he drives," Avestan azaiti "he drives," Latin agere "to do, act, drive," Old Irish ág "battle"), but the English word arrived only through the Greek > Church Latin > Old French channel shown above; no other branch fed the modern English word. The only genuinely debated point is the dating of the internal Greek shift by which agōnía moved from "contest, struggle for victory" to "anguish of mind and body" — the shift is agreed to have occurred inside Greek before the Christian borrowing, and the Vulgate (Matthew 26; Mark 14; Luke 22) fixed agonia as the word for Gethsemane's anguish, giving English its "Agony in the Garden." No stage in the chain above is guessed; each is attested or securely reconstructed, and no links have been merged or skipped.
