Garb (noun and transitive verb; here noun) refers primarily to clothing, dress, or outward personal attire, especially when distinctive, professional, national, historical, or ceremonial. Unlike generic terms like clothes or clothing, garb conveys a heightened sense of deliberate style, social role, symbolic function, or complete ensemble. It implies that the dress is not merely utilitarian coverage for the body, but an external manifestation of an identity, rank, occupation, religious affiliation, or historical era.

In addition to its concrete textile meaning, garb possesses an extensive metaphorical application denoting outward appearance, external form, packaging, design format, or a deceptive pretext (a guise). When an idea, political philosophy, or physical object appears in a new "garb," its underlying substance remains unchanged, but its outward presentation, rhetorical framing, or physical casing has been altered to suit a particular audience or social setting.

Polysemy and Specialized Modern Senses:

  • Professional, Ecclesiastical, and Uniform Attire: In institutional settings, garb designates standardized, symbolically regulated dress that instantly identifies the wearer's office or status. Examples include judicial garb (black robes and wigs), clerical garb (cassocks, habits, or clerical collars), military garb, and prison garb. In each case, the clothing is an official insignia of societal authority, communal dedication, or institutional subjugation.
  • Theatrical, Historical, and Cosplay Costuming: In theatre, cinema, and historical reenactment, garb denotes period-accurate or genre-specific attire (e.g., Elizabethan garb, medieval garb, superhero garb). Here, it denotes an external shell assumed temporarily to inhabit a fictional or historical persona.
  • Publishing, Product Design, and Editorial Formats: In publishing, typography, and industrial design—precisely as employed by Rudolf Arnheim in the context passage ("even in its tidier garb")—garb refers to the physical binding, dust jacket, paper quality, layout, or revised edition format of a book or product. Arnheim affectionately contrasts the messy, dog-eared paperback covered in studio plaster with the neat, elegant typography and refined binding of the new hardcover edition.
  • Figurative Guise, Rhetorical Facade, and Political Pretext: In political analysis and moral criticism, garb frequently denotes a cosmetic or deceitful veneer concealing a sinister or self-interested motive. Phrases such as "authoritarianism dressed in the garb of national security" or "greed in the garb of philanthropy" exploit the word's theatrical connotation of donning a costume to deceive onlookers.
  • Archaic / Literary Verb Usage: As a transitive verb (to garb), it means to clothe, array, or attire (e.g., "garbed in shimmering white silk"), common in poetic, romantic, and ceremonial prose.

Semantic Evolution and Historical Bridges:

The fascinating historical trajectory of garb traces back from Italian garbo, which originally signified grace, elegant manners, polite bearing, or pleasing symmetry (ultimately of Germanic origin, related to *garwaz "ready, equipped, prepared", whence modern English gear). When English borrowed garb in the late 16th century, it initially meant personal posture, deportment, and courtly demeanor. Through metonymic shift, the outward bodily poise and social manners of courtly gentlemen became associated with the tailored fashion and clothing that accentuated that posture. By the early 17th century, the word had transferred completely from the manner of carrying oneself to the actual garments worn on the body. From physical garments, it subsequently expanded into the realm of rhetoric and aesthetics as an outward casing or presentation.
