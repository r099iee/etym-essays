• Vibrissa: Similar because both name the long tactile hair on an animal’s muzzle; differs because “vibrissa” is the technical zoological term (from Latin for a nostril-hair), while “whisker” is the everyday English word, also used of human facial hair.
  • Moustache: Similar because both can name the hair above a man’s upper lip; differs because “moustache” names only the lip-hair, while “whisker” covers cheeks, side-hair, and the animal sense.
  • Beard: Similar because both are human facial hair; differs because a beard is the hair of the chin, jaw, and cheeks as a mass, while whiskers are the long individual hairs, especially of the lip and cheeks.
  • Sideburn: Similar because both denote the hair grown down the side of the face; differs because “sideburn” is the modern fashion term for a specific strip of cheek-hair, while “whisker” spans animal biology and the idioms of tiny margins.
  • Bristle: Similar because both describe stiff, projecting hairs; differs because a bristle is short, rigid, and coarse (as on a brush or stubble), while a whisker is long, fine, and exquisitely tactile.
  • Stubble: Similar because both are facial hairs; differs because stubble is the short, newly grown hair after shaving, while whiskers are long, full-grown hairs.
  • Filament: Similar because both can name a fine, threadlike object; differs because “filament” is the neutral technical term (in electronics, a thin wire or strand), while “whisker” is the vivid, biological metaphor for a hairlike crystal or wire.
  • Hair: Similar because a whisker is a type of hair; differs because “hair” is the general term for the whole body covering, while “whisker” picks out the long tactile or facial hairs.
  • Whisk: Similar because it shares the stem and the quick sweeping image; differs because “whisk” is the verb/noun of quick sweeping motion (and the kitchen tool), while “whisker” is the agent-noun that came to name the hair.
  • Cat’s whisker (crystal radio): Similar because it uses the word for a fine contact wire; differs because it is a specific historical technical sense (the thin wire touching a crystal in early radio), not the animal hair.
