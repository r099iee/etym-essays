"Accrued" is the past tense and past participle of the verb accrue, meaning to accumulate, build up, or grow gradually over time, especially of money, benefits, advantages, rights, or liabilities coming to belong to a person or entity as a result of continued action, passage of time, or natural increase. In its most central modern sense it describes something that has not arrived all at once but has gathered incrementally until it now exists as a possessed total.

In the source sentence — land tilled most fruitfully when the rewards of careful husbandry accrued to the family — it is used in its classic economic-moral sense: benefits naturally gathering to and vesting in the producer. This captures both the financial and the ethical nuance of accrual: increase by organic growth plus rightful belonging. The rewards did not merely appear; they grew out of labour and then attached themselves to the labourer.

  • Financial accumulation (core sense): Sums of money, interest, dividends, wages, or debts that build up over time and become owed or owned. E.g., accrued interest, accrued wages, accrued liabilities. This is the dominant technical use in accounting, where revenues and expenses are recognized when earned or incurred, not when cash changes hands. Accrued expenses are costs already incurred but not yet paid; accrued revenue is income already earned but not yet received.
  • Benefits, rights and advantages vesting in someone: Leave, pension rights, seniority, holiday pay, social-security entitlements, or legal claims that gradually become a person's possession through service or tenure. E.g., "after ten years she had accrued six weeks of annual leave"; "rights accrued to the family." The construction "accrue to" is characteristic here, marking the beneficiary with to.
  • General gradual increase: Any thing that piles up by slow addition — knowledge, experience, power, reputation, guilt, disadvantages. E.g., "advantages accrued from steady practice"; "problems accrued over decades of neglect." In this looser sense it is close to accumulate but retains a suggestion of natural growth rather than deliberate heaping.
  • Legal sense — vested claims: In law, a cause of action or right is said to accrue when it comes into existence and becomes enforceable, e.g., "the claim accrued on the date of breach." Limitation periods run from accrual. Here the emphasis is not on size but on maturation into an enforceable entitlement.
  • Biological / natural growth (older, now rare): In early modern English accrue could describe plants, bodies, or natural features growing by addition from outside, reflecting its Latin root accrescere — to grow toward, to increase. This literal growth sense survives only in the metaphor of financial and moral increase.
  • Transitive vs. intransitive nuance: Modern usage is overwhelmingly intransitive with to (benefits accrue to someone) or attributive as an adjective (accrued benefits, accrued interest). Transitive use — "he accrued a fortune" — exists, especially in American business prose, but careful style still prefers accumulate for deliberate gathering and reserves accrue for growth that happens to someone over time.
  • Register and connotation: Formal, institutional, and slightly impersonal. It belongs to finance, accounting, law, human resources, and historical economics. Unlike earned, which stresses desert, or piled up, which stresses mass, accrued stresses lawful, orderly, time-mediated growth — increase that is both natural and rightful.

Polysemy is thus unified by one image: slow growing-toward. Interest grows day by day toward the lender; leave grows month by month toward the worker; rights grow generation by generation toward the family. The semantic bridge from Latin ad-crescere (to grow toward) to modern finance is direct: what grows toward you becomes yours. Secondary extensions to problems, guilt, or power retain the growth idea but drop the beneficiary felicity — things can accrue ominously as well as benignly.
