Wheeze is both a sound and an action: as a verb, to breathe with difficulty producing a high-pitched whistling, whistling-rattling or sibilant sound; as a noun, the sound itself, a breathy whistle made when air is forced through narrowed or obstructed airways, and by extension any whistling puff, gasp or laboured exhalation.

Physiologically, wheezing occurs when bronchial tubes constrict, swell or fill with mucus, as in asthma, bronchitis, emphysema, allergies or a cold. Air squeezing through the narrowed passage vibrates, producing the characteristic wheeze. Clinicians distinguish inspiratory, expiratory and biphasic wheezes, monophonic versus polyphonic wheezes, and wheeze versus stridor and crackles. In ordinary language, however, to wheeze is simply to pant audibly: old men wheeze climbing stairs, horses wheeze, accordion bellows wheeze, and asthmatics reach for inhalers when they wheeze.

The quoted context — You would not have thought of a wheeze like that in a million years — uses the noun in its secondary, British-colloquial sense: a clever trick, dodge, ruse or clever wheeze, i.e. an ingenious plan or joky contrivance. This sense, attested from the 19th century, probably arose from the idea of puffing out something, or from thief's cant, and is now standard in phrases like a clever wheeze, a publicity wheeze, a money-saving wheeze. It has no pathos of illness; it is admiring or mildly sardonic.

  • Sense 1 — Verb: to breathe with whistle: I wheeze, you wheeze, he wheezes; wheezing and wheezed. To make a sibilant respiratory sound. Example: He wheezed with laughter. Semantic bridge is direct onomatopoeia: the word sounds like the breath it names.
  • Sense 2 — Noun: the sound: A wheeze, the wheezes (plural in medicine). An audible whistling breath, a symptom. Doctors chart wheeze as a sign, patients report wheeze as discomfort. Extension to machines: the wheeze of bagpipes, of a leaky tyre, of an old organ.
  • Sense 3 — Noun (UK informal): clever plan or trick: A wheeze meaning a bright idea, dodge or scheme. As in they thought up a wheeze to avoid queuing. The bridge is debated: either from the puff of breath that accompanies effort, or from wheeze as a joke that leaves one wheezing with laughter, or cant. Today common in journalism and politics.
  • Sense 4 — Verb extended: to utter wheezily or to move wheezing: He wheezed out an apology; the old bus wheezed up the hill. Here the respiratory sound colours speech or motion, implying age, decrepitude or comic exhaustion.
  • Sense 5 — Adjectival/attributive and idiomatic uses: Wheezy chest, wheezy laugh, wheezy accordion. Old wheezer for a broken-winded horse or by extension an old person or joke. None implies moral judgement, only physiology or affectionate mockery.

Register and nuance: in medicine wheeze is neutral-technical; in everyday speech it evokes age, fatness, smoking, cold air or mirth; in British newsrooms a wheeze is light, slightly cynical, never grand strategy. To wheeze is involuntary and audible, unlike to gasp (sudden), to pant (rapid), to sigh (emotional) or to whistle (voluntary musical). The word's long ee and final z mimic prolonged exhalation, making it strongly iconic and memorable for learners.
