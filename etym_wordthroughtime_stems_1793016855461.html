- Old English — staff and trunk: stefn, stemn names tree-trunks, posts, and the prow-timber of ships, sturdy upright timber in field and fleet.
- Middle English — garden and ship share it: stem holds up plants and holds together hulls, stem to stern becoming the sailor measure of a vessel.
- 16th–17th c. — glasses borrow it: the slender foot of a goblet becomes its stem, tableware dressed in garden language.
- 18th–19th c. — lineage and language: family stems and verb stems spread the plant image to peoples and words, stock from which branches fork.
- 20th c. — science takes the stalk: brain stems and stem cells carry the old farm word into neurology and biology, origin as medicine.
- Today — pillars and progenitors: walkers still peer through stems of trees, drinkers still lift glasses by the stem, and biologists still trace life to its stem, one upright for woods, tables, and bodies.
