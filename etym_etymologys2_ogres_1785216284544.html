Orcus — Latin, the Italic god of the underworld and, by metonymy, the underworld itself (attested from Plautus, 3rd–2nd c. BCE; also in Vergil's Aeneid). His own origin is debated: often connected with Greek Ὅρκος (Horkos), the personified Oath that destroyed perjurers, or held to be an Etruscan loanword; there is NO secure Proto-Indo-European reconstruction, so the chain is Italic > Romance. → orco — Italian, 'demon, monster, man-eating giant' — the god's name degrading in the Romance vernaculars to a popular cannibal giant (present in Straparola's Le piacevoli notti, 1550s, and Basile's Il Pentamerone, 1634–36). → ogre — French, 1697, coined or fixed by Charles Perrault in Histoires ou contes du temps passé, above all in Le Petit Poucet, as the archetypal literary man-eating fairy-tale giant. → ogre — English, first attested 1713 (with an earlier spelling hogre, 1705), borrowed through translations of the French fairy tales. → ogres — the modern English plural, the form used by Tolkien in The Hobbit (1937): 'all the horrible names of all the giants and ogres he had ever heard told of in tales.' DEBATED STAGES: the immediate source is secure (Perrault's French ogre), but the deeper line is debated — the common view derives ogre from Italian orco < Latin Orcus, while older alternative theories link the word to the Ogier / Oghur (Oghur-Turkic) peoples or to the legendary hero Ogier the Dane, and some scholars allow that Perrault may have shaped the form from folk speech rather than borrowing it purely from Italian. All scholars agree there is no reliable Indo-European root to reconstruct.
