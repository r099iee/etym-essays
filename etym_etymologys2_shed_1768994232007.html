SHED — single linear chain.
1. PIE *skei- / *skēi- "to cut, split, divide" — cf. Sanskrit root chid- "to split, cut off" (chinátti "cuts", Hindi/Odia chinn "cut off"), Greek σχίζειν (skhizein) "to split" (→ schism, schist), Latin scindere "to cut" (→ rescind, scission), Old Irish scíath "shield" (literally "a cut piece of wood").
2. Proto-Germanic *skaidaną "to separate, part, divide" — cf. Gothic skaidan "to separate", Old Norse skilja "to separate" (→ English skill), Old High German skeidan (→ German scheiden "to separate, divorce").
3. Old English scēadan "to divide, separate, discriminate; to shed" — the same root also gives Old English scēad "division, discrimination".
4. Middle English shedden.
5. English shed (verb) — c. 1200 "to cast off, spill".
6. Derived in English: watershed (19th c., the ridge that divides and sheds water), the "shed" of the hair (the parting), and the participial idiom get shed of (chiefly US dialectal, "to get rid of") — a fossil of the old past-participial adjective.
Note on nuance: the noun shed (a lean-to hut) is probably a separate word, blended with "shade" (a shelter), not a descendant of the verb's "divide" root; and scissors is a false friend, from Latin caedere "to cut" (via cīsōria), not from scindere.
