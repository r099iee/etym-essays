- 1400s–1500s - In Old/Middle French, designated a haughty, supercilious grimace, a proud stare, or a pursed-lip countenance (*faire la morgue* = to look insolent).
- 1600s–1700s - Applied to the inner gatehouse of Paris's Grand Châtelet prison, where jailers subjected incoming prisoners to a prolonged, penetrating stare (*morguer*) to memorize their features.
- 1804–1860s - Designated the official Parisian public mortuary (*La Morgue*) on the Île de la Cité, where unidentified corpses retrieved from the Seine were displayed behind glass for public viewing; entered English as the standard term for a public body-identification house.
- Late 1800s to Mid-1900s - Adopted in American journalism as newsroom slang for the clipping files and pre-written obituaries; used colloquially in British comedy (P.G. Wodehouse) for an uncomfortably depressing, silent room.
- Contemporary Era - Universal clinical and colloquial term for a hospital mortuary, forensic pathology center, or figuratively any place dead of joy.
