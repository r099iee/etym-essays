Typecasting (/ˈtaɪpˌkɑːs.tɪŋ/) is a noun (also functioning as the present participle of the verb typecast) denoting the recurring practice in theater, film, television, and media industries of repeatedly casting an actor in identical or closely similar character roles based on their physical appearance, vocal qualities, ethnicity, age, or past commercial success.

Beyond the entertainment industry, the term carries powerful polysemic and metaphorical extensions across modern psychology, sociology, management, and everyday life:

  • Theatrical & Cinematic Pigeonholing (Primary Industry Sense): The industrial tendency of casting directors and producers to pigeonhole performers into predictable archetypes (e.g., the villain, the quirky best friend, the stern detective, the romantic ingénue). For dedicated actors trained in versatile dramatic systems like Stanislavski's, typecasting represents a creative trap that restricts artistic growth and flattens multifaceted human psychology into commercial stereotypes.
  • Social Categorization & Stereotyping (Sociological Sense): The cognitive or social practice of rigidly categorizing individuals based on superficial traits, background, gender, or past behavior, thereby denying them opportunities outside of that predetermined box (e.g., typecasting someone as an "introverted techie" or "bureaucratic administrator").
  • Professional & Corporate Pigeonholing (Workplace Register): Restricting an employee’s career trajectory by assigning them exclusively to one narrow specialization or project type because they excelled at it in the past, preventing their advancement into broader leadership.
  • Literary & Narrative Characterization (Literary Register): The authorial technique or flaw of populating a narrative with flat, stock character types who lack psychological nuance or development.
  • Industrial & Printing Metaphor (Historical Origin): Derived from two distinct technological processes: movable lead printing type (Greek typos, "impression, model") and the foundry process of casting molten metal into fixed molds. In early 20th-century printing and manufacturing, "type-casting" was the literal machine casting of metallic typographic characters.

Semantic Reasoning & Historical Bridges: The synthesis of type and casting combines two rich conceptual metaphors. "Type" (from Greek typos, meaning a strike, mark, or archetype) refers to a general model or class of person. "Cast" (from Old Norse kasta, "to throw"), which evolved into theatrical jargon for throwing/distributing dramatic parts to actors. When early 20th-century Hollywood studio systems industrialized film production, actors were treated analogously to movable type in a printing press: standardized components inserted into predetermined story formulas. Thus, "type-casting" bridged industrial mass-production with the aesthetic categorization of performers.
