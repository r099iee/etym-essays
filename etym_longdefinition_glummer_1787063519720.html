Glummer is the comparative form of the adjective glum — it means “more gloomy, more dejected, more sullen, more morose”. Like “sadder” or “gloomier”, it describes a state of low spirits, but with its own texture: glumness is a quiet, withdrawn, downcast heaviness, a face that has gone still and shadowed rather than a loud or weeping sadness. In Tolkien’s sentence — “and glummer and glummer they became” — the repeated comparative traces the dwarves’ spirits sinking further and further as the days of waiting wear on.

Core meaning

  • More gloomy or dejected: a state of sadness that shows in a subdued, silent manner — “he looked even glummer than before”.
  • More sullen or morose: a touchiness and withdrawn ill-humour, the mood of someone who will not be cheered — “their faces grew glummer”.
  • More downcast in appearance: glumness is strongly visible — it lives in the face, shoulders, and silence; “glummer” marks an increase in that visible gloom.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Of circumstances, not persons: places and atmospheres can also be glum — a glum sky, a glum little room — so “glummer” can describe a scene grown more cheerless.
  • Reduplicative emphasis — “glummer and glummer”: the pattern “comparative and comparative” (glummer and glummer, colder and colder) expresses progressive, ongoing change; Tolkien uses it to show spirits sinking day by day, without a single dramatic break.
  • Informal register: glum and glummer are everyday, mildly informal words; they suggest dreariness and disappointment rather than deep grief or clinical depression.

Semantic boundary

  • Glumness is visible and silent: a glum person is not angry, tearful, or anxious — they are shut down, grey, unresponsive.
  • Compared with sad, glum is shallower and more about mood and manner than about genuine sorrow; compared with morose, glum is less bitter and less hostile.
  • The comparative glummer is therefore a precise tool for measuring the slow worsening of a mood that is already flat and quiet.

Morphology

  • Regular comparative of glum: the final -m- doubles before -er (glum → glummer, as in dim → dimmer), and the superlative is glummest.
  • The doubled consonant protects the short vowel, keeping the RP pronunciation /ˈɡlʌm.ə/ — two short syllables, the mouth pressed shut on the doubled m, exactly the face of a glum person.
  • The comparative allows the famous doubling pattern “glummer and glummer”, the English idiom for a mood sinking steadily and continuously deeper.

Usage and context

  • Occurs freely in predicative and attributive positions (“they looked glummer”; “a glummer silence”).
  • In Tolkien the word is chosen for its monosyllabic plainness — the dwarves’ gloom is simple, physical, and heavy, matched by the short blunt word and its doubled m.
  • Modern speakers use it lightly: a glummer colleague, a glummer forecast, a glummer Monday — always a notch of gloom added to what was already cheerless.
