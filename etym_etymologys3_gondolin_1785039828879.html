The name GONDOLIN is not a descendant of any real-world language, and its etymology is therefore a very different kind of story: it is a philological invention of J. R. R. Tolkien, a professional medievalist who constructed the Elvish tongues with genuine historical method and a scholar's love of sound change. Where most words are examined by tracing them backward through attested languages, Gondolin must be examined forward, through the working notes of its author — and its 'etymology' is the internal history Tolkien gave it within his invented linguistic world. The base of the name is the Common Eldarin root *GONOD-, 'stone,' reflected in Primitive Quendian as *gondō, a great block of rock. From this root the two great Elvish dialects each shaped their word for stone: Quenya, the ancient High-elven language of the Noldor, produced ondo; Sindarin, the Grey-elven speech of the Elves of Beleriand, produced gond. The city was first named in Quenya: Ondolindë, 'Rock of the Music of Water,' built from ondo ('stone') and lindë ('song, music') — a reference to the great fountain that stood before Turgon's tower and filled the hidden valley with sound. The name is poetic and allusive, celebrating the city's two defining features: the rock that hid it and the water that sang in it. When the name passed into Sindarin, Tolkien re-analysed it with a philologist's instinct for folk-etymology. He derived Gondolin from gond plus dolen, the past participle of the verb dol-, 'to hide,' from the root *DUL-, 'to conceal' — and the result, Gondolin, 'the Hidden Rock,' was far more transparently evocative of the city's essence than the Quenya original. The choice is revealing of Tolkien's method: he did not merely translate a name; he re-created it in the new language so that its meaning would glow through it, exactly as real place-names are reshaped by the speakers who use them. This is internal (or 'deep') etymology, the reconstruction of a language's prehistory from the evidence of its attested forms — the same discipline Tolkien practised as a professor of Anglo-Saxon, applied to languages he had invented himself. The same root proved productive across the legendarium. Gondor, the 'Stone-land' of the Third Age, is a parallel formation from gond and dor, 'land,' and the root surfaces in many other names: the Gondothlim, 'Stone-people,' the folk of Gondolin; the mountain rock of the city's site; and scores of compounds in the Elvish lexicons. Tolkien's treatment of the name in his later writings shows him refining the internal history — in some versions the city's hiddenness was central, in others its craft, and the name's transparency let him keep both senses alive at once. The word Gondolin thus behaves exactly like a real place-name that has been 'etymologized' by its own speakers: a name whose meaning, once explained, illuminates the place it names. In the passage from The Hobbit, Elrond's invocation of Gondolin does triple duty. It authenticates the swords as High Elven work — the blades are products of the greatest smiths of the First Age. It announces the city's ancient fall 'to dragons and goblins,' flooding the scene with the melancholy grandeur of the Elder Days. And it binds the quest to the larger mythology: the swords of Gondolin are relics of a world already lost, and their journey with the Company is part of a longer doom. A philologist's name, in short, does the work of epic elegy. The etymology of Gondolin is not a chain of attested sound-changes but a deliberate act of invention by a man who loved language so much that he made languages of his own — and the result, 'Hidden Rock,' has carried the weight of a ruined city ever since.
