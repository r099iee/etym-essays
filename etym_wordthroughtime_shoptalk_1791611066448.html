- Mid-19th c. (c. 1850s) – The verbal phrase to talk shop arose in British Victorian society as a social solecism; Victorian etiquette manuals reprimanded gentlemen and clerks for bringing commercial workshop or retail topics into refined drawing rooms.
- Late 19th c. (c. 1880s) – American English nominalized the verbal idiom into the compound noun shoptalk (or shop-talk); used in journalism and industry to describe the informal vocational chatter of telegraphers, railroad engineers, and machinists.
- Early 20th c. – Broadened into the professions (medicine, law, journalism, theatre); viewed ambivalently as both a social nuisance at dinner parties and an unavoidable bonding ritual among ambitious professionals.
- Mid-20th c. – Embraced by artists, writers, and theoretical psychologists (e.g., Rudolf Arnheim, and Philip Roth's celebrated book of dialogues Shop Talk); revalued as the essential, down-to-earth dialogue of makers sharing techniques, freed from academic pretension.
- 21st c. – Pervasive across digital technology, software development, science podcasts, and specialized online communities (e.g., developer forums, Slack channels); recognized as a primary engine of creative innovation and collaborative learning.
