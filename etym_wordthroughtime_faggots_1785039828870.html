- 14th century - 'faggot' enters English from Old French 'fagot' (a bundle of sticks), a workaday word of the pre-industrial hearth: the bound bundle of twigs and brushwood that fuels oven, forge, and winter fire.
- 15th-18th century - the word is everywhere in domestic and military life — kindling for the hearth, brushwood for bonfires — and 'faggot' is the standard unit of gathered fuel.
- 19th century - the fuel sense begins to recede as coal replaces wood, and a new homonym branches off: the British butcher's dish of 'faggots' (spiced balls of offal, c.1851) claims the same sound.
- 20th-21st century - the firewood sense is now archaic and rural, surviving in historical and literary settings (as in Tolkien's song of the reeking faggots); the word's other, later senses are entirely separate homonyms and never part of the old hearth-word's meaning.
