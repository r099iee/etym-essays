A mishap is an unfortunate accident, a piece of bad luck, or a minor error of circumstances that causes harm, damage, inconvenience, or embarrassment — but typically something small, brief, and recoverable rather than a catastrophe. The word is built transparently from the prefix mis- (“bad, wrong”) and the noun hap (“chance, luck”), so its literal sense is “bad luck, an ill-chance”.

Primary sense (minor unlucky accident)

  • An unexpected, usually small, adverse event: a sprained ankle on a walk, a dropped dish, a burst pipe, a delayed train.
  • Implies misfortune but not usually fault or blame: a mishap can happen to the careful as easily as to the careless, though a mildly bumbling overtone is common.
  • Typically singular and brief; one speaks of “a mishap” or “mishaps” in the plural, less often of a single great mishap (that would be a disaster).

Secondary and extended senses

  • Military and formal understatement: “training mishap”, “an unfortunate mishap of war” — institutional language uses mishap as a mild, non-accusatory label for accidents, avoiding the legal weight of “accident” or “incident”.
  • Comic and affectionate usage: “without mishap” is a stock reassuring phrase (“the journey passed without mishap”), and mishap is a favourite of light prose and domestic comedy for small domestic calamities — burnt dinners, tangled ropes, torn hems.
  • Legal/formal register: “personal injury by mishap” appears in older legal and insurance language as a general term for accidental harm.
  • Euphemistic shading: because it understates, mishap can be used euphemistically to soften bad news (an “unfortunate mishap” for something graver), though for truly serious events it is felt as flippant.

Semantic boundaries and register

  • Mishap sits below accident in gravity and above slip-up in formality; it is the word for “a small thing went wrong” with a hint of luck’s involvement.
  • Unlike misfortune, it does not name lasting adversity or an abstract state of bad luck; it names a single concrete event.
  • Unlike disaster or catastrophe, it has no scope, public dimension, or ruinous scale.
  • Unlike blunder or mistake, it need not involve human error at all — a branch falling in a gale is a mishap, not a mistake.
  • Register: standard; slightly literary or formal in “without mishap”; fully natural in informal speech.

Word-building and history in one glance

  • Hap (chance, luck) is the root of happen, happy, hapless, perhaps; mishap is its mis- compound. The 16th-century coinage inherited the older, heavier sense of “bad fortune” and has lightened over time.
  • The compound is fully lexicalised: speakers no longer analyse it as “mis + hap” the way they analyse “unlucky”, and stress falls on the first syllable: /ˈmɪshæp/.

Common pitfalls

  • Not to be confused with the spelling “misshap”; there is no such form.
  • In the source context, “without mishap” means “without any accident going wrong” — the group reached the little bay safe and sound.
