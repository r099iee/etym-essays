Jarred is a comparatively young word, and its entire history is visible in English. Unlike cranny or brood, it has no Old English or Indo-European pedigree that scholarship can trace; the verb jar first appears in the 1520s with a purely sensory meaning — to make a harsh, grating, discordant sound. Most etymologists treat it as imitative, or echoic: the word mimics the very noise it describes, a rough scraping or grinding that sets the teeth on edge. There is no attested ancestor in Old English, Old French, or Latin, and any attempt to reconstruct an Indo-European source would be pure guesswork; the honest verdict is that the word was coined, in effect, from the sound itself.

From sound to shock (1500s–1600s)

  • In the 1520s “to jar” meant to produce a harsh, grating noise — the clank and scrape of metal on metal, or a voice that rasps.
  • By the 1650s the sound-word had bred a motion-word: to jar something is to shake or set it vibrating by a hard impact. The logic is physical — a violent blow both shakes the object and makes the harsh noise.
  • By the later 1600s the transitive jolt turned figurative: jarring opinions, jarring tempers — things that “jar together” are in conflict; they clash.

The clashing century (1700s–1800s)

  • In the 18th century “to jar” commonly meant to quarrel or be at odds; the “jarring” of nations and parties was a standard metaphor for discord.
  • The 19th century shifted the weight to aesthetics: a “jarring note” breaks the harmony of music or prose; “jarring colours” clash to the eye. Criticism and design writing made the word theirs.
  • Throughout, the physical sense stayed alive in everyday speech — a blow that jars the arm, a cart that jars over rough stones — and this bodily sense is exactly what survives in Tolkien.

The word in Tolkien

  • In The Hobbit (1937), the dwarves strike the stone with their picks: “the handles splintered and jarred their arms cruelly”. Here jarred is fully physical — the shock of steel on unyielding rock runs up the wooden handle into the bone.
  • Tolkien’s sentence shows the transitive use at its most brutal: the blow does not merely rattle; it hurts. “Cruelly” makes explicit the pain that the jarring carries.
  • Notice also the sound-echo: splintering wood and jarred arms are part of one harsh, grating event — the word unites the two original senses of noise and shock.

Modern double life

  • Today “jarred” does double duty: the bodily jolt (“the fall jarred his spine”) and the sensory-emotional clash (“the news jarred her composure”, “the building jarred with the square”).
  • The two senses are one image: harsh, sudden, unpleasant contact — whether between steel and stone, a colour and its background, or a fact and a settled mood.
  • The word remains vivid and onomatopoeic; a “jarring” experience is one you can almost hear and feel.

False friend: the container

  • The noun jar (“a glass container”) is an entirely different word, from French jarre, ultimately from Arabic jarrah — a large earthen vessel. The two words share a spelling but not a drop of common history.
  • This homonymy is a favourite trap for students: the verb jarred (jolted or clashed) has nothing to do with jars you store jam in.
