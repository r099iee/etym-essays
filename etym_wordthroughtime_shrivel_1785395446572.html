- [Late medieval origin - to contract (15th c.)] - 'Shrivel' first appeared in dialectal English meaning to draw or contract into wrinkles; it described the physical puckering of skin and drying fruit, a humble, bodily word.
- [Loss of moisture (16th-17th c.)] - The core sense solidified: to wrinkle and shrink by heat or dryness — leaves, roots, and skin drying, creasing, and hardening; the register stayed naturalistic and plant-bound.
- [Figurative loss of vitality (18th-19th c.)] - Extended from plants to persons and powers: hopes, courage, and energies 'shrivelled', giving the word an emotive sense of wasting, diminishment, and pitiable collapse.
- [Modern apocalyptic register] - Today 'shrivel' is the violent, permanent partner of 'scorch': a quick, damaging contortion into hard creased folds, often invoked for destruction; it is sharply contrasted with reversible words like 'wilt' or 'shrink'.
