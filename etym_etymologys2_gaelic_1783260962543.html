GAELIC descends from an Insular Celtic ethnic name whose deepest ancestry is genuinely uncertain. Linear chain as far as the attested record allows: Stage 1 — Common Brittonic (British Celtic) *weidelos "wild, forest-dweller, raider" (reconstructed; late 1st millennium BCE / early 1st millennium CE; built on a Brittonic adjective for "wild, wooded," cf. Welsh gŵydd "wild, uncultivated"). DEEP ANCESTRY UNCERTAIN: possibly touching the Indo-European word for "wood/forest/wild" (cf. English wood) but not securely reconstructable. Stage 2 — Old Welsh Guoidel / Guoydel "an Irishman; a wild one, raider" (attested from the 9th century; later Welsh Gwyddel). This is an exonym coined by the Welsh for the seaborne Irish raiders. Stage 3 — Old Irish Goídel "an Irishman, a Gael" (borrowed from the Welsh form in the early medieval period; attested from the 9th century onward). The Irish reborrowed their own self-name from their Welsh neighbours. Stage 4 — Middle Irish / Classical Gaelic Goídel → Scottish Gaelic Gàidheal (noun, "a Gael") and Gàidhlig (the language, literally "of the Gaels") (Goidelic branch of Insular Celtic; medieval period onward). Stage 5 — English GAELIC, formed in the 17th century from the ethnic name Gael + the adjective suffix -ic, as the name of the Celtic speech of Ireland and the Scottish Highlands. FINAL NODE. DEBATE / NUANCE NOTES: - The pre-Celtic ancestry of *weidelos is genuinely uncertain. The old popular theory deriving the name from PIE *welh₁- "to want, choose" ("the chosen people") is now generally rejected, since the phonological and semantic bridges are weak and the documented history of the word is Insular Celtic. - A more sober reconstruction favours "wild, wooded," possibly touching the Indo-European word for wood/forest seen in English wood — but even this is admitted to be speculative, and responsible dictionaries mark the ultimate root as uncertain. - The Welsh–Irish loan direction (Welsh exonym → Irish endonym) is secure; only the stage before Brittonic is uncertain.
