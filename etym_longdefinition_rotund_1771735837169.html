ROTUND, adjective. In the Gibbon passage the word describes the engraving of Gibbon's face made by Chapman in 1807: "And the engraving of Gibbon's rotund face... hangs on the wall of my study." The historian was famously stout, and rotund — meaning round, plump, full-figured — is the gently humorous, affectionate word for his plumpness. The adjective carries neither the clinical neutrality of "overweight" nor the harshness of "fat"; it pictures a pleasing, dignified roundness, and it suits the portrait of a well-fed, contented eighteenth-century man of letters. The core senses of ROTUND:
  • Round and plump, especially of a person's body or face. The primary modern sense: a rotund figure, a rotund belly, a rotund little man. The word describes an even, full roundness of outline, without the angularity of thinness.
  • Round, circular, or spherical in shape (of objects). The literal sense: a rotund arch, rotund pillars, a rotund dome. In architecture a rotunda is a round building or hall, typically with a dome.
  • Full and sonorous (of voice or speech); grandiloquent. By extension from fullness of shape to fullness of sound: a rotund voice, rotund eloquence, rotund periods of prose — speech that is rounded, full-bodied, or pomposely grand.
Secondary and extended senses, with historical bridges:
  • Corpulent and portly. Rotund is the polite, literary synonym for fat, preferred when the writer wishes to be kind or amused: the rotund innkeeper, the rotund bishop. It stresses the roundness of the outline rather than the weight itself.
  • Full in style or manner. A "rotund" manner of speech or writing is full, flowing, and rounded in cadence, sometimes with a hint of self-importance. The bridge is the shared idea of fullness: a round body, a full round sentence.
  • Rotundity (noun) and rotundness (noun). The quality of being rotund, used of persons (plumpness) or shapes (roundness). Rotundity was the first-formed noun, from the 17th century.
The historical bridge that explains the polysemy: rotund is a direct Latin loanword, from Latin rotundus, "wheel-shaped, round," formed on rota, "wheel" (the same root as rotate, rotation, rotary, and rotor). The word entered English in the early 18th century, borrowed from Latin rather than descended through French — which is why English also has the older French-derived word round (from Old French reont/rotond, ultimately the same Latin rotundus). The meanings all radiate from the wheel: a round shape, a round body, and, by a natural extension from shape to sound, a full-bodied voice. In the 20th century the word became a stock, good-humored adjective for plumpness in journalism and fiction, keeping its classical dignity. Usage and collocations:
  • Common collocations: rotund face, rotund figure, rotund little man, rotund belly, rotund cheeks, rotund voice, rotund prose.
  • Register: formal to literary; affectionate or amused rather than clinical; never medical.
  • Family words: rotund (adj.), rotunda (a round building, from Italian rotonda, feminine of rotondo, itself from Latin rotundus), rotundity (n.), rotundness (n.), orotund ("round-mouthed," grandiloquent, from Latin os "mouth" + rotundus).
  • Comparison: rotund is kinder than obese and more formal than plump; portly suggests dignity and office, rotund suggests simple roundness of shape.
