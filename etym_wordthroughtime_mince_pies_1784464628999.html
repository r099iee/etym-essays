The original “minced pies” of medieval England were savoury — the “mincemeat” was literally finely chopped meat, suet, and spice, served as a rich Christmas and feast-day dish.
“Mince-pie” in the 18th–19th centuries drifted sweet: chopped dried fruits, candied peel, and suet replaced the meat, so the pie became a festive rather than a savoury dish while keeping its old name.
“Mince-pie” in Victorian England was fixed as a Christmas institution, served with cheese or cream at hearth-side gatherings and freighted with cosy, old-fashioned festivity.
“Mince-pie” today remains a small spiced sweet pastry of the British winter, its name still echoing the meat it no longer contains — a fossil of a taste that vanished two centuries ago.
