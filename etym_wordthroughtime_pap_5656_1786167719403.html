- 15th century – 'pap' appears in English as a nursery word (from Latin pappa, a child's word for food) meaning soft semi-liquid food for infants, invalids, or the toothless; its phonetics — two bilabial stops framing a low vowel, /pæp/ — mimic the smacking of a feeding mouth.
- 16th–17th centuries – the nursery sense broadens: 'pap' is the standard weaning food of infancy, fed by spoon or pap-boat, while the plural 'paps' also names the breast, keeping the word tied to nursing and dependence.
- 18th century – 'pap' becomes a small institution of infant care (pap-feeding, pap-boats), read as nourishing and motherly; the word is wholly gentle, the food of helplessness lovingly ministered to.
- 19th century – the great figurative shift begins: 'pap' comes to mean anything soft, undemanding, and intellectually infantile — trivial journalism, sentimental entertainment, 'pap for the masses' — the food that nourishes is now the diet that stunts.
- 20th–21st centuries – the literal food sense recedes (modern 'baby food' has replaced it), and the dominant meaning is the contemptuous one: bland, trivial, intellectually undemanding material, the same slide 'pablum' would repeat two centuries later.
