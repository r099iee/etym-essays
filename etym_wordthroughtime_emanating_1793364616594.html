- Classical Antiquity — Springs and public rumors: Latin ēmānāre describes water trickling forth from a hillside fountainhead or wellspring; Roman writers like Cicero quickly adapt it metaphorically for news, rumors, and virtues spreading outward through a city.
- Late Antiquity — Neoplatonic metaphysics: Pagan and Christian philosophers adopt emanatio to translate Greek metaphysical concepts of divine overflow, describing all creation as an eternal, undiminished outpouring from the transcendent Godhead.
- 17th c. — Early Modern English participial and nominal forms: The adjectival form emanant enters English philosophical discourse (c. 1620s) to describe acts producing outward effects, while emanation becomes a major term in theology and the corpuscular optics of Isaac Newton.
- Late 18th c. — Emergence of the modern verb: English grammarians and literary essayists back-form the verb emanate and participle emanating (c. 1780s), establishing a lyrical vocabulary for physical scents, radiant heat, and spiritual qualities pouring from their source.
- 19th c. — Romantic poetry and physical sciences: Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley) celebrate mystical light and beauty emanating from nature, while physicists describe thermal and luminous rays emanating from heated bodies.
- Today — Everyday sensory, psychological, and legal standard: Emanating is universally used to describe delicious aromas, radiating acoustic vibrations, charismatic presence, and constitutional powers flowing outward from their fountainhead.
