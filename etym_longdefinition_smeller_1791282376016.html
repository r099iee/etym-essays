Smeller in modern standard use has two main lives: literally one who smells or the nose with which one smells, and in older colloquial idiom a heavy fall or blow, especially as in to come a smeller meaning to come a cropper. The passage under review uses the second, idiomatic sense: after the brain skids, it comes a smeller in the ditch, that is, it takes a tumble and ends ignominiously. The tone is comic, dated slang, deliberately low after a lofty metaphor.

The literal sense divides further. A smeller can be a person who tests by smell, such as a professional smeller of tea, coffee or perfume. More commonly in slang it is the nose itself: to get one on the smeller means to be punched on the nose. The figurative crash sense grows out of that bodily sense: a blow that fells becomes any fall or failure.

Polysemy and secondary meanings today include:

  • Nose: colloquial and slightly dated. Example: a blow on the smeller. The bridge is agent-to-organ: the thing that smells stands for the nose.
  • Person who smells: occupational or general. Example: keen smellers detect gas leaks quickly. This is the transparent agent-noun sense from smell plus -er.
  • Heavy fall or crash: to come, take or get a smeller. Now old-fashioned comic idiom for a pratfall or business collapse. The bridge is metonymy from a stunning blow on the nose to any felling blow of fate.
  • Punch or blow: to fetch someone a smeller. Closely related to the fall sense but focused on the blow rather than the tumble, common in boxing slang.
  • Bouquet or stink figuratively: marginal uses for a nosegay or, ironically, a stinker. These play on smell as odour rather than act.
  • Detector device jocular: modern playful use for an electronic nose or sensor. A technological metaphor extending the organ sense.

Usage notes matter. The fall idiom requires the indefinite article: come a smeller, come a cropper, come a purler. It is intransitive and perfective, describing a completed tumble. The nose sense is countable: smellers plural for nostrils or noses in boxing reports. Register is informal, working-class slang elevated into upper-class comic writing for bathos. In contemporary English both slang senses are recessive, preserved in Wodehouse, period fiction and historical slang dictionaries, while the literal professional sense persists in perfumery and food science.

For revision, anchor the two poles: organ versus impact. If a sentence mentions nose, punch or sniffing, smeller is literal; if it follows come, take or get with a, it almost certainly means a fall. Remember the article is obligatory in the idiom, and the humour comes from anticlimax, dropping a grand metaphor of speeding brains and failed steering into a thump in a ditch. The form is regular, the idiom is fossilised, and recognising the article-plus-noun pattern unlocks the joke instantly. Keep nose versus fall apart and meaning stays clear.
