The evolution of “oblivion”: smoothing over the world. Oblivion enters English through a chain of borrowings: Latin oblivio, oblivionis — used by Cicero and the Augustan poets for “forgetfulness” — passed into Old French as oblivion, and Middle English took it up in the 14th century, at first purely in the sense of “loss of memory, the condition of having forgotten.” Behind the noun stands the Latin deponent verb oblivisci, “to forget,” whose formation has exercised etymologists for two centuries.
  • The debated Latin derivation. The traditional analysis — still the one most often repeated — decomposes oblivisci as ob- (“over, against”) plus a verbal stem *lev-/liv- related to levis (“smooth”): to forget is literally “to smooth over,” to rub a written record level until nothing remains legible, exactly as a Roman would erase a wax tablet by scraping its surface flat. On this view oblivion is the state of a perfectly smoothed surface, a blank where writing once stood. Modern scholarship is less confident: no verbal root of the required shape is securely attested elsewhere in Italic or Indo-European, the levis connection rests largely on this single word, and rival proposals — a link to the root *leibh- “to pour” (forgetting as dousing and covering) or to *leu- “to loosen” — have won no general assent. The honest verdict is that oblivisci remains etymologically obscure; the chain must therefore be read with that uncertainty in mind.
  • The wider root family. What is solid is the reconstructed PIE root *lei- (with the so-called s-mobile, *slei-), “slippery, slimy, smooth” — a prolific cluster whose descendants include Latin limus (“slime, mud”), līma (“a file,” the instrument that makes things smooth), and linere (“to smear, daub”), whence English liniment (a smear-ointment); English lime (both the sticky birdlime and the cement-like material, both named for adhesive slime); slime (Old English slīm); slide (Old English slīdan); slip; and, through Latin lubricus, lubricate. Sanskrit līyate, “to cling to, settle into, hide, dissolve,” with its participle līna (“absorbed, hidden, dissolved in”), is often added to the family — the semantic drift from sticky to hidden to forgotten is elegant and widely quoted, though its exact membership remains debated.
  • English history (14th–17th centuries). The English word begins as a formal, literate term of the clergy and chroniclers: “the oblivion of sin,” the forgetting of benefits and injuries alike. The 16th–17th centuries gave it institutional weight through “Acts of Oblivion” — statutes of amnesty, most famously the English Indemnity and Oblivion Act of 1660, by which the restored monarchy pardoned former enemies and decreed that their offences be remembered no more. Here the passive “state of being forgotten” becomes an act of official state erasure, a forgetting with legal force.
  • Romantic and philosophical deepening (18th–19th centuries). The Romantics and the philosophers made oblivion metaphysical. Poets speak of “the abyss of oblivion,” death as a state of being as if never known; the word names the grand, silent dark in which whole civilisations and all the dead repose. It is this register — vast, final, and strangely serene — that gives the word its modern gravity, and that Vance exploits when a wizard forces a spell back into oblivion: not a cupboard, but a void of non-remembering.
  • The modern word (20th–21st centuries). Contemporary usage domesticates the grand sense: things “fade into oblivion” through public and cultural neglect, and the word is equally at home in fantasy (an Elder Scrolls plane called Oblivion), in science fiction, and in the slang of destruction (“blasted into oblivion”). Meanwhile the adjective oblivious has drifted from “forgetful” to “unaware, heedless,” losing the sublime sense entirely, and the verb obliviate (“to cause to forget”) was revived for the fictional memory-erasing charm of the Harry Potter books — a neat return to the word’s Latin roots.
From a wax tablet scraped smooth, to the erasure of a dynasty’s crimes, to the void into which spells and ruins fall, the career of oblivion is the career of forgetting itself — a word whose ancient root may still mean “slippery,” and whose modern use slides from memory to nothingness without ever quite losing the smoothness of its origin.
