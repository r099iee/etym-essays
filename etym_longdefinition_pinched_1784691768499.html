Pinched is the past tense and past participle of the verb pinch, whose primary sense is to squeeze or grip something tightly between the thumb and finger, or between two surfaces, so as to nip or compress it. From this physical core the verb has sprouted a rich cluster of figurative senses: to be pinched with cold is to be painfully nipped by frost; a pinched face is thin and drawn, as if squeezed by want or anxiety; to be pinched for money is to be short of funds (the origin of the penny-wise phrase pinch-penny); and a pinch of salt is as much as can be held between the fingertips. But the sense at work in the passage from The Hobbit is the slang use: to pinch something is to steal it, to lift it furtively and quickly, in the manner of a pickpocket. In the narrator's comic hypothetical, a really first-class and legendary burglar — unlike poor Bilbo — would have picked the trolls' pockets, pinched the very mutton off the spits, purloined the beer, and walked off undetected.

The word is carefully chosen. Pinched belongs to the demotic end of the vocabulary of theft: it suggests a quick, deft, manual snatch, the work of nimble fingers rather than of crowbars or cold-blooded planning. Tolkien sets it in a deliberately escalating trio — picked, pinched, purloined — that moves from plain to slang to grandly literary, a small lesson in the registers of the English verb "to steal" fitted into one comic sentence.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The verb and its participle fan out in several directions, and each sense has a visible bridge from the physical squeeze:

  • Literal (primary): to squeeze or nip tightly between thumb and finger or between two surfaces — "she pinched his cheek"; "the door pinched his finger"; "pinched off" = removed by squeezing.
  • Of cold, want, or distress: "pinched with cold", "pinched with hunger" — nipped and pained; a "pinched face" is thin, drawn, gaunt, as if squeezed hollow by privation or anxiety; "pinched features" in fiction signal suffering.
  • Of money: "pinched for money" = short of funds; a "pinch-penny" is a miser, the penny pinched so hard it cannot escape; the noun "the pinch" = the painful pressure of want ("the pinch of poverty").
  • Small measure (noun): "a pinch of salt" — as much as the thumb and finger can hold; the classic kitchen measure, transferred by metonymy from the gesture of squeezing.
  • Slang: to steal (the Hobbit sense): "to pinch" = to pilfer, lift, steal furtively, especially by hand; attested in English slang from the 17th century and still very much alive — "someone pinched my bike".
  • Police slang: to arrest: from the theft sense, "to pinch" and "the pinch" came to mean an arrest and the act of being arrested ("he got pinched"), recorded from the 19th century — the hand of the law taking the thief, a second metaphor of seizing.
  • Idioms: "in a pinch" = in an emergency, if necessary; "when it comes to the pinch" = at the decisive moment; "to feel the pinch" = to suffer financially. Each re-reads the squeeze as pressure or difficulty.
  • Modern technical jargon: "pinch zoom" (the touchscreen gesture of pinching two fingers together to zoom out), "pinch point" (engineering: a place where a body part can be caught between moving parts; also a constriction in a market, pipe, or process), "pinch-hit" (baseball: to bat in place of another player) — the same hand-gesture core transplanted into new technical habitats.
  • Of smell or taste (transferred): "a pinch" can describe a sharp, nipping pungency, and the small amount that "adds a pinch of flavour" — the ghost of the hand-gesture remains even in the metaphor.

Historical bridge. The semantic map is a small wheel with the squeeze at the hub: squeeze → pain (cold, want) → constriction of money → small amount (a pinch) → the quick seizure of theft → the seizing of an arrest → by abstraction, any pressure or difficulty (the pinch). The Hobbit sense is the theft spoke, chosen for its quickness and dexterity — a "pinch" is a snatch, the opposite of a planned heist, which is precisely why it sits so well in a sentence about a burglar who is also a hobbit.
