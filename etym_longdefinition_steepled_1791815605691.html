steepled is a denominal adjective: the noun steeple plus the past-participial suffix -ed, formed on the model of towered, spired, domed, walled and thatched. Its literal meaning is “furnished with a steeple or steeples; surmounted by a steeple.” It is a descriptive, chiefly literary word — the adjective a travel writer or historian reaches for when a town is to be characterised by its skyline rather than its streets — and it is almost always applied to places, buildings, or (in a modern gesture sense) to hands.

In the source passage the adjective carries its central topographical sense. “Green field and steepled city” sets open country against built town and picks out the one feature visible from a distance: the church towers lifting above the roofs. The sentence belongs to Carlyle’s The French Revolution (1837), and its effect depends on the contrast between the eternal and the precarious — sun, evening and fields continuing while a city whose steeples point to heaven goes about its business “as if no Louis lay in danger.” Nothing in the word is doctrinally religious; the steeple is a landmark and a silhouette, and the adjective turns architecture into scenery.

Primary senses

  • Of a church or other building: having a steeple. “A steepled church”, “the steepled chapel above the harbour”. This is the oldest and least marked use; the adjective simply records a structural fact.
  • Of a town, village, city or skyline: distinguished or crowded with steeples. “A steepled market town”, “the steepled silhouette of the old quarter”. Here the word is collective and pictorial: it describes not one tower but a profile, and it implies age, parish organisation and a pre-industrial scale.
  • Of a view, horizon or landscape: punctuated by steeples. A development of the same sense, common in nineteenth-century descriptive prose and still natural in modern writing about skylines.

Extended, figurative and specialist senses

  • Of hands or fingers: pressed palm to palm with the fingertips pointing upward, so that the joined hands resemble a steeple. “He sat with steepled fingers”, “steepled hands”. This is a genuine twentieth-century extension, characteristic of descriptions of clerics, diplomats and calculating men; it carries a faint suggestion of composed, even complacent, deliberation.
  • Of flame, smoke, rock or any rising form: tapering upward like a steeple. “Steepled flames”, “a steepled pinnacle of rock”. Poetic and comparatively rare, but well formed by the same logic of resemblance.
  • The verbal source, to steeple — to furnish with a steeple, or to rise steeply like one. The participle steepling is commoner than the finite verb in modern use (“steepling cliffs”, “steepling spires”).
  • Not a synonym of steep. A persistent confusion: steepled says nothing about gradient. A “steepled roof” is a roof with a steeple, not a steeply pitched one; for the latter English has steep, pitched, raked or sloping. The two words share a Germanic base but are semantically distinct, and the slip is common in estate-agent and architectural copy.

Register, connotation and collocation

  • Register: formal to literary; rare in spontaneous speech, frequent in belles-lettres, local history, travel writing and obituary prose about old towns.
  • Connotation: generally warm, elegiac or picturesque — steeples connote parish, continuity, Christendom and the pre-modern skyline. In Carlyle the tone is cooler: the steeples are simply there, indifferent to the political crisis beneath them.
  • Typical collocations: steepled church, steepled town, steepled village, steepled city, steepled skyline, steepled roofs, steepled fingers, steepled hands.
  • Contrasts: flat-roofed, domed, minareted, steepleless, and the journalistic opposition of “steepled” to “glass-and-steel”.
  • Emotional intensity: low. It is a colour word rather than a judgment; any nostalgia comes from context, not from the lexeme itself.

Why the word matters in this reading

The passage is about the indifference of ordinary life to political catastrophe: the sun shines out, the evening fades, men ply their business. Steepled contributes the vertical dimension — the city pointing upward — and quietly invokes the institutional church whose prayers the next sentence will treat with irony. It also fixes the scene in a definite historical topography: an eighteenth-century French city where the steeple was still the tallest structure and the unit by which local time was told.
