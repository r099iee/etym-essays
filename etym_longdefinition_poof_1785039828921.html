**POOF — an interjection that is its own sound** At its plainest core, **poof** is an imitative, onomatopoeic exclamation — an interjection that reproduces the short, soft, yet slightly explosive sound of a sudden puff of breath, air, or smoke. The word is pure sound-symbolism: purse your lips, push a quick compressed burst of air past them, and the noise that leaves your mouth is already recognisably *poof*. The initial bilabial stop /p/ builds up a small pocket of pressure; the following rounded vowel releases it; and the final fricative /f/ lets it hiss away. The shape of the word IS the event it names, which is why poof has no grammar, no morphology, and no older meaning than the acoustic experience it encodes: it is an event-word, a single syllable that compresses the whole occurrence — the puff, the smoke, the vanishing — into the instant of utterance. **Primary sense: the exclamation of a sudden puff** As an interjection, poof records the sharp little report of a candle being blown out, a conjurer's trick, a puff of tobacco smoke, or any small, abrupt detonation of breath. It is deliberately light, sudden, and faintly comic — the sound of a flame extinguished rather than a bomb exploding. The word does not merely name the noise; it performs it. When a speaker says "poof!", the mouth enacts the very puff being described, and this performative quality is what makes the interjection so effective in narrative: it puts the reader physically in the middle of the event. **The literary sense in *The Hobbit*** In *The Hobbit*, when "the great fire went off **poof**! into a tower of blue glowing smoke, right up to the roof, that scattered piercing white sparks all among the goblins," the single syllable performs Gandalf's magic: it contains the entire burst of the spell — the instantaneous release of energy, the column of blue smoke rising to the cavern roof, and the shower of piercing white sparks scattering among the goblins. The exclamation is playful, theatrical, almost conjurer's patter, and it marks the sudden inversion of the goblins' power: one moment they gloated in firelight, the next they are plunged into darkness and chaos. Tolkien chooses poof precisely because it is light and antic — the wizard's overwhelming intervention is delivered with the same casual finality as a stage illusion, an understated magic that is all the more commanding for being quiet. **Polysemy and secondary modern meanings**
  • The interjection of sudden disappearance: from the 1910s, "poof!" becomes the standard word for vanishing in a puff of smoke — the verbal gesture of stage magic, cartoons, and comedy. "Poof! he was gone." This is the natural extension of the puff-of-smoke sense to the moment of disappearance itself.
  • The noun "a poof" (a puff of smoke or air): by conversion, poof can function as a noun for the small, sudden burst itself — "a poof of smoke," "a poof of dust" — especially in informal and children's writing, where the boundary between interjection and noun is fluid.
  • The magical/metaphoric sense: anything that vanishes instantly, is annihilated, or collapses into nothing can go "poof" — a fortune, a plan, a hope, a career. The semantic bridge is the stage-magic image of the puff of smoke that conceals an instantaneous disappearance.
  • The French parallel *pouf*: French independently coined its own imitative interjection, *pouf*, from the same acoustic image; English borrowed the related noun *pouffe* (1884) for an over-stuffed cushion or low seat — the cushion is the "puff" of soft filling — a parallel Romance formation, not an ancestor of the English interjection.
  • The unrelated slang noun *poof* (an effeminate man): a separate, later slang noun, c. 1850, probably derived from *puff* (the word being uttered dismissively), designating an effeminate man; in Australia extended as *poofter* (1910). This sense is entirely unrelated in meaning to the interjection and is now rightly avoided as a slur.
  • Sound-effect register: in comics, cartoons, and digital writing, poof serves as a one-word sound effect for magical teleportation, smoke bombs, and sudden disappearance — the interjection has become a fixed property of fantasy and comedy vocabulary.
**Historical bridge and semantic reasoning** The entire career of poof is a single act of onomatopoeia. Its ancestor is the imitative Germanic base *puf-, "a short, quick blast of breath or air," which produced Old English *pyffan* ("to blow with the mouth"), Middle English *puffen* and the noun *puf*/*puffe* (c. 1200), and the rich modern word *puff* — puff pastry, powder-puff, puffball, pufferfish, and the figurative senses "empty boast" (1560s) and "flattery" (1732). The English interjection *poof* is recorded in 1824 as "the sound of a puff of breath or air," and from that acoustic naming grew, by the 1910s, the sense of sudden, smoke-shrouded disappearance. No metaphor is required: the puff of smoke is the visible form of the puff of air, and the conjurer's vanishing is simply the puff's most theatrical consequence. **Register and usage** Poof is informal, playful, theatrical, and childlike. It belongs to dialogue, fantasy prose, comics, and magic shows rather than to formal or technical register. It is distinguished from *puff* (the noun and verb for the blast itself — poof is the sound-name for exactly that puff, used as an interjection); from *pop* (a sharper, harder, more metallic report); from *bang* (a loud explosive noise, far heavier than poof's soft explosion); and from *whoosh* (a heavier, more drawn-out rushing sound). Poof is deliberately light, sudden, and almost comical — the sound of a candle being blown out rather than a bomb.
