--- ROOT 1: GENEA (race, birth, generation) ---
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed c. 3500 BCE)
*ǵenh₁-
"to beget, produce, give birth to"
↓
Proto-Hellenic (reconstructed c. 2000 BCE)
*geneh-
"race, lineage, stock"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th–5th c. BCE)
γενεά (geneá)
"race, family, generation, descent"

--- ROOT 2: LOGOS (word, reckoning, study) ---
Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed c. 3500 BCE)
*leǵ-
"to collect, gather, speak, reckon"
↓
Proto-Hellenic (reconstructed c. 2000 BCE)
*legō
"to gather, choose, speak"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 8th–5th c. BCE)
λόγος (lógos)
"word, speech, computation, account"
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 5th c. BCE)
-λογία (-logía)
"discourse, study, branch of knowledge"

--- SYNTHESIS & DERIVATION ---
Ancient Greek (c. 5th c. BCE)
γενεαλογία (genealogía)
"tracing of a family lineage, pedigree"
↓
Hellenistic Greek (c. 2nd c. BCE)
γενεαλογικός (genealogikós)
"pertaining to genealogy or lineage"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th–6th c. CE)
genealogicus
"genealogical, of pedigrees"
↓
Middle French (c. 15th c.)
généalogique
"genealogical"
↓
Early Modern English (late 16th c., c. 1580s)
genealogical
"pertaining to family lineages and ancestry"

Note on morphology: The compound was established in Classical Greek as a nominal compound (geneá + -logía), which was subsequently derived into the adjective genealogikós by adding the productive adjectival suffix -ikos ("pertaining to"). In Early Modern English, this was anglicized by appending the Latinate adjectival formant -al (from Latin -alis), creating the doublet suffix -ic-al that distinguishes English formal scholarly adjectives.
