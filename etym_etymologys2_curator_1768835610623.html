curator — single linear chain: - Proto-Indo-European *kʷeys- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'to heed, to perceive, to take care' (per de Vaan; the Sanskrit kavi, 'seer', is sometimes compared, but the equation is uncertain and is noted here rather than asserted) - Proto-Italic *kʷoizā (c. 1000–500 BCE, reconstructed, per de Vaan) — 'care' - Latin cūra (3rd c. BCE–, attested) — 'care, concern, anxiety, charge' - Latin cūrāre (2nd c. BCE–, attested) — 'to take care of, to attend to, to see to' - Latin cūrātor (1st c. BCE–, attested) — 'one who has charge of a thing; an overseer of roads, finances, works, or persons under guardianship' - Old French curatour (c. 12th–13th c., attested) — 'guardian, overseer' (also curateur) - Middle English curatour (14th c., attested) — 'a legal guardian or overseer' - English curator (17th c., attested — the museum sense is established in this period) — 'the official in charge of a collection' - Modern English curator. Note: the medieval branch of the same Latin word produced cūrātus, 'entrusted with the care of souls', whence English curate and French curé — a religious sibling of the museum office. The digital 'content curator' of the 21st century is a further semantic extension of the same cūra, not a new root.
