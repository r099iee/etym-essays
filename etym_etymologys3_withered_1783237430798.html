The ancestry of withered runs back to one of the most primitive of all PIE notions — the act of blowing and breathing, *h₂weh₁-, a root so basic it generated separate words for wind in nearly every branch of the family: Latin ventus, Greek áēmi and aḗr, Sanskrit vāti and vāyu, and the Germanic wind (Old English wind, Gothic winds, German Wind). From this root, however, a second, more specialised derivative was formed by the suffix *-dʰro-, which builds state- or instrument-nouns: *h₂wé-dʰro- "the blowing state," i.e. "weather." This is the precise ancestor of the Germanic *wedra- and of Old English weder, whose meaning had widened from "wind, storm" to "weather, the condition of the sky" generally. The first great semantic fact of the word's history, then, is that the verb wither and the noun weather are not distant relatives but near twins — wither is, etymologically, "to weather."

The second great fact is the metonymic leap recorded in Middle English. In the early fourteenth century, alongside wederen "to expose to the weather" (to air out cloth, to season timber, to let the wind and sun work on something), a variant form widren, wideren came into use meaning "to become dry and shrivelled." The mechanism is transparent: what the weather does to a plant, a leaf, or a limb left out in the wind and sun is exactly to dry it, shrink it, and kill it. The word wither therefore literalises the weather — the process of being dried up by exposure — and from the fourteenth century onward (the early texts include the Cursor Mundi, c. 1300) it took on the full modern senses: intransitively, to shrivel and lose vitality; transitively, to cause to shrivel, to blast, to destroy the vigour of.

From this verbal core the participle withered became a fully adjectival word, and the figurative extension followed: what dries to nothing is not only a leaf but a hope, a love, an empire's strength. The fifteenth to seventeenth centuries saw the word move from the farm and the field into moral and religious prose: the soul withered by sin, the body withered by age or disease, the branch withered and fit only for the fire (in the biblical idiom of John 15). Shakespeare uses the adjective of age and infirmity — "this wither'd arm," "the sear, the yellow leaf" — and the preacher's tradition made "withered" a standard word for spiritual dryness, the state of being cut off from the water of life.

The bodily-political transfer. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word was fully available for the political metaphor of the body politic. If a state is an organism, its parts are limbs, and a "withered member" is a limb that has dried up and died while still hanging on the body — adding no strength, contributing nothing, and standing as a reproach. This is precisely Macaulay's figure for Ireland in the opening of the History of England: a member "adding no strength to the body politic, and reproachfully pointed at." The botanical desiccation image carries the whole moral weight: Ireland is not merely poor or weak but dried, atrophied, deadened from within, an embarrassment visibly attached to a great body. The word's etymology — to be dried by weather — underwrites the image of slow, environmental, irreversible loss rather than sudden injury.

Modern career and the withering double. In modern English the participle-adjective withered keeps the plant sense (withered leaves, withered crops), the bodily sense (a withered limb), and the figurative sense (a withered hope, a withered career, a withered institution), while the present participle withering developed an active, scorching force — "a withering glance," "withering contempt" — the power that shrivels whatever it touches. The pair thus splits the ancient metaphor: withered is the state of having been dried, withering the act of drying. Meanwhile the noun weather and the verb wither remain twins in the genetic record, two modern English words that once were one and the same PIE derivative, *h₂wé-dʰro- "the blowing state" — a reminder that the oldest meteorological word in our language is also, unexpectedly, a word for death by drying.
