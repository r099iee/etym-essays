Missionary most centrally means a person sent on a religious mission, especially a Christian envoy dispatched to preach, convert, provide education, medicine or humanitarian aid in a foreign or non-Christian area. By extension it means any zealous advocate or promoter of a cause, and as an adjective it describes anything relating to such missions. In the source quote a missionary — likely a Jesuit or Moravian among Native Americans — reports surprise at Indigenous gentleness exceeding that of civilized nations, a classic missionary-ethnographer trope combining admiration with evangelical purpose.

Full range of senses:

  • Christian envoy abroad (core noun): Ordained or lay person commissioned by a church or society to evangelize. Example: "Jesuit missionaries in New France," "a Mormon missionary in Brazil." Includes Catholic priests, Protestant ministers, Latter-day Saints elders, Jehovah's Witnesses. Often lives years abroad, learns language, runs schools/clinics.
  • Domestic / home missionary: One serving marginalized groups at home: inner-city, prisons, reservations. Example: "home missionary to the lumber camps." 19th-century Home Mission societies parallel Foreign Missions.
  • Missionary family / missionary kid (MK): Spouse/child of missionary, raised biculturally. Sociological term for third-culture kids with distinctive identity.
  • Figurative zealous advocate: Secular extension for passionate promoter: "a missionary for solar power," "missionary zeal." Retains connotations of fervour, self-sacrifice, conviction that others must be converted. Can be admiring or gently mocking.
  • Adjectival use: Missionary work, missionary society, missionary school, missionary zeal, missionary position. The last — face-to-face heterosexual intercourse — is slang of disputed origin (popularized by Kinsey, 1948, allegedly what missionaries taught converts), now standard but informal and potentially crude.
  • Historical-anthropological source: Missionaries as involuntary ethnographers: their letters, dictionaries and Relations are primary sources for Indigenous languages and customs, as in the quoted gentleness observation. Scholars use missionary accounts critically, aware of bias yet grateful for detail.

Polysemy bridges: religious sending → zealous sending for any cause is metaphorical bleaching; the fervour and travel remain, the theology drops. Adjectival uses follow naturally. The sexual sense bridges via supposed missionary instruction on proper posture — folk etymology but lexically fixed.

Connotation is contested: heroic selflessness for supporters; cultural imperialism, colonial complicity for critics. Post-colonial discourse often pairs missionary with merchant and soldier as agents of empire ("flag follows Bible"). Many modern organizations avoid the noun, preferring mission worker, missional partner. Grammar: countable noun (missionaries); adjective premodifying. Pronounced RP /ˈmɪʃənri/. Collocations: missionary zeal/fervour, missionary position, missionary school/hospital, return missionary.
