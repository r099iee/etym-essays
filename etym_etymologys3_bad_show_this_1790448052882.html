The idiom Bad show this unites three independent histories. Each word is therefore traced in isolation at full depth, without reduction.

1. bad — evolution essay

The word bad is famously one of the few common English adjectives with no secure Indo-European pedigree. Old English had yfel (evil) and ill (from Norse) for morally wrong and practically poor, but no bad. Around 1297 Middle English suddenly attests badde meaning inferior, defective, of poor quality — badde money, badde weather — and by the 14th century it has extended to morally wicked. The abruptness and the doubled -dd- suggest slang or children's language breaking into writing, as often happens with taboo and pejorative words that live long in speech before scribes admit them.

The only proposed ancestor is Old English bæddel (hermaphrodite, effeminate man) and its feminine bædling, found in glosses and penitentials around 1000. The semantic path from a term of sexual scorn to general worthlessness is entirely plausible — compare wretch, villain, sissy — and phonologically bæddel could shorten to badde. But the evidence is thin: a gap of two centuries with no attestations, uncertain vowel development, and no cognates in other Germanic languages. The Oxford English Dictionary therefore marks bad as of unknown origin, and Etymonline calls the bæddel link speculative. Honest lexicography must leave the earliest stage blank rather than invent a PIE root.

  • 1300s generalisation from quality to morals: badde spreads from bad coin and bad food to bad men and bad deeds. Chaucer uses it for both poor (badde lodging) and wicked (badde entente), showing the bridge from defective to depraved complete by 1380. The comparative worse and superlative worst, inherited from Old English wiersa / wierst (from a different root), are captured to serve as its degrees, a sure sign of full naturalisation: bad is now so central it borrows suppletion.
  • 1500s–1700s school and moral badness: Tudor schoolmasters make bad the universal contrary of good — bad boy, bad Latin, bad form, bad debt, bad cold. Shakespeare puns on it endlessly (bad women, bad dreams, bad neighbourhood), and the King James Bible fixes bad figs, bad trees as moral emblems. The public-school code that will produce bad show already pairs bad with nouns of conduct: to be bad is to let the side down.
  • Modern inversions — bad meaning good: African-American slang from the 1930s onward inverts the polarity — bad cat, badass, that's bad (meaning excellent) — via ironic praise, taken into mainstream youth slang by the 1980s (Michael Jackson's Bad, 1987). The standard senses persist alongside, so bad is now auto-antonymic in some registers. In bad show, however, the old school sense holds firm: resolutely disapproving, never cool.

In bad show this the word contributes blunt Anglo-Saxon verdict force against the Latinate theatricality of show. That clash — Germanic judgement plus French spectacle — is the engine of the idiom's bluff Edwardian charm.

2. show — evolution essay

Show begins with looking. PIE *(s)keu- meant to notice, to pay heed, to observe sharply — a root that also yields Greek akouein (to hear, by a different extension), Latin cavere (to beware, to take heed), and Germanic words for seeing and guarding. In Proto-Germanic it becomes *skauwōn, to gaze, to stare, preserved in Gothic us-skawjan (to wake up, literally to look up), Old High German scouwon (to look), and Old English sceawian (to look at, to see, to inspect, to behold). An Anglo-Saxon who sceawoð the heavens simply gazes at them; the chronicler who sceawoð an omen inspects it for meaning.

From gazing to exhibiting is a short causative step: to show is to cause to be seen. Already in Old English sceawian bears both senses — to look and to display — and Middle English schewen, shewen, showen extends them to manifesting, explaining, and proving: to show mercy (display it in act), to show a reason (make it visible to the mind). The Great Vowel Shift raises the vowel from /oː/ to /oʊ/, giving modern /ʃəʊ/, while spelling show (alongside shew until the 19th century) fixes the sh- from palatalised sc-. The past tense levels to showed with archaic shown as participle.

  • Medieval display and proof: Chaucer's shewe covers pageants, relics exhibited to pilgrims, and logical demonstration. The noun show (a thing seen, a spectacle) is attested by 1300, and showbread (shewbread, the loaves displayed in the Temple) enters biblical English. Seeing is already moralised: God shows favour by making it visible.
  • Elizabethan theatre fixes the entertainment sense: travelling players advertise a show, puppet-shows and peep-shows delight fairs, Shakespeare's dumb show mimes the plot. From this stage sense comes the evaluative schoolboy transfer: life as lived before spectators is a show, and conduct is good or bad as a performance. To say bad show is to hiss the play while pitying the actor.
  • Modern proliferation: 19th-century showroom, show trial, show business (showbiz), show-stopper; 20th-century chat show, talent show, reality show, to steal the show, to run the show. The verb keeps its evidential sense in science and law — results show, evidence shows — where making visible is proving. In bad show this the noun retains its full theatrical resonance: misfortune viewed as a flop production.

The journey from wary heed (cavere) through steadfast gaze (sceawian) to staged delight (showbiz) shows how a word for attention becomes a word for what deserves attention — and, in school slang, for how well you hold it. Even the spelling doublet shew/show preserves the memory of gaze becoming gift: what was once keenly looked at is now kindly offered to view, a fitting bridge to Bertie offering sympathy as spectacle.

3. this — evolution essay

This belongs to the oldest layer of English: the demonstratives that point. PIE distinguished *so- / *to- (that, the — the anaphoric demonstrative) from the near-deictic particle *ḱe- (here, this, attached to point at what is close). Sanskrit sa / esa (he / this one), Greek ho / houtos (the / this here), Latin iste / hic (that near you / this near me) all combine a base with such a particle. Germanic fused them as *þa- + *-si-: the þ- from PIE *to- by Grimm's law (t → þ), the -si from *ḱe-. Gothic þata, Old Norse þetta, Old High German diz (modern dies-) show the compound already.

Old English inflected it fully for gender, case, and number: masculine þes, feminine þeos, neuter þis, plural þas, with oblique cases þisses, þisse, þisum. A speaker chose the form by the noun's gender — þes mann, þeos fæmne, þis cild — and the form also marked proximity: þis near me versus þæt (that) farther off versus geon (yon) farthest. Pronunciation began with voiceless þ /θ/ as in thin, still heard in careful this /ðɪs/ with voicing of the initial by later assimilation.

  • Middle English collapse to this / these / those: after 1066 the gender and case system decays; by Chaucer only this (singular near), these (plural near, remodelled on those), that / those (far) survive. This generalises the old neuter þis to all genders. The near/far contrast sharpens into the modern deictic pair that structures discourse: this مشکل here, that problem there; this week, that week.
  • Discourse and emotional this: early Modern English extends this from spatial pointing to textual and emotional pointing — this I must tell you, this Bertie fellow, this sticky time. Shakespeare's this is the night and the Authorised Version's this is my beloved Son show this presenting the matter at hand for judgement. In bad show this the trailing this does exactly that work: it holds up the present trouble — Dahlia's worries, the prize-giving mix-up — as if pointing a finger at it while laying a hand on her shoulder.
  • Modern idiom this as sympathy: this business, this affair, this nasty turn soften verdicts by making them particular and passing rather than general. To say bad show this rather than bad show turns a maxim into a condolence: not all shows are bad, but this one lamentably is. The tiny word thus carries the whole pragmatics of Bertie's kindness.

From PIE pointing particle to Gothic compound to Old English declension to Bertie's trailing deictic, this has always done the same job: bringing what is near into shared attention so speaker and hearer can judge it together. That pointing finger, softened by Bertie into a hand on the shoulder, is why the tiny word can turn a school verdict into a consolation.

Together, blunt bad, theatrical show, and pointing this fuse in school slang into a single sympathetic verdict: a poor performance, presently before us, lamented together.
