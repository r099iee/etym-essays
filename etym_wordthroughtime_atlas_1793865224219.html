- Archaic Greece (ca. 8th c. BCE) - Emerged in Hesiod's Theogony and Homer's Odyssey as the Titan son of Iapetus punished by Zeus after the Titanomachy to hold up the vast dome of the heavens at the western borders of the earth; characterized by monumental strength and cosmic endurance.
- Classical & Hellenistic Greece (ca. 5th–2nd c. BCE) - Rationalized by mythographers, historians, and geographers (such as Herodotus and Diodorus Siculus) into a legendary ancient king of Mauretania who was a master astronomer, philosopher, and geographer, whose kingdom gave its name to Mount Atlas in Northwest Africa and the Atlantic Ocean (Mare Atlanticum).
- Roman Antiquity & Middle Ages (1st c. BCE–15th c.) - Transmitted through Ovid's Metamorphoses (where Perseus turns King Atlas into stone using the Gorgon Medusa's severed head) and Virgil's Aeneid; personified in Roman architecture as 'atlantes' (massive sculpted male figures supporting building entablatures).
- Late Renaissance (1595) - Transformed forever into a common noun by the Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator, who titled his master compilation of world maps 'Atlas sive Cosmographicae Meditationes...', choosing the learned astronomer-king Atlas as its patron and frontispiece emblem.
- 17th to 18th c. - Standardized internationally across European languages as the universal common noun for any bound compilation of maps; adopted into anatomical nomenclature (by Vesalius and Realdo Colombo) for the first cervical vertebra (C1) supporting the globe of the human cranium.
- 19th to 21st c. - Expanded into aerospace (the Atlas rocket family that launched Project Mercury and interplanetary probes); thematic publishing (anatomical, histological, and historical atlases); and twentieth-century philosophy/literature (Ayn Rand's 'Atlas Shrugged', where Atlas symbolizes the prime movers and productive intellects upholding civilization).
