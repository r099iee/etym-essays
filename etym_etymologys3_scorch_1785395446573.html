The word scorch first appears in English around 1400, a verb of stubbornly uncertain parentage, and scholarship still divides between two ancestries, each of which supplied the modern word with part of its meaning. Hypothesis A runs through Germanic and ultimately the Proto-Indo-European root *sker-, "to cut, scrape" — the parent of shear, share, shard, shirt, sharp, score, and scar, all built on the idea of cutting or scratching. From this root a Germanic stem of shrinkage and shrivelling arose — the cutting idea transferred to the drying, contracting, pinched-up look of tissue losing moisture — and this stem produced Old Norse skorpna, "to shrivel, dry up, shrink", said precisely of skin and leaves that wrinkle when water leaves them. The same extended family appears across the continental Germanic languages in schrumpfen (German), schrompelen (Dutch), and their relatives, all meaning "to shrink, shrivel". On this account, scorch was originally a word about drying and shrivelling, and only secondarily about burning. Hypothesis B runs through Romance. Latin excorticāre, "to strip off bark", formed from ex-, "off, out of", and cortex, "bark, rind" — the source of the English botanical word cortex — passed into Old French as escorchier, "to flay, strip the skin from a body", a violent word for skinning animals and enemies alike. The Romance family remained vigorous: modern French écorcher still means "to skin, to flay" (and, colloquially, "to overcharge", a different metaphor), Italian scorticare means "to graze, to flay", and Spanish descorchar, "to uncork", is literally "to strip the cork-bark". On this account, scorch was originally a word about stripping and searing surfaces, and the burning sense is the natural successor to flaying — the same violent removal of a protective layer. Most etymologists now agree that Middle English scorchen, attested from about 1400, is a blend of these two streams: from Old Norse skorpna it took the notions of shrivelling, drying, and contracting; from Old French escorchier it took the searing, stripping of surfaces and — probably — most of its actual sound, since the Old French word and the English verb are near-homophones. The two ideas fused into a single sense: a dry, surface-burning heat that shrivels as it sears. The debate is not over the existence of the two sources but over their proportions — some authorities make the Germanic element primary and the Romance element a reinforcement, others the reverse — and the OED treats the whole question as disputed, which the honest etymologist records rather than resolves. Semantically, the word then developed outward from its core image of a seared surface. In the sixteenth century the sense of surface-burning settled firmly: scorching meant singeing cloth, flesh, or vegetation with dry heat so that the outer layer discoloured, blackened, or blistered. From this the moisture sense grew — a scorched land or a scorched lawn is one dried and browned by sun or drought, with fire itself absent; and the sun became the great scorcher of the English climate, so that scorching heat and a scorching day entered the meteorological vocabulary. The domestic register followed the same path into the kitchen, where scorched rice, scorched milk, and scorched sauce name the thin burnt layer left on a pan, and into the vocabulary of ironing, where a hot iron scorches a shirt. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries gave the word its most famous compound and its fiercest figurative uses. Scorched earth — the military strategy of burning everything an advancing enemy could use — was named in English from the Russian practice during the Napoleonic invasion and the First World War, and it remains one of the most chilling phrases in the language, generalised since to any policy of total, ruinous denial. Beside it, scorching grew a slang and journalistic life as an intensifier: a scorching pace, a scorching review, a scorching rebuke — the verbal heat of anger transferred to speed, criticism, and condemnation, so that to scorch someone is to denounce them blisteringly. Socio-linguistically, scorch has always been a word of surfaces and of practical life, and its register reflects that. It is standard English, at home in the kitchen, the garden, the weather forecast, and the war report, but it never lost the violence of its Old French ancestor or the dryness of its Old Norse one. Its paired use with shrivel in curses and threats — "Shrivel and scorch!" in The Hobbit — is a perfect summary of its double inheritance: the surface seared black by a stripping heat, the substance underneath shrivelled and dead. The word's disputed family tree, with its two well-attested parents and its blended child, is itself a small monument to the way English repeatedly fuses Germanic and Romance material into a single, irreducibly English word.
