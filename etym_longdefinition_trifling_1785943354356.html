TRIFLING is the present participle and adjective formed from trifle, and it has two great families of meaning: (1) of things — small, unimportant, insignificant, of little value or consequence; and (2) of people and behavior — frivolous, silly, idle, and, in an older and harsher register, given to mockery or dishonesty. A "trifling sum" is a sum too small to matter; a "trifling person" is someone who treats serious matters lightly, or who cannot be relied upon; and the word still faintly carries the older sense of "making a mock of something" — treating it as a toy. Because the underlying noun trifle itself means "a thing of little value or importance," the adjective is essentially the emphatic form of "small," but it has developed a rich and sometimes surprising set of secondary senses.

Core senses
  • Of little value or importance (primary sense): A trifling amount, matter, or detail is one beneath notice — not worth money, effort, or anger. "They quarreled over a trifling sum" means the sum was absurdly small.
  • Frivolous or silly behavior: A trifling person or a trifling mood wastes time on idle or silly things, refusing seriousness — "trifling away the afternoon."
  • Mocking, jesting (older literary sense): Underlying the noun is the Old French idea of mockery and trickery; "to trifle with someone" still means to treat them lightly, to tease, or to deceive — "do not trifle with my affections."
  • Legal and financial use ("a trifling sum"): In courts and contracts, "trifling" marks an amount so small that the law will not take it seriously — a de minimis matter that litigation should ignore; the word does real work in legal writing about damages, fines, and disputed pennies.
  • Colloquial slang ("a trifling person"): In informal speech, "trifling" (or "triflin'") condemns someone as deceitful, shallow, worthless, or a time-waster — a harsher moral judgment than the neutral "trivial."
  • Homonymic heritage (culinary and botanical echoes): The noun trifle is also the name of the English dessert of sponge cake, fruit, and custard (17th c.), so called because the dish was at first considered an easy, unimportant confection; and the related word truffle (the mushroom) shares an ancestor in Old French trufle. These are relatives of the noun, not meanings of the adjective, but they color the word family's associations with smallness and indulgence.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge
Old French trufle (or trufe) meant "mockery, jest, trickery, deceit" — and also the truffle mushroom. Middle English borrowed it as trifle, at first meaning "a false tale, a trick, a piece of mockery," then, by a natural depreciation, "a thing of small worth." The semantic bridge runs from deception to diminishment: something treated with mockery is something not taken seriously; something not taken seriously is something small. From "a trick" to "a toy" to "a tiny matter" is a short journey, and once trifle meant "small matter," the adjective trifling (16th c.) simply meant "of the nature of a trifle." The modern slang sense re-attaches the older sting: a "trifling" person is one who, like a trickster, cannot be taken seriously.

Related words and grammar
  • trifle (n.): a thing of small value or importance; also the dessert.
  • trifle (v.): to talk or act idly or lightly; to mock or toy with (trifle with).
  • trifler (n.): a person who trifles — an idle, unserious person.
  • triflingly (adv.): in a trifling manner.

Register and use
Trifling is a conversational and literary word, slightly old-fashioned in its mocking senses but fully alive in everyday use ("a trifling matter," "don't waste time on trifling details"). In American colloquial speech it has been reinvigorated as a slang insult, while in legal prose it functions as a precise marker of negligible magnitude. The word is a favorite of moralists who warn against spending energy on "trifling pursuits.
