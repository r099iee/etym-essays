ducked — When "the kid" enters the tent, he does not walk or step: he ducks. The verb describes a quick, practiced compression of the body — the head and shoulders lowered to pass under a low obstruction — and in McCarthy's sentence it is the first physical fact we know about the boy: a movement of concealment and economy, the body adapting itself to a mean and cramped space. The tone is kinetic and unheroic; there is nothing graceful about it, only a poor boy's reflex for getting under cover. Because the tent is a low canvas shelter packed with the "wet and bathless," the ducking is also a gesture of submission to squalor: the body bows itself into the stench rather than entering upright and open. The punctual past form "ducked" (not "was ducking") makes the action a single completed move — the decisive instant of crossing the threshold from rain to reek.

Polysemy of "duck."

  • Core physical sense. To lower the head and body quickly, especially to pass under something or avoid a blow or missile — "duck under the branch," "duck and weave."
  • The bird. The noun "duck" (the waterfowl) is historically the same word — the bird that "ducks" for food; it replaced the older Germanic bird-word (ened).
  • Brief submersion. To duck someone is to plunge them briefly under water; "to take a ducking" is to be held under.
  • Figurative evasion (modern). "Duck out," "duck a question," "duck a responsibility" — to shirk or avoid, the physical dodging transferred to conduct.
  • Specialized and idiomatic. In cricket, a "duck" is a score of nought (from "duck's egg"); in British English, "duck" is a term of endearment; "duck and cover" was the Cold War civil-defense drill of lowering and shielding.

Boundary comparisons. To dive is a deliberate, athletic, self-endangering commitment into water or air; to dip is to lower briefly and partially, with the implication of raising again; to stoop is to bend the torso forward and down, retaining a measure of dignity; to crouch is to lower the whole body, bending the knees to hide, spring, or wait; to bob is a rapid up-and-down motion suggesting resilience. "Duck" uniquely combines avoidance with compression: it is the only one of the set that can be used figuratively of shirking, which makes it both the most physical and the most evasive word here.

ratty — The tent is not merely old: it is "ratty," an epithet that fuses shabbiness with vermin. McCarthy does not say the tent is dirty or torn; he makes it rat-like, gnawed, soiled, and infested, the canvas itself part of the general filth of the "wet and bathless" crowd inside. The word transfers the rodent to the fabric, so that the shelter and its occupants share one condition of dirt and deprivation. The tone is squalid and contemptuous, delivered without explanation, as if the assessment were obvious.

Polysemy of "ratty."

  • Resembling a rat (1812). "Of or like a rat" — the rodent transferred to a quality; a "ratty" face might be sharp, pointed, and mean.
  • Infested with rats (1840s). "A ratty cellar" — the animal becomes an infestation.
  • Shabby, worn (1867). "A ratty old coat" — the vermin-sense slips to general squalor, gnawed and soiled; this is McCarthy's sense.
  • Irritable, snappish (1880s, chiefly British). "He was ratty with tiredness" — the worn thing becomes a bad temper, as if bitten.

Boundary comparisons. Shabby is worn through neglect but comparatively neutral, even pitiable; tattered emphasizes rips rather than dirt; dingy emphasizes dullness of color and light; mangy is literally scabby, of animals, carrying disease and patchy hair; scruffy is unkempt and untidy; grubby is soiled with dirt; seedy points to moral tawdriness rather than actual vermin; decrepit is broken down by age, of persons and buildings. "Ratty" is the only one that makes the object verminous — gnawed, filthy, infected — and it alone can describe a person's temper as well as their coat.

heady — The "reek" — a heavy, foul, smoke-like smell — is called "heady": so strong, dense, and concentrated that it goes to the head, acting on the senses like alcohol or a drug. The word is doubly telling, because "heady" normally carries a seductive glamour (a heady wine, a heady triumph), and McCarthy applies it to a stench of unwashed bodies in a packed tent. The glamorous word for intoxication becomes a word for nausea; the reek overwhelms the consciousness precisely as a drug might, and the men are driven out "for fresh air" by the same force that a heady draught would produce.

Polysemy of "heady."

  • Headstrong, impetuous (14th century, now archaic). The first sense — acting from the head alone — survives in the noun "headiness."
  • Intoxicating (c. 1570). "Apt to affect the head, going to the head": applied to wine, perfume, aroma — a force that acts on the senses like alcohol or a drug.
  • Exhilarating overload (modern). "Heady days," "heady heights," "heady success": at once glamorous and dizzying, the vocabulary of elevation.

Boundary comparisons. Strong says nothing about effect on the mind; potent emphasizes power, especially of alcohol and remedies; intoxicating says plainly that something acts like liquor, usually admiringly; pungent is sharp and biting to nose or tongue; overpowering describes what physically overwhelms the senses; acrid is harsh, bitter, and irritating, especially of smoke and fumes; oppressive emphasizes the weight and closeness of air. "Heady" is distinct because it specifies the organ affected — the head — and the result: a dizziness, a reeling, a loss of clear-headedness, exhilarating or sickening, and in the same sentence both at once.

sally — The men do not merely leave the tent: they "sally forth," and the verb carries the full military weight of a sortie — a sudden, deliberate rushing-out of a besieged garrison against the enemy. In the passage the besieged garrison is a crowded, stinking tent, and the enemy is the lack of air; the men make a comic sortie into the downpour "now and again for fresh air before the rain drove them in again." The mock-heroic dignity of the word against the squalor of the occasion is McCarthy's signature: a word from siege warfare applied to men gasping for breath.

Polysemy of "sally."

  • Military sortie (16th century). A sudden rush-out of a besieged garrison against the besieger, launched from the "sally port," the gate in the fortification.
  • Witty retort (17th century). "A sally of wit" — a sudden pointed verbal attack, the leap extended to speech.
  • Excursion (modern). An outing or brief trip; "sally forth" survives as deliberately archaic, mock-heroic diction for an old-fashioned, purposeful breaking-out.

Boundary comparisons. To venture is to go forward in the face of risk, with deliberation and uncertainty; to rush is to move with haste and without ceremony; to dash is to move quickly and impetuously; to burst out is to emerge suddenly and uncontrollably; to issue (or "issue forth") is formal and archaic, describing an ordered coming-out; to foray is to raid into enemy territory. "Sally" is the only one that implies a garrison, a siege, a deliberate and even gallant breaking-out against a besieger; it carries a sense of purpose and of adverse surroundings to be breached.
