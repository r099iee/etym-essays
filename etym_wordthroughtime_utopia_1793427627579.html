- 1516 — Humanist coinage: Sir Thomas More coins Utopia in Neo-Latin for his fictional island, embedding a witty bilingual pun on Greek ou-topos (‘no-place’) and eu-topos (‘good-place’).
- 1551 — Vernacular translation: Ralph Robinson translates More’s book into English, introducing Utopia as a common noun for any imaginary realm of political perfection.
- 17th c. — Speculative expansion: philosophers like Francis Bacon (New Atlantis) and Tommaso Campanella (City of the Sun) build upon the model, establishing utopian writing as a distinct intellectual tradition.
- 18th c. — Satirical and Enlightenment weapon: Jonathan Swift and Voltaire wield the concept satirically in Gulliver’s Travels and Candide, contrasting Gulliver’s Houyhnhnms and Candide’s El Dorado with corrupt European civilization.
- 19th c. — Socialist experiments and pejorative shift: Robert Owen and Charles Fourier establish real-world intentional communities, while Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels critique ‘utopian socialism’ as naive daydreaming disconnected from historical materialism.
- 20th c. — Dystopian inversion: the horrors of totalitarianism flip the term on its head, giving rise to classic anti-utopias (Zamyatin, Huxley, Orwell) where the pursuit of perfection becomes a coercive nightmare.
- 21st c. — Silicon Valley and ecological visions: reimagined in debates over ‘techno-utopianism’ (AI, post-scarcity, transhumanism) and ‘solarpunk / ecotopias’, poised between digital hubris and planetary survival.
