The verb reared is a heritage Germanic formation whose deep history illustrates the intricate phonological evolution of West Germanic causative verbs, the development of doublet pairs in the wake of the Scandinavian invasions, and the long-standing semantic interplay between physical construction and human nurture.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Proto-Germanic Causative Formation

The ultimate ancestry of rear traces to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₃er- (alternatively reconstructed as *er-), signifying "to move, stir, set in motion, or rise." This ancient root gave rise to a vast family of Indo-European cognates across multiple branches, including Sanskrit ṛṇóti ("rises, moves"), Ancient Greek órnumi (ὄρνυμι, "to stir up, rouse, excite"), and Latin oriri ("to arise, originate"), from which English derives origin and orient.

In Proto-Germanic, this root generated the primary strong verb *rīsaną (Class I strong verb, preterite *rās, past participle *risanaz), meaning "to rise, get up, move upward," which directly yielded modern English rise. From the preterite stem of this strong verb (incorporating Proto-Indo-European o-grade vocalism), Proto-Germanic formed an active causative weak verb by appending the causative suffix *-janą with concomitant Verner's Law voicing of the medial fricative: *raizijaną, literally meaning "to cause to rise, to lift up, to erect, to awaken."

2. West Germanic Rhotacism and Old English Development

As Proto-Germanic split into regional dialects, the causative verb *raizijaną underwent pivotal phonological transformations in the West Germanic branch. Under the regular phonetic law known as rhotacism, the voiced alveolar fricative *z shifted into the alveolar liquid *r between vowels. Simultaneously, the suffix *-janą triggered i-mutation (umlaut) of the root vowel *ai, fronting it to long *ǣ.

  • In North Germanic (Old Norse), rhotacism also occurred, but the *z became an Old Norse *r without the same diphthongal shift, giving Old Norse reisa ("to raise, lift, build").
  • In Old English, the regular sound changes produced the Class I weak verb rǣran (preterite rǣrde, past participle gerǣred), bearing the primary senses of "to raise up, erect, build, set on end, lift; arouse, excite, cause to exist, awaken."

In Anglo-Saxon prose and verse, rǣran was employed with dramatic physical and psychological weight. It appears in the Alfredian translation of Orosius's Histories and in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle to describe the building of fortifications and walls, the raising of battle banners, and the awakening of slumbering spirits. It was already applied both to physical architecture (raising roof-beams of halls) and to moral instigation (raising conflicts or laws).

3. Middle English Evolution and the Splendid Doublet with 'Raise'

Following the Norman Conquest and the intensive linguistic contact with Scandinavian settlers in the Danelaw, Middle English inherited two distinct reflexes of the identical Proto-Germanic ancestor *raizijaną:

  • The native inherited Old English verb rǣran evolved regularly through vowel reduction into Middle English rēren (spelled variously as rere, reere, rearen; preterite rērede, past participle rēred).
  • The Scandinavian loanword reisa entered northern dialects and spread south as Middle English reisen (yielding modern raise).

English thus acquired an etymological doublet: rear and raise are phonetic cousins derived from the same Proto-Germanic root. During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), rēren expanded its semantic boundary dramatically. In addition to setting up stone altars, timber crosses, and castellated ramparts, it began to be systematically applied to the biological sphere: bringing up children, fostering youngsters, and rearing domestic cattle and sheep. Texts such as the Ancrene Wisse (c. 1225) and John Trevisa's translation of Bartholomaeus Anglicus's De Proprietatibus Rerum (1398) demonstrate rēren applied to maternal care, animal breeding, and building shelters.

4. Early Modern English, Historiography, and Classical Prescriptivism

By the 16th and 17th centuries, the form had settled into early modern orthography as rear and its past form reared. Shakespeare utilized it with multifaceted versatility, from rearing children and standards to the menacing posture of an enraged beast. During this era, the specific equestrian sense crystallized: a horse rearing upon its haunches was described as setting itself upright on end.

In the 18th century, Enlightenment historians such as David Hume (notably in his monumental The History of England, 1754–1761, from which the immersion context of this card is drawn) chose reared as a dignified, evocative verb to characterize the primitive construction efforts of early Britons: "they dwelt in huts, which they reared in the forests and marshes..." The word imparted an organic, pastoral authenticity distinct from the colder Latinate constructed or fabricated.

In the late 18th and 19th centuries, prescriptive grammarians in Britain and America debated the division of labor between the twin verbs rear and raise. British stylistic tradition canonized the rule that human children and livestock are properly reared, reserving raise for crops, money, and inanimate weights. While 20th-century American English largely collapsed this distinction in favor of raised, British and literary registers retain reared as a mark of classical refinement and evocative historical prose.
