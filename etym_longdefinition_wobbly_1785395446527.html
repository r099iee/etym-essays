WOBBLY, adjective: unsteady, rocking, or teetering — tending to sway from side to side rather than to tremble in place. Applied to Bilbo's legs after the escape from the goblins — 'his stomach felt all empty and loose and his legs all wobbly, now that the excitement was over' — it captures the physiological aftermath of terror and relief: knees gone rubbery, limbs swaying as the adrenaline drains away, the body no longer held rigid by fear. The word is informal, homely, and slightly comic — the ordinary domestic word for a rickety chair — and Tolkien's use of it domesticates the peril: the hero's collapse becomes a pudding-kneed stagger. Crucially, it is placed 'now that the excitement was over': the wobbliness is not cowardice but the sag of courage spent, the exact physical signature of a body switching off its fight-or-flight response, and the homely word dignifies the moment even as it makes it human. The sentence strings the three words together — empty, loose, wobbly — as one dwindling inventory of the body's surrender. The adjective's full range runs from furniture to feelings:
  • Physical objects — a wobbly table, chair, or bridge; anything that rocks or shimmies instead of staying put; the everyday word for instability of things.
  • Bodies — wobbly legs, wobbly knees, a wobbly walk; the physical signature of faintness, dizziness, exhaustion, or fear, of children learning to walk and of the infirm.
  • Emotional and mental — a wobbly voice that cannot hold its note, wobbly nerves, the unsteady state of a person about to cry or collapse.
  • Figurative abstraction — wobbly plans, wobbly prices, a wobbly argument or performance; anything unreliable, unstable, or uncertain in quality or outcome.
  • British idiom — 'to throw a wobbly' (20th century) means to lose one's temper or have a fit of panic, sealing the word's homely, faintly ridiculous register.
  • Scientific and technical — a wobble board, wobble in a wheel or rotor, the 'wobble' hypothesis in genetics (1966), and astronomical wobble as evidence of orbiting planets.
The word must be set against its near-synonyms. 'Shaky' suggests rapid fine trembling or vibration (cold hands, nervous fingers) rather than slow swaying; 'unsteady' is the neutral, formal term for any loss of stability, without the comic warmth of wobbly; 'rickety' describes structural weakness and decay in objects and is harsher in tone; 'tottery' describes a wavering gait — close to wobbly but rarer and more old-fashioned; and 'teetering' is a deliberate, precarious balancing on a brink, which suggests agency rather than exhaustion. Where 'shaky' reports a symptom and 'unsteady' delivers a verdict, WOBBLY renders the feeling from inside: it is the word for a table that rocks, a wheel that shimmies, jelly that jiggles, and a voice that cannot hold its note. The doubled consonant and rocking vowels of the word — wob-bly — mimic the very motion it names, and that self-demonstrating quality is why the word carries warmth even when it describes collapse: it is the sound of instability itself, pronounced with a comic, sympathetic wobble. In register it is firmly informal and domestic, the natural language of kitchens, playgrounds, and family jokes rather than of clinical or technical prose, and it is a favourite of children's writing and of any writer who wants to make danger human.
