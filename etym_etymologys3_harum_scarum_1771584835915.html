Harum-scarum has no single ancestor, and its biography is therefore a story about how English coins words rather than about how it inherits them. It first appears in print in the 1670s, a rhyming compound that means, at its most literal, "harrying and scaring." The ending -um is a jocular mock-Latin flourish — the Latin genitive-plural hārum, "of these" — which gives the whole word a comically pedantic air, as though some bookish jester had latinised a pair of Anglo-Saxon verbs. The first element is probably the verb hare, "to chase, harry," from the animal, or its close cousin harry; the second is scare. Put them together and the word is a kinetic sketch of someone who dashes headlong through life, chasing and being chased, frightening and being frightened, all in one breathless tumble. For such an expressive formation, the origin of the component parts still matters, and each has its own separate history.

Hare is an ancient Germanic word. Old English hara descends from Proto-Germanic *hasan-, "the gray one," built on the PIE root *ḱas-, "gray" — the hare being named, like so many animals, for its colour. The same root plausibly produced Sanskrit śaśa- (शश), "hare," and certainly produced German Hase, Dutch haas, and Old Norse heri. From the noun came a verb, to hare, "to run wildly like a hare," and to harry someone — a word whose pedigree is different again. Harry descends from Old English hergian, "to make war, ravage," from Proto-Germanic *harjōną, "to raid with a war-band," itself from *harjaz, "army," and the PIE root *koro-, "war-band, host," with proposed relatives in Greek κοίρανος (koíranos, "commander"), Old Irish cuire ("troop"), and Lithuanian kãrias ("army"). English got harry twice over, from Old English here, "army" (still visible in the town name Hereford, "army-ford"), and again from the Old Norse herja that accompanied the Viking raids. Scare, meanwhile, came north by a different route: Middle English skerren borrowed Old Norse skirra, "to frighten away, to shun," a causative of the adjective skjarr, "shy, timid," from Proto-Germanic *skirjaną. Its deeper PIE ancestry is not securely established — the common suggestion of *sker-, "to cut, to spring aside," remains speculative — but its Germanic shape is clear, and by the late fourteenth century scare was a settled English verb, giving us scared and, in the sixteenth century, the scarecrow.

The compound they built, harum-scarum, began as an adjective ("harum-scarum ways"), became a noun meaning "a reckless, impetuous person" by 1803, and also ran adverbially ("to go harum-scarum about"). It belongs to a whole family of English rhyming reduplications — helter-skelter, pell-mell, hurly-burly, higgledy-piggledy, topsy-turvy — words that picture disorderly motion by sounding like it, jingling through the same i-a and u-a vowel alternations. Structurally they are siblings, though genetically unrelated. The socio-cultural context is a seventeenth-century England fond of comic mock-classicism: the fashion for fake Latin endings (-um, -us) on English words made even a scramble sound learned, and the gentleman-jester's hārum "of these" gave the street word a mock-scholarly sheen. In the verse-portrait of Bao-yu in The Story of the Stone, the noun does exactly what its history promises: it opens the poem with a burst of kinetic comedy, painting the boy as a creature of headlong impulse, "to all duty blind," a "doltish mule" beyond the reach of study. The word's mock-Latin flourish is apt — the adult world wraps its exasperation in learned-sounding jest — while its hare-and-scare roots capture the boy's zigzag, unfixable energy. Bao-yu is a harum-scarum: the very word the household uses to laugh at him names, in miniature, the whole unmanageable movement of his nature. The word's comic register has kept it alive in British English long after other rhyming compounds faded, precisely because it is not a plain description but a kinetic picture — a running, scattering, scaring little drama in four syllables.
