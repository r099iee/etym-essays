“To wag” has meant to move something rapidly to and fro — a short, oscillating play — since its earliest English life, applied to tails, tongues, and later beards and fingers.
“Wag” through the idiom “to wag the tongue” (and “tongues wag”) became a fixture of gossip talk, while “to play the wag” meant to be a joker or merry prankster in 18th–19th-century speech.
“Wag” in 19th-century British school slang came to mean a truant — one who plays about instead of attending — and “to wag school” meant to skip it.
“Wagged” today still most vividly pictures a dog’s tail or a swinging shadow — the concrete little motion Tolkien uses for the shadow of Gandalf’s beard keeping time in the firelight.
