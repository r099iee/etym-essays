The noun affray is the legal and literary fossil of a Germanic idea of peace wrapped in a Romance verbal construction — a word whose two halves come from different language families and whose meaning has moved from fear to fight while keeping both. It begins deep in Proto-Indo-European with the root *priH-, "to love, to be kind to, to be pleased" — the root that in Sanskrit gives prīyáte "is pleased" and in Germanic gives the verb "to love." From this root of affection and favour, the Germanic branch formed a tu-stem noun, Proto-Germanic *friþuz, meaning "peace" — not merely the absence of war but the protected, inviolable social order: truce, sanctuary, the bond that keeps a community safe under law. The same family produced *frijōndz, "loving one, friend," so that "peace" and "friend" are built from the same root of being dear to one another.

This Germanic *friþu had a vigorous career in the north. Old English friþ means "peace, protection, truce"; Old High German fridu gives modern German Friede/Frieden; Old Norse friðr and Dutch vrede are the same word. The deeper Indo-European association often proposed ties the whole family to love and pleasing: peace as the state in which people are "dear" to one another under law and custom — a strikingly warm root for the coldest of political concepts.

The crucial step for affray happened in the contact zone between Germanic and Romance on the Continent. In the centuries after the Germanic migrations into Gaul, the Gallo-Romance speech of northern France borrowed the Germanic word for peace into its own vocabulary, and then built from it a dramatic opposite. With the Latinate prefix of removal ex- (Old French es-, af-), Vulgar Latin is reconstructed as producing a verb *exfridāre: literally "to take (someone) out of peace," hence "to disturb, to startle, to put in fright." The form is reconstructed, not directly attested — this is one of the points where the honest etymologist must flag the uncertainty — but the pattern is well documented in the family of Romance words for "to frighten" built on the Germanic *friþu. Out of it came Old French esfreer/effreer, "to frighten, to disturb," and the noun effroi (modern French effroi, "terror, dread"), along with the variant affrai — a disturbance, an alarm.

The noun crossed the Channel with two shapes and two senses. In Middle English, affray first means "fright, alarm, the fear of sudden attack": a person could be "in an affray," meaning in a state of terror. The parallel verb to affray meant "to frighten," and it is from the past participle of this verb that English has the common adjective afraid — originally "affrayed," put into a state of fright by disturbance. This is a remarkable double inheritance: the modern ordinary word afraid is the fossilised participle of the same verb that gave the formal legal word affray.

Semantic drift summary:
  • PIE *priH- "to love, be pleased" → Proto-Germanic *friþuz "peace, protection" (reconstructed)
  • → Old English friþ; Old High German fridu (→ German Friede); Old Norse friðr; Dutch vrede
  • → Gallo-Romance borrowing + Latin ex-: Vulgar Latin *exfridāre "to take out of peace, to disturb" (reconstructed)
  • → Old French esfreer "to frighten"; nouns effroi/affrai "alarm, disturbance" (11th–12th c.)
  • → Middle English affray "fright, alarm" and verb to affray "to frighten" (14th c.); whence afraid
  • → modern affray: a fight in a public place disturbing the peace (legal sense; Public Order Act 1986).

The semantic history therefore runs a tight sequence: protected peace → removal from peace → fright → noisy public fight. By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the noun had shifted from the terror itself to the noisy disturbance that provokes it — a quarrel or brawl that alarms the public. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the fight sense hardened and the word moved into law, naming a public breach of the king's peace; "affray" became a charge, an indictment, a statutory offence. The legal sense required fighting (two or more persons) of such a kind as to frighten those present — the word's whole history, peace broken and terror caused, compressed into a single technical definition that survives in English law to this day (Public Order Act 1986, section 3).

In the narrative line where "the ensuing affray" leaves one party wounded and later dead, the word still carries its old double charge: physical violence and the violation of ordered peace. Its register is formal, legal, historical, and slightly archaic; everyday speech prefers brawl or fight. Socially, the word's history reflects the long European effort to turn the protection of "peace" into law — from the tribal friþ that sheltered the friend to the modern public-order statute that criminalises the fight in the street. A small word, but it carries in its two syllables the whole story of how European society learned to call violence a breach of the peace.
