BLOTTED — derivation chain Old Norse blettr "a spot, a blemish" (attested Old Norse; the usual source proposed for the English word, c. 9th–11th c.) → Middle English blot "a spot or stain, especially of ink" (attested 14th c., c. 1369) → English verb to blot "to stain, smear, obscure; to dry with a blotter" (15th c.) → English phrasal verb to blot out "to obliterate, erase" (15th–16th c.) → Modern English blotted "stained, obscured, wiped out" (past participle).

DEBATE / NUANCE NOTE: The origin of "blot" is marked obscure or uncertain by the OED and Etymonline. Old Norse blettr is the leading hypothesis, not a certainty; some scholars hear an imitative/expressive word (the soft splat of ink). No Proto-Indo-European etymon can be securely reconstructed, so the chain begins at the attested medieval stage.

ERE — derivation chain PIE *h₂éyer- / *áyer- "day, morning" (reconstructed; cf. Vedic Sanskrit áhar "day", Avestan ayar "day", Greek ἦρι "early, at dawn") → Proto-Germanic *airi "early, at daybreak" (adverb, c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Proto-Germanic *airiz "earlier, before" (comparative of *airi) → Old English ǣr "before, previously, sooner; earlier in time" (adverb, preposition, and conjunction; attested in the earliest Old English records, 7th–8th c.) → Middle English er "before" (12th–15th c.) → Modern English ere "before" (archaic and poetic, 16th c.–present).

NUANCE NOTE: Old English ǣr is itself a comparative form; the positive degree of the root survives in English "early" (Old English ǣrlīce) and in "erstwhile" (Old English ǣrest "first"). Cognates include Old Saxon ēr, Old High German ēr, and Gothic air.

SINISTER — derivation chain [No secure PIE etymon — pre-Latin history unknown; de Vaan marks sinister as of uncertain origin, possibly a substrate or loanword; tentative links to roots meaning "left, half" are not generally accepted] → Latin sinister "left, on the left side" (attested in Plautus, 2nd c. BCE; with secondary senses "awkward, perverse, unlucky") → Latin sinistra (feminine, used substantively for "the left hand") → English sinister "left, on the left side" (borrowed directly from Latin c. 1400, first as a heraldic and anatomical term) → English sinister "unlucky, evil, threatening, ominous" (from the 15th c.; the moral sense established by the 16th–17th c.) → Modern English sinister "menacing, evil-omened, wrong".

DEBATE / NUANCE NOTE: Because no PIE ancestor can be reconstructed, the chain begins at attested Latin and the gap is stated explicitly. In Roman augury the left was not always unlucky — an augur facing south had the favorable east on his left — and English imported chiefly the Greek-influenced and popular Roman associations of left-handedness with awkwardness and ill luck.
