• Daṇḍakāraṇya: Similar because both are famous forest locations traversed by Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa during their fourteen-year exile; differs because Citrakūṭa is an idyllic, peaceful mountain retreat of gentle hermits located on the northern threshold of exile, whereas Daṇḍakāraṇya is a vast, perilous, monster-infested primordial jungle stretching across central and southern India where violent battles with rākṣasas occur.
  • Pañcavaṭī: Similar because both are sacred riverbank hermitages built by Lakṣmaṇa for Rāma and Sītā; differs because Citrakūṭa is situated on the Mandākinī River where peaceful domestic bliss and the joyful reunion with Bharata occur, whereas Pañcavaṭī is situated on the Godāvarī River in the south, becoming the tragic site of Śūrpaṇakhā's mutilation and Sītā's abduction by Rāvaṇa.
  • Kāmadgiri: Similar because Kāmadgiri is the primary sacred hill located in the heart of modern Chitrakoot; differs because Kāmadgiri (literally "the wish-fulfilling hill") is the specific geological hill encircled by pilgrims during circumambulation (parikramā), whereas Citrakūṭa is the comprehensive epic mountain system and wider geographical region.
  • Kiṣkindhā: Similar because both are dramatic mountainous territories in the Rāmāyaṇa; differs because Kiṣkindhā is the rugged monkey kingdom ruled by Vālin and Sugrīva located in modern Hampi (Karnataka), characterized by boulders and caves, whereas Citrakūṭa is a pastoral, wooded mountain sanctuary of Vedic sages.
  • Prayāga: Similar because both are holy pilgrimage nodes situated along Rāma's outbound exile journey; differs because Prayāga is the sacred river confluence (Triveṇī Saṅgam of Gaṅgā, Yamunā, and subterranean Sarasvatī) where the hermitage of sage Bharadvāja stands, while Citrakūṭa is the isolated mountain retreat Bharadvāja directed Rāma to occupy.
  • Naimiṣāraṇya: Similar because both are renowned sacred forests in Hindu literature; differs because Naimiṣāraṇya is an ancient sacrificial forest where 88,000 sages gathered to hear the Purāṇas and Mahābhārata recited by Sūta Gosvāmī, whereas Citrakūṭa is an intimate epic landscape defined by Rāma's personal presence and exile narrative.
  • Kailāsa: Similar because both are revered holy peaks in Indian sacred geography; differs because Kailāsa is a snow-covered Himalayan peak revered as the transcendental abode of Lord Śiva and Goddess Pārvatī, whereas Citrakūṭa is a lush, forested Vindhyan hill deeply connected with Lord Rāma and Vaishnava devotional poetry.
  • Vindhya: Similar because Citrakūṭa is geologically situated within the northern reaches of the Vindhya mountain range; differs because Vindhya denotes the immense east-west continental mountain barrier dividing northern and southern India, while Citrakūṭa is a specific holy hill and spiritual sanctuary within that system.
  • Ayodhyā: Similar because both are central geographic poles of the Rāmāyaṇa narrative; differs because Ayodhyā is the wealthy, fortified dynastic capital city of the Ikṣvāku kings characterized by urban splendor and political rivalry, whereas Citrakūṭa represents the antithesis of urban life—a peaceful retreat of pure nature and ascetics.
  • Chittorgarh: Similar because Chittorgarh derives its historic etymological name directly from Citrakūṭa-durga; differs because Chittorgarh is a massive, war-torn medieval Rajput military bastion in Rajasthan famous for heroic sieges and jauhar, whereas Citrakūṭa is an ancient spiritual forest hermitage of peace and non-violence.
