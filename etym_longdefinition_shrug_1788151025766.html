shrug (verb and noun) — to raise the shoulders for a moment, usually as a deliberate gesture, and to let them fall: the universal silent answer that says "I don't know," "I don't care," "what can I do?" As a noun, the gesture itself ("he gave a shrug"); as a verb it is used transitively only with "shoulders" ("she shrugged her shoulders") or intransitively ("he shrugged and walked away"). Cross-cultural studies of gesture class the shoulder-raise among the most widespread signals of puzzlement or resignation on earth — and English has given that gesture a word of remarkable durability.

Primary sense — the gesture. The core modern sense is the small, quick raising of one or both shoulders, typically with eyebrows or palms contributing, expressing ignorance, indifference, reluctance, or helpless dismissal: "I asked him why, and he shrugged." The verb is almost always intransitive with the meaning of the gesture implied ("she shrugged helplessly"); the transitive use takes only the body part across which the gesture runs ("shrug one's shoulders"). The emotional range is wide but low-intensity: the shrug is the gesture of resignation rather than anger, of bafflement rather than contempt.

Secondary senses and extensions.

  • Phrasal verb "shrug off." The most productive modern extension: to dismiss something as unworthy of concern — "shrug off criticism," "shrug off a defeat" — or to recover from or repel it: "shrug off a tackle" (shake off an opponent in sport), "shrug off a cold" (recover quickly). The image is of shedding a burden by a movement of the shoulders, as one would slip a pack or a clinging hand from one's back. First attested in the late nineteenth century, "shrug off" is now the idiomatic heart of the word.
  • The noun — the garment. Since the late nineteenth century, a "shrug" is also a very short jacket or cape, sometimes cut to end at the shoulders or mid-upper-arm, worn over a dress or blouse (a "bolero"-like garment; in knitting patterns, a "baby shrug" or "evening shrug"). The bridge from the gesture is metonymy: the garment is defined by how far it covers — the shoulders — i.e., precisely the part that shrugs. The fashion sense is a cheerful accident of naming and has survived in retail and knitting vocabulary for a century and a half.
  • Older senses — shiver, shudder, shrink. In Middle English the ancestors of the word (shruggen, schruggen) meant "to shudder, shiver, or shrink back": fear and cold, not indifference, produced the movement. That older range survives in the 19th-century dialect and literary use "to shrug at" in the sense of recoiling from something unpleasant, and in the obsolete transitive "to shrug" meaning "to shrug at, despise." The modern shoulder-raise sense appears in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries and gradually displaces the shudder sense entirely.
  • Figurative and journalistic use. "A collective shrug of the shoulders" — of a public that declines to care; "the shrug of indifference"; occasionally a verb of public mood ("voters shrugged at the new tax"). The word's expressive value is so clear that it has become the metaphor of institutional helplessness in modern journalism.

Semantic reasoning — the historical bridge. The chain runs from shuddering to shoulder-raising: the medieval word for a convulsive movement of the body — shrugging with cold or fear — narrowed to the one voluntary, communicative movement the shoulders can make, the raise-and-drop of resignation. The garment sense then attached to the shoulders by location, and "shrug off" attached to the gesture by logic (dismissal through the shoulders). Each extension is metonymic: body part, movement, garment, attitude.

Grammar and distribution. Verb (intransitive; transitive with "shoulders"), noun (count: "a shrug"); strong epitome: "with a shrug." Register: everyday, informal-to-neutral — a working word, not a learned one, used in speech, prose, gesture descriptions, and, in the twenty-first century, as the name of an emoji (the "shrug" 🤷). Related and inflected forms: shrugs, shrugged, shrugging, shrugger (rare). The word has no Latin or Greek relatives in English; its etymology is Germanic and uncertain, and it sits alone in the vocabulary — there is no other English word that names exactly this gesture.
