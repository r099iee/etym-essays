sissy is a noun and adjective denoting, in its dominant modern sense, a boy or man regarded as effeminate, timid, cowardly, or insufficiently masculine, and by extension any person, action, or thing dismissed as feeble, soft, or lacking toughness. As a noun it labels the person ("don't be such a sissy"), as an adjective it modifies behaviour or objects ("a sissy punch", "sissy chores"), and as an insult it polices gender norms rather than describing objective weakness. The force is contemptuous and diminutive, carrying a sneer that the target has failed a code of hardiness, courage, or conventional masculinity.

In contemporary usage the core senses cluster around three poles. First, the gendered sense: an effeminate male, a boy who likes dolls, avoids rough play, cries easily, or shows tenderness, coded as unmanly. Second, the courage sense: a coward, a whiner, someone who complains about pain, avoids fights, or needs protection — applied to any gender in schoolyard and sports talk ("take the sissy route"). Third, the trivialising sense for things: a sissy drink, sissy job, sissy workout, meaning weak, watered-down, or easy. All three share the same evaluative logic: softness as failure, smallness as shame.

Secondary, historical, and regional senses complicate this picture. Originally sissy was simply an affectionate diminutive of sister, used for a sister, a female cousin, or a little girl, and as a familiar address ("come here, sissy"). In parts of the American South and in Black American family speech, sissy survives as a neutral or loving term for a sister. In extended use it can denote an effeminate gay man, though that sense is now widely regarded as homophobic slur rather than description. In institutional slang (prison, military, boarding school) a sissy could historically denote a younger or subordinate male in a feminised role. As an adjective in childcare and nursing, a "sissy" temper once meant merely delicate or sickly, without moral condemnation.

The semantic bridge from affectionate sister to insult is transparent: take a pet-name for a little sister, apply it to a boy, and in a culture that devalues femininity in males the endearment becomes a taunt. The diminutive suffix -y does double work — it makes the word small and cute, then weaponises that smallness. Calling a boy "sister" implies he belongs with the girls, is soft like a baby sister, cries like a girl, and therefore deserves mockery. Feminist linguistics cites sissy alongside pansy, fairy, and mama's boy as evidence of how misogyny and homophobia jointly police boys through feminine comparison.

  • Noun — effeminate or timid male: the primary insult sense; a boy or man who is girlish, soft-spoken, tearful, or averse to rough play, fighting, or risk.
  • Noun — coward in general: anyone who whines, quits, complains, or avoids hardship; schoolyard, locker-room, and military usage without necessary reference to effeminacy.
  • Adjective — feeble or unmanly: describing punches, excuses, drinks, jobs, workouts, or rules as weak, easy, overcautious, or lacking punch.
  • Noun — affectionate sister (archaic/regional): a sister or little girl, especially as a nursery pet-name or Southern familiar address; the original innocent sense.
  • Noun — effeminate gay man (derogatory, dated): a homophobic slur sense, now offensive and avoided in neutral prose; survives in historical texts and bullying speech.
  • Literary nuance: in the source sentence — "Start to look at words just for pleasure and you're a sissy, a poet" — the word is used ironically to expose masculine anti-intellectualism: caring about language is stigmatised as soft, and the poet is reclaimed as the proud sissy.

Register is informal and often offensive. Applied to children it is bullying; applied to adults it is emasculating; reclaimed occasionally in queer or literary contexts with ironic pride, it remains unsafe as neutral description. Plural sissies, comparative sissier, superlative sissiest, adverbial sissily, nominal sissiness all persist in colloquial dictionaries.
