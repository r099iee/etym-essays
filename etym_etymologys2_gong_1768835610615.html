NON-PIE ORIGIN — stated explicitly: gong is not an Indo-European word and has no PIE root. It is an onomatopoeic Austronesian word, an imitation of the instrument's deep, reverberant stroke. Malay gong "a large hanging disc of cast bronze; the court-orchestra instrument" (attested across the Malay archipelago; the instrument and its name are centuries old, with a family of related mainland forms that are ancient areal loans rather than genealogical relatives: Thai ฆ้อง khōng, Khmer គង kong, Burmese gong, and the Javanese/Balinese gong, including the sacred Javanese gong ageng "the great gong" that frames gamelan cycles) → Portuguese gongo (16th century) — the first recorded European form, taken directly from Malay in the accounts of the East Indies → English gong (c. 1600) — recorded in Richard Hakluyt's voyage collections; the word entered with the instrument along the maritime trade routes → English gong (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE/NUANCE: the ultimate origin is straightforwardly onomatopoeic (an imitation of the sound), so no deeper root is sought or required; the only nuance is the exact route of transmission into European languages — via Portuguese accounts or directly through Dutch East India Company (VOC) contact, both of which operated in the 16th–17th centuries — and the status of the mainland Southeast Asian forms as areal loans within a shared metallurgical-musical vocabulary.
