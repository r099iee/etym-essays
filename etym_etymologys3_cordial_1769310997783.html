Cordial is one of the few words whose entire career can be watched across the whole Indo-European family, because its root — the word for the heart — is one of the family's most stable possessions. The story runs from the earliest reconstructed ancestor language to a British supermarket shelf, and every stage of the journey is documented.

I. The PIE heart: *ḱerd-

The Proto-Indo-European word for the heart, reconstructed as *ḱerd-, is attested in nearly every branch of the family, from its oldest documents to its living descendants. Hittite, the oldest recorded Indo-European language, has kēr (genitive kardiyaš); Greek has kardía, which gave us cardiac, cardiology, and electrocardiogram; Sanskrit has hṛd and hṛdaya, surviving in Hindi and Bengali as hṛday; Avestan has zərəd-; Armenian sirt; Latin cor; Gothic haírtō; Old English heorte (our heart); Lithuanian širdis; Old Church Slavonic srĭdĭce (Russian serdce, Polish serce); and Old Irish cride. The root is so stable because it names the one organ every culture knew to be the seat of life, courage, and feeling. From the very beginning, every branch built the same emotional metaphors on the organ: the heart as the seat of love, courage, memory, and truth.

II. Latin cor and the birth of cordiālis

In Latin the inherited *ḱerd- produced cor (genitive cordis), the ordinary word for the heart. Latin built a remarkable family on the heart-as-feeling metaphor: concordia "concord", literally "hearts together"; discordia "discord", literally "hearts apart"; recordārī "to remember", literally "to call back to the heart"; and cor in fixed phrases such as cordī est mihi, "it is dear to my heart". From cor, cordis Latin derived the adjective cordiālis, "of the heart, heartfelt", attested in Late Latin and standard in Medieval Latin with the sense "of the heart; reviving the heart". The derivation is transparent: cor + the adjectival suffix -ālis ("pertaining to"), the same suffix seen in mortalis "mortal", vitalis "vital", legalis "legal".

III. Old French and the medicinal century

The word passed from Latin into Old French as cordial, and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as a term of medicine. In the age before modern pharmacology, physicians believed that certain substances "comforted" or strengthened the heart and vital spirits, and these were called cordials — heart-restoring medicines. The earliest English uses are purely medical: a cordial is a restorative drink, a medicine "agreeable to the heart", a comfort for failing strength. The anatomical literalness is striking: at this stage cordial really is "of the heart" in a physical sense, and the heart, as the engine of life, was the organ a dying patient's medicine had to reach.

IV. The drift from medicine to feeling

From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the adjective drifted from the physical organ to the feelings the organ was believed to house. To be cordial was to be "proceeding from the heart", and so genuine, sincere, heartfelt — applied first to feelings, then to words, and then to manner. The shift is exactly parallel to the drift of the noun heart itself in idioms: "from the bottom of my heart", "heartfelt", "heartwarming". By the seventeenth century cordial had settled into the modern sense of warm, sincere, heartfelt friendliness in manner and relations. It is this sense that Jane Austen deploys with such precision when she writes of Elizabeth Bennet remaining "with no very cordial feelings toward him": the word measures the coldness of withheld heart-warmth against the full standard of the heart, and the negative construction does the damage.

V. The drink: from tonic to liqueur

Alongside the adjective, the noun had its own career. In the fifteenth century cordial already named the restorative medicine as a drink; as sweetening and flavouring were added, the medicinal tonic became a sweet liqueur and, in Britain and Australia, the concentrated fruit syrup sold as "orange cordial" or "lime cordial" to be diluted with water. In American English the noun settled on the sweet liqueur sense. The sweet drink and the warm greeting are therefore etymological siblings: both are "things that do the heart good", one by stirring the organ, the other by stirring the feelings.

VI. Sound changes and form

  • The PIE palatal stop *ḱ developed as k in Latin (cor) but as h in Germanic (heorte, heart) and as s in Slavic and Baltic (srĭdĭce, širdis); Greek kept the k in kardía.
  • Latin cordis (genitive) shows the PIE ablaut form *ḱr̥d- "heart" with a zero-grade vowel; the nominative cor has the full-grade *ḱerd-.
  • The English spelling cordial preserves the Latin stem cord- plus the suffix -ial (from Latin -ālis through French), and stress falls on the first syllable: /ˈkɔːdɪəl/.
  • Compare the cousin family built on the same Latin stem: concord, concordance, discord, cordiality (the noun of the adjective), and, through Greek kardía, cardiac and cardiology.

VII. Register and modern usage

  • In modern English cordial is formal and slightly literary; it is standard in diplomacy ("cordial relations") and in the phrase "cordial welcome", and it remains in everyday British use as the name of a sweet drink.
  • The abstract noun cordiality names the quality of heartfelt warmth; the adverb cordially is a fixed formula for closing formal letters.
  • The word's double survival — a feeling and a drink, both "of the heart" — makes it a small museum of the ancient metaphor that located emotion in the organ that pumps the blood.
