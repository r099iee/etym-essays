- 16th century - English borrows 'bog' from Irish 'bogach' (soft ground), from Irish 'bog' (soft); it arrives as the characteristic word of the Irish landscape — quaking, sodden, treacherous ground.
- 17th-18th century - 'bog' generalizes to all soft, waterlogged, spongy ground; it enters proverbial English as the type of deceptive footing — something that looks firm and swallows whatever steps on it.
- 19th century - with bog-iron, bog-oak, and the new science of peatlands, the word gains ecological weight; writers of Irish Gothic and English landscape poetry mine its menace and its strange beauty of vivid mosses and bright flowers.
- Contemporary - 'bogs' remains the standard word for acidic, mossy, peat-forming wetlands; its deceptive-crust sense still makes it the natural metaphor for any danger that looks inviting, and it reads as quieter and more treacherous than swamp or marsh.
