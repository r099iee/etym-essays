AUGUSTUS is a Latin proper name and title borrowed directly into English. The chain runs from a PIE verbal root to the Latin honorific and then into English. - PIE *h2ewg- "to increase, to grow" (reconstructed root; attested in the family of Latin "augeo", Greek "auxano" "to increase", and Germanic words for "wax, grow") to Latin "augeo, augere" "to increase, enlarge, enrich" (verb, attested from the 3rd c. BCE). - to Latin "augustus" (adjective) "consecrated, venerable, majestic, increased with divine favour" (derived from the root of "augeo" / "augur" with the suffix -tus; attested from the 2nd-1st c. BCE, e.g. in the sense of sacred things and, pre-eminently, as a religious epithet). The semantic path runs through augural language: what is "august" is what the gods have blessed and increased. - to Latin "Augustus" (cognomen / title) "the Venerable, the Consecrated One" - formally conferred on Gaius Octavius by the Roman Senate on 16 January 27 BC, and thereafter borne by all Roman emperors (feminine "Augusta" for empresses); attested in inscriptions and literature from 27 BC onward. - to English "Augustus" "the first Roman emperor; the imperial title" (borrowed directly from Latin in historical writing; attested in English from the 16th c., e.g. in Tudor histories of Rome) to AUGUSTUS (modern English form). DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The connection between "augustus" and "augeo" ("to increase") on the one hand and "augur" ("divine augury") on the other is a classic crux. Most modern scholarship (following de Vaan and others) derives the whole cluster - "augeo", "augur", "augustus", "auctor", "auctoritas" - from the single root *h2ewg- "to increase, grow", with the religious sense arising from the notion of divinely increased power. The ancients themselves, however, explained "augustus" from "augur" and from "augeo" interchangeably, and the exact derivational route (whether the adjective comes directly from the verb or through augural language) remains debated. The English adjective "august" (with stress on the first syllable) is a separate, later borrowing of the same Latin adjective, distinct from the proper name.
