- Medieval English spenne / spinney - a thorn hedge or thorny thicket - the word is practical, dense and defensive, describing boundary vegetation
- Sixteenth century - “a small wood or copse” becomes the dominant sense - the thorny origin fades and the word settles into rural landscape description
- Seventeenth to eighteenth centuries - estate maps and game management use spinneys as coverts - the word acquires a faint association with hunting and landed estates
- Nineteenth century - nature writing and dialect literature adopt it - “spinney” becomes a soft, nostalgic, southern English ruralism
- Twentieth century - conservationists and planners value small woods as wildlife corridors - the word gains an ecological, heritage flavour
- Twenty-first century - “spinney” survives mainly in place names, nature writing and British English - it now signals a small, cherished, slightly old-fashioned patch of woodland
