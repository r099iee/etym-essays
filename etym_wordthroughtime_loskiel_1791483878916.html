- 1740–1765, Courland - the name belongs to a Lutheran pastor's son in the Baltic German clergy; it is local, respectable and wholly obscure.
- 1765–1785, Livonia - after ordination the name circulates in Moravian congregational correspondence as a promising young preacher and administrator.
- 1789, Leipzig - the publication of the mission history turns the surname into an author's name; “Loskiel” now means a book as much as a man.
- 1794, London - Latrobe's translation gives the name its English spelling and citation form, and British readers begin to quote “Loskiel” as an authority on Indian missions.
- 1802–1811, Pennsylvania - as presiding bishop the name carries ecclesiastical weight in the American province; it is spoken in synods and visitation reports.
- 1814–1880s - after his death the name settles into the footnote: a standard source cited by historians of missions and of the American frontier.
- 1880s–1940s - Sumner and other social theorists lift isolated sentences from the history into arguments about race, industry and social evolution, giving “Loskiel” a second life as a witness for theories he never contemplated.
- 1950s–1990s - ethnohistorians and Moravian archivists return to the book as a documentary treasury, and the name is revalued as a source to be decoded rather than a verdict to be trusted.
- 21st century - postcolonial scholarship reads Loskiel's page as evidence of missionary assumptions about labour and property; the name is now a case study in the politics of the colonial archive.
