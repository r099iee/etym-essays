One word, two histories. No word in the English lexicon better illustrates the accidental convergence of language than "shanty," and its plural "shanties" is a standing monument to homonymy: two completely unrelated words — one born in a French-Canadian lumber camp, the other on the deck of a 19th-century sailing ship — now wear the same spelling, sound the same, and are told apart only by context. The two biographies deserve to be told separately before their merger is understood. The hut: from a French yard to a North American camp. The oldest sense, the crude hut, begins in medieval France. Old French chantier meant a wooden support, a stack of beams, a log-yard, or a building site — the word descending from Latin canthērius, "a gelding," which by a striking technical metaphor also named a beam or rafter, the wooden "horse" of the builder's frame. (The deepest origin of canthērius is itself debated: a traditional derivation connects it with Greek kanthḗlios, "a pack-ass, beast of burden," but modern scholars are cautious, and the Latin word's ultimate source remains uncertain.) In France, chantier developed the sense "workplace, building yard," and when French settlers and voyageurs carried the word to Canada, the building trade's "yard" became the logger's world: the Canadian French chantier came to mean a lumber camp — and the rough log hut where the lumberjacks lived and slept. In the early 19th century, as American and Canadian lumbering boomed, English borrowed the word: "shanty," first recorded in the 1820s, meant the lumberjack's cabin, and by extension any crude, makeshift dwelling, the shack of the frontier, the squatter, and the urban poor. From it grew the compound "shantytown," the settlement of scrap-built huts on the margins of the industrial city, and the word became a sociological fixture of the modern world, describing the informal housing of the displaced and the migrant worker from the nineteenth-century slums to the megacity shantytowns of today. The song: from the Indo-European root of singing to the sea. The musical sense is entirely independent, and vastly older at its root. It reaches back to the Indo-European verb *kan-, "to sing," one of the family's great words of voice and song, whose descendants ring across Europe: Latin canere "to sing" (with carmen "song," cantāre "to keep singing," and incantāre "to sing a spell," source of "enchant"), Old Irish canid "sings," and Welsh canu "to sing." From Latin cantāre descended the Romance verbs of singing — French chanter, Italian cantare, Spanish cantar — and in Gallo-Romance the shift of Latin c before a to ch gave Old French chanter, "to sing." The word reached English by a distinctly nautical door. In the mid-19th century, as the great age of sail reached its peak, American sailors and writers borrowed a word for the labour-songs of the forecastle, the rhythmic call-and-response songs that timed the heave of the halyard and the walk round the capstan; the English form was taken directly from the French chanter, "to sing," spelled at first chanty and chantey, and recorded from the 1850s–1860s. The song itself — the work-song of the merchant sailor, led by a solo shantyman answered by the crew's chorus — was one of the great oral traditions of the English-speaking sea, carried from ship to ship until the coming of steam made the long pull obsolete, and preserved by the folk revival of the 20th century. The merger: how the hut took over the song. The two words met on the printed page. Throughout the later 19th century, "chantey" and "chanty" competed for the song sense while "shanty" carried the hut sense, but the two worlds — the logger's camp and the sailor's deck, both full of rough working men, both given to song and both already connected by the transatlantic timber trade — kept the words in accidental proximity. Under the pull of the identical-sounding hut word, the spelling "shanty" began to appear for the song as well, and by the early 20th century "sea shanty" had become the standard form, so that the plural "shanties" now refers equally to crude huts and to sailors' songs, the meaning determined purely by context. The OED and the handbooks treat the two senses as homonyms: not variants of one word, but two words that have converged, in the way that English "ear" (of the body) and "ear" (of corn) have merged from unrelated roots. Modern life and the revival of the song. In the 20th century the two senses had very different careers. The hut sense went sociological and historical: "shantytown" became a global term for informal settlements, while the single "shanty" survives as a nostalgic or journalistic word for the rough dwelling of lumberjack, squatter, or slum-dweller. The song sense, meanwhile, was reborn. The folk-song revival — from the collecting of the late 19th century through the recordings and festivals of the mid-20th century — turned the sea shanty from a vanished work-song into a beloved folk genre; "Blow the Man Down," "Shenandoah," and "What Shall We Do with the Drunken Sailor?" became classics of the folk repertoire. The revival reached a spectacular popular peak in 2021, when a Scottish postman's recording of the New Zealand whaling shanty "The Wellerman" went viral across the internet, and the word "shanty" entered the mainstream vocabulary of a generation that had never been near a sailing ship. The plural "shanties" thus spans the full social range of the English language — from the scrap-built hut of the urban poor to the most beloved songs of the maritime folk tradition — a single form holding together two entirely different histories, joined only by the accident of spelling.
