Mooting is the present participle of the verb moot in its "propose for discussion" sense: to raise, bring forward, suggest, or float an idea, plan, or scheme so that it can be debated, considered, or tested. In the source sentence — "you are mooting precisely the same drivelling scheme" — a speaker accuses an interlocutor of reviving a foolish proposal for fresh debate, as if putting it on the meeting table again. The tone is dismissive: to moot something here is not to enact it but to utter it as a candidate for talk, and the speaker implies it does not even deserve that.

The verb's peculiar flavor comes from its origin in the Old English gemōt, an assembly or moot where freemen gathered to debate and judge. To moot a point was originally to submit it to such an assembly for argument — hence the intimate link with discussion rather than decision. Modern uses preserve that consultative core across domains. A colleague moots a date for a meeting; a think-tank moots reform; a journalist reports that a tax rise "is being mooted"; planners moot a new road. In each case the proposal is provisional, open to objection, and not yet adopted. Saying "we're just mooting ideas" explicitly disclaims commitment.

Crucially, moot carries two famous, apparently opposite legalOx shades that learners must reconcile. In British English a moot point is one that is debatable, unresolved, and still open to argument. In American legal English a case that is moot (or mooted) is one that is no longer worth arguing because circumstances have resolved it — "the issue is moot" means it is academic, hypothetical, of no practical importance. Both derive from the same assembly practice: points submitted to a moot (student debate court) are by definition arguable yet without real-world effect, hence simultaneously "worth debating" and "of no consequence." Mooting as activity also names the student practice itself: Oxford, Harvard, and Inns of Court moots / mooting competitions are mock trials where law students argue hypothetical cases.

  • Proposing for discussion (to moot an idea/plan): the primary contemporary sense. Example: "The committee mooted a merger with the northern branch." Bridge: submitting a matter to the ancient moot-assembly generalizes to submitting any matter to any discussion.
  • Suggesting tentatively / floating (journalistic): a softer, media-favored nuance. Example: "A June election is being mooted in Westminster." Here mooting signals unattributed, trial-balloon leaking: someone wants the idea discussed without owning it.
  • Debating as exercise (mooting / moot court): law-school sense — to argue a hypothetical case for training. Example: "She spends weekends mooting at the Inns of Court." The assembly becomes a classroom; discussion becomes pedagogy.
  • Rendering academic / depriving of practical effect (American to moot): to make a question moot by resolving the underlying dispute. Example: "The settlement mooted the appeal." The bridge is that a point decided elsewhere is fit only for student debate, not real judgment.
  • Calling into question / disputing (to moot a scheme pejoratively): as in the source, to utter a proposal so as to expose it to contempt. Example: "He mooted the same drivelling scheme again." The verb frames the proposal as mere talk, undercutting its pretensions to action.

Register is formal-journalistic and committee-like; friends brainstorming over coffee rarely say "let me moot something" except jocularly. Collocations: moot an idea / proposal / plan / suggestion / possibility / reform / candidacy; widely / reportedly mooted; mooting competition / mooting society. The progressive mooting emphasizes the ongoing, deliberative process — the talking itself — which is why the source's "you are mooting" stings: it accuses the target of doing nothing but talking the same nonsense again.
