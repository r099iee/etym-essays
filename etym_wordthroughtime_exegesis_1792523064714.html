- [5th–4th c. BCE Classical Athens] — Civic and sacred function performed by an exēgētēs, an officially designated sacred interpreter or expounder of unwritten laws, religious taboos, ancestral omens, and sacrificial purification rites — [perception: authoritative, divinely sanctioned civic guidance on sacred tradition and state religion]
- [1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE Hellenistic Alexandria & Early Christianity] — Formalized into literary and theological exposition by Jewish scholars (such as Philo of Alexandria) and Christian Church Fathers (Origen, Clement), who developed allegorical, typological, and literal textual interpretation of sacred scriptures — [perception: revered scholarly art of unlocking esoteric divine wisdom concealed beneath literal words]
- [16th–17th c. Renaissance & Protestant Reformation] — Reinvigorated by humanist philologists (Desiderius Erasmus) and Protestant reformers (Martin Luther, John Calvin), who rejected medieval scholastic allegories in favor of rigorous Greek and Hebrew historical-grammatical exegesis based on sola scriptura and ad fontes — [perception: paramount intellectual weapon of religious liberation and theological truth]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & German Higher Criticism] — Secularized and elevated into a rigorous academic science across German universities (Friedrich Schleiermacher, Julius Wellhausen), subjecting sacred scriptures to objective historical, literary, and source criticism — [perception: objective, intellectually formidable academic discipline separating historical fact from mythological embroidery]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global Discourse] — Expanded far beyond theological boundaries into constitutional jurisprudence (statutory exegesis), literary critical theory (close reading), philosophy, and comparative religious studies (such as Vedic Mīmāṃsā exegesis) — [perception: quintessential term for meticulous, rigorous textual analysis and hermeneutic rigor]
