boles is the plural of bole, a forestry and literary noun meaning the main stem or trunk of a tree — the thick upright column from ground-roots to first major branches, stripped in imagination of leaves, twigs, and crown. To speak of boles thirty or forty feet through, tossed and shattered is to evoke giants: sequoia-like shafts wider than houses, now felled and broken like columns after an earthquake. Bole stresses mass, timber-value, and monumentality; it is the tree as pillar and log, not as leafy organism.

The distinction from near-synonyms matters. Trunk is the everyday word for the same object; bole is its loftier, woodman's, poet's counterpart, favouring great girth and clean length. Stem suits saplings and plants; stock suits grafting; log is a bole after felling and cutting; timber is boles as commodity. A forester cruises boles for board-feet; a poet leans against a bole for shade; a geologist reads boles for rings. In all, bole frames the tree as body-torso: roots are feet, bole is torso, crown is head.

  • Forestry-timber core: merchantable bole, clear bole, bole length, bole diameter. Means the usable straight section yielding planks. Boles thirty feet through is hyperbolic-old-growth: real redwoods reach 20-30 feet diameter; forty feet belongs to myth or alien worlds. Your shattered boles imply cataclysm — storm, battle, or giants — that snapped even titans.
  • Literary-landscape use: mossy boles, gnarled boles, cathedral boles. Favoured by Hardy, Tolkien, Le Guin for ancient woods. Conveys age, endurance, verticality. To walk among boles is to walk among pillars; light filters between them as in a nave.
  • Horticultural nuance: bole height (grafting roses and standards): the clear stem before head branches. Standard roses on three-foot boles. Technical-pruning sense, neat and measured.
  • Plural force: boles almost always appears plural in description because forests are collectives — boles rose around, boles lay tossed. Singular bole isolates one pillar for leaning, hiding, measuring.
  • Homograph warning — bole (earth/pigment): an entirely different word spelled identically: red bole, Armenian bole, a clay pigment used in gilding and medicine, from Latin bolus lump, from Greek bolos. Pronounced same /bəʊl/, but etymologically unrelated to tree-bole from Old Norse. Dictionaries number them bole¹ (trunk) vs bole² (earth). Your context is unambiguously bole¹: trees have boles only in trunk sense; earth-boles cannot be thirty feet through and shattered.
  • Idioms and compounds: bole rot (decay of trunk heart), bole scar, bole shoot. No common idiom like bark up; bole stays literal-descriptive, lending gravity when used.
  • Grammar: countable noun: a bole, two boles, the boles. Pronounced /bəʊl/ rhyming with hole, pole, soul; plural /bəʊlz/ with voiced s to z. Often collocates with adjectives of size and age — massive, straight, towering, fallen, shattered — and prepositions — between the boles, against a bole, through the boles.

Ecologically, the bole is transport and support: xylem lifts water, phloem distributes sugar, bark protects, cambium grows outward into rings. Culturally, giant boles mark wonder and vulnerability at once: wonder that wood can stand so vast, awe that even it can lie tossed and shattered. That is your sentence's work — to make the reader feel scale by breaking scale.
