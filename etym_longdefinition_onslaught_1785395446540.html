ONSLAUGHT is a fierce, violent, and overwhelming attack — an assault so heavy and sustained that it presses forward with crushing force and leaves the victim no room to do anything but shelter. The word concentrates on the weight and momentum of the aggression itself rather than on the identity of the attackers: an onslaught of armies, of artillery, of criticism, of advertising, or of falling rocks all have the same quality of an unstoppable mass bearing down. It is rarely used as a verb; its register is formal-to-literary, martial in origin, and it still carries the full weight of its violent etymology. PRIMARY SENSES
  • A fierce or destructive attack — the military sense, of troops, cavalry, artillery, or weapons pressing forward with crushing force; the oldest and core sense.
  • A heavy, sustained assault of any force — an onslaught of criticism, of advertising, of email, of paperwork: the aggressor may be anything at all, and the victim is merely swept along.
  • A natural force — an onslaught of wind, rain, fire, or falling rock: impersonal violence from the elements, pressing down upon a landscape or a people.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED USES
  • Figurative and rhetorical — "an onslaught of questions", "an onslaught of offers", "an onslaught of new information": an overwhelming quantity pressing in upon one; the word keeps its sense of mass and inevitability even when the "attack" is harmless.
  • Editorial and advertising jargon — onslaught is used of marketing campaigns and media coverage that flood a market or an audience, e.g. "an onslaught of holiday advertising", "a pre-Christmas media onslaught".
  • Informal hyperbole — "the onslaught of the commute", "the onslaught of Christmas shoppers", "the January onslaught of bills": applied with comic or weary exaggeration to ordinary pressures, always with the image of an overwhelming mass.
  • Sports and competitive use — an onslaught of goals, an onslaught of attacks on the goal: the word borrowed into match commentary for a period of unbroken pressure.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES Onslaught was borrowed into English in the 17th century from Dutch aanslag, "a striking upon; an attack, onset, assault", itself built from aan "on" + slag "a blow, stroke". The image is of a blow struck upon something, and the Dutch word also meant a levy or tax assessment (a "striking on" of the roll), a sense English did not take. From the strictly military beginning the word widened in the 18th–19th centuries to storms and the elements, and in the 20th–21st centuries to fully metaphorical use. The spelling was reshaped under the pull of the unrelated word slaughter, giving the modern -aught ending — a curious, satisfying echo, since slaughter comes from the same ultimate root by a different route. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In the landslide episode of The Hobbit the falling rocks become an onslaught: the mountain-side, set in motion by the stone-giants quarrelling above, charges down upon the company like an enemy formation, and the boulders bound and spin among bracken and pine-roots far below. The choice of word is deliberately martial — it frames a natural accident as a deliberate assault, endowing the mountain itself with hostile intent and deepening the menace of the whole episode: the company is not merely caught in a rockslide; it is under attack. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Attack — the general word for any aggressive action, whether a blow, a campaign, or a piece of criticism; onslaught insists on mass and momentum rather than a single blow.
  • Assault — a direct, usually physical, attack upon a person or position; assault is more formal and legal, onslaught more violent and overwhelming.
  • Charge — a headlong onrush by troops, cavalry, or animals; a charge is forward motion into contact, while an onslaught is the whole crushing weight pressing in.
  • Barrage / bombardment — concentrated, sustained artillery or missile fire, continuous rather than breaking forward; a barrage is specifically a curtain of fire, while an onslaught may be of anything.
  • Blitz — from German Blitz "lightning", stressing suddenness and rapidity; a blitz is a swift, intense campaign, while an onslaught stresses overwhelming mass.
  • Raid — a sudden, brief incursion for a limited purpose; an onslaught is heavy, sustained, and destructive.
  • Onset — an attack or the beginning of something unpleasant; older and more formal, and now often medical or natural (onset of winter), while onslaught remains aggressive and violent.
GRAMMATICAL AND FORMAL NOTES Onslaught is a countable noun, typically in the singular with "an onslaught of X". It is almost never a verb (unlike attack), and it does not make an agent noun. Its power comes from its concentration on mass and momentum: an onslaught is not a single blow but an overwhelming, unstoppable press of force, which is exactly how the falling rocks of the mountain-side bear down upon the company.
