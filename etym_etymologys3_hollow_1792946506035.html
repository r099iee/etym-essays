The history of hollow is the history of a cupped hand of earth. It begins with Germanic speakers naming what the land does when it sags inward, and it ends with modern headline writers mocking promises that echo because nothing fills them. Between those points the word never leaves the image of the bowl: every sound-shift keeps the round vowel, every spelling keeps the soft ghost of the hole it came from, and every new sense keeps the emptiness at the centre.

From concealment to cavity: The deepest proposed root is PIE *kel-, to cover or conceal, the root that also gives cell, cellar, helmet, and hall in various branches: all things that cover, hide, or enclose. Germanic narrowed the covering into the covered place itself, the den or pit that the earth hides within itself. Old English holh already shows that settlement: a hollow place, a hole, a water-worn dip, recorded in charters for boundary dips and in glosses for burrows. The vowel alternation with hol, hole, is ancient ablaut of the same base, the noun pair dividing labour so that hole pierces and hollow cups.

Old and Middle English consolidation:

  • Old English holh (9th–11th c.): a concrete landform word in a farming and hunting vocabulary, the dip where water stands, where beasts lie, where fugitives hide. Charters use it to walk the bounds of estates from hollow to hedge to stream.
  • Early Middle English (12th–13th c.): spelling loosens toward holewe and holowe under French scribal habits, and the adjective use hardens: hollow trees, hollow vessels, hollow ground. The Katherine Group and bestiaries moralise it early, contrasting hollow show with solid worth.
  • Late Middle English (14th–15th c.): Chaucer and Langland use it for both terrain and trickery: hollow hills hide hermits, hollow words hide intent. The verb to holowen, to scoop out, appears for carpenters hollowing canoes and masons hollowing fonts.

Sound change and spelling: The Middle English final -owe simplifies to modern -ow, pronounced with the low rounded vowel that still sounds like cupped air. The initial h survives intact, never lost as in some dialects, because the word stayed common in speech. The double ll stabilises early, distinguishing the adjective from the noun hole on the page even as the ear keeps them cousins.

Semantic drift into morals and music:

  • Acoustic sense: empty vessels boom, so by the 1300s hollow describes reverberant sound; bell-founders, then musicians and phoneticians, keep it for tones that seem to roll inside a larger space than the instrument.
  • Moral sense: Reformation preaching makes hollow hearts and hollow faith stock phrases: England hears sermons against hollow profession, Shakespeare gives us hollow crowns and hollow friendship, and the 18th c. adds hollow victory and hollow applause.
  • Bodily sense: physicians describe hollow eyes, hollow cheeks, and hollow coughs, hunger and illness read as landscape on the face, a usage the novel cements from Defoe through Dickens to Tolkien's own famished travellers.
  • Literary landscape: Romantic poets restore affection to the landform: Wordsworthian hollows shelter cottages, Hardy hollows hide trysts, and Tolkien inherits the kindly hollow as walkable Shire-like terrain just beyond the Hedge, homely yet edged with strangeness.

Modern range: Today hollow lives comfortably in three registers at once: the walker map-word for dips and combes, the workshop word for scooped and echoing things, and the critic word for empty talk. Its durability comes from that triple grip on hand, ear, and conscience: you can stand in a hollow, tap a hollow, and distrust a hollow promise without ever feeling you have changed words, only tilted the same cupped emptiness toward ground, object, and soul.
