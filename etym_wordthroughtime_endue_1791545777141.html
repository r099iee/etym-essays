- Late 14th c. — Entered Middle English via Old French; exhibited an intricate blend of senses ranging from providing an estate (confused with endow) to physically clothing oneself (from Latin induere) and physiological digestion.
- 15th c. — Increasingly employed in devotional texts and early legal instruments to describe being invested with spiritual qualities, rights, or virtuous dispositions.
- 16th c. — Canonized in the highest registers of Tudor religious English through the Book of Common Prayer (1549/1552) and Protestant biblical translations; perceived as an exalted liturgical verb for divine empowerment.
- 17th c. — Flourished in Renaissance literature and epic poetry (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton); understood as the ideal term for souls equipped by God with reason, celestial dignity, and moral grace.
- 18th–19th c. — Surrendered common institutional and legal ground to endow; relegated almost exclusively to church liturgy, theological discourse, and deliberate poetic archaism.
- 20th–21st c. — Rare and archaic in daily vernacular; preserved almost entirely in ecclesiastical recitation (the Anglican Litany, Luke 24:49 in the King James Bible) and stylistic historical prose.
