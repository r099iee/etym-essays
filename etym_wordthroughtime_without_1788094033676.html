- Old English "wiþūtan": purely spatial — "on the outside, outside of" (the enemy camped without the city walls)
- By c. 1300: privative extension — "not having, in the absence of" (without money, without doubt) — what lies outside one's possession is by definition lacking
- 15th–17th c.: the spatial sense retreats into poetry and fixed phrases ("within and without", "without doors") while "outside" seizes the spatial job
- 18th–21st c.: purely privative in ordinary speech; survives as adverb in finance ("without dividend"), legal formulas ("without prejudice"), and idioms ("do without", "go without", "without fail")
- Today: the default, neutral word for absence; its spatial ancestry surfaces only in archaism and fixed expressions.
