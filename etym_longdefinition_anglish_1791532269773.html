Two words in one. Anglish is best understood as a double-stranded word. Historically it is an archaic spelling and variant of English, formed from the name of the Angles rather than from the Saxons, and it is attested in Middle English and early modern English as a by-form of the language's name. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries it has been revived as the name of a linguistic register or movement: English written with native Germanic words wherever possible and with Latin, French and Greek borrowings replaced by compounds, revivals or calques.

The historical sense: a name for English. Old English Englisc means "of the Angles" and, by extension, "the language of the Angles." The stem is Engle, the Angles, plus the adjectival suffix -isc, which becomes modern -ish. Because the Angles gave the whole speech community its name, the language was Englisc even though the Saxons were politically dominant in much of England. Middle English spellings include Engelish, Englisch, Inglish and Anglish; the last reflects the a-vocalism of the tribal name Angli and survives as a minority spelling into the modern period.

The context of the card. The sentence in the card explains that "our language took its name ... from that of one of the tribes." The tribe in question is the Angles, and the standard modern answer is English. Anglish is the same name in an older or revived spelling; it makes the derivation from the Angles visible in a way that the standard form obscures.

The modern movement. Since the mid-twentieth century, writers and hobbyists have used Anglish for a kind of English purged of non-Germanic vocabulary. The movement has no single founder or academy, but it has produced a body of practice:

  • Vocabulary replacement: Latin and French words are replaced by native equivalents — translation becomes oversetting, atom becomes uncleft, grammar becomes speechcraft, science becomes witship or knowledge.
  • Revivals: obsolete Old and Middle English words are brought back, such as yclept, wight or dialect terms.
  • Calques: loan translations of classical compounds, so that television might become far-sight and telephone far-sounder.
  • Register: the result is often deliberately plain, alliterative and Anglo-Saxon in rhythm, echoing the prose of the Authorized Version or of William Morris.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • The Angles' language: in antiquarian writing "Anglish" can mean the speech of the Angles, or Anglian, the dialect of the Anglian kingdoms (Mercia and Northumbria) as opposed to West Saxon.
  • Archaic English generally: in literary pastiche, "Anglish" evokes a pre-Conquest or medieval Englishness, and is used for flavour rather than precision.
  • Purist ideology: the modern sense carries a stance — that native vocabulary is clearer, more honest or more authentically English — and is therefore a word with an argument built into it.
  • Conlang and world-building: "Anglish" is used in constructed-language circles for a Germanic-only register of English, and appears in alternate-history fiction.
  • Internet culture: the Anglish wiki and discussion forums have made the word a small online community label, with style guides and word-lists.

Semantic bridges. The path runs from a tribal name to a language name to an ideology of language. Angli names a people; Englisc names their speech; the archaic spelling Anglish keeps the tribal connection visible; and the modern movement turns that visibility into a programme, treating the pre-1066 Germanic layer as the "true" English and the Norman-French layer as an intrusion. The word thus moves from description to prescription, which is why it now names a style as much as a language.

Technical notes. In linguistics the neutral term for the native Germanic element in English is "native" or "Germanic" vocabulary; "Anglish" is a movement label, not a technical classification. The related term "Anglian" is the proper dialectological adjective for the Anglian dialects of Old English. "Anglo-Saxon" is the older name for the Old English language and period. None of these is interchangeable with the modern sense of Anglish.
