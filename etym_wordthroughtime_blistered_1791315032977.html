- c. 1300–1500s: Attested in Middle English as a somatic noun and verb referring strictly to painful, fluid-filled elevations of the skin caused by burns, plague, or chafing. - Perceived with dread as a symptom of physical suffering, pestilence, or severe labor.
- 1600s–1700s: Extended figuratively into dramatic literature (Shakespeare) to describe caustic speech, slanderous attacks that defile a good name, or moral contamination. - Perceived as a visceral metaphor for stinging shame and disfiguring censure.
- 1750s–1880s: Adopted into industrial metallurgy and building construction to describe 'blister steel' (cementation steel with gas bubbles) and defective, bubbled paint or varnish. - Perceived as a standard technical and manufacturing descriptor for material flaw or specialized heat-treatment.
- Early 20th Century: Popularized in British humorous prose (notably P.G. Wodehouse) as a comic adjective denoting someone looking thoroughly lovesick, emotionally scorched, haggard, and martyred. - Perceived as an amusing, hyperbolic description of social and romantic dejection.
- Late 20th–21st Century: Expanded into high-end culinary terminology for charred, flash-seared vegetables (blistered shishitos, blistered tomatoes) while retaining its foundational medical and materials science definitions. - Perceived simultaneously as a sophisticated culinary badge of flavor and a universal physical ailment.
