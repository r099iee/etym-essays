- c.1300–1500s: bringing to peace — to appease means to reconcile, to restore the king's peace, to pacify wrath; perception is chivalric and legal, honourable peacemaking.
- 1500s–1700s: religious and personal satisfaction — appeasement of God, conscience, hunger; the Geneva Bible and devotional prose use it for atonement; perception is solemn, theological, the soul settled.
- 1800s–1937: respectable diplomacy — appeasement is prudent statecraft, easing tensions by redressing grievances; The Times and parliamentary debate use it positively; perception is wise, liberal, civilised.
- 1938–1945: Munich reversal — Chamberlain's appeasement of Hitler turns the word into an accusation of cowardice and blindness; cartoons, Churchill's speeches, and post-war histories fix it as shameful surrender; perception becomes contemptuous, a warning label never to be self-applied.
- 1950s–present: split register — in politics appeasement remains toxic insult; in everyday life and ethology it recovers neutrality as calming or submissive behaviour; perception depends entirely on domain, from insult to clinical term.
