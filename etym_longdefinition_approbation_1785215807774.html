approbation is a noun meaning warm or formal approval; commendation, praise, sanction, or authoritative endorsement of a person, an action, or a work. Etymologically the word means literally "having been proved good": it belongs to the family of Latin probāre "to test, try, prove" (whence English prove, proof, probe, probation), and it therefore carries — even in modern use — a residual flavour of judgement after testing, of approval that has been earned and verified rather than casually granted. It is the most dignified and impersonal member of the approval family: to grant approbation is to pass a considered verdict of worth.

In the dedication to On Liberty (1859), John Stuart Mill sets approbation as the exact counterpart of incitement: Harriet Taylor's "exalted sense of truth and right" was his strongest incitement, "and whose approbation was my chief reward". The sentence declares her moral-intellectual approval the highest and most precious return for all his labours — a judgement of worth rendered by the one person whose sense of truth he trusted absolutely. The word here carries the full weight of solemn, considered, morally grounded approval, not mere liking or casual assent; it is the approval of a judge whose verdict the author valued above fame, wealth, or public success.

Across its six-century history in English, the noun has developed a range of senses and registers:

  • Formal or warm approval (primary sense) — the favourable judgement of a person, deed, or work by a respected authority: "with the approbation of the council", "to receive the approbation of one's peers".
  • Sanction or authoritative endorsement — official or institutional approval that confers legitimacy: an act done "with the approbation of the sovereign", or the approbation required of a licensing body.
  • The tested, "proved good" sense — the oldest flavour, from Latin probāre: approval given only after trial or examination, as of a candidate, a design, or a piece of work that has been put to the proof. This is what distinguishes approbation from mere liking.
  • The theological and moral sense — divine approbation, or the approval of one's conscience; prominent in 17th–19th-century religious prose, where the "approbation of God" or "of a good conscience" is the highest sanction.
  • The philosophical sense — in moral philosophy (especially 18th-century British moralists such as Hutcheson, Hume, and Smith), "approbation" names the sentiment by which we approve or disapprove of actions and characters; Hume writes of the "sentiments of approbation" awakened by virtue. This is a technical, systematic use of the word.
  • Parliamentary and legal usage — formal assent recorded in deliberative bodies: an act "read and passed with approbation", or the approbation of a court. In some historical legal contexts it could also mean the trial of a thing's goodness or worth.

Historical semantic bridge. All these senses descend from a single Latin path: probāre "to test, to deem good" → approbāre (with the prefix ad- "to, towards") "to assent to, regard as good" → approbātiō "approval, sanction". The bridge is the ancient Roman idea that approval is the verdict of a test: you can only truly approve what has been proved. English inherited this tested flavour through Old French approbacion in the 14th century, and it has never fully lost it — which is why the word still sounds weightier, more judicial, and more impersonal than plain approval.

Boundaries, collocations, and register. Approbation differs from approval (the everyday, less formal term for a favourable opinion), from commendation (spoken or written praise of excellence, often ceremonial), from sanction (authoritative, often legal permission), and from endorsement (in modern use, public, often commercial or political backing). It collocates with with and of ("with the approbation of", "the approbation of the critics"), with verbs like receive, win, gain, seek, give, grant. In register it has retreated from ordinary speech since the early twentieth century: plain approval now dominates everyday use, while approbation survives in formal, academic, and slightly archaic registers — exactly where its tested, weighty connotation is prized. Its antonym is disapprobation (formal disapproval, also from the same root).
