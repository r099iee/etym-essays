PIE: *sed- "to sit" (also the source of Latin sedēre, Greek hézomai, Sanskrit sīdati) — the root is securely attested across the family.
Proto-Germanic: *sitjaną "to sit" — from *sed- via Germanic *s- umlaut of the root vowel (a/e-grade), and with gemination in the present stem.
Old English (c. 700–1100): sittan "to sit" — a strong verb of class 5, with past singular sæt and past plural sǣton; the second-person singular past was sǣte (stem sæt + ending -e).
Middle English (c. 1150–1500): sat / satte "sat," with the new second-person singular past sat(te)st or satst formed by adding the generalized -st ending after the loss of final -e.
Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700): satst "thou sat" — used in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton.
Modern English (archaic): satst — survives only in archaizing or scriptural diction; standard English uses sat for all persons.
NOTE ON DETAIL: The Old English second-person singular past sǣte carried the old -e ending; the -st ending that produces satst was generalized from the present tense and from the preterite-present verbs during Middle English. The spelling satst (vs. satest) reflects the contracted, non-syllabic form preferred when metre or prose rhythm allowed it.
