- Latin to Old French (c. 1st c. BCE–12th c. CE) - [Vocal Crying & Ritual Mourning] - Latin *lāmentārī* described loud wailing, ritual weeping, and weeping songs at Roman funerals.
- Middle English (late 14th–15th c.) - [Passionate Vocal Expression of Grief] - Entered English via French to denote vocal cries of agony, public mourning for fallen leaders, and liturgical sorrow.
- Early Modern / Renaissance (16th–17th c.) - [Literary Elegies & Tragic Reflection] - Broadened from raw weeping into structured poetic laments, tragic soliloquies (Shakespeare, Spenser), and biblically inspired mourning.
- 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment to Victorian) - [Elegiac Adjective of Honor & Nostalgic Regret] - Solidified as a reverent participial adjective (*"our late lamented friend"*) and an expression of philosophical regret for lost eras or youth.
- 20th–21st Century (Contemporary) - [Formal Mourning & Critical Expression of Loss] - Employed in formal obituaries, political commentary, and literature to denote deep regret over vanished traditions, extinct species, or missed opportunities.
