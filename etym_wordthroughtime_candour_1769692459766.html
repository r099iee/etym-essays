- [16th century] - "Candour" is borrowed from Latin candor ("brilliant whiteness"), retaining a literal sense of dazzling brightness and, by extension, spotless purity.
- [17th century] - The word migrates from light to character: candour becomes moral whiteness — fairness, freedom from prejudice, and an unclouded, impartial judgment.
- [18th century] - In Austen's Britain, candour takes on its classic warmth: a sweet, generous disposition to think well of others and refuse bad motives — benevolent purity of heart rather than blunt honesty, and a prized social virtue.
- [Contemporary sense] - Candour shifts to frank, open, and honest expression — saying plainly what one thinks. The 18th-century benevolence fades, leaving the modern, slightly colder sense of straightforward truth-telling.
