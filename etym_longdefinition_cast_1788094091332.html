Cast is one of the most versatile words in English: a verb meaning “to throw with force”, “to shape by pouring into a mould”, “to assign (actors) to roles”, “to calculate or reckon”, and “to turn or direct (the eye, a glance)”; and a noun meaning “a throw”, “an object made in a mould”, “the plaster casing for a broken bone”, “the actors in a play”, “a slight squint”, “a tinge or shade of colour”, and “the form or character of something”. All of these senses descend from a single Old Norse verb meaning “to throw”. In the source context — “When the barrels were empty the elves cast them through the trapdoors” — the word is used in its oldest, purest sense: to throw, fling, hurl. The elves throw the empty barrels down through the trapdoors into the river, where they bob away towards the lake; the dwarves’ escape depends on exactly this ancient meaning.

Primary senses of the verb

  • To throw, fling, or hurl — “cast a stone”, “cast the barrels through the trapdoors” (Tolkien): formal or literary now in the plain throw-sense, replaced in speech by throw; survives in fixed expressions: “cast a net”, “cast dice”, “cast a line” (fishing), “cast anchor”, “cast lots”.
  • To shape by pouring (molten metal, plaster, glass) into a mould — “cast in bronze”, “cast iron”: the metal is “thrown” into the mould; the product is “a cast” or “a casting”.
  • To assign roles in a play or film — “to cast the play”, “cast in the role of”: originally “to allot parts” as one allots lots; hence the noun “the cast” = the company of actors.
  • To direct, turn, or bestow — “cast a glance”, “cast one’s eyes”, “cast doubt on”, “cast a shadow”, “cast a vote”, “cast a spell”, “cast blame”: the throw becomes a directed beam of attention, light, suspicion, or influence.
  • To calculate, reckon, or add up (archaic-to-formal) — “cast accounts”, “cast a horoscope”: from the old practice of throwing counters on a counting board; preserved in “cast up an account”.
  • To shed or discard — “cast off” (knitting: to bind off stitches; nautical: to let go the mooring ropes), “cast a skin” (of snakes), “cast off” clothes: the throw as a letting-go.

Primary senses of the noun

  • The actors of a play or film — “the cast of characters”, “an ensemble cast”: the most common modern noun sense; “a cast of thousands” (from the film-credit cliché).
  • An object shaped in a mould — “a bronze cast”, “a plaster cast” (of a statue or the body): the moulded form itself.
  • The plaster or fibreglass casing for a broken limb — “his arm was in a cast”: the limb is encased in a mould of plaster.
  • A throw, or the distance of a throw — “a cast of the dice”, “a long cast” (fishing); archaic in general use.
  • A slight squint or turn of the eye — “a cast in one eye”: the gaze is “thrown” sideways.
  • A tinge, shade, or tendency of colour or character — “a greenish cast”, “the cast of his features”, “a cast of mind”: the surface “throw” of appearance or disposition.
  • Archaic and technical: shape, form, waste matter — “the cast of a face”; also “worm cast” (the soil thrown out by an earthworm), “a hawk’s cast” (the pellet thrown up), from the sense “that which is thrown out”.

Specialised senses

  • Computing — “type casting”: converting a value from one data type to another (integer to string and so on); the value is “thrown” across type boundaries; casting is standard in C, Java, Python, and most typed languages.
  • Medicine — “cast” as the rigid dressing for fractures; “to cast a limb” is to apply one; in ophthalmology a “cast” is the squint.
  • Geology and zoology — “worm casts” (soil extruded by earthworms); a fossil “cast” is the impression of an organism filled by rock.
  • Nautical — “to cast off” (let go the moorings), “to cast the lead” (heave the sounding lead overboard to measure depth).

Semantic bridge: every sense of cast is a “throw”. The Old Norse verb kasta “to throw” gave English a single gesture — a decisive fling — which English then applied to every kind of directed release: throwing metal into a mould (to shape), throwing parts to actors (to allot), throwing the eye or the voice (to direct), throwing counters (to reckon), throwing off a skin or a rope (to shed), throwing a shadow or a spell (to project influence). The noun followed the verb: a throw became the thing thrown (the moulded object, the squinting gaze, the shade of colour, the worm’s cast) and then the whole group of people thrown together in a play — “the cast”. The word’s extraordinary breadth is a monument to the fertility of a single concrete image.
