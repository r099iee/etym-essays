- 13th c. — Anglo-Norman chivalric adoption: Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman lignage, functioning as an aristocratic legal term to denote noble blood, succession to feudal fiefdoms, and honorable family origins.
- 14th–15th c. — Biblical and dynastic chronicles: Centralized in Middle English literature (Chaucer, Wycliffe's Bible, Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur) to validate the royal descent of kings and trace the messianic lineage of Christ back through King David to Abraham.
- 16th–18th c. — Heraldic and racial extraction: Codified during the Tudor, Stuart, and Georgian eras within the College of Arms as an indispensable marker of gentry status; broadened to describe national and racial extraction in imperial travelogues.
- 19th c. — Darwinian evolutionary revolution: Radically transformed by Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), shifting from aristocratic human pedigree to the deep-time biological lineage of species evolving through natural selection.
- 20th–21st c. — Anthropological kinship, phylogenetics, and genomics: Formalized in social anthropology to classify unilineal descent groups; integrated into modern cladistics and mitochondrial DNA analysis to map human evolution; widely used metaphorically for intellectual, artistic, and corporate traditions.
