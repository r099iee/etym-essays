salle de bain is a French feminine noun phrase meaning bathroom, literally room (salle) of (de) bath (bain): the private domestic room equipped for washing the body, typically containing a bathtub or shower, washbasin, mirror, and often, in modern layouts, a toilet, though traditional French usage separates the toilet (WC, toilettes) into its own compartment. Pronounced roughly sal duh ban with nasal vowel, it is one of the first housing words learners meet, appearing in estate listings as appartement 2 chambres, salle de bain, WC séparé. In the Wodehouse passage, the narrator pads along the corridor to the salle de bain in his dressing-gown: the French term adds a faint hotel-like glamour to an English country-house corridor, making even a sulk in a bathrobe sound continental.

Grammatically salle is feminine (la salle de bain, une grande salle de bain), plural les salles de bain or, in modern orthography, salles de bains. Current French prefers plural salle de bains on the logic that one takes many baths there, but singular salle de bain remains widespread, especially in older texts and in English borrowings. The phrase governs verbs like prendre son bain, se laver, faire sa toilette dans la salle de bain, and prepositions dans, vers. Related compounds include salle de séjour living room, salle à manger dining room, salle de classe classroom, salle d'eau shower room, salle des fêtes village hall, showing salle as the generic large room.

Culturally the salle de bain condenses French attitudes to hygiene, privacy, and pleasure: the bath as comfort and self-care rather than mere cleaning, the separation of bathing from excretion as propriety, the mirror and lighting as theatre of la toilette. English speakers borrowing the phrase hear chic and cleanliness together.

  • Core domestic sense: room with bath or shower for bodily washing. The primary meaning is architectural and functional: a tiled, water-proofed room with tub or shower tray, basin, hot water, and storage. The semantic bridge from words is compositional: a salle (originally a large hall) narrowed to any large room, then specified by de bain to bathing purpose. This distinguishes it from bedroom or kitchen by activity.
  • Listing and real-estate sense: unit of comfort. In advertisements salle de bain counts comfort (one or two bathrooms, en-suite or shared). The bridge is economic: bathrooms signal modern plumbing, hot water, and status. A flat with salle de bain + WC séparé promises French propriety; salle d'eau promises a smaller, shower-only economy.
  • Ritual and intimate sense: place of retreat and preparation. One withdraws to the salle de bain to bathe, dress, apply make-up, weep, or sulk — as Wodehouse's hero does while rankling. The bridge is privacy: lockable, mirrored, warm, it becomes a stage for the self. French literature from Colette to Duras eroticises and psychologises this retreat.
  • Euphemistic and polite sense: place to go. Like English bathroom, it can euphemise excretion (où est la salle de bain ?) even where the toilet is separate. The bridge is politeness: bathing sounds cleaner than excretion, so the cleaner activity lends its name.
  • Anglicised exotic sense: French flavour in English text. When English novelists write salle de bain instead of bathroom, they borrow prestige, suggesting hotels, chateaux, or sophistication. The bridge is code-switching: foreign words dignify familiar things. Wodehouse uses it for comic elevation of a sulky pad in a dressing-gown.
  • Technical distinction: salle de bain versus salle d'eau versus cabinet de toilette. Professionals reserve salle de bain for rooms with a bathtub, salle d'eau for shower-only, cabinet de toilette for basin-only. The bridge is regulation and plumbing vocabulary, important for renovation and hotels. Learners must not treat them as free synonyms.

In short, salle de bain is far more than three small words: it is a hall shrunk to a home, water warmed to comfort, and privacy tiled into architecture — the room where the French body is washed, prepared, and briefly left alone.
