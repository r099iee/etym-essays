PERSPICUOUS (adj.) — Clearly expressed and easy to understand; lucid, transparent, and unambiguous in style or statement. The word is used of language, writing, explanation, and argument that can be "seen through" at once — meaning made fully visible to the reader.

Core senses

  • Clarity of expression (primary sense): perspicuous prose, style, explanation, or language is so clear that the meaning is immediately apparent. "The lawyer gave a perspicuous explanation of the complex contract terms," "a perspicuous account of the theory." The quality named is in the text or utterance — it is the expression, not the speaker, that is perspicuous.
  • Transparent, clear to the eye (original, now rarer literal sense): in its earliest English use the word retained its Latin meaning "transparent, clear to the sight" — perspicuous water, perspicuous glass. This literal sense of physical transparency is now rare but survives in technical or archaising writing.
  • Plain, evident, obvious (attenuated sense): by extension, anything perspicuous is so clear it needs no interpretation; the meaning lies open on the surface.

Polysemy, register, and confusions

  • The classic confusion with perspicacious: perspicuous is frequently mixed up with perspicacious (keen-minded, sharp in perception). The two share the root (Latin specere, "to look," with per-, "through") but differ fundamentally in subject: a perspicacious person is one who sees through things — mentally acute, shrewd, discerning; a perspicuous text is one that can be seen through — clear in itself. Perspicuous applies to the clarity of the expression; perspicacious to the sharpness of the mind. The historical bridge: both come from the same verb perspicere, "to look through," but the adjective perspicuus ("visible through, transparent") names the property of the thing looked at, while perspicax ("sharp-sighted") names the ability of the one looking.
  • Perspicuity (noun): the abstract noun, meaning "clarity, lucidity, transparency of style," is the older and now the more commonly used of the pair; "perspicuity of style" is a classical rhetorical ideal.
  • Rhetorical and critical sense: in rhetoric and literary criticism, perspicuity has been, since the 17th century, the cardinal virtue of expository prose — the first requirement that writing be immediately intelligible, before ornament or elegance.
  • Formal, learned register: the word is bookish, Latinate, and today somewhat rare in speech; it belongs to formal writing and to careful stylistic judgement, and is often used approvingly of technical exposition that manages to be clear.
  • Technical and legal sense: "perspicuous" is used of legal drafting, contracts, statutes, and instructions that must admit of no misreading; a perspicuous clause is one whose meaning cannot be misunderstood.

Connotation and usage notes — Perspicuous always carries approval: it praises the text's lucidity, never the reader's effort. Its connotation is of complete transparency of meaning — nothing hidden, nothing ambiguous, nothing requiring an interpreter. The word is typically paired with nouns of language and communication: style, language, explanation, statement, account, argument, prose. Note that it is the expression that is perspicuous; a perspicacious reader understands it, and a perspicacious writer produces it — but the adjective perspicuous attaches to the text itself.

Morphology and related words — perspicuous (adj.) + -ly = perspicuously (adv.); perspicuousness (a rarer noun); the classical noun perspicuity (Latin perspicuitas). Related by root: spectator, aspect, inspect, respect, prospect, perspective, suspicious, specimen — all from Latin specere, "to look." The near-homophone perspicacious is the adjective of the mind's keenness; the two should never be interchanged.

Historical semantic bridge — From Latin perspicuus, "transparent, clear to the eye," from perspicere, "to look through," from per-, "through," + specere, "to look." The word entered English in the late 16th century meaning literally "transparent" (of water, glass); the transfer to language came almost immediately, because a perspicuous text is one you can "see through" to the meaning, exactly as you see through clear water to the bottom. The physical transparency of the Latin adjective became the intellectual transparency of the English one — the bridge from eye to mind was crossed within a single generation of the word's life in English.
