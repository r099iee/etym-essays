- Barnyard strut (12th–15th c.) — French coq “rooster”; the verb coqueter and the diminutive coquet describe a strutting, self-displaying cock — still purely about birds.
- Human vanity (15th–17th c.) — coquet is applied to a vain, gallant man, and its feminine coquette to the woman who plays at love; the metaphor makes courtship a bird's display.
- English fashion (1610s – ) — borrowed into English as a polite noun for a flirt; the adjective coquettish and noun coquetry follow; the word sounds chic and French in the mouths of the courtly.
- Stage and sermon (18th c.) — the coquette becomes a stock character of comedy and a cautionary figure of conduct literature; the moralists condemn her, the playwrights adore her.
- Sentimental century (19th c.) — Romantic ideals recast coquetry as a ravishing danger; naturalists give the name to the flame-crested hummingbirds of genus Lophornis, whose males display like little cocks.
- Literary eclipse (20th c.) — plain flirt takes over everyday speech; coquette survives as polished, slightly dated vocabulary of novels and essays.
- Reclamation (2020s – ) — “coquette” returns as a playful fashion and social-media aesthetic of bows and demure femininity; the old reproach is worn like costume jewellery.
