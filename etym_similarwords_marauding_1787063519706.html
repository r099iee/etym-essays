• raiding: similar because both mean attacking to plunder; differs because raid names a single organised strike on a target, while marauding stresses continuous, ranging, hit-and-run movement across a territory.
  • plundering: similar taking goods by force; differs because plunder names the act of seizing valuables, while marauding includes the roving and the destruction as well as the theft.
  • looting: similar stealing during disorder; differs because loot is opportunistic stealing from the already-fallen (looted shops), while marauding is predatory movement that causes the fall.
  • pillaging: similar violent plundering; differs because pillage is a formal, historical word for sack and plunder, while marauding adds the wandering, ranging quality.
  • sacking: similar destroying and robbing a place; differs because sack is a single crushing assault on a city or camp, while marauding is dispersed, mobile depredation.
  • ravaging: similar laying waste; differs because ravage emphasises the damage done to land and people, while marauding emphasises the roving predator's journey.
  • despoiling: similar stripping of goods; differs because despoil is literary and focused on the seizure, while marauding is the wider roving-and-raiding activity.
  • foraging: similar ranging in search of supplies; differs because forage is neutral and practical (animals forage for food, soldiers forage for provisions), while marauding is violent and criminal.
  • ransacking: similar searching a place violently for goods; differs because ransack is confined to a single building or room, while marauding ranges over a whole region.
  • depredating: similar preying upon and plundering; differs because depredate is formal and technical, while marauding is vivid, narrative, and predatory.
  • scavenging: similar moving about gathering food; differs because scavenge is picking over leftovers and refuse, while marauding is taking by force from the living and the whole.
  • marauder (noun relation): the agent noun of the same verb; differs because it names the doer — a marauder is one who marauds — whereas marauding names the act and the quality.
