Eulogium (plural eulogiums or eulogia) is a formal, stately noun denoting an elaborate, structured speech, formal oration, or written composition delivered in high praise of a person, heroic accomplishment, virtue, or public institution. Historically a Latinate double or twin of the more common English word eulogy, an eulogium is distinguished by its elevated rhetorical architecture, monumental dignity, and classical Latinate gravitas. While modern English frequently uses eulogy almost exclusively for funeral speeches, eulogium historically encompassed grand panegyrics pronounced in honor of living benefactors, civic milestones, philosophical achievements, and military triumphs, as well as solemn posthumous commemorations.

In the source text from Samuel Johnson’s essay in The Rambler (No. 144, 1751), the word plays an indispensable rhetorical role in exposing moral hypocrisy: ‘There is, I fear, so much malice in the hearts of most men, that they are chiefly jealous of that praise which can give the greatest pleasure, and are then most liberal of eulogium when it can no longer be enjoyed.’ Johnson specifically selects the Latinate, heavyweight form eulogium to highlight the grandiosity and theatrical excess of the praise. It costs an envious man nothing to bestow a magnificent, florid eulogium upon a corpse, because the dead rival is safely interred and can no longer feel the satisfaction of literary acclaim or threaten the living speaker’s vanity. The Latinate dignity of eulogium underscores the hollow pomposity of posthumous flattery.

Polysemic Senses and Rhetorical Domains:

  • 1. High Formal Encomium and Panegyric (Primary Classical Sense): A formal speech or public written tract composed according to the established rules of classical epideictic oratory (the rhetoric of praise and blame). In this sense, an eulogium celebrates moral virtues, military heroism, scholarly genius, or civic philanthropy with ornate diction, rhythmic cadence, and balanced syntax.
  • 2. Posthumous Memorial Tribute and Funeral Oration (Commemorative Sense): A formal commemorative discourse delivered at the obsequies, grave, or memorial service of a deceased person of distinction. The semantic association with tombstones and monuments was heavily reinforced during the Renaissance by the formal conflation of Greek eulogia with Latin elogium (an inscription on a sepulchre or bust).
  • 3. Scholarly Commendation and Critical Acclaim (Literary Review Sense): A published essay, foreword, or academic review showering elaborate approbation upon an author's magnum opus, scientific discovery, or lifetime contribution to learning.
  • 4. Institutional and Civic Proclamations (Official Rhetoric Sense): Formal resolutions, citations, or testimonials adopted by parliamentary bodies, learned societies, or municipal councils to confer solemn public honor upon a retiring magistrate, visiting statesman, or national benefactor.
  • 5. Satiric or Ironical Bestowal (Pejorative Inversion): Used by moralists, satirists, and critics to denote hypocritical, hollow, or opportunistic flattery that is showered upon individuals only when it is politically advantageous or safely disconnected from real assistance (precisely the ironic usage exemplified in Johnson's text).

Register and Connotations: Eulogium occupies the highest register of formal English prose. Unlike the everyday word eulogy, which is routinely delivered by family members and friends at modern funeral services, eulogium carries the grand, polished ring of the eighteenth-century Augustan forum, the Roman Senate, and neoclassical monuments. When employed in modern writing, it is chosen deliberately to evoke historical elegance, neoclassical monumentality, or an arch, self-conscious irony regarding excessive praise.
