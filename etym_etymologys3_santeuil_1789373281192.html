The onomastic and etymological history of Santeuil represents a fascinating convergence of ancient Celtic toponymy, Gallo-Roman linguistic evolution, seventeenth-century French literary history, and twentieth-century modernist literary discovery.

Toponymically and linguistically, Santeuil is a French place-name compound of Gaulish (Celtic) origin. It derives from the reconstructed Gallo-Roman compound *Sentó-ialon, composed of two distinct Celtic lexical roots:

  • The First Element (*sentu-): Tracing to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *sent-, meaning "to head for, go, travel, find out" (the ancestral source of Germanic *sinþa-, "journey, time," Old High German sinnan, "to travel, ponder," and Modern English send). In Proto-Celtic, this root became *sentu-, meaning "way, path, road" (retained in Old Irish sét and Welsh hynt, "path, journey"). In Gaulish toponyms, it designated a vital trade road or transit track.
  • The Second Element (*-ialon): A highly productive Gaulish toponymic suffix meaning "clearing, glade, open field in a forest" (derived from Proto-Indo-European *yeh₂-, "to ride, go," cognate with Welsh iâl, "cleared, open upland"). This Gaulish suffix *-ialo- is the historical ancestor of hundreds of French place-names ending in -euil, -ueil, and -œil, such as Verneuil ("alder glade"), Argenteuil ("silver glade"), and Auteuil ("high glade").

Consequently, the original Celtic compound *Sentó-ialon possessed the transparent topographic meaning of "the clearing by the road" or "the road-glade," denoting an ancient settlement established in a forest clearing along a Celtic communication route in northern Gaul. During the Middle Ages, the name is attested in Latin cartularies of Chartres and the Île-de-France as Sentolium (attested in the 10th and 11th centuries in the cartulary of the Abbey of Saint-Père-en-Vallée), Santoilum, and Senteuil. Through regular Old French sound changes—the vocalization of the unstressed syllable, the nasalization of the initial vowel, and the palatalization of the final lateral consonant into the palatal liquid [ʎ] (orthographically rendered -il or -uil)—the name crystallized as Santeuil.

In cultural history, the surname rose to prominence through Jean-Baptiste Santeul (or Santeuil, 1630–1698), one of the most celebrated figures of the Grand Siècle. A canon regular of the Abbey of Saint-Victor in Paris, Santeul was acclaimed as the supreme Neo-Latin poet of his age, composing monumental hymns for the revised Paris Breviary that were sung across France. Santeul was equally famous for his bizarre, mercurial personality and sharp wit. The Duc de Saint-Simon devoted a lengthy, brilliant passage in his Mémoires to Santeul's eccentricities, describing him as a manic genius who wrote verses with wild physical contortions and died tragically after the young Duke of Bourbon mischievously emptied a box of Spanish snuff into Santeul's wineglass at a banquet in Dijon. La Bruyère immortalized him in his Caractères under the pseudonym of "Théodate."

When Marcel Proust embarked on his first ambitious attempt at a large-scale contemporary novel in 1895, he required a surname for his young hero that resonated with French aristocratic history, intellectual sensitivity, and classical elegance, while avoiding the immediate banality of common Parisian names. Proust, whose mother’s family had roots in the Beauce and who knew the local geography near Chartres intimately (where Santeuil is a neighboring commune to Illiers), selected Jean Santeuil.

Between 1895 and 1899, Proust accumulated hundreds of loose, unpaginated leaves recording Jean's inner life. However, overwhelmed by the unwieldy, fragmented third-person narrative structure, Proust abandoned the manuscript in 1899, leaving the papers scattered in trunks. Following Proust's death in 1922, the manuscript remained entirely unknown until the early 1950s, when the young scholar Bernard de Fallois painstakingly sorted and deciphered the papers in the apartment of Proust's niece, Suzy Mante-Proust. Published by Gallimard in 1952, Jean Santeuil was recognized as an international literary sensation, shedding invaluable light on the genetic gestation of À la recherche du temps perdu.
