- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed from Old French *publier* with the English inchoative suffix *-ish*; meant to proclaim, announce openly, or divulge news and official decrees to the common people.
- Late 15th to 16th Century (Tudor Era & Print Revolution) - With the arrival of William Caxton's press in Westminster, the word undergoes a profound technological shift, expanding from oral proclamation to the mechanical printing and public sale of books.
- 17th Century (Stuart Era & Civil War) - Deepens in legal and ecclesiastical usage; codified in Anglican church law ("publishing the banns") and parliamentary censorship acts licensing printed tracts.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Formalized in defamation jurisprudence (the legal concept that libel requires publication to a third party) and in the flourishing world of commercial Grub Street literature.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes the hallmark of professional authorship in serialized fiction (Dickens, Eliot); to be a "published author" conveys recognized intellectual and social status.
- 20th to 21st Century - Expands from traditional book printing into the academic metric of "publish or perish," and subsequently into digital media, web hosting, and software deployment pipelines.
