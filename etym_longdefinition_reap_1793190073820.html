Reap means in its first and founding sense to cut and gather grain at harvest — to mow the ripe corn with sickle, scythe, or combine harvester and carry it home — and in its great extended sense to obtain or receive something as the consequence of earlier action, as in to reap profits, to reap praise, or to reap what one sows.

In the source sentence twelve hay-cutters reap the meadow grass while as many more with rakes turn it: the literal agricultural labour in full swing, the wonder-tale meadow harvested by fairy numbers with fairy speed. The verb here is delightfully exact — mown grass for hay is genuinely reaped — and the scene preserves a picture of communal harvest work that mechanised farming has nearly erased.

Core meaning in detail: Reaping is the climax of the farming year: the grain is ripe, the weather holds, and every hand cuts, binds, and gathers before rain spoils the crop. Traditional reaping used the sickle's one-handed arc or the scythe's long sweep, followed by binding sheaves and stacking stooks; the mechanical reaper of the 1830s and the modern combine collapsed all these steps into one pass. The verb governs both the crop (to reap wheat, to reap hay) and, metaphorically, the reward (to reap dividends, to reap a knighthood), the two objects marking the literal-metaphorical divide.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To cut and gather grain or hay (primary literal sense, from Old English): they reaped the wheat before the storm, reaping machines in August fields. The bridge is the act itself: severing ripe stalks and bringing the harvest home, the agricultural year made verb.
  • To obtain as a return on effort or investment (chief figurative sense): to reap profits, to reap the benefits of reform. The bridge carries the harvest indoors: effort is seed, time is the season, and the gain is the crop gathered in autumn.
  • To reap what one sows (moral proverb, Biblical): you shall reap what you have sown, from Galatians. The bridge moralises agriculture: good and evil are seeds with inevitable harvests, so consequences are crops no one escapes gathering.
  • The Reaper and the Grim Reaper, death as harvester (poetic and popular): death reaps souls with his scythe. The bridge is the medieval allegory of humanity as God's grain: lives ripen, the scythe falls, and the harvest is eternity — an image in church carving centuries before cartoons.
  • To glean souls or converts (religious missionary register): to reap a harvest of souls. The bridge softens the scythe: evangelists gather people as farmers gather wheat, the mission field yielding its crop of believers.
  • To win or secure honours (extended social): to reap awards, to reap applause. The bridge generalises profit to prestige: any coveted outcome becomes a harvest the deserving carry home.

Register and connotation: literal reap is rural and seasonal — golden, communal, and increasingly nostalgic, since few modern speakers have swung a scythe. Figurative reap splits two ways: warm and deserved in reap the rewards, stern and minatory in reap the whirlwind and you reap what you sow. The Grim Reaper keeps the word's darkest colouring alive in fancy dress and cartoon, scythe over shoulder, while boardrooms use reap bloodlessly for quarterly gains. Few verbs span the hayfield, the pulpit, and the profit statement so completely.
