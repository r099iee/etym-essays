Grubbed is the past tense and past participle of the verb grub, meaning fundamentally to dig, scrape, or search laboriously in soil, rubbish, or confined places, and by extension to obtain a living by hard mean labour, to rummage, or to uproot.

Its primary, most concrete sense remains agricultural and manual: to dig shallowly, to clear stumps and weeds, to turn earth with hands or a hoe. To grub potatoes is to dig them out. To grub up brambles is to extirpate them roots and all. This is hard, low, bent-over work, and the word carries that bodily posture inside it.

A second, equally central modern sense is rummaging search: to grub about or around means to grope, fumble, and hunt through drawers, huts, pockets, or ruins for something edible, useful, or lost. Your context — she grubbed about the tent for whatever edible things lay to hand — is this foraging sense: not systematic excavation but hungry, opportunistic poking about. The preposition matters: grub about/around/through signals search, grub up/out signals extraction.

  • Dig and clear land: to break ground, remove roots, hoe weeds. Example: farmers grubbed out stumps before ploughing. This retains the oldest Middle English sense.
  • Forage and rummage: to search by turning things over, to scavenge. Example: she grubbed in her bag for coins; pigs grub in leaf litter. Implies persistence rather than skill, need rather than curiosity.
  • Toil drudgingly, especially for money: to grub for a living, a money-grubber, a grubstreet hack. Here the digging metaphor becomes moral: life as endless low scraping for gain. It is always slightly contemptuous.
  • Grub as noun bridge — larva and food: understanding grubbed requires knowing the noun grub: first the white larva that burrows (a grub-worm), then slang for food itself (pub grub, grub's up), then a dirty person (a grubby child). To be grubby is to bear the marks of grubbing — soiled, unkempt. To grub worms for fishing links verb and noun directly.
  • Figurative unearthing of facts: journalists and scholars grub up scandals, grub out the truth. Like dig up dirt, it implies the fact was buried, dirty, and required unpleasant effort to expose.

Register and connotation are consistently humble and faintly pejorative. One does not grub in a palace; one grubs in dirt, ruins, bargain bins. It suggests poverty, necessity, or greed, never leisure or elegance. Search is neutral, rummage is playful, forage can be romantic, but grub is blunt and bodily: hands dirty, back bent.

Grammatically, grubbed functions as simple past (she grubbed about), present perfect (he has grubbed up), passive participle (land grubbed clear), and adjective (grubbed earth). It regularly takes adverbial particles: about, around, along, up, out, through. Without a particle it can sound incomplete in modern use except in agricultural contexts.

Domain-specific uses include: agriculture (grub-hoe, grubber implement for tillage), fishing (grubbing for bait), mining (grubstake = stake of provisions/money to a prospector), entomology (grub stage of beetles), and military slang (grub = rations). In American English grubbing can also denote begging for food or money (to grub a cigarette).
