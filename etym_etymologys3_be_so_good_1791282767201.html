Be so good is not a single word but a fossilised polite formula, and its history must be told through its three components plus the construction that binds them. BE is the most irregular and ancient verb in English, a suppletive merger of three distinct Proto-Indo-European roots: *h₁es- (to be, as in is), *bʰuH- (to grow, become, as in be, been) and *wes- (to dwell, as in was, were). Old English beon (to be, become) already showed this mixture, and Middle English be inherited a paradigm stitched from different stems. In the imperative be, the verb commands existence or behaviour: be quiet, be kind, be off. The politeness formula be so good exploits this imperative of conduct: literally be good enough in character to do something.

SO comes from PIE demonstrative *so- (this, that), which in Proto-Germanic became *swa (so, as, thus) and in Old English swa (so, just as, therefore). Old English swa was enormously versatile — oath (swa me God helpe), comparison (swa...swa, as...as), and degree (swa good, so good). Middle English reduced swa to so and specialised it as an intensifier and anaphor: in this manner, to such an extent. In be so good, so is degree-anaphoric: good to the extent required for my request. Parallel formulas — be so kind, be so obliging, be so good as to — all use so to measure goodness by compliance.

GOOD has a famously obscure etymology. Proto-Germanic *gōdaz (good, fitting, suitable) has no secure PIE ancestor; it is likely a Germanic innovation from *gʰedʰ- (to unite, fit together, suit), though this is debated. Old English gōd meant excellent, virtuous, kind and socially valuable, applied to people, swords, land and actions alike. Unlike Latin bonus, which moral philosophers analysed, good remained a blunt Germanic word of approval. By Middle English good had absorbed courteous senses: a good man was not only morally upright but affable and obliging. Hence to call someone good in a request is flattery with an expectation: your goodness will show itself in helpfulness.

  • Construction history: 16th–17th century courtesy books urge be so good as to + infinitive as a genteel alternative to blunt imperatives (do this); 18th-century letters normalise be so good as to inform me, be so kind as to call; 19th-century servants' dialogue and Wodehouse parody freeze it as upper-class master-to-servant diction.
  • Phonetic notes: be /biː/ from Old English beon with loss of -on; so /səʊ/ from swa with monophthongisation of /aː/ to /oː/ and Great Vowel Shift raising; good /ɡʊd/ with shortening of long ō before dentals.
  • Pragmatic drift: from sincere compliment (you are good, therefore help me) to conventional politeness marker (please) to condescending or comic peremptoriness (Bertie's be so good, Jeeves, which is an order in velvet gloves).
  • Literary attestations: Shakespearean be so good and be so kind courtesies; Austen's epistolary be so good as to write; Dickensian servants ordered with be so good; Wodehouse's Bertie Wooster using be so good to Jeeves as signature toff imperiousness.

The combined phrase be so good as to + infinitive peaked in Victorian and Edwardian polite English as the quintessential euphemised command. Masters used it to servants, hosts to guests, and officials to subordinates precisely because it preserved hierarchy while pretending to beg a favour. Bertie Wooster's be so good, Jeeves, as to shove that bally black thing... is textbook: grammatically a wish about Jeeves's goodness, pragmatically an unambiguous order about a dinner jacket. Modern English has largely replaced it with please, would you mind, or could you, leaving be so good sounding consciously old-fashioned, upper-class or archly ironic — which is exactly why Wodehouse keeps it.

Debate note: the origin of good remains disputed. The traditional link to PIE *gʰedʰ- (to join, fit) via Proto-Germanic *gōdaz is plausible but not proven by exact cognates outside Germanic; some scholars treat *gōdaz as of unknown origin. The be-verb merger and the development of so from *so- to *swa are, by contrast, securely attested.
