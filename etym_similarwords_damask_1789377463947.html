• Brocade: Similar because both are rich, ornate, figured woven textiles historically associated with luxury and courtly splendor; differs because brocade is a supplementary-weft fabric where colored or metallic threads are woven on top of a background weave to create raised, non-reversible relief patterns, whereas damask is a flat, reversible fabric whose patterns are created entirely by contrasting warp and weft satin weaves of the same fiber.
  • Jacquard: Similar because both refer to complex patterned fabrics used in upholstery, drapery, and high-end textiles; differs because jacquard is a broad mechanical category naming any fabric woven on a Jacquard loom (including brocade, tapestry, and matelassé), whereas damask is a specific traditional weave structure characterized by reversible matte-and-sheen contrast.
  • Tapestry: Similar because both are heavy, decorative woven textiles depicting intricate designs; differs because tapestry is an artisanal, pictorial, non-reversible weft-faced textile woven with heavy multi-colored yarns on a vertical loom for wall hangings, whereas damask is a smooth, lightweight or medium-weight reversible fabric intended for tablecloths, clothing, and furniture coverings.
  • Crimson: Similar because both can function as descriptive color adjectives denoting rich, deep reddish hues; differs because crimson is a specific optical color term describing a dark red with purplish-blue undertones (derived from kermes dye), whereas damask carries botanical and textural connotations of velvety texture, grayish-pink rose tones, or the variegated blending of red and white.
  • Chintz: Similar because both are historical decorative fabrics with floral motifs popular in classic English interior design; differs because chintz is a printed, calico cotton fabric finished with a stiff, shiny glazed surface, whereas damask achieves its patterning purely through the structural geometry of the loom weave without glazed finishes.
  • Moire: Similar because both textiles display shimmering, light-reactive optical effects across their surfaces; differs because moiré achieves its wavy "watered" appearance by crushing ribbed fabrics (such as taffeta) between heated rollers under tremendous hydraulic pressure, whereas damask creates its design directly during the weaving process.
