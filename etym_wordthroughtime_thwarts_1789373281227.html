- 13th Century (c. 1200–1250) - Enters Middle English via Old Norse (*þverr* and *þvert*); initially recorded primarily as an adverb, preposition, and adjective meaning "across, athwart, from side to side," and describing cross, contrary, or perverse temperaments.
- 14th Century (c. 1300s) - Establishes the nautical noun *thwart* for the cross-benches of rowing vessels; simultaneously undergoes verbal conversion (*thwarten*), acquiring the active transitive meaning of opposing, crossing, or contradicting someone's desires.
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance Era) - Flourishes in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama and poetry (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton); *thwarts* is used dynamically to describe fate, rivals, or moral principles crossing human ambition, while *athwart* remains common for transverse spatial orientation.
- 18th to 19th Century - The adjectival and adverbial senses become largely archaic or dialectal, while the nautical noun *thwarts* remains standard in maritime architecture, and the verb *thwarts* cements itself as a dramatic, elevated literary term for decisively frustrating plans.
- 20th to 21st Century - Prominent in political analysis, epic literature, and high-register philosophy (such as meditations on how time thwarts memory); universally recognized as a sharp, forceful verb denoting the decisive obstruction of human purpose.
