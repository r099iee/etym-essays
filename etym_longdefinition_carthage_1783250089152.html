Carthage (proper noun). Primary sense in this context. In the encyclopedic sentence that “Carthage was one of the most important trading hubs of the Ancient Mediterranean and one of the most affluent cities of the classical world,” the name stands as the archetype of the great mercantile republic. The tone is respectful and slightly awestruck: Carthage is presented not merely as a city but as a whole order of civilisation built on trade, wealth, and sea-power, a thalassocracy whose silver, purple dye, and commerce made it the richest state of its age. Historically the name is freighted with tragedy: the Phoenician colony that grew into Rome's greatest rival, only to be utterly destroyed in 146 BC, so that the word “Carthage” today conjures both fabulous wealth and total ruin — “Carthage must be destroyed” (Carthago delenda est) being one of the most famous political catchphrases of antiquity. The full polysemy of the name. Carthage is at once a place, a polity, a metonym, a symbol, and a living toponym, and each sense has its own historical bridge:
  • The historical city. The Phoenician colony on the Bay of Tunis, traditionally founded in 814 BC by Tyrian colonists led by Elissa (Dido), with its triple wall, the Cothon harbours, the citadel of Byrsa, and the fertile agricultural hinterland of the Bagradas valley. This is the physical city whose remains, later overbuilt by Rome, are excavated today.
  • The Carthaginian state. The sovereign republic and empire that by the 3rd century BC controlled North Africa, Sardinia, Corsica, western Sicily, and much of Spain; governed by two Suffetes, a Council of Elders, an Assembly of the Hundred and Four, and hereditary military families such as the Barcids. In this sense Carthage names a political community and its institutions, not a mere market.
  • Metonym for commerce and wealth. Because Carthage was above all a trading state — the hub of Iberian silver, murex-purple dye, textiles, grain, and slaves, with trade treaties (e.g. with Rome in 348 BC) — the name stands in shorthand for fabulous mercantile prosperity; “one of the most affluent cities of the classical world” draws exactly on this sense.
  • Symbol of annihilation. Through the three Punic Wars (264–146 BC) and the city's destruction in 146 BC by Scipio Aemilianus, Carthage became the stock emblem of total ruin — Cato the Elder's repeated cry “Carthago delenda est” (“Carthage must be destroyed”), the alleged salting of its fields, and the moral lesson that hubris meets erasure. “Carthaginian peace” still means a settlement that destroys the defeated party.
  • The archaeological site. Since nineteenth-century excavation, Carthage also names the ruins and museums near modern Tunis — the Tophet, the Punic ports, the Antonine Baths — a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1979).
  • Modern toponymy. The name travelled the globe: Carthage, Missouri; Carthage, New York; Carthage, Tennessee; and, through the Roman refoundation Carthago Nova (“New Carthage”) in Spain, the port Cartagena and its colonial namesake Cartagena de Indias in Colombia.
  • Historiographical shorthand. In military and political history the name stands for Hannibal's Carthage, the Barca dynasty, and the three Punic Wars that decided the fate of the western Mediterranean.
How the secondary senses developed (semantic bridges). The wealth sense is original: the city was founded as a trading colony, and its very name — “New City” — advertised renewal, not luxury. The symbol-of-ruin sense is entirely historical, forged by the Roman destruction and kept alive by Latin literature (Cicero, Livy, Florus) and by the catchphrase Carthago delenda est. The metonymic force works because Carthage was an extreme case — the richest and most powerful state ever destroyed outright — so the name telescopes two opposites, success and erasure, into a single word. The toponymic spread reflects the Roman habit of refounding cities and the later European practice of naming New-World towns after classical models, so that today a dozen towns quietly bear the name of a city annihilated in 146 BC. Distinctions from related terms. An emporium is a trading mart and isolates the commerce; Carthage names the whole political community. A metropolis is a mother-city; Carthage was the greatest of colonies, genealogically subordinate to Tyre. Tyre and Sidon are the Phoenician parent-cities that grew old and declined while the daughter of Tyre outshone them. Punic (from Latin Poenus) is the adjective for Carthaginian language and people and the name of the Semitic dialect the Carthaginians spoke. Register and collocations. Carthage is a formal historical proper noun, appearing in encyclopedic prose, historiography, and political rhetoric. Typical collocations: “the destruction of Carthage,” “Carthage must be destroyed,” “the ruins of Carthage,” “New Carthage” (Carthago Nova), “Carthaginian” (adjective and noun), “Punic Wars,” “Carthaginian peace.” It is rarely used in everyday speech except as a byword for a fallen commercial empire — the medieval schoolboy, the Victorian parliamentarian, and the modern historian all reach for the same resonant name.
