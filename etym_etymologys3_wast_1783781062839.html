Wast is a single fossilized word that opens a window onto how English built its strangest verb. The English verb be is suppletive: its forms are drawn from at least three different Indo-European roots, a situation inherited from the parent language itself. The present forms come from *h₁es- (am, is) and *er- (are); the infinitive and the be-forms come from *bʰuH- "to grow, become" (be, been, being); and the past forms — was, were, and their archaic relatives — come from *wes- "to remain, stay, dwell." This tripartite structure was already in place in Old English, where wesan and bēon coexisted, and it explains why the past tense of "to be" does not look like its present: the word for "I was" literally goes back to an ancient verb meaning "I remained."

The deep root *wes- is itself attested across the Indo-European family with the sense "to remain, dwell, pass the night": Sanskrit vásati "to dwell," Greek aéthlesa (in aúlē "courtyard, where one spends the night"), Latin Vesta (the goddess of the hearth, the dwelling-fire), and Gothic wisan "to be." The semantic line is clear and old: "to remain, to stay" came to mean simply "to be" in Germanic, and it is that verb that supplies the past tense of the whole paradigm. When Old English speakers said wæs ("was"), they were using a past tense whose root originally meant "dwelled" or "remained."

The interesting part of wast's story is not its root but its ending. In Old English the second-person singular past of "to be" was not built on wæs at all: it was wǣre ("thou was"), the regular descendant of the *wes-/*es- conjugation with the old second-person ending. During the 13th century, however, speakers rebuilt the form. English was in the middle of generalizing the ending -st across all second-person singular pasts — the same analogy that produced satst ("sat + -st") and didst ("did + -st"). The strongly irregular wǣre, which no longer fit the pattern, was replaced by the transparent was + -st = wast. This is a textbook case of analogical leveling: the most irregular form in the grammar was regularized on the model of the strong verbs, and the older form did not die but was demoted to a different function, surviving as the subjunctive wert.

By the 14th century wast was the standard indicative form, and it entered the early modern period fully productive. The Authorized Version of 1611 uses wast dozens of times for plain, factual past existence and state: "thou wast with me," "where wast thou?" The distinction between wast (indicative, "you were [as a fact]") and wert (subjunctive, "you were [hypothetically]") was observed with real consistency in the 1611 text and by Milton, and it is one of the most reliable tests of a writer's command of the old verbal system. Milton's usage in Paradise Lost (1667) is exact: the Spirit "from the first / Wast present" — a plain assertion of past existence, hence wast, not wert. The verb form here does real work, carrying the second person singular and the reverential address to the Holy Spirit that governs the entire opening invocation.

The death of wast is the death of thou. In the 17th and 18th centuries, standard English collapsed the singular and plural into you, partly for politeness and partly through the general loss of inflections. With you the verb took a single form for all persons, and the whole -st paradigm — wast, wert, satst, didst, dost, hast — left the living language together. Wast survived where the thou-forms survived: in the King James Bible, in the Book of Common Prayer, in Quaker usage, and in archaizing poetry. Today it is a deliberate archaism, a marker of scriptural or early modern diction, instantly recognizable from the cadence of the Authorized Version.

Summary of the evolutionary chain:

  • Roots: the past of be builds on PIE *wes- "to remain, dwell" (not on the *h₁es- of the present); the verb is triply suppletive from the start.
  • Old English: wæs (1st/3rd sg past), wǣre (2nd sg past) — the old ending system.
  • Middle English (13th c.): analogical wast = was + -st, regularizing the irregular form; wert split off as the subjunctive.
  • Early Modern English: wast as the standard indicative thou-form in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton (Paradise Lost I, "Wast present").
  • Later centuries: the collapse of thou into you removes wast from speech; it lives on in liturgy, scripture, and poetic archaism.
  • Function today: a fossilized sign of the second person singular and of solemn, scriptural, or early modern diction.
