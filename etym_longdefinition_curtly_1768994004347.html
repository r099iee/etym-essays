curtly: In an abruptly brief manner — speech cut short, reduced to its minimum, so that the brevity itself communicates impatience or dismissal. Bellman agrees with his companion's observation ("It can't very well be anything else") "rather curtly": he does not dispute it, but he will not waste words on the obvious. The nuance is clipped, dry, and slightly impatient — the answer of a man who finds the remark beneath him or who is preoccupied with the danger at hand. The adverb suggests the sentence was snapped off rather than offered; the agreement is given with its edges trimmed, stripped of politeness and padding. In the wider context the curtness carries suppressed urgency: having conceded the point in two words, the Bellman at once moves to action ("I move that we start looking for shelter"), so the curt reply functions as a verbal economy under pressure — the cutting-off of discussion in favor of motion, the speech of a leader who will not debate the obvious while a storm approaches. Primary and extended senses.
  • Rudely brief, abrupt (the dominant sense): curtly describes speech or manner that is pointedly, often rudely brief — words cut to the minimum, trimmed of politeness, pleasantry, and padding. The rudeness may be deliberate (dismissal, impatience) or situational (haste, emergency).
  • Simply brief, without elaboration (the neutral sense): in earlier and formal use, curt could mean merely "short, brief" without rudeness; the adverb curtly retains this neutral thread when the brevity is efficient rather than rude, though in modern usage the edge of impatience almost always shows.
  • Of style and writing: a curt style or a curt reply is one that wastes no words, often admired for economy even as it is felt as cold; the word spans the space between "terse" and "brusque."
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word is the adverbial form of curt, which descends from Latin curtus, "cut short, shortened, docked, maimed" — a word used of a docked tail, a stunted plant, and, tellingly, of speech, since the Romans already applied curtus to words and replies clipped of their length. The whole family belongs to the PIE root *sker-, "to cut" (with the s-mobile variants *ker-, *kert-), the same root that gives English short, shirt, skirt, and curtail, Hindi kāṭnā "to cut," Greek keírō "I cut, shear," and Old Irish scaraim "I separate." The semantic bridge is a cutting: a curt reply is a sentence that has been cut to the stump, snapped off at the minimum, and the word itself — curt, curtly — is short, as if the blade had shaped the word that names it. Register and comparison. Curtly sits between the neutral and the rude, and its force is always in the deliberate shortening of the sentence. abruptly stresses suddenness of onset; brusquely adds roughness and a lack of courtesy; tersely is economical but not necessarily rude; shortly is neutral; peremptorily carries commanding authority; snappishly adds irritability; testily adds ill-tempered touchiness. Only curtly fuses brevity with dismissiveness — the word itself "cut short," implying that the speaker deliberately shortened his sentence, as the Bellman shortens his assent to the bare minimum required by politeness, all the more striking because the brevity conceals a storm.
