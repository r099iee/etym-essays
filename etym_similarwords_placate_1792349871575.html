• appease: Similar because both involve making concessions to calm anger or hostility; differs because appease carries a much stronger historical and moral stigma (especially following Neville Chamberlain's policy toward Nazi Germany in 1938), implying dangerous, cowardly surrender of principle to buy temporary peace from an insatiable aggressor, whereas placate is broader, less inherently treacherous, and common in interpersonal and commercial contexts.
  • pacify: Similar because both aim to reduce conflict and establish peace; differs because pacify emphasizes subduing turbulence, riot, or resistance—often through overwhelming physical force, martial pacification, or firm authority—whereas placate relies on sweetening the situation through verbal soothing, apologies, or concessions.
  • mollify: Similar because both mean to soften anger or temper resentment; differs because mollify (from Latin mollis, "soft") focuses on softening the internal emotional hardness or hurt feelings of an individual, operating primarily on an affective psychological plane, whereas placate leans more toward external actions, bargains, and tangible concessions.
  • propitiate: Similar because both describe winning over a hostile power; differs because propitiate is an elevated, specialized religious and classical term specifically denoting the appeasement of divine or supernatural wrath through sacrifices, atonement, and formal ritual liturgy.
  • conciliate: Similar because both seek to overcome distrust and hostility; differs because conciliate implies winning over someone through active goodwill, mediation, diplomatic compromise, and constructive dialogue, establishing a cooperative relationship rather than merely soothing immediate wrath.
  • assuage: Similar because both reduce the severity of negative feelings; differs because assuage typically governs non-personal direct objects representing emotional states or physical deprivations (e.g., to assuage grief, guilt, fears, or hunger), whereas placate almost always governs people, deities, or factions.
  • allay: Similar because both alleviate emotional disturbance; differs because allay applies specifically to dispelling doubts, anxieties, fears, or suspicions rather than actively quieting explosive anger or grievance.
  • soothe: Similar because both calm emotional irritation; differs because soothe is a softer, more intimate Germanic word focusing on gentle comfort, tender physical relief (soothing a burn or crying infant), whereas placate is transactional, strategic, and often political.
  • humor: Similar because both involve giving in to another to keep the peace; differs because to humor someone means to indulge their whims, oddities, or unreasonable demands temporarily with an attitude of patronizing tolerance, without necessarily facing real anger or hostility.
  • pander: Similar because both involve catering to another party's desires; differs because pander carries severe moral disapprobation, meaning to gratify low, base, immoral, or vulgar tastes for self-interested exploitation.
  • mitigate: Similar because both involve making something less harsh; differs because mitigate applies to circumstances, penalties, disasters, or damages (e.g., mitigating climate change, mitigating sentencing) rather than placating conscious, angry human beings.
