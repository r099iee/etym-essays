The PIE root: a thin but real reconstruction. The verb fell belongs to the great Germanic family of "fall" words, and the family points back to a Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *pol- (some handbooks write *pʰel-), meaning "to fall". The evidence is thinner than for many roots, because outside Germanic the cognates are few: the most frequently cited relative is Lithuanian pùlti "to fall, to throw oneself", with possible Slavic contacts (Polish paść "to fall") in the same zone. The Germanic forms themselves are unambiguous: Old English feallan "to fall", Old Norse falla, Old High German fallan, Gothic *fallan (attested only in compounds) — all from Proto-Germanic *fallaną. Both the OED and Etymonline flag the Indo-European connection as uncertain rather than proven; the honest lexicographic position is that Germanic "fall" is certainly ancient within Germanic, and probably — but not certainly — Proto-Indo-European.

Germanic: the intransitive and its causative. What makes fell so interesting is a grammatical fact inherited from the oldest Germanic: the dialects built pairs of verbs — an intransitive "fall" and a causative "make fall" — joined by a suffix. From *fallaną "to fall" was derived the causative *fallijaną "to cause to fall, to fell" (with the class-1 weak causative suffix *-j-), exactly as set relates to sit and raise to rise. This double structure is attested throughout the older dialects: Old Saxon fellian, Old High German fellen (modern German fällen), Old Norse fella, Dutch vellen — all "to fell, cause to fall", standing beside their plain "fall" counterparts.

Old English: fællan and fellan. The causative entered Old English as fællan (Anglian) and fellan (West Saxon), a class-1 weak verb meaning "to cause to fall, strike down, cut down, kill". The alternation æ/e reflects the i-umlaut of the root vowel under the old causative *-j- suffix — the same mechanism that turned *fallijaną into *fælljan. The verb is attested throughout the Old English period in prose and verse: kings fall enemies, storms fell trees, executioners fell criminals; the closely related noun is fiell "a fall, destruction" and the compound oferfellan "to fell utterly, destroy". A sibling confusion to avoid: Old English fyllan (umlaut of full "full") means "to fill"; fyllan "to fill" and fellan "to fell" are distinct verbs that modern spelling carefully keeps apart.

Middle English: fellen. In Middle English the verb settles as fellen ("to cause to fall, to strike down, to cut down trees"), with the weak past felde / fellede — the direct ancestor of our felled. The period fixes the two great registers that still govern the word: falling timber and falling enemies. Husbandry is fully represented — forests are felled for the plough, the shipwright and the builder — and so is warfare, where "fellen" describes armies mown down and champions struck to earth; the hunting register adds game brought down. The Old English "kill" sense thins out: by the end of the period "to fell" means "to knock or cut down", no longer necessarily "to kill", though hunting prose keeps "felled" for animals brought down.

Early modern English: the participle comes of age. The 16th–17th centuries saw felled harden into its modern roles: passive constructions ("the trees were felled"), participial adjectives ("felled timber", "felled oaks"), and perfect tenses ("has felled"). The grammar of the fall/fell pair was standardised and taught — the schoolroom distinction between "the tree fell" and "he felled the tree" became a fixture of English grammar books — and the verb entered the fixed phrase "to fell a tree", the unmarked term of forestry that survives unchanged in every logging manual today.

The 19th–20th centuries: industry and metaphor.

  • Industrial timber: railways, telegraph poles and the world's timber trade turned tree-felling into a vast industry; "felling" became a measurable, licensed activity (felling licences, felling seasons, felling records); the participle became the word of the stockyard and the mill, applied to millions of tons of oak, pine and jarrah.
  • Sport: boxing and football reportage adopted "felled" for the knock-down blow — "felled by a right hook", "felled in the penalty area".
  • Medicine and misfortune: from the 19th century, impersonal forces fell humans: "felled by fever", "felled by a stroke", "felled by grief"; the tree image is explicit — the victim stood tall and was brought down at one blow. This has become the participle's most common figurative home.
  • Tailoring: "felled seams" (flat-felled seams) preserve the old sense "to beat flat, to cause to lie flat", a technical fossil of the verb's wider medieval range.

Literary test case: Tolkien's dragon. The context sentence — "With a shriek that deafened men, felled trees and split stone, Smaug shot spouting into the air, turned over and crashed down from on high in ruin" — uses "felled" as a participial adjective modifying "trees": the dragon's crashing weight has already knocked the trees flat before the sentence even finishes describing the fall. The word spans the whole history of the verb in a single phrase: the physical violence of striking down, the forestry sense of flattened timber, and the epic register of a great thing brought low.

Homonym distractions. Modern English has four distinct fell words — the causative verb of this card, the past tense of fall, the adjective "fierce, cruel, deadly" (from Old French fel), and the northern noun "a hill, a stretch of moorland" (from Old Norse fjall). The past participle felled belongs unambiguously to the causative; the philological neatness is that only the causative produces this -ed participle.

Summary of the drift. Felled descends, via Germanic's causative machinery, from a PIE root of falling: *pol- → *fallaną "to fall" → *fallijaną "to make fall" → Old English fællan/fellan → Middle English fellen → fell, with the participle felled — "struck down" — carrying thirteen centuries of axe-blows, battle-strokes, logging-mills and obituary sentences, always meaning the same crisp transformation: the tall thing made horizontal.
