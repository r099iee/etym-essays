From the proto-language to Greek. The chain behind CATARACT reaches back to the Indo-European adverb *kom, "beside, with", whose reflexes include Latin cum; the Greek preposition-adverb katá, "down, downwards, along", is thought to continue an old case-form of this root ("along by, down along"), though the precise pre-form is debated and Beekes treats the details as uncertain. Whatever its exact PIE shape, katá became one of the most productive prefixes of Greek, and in the verb katarássein, "to dash against, strike down", it generated the noun kataráktēs: "the thing that rushes down". The second element, arássein, "to dash, smite", is itself a puzzle — it matches nothing securely in the other Indo-European languages and is now regarded by many linguists as a pre-Greek (Aegean substrate) verb absorbed into Greek, which would make the compound older, in effect, than the rest of its vocabulary. The word is attested in Greek prose of the 4th century BCE in the general sense "down-rushing"; by the Hellenistic age it is the ordinary Greek word for a waterfall.

The Nile made it famous. From the 5th century BCE Herodotus had described the rocky barriers of the upper Nile below Elephantine, where the river narrows and pours between granite islands; later geographers — above all Strabo in the early Roman Empire — use kataráktēs as a quasi-technical name for the great falls of the river at and beyond Syene. These are the Nile Cataracts of geography: six principal steps between Aswan and Khartoum, of which the Second, on which Wilde's swallow alights, lies just north of the modern Sudan border. For three millennia they functioned as Egypt's natural southern wall: the pharaohs garrisoned the First Cataract at Elephantine and later at Philae; the Romans kept frontier posts there; and the "Cataract" became a byword for an impassable, roaring barrier of water. In Latin, Pliny the Elder (Natural History 5) describes the celebrated cataractae of the Nile, while Imperial Latin also uses cataracta of a machine — the portcullis or floodgate with which a fortress or harbour could be closed: another thing that "dashes down".

The eye is added. Greek medicine knew an eye disease in which the pupil was clouded, called hypóchysis, "a pouring under" — the theory being that a humour flowed down into the eye and settled before the pupil. The same image of descending water made kataráktēs a natural name for the opacity, and Byzantine medical Greek of the 6th–7th centuries (Aetius of Amida, Paul of Aegina) uses it of the condition, describing couching — pushing the opaque lens out of the line of sight with a needle. Latin medical writing of the 5th century already glosses the affliction, and the Latin term cataracta passes the word into medieval European medicine, where Arabic scholarship (al-Razi, and the 10th-century oculist Ammār ibn ʿAlī of Mosul and his suction-needle) preserved and refined the surgery. The result is the striking fact of the word's history: one root produced both the thunder of rivers and the dimming of sight, because ancient eye-theory itself was built on the metaphor of a waterfall.

English careers. Middle English takes cataract in the 14th–15th centuries in the water senses; the 1611 Bible and its successors give the word its Deluge grandeur, and Milton, describing the Flood in Paradise Lost, opens "the cataracts of heaven". The eye sense is firmly installed in English from the 15th–16th centuries. The 18th century brings Jacques Daviel's planned lens extraction (1752), the 20th century Harold Ridley's intraocular lens (1949) and Charles Kelman's ultrasound phacoemulsification (1967) — so that today the word's loudest home is the operating theatre, not the gorge. Meanwhile the Romantic century transformed the watery sense into an emblem of the sublime — Wordsworth's "sounding cataract / Haunted me like a passion" (Tintern Abbey, 1798) — and journalism keeps the metaphor alive: a "cataract" of speech, of data, of missiles. Both senses, the geological and the medical, remain fully current, joined by the single, hoarse image of something crashing down.

  • Eras: PIE adverb *kom → Greek katá + arássein → kataráktēs → Latin cataracta → Old French cataracte → Middle English → modern English.
  • Sound shifts: the Greek cluster -kt- passes into Latin unchanged; French and English spelling disturb the vowel but keep the -cataract kernel; English stress falls on the first syllable.
  • Society and culture: the Nile frontier, the portcullis, the Scripture flood, Romantic sublimity, the ophthalmic clinic.
