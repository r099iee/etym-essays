- 1802 — "to let saliva run" — drool enters print as a dialectal variant of drivel and is still purely physical.
- 1826 — "to talk nonsense" — the mouth that cannot control its saliva becomes an image of the mind that cannot control its words.
- 1860s — "saliva" — the noun appears, and the substance and the act share one form.
- Late 19th c. — "foolish talk" — the gerund drooling becomes a dismissive label for feeble or sentimental utterance.
- 20th c. — "to show excessive desire" — drooling over a person, a car or a meal; appetite replaces idiocy.
- Late 20th c. — "clinical and comic" — the word names uncontrollable salivation in care settings and open lust in slang.
- 21st c. — "drool-worthy" — internet slang turns the word into a lightweight compliment, while the clinical sense remains in medical writing.
