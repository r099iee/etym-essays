Superfluity descends without break from Latin superfluitas, from superfluus "overflowing" from super- "over, above" + fluere "to flow," from PIE *plew- / *plou- "to flow, float, pour." The image is water spilling over a vessel's rim: what flows over and above capacity. Latin fluere yields a large family: fluent, fluid, flux, influx, effluent, confluence, fluvial. Super- is the productive prefix over-, as in superabundant, supererogatory. Superfluus in Classical Latin meant literally overflowing (superfluo flumine) and figuratively unnecessary, redundant, as in Quintilian's superflua verba (redundant words). The abstract noun superfluitas (genitive superfluitatis) is Late Latin, attested in Tertullian, Jerome and Augustine for excess of possessions, words, or humours.

Old French borrowed it as superfluite (c. 1300, also superfluité) meaning excess, luxury, wordiness. Middle English took superfluite / superfluyte by the early 14th century via Anglo-Norman, initially in Wycliffite sermons and Chaucerian prose condemning superfluite of mete and drinke and superfluite of wordes. The modern -ity ending reflects 15th-16th-century Latinization: English remodels French -ité to Latinate -ity (as in vanity, charity), giving superfluity by Caxton's time, fixed in Elyot (1531) and the King James Bible (1611: "superfluity of naughtiness" James 1:21).

  • Classical Latin (1st c. BCE - 5th c. CE): superfluus as rhetorical and moral term; Seneca warns against superflua (luxuries); Roman law uses superfluum for surplus estate.
  • Medieval moral theology (500-1400): superfluitas becomes a vice-word in monastic rules: superfluity of food, clothing, speech violates poverty and silence. Aquinas treats superfluum as what exceeds need and therefore belongs by natural law to the poor — the exact sense in the source quote where friends supply deficiencies from superfluity.
  • Early Modern humanism (1500-1700): Shakespeare uses superfluity 5 times, most famously Lear 2.4: "O, reason not the need! ... Allow not nature more than nature needs, Man's life's as cheap as beast's" — superfluity as both luxury and rhetorical excess. The King James translators use it for moral overflow.
  • Enlightenment political economy (1700s-1800s): Adam Smith, Malthus and travellers use superfluity for surplus produce that can be shared, traded or taxed. The quoted passage — liberal sharing of superfluity — echoes Smithian and missionary reports of Indigenous generosity.
  • Modern formal register (1900-present): Superfluity retreats to literary, legal and academic style, replaced in speech by surplus, excess, redundancy. Retains moral-literary colour: superfluity of goods, superfluity of evidence, superfluity of staff.

Phonologically the path fluere → superfluity shows regular palatalization and stress shift: Latin /su.perˈfluː.i.taːs/ → Old French /sy.pɛʁ.flɥi.te/ → Middle English /suː.pərˈfluː.ɪ.te/ → modern RP /ˌsuː.pəˈfluː.ɪ.ti/ with secondary stress on su- and primary on flu-. Orthographic -y marks the Latin -itas abstract noun. No debated origin: the super- + fluere analysis is unanimous from Varro to de Vaan; PIE *plew- is securely attested in Greek plein, Sanskrit plavate, Old English flowan.

Literary attestations abound: Chaucer's Parson on superfluitee, Shakespeare's Lear and Merchant of Venice, Milton's Paradise Lost (superfluous branches), Gibbon's Decline on superfluity of wealth, Thoreau on superfluous wealth. The word's persistence owes to its vivid liquid metaphor — excess as overflow — still felt by speakers who hear fluid inside superfluity.
