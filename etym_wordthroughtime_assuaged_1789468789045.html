- Early 14th century (Middle English) - Borrowed from Anglo-French *assuager*, meaning to soften, alleviate physical pain, relieve illness, or lessen the severity of a hardship - Compassionate, medicinal, devotional.
- 15th to 16th century - Expanded to psychological and emotional realms, commonly used for calming wrath, soothing grief, softening divine vengeance, and appeasing wounded honor - Theological, chivalric, rhetorical.
- 17th to 18th century - Frequently employed for physical appetites and visceral needs, such as assuaging hunger, thirst, or restless desires - Classical, literary, descriptive of sensory balance.
- 19th to Present - Retains high literary, psychological, and diplomatic registers, commonly applied to soothing guilt, mitigating anxiety, pacifying geopolitical tensions, or bringing solace to existential suffering - Refined, evocative, contemplative.
