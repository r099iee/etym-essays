- Late 1880s to 1890s - Emerges in English literary journalism following the groundbreaking English and French translations of *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*; initially applied as a literary epithet praising unmatched psychological realism, panoramic social breadth, and vast narrative vitality.
- 1890s to 1910 - Shifts rapidly into a fiery socio-religious and political signifier as Tolstoy's radical Christian anarchist tracts (*The Kingdom of God Is Within You*) circulate globally; "Tolstoyan" colonies and agricultural communes sprout across Britain (Purleigh), the United States, and South Africa (Gandhi's Tolstoy Farm), while the Russian Orthodox Church excommunicates Tolstoy in 1901.
- 1910s to 1940s - Following Tolstoy's death at Astapovo railway station in 1910, the term bifurcates: in the young Soviet Union, Tolstoyan political anarchism and religious communes are systematically suppressed by the Bolsheviks, while his literary realism is canonized; in Western democracies, "Tolstoyan" becomes the gold standard for colossal realist fiction.
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Deepens in critical and philosophical prestige through influential scholarly works such as Isaiah Berlin's *The Hedgehog and the Fox* (1953) and George Steiner's *Tolstoy or Dostoevsky* (1959), solidifying "Tolstoyan" as an enduring archetype for the artistic genius who encompasses the infinite, tangible variety of the world.
- 21st Century - Retains universal renown both in literary criticism—signifying an insatiable narrative hunger for the full tapestry of modern civilization—and in moral philosophy as an emblem of uncompromising ethical conviction against state militarism.
