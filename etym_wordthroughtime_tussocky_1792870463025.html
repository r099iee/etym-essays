- 1540s — the noun appears: tussock surfaces for a tuft or clump of grass, of obscure origin, a dialect word pushing into print.
- 1600s — field use spreads: farmers and travellers use tussock for the coarse clumps stock will not eat, the rough with the smooth of pasture.
- 1700s — the adjective follows: tussocky forms by the regular -y rule to describe land abounding in such clumps, first in agricultural writing.
- 19th c. — empire carries it worldwide: settlers in New Zealand, Australia, and the Falklands name vast tussock grasslands, and the word grows from parish to continent.
- 20th c. — ecology adopts it: tussocky sward becomes a technical habitat term for vole runs, nesting skylarks, and overwintering insects.
- Today — the walker’s warning: still the fittest single word for ankle-turning clump-grass, kept alive by ramblers, poets, and Tolkien’s hindering sward.
