- Old English — the messenger speaks: bodian means to announce, proclaim, or command, the herald's verb, from boda, a messenger.
- Early Middle English — announcing fate: to bode a thing is to declare what shall be, prophecy as a kind of official notice served on the future.
- Late medieval — the omen takes over: the sense shifts from uttering predictions to being a prediction, as signs and delays speak for themselves.
- 16th–17th c. — Shakespeare fixes the idiom: things bode well and bode ill across the plays, from weddings to battlefields, and the collocation hardens.
- 18th–19th c. — the novelist's weather-glass: Austen and Dickens let manners, letters, and skies bode the turn of plots, domesticating the seer into the drawing room.
- 20th c. — Tolkien's wizard: Gandalf's lateness boding ill shows the verb doing ancient work in modern fantasy, reading character as omen.
- Today — the fixed pair survives: free uses fade, but bodes well and bodes ill remain the standard English verdict on any doubtful sign.
