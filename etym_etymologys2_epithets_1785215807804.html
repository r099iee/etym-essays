EPITHETS — compound chain built on two PIE roots. Chain for each element: CHAIN 1 — the *epi- element ("on, upon, at"): - Proto-Indo-European *epi "on, upon, at, near" (reconstructed; also *opi; attested in the ancient daughter languages as Greek epí, Latin ob "towards, against," Sanskrit ápi "beside, upon") → Ancient Greek epí "upon, on, at" (preposition and prefix, attested from the earliest Greek records, c. 8th c. BCE) → the prefix epi- "upon" as used in Greek compounds. CHAIN 2 — the *dheh₁- element ("to put, place, set"): - Proto-Indo-European *dheh₁- "to put, place, set" (reconstructed; the source of Sanskrit dadhāti "he places," Avestan dadāiti "he puts," Greek tithénai "to put," Latin facere "to make, do," and the Germanic do/deed) → Ancient Greek tithénai "to put, place, set" (verb, attested) → Greek base the- / the- (zero-grade of the root as seen in thetón "placed"). CHAIN 3 — the compounded Greek formation: - Ancient Greek epitithénai "to put upon, place on, lay on" (compound of epi- "upon" + tithénai "to put"; the verb is attested from classical Greek) → Greek epithetós "put on, added, attributed" (verbal adjective from epitithénai; attested) → Greek tò epítheton (ónoma) "the added name, an attribute, a descriptive appellation" (neuter substantivized form, attested as the grammarians' term for the adjective, 4th c. BCE onwards, e.g. in the grammatical tradition and in Aristotle's rhetoric) → Latin epítheton "a descriptive epithet, an attributive word appended to a noun" (loan from Greek; attested in Roman grammatical and rhetorical writers, 1st c. BCE–CE) → French épithète "epithet, attributive adjective" (learned loan, attested from the 12th–16th c.) → Early Modern English epitheton / epithet "a descriptive term, an attributive word" (attested 16th century) → Modern English EPITHETS (plural noun, "descriptive or characterizing words or phrases applied to a person or thing"). Notes and nuances: - The two PIE roots are not a single chain: *epi- and *dheh₁- combined only within Greek, when epi- was prefixed to tithenai. The unified Greek compound epitithénai is the true point of fusion, and everything after it is a single attested line. - The ultimate source of the whole modern word is therefore Greek, not Latin: Latin merely borrowed the finished noun epitheton from Greek and passed it on through French into English. - Sister loan-translation: the grammatical category "adjective" is a calque of the same Greek epitheton ónoma — Latin adjectivus "that which is added," from ad- "to" + iacere "to throw," rendered "added to the noun." It is a parallel expression of the identical idea, not a stage in the chain. - The Greek root *dheh₁- has famous siblings in English (do, deed, deem, the- in theme/thesis) and in Sanskrit (dadhāti "he places"); these are cognates, not ancestors of epithet. - No stage is invented: the verbal adjective epithetós and the substantivized epitheton are both attested in Greek, the Latin and French forms in the written record, and English epitheton (16th c.) before the clipped epithet.
