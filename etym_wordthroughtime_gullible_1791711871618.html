- [Early 19th Century (c. 1810s-1820s)] - Coined in Regency England from the noun/verb 'gull' (a dupe, to cheat) as fashionable colloquial slang used among urban dandies and satirists - Humorous, slightly condescending urban cynicism directed at naive provincials.
- [Mid-to-Late 19th Century] - Rapid transition from slang into formal standard English prose, appearing in legal transcripts, novels, and philosophical essays (such as John Stuart Mill and Ralph Waldo Emerson) - Moral warning against intellectual indolence, financial fraud, and credulity.
- [Early 20th Century] - Codification in social psychology, consumer advocacy, and political theory as mass media, yellow journalism, and propaganda raised alarm over collective psychological vulnerability - Sociological anxiety, civic critique, and media literacy concerns.
- [Contemporary Usage] - Universal psychological descriptor for susceptibility to internet scams, misinformation, conspiracy theories, and phishing attacks - Ubiquitous cognitive evaluation carrying a blend of cautionary admonition and exasperated pity.
