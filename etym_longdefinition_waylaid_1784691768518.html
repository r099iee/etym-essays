WAYLAID is the past tense and past participle of the transitive verb waylay, meaning to lie in wait for a person or party upon a road or path in order to stop, accost, rob, or attack them. The word denotes a deliberate and premeditated interception: the agent conceals himself beside the route, allows the victim to approach unsuspecting, and then springs out to block the journey. Historically the term belonged to the vocabulary of highway robbery, brigandage, and military ambuscade — the verb named the stock profession of the outlaw who preyed upon travellers on the king's highway. In modern usage it has widened to describe any unexpected and importunate stopping of someone — being waylaid in a corridor by a colleague eager for conversation — but the defining ideas remain those of surprise, secrecy, and the interruption of onward progress. It is a verb of obstruction as much as of violence: to waylay someone is to halt their movement by stealth rather than to pursue them openly.

Polysemy and modern usage:

  • Highway-robbery sense (the original and core sense): to intercept and rob, attack, or capture a traveller by lying in wait beside a road or path; the stock crime of the highwayman and the outlaw, and the sense that dominates historical and adventure writing.
  • Military sense: to ambush a marching force, a convoy, or a courier by concealing troops beside their line of advance; close to 'ambushed' but stressing the concealment beside a route of travel rather than at a chosen point of battle.
  • Metaphoric, everyday sense (the modern widening): to be stopped, detained, or cornered unexpectedly — 'I was waylaid in the corridor by a colleague,' 'waylaid by a phone call,' 'waylaid by paperwork'; the violence has drained away, leaving surprise, secrecy, and interruption.
  • Figurative of circumstances: to be overtaken and impeded by events — 'waylaid by illness,' 'waylaid by the weather' — where forces of circumstance are imagined as hidden agents lying in wait on one's path.
  • Passive and adjectival use: as a participle, waylaid is very common after 'be' or 'get' ('they were waylaid,' 'got waylaid'), and it can function adjectivally ('a waylaid messenger,' 'the waylaid caravan').

Historical and semantic bridge: the bridge from 'rob on the road' to 'interrupt unexpectedly' is the image of the ambush itself. The verb was born in the world of medieval and early modern highway robbery, where the concealed outlaw who sprang from the roadside was the terror of every journey. As highway robbery declined, the word's drama was transferred to any sudden, secret, unwelcome interception: the talkative colleague in the corridor is the modern highwayman, and the person stopped is 'waylaid.' What survives in every sense is the geometry of the ambush — a moving traveller, a concealed agent, a sudden halt. The word thus keeps its old cunning and menace even in its most mundane modern uses, and it is never quite neutral: to be waylaid is to be caught off guard.

In the literary context of this card (from Chapter 2, 'Roast Mutton', of The Hobbit), the word is used with its full old-fashioned force. Gandalf is reporting what two of Elrond's people told him: three trolls had come down from the mountains, settled in the woods not far from the road, frightened everyone away from the district, and waylaid strangers. The setting is a wilderness road in Middle-earth, and the trolls' waylaying is a predatory economy — they lie hidden among the trees beside the highway and intercept lone travellers, whom they capture, cook, and eat. The word therefore carries menace and cunning: the trolls do not pursue; they wait, concealed, exploiting the certainty that travellers must pass that way. The participle also points forward to the very next scene, in which Bilbo, sent to investigate a fire, is himself seized by the trolls while trying to pick Tom's pocket — a stranger waylaid by an enemy he never saw until it was too late.

Grammar and register: waylaid is an irregular past form following lay → laid (never 'waylayed'); it is accented on the second syllable. In register the word is formal-to-literary and slightly old-fashioned; its natural homes are historical fiction, fantasy, journalism (for dramatic interruption), and any account of being caught unawares.
