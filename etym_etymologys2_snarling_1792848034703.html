Middle Dutch and Middle Low German (13th–14th c.)
snarren
‘to growl, grumble, rattle’
↓
Middle English (late 14th c.)
snar
‘to growl, snap at’
↓
Early Modern English (1590s)
snarl
‘to growl with bared teeth’
↓
Modern English (participle)
snarling
‘growling fiercely, speaking harshly’

Note: snarl in this growling sense is a frequentative built on Middle English snar with the -le suffix marking repeated action. Its deeper ancestry is imitative Germanic vocabulary shared with Dutch snarren and German schnarren, rather than a datable classical root, and scholars describe the ultimate origin as echoic. It is wholly distinct from snarl meaning a tangle, which descends from snare.
