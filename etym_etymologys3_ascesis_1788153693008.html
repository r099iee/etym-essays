1. The gymnasium: training in ancient Greece. The word ascesis begins in Greek sport. The verb askeîn first meant "to work raw materials — to fashion, to adorn" — a craftsman’s word — and from this the sense of "to train, to exercise" developed in the 5th century BCE: the athlete’s askēsis was his whole regimen of diet, oil, exertion, and repetition by which nature was perfected into victory. For the Greeks the gymnasium was a moral institution: training formed character as much as muscle, and the disciplined body was a civic ornament. The noun sat at the centre of Greek pedagogy, uniting the physical and the ethical in one idea of practised excellence.

2. From athletes to philosophers. The philosophical schools seized on the prestige of athletic askēsis. Socrates spoke of the "training" (askēsis) of the soul; the Cynics made spectacular self-hardening — poverty, endurance, shameless plainness — their public demonstration of freedom; the Stoics systematised "exercises" of attention, acceptance, and self-command, so that the sage’s virtue was, in effect, a lifelong gymnasium of the will. In all these uses the word keeps its athletic optimism: discipline is not punishment but strength-building, the way a body or soul becomes what it is meant to be.

3. The Christian transformation. Early Christianity took over the vocabulary of training for the pilgrimage of the soul. The ascetics of Syria and Egypt — Antony, the desert fathers, the stylites — practised an ascesis of fasting, vigil, celibacy, and prayer, conceived explicitly as athlēsis, the athlete’s contest, against the passions and demons. The Church’s monastic rules (Benedict, Basil, Pachomius) institutionalised the regimen; Latin Ecclesiastical writers naturalised the Greek noun as ascesis, so that medieval Europe inherited not asceticism as bare denial but a method of spiritual formation continuous with the gymnasium.

  • Religious register: ascesis = the rule and practice of ascetics, governing food, sleep, labour, prayer.
  • Philosophical register (20th c.): Foucault’s late seminars restore askēsis as "the practices of the self" — techniques by which a subject actively constitutes itself, freed from the model of mere prohibition.

4. Ascesis in art and criticism. In the 19th century the word migrates from religion to aesthetics. Pater uses it here with deliberate oxymoronic charm: the "freshness" of the Renaissance’s youth is the "charm of ascesis" — of the austere, serious girding of the loins — over against the "refined and comely decadence" of its autumn. For Pater, Wilde, and their heirs, the greatest art is born of rigorous renunciation: discipline of means, scorn of the easy effect. Modern movements (Flaubert’s "art for art’s sake", Pound’s craft-dogma, minimalist aesthetics) continue this line, and scholarship now uses ascesis as the precise term for the artist’s and ascetic’s shared technique: deliberate self-limitation in the service of excellence.

5. Modern standing.Ascesis remains a learned word: exact in the history of religion, vital in the theory of the self, and quietly indispensable to criticism describing how anything — a body, a soul, a poem — is formed by discipline. Its root meaning has never been lost: ascesis is training, and training is how greatness is made.
