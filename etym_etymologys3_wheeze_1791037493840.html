Wheeze is a textbook imitative word whose form has barely changed in a thousand years because its sound keeps refreshing its meaning. Its history is the history of breath heard and written down.

The deepest root is Proto-Indo-European *hwes- or *kwes- to breathe out, blow, hiss, which also yields Latin questus complaint, Sanskrit svas- to breathe, and Germanic hw- breath words. In Proto-Germanic this became *hwesan, *hwestan to wheeze, whistle, hiss, preserved in Old Norse hvæsa, hvaesa to hiss, Old English hweos wheezing and Middle English whesen, qwesen. The initial wh- /hw/ cluster itself is breathy: voiceless labiovelar approximant plus vowel, a puff of air. The long vowel ee /iː/ sustains the whistle; the final -ze /z/ prolongs frication. Spelling wobbles — whese, wheze, queeze — reflect dialect attempts to catch the noise. By Chaucer's time whesen meant to make a hissing or whistling sound in breathing, used of men, horses and bellows.

  • Sound symbolism: wh- onset as exhalation, -ee- as high tone, -ze as continuous friction; the word is iconic across Germanic.
  • Cognates: Norwegian hvese to hiss, Swedish väsa to hiss, Dutch hijzen dialectal; English whistle, whisper, whew share the breathy wh- family.
  • Early attestations: 14th-c. medical and farriery texts for broken-winded horses; 16th-c. wheezy for asthmatic persons.

From the 18th century physicians systematized wheeze as a technical sign. Laennec's stethoscope (1816) let doctors distinguish rhonchi, sibili and wheezes; Victorian textbooks list wheezing as cardinal in asthma, croup, bronchitis and emphysema. The noun pluralized — wheezes — and adjectivized — wheezy chest, wheezy laugh. Industrial fogs, cigarette smoking and London particulars made wheezing a public sound; the old wheezer horse became a metaphor for any worn-out breather, human or machine. Accordions, concertinas, harmoniums and leaky tyres were said to wheeze, extending the sense from lungs to bellows-machines that also push air through reeds.

  • Medical shift: lay symptom → auscultatory sign → asthma-action-plan trigger; inhalers and peak-flow meters now accompany the word.
  • Literary colour: Dickensian clerks wheeze, Wodehouse butlers wheeze with suppressed laughter, war poets wheeze with gas.

The slang sense — a clever wheeze — emerges in early 19th-century cant and university slang. Partridge suggests it derives from horse-dealers' wheeze to hide a horse's broken wind by a trick, hence any dodge, or from the puff of self-satisfaction after a joke. By the 1860s newspapers wrote of a new financial wheeze and a clever election wheeze. The sense flourished in British journalism: publicity wheeze, cost-cutting wheeze, tax wheeze. It never fully crossed to American English, where gimmick, shtick, ploy dominate, preserving its British flavour. The context You would not have thought of a wheeze like that in a million years is exactly this admiring sense: what an ingenious dodge.

Phonetically the Great Vowel Shift raised Middle English /eː/ to modern /iː/, so whese /hweːzə/ became /wiːz/. The silent h in wh- is now variably pronounced; RP keeps /w/ while some Scots and Americans keep /hw/. No affixation was needed; conversion supplied noun from verb and back. Today the two senses coexist without confusion because context disambiguates lungs from laughs.
