SLITHERED is the past tense and past participle of the verb SLITHER: to slide or glide along a surface with an unsteady, unstable, often writhing motion, especially where footing or friction is lost and control is imperfect. The word names a movement that is neither a clean slide nor a fall: slithering is sliding with difficulty — the feet repeatedly lose their grip, the body skids and recovers, the motion is prolonged, grating and hard to stop. It is the motion of a snake across the ground, of a climber or rider skidding down loose, steep scree, of a heavy animal or person on mud or ice who slides a little, catches, slides again. Three features define it: unsteadiness (the movement is not under full control), friction (the body scrapes against the surface — slither is a word full of the sound and feel of contact), and repetition (it goes on, in a series of small uncontrolled slips, rather than in one clean motion). Where slide is smooth and deliberate and slip is sudden and short, slither is the drawn-out, grating, ungainly middle ground — the motion of anything that wants to grip but keeps losing its footing. In The Hobbit the word appears at the head of Chapter 3, in Bilbo's memory of the descent into the secret valley: "Bilbo never forgot the way they slithered and slipped in the dusk down the steep zig-zag path into the secret valley of Rivendell." The verb is exactly right for the scene: the travellers are coming down a steep, zig-zag mountain path in the dusk, the light failing, the footing loose, so that they neither walk properly nor fall — they skid and slide and catch themselves, sliding and slipping and recovering all the way down. 'Slithered and slipped' pairs a prolonged, grating, repeated motion with a sudden, short one, giving the descent its alternation of uncontrolled slides and sharp stumbles; and because Bilbo 'never forgot' it, the slithering is charged with the strangeness and danger of that first approach to the hidden valley — a threshold crossed by a perilous, comic, undignified descent. The word keeps a faint physical comedy, the picture of laden dwarves and a hobbit skidding down the zig-zags, that is characteristic of the novel's earthy humour. Polysemy & secondary senses:
  • Animal motion: the signature verb of snakes and reptiles — a serpent slithers, writhing and gliding without legs; by extension, eels, worms, slugs and any limbless or gliding creature slithers.
  • Human and vehicle motion: people slither down banks, slopes, mud, ice, scree and loose stone; vehicles slither on ice or loose ground; the sense is always of unstable, half-controlled sliding.
  • Figurative and extended: 'slither' as a noun names such a glide or a snake-like path; 'slithery' (the adjective) means slippery, unsteady, evasive; to slither into, out of or away is to move evasively, stealthily, avoiding direct confrontation — something 'slithers' through bureaucracy, or a rumour slithers through a community. In political or moral language, 'slithering' can suggest a slippery, evasive course or a decline without control.
  • Technical and dialect: in dialect use slither is also a sliddering, grating slide; 'slithered' is the ordinary past of the modern verb, formed regularly with -ed, and is now by far the commoner spelling and sense of the ancient frequentative.
  • The -th- sound: slither's soft 'th' gives the word its long, slithering, friction-heavy sound, and the verb is often used precisely for its onomatopoeia — you can hear the scrape in 'slithered.'
Compared with its family: slide is smooth, continuous, often deliberate and controlled; slip is sudden, short and involuntary, over in an instant; skid is the loss of traction of a wheel or vehicle; skate is a smooth glide on ice or rollers; skitter is quick, light, skipping movement over a surface; scramble is fast, ungainly, effortful movement over rough ground; stumble is tripping and lurching while walking upright; writhe is twisting and contorting in place. Slither uniquely combines sliding, friction, repetition and imperfect control — it is the verb of losing one's footing repeatedly without ever quite falling.
