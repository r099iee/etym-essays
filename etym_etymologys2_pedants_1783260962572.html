PIE root *peh₂u- / *peu-, "small, little, few" (attested widely in Indo-European: cf. Latin paucus "few", English few, Greek pauros) ↓ Ancient Greek παῖς / pais (genitive παιδός / paidós), "child" — from *peu-, the "little one"; attested from Homer (8th c. BCE) ↓ Ancient Greek παιδαγωγός / paidagōgos, "child-leader; one who leads boys to school; tutor" — compound of παῖς "child" + ἀγωγός "leading" (from ἄγειν "to lead", PIE *h₂eǵ- "to drive"); attested from the 5th c. BCE ↓ Latin paedagōgus, "tutor, pedagogue" — Greek loanword; attested from the 1st c. BCE (Cicero) ↓ Late Latin paedagōgans / *paedagōgantem (present participle), "one who teaches, instructing" — the Vulgar Latin present-participial form traditionally held to underlie the Italian word (the intermediate is reconstructed, not directly attested) ↓ Italian pedante, "schoolmaster, tutor" — attested from the early 16th century (Tuscan usage) ↓ Middle French pédant, "schoolmaster" — attested from 1566 ↓ English pedant, "schoolmaster; one who makes an ostentatious display of learning" — first recorded 1588 ↓ pedants (plural of pedant) — the target word, "persons excessively concerned with minor details and rules in displaying academic learning" ◄ TARGET DEBATED / NUANCED: The step from Latin paedagōgus to Italian pedante is the one debated link in the chain. The standard account (Diez; modern Italian etymological dictionaries) posits a Vulgar Latin present-participial agent noun *paedagōgans/*paedagōgantem "one who teaches", which would regularly yield Italian -ante forms; an alternative holds that pedante arose within Tuscan directly from pedagogo with the participial suffix. Either way the Greek base παιδαγωγός is certain. The PIE etymon *peh₂u-/*peu- "few, little" is well established for παῖς "child" (the "little one"), though some scholars prefer a root *peh₂-w- with different laryngeal detail; the second element rests securely on *h₂eǵ- "to drive". No further links are genuinely uncertain.
