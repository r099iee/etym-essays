“Parlour” entered English as the private room of conversation — the place set aside in a house for talking with guests, rather than for cooking or sleeping.
“Parlour” in the 19th century became the Victorian parlour of gentility: the formal, best-furnished guest room where families received visitors and played parlour games, and where the parlour-maid served.
“Parlour” in the 20th century softened and migrated — ice-cream parlours, beauty parlours — while the home’s formal room was renamed the living-room, leaving “parlour” cosy, old-fashioned, and homely.
“Parlour” today survives as both a period word for a sitting-room and a commercial suffix, still faintly humming with guest-facing hospitality.
