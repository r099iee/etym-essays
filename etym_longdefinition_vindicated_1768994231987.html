vindicated — the past participle of to vindicate, "to clear from blame or doubt, to justify or prove right" — is here given a philosophical charge rare in ordinary use. The sentence runs: standing over the fallen men "he feels mankind itself vindicated." The word's nuance in this literary context is existential rather than legal: it is not a court clearing the accused but the whole human species being proved worthy, justified, redeemed as a project, by the sheer outlandish diversity of its members — men "from lands so far and queer" that their mere existence, even bleeding in the mud, constitutes proof. The tone is grim and awe-struck: vindicated names a sudden settling of an unspoken question — was mankind worth it? — answered in the affirmative by the unimaginable variety of human forms. There is violence in the vicinity of the word — the men lie bleeding — yet vindication here transcends the immediate cruelty; it is the judgment passed by a shocked witness on the species to which he and the strangers both belong. The word thus carries a double weight: it admits that mankind stood accused (of poverty, narrowness, failing its own promise), and it announces that the accusation has been answered.

Primary senses: (1) to clear a person of blame, suspicion, or a charge; (2) to prove or justify a claim, cause, idea, or decision to be right after it has been doubted or attacked. In both senses the core is rehabilitation against doubt — the rightness must first be challenged for vindication to be possible.

  • Legal sense: to clear an accused person of a charge or imputation; to establish innocence. Historically the strongest and oldest sense — the vindicated man walks out of court.
  • Intellectual sense: to defend and prove a thesis, cause, or reputation against attack; 17th–18th-century polemics were full of "vindications" of religious, political, and literary positions.
  • Everyday/prophetic sense: modern usage, in which a prediction, decision, or instinct is "vindicated" by later events — proven right after doubt, often without any formal accusation at all.
  • Philosophical/existential sense: the sense here — a species, a way of life, or an ideal is vindicated when its worth is demonstrated by events; the word can climb from the courtroom to the judgement of all humanity.
  • Etymological ghost-sense: the underlying Latin vindicāre also meant "to claim, to avenge," and that older violence still lingers in vindictive, revengeful, and the legal vindicta of Roman law — vindication's "triumph" retains a faint trace of its old "enforcement" force.

Boundary words map the word's special character. Justified is the closest, but it argues a rightness in terms of reasons or principles and is coldly intellectual; vindicated implies that the rightness was challenged, doubted, or attacked and has now been triumphantly confirmed — it is justification after a fight. Exonerated and acquitted are strictly legal, clearing an individual of a specific charge by inquiry or trial, and cannot easily be applied to a species; vindication can. Validated confirms that something is sound or effective — an experimental or technical approval — where vindicate asserts moral or epistemic rightness against opposition. Redeemed is theological, implying sin and salvation, whereas vindication involves doubt and proof rather than guilt and grace. Avenged preserves the old root's violence, requiring a wrongdoer to be punished; vindication may involve no wrongdoer at all. In collocation the word runs from the courtroom ("the defendant was fully vindicated") through the laboratory ("the theory was vindicated by experiment") to the existential ("mankind itself vindicated"), and its register is formal and triumphant — the register this passage exploits at full stretch.
