LADEN — evolution essay

The story of "laden" is the story of a participle that grew up and left home. Its Germanic ancestor is a strong verb of loading and piling, reconstructed as Proto-Germanic *hlaþaną, "to load, to heap up, to draw up (water)," attested in every major branch of the family: Old English hladan, Old Norse hlaða, Dutch laden, German laden. The verb's ultimate Indo-European pedigree is itself a matter of scholarly debate: the most commonly cited reconstruction is a PIE root *klā- or *kleh₂-, "to spread out flat, to lay out," from which the Germanic "load" verb would have taken its sense of laying a burden upon a carrier; but the reconstruction is not universally accepted, and some authorities prefer to leave *hlaþaną without a secure Indo-European etymology. What is certain is that the family is genuinely Germanic and old, with no clear cognates in the southern branches of Indo-European — an interesting fact in its own right, since it suggests that the Germanic-speaking peoples developed their own word for the labor of loading.

In Old English the verb hladan was already rich: it meant "to load, to burden, to charge" — a ship, a wagon, a beast, a man — and, in a separate and striking sense, "to draw up water," of a bucket, a pump, or a ship taking on water; from this second sense descends the noun ladle, the tool for lifting liquid (Old English hlædel, "a ladle," from the verb). The past participle hladen ("loaded, charged") names the resulting state: a ship hladen with goods, a man hladen with cares. Middle English continues the participle as laden and extends it: ships, wagons, and beasts are laden with goods; trees are laden with fruit; and the word begins to attach itself to the moral freight of the period — the soul laden with sins, the heart laden with sorrow.

The decisive modern development is the participle's emancipation. In the 15th–17th centuries "laden" detaches from the verb "lade" (which itself falls out of common speech, surviving only in dialect and in the sense "to draw water") and hardens into a pure adjective: it now stands alone, meaning "heavily loaded, weighed down," and it carries with it a grave, formal, even scriptural tone. The King James Bible and the homiletic tradition made the figurative sense permanent: "a conscience laden with guilt," "a heart laden with care," "souls laden with sins" — the participle became the standard word for moral weight, the heavy thing that presses on the inner person. In the 18th and 19th centuries the word is equally at home in physical and figurative registers: a ship laden with treasure, a table laden with food, a woman laden with jewelry, a sky laden with rain, and, in the great age of empire and commerce, the laden holds and laden backs of the colonial economy.

The 19th and 20th centuries gave the word a new, productive life in compounding. Because "laden" so neatly means "full of, saturated with, weighed down by," it became a combining workhorse of journalism, science, and advertising: debt-laden, stress-laden, gas-laden, carbon-laden, image-laden, legend-laden, guilt-laden, information-laden, traffic-laden. In this modern use the word has lost none of its dignity — a "debt-laden" economy is weighed down in exactly the old sense — but it has widened from concrete cargo to every kind of burden, literal and metaphorical. Throughout, the historical connection to "load" (a separate Old English word lād, "way, course, journey," whose sense converged with "lade") has made the whole family feel like one word, so that "laden," "lade," "load," and "ladle" are felt as siblings even though their recorded histories are partly independent.

In the passage before us the word keeps its oldest physical meaning with full force: "laden beasts of burden" — animals visibly bowed under their packs, part of a scene of iron-veined rock, ferro-oxide, and basaltic dust in which everything is heavy, mineral, and slow, and the darkness beyond is "heavy as if with a weaving of black webs." "Laden" thus spans the whole history of the load: from a Germanic verb of piling goods, to the participle of the cargo itself, to the modern adjective of every burden the world lays on anything, animate or inanimate, that must carry it.
