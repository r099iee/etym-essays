The evolution of piqued is the story of a single physical image — the prick — travelling from the prehistoric workshop and hunt to the psychology of the drawing room. The trail begins in the reconstructed Indo-European verbal root *peig- (variant *peik-), meaning "to pierce, prick, stick, or mark by punching." In the earliest attested Indo-European cultures this root governed decoration, ritual marking, and weaponry: Latin pingere "to paint, embroider" originally described pricking threads through fabric, Greek pikros "sharp, bitter" named the taste that stings the tongue, and Germanic *pikkōną described the pecking of a bird's beak, giving English pick, peck, and (through a spear sense) pike.

Into this sharp world came the Romance interlude. In Late Latin and early medieval speech a new verb *piccāre "to prick" appears across Western Europe. Whether it descends directly from *peig- or was independently coined as an imitative "sharp" sound-word is a genuine scholarly debate; what is certain is that it produced Italian piccare, Spanish and Portuguese picar, and Old French piquer. In French the verb grew extraordinarily fertile. It gave military vocabulary: picquier the pikeman, picquet the sharp stake driven into the ground to tether horses — later the picket of a protest line. It gave culinary vocabulary: piquant, food that "pricks" the tongue with agreeable sharpness. And, crucially for our word, it began to be applied to the mind: a clever remark or an intriguing idea could "prick" the attention just as a needle pricks the skin.

The decisive semantic events happened in France and then England during the 16th and 17th centuries. In French, the noun pique settled into a specific psychological meaning — a sudden fit of resentment, a stinging of wounded pride caused by a slight. The verb piquer simultaneously developed its reflexive sense "to take offence," and English borrowed both. OED's record shows the noun pique in the resentment sense from the early 17th century and the verb to pique by the 1660s. For a generation the verb still carried its physical force — one could "pique" a thing meaning to prick or wound it — but the psychological senses quickly dominated.

By the 18th and 19th centuries piqued had split into the two channels it occupies today, and it became a favourite of the novelists. Jane Austen uses the reflexive "piqued herself upon" to render Mary's self-complacent pride in Pride and Prejudice; elsewhere in the same novel she writes of curiosity being piqued and of tempers being piqued. The pull between "stimulated" and "irritated" was already complete. Notice that the same root produced two separate English borrowings that must not be confused with senses of piqued: piqué, the quilted cotton fabric (from French "pricked, stitched"), and piquet, the card game.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *peig- → Vulgar Latin *piccāre → Old French piquer (the intervocalic *-kk- becomes a single [k] in French orthography) → English pique [piːk]; the final -d of the participle is the regular English past suffix.
  • Semantic drift: concrete "prick" → culinary "sharp taste" (piquant) → military "sharp stake" (picket) → mental "prick of curiosity" → "prick of wounded pride."
  • Socio-cultural factors: courtly and drawing-room culture prized the vocabulary of refined emotional precision; the sharp "smart" of wounded vanity was exactly the register Austen and her circle needed.
  • Literary attestations: Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice (1813) — "piqued herself upon the solidity of her reflections"; the word recurs throughout 18th–19th-century fiction as the standard verb for curiosity woken and pride stung.
  • Modern situation: "piqued my curiosity" is standard and lively; "piqued by a snub" is formal-literary; the reflexive "pique oneself upon" is archaic and now only echoes in pastiche.

Today piqued is a compact lesson in how a language turns hardware into psychology. The needle that once tattooed hides, pierced ears, and stung tongues now pricks curiosity awake and wounds pride — three thousand years of meaning folded into one sharp point.
