- Classical Latin - Physical origin: *Callōsus* meant thick-skinned, hardened, or covered with hard skin (*callus*).
- Late 16th Century (c. 1570s) - English medical use: *Callous* entered English as a medical adjective describing hardened bodily tissue or skin thickened by friction.
- Early to Mid-17th Century - Metaphorical moral transfer: Transitioned into moral philosophy, theology, and drama to describe a hardened conscience, an unfeeling heart, or moral desensitization; the adverb *callously* formed in the 1650s.
- 18th to 19th Century - Social and narrative prominence: Widely used in Victorian fiction, journalism, and legal courtrooms to describe cruel disregard for poverty, friendship, or human suffering.
- Contemporary Usage - Standard, powerful adverb across journalism, literature, law, and everyday speech denoting reprehensible emotional coldness and hardened indifference to others' plight.
