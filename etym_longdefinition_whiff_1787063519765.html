Whiff (noun, also verb) denotes something that is present only faintly, briefly and at the very edge of perception. In Tolkien's sentence, Smaug's breathing produces “scarcely a snore more than a whiff of unseen steam” — that is, the faintest, most barely-there wisp of breath. The word's genius is its capture of brevity and slightness: a whiff is a momentary, delicate presence, whether of air, scent, smoke or — by figurative extension — a hint or trace of an idea, quality or emotion. It always implies the thinnest edge of awareness: enough to register, but not enough to grasp or hold.

Core senses.

  • Sense 1 — a faint odour or scent (most common): “a whiff of perfume”, “the whiff of freshly baked bread”. The smell is usually carried on the air, encountered briefly, and is often pleasant or evocative, though it can be unpleasant (“a whiff of stale beer”).
  • Sense 2 — a puff or current of air, smoke or vapour: “a whiff of smoke”, “a whiff of cool sea air”. This is the physical, meteorological sense from which all others derive.
  • Sense 3 — figurative, a slight trace or hint (secondary modern sense): “a whiff of scandal”, “a whiff of danger”, “the whiff of revolution in the air”. Here the word refers not to literal smell but to the faint early signs or atmosphere of something — usually something subtle, suggestive, or mildly threatening.
  • Sense 4 — informal, a brief sniff or intake of breath: to take a whiff of something is to sniff it quickly and briefly.

Verb uses. As a verb, whiff means to blow or puff gently; to sniff or smell briefly (“she whiffed the flowers”); and, in baseball slang, to swing and miss (“to whiff at a pitch”), a vivid figurative transfer from the air that the bat meets instead of the ball. The verb is far less common than the noun. In British usage the verb is occasionally found meaning “to emit a smell” or “to travel through the air”, and in colloquial American English “to whiff” is the standard sports-word for striking out.

Grammar and morphology. Whiff is a countable noun (plural whiffs), usually used with “a ... of” constructions (“a whiff of ...”). It is also an intransitive and transitive verb. There is a related informal verb whiffle (to move or blow in puffs or gusts, or to vacillate), which may share the word's expressive origin. No common adjective is formed directly from the noun.

Phrases and collocations.

  • “A whiff of smoke / garlic / perfume / sea air” — faint scent or vapour.
  • “Catch a whiff of something” — to smell it faintly and briefly.
  • “A whiff of scandal / danger / controversy” — a hint or early trace.
  • “Not a whiff of ...” — nothing at all (“not a whiff of evidence”).
  • “To whiff at a pitch” (baseball) — to miss the ball completely.

Register and tone. The word is everyday but slightly vivid; it is more concrete and evanescent than “smell” and more delicate than “puff”. In literary usage (as in Tolkien) it contributes atmosphere and immediacy, describing precisely the kind of barely perceptible sensory detail that conjures a scene. Because a whiff is faint and fleeting, the word often carries a sense of the ephemeral, the elusive, or the tantalizingly incomplete — a quality exploited in poetry, fiction and journalism alike. It is also frequently used with a hint of delight or warning, since faintness makes the object either tempting (a whiff of a delicious dinner) or ominous (a whiff of danger).

Historical note on senses. The earliest recorded sense (16th century) was physical — a sudden light gust of wind or puff of air or smoke. From the late 16th century, the sense of “a faint odour” arose, and by the 19th–20th centuries the figurative “trace/hint” sense had fully developed, so that today one speaks of a “whiff of controversy” as readily as a “whiff of perfume”. The semantic engine behind this development is the simple fact that a smell is experienced only as air reaching the nose — the puff of air and the scent it carries are inseparable in human experience, so the word for the puff naturally became the word for the scent, and then for any faint trace at all.
