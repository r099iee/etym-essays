Linear derivation chain for MAGNETISM (the root is non-Indo-European; no PIE root is reconstructable): Stage 1 — Pre-Greek (Aegean / Anatolian substrate) name *Magn-, origin unknown: the stem underlying the Greek place-name Magnesia (Μαγνησία) "Magnesia," the district of Thessaly, and the tribe-name Magnetes (Μάγνητες) "the Magnetes." No PIE root can be reconstructed; it is a toponymic fossil of the pre-Indo-European substrate. Stage 2 — Ancient Greek Μαγνῆτις λίθος (Magnetis lithos), attested by the 6th–5th centuries BCE: "the Magnesian stone," i.e. the lodestone, the magnetic iron ore found near Magnesia (and near Magnesia ad Sipylum in Lydia). Stage 3 — Ancient Greek μαγνήτης (magnetes), attested c. 380 BCE (Plato, Ion): "the magnet," a substantivized adjective; already used figuratively in the Ion of poetic attraction, the poet "like a magnet" drawing his audience through a chain of inspiration. Stage 4 — Latin magnes, genitive magnetis, attested 1st century BCE–CE (Pliny the Elder, Natural History, 1st century CE): "lodestone, magnet," a direct loan from Greek. Stage 5 — Late / Medieval Latin magneticus, attested from late antiquity (e.g. Isidore of Seville, 7th century CE): "of the magnet, magnetic." Stage 6 — Neo-Latin magnetismus, 17th-century scientific Latin, established in the age of William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600): "magnetic attraction," the abstract noun of the new science. Stage 7 — English magnetism, attested from the 1610s in the physical sense; the figurative "personal magnetism" develops in the 19th century, transmitted through Mesmer's "animal magnetism" (1770s–1780s). Side-branch note: the same toponym underlies Latin magnesia, the white alkaline earth, and the element magnesium (coined by Humphry Davy, 1808); the ore magnetite (Fe3O4) also bears the name. Debated / nuanced note: The origin of the names Magnesia and Magnetes is unknown and generally held to be pre-Greek; some scholars have pointed to Anatolian onomastics, but there is no consensus. This is stated rather than guessed.
