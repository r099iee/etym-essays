ENFRANCHISEMENT is a noun denoting the act of granting a right, freedom, or privilege, and above all the legal admission of a person or class to the full rights of citizenship, most prominently the right to vote. In the passage that triggers this card, an article on “the enfranchisement of women” — almost certainly Harriet Taylor Mill's essay The Enfranchisement of Women (1851), first published in the Westminster Review — is made “the occasion for another encomium,” that is, yet another formal speech of praise. The word here carries its solemn, constitutional, reformist resonance: it does not name a casual permission or a minor indulgence but the deliberate legal act by which a disenfranchised class is admitted into the body of citizens. Mill's usage is characteristic of nineteenth-century political philosophy, where enfranchisement was the keyword of the age of reform — the Reform Act of 1832, the Chartist campaigns, and the long suffragist struggle were all, in the vocabulary of the time, struggles for enfranchisement.

Primary sense — admission to political citizenship and the vote. In modern legal and political usage, enfranchisement is first of all the conferring of the franchise, the right to vote in public elections. To enfranchise is to admit a person or group to the suffrage; enfranchisement is that act of admission, whether achieved by a single statute (such as the Representation of the People Act 1918, which enfranchised most British women) or by a gradual constitutional process. This is the sense in which the word is used of former slaves after the American Civil War (the Fifteenth Amendment's enfranchisement of Black men in 1870), of women in Britain and America, and of any excluded class admitted to the ballot.

  • Historical civic sense — the freedom of a borough or city. Before the modern vote, enfranchisement named admission to the franchise of a borough or city: the grant of the freedom of a town, carrying rights to trade, to hold property, and to take part in municipal government. A burgess “enfranchised” by his town was a full citizen of that community.
  • Manumission sense. In its oldest English use, enfranchisement meant liberation from slavery or serfdom, the release of a bondman or villain. The medieval and early-modern acts of enfranchising a serf and of enfranchising a borough rested on the same root sense: the making of a man free.
  • Extended and figurative senses. By extension the word is used of any admission to a right or benefit previously withheld: the enfranchisement of a profession, of a colony, or of a subordinate class. In rhetorical and moral writing it may denote the freeing of a person or mind from constraint — a mental enfranchisement from prejudice.

Polysemy and register. The word is formal, legal, and constitutional; it belongs to statutes, political journalism, and reformist oratory rather than to ordinary conversation. Its characteristic collocations are “political enfranchisement,” “enfranchisement of women,” “civic enfranchisement,” and “universal enfranchisement.” Its antonyms are disenfranchisement and disfranchisement, the withdrawal of the vote or of civic rights; its verb is enfranchise, and the person or class admitted is “enfranchised.” It must be distinguished from franchise, the abstract right itself, and from suffrage, the vote or the practice of voting: enfranchisement is the process of bestowing what franchise and suffrage name.

Semantic history in brief. The word descends from the ethnic name of the Franks, who as conquerors of Gaul were the free class of the realm, so that the Frank's name became in Old French the adjective franc, “free, exempt, not servile.” The verb enfranchir, “to make free,” was built on it in feudal law and applied to manumission and to the freedom of boroughs; from that verb the noun enfranchisement was formed with the suffix -ment and entered English in the later medieval period. The political sense we know today — the granting of the vote — is a modern narrowing and elevation of that ancient image of the freed man: to enfranchise is still, etymologically, to make free in the manner of a Frank.
