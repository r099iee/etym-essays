The etymological journey of the verb "to tip" is a fascinating and complex one, characterized by multiple distinct origins that have converged into a single homographic form in modern English, leading to its remarkable polysemy. While the word "tipped" is simply the past tense and past participle of the verb "tip," understanding its evolution requires delving into these separate historical threads. One of the primary and earliest senses of "to tip" (which we can label *tip v.1*) is **"to strike lightly, to tap."** This verb first appeared in English in the 16th century, with early attestations around the 1530s. Its origin is widely believed to be from Low German *tippen* or Dutch *tippen*, both meaning "to tap" or "to touch lightly." These Germanic forms are likely onomatopoeic in nature, mimicking the sound or action of a light, quick touch. This root is also related to the English verb "tap" (v.1), which shares a similar meaning and probable onomatopoeic origin. The phonetic similarity and semantic overlap suggest a common, perhaps Proto-Germanic, imitative root, *tipp-*. Early uses of this sense can be found in texts describing a gentle touch or a quick, slight blow, such as "to tip a person on the shoulder." From this initial sense of a "light touch," several other meanings appear to have developed, though their exact pathways are often debated and marked by some obscurity. The sense most relevant to the provided context, **"to cause to lean, tilt, or overturn"** (*tip v.3*), emerged slightly later, around the late 16th century (c. 1590s). The Oxford English Dictionary (OED) notes its origin as "obscure," but suggests it might be a special application of *tip v.1* (to strike lightly) or related to the noun *top* (n.1) or the verb *tup* (v.1, meaning "to butt or push"). The semantic bridge from "light touch" to "tilting" can be understood if one considers that a light touch or push on an unstable object can indeed cause it to lean or fall. Imagine a light tap on a precariously balanced object causing it to *tip over*. This connection, while not definitively proven, offers a plausible evolutionary path. Alternatively, if related to *top*, it might imply turning something "top over bottom." The sense of "to overturn" or "to cause to fall" became quite prevalent, describing actions like "tipping a cart" or "the boat tipped over." This meaning is dynamic and implies a change in equilibrium. Another significant development is the sense of **"to give a gratuity or a small sum of money"** (*tip v.4*), which became common in the early 18th century (c. 1706). The origin of this sense is also somewhat obscure. It could be an extension of *tip v.1* (a small, quick transfer of money, a "light touch" of payment), or perhaps from *tip v.3* (a slight "overturning" or "handing over" of money). Another theory suggests it might derive from 17th-century thieves' cant, where "tip" meant "to give" or "to hand over" (attested c. 1610s), possibly related to the idea of a quick, discreet exchange. The practice of tipping itself has roots in earlier forms of patronage and recognition for service, but the specific verb "to tip" for this action solidified in the Georgian era, reflecting changing social customs and the growth of service industries. This usage quickly became widespread and remains a prominent meaning today. The sense of **"to provide secret or confidential information or a warning"** (*tip v.5*), often in the phrasal verb "to tip off," also emerged in the 18th century, gaining prominence in the 19th and 20th centuries. This, too, is of obscure origin but is often linked to the idea of a quick, discreet communication, similar to a "light touch" of information, or a subtle "tilting" of the scales of knowledge. It implies giving someone an advantage or forewarning, often in a clandestine manner. This sense is particularly common in contexts of crime, business, or sports, where insider information can be crucial. Distinct from these senses, there is another verb "to tip" (*tip v.2*) that means **"to furnish something with a tip or point"** or **"to remove the tip of something."** This verb directly derives from the noun "tip" (n.1), meaning "the extreme end or point of something." The noun "tip" itself has an older Germanic origin, related to Old English *topp* ("summit, tuft") and Proto-Germanic *toppaz*. This noun entered Middle English as *tip* or *tippe* and referred to the apex or extremity of an object. Thus, "to tip an arrow" (to put a point on it) or "to tip a plant" (to remove its growing tip) are direct verbalizations of this noun. This etymological thread is quite separate from the "light touch" or "tilting" senses, though the homography has led to a single word form. In summary, the evolution of "tipped" as the past participle of "tip" showcases a remarkable linguistic journey. What began as an onomatopoeic verb for a light touch (*tip v.1*) likely branched into the dynamic sense of causing something to tilt or fall (*tip v.3*), and later into the socio-economic act of giving a gratuity (*tip v.4*) and the communicative act of providing secret information (*tip v.5*). Simultaneously, a separate verb (*tip v.2*) arose directly from the noun "tip" (meaning "point"), leading to the modern homographic cluster. The context "tipped me in!" clearly draws upon the *tip v.3* sense, emphasizing a sudden, forceful displacement or loss of balance, a meaning that has been robustly present in English for centuries, describing actions that disrupt equilibrium and cause movement into or out of a particular state or location. The word's ability to encapsulate such varied actions, from a gentle tap to a complete overturn, from a monetary gift to a secret warning, is a testament to the dynamic and often unpredictable nature of semantic change in language.
