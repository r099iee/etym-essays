Somersault names the complete aerial revolution of the body — heels vaulting clean over head in a tucked wheel through the air, landing upright facing the way one came, or facing away. It is the atom of acrobatics: the first trick every tumbler learns, the boast of every playground, the unit from which gymnasts, divers, clowns, and fighter pilots build all their loftier revolutions.

In the source sentence Tolkien gives the trick to a dragon: the firework-beast, having passed like an express train, turned a somersault — threw its whole burning length over its own head in the sky above Bywater — before bursting with a deafening explosion. The image works because the somersault is the most joyful shape motion can take: disciplined, exuberant, and faintly reckless, a loop drawn in fire across the night, gone almost before the crowd can gasp.

Core meaning in detail: Physically the somersault divides into the forward roll extended into the air (tuck the chin, drive the knees over the shoulders, spot the ground, land soft) and its mirror, the backward somersault, with the side aerial and the twisting somersault as its showier cousins. Children learn it on mattresses and grassy banks; gymnasts perform it on floor, beam, and vault; divers carry it off ten-metre platforms; judokas drill it as the safe way to be thrown. The older English form somerset — still heard in dialect and in the surname Somerset — is the same word in earlier dress, and the verb phrase to turn a somersault preserves the trick’s original grammar: the feat is turned the way a wheel is turned.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • An aerial heel-over-head revolution of the body (primary sense, from the 1520s): the tumbler’s forward flip, the diver’s double, the dragon’s loop over Bywater. The bridge from the French original is direct: Old French soubresaut meant a leap clean over, and English acrobats kept exactly that feat.
  • A complete reversal of opinion, policy, or allegiance (figurative, 17th c.): the minister turned a political somersault, landing facing the enemy he marched against. The bridge is the body’s own geometry: just as the tumbler lands facing backwards, so the turncoat lands facing the opposite cause — the same word serves muscle and morals.
  • An aircraft’s loop or aerobatic revolution (technical, 20th c.): the stunt pilot turned a slow somersault above the field. The bridge carries the tumbler’s wheel into the sky: the aeroplane draws in smoke the circle the gymnast draws in sawdust.
  • A sudden violent overturn or collapse (extended, 19th c.): prices turned a somersault, the cart turned a somersault down the bank. The bridge generalises the flip to anything that goes heels-over-head — markets, vehicles, fortunes — keeping the shock and the completeness of the inversion.
  • The gambolling leap of animals at play (poetic): lambs turning somersaults in the spring meadow, porpoises somersaulting off the bow. The bridge is sheer exuberance: young creatures celebrate exactly as young acrobats do, and the same glad word covers both.
  • The historical form somerset (variant, 16th–19th c.): tumblers at fairs turned somersets for pennies. The bridge is folk etymology at work: English ears reshaped French sobre- into familiar somer-, as if the leap belonged to summer — and in a sense it does, for somersaults are the gymnastics of long light evenings.

Register and connotation: Somersault is a happy word in every sense — even the political one carries a wink, picturing the solemn minister upside down with coat-tails flying. It belongs to circuses, beaches, gymnasiums, and air shows, and lends its gladness wherever it goes: a share-price somersault hurts the wallet but the word still makes the reader smile faintly at the picture. Tolkien knew this precisely: his dragon does not merely explode, it turns a somersault first, giving the crowd one perfect loop of delight before the bang.
