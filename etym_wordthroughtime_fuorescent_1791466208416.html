- 1556 - fluor names fluorspar, the "flowing" mineral used as a flux; the word belongs to miners and alchemists, with no light sense at all
- 1852 - Stokes coins fluorescence after fluorite's glow under invisible rays; fluorescent is a laboratory adjective for a curious optical effect
- late 19th c. - the term spreads through physics, chemistry and mineralogy; it is technical, neutral and slightly exotic
- 1930s–1950s - fluorescent tubes transform offices, factories, schools and kitchens; the word becomes ordinary, domestic and institutional, and its light is praised as efficient and clean
- 1960s–1980s - the harsh colour rendering and constant hum give fluorescent a connotation of the clinical and the cheap; fluorescent colours become the signature of the artificial and the garish
- 1990s–present - compact fluorescents promise energy saving, then lose ground to LEDs; the word survives in "fluorescent lighting" and in figurative uses for lurid brightness, while the misspelling fuorescent circulates as a typo with no history of its own
