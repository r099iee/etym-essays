- Early 13th c. – Feudal combat & legal contention - Borrowed from Old French to denote both physical sword combat between knights and acrimonious disputes before feudal courts.
- 14th to 15th c. – Theological feuds & spiritual temptation - Employed by Chaucer and Wycliffe to describe heretical religious divisions, biblical battles, and the soul's inner struggle against sin.
- 16th to 17th c. – Dynastic mutiny & civil war - Became a primary rhetorical term in Shakespeare and Renaissance histories for domestic rebellions tearing kingdoms and families apart.
- 18th to 19th c. – Industrial unrest & imperial clashes - Shifted into political economy to characterize bitter labor strikes (industrial strife) and colonial border wars.
- 20th c. to Present – Sectarian violence & psychological anguish - Solidified in serious journalistic and sociological prose for protracted ethnic/religious bloodshed (sectarian strife) as well as internal existential torment.
