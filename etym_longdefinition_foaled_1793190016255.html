Foaled is the past tense and past participle of the verb to foal: to give birth to a foal, said of a mare bringing forth her young. In the source sentence — find out where it was foaled, and perchance thou mayest obtain another as swift — it asks after the birthplace of Koshchei's matchless horse, on the folk logic that swiftness runs in blood and soil: learn where this wonder was born and you may find another of the same stock.

The word belongs to the exact vocabulary of horse breeding, where every thoroughbred's identity begins with the formula foaled plus date, place, and parentage: foaled March the fourth, at such a stud, by such a sire out of such a dam. Where ordinary speech says born, the stable says foaled, just as the kennel says whelped, the sheepfold lambed, and the byre calved — each birth keeping the name of its species.

Core meaning in detail: Strictly, the mare foals (intransitive: she foaled in the night) or foals her young (transitive: she foaled a strong colt); the passive was foaled shifts attention to the young animal and its origin, which is why stud records and sale catalogues favour it. A foal as noun is a horse of either sex under one year old, still beside its dam; at one year it becomes a yearling, and the sexes sort into colt (young male) and filly (young female). The set phrase in foal describes the pregnant mare herself, completing a tidy lexical family around one root.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Given birth to by a mare (primary verbal sense, from c. 1600): the mare foaled twins, she foals easily. The bridge is denominal conversion: the noun foal, young horse, becomes a verb naming the act that produces it, exactly as lamb and calf became verbs.
  • Born, in the passive was foaled (stud-book sense): where was it foaled, foaled in Kentucky. The bridge shifts viewpoint from mother to offspring and from event to origin, turning a birth into a pedigree fact of date and place.
  • The noun foal, young horse under a year (root sense, from Old English): a foal at foot, to wean a foal. The bridge is the source of the whole family: the young animal itself, leggy, large-eyed, and unsteady, known to every rider.
  • Colt and filly as sexed young (associated senses): a foal may be a colt foal or a filly foal. The bridge sorts the generic young by sex, the vocabulary every breeder reaches for at the first handling.
  • In foal, pregnant of a mare (phrasal adjective): the mare is in foal to a famous stallion. The bridge carries the young forward in time: she who will foal is already described by the coming foal.
  • Extended to other equids and rarely beyond (zoological margin): a zebra or donkey mare may be said to foal. The bridge is family likeness: all horse-kind foal, while cows calve and sheep lamb, each birth verb guarding its species.

Register and connotation: foaled is the language of stable, stud, and racecard — precise, proud, and commercial, since where and when a horse was foaled fixes its age, eligibility, and value. To horsemen it sounds as natural as born; to outsiders it signals expertise, the speaker's membership in horse society. The fairy-tale use in the source sentence borrows that precision for magic: even a supernatural steed has a foaling place, and knowing it means power over its kind.
