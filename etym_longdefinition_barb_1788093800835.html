Barb (noun; also verb) denotes, first and most concretely, a sharp backward-pointing spike or projection — one of the small hooked points set near the tip of an arrow, dart, harpoon, spear or fish-hook so that once the weapon or hook has struck it cannot easily be pulled free. In the source sentence, from the death of the dragon Smaug in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the three nouns "barb, shaft and feather" enumerate the three parts of a complete arrow — the barbed point, the wooden shaft, and the fletched feather — so that the whole weapon stands, by synecdoche, for the single Black Arrow that pierced the dragon's breast.

1. Primary senses.

  • Weaponry and tackle: the hooked spike of an arrow, dart, harpoon, gig or fish-hook; "a barbed arrow", "a barbed spear", "a barbed harpoon". Its function is purely mechanical: the backward angle lodges the point in flesh so that tugging the shaft only seats it deeper. Anglers deliberately buy barbless hooks when they want easy release — the barb is optional proof of the hook's malice.
  • Feather anatomy (ornithology): each of the lateral branches of a feather's vane that spring in parallel rows from the central shaft (the rachis); the barbs together form the vane, and each barb bears smaller branches (barbules) with microscopic hooks (barbicels) that zip the vane into a firm, air-tight surface for flight. This technical sense, standard in natural history since the 18th century, rests on the same ancient metaphor — the beard, a fringe of bristling projections — and it links the word to the craft of fletching, since an arrow's "feather" is itself built of barbs.
  • Botany and zoology: stiff, sharp, frequently recurved outgrowths — the barbs of a burr, of a thistle, of a barley awn; the backward-pointing spines of a porcupine quill; the recurved teeth of some fish spines. Anything that bristles like a beard and refuses to let go can be called barbed.

2. Extended and figurative senses.

  • A verbal barb: a sharp, pointed, wounding or sarcastic remark — "her answer was a barb", "to trade barbs", "barbed comments". The semantic bridge is the projectile metaphor: words are shot like arrows, and a truly cutting remark is barbed, so that it sticks in the victim's memory and stings on withdrawal. Recorded from the 19th century, this is now one of the commonest uses of the word, especially in journalism about politics, sport and celebrity feuds.
  • Barbed wire: fencing wire armed at intervals with sharp points, patented in the American Midwest in the 1860s (the term "barbed wire" is recorded from 1862). It transformed the open range, and in the First World War it became the emblematic obstacle of trench warfare — coils of concertina wire entangling men under machine-gun fire — giving "barbed" a dark modern resonance of entrapment and wounding by design.
  • The verb "to barb": to furnish with a barb or barbs ("to barb an arrow"), and by extension "to barb one's words" — to make them cutting. The participle "barbed" (a barbed remark, a barbed hook, barbed wire, barbed wit) is ubiquitous, and "barbed" almost always carries menace.
  • Jargon and slang: in ornithology "barb" is the standard term for a vane branch; in angling the barb is a measurable, regulated feature of hooks; in 20th-century slang "barbs" is a clipping of "barbiturates", the sedative pills of the mid-century — a purely coincidental homonym formed by abbreviation, not by meaning.

3. A caution about homonyms. English has two distinct words spelled barb. The word defined here descends from Latin barba "beard" through Old French barbe, and its whole semantic history is the beard metaphor. A separate capitalised Barb names a horse of the Barbary breed — formed from Barbary, the old European name for the Berber lands of North Africa, ultimately from Greek bárbaros "foreign". In the Tolkien passage there is no ambiguity: the barb is the point of the Black Arrow that slew Smaug, and "barb, shaft and feather" is a perfect miniature of the word's archery home.

4. Grammar and morphology. Countable noun, plural barbs; verb barb, barbed, barbing; adjective barbed, with the derived barbless (an everyday angling word: "barbless hooks"); compound adjectives such as barb-tipped occur in technical prose. The word is fully integrated into English morphology.

5. Summary. A barb is, literally, a backward-pointing spike on an arrow, hook or spear that makes it hold fast — the old "beard" of the point; technically, one of the bearded branches of a feather vane or the recurved spine of a plant; and figuratively, a stinging, pointed remark that lodges in the memory as firmly as a hook in a fish: a word whose whole career is the migration of the beard from the chin to the arrowhead to the tongue.
