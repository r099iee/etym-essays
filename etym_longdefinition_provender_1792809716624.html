Provender is food laid in beforehand — provisions, stores, and fodder gathered and kept ready against need. In its oldest English sense it meant the measured allowance dealt out to soldiers, monks, and horses: rations, hay, and oats issued by authority. In its modern sense it has widened to any hearty fare or sustaining supply, from the farmer’s winter feed to the writer’s intellectual provender, the books and ideas on which a mind fattens.

In the source sentence the word does quiet comic work: Bringing up young hobbits took a lot of provender. Hobbit children — perpetually hungry, growing sideways as much as upwards, entitled by custom to six meals a day — are presented as livestock to be fed: the parental labour of the Shire measured out in sacks and servings like the feeding of calves. Tolkien’s choice of the old quartermaster’s word over plain food makes the joke formal — a muster-roll solemnity applied to second breakfast — and any parent who has watched a teenager empty a refrigerator will recognise the campaign.

Core meaning in detail: Provender always implies foresight: food not merely eaten but provided — fetched, stored, portioned, and issued. The quartermaster’s provender is counted in rations; the farmer’s provender is stacked in barns against winter; the innkeeper’s provender is laid in against the fair. This note of preparation distinguishes it from food (anything eaten) and fodder (feed for animals specifically): provender is supply considered as a duty fulfilled in advance, the full manger as evidence of a provident master.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A measured allowance of food, especially for animals or troops (oldest English sense, 14th c.): the knight’s horses ate their nightly provender, the garrison’s provender ran short. The bridge from the Latin original is direct: Medieval Latin praebenda was the fixed due furnished to each mouth, and English kept that sense of authorised ration.
  • Dry feed for livestock — hay, oats, corn, and straw (agricultural, 15th–19th c.): to buy provender for the winter, the stable-yard heaped with provender. The bridge narrows the ration to its most visible form: the horse’s nose-bag and the manger, so that provender became the farmer’s standing word for bulk winter feed.
  • Human food and provisions generally, often hearty and plain (literary): country provender, a table groaning with good provender. The bridge lifts the feed-bag to the table: soldiers and travellers ate from the same stores as their beasts, and the word followed the ration from stable to kitchen without changing its coat.
  • Figurative sustenance — food for the mind or spirit (19th c.): intellectual provender, spiritual provender, a sermon full of rich provender. The bridge is the oldest metaphor in the language: as the body fattens on oats, so the mind fattens on books — Milton’s readers and Victorian essayists alike stocked their libraries as farmers stock their barns.
  • Supplies and necessaries in general, beyond food (extended, 17th c.): military provender including powder and match, travelling provender of cloaks and coin. The bridge generalises provision itself: whatever an expedition must lay in beforehand can be called its provender, the quartermaster’s remit being total.
  • Ecclesiastical prebend — the doublet (technical, surviving): a cathedral prebend, the prebendary’s stipend. The bridge is shared ancestry, not borrowing: prebend and provender are twin children of Latin praebenda, one raised in the cathedral chapter and one in the stable-yard — the same allowance paid in prayer or in oats.

Register and connotation: Provender is archaic, hearty, and faintly jocular — the word smells of stables, campaigns, and country inns, never of restaurants or diet plans. It flatters abundance: to speak of good provender is to praise plenty without ceremony, and Tolkien’s hobbit-parents wield it with exactly that affectionate exasperation. The ecclesiastical doublet prebend alone stayed dignified, drawing its stipend in Latin while its twin brother hauled hay.
