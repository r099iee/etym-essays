guttered — The verb gutter is a late and homely development of one of the oldest words in the passage’s world: the word for a drain. Middle English borrowed goter, guter from Old French gotiere, itself from Latin gutta, “a drop” — the gutter being, in the oldest understanding, the little channel that collects and carries drops of rainwater. The romance word for “drop” may itself reach back to an Indo-European root meaning “to pour,” though the ultimate connection is debated and best labelled uncertain. From this humble plumbing word grew, in the 17th and 18th centuries, a candle-verb: a candle “gutters” when its flame sputters and the melted wax runs down its sides in little channels — each rivulet a miniature gutter — so that the flame is slowly strangled and drowned in its own melt.
  • Earliest English (13th–14th c.): goter is purely structural — the channel, drain, or conduit that carries water off a roof or through a street; no flame, no emotion, no ruin.
  • The 16th-century extension: the noun generates a verb meaning “to form or flow in channels” — water guttering along a street — and the concrete image of liquid running in channels is now available to the language.
  • The candle-shift (17th–18th c.): the verb attaches to candles — “the candle gutters” — and the whole domestic scene of the sputtering, wax-streaked, dying flame enters the word. Samuel Johnson’s dictionaries and the literary usage of the 18th–19th centuries fix the sense: guttering is what candles do when they burn badly, fitfully, and messily toward their end.
  • Figurative burnout (19th–20th c.): from the candle to the life. Passions, hopes, love, and days “gutter” — they sputter, sink, and go out unevenly, choked by their own residue. The metaphor is precise: it is not a clean ending but a messy, self-consuming, gradual death.
  • Modern split life: the noun has meanwhile drifted toward the degraded — the gutter press, the gutter, the depths of society — so that the word family now spans water channels, candle death, literary extinction, and social degradation at once.
In the corrected passage, “guttered” does the work of an entire elegy. The carvers’ “days of love” are given the exact physics of a dying candle — sputter, melt, channel, drown — and the wooden sculpture they afterwards carve becomes the hardened residue of that burnt-out flame.
  • Attestations of the candle-sense: the “guttering candle” is a fixed image in English poetry and prose from the 18th century onward (the phrase appears in the essayists and novelists of the period), and the verb is recorded in the dictionaries of Johnson and his successors with exactly this sense of sputtering, running, sinking combustion.
  • The channel-metaphor reversed: the noun gutter is a channel that carries water away; in the verb the candle’s own melted wax forms channels on its sides — so the word internally links the drain of the roof with the drain of the flame, waste and water and wax all running together.
  • Modern restricted use: today the verb survives almost exclusively in this flame-and-life imagery; the everyday language of light prefers flicker, fade, or go out, which is why the word, when it appears, carries such deliberate literary weight.
  • Sound-shape: the verb is a straightforward denominal of the noun with the English -er formation preserved through Middle English spelling variants (goter, guter), and its blunt, gutteral-sounding cluster suits the grimy, domestic scene it describes.
The word thus does in one syllable what a paragraph could only approximate: it marks the exact moment when love stopped burning steadily and began to sputter and drain — and it leaves the reader watching the wax. muddle — The word muddle keeps its origin on its sleeve: at bottom it means “to make muddy,” and it arrived in English in the late 16th century from the Dutch and Low German world of ditches and polders, where moddelen meant to dabble in modde, mud. The root itself is stubbornly opaque — no secure Indo-European ancestor has been established for the Dutch mud-word, and the honest account of the etymology begins with the attested forms and stops there. What the English language did with the borrowed verb is wonderfully rich: within a century of its first appearance, “to muddle” had travelled from stirring water into mud, to stirring affairs into disorder, and then to stirring the mind itself into bewilderment.
  • Late 16th century: the verb means literally to make muddy or turbid — “muddying the water,” stirring up the sediment so that what lies beneath cannot be seen; the physical scene is complete.
  • Late 17th century (1690s): the sense broadens to mismanagement — to muddle a business, to muddle one’s affairs — the image of stirred, clouded water applied to plans and undertakings.
  • 18th century: the mind is drawn in — to be muddled with drink, muddled thoughts, a muddled head — so that the word becomes the standard term for bewilderment and befuddlement, clouded cognition.
  • 19th century: the noun is formed — “a muddle” (attested c. 1818) as a state of confusion and disorder — and the idiom “muddle through” (to get by clumsily, by trial and error, never quite in control) enters the language, one of the most characteristically British of expressions.
  • 20th–21st century: the word is everywhere in the everyday vocabulary of confusion — a muddle of papers, muddled thinking, muddle-headedness — always with the faint ghost of stirred mud and clouded water beneath the mental disorder.
In the corrected passage, “the muddle of huts” is precisely right: the dwellings are not merely jumbled but muddy — unplanned, accidental, stirred together at the foot of the wall like sediment — and out of that murky confusion a score of skilled craftsmen still emerge, so that the muddle and the mastery stand in sharp, quiet contrast.
  • Related formations: muddle is the verb and noun of the same family as mud, muddy, muddle-headed, and the dialect muddlesome; the whole group keeps the image of stirred sediment and clouded water at its centre.
  • Semantic scale: muddle names disorder with a hint of muddiness and confusion — lighter than chaos, less violent than shambles, more confused than disarray — and in the passage it makes the huts look accidental, sedimented, and unplanned, which is precisely their social meaning.
  • Idiomatic depth: “to muddle through,” “to make a muddle of it,” “muddled thinking” — the family of idioms all keep the image of clouded water beneath the mental disorder, so that the word always hints that clarity is possible once the mud settles.
The word is therefore the perfect counterweight to guttered: where the days of love guttered away messily into wax and memory, the huts among which the carvers live are a muddle — and out of that murky sediment the craftsmen still rise, carving their strange blazoned figures in the annual light of the June morning.
