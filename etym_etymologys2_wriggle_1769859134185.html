PIE *wreik- / *wreiǵ- "to turn, twist, bend" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; the exact velar is debated — see note) → Proto-Germanic *wrigjaną "to turn, twist, move to and fro" (reconstructed; the base is the same as that of wring "to twist forcibly" and, through the "twisted" sense, wrong) → Old English wrigian "to turn, move, go, twist, deviate" (attested from the 8th–10th c. in early glosses) → Middle English wriggen "to twist, move quickly, wriggle" (attested 14th–15th c.) → Middle English wrigglen — a frequentative verb formed on wrig- with the iterative suffix -len ("to twist to and fro with quick sinuous movements"; attested c. 1490s, late 15th c., in the OED) → Modern English wriggle (verb and noun) "to twist and turn with quick writhing movements; to extricate oneself evasively" → Modern English WRIGGLE "twist and turn with quick writhing movements" ◄ TARGET rootWord Debate / nuance note: The reconstructed PIE root is given in several forms — *wreik-, *wreiǵ-, *wreyk- — and the exact velar consonant is not fully settled; the safest statement is that the root means "to turn, twist, bend." The Germanic path (wrigian → wriggen → wrigglen) is fully attested and certain, with the frequentative -l- suffix (as in wriggle, giggle, trample) appearing only in late Middle English. The English word wring (to twist forcibly) and wrong (originally "twisted, crooked") share the same prehistoric base.
