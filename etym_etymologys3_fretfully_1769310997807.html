The history of fretfully is the story of a word being eaten from the outside in. Its ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European verb meaning simply "to eat"; through Germanic it becomes the animal verb "to devour"; in English the same verb is turned to describe rust eating iron and waves eating the shore; and finally the eating moves indoors, into the mind, where worry "eats at" a person — producing the fretful, the fretfully, and the whole modern vocabulary of anxious irritation. Every time Austen's Mrs Bennet speaks fretfully, she is performing, in miniature, a metaphor three thousand years old.

The root: to eat (PIE and Germanic)
  • The chain begins with Proto-Indo-European *ed-, the family's basic verb "to eat." It is one of the most securely attested roots in the language family: Latin edere ("to eat," source of English edible and edacious), Greek édein, Sanskrit admi ("I eat"), and Old Church Slavonic jasti all descend from it. The root is ordinary, domestic, and ancient.
  • Proto-Germanic inherited *etaną, "to eat," and — crucially for our word — created an intensive compound, *fra-etaną, "to eat up completely, to devour," by prefixing *fra- ("completely, forth, away") onto the simple verb. Where simple etaną was used of people eating their meals, fra-etaną was the verb of beasts feeding — tearing, gulping, consuming entirely.
  • This two-verb distinction survives on the Continent to this day: German essen is human eating, while fressen is animal devouring — the same pair as English eat and fret, and the same logic that made fret a word of violence and voracity.
The Anglo-Saxon verb: devouring (Old English)
  • Old English inherited the compound as fretan, "to devour, eat up." The written record uses it with full animal energy: monsters in Beowulf devour their victims, beasts tear prey, and — already in the earliest prose — fire and rust "fret" what they consume. The verb is physical, destructive, and insatiable.
  • Notably, Old English fretan also carried the sense of mechanical wear: metal, water, and time fret and eat away surfaces. Corrosion is the natural meeting-point of eating and wearing, and it is this sense that will carry the word forward into the mechanical age.
  • The simple verb etan survived as English "eat," while fretan, losing its initial prefix-vowel (fra- → fre-), survived as Middle English freten, "to devour, to gnaw, to corrode." The pair eat/fret is thus a fossil of the ancient Germanic human-eating vs. beast-devouring split.
The turn inward: from corrosion to worry (Middle English and Renaissance)
  • Through the Middle English period fret broadens from devouring to chafing: ropes fret against wood, waves fret the shore, acid frets metal. The image is always the slow, persistent eating-away of a surface by repeated friction or corrosive action.
  • In the sixteenth century the verb makes the decisive metaphorical leap. Writers begin to use fret of the mind: anxiety, envy, and grief "fret" the heart and soul just as rust frets iron — they gnaw at a person from within. Shakespeare's usage is central; his characters fret, and his word "fretful" (c. 1590) names the person so eaten: one disposed to anxious, peevish vexation.
  • The shift is perfectly natural because the physiology of worry resembles the physics of corrosion: constant small irritations, repeated gnawing thoughts, slow wearing down of patience. The metaphor — being consumed from within — is as vivid today as it was in 1600.
The adjective and the adverb (17th century onward)
  • From the worried verb came the adjective fretful, "full of fret, disposed to vexation," formed with the common suffix -ful ("full of"). A fretful person is not merely worried; they are characteristically, habitually chafed by petty irritations — anxious, restless, easily provoked, given to complaint.
  • From the adjective came the adverb fretfully in the seventeenth century, "in a fretful manner." It quickly became a favourite of narrative prose, where it lets an author report both a character's outward manner and inner state in a single word: restless motion, vexed tone, anxious complaint.
  • Jane Austen masters the word as an instrument of characterisation. When Mrs Bennet insists "I do not believe a word of it," said fretfully, the adverb performs the whole comedy of her nervous volatility — the maternal anxiety, the impatience at contradiction, the querulous chafing against uncertainty — in one stroke of narration.
The family today
  • Modern English keeps the full family: to fret ("to worry" — the dominant verb), fret (noun, "a state of worry": "in a fret"), fretful and fretfully (the anxious-peevish adjective and adverb), and the archaic-literal fret ("to corrode, to gnaw," surviving in literary and technical descriptions of rust and waves).
  • The modern worry-sense has entirely eclipsed the devouring-sense in ordinary speech, but the image is still alive beneath the surface. When someone frets, the old Germanic picture flickers: they are being eaten away from within by their own thoughts, like metal in acid.
  • Two homonyms keep company with the word but are unrelated: the guitar fret (a ridge on the fingerboard), from Old French frete, "a band, ferrule," and the ornamental fret (interlaced carving), from Old French frete, "interlaced work." They share spelling, not ancestry — a reminder that the fret in fretfully is purely the gnawing one.
  • Thus fretfully, a small adverb in a Jane Austen novel, contains within it the whole epic of a word: an Indo-European meal, a Germanic beast at its prey, Anglo-Saxon monsters devouring the hall, rust eating iron, and finally — the last, quietest stage — a worried mother being consumed by her own anxious thoughts.
