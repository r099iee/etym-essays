- Late 16th to Early 17th Century (Classical Rhetorical Import) - Borrowed directly from Hellenistic Greek and Late Latin treatises as a specialized rhetorical figure (*euphemismus*); understood strictly by Renaissance grammarians and rhetoricians (such as George Puttenham and Thomas Blount) as an oratorical device for substituting auspicious or courteous words in place of foul or ominous expressions.
- 18th Century Enlightenment & Augustan Politeness - Broadened from formal academic textbooks into elite literary and salon culture; celebrated by writers and conversationalists as a vital instrument of classical decorum, gentlemanly restraint, and neoclassical urbanity to prevent vulgarity from intruding upon civilized discourse.
- 19th Century Victorian Prudery & Institutional Modesty - Reached an apex of pervasive social dominance throughout the Victorian era, where polite society mandated elaborate verbal veils for bodily anatomy, sexual reproduction, childbirth, physical illness, and death; euphemism was viewed as an essential moral duty, though later satirists mocked it as prudish bourgeois hypocrisy.
- Mid-20th Century Post-War Skepticism & Orwellian Critique - Transformed permanently under the influence of mid-century political critique, most famously crystallized by George Orwell's 1946 essay *Politics and the English Language*; public perception shifted radically from viewing euphemism as polite decorum to viewing it with suspicion as a dangerous tool of totalitarian propaganda, bureaucratic whitewashing, and military deception (e.g., "pacification" and "liquidation").
- Late 20th Century Sociolinguistics & the "Euphemism Treadmill" - Systematically analyzed by sociolinguists and cognitive scientists (such as Steven Pinker); recognized as a dynamic linguistic process where sanitized terms inevitably degrade and absorb the stigma of underlying realities, sparking heated debates over political correctness, inclusive terminology, and institutional naming conventions.
- Contemporary 21st-Century Usage - Pervasive across corporate communication (workforce optimization), public relations, politics, and digital culture; modern social media platforms have fostered algorithmic euphemisms ("algospeak", such as "unalive" or "panini") designed to bypass automated content filters, cementing euphemism's dual identity as both an unavoidable social necessity and a subject of modern digital satire.
