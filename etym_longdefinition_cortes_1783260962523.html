Cortes (proper noun) — Macaulay's anglicized spelling of the Spanish surname Cortés, borne by Hernán Cortés (1485–1547), the Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that caused the fall of the Aztec Empire, and, by extension, the emblem of conquest won by audacity, strategy, and the sword. In Macaulay's History of England the name stands in the same comparative sentence as Pizarro: "how, in America, the British colonies rapidly became far mightier and wealthier than the realms which Cortes and Pizarro had added to the dominions of Charles the Fifth." The two surnames together carry the argument that the Spanish method of empire — seizure by conquest, extraction of gold, destruction of native civilization — built nothing durable, while the English colonies grew mighty by settlement, law, and commerce. CORE SENSES AND USES
  • The historical person. Hernán Cortés, born 1485 in Medellín, Extremadura, a law student turned soldier who sailed to Hispaniola and Cuba, and in 1519 led the expedition to Mexico that overthrew the Aztec Triple Alliance. Landing with a few hundred men, he exploited the resentments of peoples subject to Aztec rule, formed decisive alliances (above all with the Tlaxcalans), besieged and destroyed Tenochtitlan in 1521, and became master of a vast realm — before being recalled, investigated, and largely shelved by the crown he had enriched.
  • The surname as metonym. In 18th- and 19th-century historiography "Cortes" stands for the conquest of Mexico itself and for conquest as a strategic, self-justifying enterprise — a conquistador who wrote his own vindication (the Cartas de Relación, his five letters to Charles V) and bound his conquest into the structure of the Spanish empire.
  • The archetype. In later usage the name can denote the type of the brilliant, ruthless empire-builder: audacity, diplomacy, and cruelty combined — the counterweight to Pizarro's plain rapacity.
  • Homonym caution. In Spanish, cortes (lower case) is also the plural of corte, "court," naming the parliamentary assemblies or estates of Spain and Portugal; the surname Cortés/Cortes is historically linked to this court-word but is a separate proper noun in English.
DISTINCTIONS AND BOUNDARIES
  • Cortes vs Pizarro. Both are Macaulay's paired emblems of Spanish conquest; Cortes is the type of conquest with strategy — alliances, propaganda, letters justifying the war — while Pizarro is the type of naked seizure and greed.
  • Cortes vs conquistador. conquistador is the generic Spanish class noun for the soldiers who conquered the New World; Cortes names the single most famous of them, the conqueror of Mexico.
  • Cortes vs colonizer / settler. colonizer implies planting population and lasting institutions; Cortes conquered and then governed an already populous civilization, imposing Spanish rule rather than settling empty land.
  • Cortes vs adventurer. In his own time Cortes was an adventurer, a licensed fortune-hunter holding a royal commission; modern adventurer has lost the military-predatory sense.
  • Cortes vs explorer. The enterprise was conquest, not discovery in the scientific sense; though Cortes's letters report geography, the aim was dominion and wealth, not knowledge.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE AND HISTORICAL WEIGHT The name Cortes, like Pizarro, is metonymic: a proper noun whose lexicographic interest lies in what it is made to stand for. The fall of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521) was the most dramatic act of the European conquest of America, and its leader's name became the type-case of the audacious conqueror who topples a civilization. British imperial historians of the 18th and 19th centuries used the pairing "Cortes and Pizarro" to define, by contrast, what British empire was not: empire built by trade, law, and settlement rather than by the sword. Macaulay's sentence compresses that two-century comparison into two surnames, and Cortes in English therefore carries the same dark diagnostic charge as Pizarro — the conqueror who added realms to a distant crown and built nothing durable in their place.
