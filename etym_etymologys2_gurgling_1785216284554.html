Imitative / echoic base *gurg- (onomatopoeic, not a normal PIE root: a reduplicative sound-symbolic base of the type g-r-g, meaning “to emit a bubbling, throaty, swallowing sound”; reconstructed from the parallel forms in Latin and other ancient languages rather than inherited) │ └── Latin gurgulio “gullet, throat, windpipe” (1st c. BCE, Varro and later medical writers); sibling forms gurges “whirlpool, flood” and gurgustium “miserable hovel” └── Late Latin gurgulare “to gargle, gurgle” (attested in Late Latin and medieval glossaries, 4th–6th c.), a denominative verb from gurgulio └── Middle English gurgelen “to gurgle, make a bubbling throat-sound” (early 15th c.; OED: probably of native imitative formation, reinforced by Middle Dutch gorgelen and Middle High German gurgeln, both “to gurgle”) └── English gurgle “to make a bubbling, liquid, or throaty sound” (late 16th c., Shakespeare’s generation; intransitive first, then transitive) └── English gurgling = gurgle + participial suffix -ing (present participle and participial adjective, 16th c. onward) └── Modern English: gurgling Debated stage: whether Middle English gurgelen descends directly from a Romance/Latin gurgulare line or is an independent native echoic coinage reinforced by Germanic neighbours (Middle Dutch gorgelen, German gurgeln) is not fully settled; OED treats the English verb as imitative in origin and notes the continental parallels without asserting a single provable line of descent. The chain above records the attested forms with that caveat. The base is not a regular PIE root but a sound-symbolic formation, so no PIE reconstruction can be given with confidence.
