- [Classical Antiquity c. 8th c. BCE–4th c. CE] — Originated in Homeric epic (Aiolos, keeper of winds in the Odyssey) and ethnography (Aeolis and the Aeolic Greek dialect of Sappho and Alcaeus) — [perception: revered mythical warden of tempestuous storms, combined with a proud ancestral tribal name of northern Hellenic civilization]
- [16th c. Renaissance & Humanism] — Adopted into Latin musicological theory by Henricus Glareanus in Dodecachordon (1547) to codify the natural minor scale ("Aeolian mode") as a cornerstone of polyphonic harmony — [perception: celebrated humanist rediscovery of classical Greek musical proportion and modal gravity]
- [17th c. Early Modern Scientific Era] — Athanasius Kircher published the acoustic design of the wind-activated harp in Phonurgia Nova (1673), designating it the Aeolian harp in honour of the wind god — [perception: delightful acoustic marvel demonstrating natural harmony through physical vortex shedding without human touch]
- [Late 18th–Early 19th c. Romantic Era] — Elevated by Coleridge, Shelley, and Wordsworth into the supreme metaphorical icon of poetic inspiration and spontaneous creative imagination ("Aeolian visitations") — [perception: sacred symbol of the soul's sensitivity to the living breath of Nature and divine artistic influx]
- [Late 19th–21st c. Modern Scientific & Global Era] — Institutionalized in geology and planetary science (NASA Mars exploration) to classify wind erosion, sand dunes, and loess deposition ("aeolian landforms"), while remaining standard terminology in linguistics and jazz modal harmony — [perception: precise physical earth science terminology alongside classical music and historical philology]
