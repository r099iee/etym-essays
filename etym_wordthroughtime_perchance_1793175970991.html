- 14th c. — born as a phrase: Middle English per chance, two words meaning by chance or by fortune, borrowed from French usage.
- Late 14th–15th c. — the hedge hardens: fused into one adverb, it slides from by accident toward perhaps, hedging claims as well as describing luck.
- 16th–17th c. — the poet's hedge: Shakespeare, Spenser, and the prayer book lift it high; Hamlet's perchance to dream fixes it in English memory forever.
- 18th–19th c. — gently displaced: perhaps and possibly take over ordinary prose while perchance retreats to poetry, sermons, and deliberate archaism.
- 20th c. — the translator's friend: renderings of folktales, epics, and liturgy keep it alive as period colour, exactly as in the Russian wonder tale here.
- Today — a costume word: never neutral, always a signal that the speaker has stepped into story-time, speculation, or quotation.
