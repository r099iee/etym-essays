SUBTLETY is built on a compound root (Latin sub "under" + tela "web"), so the two root elements receive separate linear chains, followed by the combined line to the modern word. ROOT WORD ONE — the "under" element (Latin sub) LINEAR CHAIN 1 (under): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *upo “under, beneath, from under” (c. 4000 BCE), a preposition/adverb of position; the source of Greek hupó (as in hypodermic, hypothesis), Sanskrit úpa, and Latin sub. Stage 2 — Latin sub “under, beneath” (preposition), the source of the prefix sub- used in the compound subtīlis (“woven close beneath”). ROOT WORD TWO — the "weave" element (Latin tela) LINEAR CHAIN 2 (weave): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *teḱs- “to weave, fabricate, build” (c. 4000 BCE), the root of craftsmanship — Latin texere, Greek téktōn “builder,” Sanskrit tákṣati “to fashion, carve.” Stage 2 — Latin texere “to weave, plait, construct” (Classical Latin), the verb of the loom, which also governed the building of anything fitted together — walls, plots, speeches, webs of words. Stage 3 — Latin tela “a woven web, cloth” (from the root with a tool-suffixed noun), the fabric on the loom; the source of English subtlety's second element. Stage 4 — Latin subtīlis “finely woven, fine-textured, thin; precise, penetrating” (sub “under/beneath” + tela “web” — literally “woven close beneath”; the adjective of fine cloth and, by extension, of fine judgement). Stage 5 — Latin subtīlitās “fineness, precision, subtlety” (the abstract noun on subtīlis). COMBINED COMPOUND (the word itself) Stage 6 — Old French subtilite / soutil “fineness, cunning, subtlety” (from Latin subtīlitās; recorded in medieval French). Stage 7 — Middle English sotilte / subtilte “subtlety, craftiness, delicacy” (first recorded 14th c., via Old French). Stage 8 — English SUBTLETY “fineness of perception or construction; a fine distinction; (hist.) an elaborate medieval sugar sculpture” — the modern form retains the silent -b- of the learned Renaissance respelling subtle (16th–17th c.), overwriting the earlier sotil. NOTES - The analysis of subtīlis as sub + tela is the traditional and standard one (already given by the Roman grammarians); it is not formally perfect — the -tilis suffix is a little unusual — but no better etymology has been offered, and modern dictionaries (OED, de Vaan) accept the derivation as the best explanation while noting the formal irregularity. - The parallel Indo-Iranian form *tetḱ- “to hew, fashion” (a reduplicated variant of *teḱs-) produced Sanskrit tákṣati “to carve” and tákṣan “carpenter.” - The culinary “subtlety” (a medieval sugar sculpture) is the same word, specialised from “something delicate/finely made.”
