Note on morphological compounding and semantic shift: The base verb emere originally meant "to take" (as still seen in Latin sūmere < *sub-emere "to take up" and eximere "to take out"), before narrowing in commercial Roman life to "to buy" (literally "to take for a price"). When prefixed with ex- ("out"), eximere signified taking a single item out of a larger lot to test its quality. The resulting noun exemplum was literally "that which is taken out as a representative sample." In medieval chancellery practice, compounding with -ficāre (the causative combining form of facere, "to make") generated exemplificāre, literally "to make an exemplum"—which meant generating an official, verified duplicate that carried the full evidentiary force of the original.
