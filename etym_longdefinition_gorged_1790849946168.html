The past participle and adjective gorged describes the condition of being stuffed, distended, or excessively filled to the point of over-satiation, discomfort, or gross surfeit as a result of voracious, unrestrained consumption. Rooted in the anatomical term for the throat (gorge), the word conveys the visceral physical sensation of having consumed so copiously that nourishment backs up into the gullet. Beyond its primary alimentary meaning, gorged is widely used across pathological, geographic, heraldic, and literary domains to describe any vessel, river, organ, or consciousness swollen to bursting with matter, blood, water, language, or sensory stimulation.

The diverse polysemic branches of gorged include:

  • Visceral Alimentary Sense: Describes animals or humans that have indulged in ravenous, gluttonous overeating, leaving them heavy, torpid, and immobile (e.g., "a gorged python digesting its massive prey in the sun," or "the revelers sat gorged on roast fowl and rich pastries").
  • Medical and Pathological Sense: In physiology and pathology, it describes blood vessels, capillaries, or organs that are abnormally congested, swollen, or distended with blood or other bodily fluids (e.g., "the tick's abdomen was heavily gorged with host blood after feeding").
  • Hydrological and Topographical Sense: Describes rivers, reservoirs, or drainage systems swollen to extreme capacity by torrential rains, snowmelt, or flash floods (e.g., "the mountain torrents were gorged with spring runoff, threatening downstream villages").
  • Literary, Semiotic, and Intellectual Sense: In literary theory and aesthetic criticism (as illustrated in Roland Barthes's The Pleasure of the Text), gorged is utilized metaphorically to portray the ecstatic yet overwhelming sensory saturation of the reader who is "gorged with language." Here, the condition evokes a childlike, uncurbed indulgence in signifiers, where absolute plenitude teeters delightfully on the edge of excess and bliss.
  • Heraldic and Armorial Jargon: In heraldry, gorged has an entirely distinct, specialized technical meaning: it describes a heraldic beast (such as a lion, swan, or stag) that wears a collar, coronet, or chain encircled around its neck (e.g., "a swan argent, gorged with a ducal coronet"). This usage directly preserves the French anatomical noun gorge ("throat/neck").
  • Figurative and Psychological Sense: Portrays individuals or societies intoxicated and swollen with unearned wealth, power, ego, or sensationalist media (e.g., "gorged on propaganda, pride, and material hubris").

The psychological power of gorged stems from its visceral embodiment of boundary transgression. It captures that precise threshold where abundance ceases to be mere satisfaction and transforms into an overwhelming, swollen state of luxury, intoxication, or structural strain. In modern prose, it provides an exceptionally vivid, physical vocabulary for articulating overconsumption in consumerist culture, media overload, and artistic decadence.
