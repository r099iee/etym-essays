TRAILING is the present participle and verbal noun of the verb to trail. At its core it names the state or action of being drawn along behind something that moves: a rope, a garment, a branch, a stream of smoke, a line of dust. Something that trails keeps continuous contact with the ground or with the medium through which it moves, leaving a long wake or track behind it. In the passage from William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity, TRAILING appears inside the idiom "trailing my coat," which means deliberately provoking argument by broadcasting an aggressive or eccentric opinion. Empson admits that "apart from TRAILING my coat about minor controversies," he had claimed the right to define "ambiguity" however he pleased. The nuance is self-deprecating and combative: to trail one's coat is to drag it provocatively along the ground in the expectation that someone will step on it and start a fight — a figure inherited from the dueling age, when dragging a cloak or coat was a studied insult inviting a challenge. Empson pairs the metaphor with "minor controversies" to signal that his provocations were deliberate but not grave, bait cast for the sake of lively dispute rather than real battle. The physical and extended senses of TRAILING are numerous and form a clear family.
  • Dragging along behind (literal). A dress, scarf, coat-tail, rope, or vine that hangs down so as to touch or sweep the ground behind a moving person or thing: her long scarf trailing in the dust, the trailing hem of a wedding dress.
  • Creeping or spreading (botany). Of plants, growing along the ground or over a surface instead of standing erect: trailing vines, trailing ivy, a trailing rose. The botanical jargon "trailing habit" describes a prostrate or procumbent growth form.
  • Streaming or drifting (of fluids, dust, and light). Smoke, steam, dust, sparks, or clouds that spread out in a long wake behind a moving body: trailing smoke, the comet's trailing tail of dust.
  • Following or tracking. To follow the trail or track of a person or animal, often secretly: the detective trailing the suspect. In hunting, trailing means following a scent or spoor left by the quarry.
  • Falling behind (competition). To be behind in a race, contest, or election: trailing by ten points, the candidate trailing in the polls. Here the metaphor of the wake is reversed: the loser trails the leader.
  • Fading or diminishing (of sound or strength). The phrasal verbs trail off and trail away mean to become gradually fainter or weaker: his voice trailed off.
Modern technical jargons extend the word far beyond its physical base.
  • Aeronautics. The "trailing edge" is the rear edge of an airfoil or wing, opposite the leading edge; the "trailing vortex" is the wake of turbulence left behind an aircraft.
  • Finance and statistics. A "trailing stop" is a stop-loss order that follows a rising price; "trailing twelve months" (TTM) and "trailing earnings" measure the most recent four quarters of data.
  • Computing. A "trailing comma" or "trailing whitespace" is a comma or space at the end of a line, often a linting concern; a "trailing slash" is the forward slash at the end of a URL path.
  • Everyday and literary metaphors. A "trailing spouse" is a partner who relocates for the other's career; "trailing clouds of glory" is Wordsworth's famous phrase in the Ode: Intimations of Immortality for the divine glory children are said to bring with them at birth.
The semantic bridge from "dragging behind" to "deliberately provoking" is historical and cultural: the dueling-age gesture of dragging one's coat so that an insulted opponent could step on it — and so be forced to issue a challenge — turned a physical image into an idiom of studied provocation. Empson, writing as a combative young critic, knowingly exploits that rakish register, signalling that his provocations are bait for lively dispute rather than grave quarrels. In register the word is flexible: neutral and descriptive in the physical senses, informal-to-cultured and lightly combative in the idiomatic use, and technical in the jargons of aeronautics, finance, and computing.
