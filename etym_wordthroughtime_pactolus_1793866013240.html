- 7th to 6th century BCE - [Historical Lydian Economic Engine] - Known as the sacred river of Sardis flowing from Mount Tmolus, whose natural electrum-bearing alluvial sands financed the Lydian kingdom and enabled King Alyattes and King Croesus to mint the world's first state-guaranteed coinage. - 5th to 1st century BCE - [Classical Greek Mythological Hydronym] - Integrated into Greek mythology as the river god Pactolus (son of Oceanus and Tethys) and the miraculous site where King Midas cleansed himself of his golden touch on the command of Dionysus, transferring the golden curse into the riverbed. - 1st century BCE to 1st century CE - [Augustan Roman Poetic Trope] - Immortalized by Roman poets (Virgil, Ovid, Horace, Propertius) as the definitive poetic trope for an auriferous, glittering stream and an emblem of immense, exotic Eastern opulence. - 16th to 19th century - [Early Modern English Literary Metaphor] - Used extensively in Renaissance and Victorian literature (Spenser, Milton, Keats, Melville, Hawthorne) as an evocative symbol of natural abundance, boundless wealth, or treacherous, soul-corrupting gold. - 20th century to Present - [Archaeological and Geological Landmark] - Modernly known geographically as the Sart Çayı in Turkey, studied by Harvard-Cornell archaeologists who excavated the ancient Lydian gold-refining refineries on its banks, while surviving in educated prose as a rare literary synonym for a golden stream or boundless source of riches.
