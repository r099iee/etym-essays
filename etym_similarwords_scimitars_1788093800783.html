• Sabre (saber): Similar because both are curved, single-edged cavalry swords with guarded hilts; differs because the sabre's curve is lighter and it was a European military service weapon of the 18th–20th centuries, while the scimitar is the heavier, more deeply curved blade of the Islamic world.
  • Shamshir: Similar because it is the Persian curved sword from which the whole scimitar idea (and possibly the name) derives; differs because the shamshir's blade curves through almost its whole length and is exclusively a horseman's weapon, whereas "scimitar" in English is the looser cover term.
  • Kilij: Similar because it is a Turkish curved sabre, one of the classic scimitar-family blades; differs because the kilij flares into a broadened, often double-edged tip (the yelman) optimised for thrusting, while the classic scimitar profile widens gradually toward the point.
  • Tulwar (talwar): Similar because it is an Indian curved blade of the same family; differs because it carries a distinctive disc-shaped hilt, a shallower curve, and its own regional forging tradition, and is seldom called a scimitar by specialists.
  • Yataghan: Similar because it is a long, curved blade of the Ottoman Balkans with no crossguard; differs because it curves forward at the tip, has no guard at all, and is a walking-weapon of the janissary era rather than a cavalry saber.
  • Cutlass: Similar because it is a single-edged, slightly curved blade; differs because it is short and broad, a naval boarding weapon made for crowded ship decks, with none of the scimitar's deep sweep or eastern identity.
  • Falchion: Similar because it is a medieval European single-edged sword with a curved, cleaving blade; differs because it is a straight-spined infantry chopper of the 13th–15th centuries, unrelated to the eastern sabre tradition.
  • Broadsword: Similar because it is a sword; differs because it is straight and double-edged, built for two-edge cutting and thrusting, the structural opposite of the scimitar's single curved edge.
  • Rapier: Similar because it is a sword; differs because it is a thin, straight, double-edged thrusting weapon of civilian duelling, with no cutting curve at all.
  • Saif: Similar because it is the Arab straight-or-slightly-curved sword of the same historical world; differs because the Arab saif is often straight rather than deeply curved, and the word names a cultural category more than a blade shape.
