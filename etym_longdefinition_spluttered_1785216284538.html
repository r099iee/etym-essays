Spluttered is the past tense and past participle of the verb splutter: to speak in a rapid, confused, half-choked way, with spitting, explosive fragments of sound, or (of a flame, engine, or machine) to emit a series of sputtering, irregular noises before failing. In The Hobbit, the word is the final rung of Gollum's collapsing composure: "He hissed to himself, and still he did not answer; he whispered and spluttered." Each verb loses a degree of control: the hiss is a fierce breath through the teeth, the whisper is quieter but still articulate, and the splutter is the voice physically breaking apart — wet, popping, involuntary fragments forced out as fury and frustration overflow. To splutter is to lose the fluency of speech itself; the mouth becomes a machine that spits rather than speaks.

The word's expressive anatomy. Splutter is a blend of splash and sputter, and the blend is semantically exact: the spluttering speaker sprays syllables like droplets, while the mechanism of the sound is sputter's explosive, irregular emission. It is wetter than stammer (nervous hesitation in forming words), louder and messier than mutter (low, indistinct speech under the breath), more explosive than stutter (the mechanical repetition of initial sounds), and far less composed than protest (a reasoned objection). The typical splutter is the sound of indignation, rage, or amusement overwhelming speech — a wordless spray of spittle and syllables.

Exhaustive polysemy.

  • Of speech — incoherent, spitting speech (core, from 1704). To speak rapidly and confusedly, with explosive spitting noises: "he spluttered an apology", "she spluttered with indignation". Often the sound of being caught out, furious, or laughing so hard the words break. The bridge to the mechanical senses is the shared pattern of irregular, explosive emission.
  • Of flames, candles, and lamps (18th–19th c.). A candle or flame splutters as it gutters — burning unsteadily, flickering and spitting drops of melted wax before dying. The flame's irregular flaring is heard as a series of small explosions; the word describes the death-throes of fire.
  • Of engines and machines (20th c., mechanical jargon). An engine splutters when it misfires — irregular combustion producing coughs and pops before stalling; a dying car engine "splutters to a halt". Radio and electronics extend it: a signal or loudspeaker can splutter as it degrades. The bridge is the mechanical parallel of a failing, irregular rhythm.
  • Of liquids — to spatter or throw drops (secondary, physical). A spluttered liquid is one violently scattered: "he spluttered out his soup when he laughed", soup "splutters" in a pan. Here the word overlaps with spatter and splash, and the vocal and physical senses feed each other — the spitting mouth and the spitting pan are the same event.
  • Figurative and extended uses (modern). To "splutter" can mean to protest incoherently or express outrage without eloquence: "he spluttered in disbelief"; a spluttering defence is a failed, faltering one. By extension, anything that breaks down into noisy irregular bursts "splutters" — a spluttering phone call, a spluttering career, a spluttering start to a project. The connotation is nearly always comic or contemptuous: loss of composure made audible.
  • As a noun. "A splutter" is a burst of such sound — "a splutter of protest", "the splutter of the engine", "a splutter of laughter" — and in technical use, a brief irregular burst of sound or combustion.

Register and tone. Splutter is informal and expressive, carrying a faintly comic, slightly contemptuous charge: it is the word for speech that fails dramatically, for machines that die noisily, and for fires that gutter. Its blend of splash and sputter is itself onomatopoeic — the mouth-filling cluster spl- and the repeated -utter convey the wet, explosive, scattering quality of the thing named. Historically the word appears (1704) at a moment when English was coining many such expressive verbs, and it has stayed in the everyday register ever since, never fully rising to formal dignity — which is precisely why Tolkien can use it to make Gollum's breakdown both frightening and faintly absurd.
