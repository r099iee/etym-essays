The somersault begins in the muscles of a leaping animal. Its deepest ancestor is the reconstructed Indo-European root *sel-, to leap or spring — the same spring that survives in Latin salīre, to jump, from which Rome derived saltus, a leap, and a whole declension of dancing words: salii, the leaping priests of Mars who danced the city safe each spring, and exultare, to leap up, the ancestor of English exult. To leap, for the Romans, was already half a prayer.

Over the top in Old French: Medieval France joined its inherited saut (leap, from saltus) to sobre, over — from Latin supra and super, above — and coined soubresaut: not just a leap but a leap clean over, the startled bound of a deer clearing a wall, the dancer’s sudden spring. By the 1300s the word described both the hunter’s quarry bolting upright and the court tumbler throwing his heels skyward for applause; the start and the stunt shared one verb because they shared one shape — the body briefly vertical against gravity.

  • Salīre to saut: Latin’s crisp saltus softens through centuries of Gallic mouths into the one-syllable saut, keeping the leap and losing the grammar.
  • Sobre + saut: the prefix stacks height onto motion — over-leap, the jump that clears instead of merely rising.
  • Soubresaut to sombresaut: the intrusive m creeps in through nasalisation in 15th-century French, darkening the vowel before the b.

The Channel crossing (1520s): English tumblers — the professionals of fairs, May games, and noble households — imported the trick and its name together, and English ears at once began rebuilding the foreign word from native timber. French sobre- meant nothing to a Suffolk acrobat, but somer- sounded like summer, the season of fairs and open-air leaping, and perhaps like the great surname Somerset; so soubresaut became somersault and somerset, both spellings tumbling through Tudor records. The folk etymology stuck so fast that four centuries of scholars have failed to dislodge it — and small wonder, for summer is when somersaults are turned.

Fools, turncoats, and preachers (17th–18th c.): The trick’s metaphorical career began the moment divines noticed its moral geometry: the man who turns a somersault lands facing the way he came from, the perfect emblem of the turncoat, the trimmer, the Vicar of Bray. Sermons and satires of the Civil War and Restoration are full of political somersaults; the clown’s tumble became the weathercock’s shame. Meanwhile real tumblers flourished — Bartholomew Fair, Southwark, and every country wake had its somerset-man, and the word stayed sweaty and honest in their mouths whatever the preachers made of it.

Circus, gymnasium, and sky (19th–20th c.): The Victorian circus made the somersault the measure of the aerialist, and Friedrich Jahn’s German gymnastics made it the measure of the schoolboy; coaches broke the wheel into progressions — tuck, pike, layout — and judges learned to score its rotations. Aviation then stole it wholesale: the loop-the-loop is a machine’s somersault, and barnstormers who turned somersaults above county fairs thrilled the same crowds their tumbling grandfathers had thrilled on sawdust. Cinema completed the apotheosis: every screen hero now backflips away from an explosion, and Tolkien — who loved the homely English word — let his firework dragon turn one over Bywater, the oldest trick in the newest sky, drawn in dragon-fire for one delighted second before the bang.
