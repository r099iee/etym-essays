- 1590s–1650s - Entered English through diplomatic and travel accounts describing the fierce, semi-nomadic, self-ruling martial frontiersmen along the Dnieper and Don rivers, perceived as wild, untamed defenders on the European-Asian borderland.
- 1700s–1815 - Evolved in Western European perception during the Napoleonic Wars into legendary, terrifying light cavalrymen of the Russian Tsar, renowned for guerilla strikes and pursuing Napoleon's retreating Grande Armée across freezing terrain.
- 1850s–1917 - Connoted elite imperial horsemen, state enforcers, and fearsome crowd-control shock troops of the Romanov dynasty, while maintaining romantic literary associations with dashing steppe liberty in the works of Pushkin, Gogol, and Tolstoy.
- 1917–1980s - Associated with the tragic upheavals of the Russian Civil War, brutal Decossackization (raskazachivaniye), anti-Bolshevik White armies, exile, and subsequent ideological repurposing in Soviet historical epics such as Sholokhov's And Quiet Flows the Don.
- 1990s–Present - Re-emerged as a badge of ancestral heritage, regional revival, folk culture (acrobatic dances and choral music), and modernized civic-paramilitary patrol corps in Eastern Europe.
