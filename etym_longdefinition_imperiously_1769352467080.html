imperiously: in an arrogant, domineering, commanding, or overbearing manner; with a proud expectation of instant obedience, as though one had the right to give orders. The adverb of the adjective imperious, it describes the manner of an action — how someone gestures, speaks, waves, or rules — and it always carries the nuance of assumed authority: the imperious person commands not because their rank entitles them but because they behave as if it did. In the example sentence "He gestured imperiously for the servant to leave the room," the adverb turns a simple hand-wave into a small exercise of power — a gesture so certain of its own authority that no word needs to be spoken. The nuance is best captured as proud, lordly command regardless of actual rank: the word judges the behaviour, not the title. Grammar, register, and range
  • Adverb of manner: modifies verbs of speech, gesture, command, and rule — "she spoke imperiously," "he waved imperiously," "she ruled imperiously."
  • Register: literary and descriptive; it belongs to narrative prose, biography, and critical description of behaviour, and is more forceful than "arrogantly" because it specifically implies the giving of orders.
  • Derivation: imperious + the adverbial suffix -ly; the adjective (1540s) comes via French impérieux from Latin imperiōsus, "commanding, powerful, possessed of authority," from imperium, "command, authority, empire."
Polysemy & the semantic family
Imperiously sits at the centre of a large family of authority words that all descend from Latin imperāre, "to command":
  • Noun imperiousness: the abstract quality of commanding haughtily.
  • Noun empire: the area or political body under a single command (imperium), through Old French from Latin.
  • Adjective imperial: of or belonging to an emperor or empire — majestic, grand, state-level authority, distinct from the personal haughtiness of imperious.
  • Noun emperor: the holder of the imperium, via Old French empereor from Latin imperātor.
  • Adjective/Noun imperative: the grammatical mood of command; that which is essential and not to be negotiated — the same root of "commanding" extended to grammar and to necessity.
  • Modern technical/jargon uses: "imperious" in modern prose is almost wholly negative — bossy, overbearing, condescending; while "imperial" keeps the neutral-to-grand register of empire. The pair is a textbook example of two adjectives splitting from one root into a pejorative personal sense and an institutional state sense.
Historical semantic bridge
The chain runs: Latin imperāre ("to command, to rule" — literally "to impose a command upon," from in- "upon" + parāre "to make ready, prepare") → imperium ("supreme command, authority, empire") → imperiōsus ("endowed with command, commanding, imperious") → French impérieux → English imperious (1540s). The semantic bridge is the transfer of the command itself from the institutional to the personal: when a Roman general held the imperium, he had the legal right to command armies and provinces. When 17th-century English writers began to call a person "imperious," they borrowed that institutional grandeur and applied it to personal behaviour — the imperious person behaves with the manner of an emperor though he holds no empire. The bridge is thus institutional authority → personal arrogance, with the pejorative force growing as the institutional sense receded: "imperious" increasingly meant "like an emperor without being one." Metaphoric shifts and modern extensions
  • Imperial grandeur → personal pushiness: the core shift; by the 18th century "imperious" was fully personal and disapproving — command assumed, not earned.
  • Grammar (imperative): the same root supplies the grammatical term for the command mood, a technical-jargon offshoot that keeps the neutral "commanding" sense.
  • Abstract demands: "the imperious demands of duty," "an imperious need" — the word extends from persons to abstract forces that command obedience (desires, needs, circumstances), a 19th-century development still alive.
  • Register split with "imperial": modern English keeps "imperial" for state/majesty and "imperious" for bossy personal behaviour — a clean modern division of labour between two adjectives from one root.
Boundary against near-synonyms
Imperiously describes proud, lordly command of others. Domineeringly stresses tyrannical crushing of others' wills; arrogantly stresses self-admiring superiority without necessarily commanding; dictatorially imports political autocracy; authoritatively is neutral and justified; high-handedly adds arbitrary exercise of power. The imperious person is defined precisely by the assumption of a right to command — the behaviour of an emperor without the crown.
