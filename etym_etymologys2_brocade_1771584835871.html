(Origin uncertain — pre-Roman substrate; no secure PIE ancestry) *brokk- / *brocc- "something pointed, projecting; a sprout, spike, stub" (reconstructed substrate word, possibly Gaulish or another pre-Roman Mediterranean source absorbed by Latin) -> Latin broccus, brocca "projecting, jutting out (of teeth)" (classical Latin, plausibly a loanword of substrate origin; the source is debated — no established PIE ancestry, do not force one) -> (Italo-Romance) Italian brocco "a sprout, a shoot, an offshoot; a spike, a nail, a stump" (from the same "pointed, projecting" image) -> Italian broccare (verb) "to stud, to prick, to work with raised figures, to emboss" (14th–15th c., at the textile workshops of Lucca, Venice, and Genoa) -> Italian broccato (past participle) "embossed, studded"; as a substantive "rich cloth woven with raised figures" (14th c.) -> French brocart (17th c., loan from Italian broccato) -> English BROCADE (borrowed c. 1650s, first in mercantile accounts of luxury goods; French brocart → English brocade, with the -ade ending reflecting the textile-word family cf. parade, colonnade) Note on the origin: the ultimate source of broccus is unknown. Some scholars point to a Gaulish (Celtic) word meaning "pointed" or to a badger word (the badger's pointed snout and gapped grin), but the evidence is thin; the responsible statement is that the word is of pre-Roman substrate origin, possibly Celtic, and that no Proto-Indo-European ancestry can be securely reconstructed. The same "pointed thing" family also produced, in Italian, broccolo "little sprout, cabbage shoot" — the plural broccoli giving English broccoli (17th c.) — and, from the Gallo-Romance branch, Old French broche "spit, skewer, pointed rod" giving English broach and brooch.
