• antiquary: Similar because it denotes a student of old things; differs because it is the older noun for the person, now slightly affected, while antiquarian covers both person and pursuits including the book trade.
  • archaeological: Similar because it studies the material past; differs because archaeology is a scientific field method with excavation, whereas antiquarian is often amateur, textual, or collecting in tone.
  • historical: Similar because it concerns the past; differs because historical explains change and causation, while antiquarian may merely preserve or admire.
  • pedantic: Similar because both can reproach narrowscholarship; differs because pedantic stresses ostentatious rule-insistence, while antiquarian stresses love of oldness itself.
  • bookish: Similar because it suggests devotion to books over life; differs because bookish is general and often affectionate, whereas antiquarian specifies old books and bygone things.
  • fusty / fustian: Similar because they evoke musty old-fashionedness; differs because fusty is bluntly pejorative about stale air and ideas, while antiquarian can still be respectful.
  • nostalgic: Similar because both look backward fondly; differs because nostalgic names sentimental longing, while antiquarian names scholarly or collecting practice.
  • archaic / archaising: Similar because they involve old forms; differs because archaic describes the forms themselves, while antiquarian describes the person or pursuit devoted to them.
  • curatorial: Similar because it preserves and orders artefacts; differs because curatorial is professional museum practice, while antiquarian can be private and unsystematic.
  • dilettante: Similar because the antiquarian amateur can resemble one; differs because dilettante dabbles without expertise, while the best antiquarians know their corner deeply.
