FROM SEEKING TO CONQUERING: THE ROMAN FORGE OF THE SPANISH WORD Conquistador is a Spanish formation through and through, but its deepest layers are Latin and even Indo-European. The story begins with a verb of wanting and seeking: Latin quaerere, "to seek, ask, procure." Behind that verb stands a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root usually given as *kʷer- "to obtain, acquire, make," though the exact shape and semantics are not beyond dispute — de Vaan hesitates over a pre-Latin *kʷaes-/*kʷais- — and the traditional reconstruction in Pokorny is *kʷer-. From quaerere Latin built a large and busy family: quaestiō "a questioning" (whence question), quaestus "gain, profit" (whence quest), quaestor "the seeker-collector of public money," and, with the intensive prefix con- "together, thoroughly," conquirere "to seek out everywhere, collect, assemble." In classical prose conquirere meant to hunt out, gather, and procure things — often by legal or military compulsion. THE PARTICIPLE BECOMES A NOUN: *conquista The decisive turn came through the perfect passive participle conquīsītus, "sought out, acquired, won." From the feminine of the participle, Late Latin formed the noun conquista, "that which has been acquired" — an acquisition, a conquest. The shift is already a semantic migration: what begins as "the result of searching" becomes "the result of winning," because in war the victorious army "seeks out" and "acquires" the enemy's land and wealth. In the Romance languages this participle-noun split into two branches: the Old French conqueste, which entered English as conquest in the thirteenth century, and the Old Spanish conquista, recorded from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Spanish then formed from conquista a denominative verb, conquistar, "to conquer, win over," and from that verb the agent noun conquistador, built with the ordinary Spanish suffix -dor. THE -dor SUFFIX AND THE MEANING OF THE AGENT The suffix -dor is the Spanish reflex of Latin -tor (accusative -tōrem), the agent suffix of Latin, so conquistador is exactly "the one who conquers," in the same mould as cantador "singer," trabajador "worker," and conquistador itself. Every Spanish speaker in 1500 would have parsed the word as transparently as an English speaker parses "conqueror": a man who does conquering. The specifically American sense is not built into the word — it is built into history. THE 16TH CENTURY AND THE AGE OF CONQUEST Between 1492 and 1600 the word filled with the men it now names. Cortés took Tenochtitlan in 1521; Pizarro seized Cajamarca and executed Atahualpa in 1533; Coronado marched into the North American Southwest, de Soto through the Southeast; Valdivia and others pushed into Chile; and across the Caribbean and Central America a generation of captains, most of them hidalgos of modest means, fought under the banner of Castile for gold, land, encomienda, and salvation. The conquistador was at once soldier, explorer, entrepreneur, and governor — a private contractor of empire whose licence came from the crown and whose labour was rewarded with a share of conquest. The word, used in Spanish all along, began its second life in English. THE ENGLISH BORROWING, 19TH CENTURY English knew the men as "Spanish conquerors" for three centuries, but when it finally took the word itself, in the 1830s, it did so as a deliberate foreignism. William Hickling Prescott's History of the Conquest of Mexico (1843) and History of the Conquest of Peru (1847) made the Spanish word the standard label in English historiography, prized precisely because it is not "conqueror" — it carries the Spanish imperial frame, the 16th-century date, and a whiff of the New World that the home-grown word could not supply. Because it arrived as a loanword, it has kept its Spanish plural conquistadores, its Spanish-flavoured pronunciation, and a persistent italic in careful prose. A CONTESTED MODERN WORD Since the late 20th century conquistador has carried a double charge. In romanticized accounts it still names heroic enterprise — bold men who toppled vast empires through daring and firearms; in critical scholarship and public memory it names the instrument of colonial violence, enslavement, and demographic catastrophe, and the word is used with irony or condemnation accordingly. The figurative extension of the 20th century — the "corporate conquistador" who seizes markets, the "digital conquistador" who colonizes an industry — shows the semantic core surviving intact: an aggressive, morally ambiguous conqueror operating at the edge of an empire's reach, precisely the sense the Roman participle had when "seeking out" first became "winning by arms."
