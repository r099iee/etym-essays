- 1st c. BCE — Atticus is an agnomen earned by residence: Titus Pomponius, long settled in Athens, is "the Athenian" to his friends, and simply Atticus in Cicero's letters.
- 1st c. CE — Cornelius Nepos's Life of Atticus turns him into a moral type: the wealthy, cultured, politically detached equestrian who keeps faith with his friends.
- 1345 — Petrarch discovers Cicero's letters to Atticus at Verona, and the name becomes central to the Renaissance recovery of Cicero; Ad Atticum is henceforth a byword for intimate correspondence.
- 18th c. — English writers use Atticus as a type-name for the urbane patron of letters, most famously in Pope's portrait of Addison in the Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot (1735).
- 19th–20th c. — the name survives in classical scholarship and historical fiction until Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird (1960) gives it a new association with quiet, principled courage.
- 21st c. — chiefly a given name, chosen for its literary resonance rather than for the Roman original.
