The noun colts possesses an ancient Germanic and Indo-European ancestry, originating in roots associated with embryonic life, the maternal womb, and tender offspring before narrowing into one of the core terms of English horsemanship.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Germanic Origins:

  • Historical linguists trace colt to the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷel- or *gʷelbʰ-, which carries the core meaning of "womb," "fetus," or "young animal born of the womb."
  • This root is extraordinarily fertile across the Indo-European family: in Ancient Greek, it gave rise to delphys (δελφύς, "womb", source of Delphi and dolphin, literally "womb-fish") and adelphos (ἀδελφός, "brother", literally "from the same womb"); in Sanskrit, it yielded gárbha (गर्भ, "womb, embryo").
  • In the Germanic branch, the root developed into Proto-Germanic *kultaz ("young animal, offspring, something rounded or plump"). In Old Norse, related descendants include koltr ("young lad, boy, or knoll"), and in Swedish dialectal usage, kulte ("little boy, young pig").

2. Old English Development:

  • In Anglo-Saxon England, the word emerged as colt (nominative/accusative plural coltas). In Old English glosses and biblical translations, colt was not yet strictly restricted to male horses; it was used broadly for the young offspring of equines and beasts of burden.
  • For instance, in the Old English translation of the Gospels (e.g., the Lindisfarne and West Saxon Gospels), the ass's young upon which Christ rode into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday (Latin pullus asinae) was rendered as ass-colt or colt.

3. Middle English Semantic Specialization and Figurative Blossoming:

  • During the Middle English period (1150–1500), the term was spelled colt, colte (plural coltes). As horse breeding and chivalric horsemanship expanded in feudal England, the word began to differentiate itself from foal (from Old English fola, ungendered young horse).
  • Concurrently, Middle English writers seized upon the lively, rambunctious nature of young horses to coin figurative expressions. In Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales (late 14th century), the Wife of Bath famously describes her youthful, passionate vitality using the image: "How coude I daunce to an harpe smale, / And synge, y-wis, as any nightingale, / Whan I had dronke a draughte of swete wyn! / Metellius, the foule cherl, the swyn, / That with a staf birafte his wyf hir lyf, / For she drank wyn, thogh I hadde been his wyf, / He sholde nat han daunted me fro drinke! ... I was yong and ful of ragerye, / Stiborn and strong, and joly as a colt."
  • Chaucer also used the verb to colten to mean "to be wanton, frisky, or lustful."

4. Early Modern English to the 19th Century:

  • By the Elizabethan era, colt had settled into its strict modern equestrian definition: a young male horse under four years old, neatly balanced against filly (from Old Norse fylja) for young females.
  • Shakespeare made frequent use of the word's double meaning in plays like The Merchant of Venice (where Portia mocks the Neapolitan prince: "Ay, that’s a colt indeed, for he doth nothing but talk of his horse") and Henry VIII.
  • In the 18th and 19th centuries, the rise of organized thoroughbred horse racing codified the status of three-year-old colts in classic stakes races across Newmarket, Epsom, and later North America.

5. Modern and Contemporary Legacy:

  • Today, colts remains an indispensable term in global equestrian science, racing, sports culture, and idiom, retaining its evocative ancient heritage of youthful vigor and untamed power.
