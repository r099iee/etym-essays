TUGGING is the present participle of the verb to tug: pulling hard or repeatedly, with visible effort, usually against resistance or friction. The word names not a smooth, coordinated pull but a series of straining, unsteady jerks — the effort of a smaller body hauling against something that pulls back. Where pulling is neutral and effortless, tugging is always effortful: the muscles are visibly at work, the pull is repeated or maintained against opposition, and the motion has a jerky, insistent quality. It is used of people, animals, machines, and even of emotional forces (the "tug" of nostalgia, a heart-tugging scene). Literary context — The Hobbit. The sentence describes the goblins' capture of the company: they chain the prisoners' hands behind their backs, link them together in a line, and drag them to the far end of the cavern "with little Bilbo tugging at the end of the row." The participle does exact physical work. Bilbo is "little" — the smallest, the most vulnerable — and he is at the tail of the chain, so while the goblins drag, Bilbo tugs: his body strains, jerks, and scrambles to keep its footing against the pace of the whole line hauling him along. The word dramatises his desperate effort to stay upright, his helplessness, and his comic-small stature among dwarves and goblins alike. It also quietly frames the reader's sympathy on the smallest member of the Company at its most defeated moment. Polysemy and related senses. The tug-family has grown well beyond the simple verb:
  • Verb, literal — pull hard or repeatedly: "He tugged at the rope." "The child tugged her sleeve." The core sense: effortful, repeated, insistent pulling.
  • Verb, of emotions — to pull at the feelings: "Her heart was tugged by the sight." A figurative transfer: something pulls insistently at the affections or conscience.
  • Noun, sense 1 — a hard pull or jerk: "She gave the rope a tug." A single instance of the action.
  • Noun, sense 2 — a tugboat: a small, powerful vessel designed to tow or push large ships in harbours and narrow waters. "The tug nudged the liner into the dock." The nautical sense appears in the 19th century with the coming of steam tugs.
  • Noun, sense 3 — tug-of-war: a contest in which two teams pull opposite ends of a rope; metaphorically, any hard-fought contest or struggle between opposing forces ("a tug-of-war between duty and desire").
  • Tug of the heartstrings / heart-tugging: a fixed idiom for sentimental appeal; the "strings" are the tendons imagined in old physiology to anchor the heart, and the metaphor of a hard pull on them goes back centuries.
  • Technical and mechanical uses: in machinery, a tug is a pulling strain on a part; in textiles and rope-making, tugging describes straining fibres; in prose and film criticism, "tugging" describes performers or scenes that pull insistently at an audience's emotion.
  • Linguistic offshoots: tugboat (1817), tug-of-war, and probably toggle (1769, a frequentative of tug) — a small peg or crossbar whose action resembles pulling and fastening.
Semantic boundaries. Pulling is the neutral general term, without strain or repetition. Dragging (used of the goblins themselves) means hauling something that resists over a surface, the agent moving while the object is inert. Hauling implies heavy loads and long distances. Yanking is a single sharp, violent, almost contemptuous jerk. Wrenching adds twisting and the sense of tearing free. Towing is pulling by a rope or chain, as a boat or vehicle. TUGGING uniquely combines repetition with resistance: a tug is a hard, insistent, effortful pull repeated against something that pulls back — precisely the physical situation of Bilbo at the end of the goblins' line.
