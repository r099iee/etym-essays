The history of Outlaw as a verb meaning “to ban, prohibit, make illegal” — the sense required by “Outlaw them and the whole proceeding assumes an abiding air of flattery” — is a textbook case of a concrete Germanic legal status bleaching into a general abstract prohibition, and it can only be understood by starting with the Old Norse and Old English law of outlawry.

In early Germanic society law was not primarily punishment but protection. To be inside the law (Old Norse innan lags) meant to enjoy wergild, oath-help, and the peace of the assembly. To be put utlagr — literally “out-lying” with respect to law — was to be cast outside that shelter. The Old Norse noun utlagi and Old English utlaga denoted such a person: an exile, fugitive, or wolf’s-head (caput lupinum in Latin writs) who could be killed with impunity, whose property was forfeit, and who could receive no food or shelter. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Cnut’s laws, and later Bracton all treat the utlaga as legally dead. The word was therefore a compound of utterly transparent parts: ut- “out” plus lagu/lag “law,” itself from Proto-Germanic *lagą “thing laid down,” from PIE *legh- “to lie, lay.”

Key stages in the evolution:

  • Old Norse utlagi / Old English utlaga (9th–11th c.): a technical legal noun. Connotation is terror and social death. An outlaw is not a romantic rebel but a non-person. The verb does not yet exist independently; one “does” outlawry to someone by sentence (geutlagian appears rarely in late Old English).
  • Middle English outlawe / outlawen (13th–14th c.): after the Norman Conquest the Scandinavian term fuses with native utlaga and becomes standard in common-law writs of exigent. The verb outlawen is now fully attested: “to deprive of the benefit of the law, to banish.” Chaucer, Langland, and the Robin Hood ballads use the noun, while legal rolls use the verb. The romantic counter-current begins: the greenwood outlaw is both criminal and critic of corrupt justice.
  • Early Modern generalization (16th–18th c.): as formal outlawry in civil process becomes a procedural fiction (a defendant who fails to appear is “outlawed” without ever fleeing to the woods), the verb’s meaning widens from “to strip a person of legal protection” to “to declare any practice illegal.” Parliament “outlaws” popery, smuggling, or prize-fighting. Shakespeare uses both poles: the banished Duke in As You Like It and the proclamation that “outlaws” a deed. The capital-letter verb in the source sentence belongs here: an intellectual move, a motive, a competition is “outlawed” — excluded from legitimate procedure.
  • American frontier romanticism (19th c.): Jesse James, Billy the Kid, and the dime novel invert the moral charge. The outlaw becomes heroic individualist, and the verb gains a libertarian flavor: to outlaw something is what a distant, puritanical state does to authentic custom. Legal historians (Pollock and Maitland, 1895) recover the medieval technical sense and fix the etymology in scholarship.
  • Twentieth-century abstraction and subculture (1900–present): formal outlawry is abolished in England (1879 for civil, 1938 wholly) and falls into desuetude in America, so the noun and verb float free. Legislatures “outlaw” discrimination, chemical weapons, or texting while driving — pure synonym of ban/prohibit with a sterner, more archaic resonance. Simultaneously, “outlaw” becomes a positive style label: outlaw country music, outlaw bikers, outlaw poetics — deliberately embracing the excluded position. Capitalized sentence-initial Outlaw preserves the imperative force: banish it from the canon.

Phonetically the development is uneventful, which is itself diagnostic of a native compound: Old Norse ut- and Old English ut- merge without French interference; the second element -lagi/-laga levels to -law by regular Middle English reduction of unstressed vowels and voicing of /g/ to /w/ before back vowels. No Latin sound-shift intervenes, unlike legal, loyal, or liege, which are French doublets of the same PIE root.

Literary attestations anchor each phase: Beowulf’s Grendel as an utlaga-like exile; the 13th-century Tale of Gamelyn; the Robin Hood Gest (c. 1450) where “outlawes” live “by light of moon”; Shakespeare’s Two Gentlemen of Verona (“I must be an outlaw”); Scott’s Ivanhoe; and modern statutes (“smoking is outlawed in public buildings”). The source sentence’s use — “Outlaw them and the whole proceeding assumes an abiding air of flattery” — belongs to rhetorical polemic: exclude competitive and playful motives from stylistics and criticism becomes sycophancy. It revives the oldest sense (put outside protection) as a methodological ban.
