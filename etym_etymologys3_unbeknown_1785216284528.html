The story of unbeknown begins in Proto-Indo-European with the verbal root *ǵneh₃-, "to know, perceive, recognize," one of the great mental verbs of the ancient language, already double-faced: it could mean the act of coming to know or the settled state of knowing. From this root nearly every Indo-European branch forged its own verb of knowledge — Sanskrit jānāti, Greek gignṓskō, Latin (g)nōscō, Old Church Slavonic znati — and in the Germanic branch it produced Proto-Germanic *knēaną, which became Old English cnāwan, "to know, recognize, perceive." This is the verb behind Chaucer's knowen and our modern know.

The prefix be-. Old English already possessed a prefixed formation, becnāwan, "to recognize, acknowledge," built with the intensifying particle be- (the same particle found in become, befriend, belittle). The prefix sharpened the plain verb: where cnāwan meant "to know," becnāwan meant to know by recognizing or owning something, to take knowledge of it to oneself. It passed into Middle English as beknowen, and its past participle beknown, "known, acknowledged," survived into Early Modern English chiefly in dialects and in legal-style phrases such as "well beknown to all." It is this participle that carries the word forward.

Negation. The decisive step came when speakers negated the participle with the privative prefix un-, yielding unbeknown, "not known," first recorded in the 1630s, and then, most distinctively, pressed it into an adverbial-predicative slot: unbeknown to him, "without his knowing." Here the semantics tipped from "not known (by anyone)" to "not known by a particular person," an epistemic narrowing that gave the word its signature grammar — the to-phrase naming the party left in ignorance. The word could not be used as a simple attributive adjective; it lived in the construction "unbeknown to X," and that fixed grammar preserved it as an archaic fossil long after the verb beknow died out of ordinary use.

The excrescent -st. In the nineteenth century the word gained a second life. Around 1848 an excrescent -st appeared on the model of amongst and against, producing unbeknownst, which spread widely in American usage though it never displaced the older form in Britain. The -st is purely analogical — it has no grammatical function — but it gave the word a fresh, slightly old-fashioned charm, and today unbeknownst is in fact the more frequent form in American English.

Register and literary life. The word carries an archaic, dialectal, storytelling flavour. Because the simple verb beknow had died out, unbeknown had no living family to anchor it; it survived as a relic, treasured by writers who wanted an old-fashioned, folk-tale cadence. Tolkien, a professional philologist steeped in older English, deliberately employs it in The Hobbit (1937): "there are other things living unbeknown to them that have sneaked in from outside to lie up in the dark." The word does double work in the sentence — it names the goblins' ignorance and, by its very archaism, signals that the narrator stands outside time, confiding a secret to the reader that even the cave-owners do not know. The dramatic irony is built into the vocabulary.

Sound and semantics. The word's sound matches its meaning: the unstressed un-, the flattened vowels of be-known, and the weightless tail give it a hushed, conspiratorial quality. Its semantic career is one of steady restriction: a general Indo-European verb of knowing → a Germanic participle of being known → a negated adjective of not being known → a fixed idiom of another's ignorance. Every step is attested, and the word that began as the common property of the whole Indo-European family narrowed over three thousand years into a single, almost folkloric construction: unbeknown to him — without his knowing, forever.

Key sound shifts and forms.
  • *ǵneh₃- → Germanic *knēaną: the palatal *ǵ shifted to velar *k in Germanic (Grimm's law); the laryngeal *h₃ coloured the vowel and was lost.
  • *knēaną → Old English cnāwan: the *ē of Proto-Germanic became Old English ā; the initial kn- cluster survives (silent since Early Modern English).
  • cnāwan → becnāwan: Old English prefix be- "about, thoroughly," giving "to recognize."
  • becnāwan → beknown: the strong past participle -en of the verb, yielding "known, acknowledged."
  • beknown → unbeknown: privative un- "not" + participle; first recorded 1630s.
  • unbeknown → unbeknownst: excrescent -st, c. 1848, on the model of amongst/against.
