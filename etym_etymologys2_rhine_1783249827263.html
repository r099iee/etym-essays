Chain 1 (Celtic main branch): PIE *h₃reyH- 'to flow, run, stream' (reconstructed; the family includes Greek rhéō 'to flow', Latin rīvus 'stream', Vedic Sanskrit rīyate 'flows', Old Irish rían 'sea, way, path', Proto-Germanic *rinnaną 'to run, flow') → Proto-Celtic *Rēnos 'that which flows' (a generic word for running water) → Gaulish Rēnos 'the Rhine' (recorded by Roman authors) → Latin Rhenus 'the Rhine' (Latinized; spelling influenced by the Greek form Ῥῆνος Rhênos fixed by the Greek geographers) → English Rhine (via Latin Rhenus, keeping the -h- spelling; also Rhenish < Latin Rhenānus 'of the Rhine'). Chain 2 (Germanic side branch): Proto-Germanic *Rīnaz 'the Rhine' (borrowed from Celtic *Rēnos) → Old English Rīn 'Rhine' → Middle English Rine → English Rhine (modern spelling re-Latinized from Rhenus); Old High German Rīn → Middle High German Rīn → German Rhein; Old Saxon Rīn → Middle Dutch Rīn → Dutch Rijn; Old Norse Rín 'the Rhine' (in the sagas). Note: *Rēnos was not originally a proper name but a descriptive hydronym ('the flowing one'), applied by the Celts to several rivers; the same pattern underlies the Rhône (< Latin Rhodanus, Gaulish *Rodanos 'the flowing one'). The river-name is a place-name tradition, not an invented conceptual root.
