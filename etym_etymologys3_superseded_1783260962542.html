superseded is the end of a chain that begins in the oldest posture of Indo-European life: sitting. The Proto-Indo-European root *sed-, "to sit," is one of the most productive in the family, giving English sit, set, seat, and saddle no less than the Latin verb at the heart of this word. In the Italic branch *sed- became Proto-Italic *sedē- and then Latin sedēre, "to sit." Upon this verb Latin built the compound that concerns us by adding the prefix super-, "over, above": supersedēre, literally "to sit above." The image is of a judge or magistrate enthroned above a court. From that commanding posture the verb acquired two figurative lives at once. On one side it meant "to be superior to, to take precedence over," since the one in the higher seat outranks all below. On the other side it meant "to refrain from, to forbear, to desist," for one who sits above a dispute may choose, from that height, not to act upon it, letting the matter rest below him. Both the sense of pre-eminence and the sense of refraining flow from the same picture of the elevated seat.

It was the second meaning — "to forbear, to suspend" — that travelled with the word into French. Middle French superseder meant "to defer, postpone, hold over," and in the sixteenth century English borrowed the verb. In early English use one could "supersede" a prosecution or a law, meaning to abstain from pressing it forward; the noun supersedeas, Latin "you shall desist," named the writ that stayed legal proceedings. The word's spelling history is itself a small lesson in learned philology. The French form had produced spellings like superceder that risked confusion with the great family of Latin-derived verbs in -ceed and -cede (exceed, proceed, concede). English consciously re-Latinized the word, restoring the ending -sede to mirror Latin supersedēre, so that supersede remains the only common English verb to end in -sede rather than -ceed or -cede.

Then, in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the modern sense emerged and prevailed. The verb shifted from "to forbear, to suspend" to "to occupy a superior or later position and thereby set the older thing aside": a new law supersedes an old one, a new constitution supersedes the earlier charter, a new technology supersedes the outdated machine. The semantic bridge is easy to state: the one who sits above the matter is superior to it, and what is superior comes later and replaces what went before. The word's register also settled — calm, formal, almost judicial, carrying no violence. It is the vocabulary of orderly progress rather than violent ousting, which is why the historians of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries could use it of whole institutions, languages, and peoples.

In the sentence on this card the word performs precisely this late, formal work: "In our island the Latin appears never to have superseded the old Gaelic speech, and could not stand its ground against the German." The claim is that Latin, despite the prestige of Rome and the later reach of the Church, never managed to take the superior seat over Gaelic; it neither displaced the native tongue nor prevailed against the incoming Germanic speech. Superseded here is chosen for its judicial calm: the failure of Latin is stated as an orderly fact of historical priority, not as a violent defeat.

  • Sound and spelling: Latin sedēre "to sit" → French -seer/-cer forms → re-Latinized English -sede; the final past-participle suffix -ed is the ordinary English weak-verb ending.
  • Semantic drift: sit above (physical) → take precedence / forbear (legal-administrative) → set aside and replace (modern).
  • Cognate family in English: sedēre also yields sedentary, session, sediment, assess (from assidēre), possess, preside, president, siege, while the same PIE root appears natively in sit and set.
  • Socio-cultural context: the word's rise in the 17th–18th centuries coincides with the age of statute, codification, and technological progress, when "being superseded" became the normal fate of laws, customs, and machines.
