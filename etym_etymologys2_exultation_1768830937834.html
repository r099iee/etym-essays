[CHAIN FOR exultation]
1. PIE *sel- “to spring, leap, move quickly” (reconstructed root; attested in Sanskrit sárati, “to run, to flow,” Greek hállomai, “to leap,” and Latin salīre, “to leap”). No direct attestation survives; its reality is inferred from these cognates.
2. Latin saliō, salīre “to leap, to spring, to jump” (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; Plautus, Ennius). The leap-verb at the root of the family.
3. Latin saltō, saltāre “to leap about, to dance, to jump repeatedly” (frequentative of salīre; attested in classical prose and comedy). The -t- frequentative marks repeated or habitual action.
4. Latin exsultō, exsultāre “to leap up again and again, to bound and prance; figuratively, to rejoice triumphantly” (attested from the 1st c. BCE; ex- “out, up” + saltāre). The Vulgate (4th c. CE) uses it for the rejoicing soul.
5. Latin exsultātiō, exsultātiōnis “a leaping for joy; rejoicing” (attested in Christian Latin; the abstract noun of the verb).
6. Old French exultation / exultacion “rejoicing” (12th–14th c.), from the Latin noun.
7. Middle English exultacioun, exultation “triumphant joy” (15th c.), from Old French; the verb exult (from Latin exsultāre via French exulter) follows in the 16th c. — target exultation.

NOTE ON NUANCE: Medieval and early modern writers frequently conflated exsultāre (“to leap for joy”) with exaltāre (“to raise high,” from altus, “high”), and the confusion occasionally bled into early English spelling and usage. The two verbs are etymologically unrelated despite their similar form.
