- Clumsy motion (c. 1300) - The verb to lumber is recorded first — 'to move heavily and clumsily' (Middle English lomere, from a Scandinavian source); a word about lameness and awkwardness, kin to lame itself.
- Bulky clutter (1550s) - The noun lumber appears meaning 'heavy, clumsy, useless objects' — the odds and ends that gather in attics; lumber-room and lumber-house are coined for the storerooms, and by grim extension the pawnshops, where forfeited goods collected.
- New England narrowing (c. 1660) - Massachusetts records seize the British noun for the great export of the forests: lumber becomes 'timber sawn into rough planks for use' — from any clumsy discarded bulk to the specific, marketable bulk of sawn wood; a narrowing that never fully reached Britain, where lumber still means attic clutter.
- Industrial vocabulary (18th–19th c.) - The American sense grows a whole trade: lumber-yard (1777), lumber-mill (1830), lumber camp (1839), lumberjack (1830s), and the verb to lumber, 'to cut forest trees' (1809), which returns to English from its own noun.
- Dual life (20th c.–present) - Lumber is the flat, utilitarian standard American word for building material — the vocabulary of the yard and the freight ledger — while the old junk-sense survives in BrE and the clumsy-motion verb lumbers on beside it.
