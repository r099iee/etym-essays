- In its earliest recorded English use (Old English bylgian) it meant the deep roaring cry of animals, above all cattle and stags — a strictly zoological, onomatopoeic sound.
- Through Middle English (belwen) it stayed a country word: the belwering of oxen, bulls, and deer, heard by shepherds and hunters, never in polite conversation.
- In the 1500s-1600s it extended to human voices — but only when a man's shout sank to animal depth: rage, pain, drunkenness, or the bawling of soldiers; the word lost its purely rural colour and took on an aggressive, bestial edge.
- From the 17th century it spread to things without throats: bells, thunder, the sea, cannon, and later engines and machinery — "bellowing" became the standard word for a deep, swelling, resonant noise that seems to surge and fall like breath.
- In modern usage it is equally at home in the barnyard and the boardroom: a bull bellows, but so does an angry foreman; media now speak of "bellowing" crowds, "bellowing" furnaces, and even "bellowing" subwoofers — the animal cry remains the vivid core beneath every metaphor.
