• assembly: Similar because both refer to a group of people gathered together in a single place for a shared purpose; differs because assembly is a broad, generic term covering everything from a casual school morning gathering to a political legislature, whereas convocation carries an inherently ceremonial, solemn, and authoritative character specifically summoned by high authority.
  • congregation: Similar because both denote an organized body of people assembled together; differs because congregation is predominantly religious and pastoral, describing the body of worshippers gathered in a church, synagogue, or mosque for divine service, whereas convocation applies to academic degree conferrals, synodical governance, or formal summons.
  • conclave: Similar because both refer to authoritative institutional gatherings; differs because a conclave (literally "under lock and key") emphasizes absolute secrecy, seclusion, and high-stakes election (most famously the College of Cardinals electing a Pope), whereas a convocation is open, public, ceremonial, and transparent.
  • synod: Similar because both can describe a formal council of church officials; differs because synod (from Greek synodos, "walking together") is exclusively ecclesiastical and focuses on doctrinal, theological, and canonical policymaking, whereas convocation extends extensively into collegiate graduation ceremonies and fraternal meetings.
  • convention: Similar because both involve people traveling to gather for a structured event; differs because convention typically describes large commercial, professional, or political party gatherings (e.g., trade conventions, political nominating conventions) featuring booths, elections, and networking, lacking the ancient ceremonial ritual of an academic convocation.
  • symposium: Similar because both are intellectual and institutional gatherings; differs because a symposium focuses specifically on academic presentations, intellectual debates, and specialized research papers on a single topic, whereas a convocation is a ceremonial ceremony celebrating milestones or issuing addresses.
  • commencement: Similar because in North American universities, commencement and convocation are often used interchangeably to denote graduation ceremonies; differs because commencement etymologically emphasizes "the beginning" of a new professional life upon receiving a degree, whereas convocation emphasizes the physical act of "calling together" the community.
  • council: Similar because both denote a body of members assembled for deliberation; differs because a council is typically a standing, ongoing executive or administrative body (e.g., city council, security council) that meets frequently to manage affairs, whereas a convocation is an episodic, grand ceremonial event.
  • muster: Similar because both involve summoning individuals to a central assembly point; differs because muster is strictly military or naval, involving the assembling and roll-call inspection of troops or sailors for combat preparedness.
  • diet: Similar in constitutional history because both represent formal political assemblies convened by sovereign summons; differs because diet is an archaic political term specifically designating imperial or national legislative assemblies in central Europe (such as the Diet of Worms or the Japanese Diet).
