- Early 17th Century - First emerges in Jacobean literature and humanistic translations (e.g., Philemon Holland); used in a literal and occasionally cautious sense to describe conditions or honors capable of drawing the dangerous, superstitious malice of the "evil eye."
- Mid-17th to 18th Century - Broadens across Enlightenment philosophy and polite essays (Milton, Dryden, Addison); shifts from a cautionary warning of danger to a complimentary descriptor of prosperous worldly fortune and intellectual excellence worthy of emulation.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes an established staple of literary journalism, academic biography, and parliamentary debate; the litotic negative construction ("not an enviable task," "hardly an enviable duty") crystallizes as a permanent idiom of British understatement for thankless, grueling, or politically fraught labor.
- 20th Century - Proliferates across corporate finance, sports journalism, and geopolitical commentary (e.g., "enviable market share," "enviable track record"), functioning as an objective benchmark of competitive supremacy, organizational resilience, and elite prestige.
- 21st Century - Continues as a standard high-register adjective in academic prose, aesthetic critique, and cultural analysis, distinguishing genuine, enduring human flourishing from the ephemeral, algorithmic envy generated by digital social media feeds.
