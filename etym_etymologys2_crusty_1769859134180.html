PIE *kreus- "to freeze, form a crust, congeal" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; debated — see note) → Latin crusta "a hard outer covering, rind, shell, crust, bark" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE in Plautus and classical Latin prose; also crustum "baked pastry, hard pie-crust") → Old French crouste / croste "the baked outer layer of bread or pastry; rind, shell" (attested 11th–12th c. CE; modern French croûte) → Anglo-Norman and Middle English crust(e), crouste "the hard outer part of bread; the pastry covering of a pie; any hard outer layer" (attested c. 1300, 14th c.) → Middle English crust + adjective suffix -y (from Old English -ig) = crusty "having or resembling a crust; hard, brittle" (attested 14th c.) → English crusty, figurative human sense "gruff, short-tempered, irritable" (attested from the 1560s, the earliest OED citations applying the word to persons) → Modern English CRUSTY "easily irritated, gruff; also having a hard crust" ◄ TARGET rootWord Debate / nuance note: The reconstructed PIE root behind Latin crusta is disputed. Many authorities set up *kreus- "to freeze, form a crust," linking Greek krúos "icy cold," krustállō "to freeze," and krustallos "ice," with the semantic core "to congeal into a hard layer"; under this view the *-eu- diphthong reduces to -u- before the -st- cluster. Other scholars treat Latin crusta as an independent formation of unknown prehistory and caution that the Greek and Latin forms may be parallel but not identical. Either way, the Romance and English descent of crusta is certain.
