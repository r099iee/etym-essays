- [Early 16th century (c. 1510)] - Borrowed as a verb meaning "to send off a message or messenger with haste" and "to finish business promptly"; the spellings dispatch and despatch compete from the start.
- [16th–17th century] - The noun emerges: "a despatch" is the message or orders sent off, especially military despatches borne by courier; still concrete and physical.
- [18th–19th century] - The abstract sense grows — "prompt settlement of business, expedition, haste"; despatch becomes the commoner British spelling; a sinister offshoot also means "to kill, to put out of the way".
- [Modern register] - Formal and literary; denotes brisk, decisive, economical speed in finishing work (an editor's virtue) — with the concrete sending sense surviving in news "dispatches".
