Cucamonga is a proper noun denoting a city in southwestern San Bernardino County, California — formally Rancho Cucamonga since the 1977 consolidation of Alta Loma, Cucamonga, and Etiwanda — and, far more famously, an evocative piece of Americana: a funny-sounding, sun-baked, Route-66 place-name that functions in American speech as shorthand for a distant, slightly absurd, vaguely exotic suburb at the edge of the desert.

In the source sentence — buying a new car “to get to Cucamonga and back” — the word is used precisely in this second, symbolic sense: not as a navigational destination requiring a map, but as an emblem of ordinary practical motoring, the everyday there-and-back trip. The humor and force of the example depend on the reader hearing the name as innately comic and remote, a place one drives to simply because cars exist.

  • Primary denotation — the place itself: a city of roughly 175,000 people on the Cucamonga Plain beneath the San Gabriel Mountains, part of the Inland Empire; a former wine- and citrus-growing district on historic Route 66, now a logistics, retail, and commuter suburb of Los Angeles.
  • Administrative form — Rancho Cucamonga: the official municipal name, preserving the 19th-century Mexican rancho name; used in addresses, government, and formal registers, while “Cucamonga” alone survives in colloquial speech, song lyrics, and jokes.
  • Comic-evocative use — funny-name Americana: from the 1940s onward, especially via radio comedian Jack Benny’s announcer intoning “Anaheim, Azusa and Cu-camonga!”, the name became a national gag, prized for its rhythmic vowels, pseudo-Spanish–pseudo-“Indian” sound, and suggestion of remoteness; to say “Cucamonga” is to conjure railroad-station comedy, kitsch postcards, and desert sprawl.
  • Metonymic use — any far-flung suburb: writers use “Cucamonga” the way they use “Walla Walla,” “Kokomo,” or “Timbuktu”: an anyplace-remote, a comic stand-in for the outer edge of commuting range, the place you drive to and back to prove the car’s practical purpose.
  • Attributive and commercial use: Cucamonga wines, Cucamonga Valley AVA (American Viticultural Area), Cucamonga Peak, Rancho Cucamonga Quakes (minor-league baseball); the name brands local produce, trails, and teams with heritage authenticity.
  • Cultural-historical resonance: citrus groves, Pacific Electric red cars, Route 66 service stations, Chicano cruising culture, and later warehouse sprawl; the word carries layers of California history — Tongva land, Spanish missiontancho era, Anglo grove town, freeway suburb — compressed into four musical syllables.

Connotation is affectionate rather than contemptuous: sunny, sprawling, slightly ridiculous, stubbornly real. Unlike “the sticks” or “nowhere,” Cucamonga names a genuine community while winking at the listener; the joke is shared, not cruel. In literary prose it signals the author’s ear for American vernacular music — the pleasure of saying place-names aloud — and punctures the solemn “practical purpose” theory of cars by reminding us that destinations are also sounds, jokes, and dreams.

Usage notes: always capitalized; often preceded by “Rancho” in formal contexts but bare “Cucamonga” in comic or nostalgic contexts; pronounced in California roughly “koo-kuh-MUNG-guh”; never pluralized except in historical discussion (“the Cucamongas” for the three towns). To “go to Cucamonga and back” is thus both a literal commute and a small mythic journey into the American funny-place-name imagination.
