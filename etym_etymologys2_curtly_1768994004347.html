curtly - PIE root *sker- "to cut" (reconstructed; with the s-mobile variants *ker- / *kert-) — one of the most productive cutting roots in the family; reflexes include Sanskrit kṛntáti "he cuts, cuts off," Greek keírō "I cut, shear, clip," Latin curtus "cut short," Old Irish scaraim "I separate, cut off," and Germanic *skurtaz "short, cut off." - Latin curtus "cut short, shortened, docked, maimed" (attested classical) — used of a docked tail, a stunted plant, and of speech clipped of its length. - Latin curto, curtāre "to shorten" (attested Late/Medieval Latin) — the verb of shortening. - English curt "short, brief" (borrowed c. 1620, from Latin curtus, possibly through Middle French court "short") — the adjective; the same French word gave curtail (16th c., from courtault "short-tailed," reshaped against tail). - English curtly "in a curt manner" (adverb, 17th c., from curt + -ly) — the adverbial form required by the passage. - English curtly. Note on debate/nuance: the chain is secure — *sker- is one of the best-attested PIE roots, and Latin curtus sits in it without controversy. The only nuance worth flagging is the double source of English curt: the OED allows either direct borrowing from Latin curtus or adoption via Middle French court "short" (which is also the ancestor of English curtail); both routes converge on the same Latin word. A false-friend warning: curtsy and courtesy descend from Latin cohors, "courtyard" — the behaviour of the court — and are completely unrelated to curt. The Germanic branch of *sker- independently produced short (Old English sceort), shirt and skirt (the "cut" garments), and German kurz.
