- 14th c. - adage enters English from French with the neutral classical sense, a saying, a proverb, a maxim; it is admired rather than mocked, part of the culture of proverb-mongering that the fourteenth century inherited from the Latin grammarians
- 15th-16th c. - the word acquires authority and attachment: a saying of rule, a scriptural text treated as decisive, and by extension the motto or legend over a coat of arms; the Latin sense of a jest or witty remark also crosses over
- 1600s-1700s - the reversal, in the age of classical education and sharpening satire: an adage becomes a trite, stale, worn-out saying, something a learned man repeats without asking whether it is true
- 1700s-1800s - a neutral and slightly dusty register: a proverb current among a nation, trade or school, as in the adages of the Romans or of the ancients; eighteenth-century essayists use it of received wisdom in general
- 1800s-1900s - a maxim, an aphorism in the respectable sententious tradition; the word is formal and unfashionable, used from a slight distance, and increasingly in the tone of a man who has heard it before
- 1900s-1950s - the dismissive sense consolidates; an old adage becomes a set phrase meaning a platitude, and the word joins cliché and commonplace as a term for empty, circulating wisdom
- 1960s-2000s - irony takes over: the word labels circular reasoning and the canned wisdom of institutions, sport, business and politics; there’s an adage for that and as the adage goes are stock formulae
- 2010s-present - adage and platitude are practically interchangeable for most speakers; the word has become a slightly grander, more literary way of saying cliché, and it survives as a term of art in branding, where a slogan is an adage waiting to be launched
