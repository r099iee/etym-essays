- Curse and denounce (late 16th c.) - Enters English as a strong, quasi-religious word meaning to curse or denounce, to call evil down upon a person - carrying the old sense of testifying against someone as an accuser.
- Denunciation cooling to dislike (17th c.) - The religious force softens into settled personal aversion: to detest is no longer to invoke harm but to hold someone in weighed, deliberate, almost judicial contempt.
- The age of the verdict (18th-19th c.) - Becomes the formal, morally tinged word for reasoned dislike; Austen's 'She began to detest the very sight of him' shows an insult hardening in real time into a permanent sentence passed upon the whole person.
- Modern register - Retreats from everyday speech as hate absorbs ordinary use; survives in emphatic, slightly grand contexts ('I detest early mornings') still carrying a faint undertone of quiet accusation.
