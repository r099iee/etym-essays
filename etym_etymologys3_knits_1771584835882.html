The root is Germanic-only. English knit comes straight down the Germanic line: Old English cnyttan, “to tie with a knot”, recorded glossing Latin nectere and ligare, from Proto-Germanic *knuttjaną, a verb built on the noun *knuttaz “knot”. The cluster begins as a Germanic innovation: words of the shape kn- + vowel + -tt-/-t- form an expressive family across the North and West Germanic languages—Old Norse knyta and knútr, Middle Dutch cnutten and cnode, Middle Low German knütten, Middle High German knote (modern German Knoten). Beyond Germanic the family dissolves: there is no solid Indo-Iranian, Latin, or Greek cognate. Etymologists tentatively reach toward PIE *gen- “to pinch, compress, make tight” (the ancestor, if the link is sound, of knuckle and knock), but the kn- onset and geminate -tt- have the look of a reduplicative, sound-symbolic formation, and the honest verdict is “no secure PIE ancestry”. This is why the card’s chain begins at the Proto-Germanic level rather than at a reconstructed Indo-European root. The English word splits into two senses almost from the start: to tie a knot, and—by the late Old English period—to join threads into a fabric. Middle English knitten regularly means to form a mesh with needles, and by Shakespeare’s day the textile verb is fully fixed—“Sleep that knits up the ravell’d sleeve of care” (Macbeth) still draws on the weaving image. The “brow” sense arises by metaphor: the eyebrows are drawn together as if tied by a single cord, the two brows “knitted” into a knot of frown. The idiom to knit one’s brow(s) appears in Early Modern English prose and drama of the sixteenth century, and it becomes the stock literary gesture of concentration or displeasure—precisely the gesture Bao-yu playfully attributes to Dai-yu in The Story of the Stone, where the weaving joke turns the brow-movement into an ornament of her name. A separate branch of meaning runs through bones knitting (fracture healing), communities knitting together, and the modern close-knit and tightly-knit compounds. The semantic movement in every case is the same: from physical tying to figurative uniting. In the textile sense the word acquired its vast modern importance with the spread of hand knitting as a domestic craft (14th–15th centuries onward), the rise of the knitted garment industry, and machine knitting in the nineteenth century; in the figurative sense it remains one of English’s favourite images for anything bound closely together. Sound symbolism and phonology:
  • The English kn- cluster is famous for its “silent k”: originally pronounced [kn-], the cluster simplified to [n-] in the standard language during the 17th–18th centuries, so that knit is now homophonous with nit; the spelling preserves the older consonant cluster, and the IPA of the card, /nɪts/, reflects the modern pronunciation of knits.
  • Expressive phonology: kn- + -tt- suggests compression, smallness, and repeated contact (cf. knot, knuckle, knock, knick-knack)—a sound-symbolic family typical of Germanic word-creation, which is one reason the root resists a clean PIE etymology.
  • Semantic drift: “to tie a knot” → “to bind threads into fabric” (textile) → “to draw the brows together” (frown) → “to join closely” (people, bones, arguments)—consistently from physical tying to figurative uniting.
  • Related but distinct roots: PIE *mezg- “to plait, knit” produced the Balto-Slavic and Indic words (Lithuanian megzti, mazgas; Sanskrit muñja- “reed-grass for plaiting”) and is NOT the ancestor of English knit; PIE *ned- “to bind” gave Latin nōdus and nectere and Old English nett, whence English net. Both must be kept separate from the Germanic kn- family.
  • Literary attestations: Old English glosses (10th c.); the Ormulum and Middle English religious prose; Chaucer’s uses of knit as tied or bound; Shakespeare’s Macbeth (“knits up the ravell’d sleeve of care”); and Hawkes’s The Story of the Stone (“she knits her brows together in a little frown”), where the craft sense and the brow sense meet in a single playful sentence.
