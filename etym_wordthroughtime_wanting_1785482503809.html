- [Norse inheritance (c. 1200)] - From Old Norse vanta, "to be lacking," Middle English wanten meant "to lack, be without" — the word began purely as deficiency, absence, and lack.
- [The noun want (14th–17th c.)] - The noun want, "lack, deficiency," hardened into "poverty, need" — a person in want is in want of necessities — while the participle wanting, "lacking, deficient," settled in as adjective and preposition.
- [The great reversal (early 18th c.)] - From "to feel the want of something" came the modern verb of desire: "I want" originally meant "I lack," and only later "I wish for" — the word's centre of gravity migrated from absence to appetite.
- [The fixed idiom "found wanting" (16th c. onwards)] - Meanwhile the set phrase "found wanting" — with the ring of a weighing, a trial, a verdict — fixed the ancient lack-sense in official, quasi-judicial English and kept it alive for centuries.
- [Modern register] - Because want now chiefly means "desire," "found wanting" feels like a deliberate archaism preserved in formal idiom: a shortfall discovered by scrutiny, insufficiency measured against a standard.
