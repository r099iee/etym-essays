- Old English (c. 10th c.) — scunian: expressing visceral dread, abhorrence, and physical recoil; used to describe shrinking away in horror from demons, mortal sins, or terrifying peril.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1400) — schonen / shonen: semantic shift from physical flight in horror to deliberate evasion and moral rejection; one begins to shun deceit, dangerous companions, or bad counsel.
- 16th–17th c. — Early Modern religious & social codified practice: used in biblical translations and Protestant moral tracts to denote separating oneself from idolaters; simultaneously institutionalized in Anabaptist communities (Mennonites, Amish) as Meidung (communal social ban).
- 18th–19th c. — Romantic & Victorian social ostracism: used to describe the absolute polite exclusion of individuals who had suffered moral disgrace, bankruptcy, or breached Victorian etiquette.
- 20th–21st c. — Psychological and contemporary institutional usage: applied to the silent treatment in interpersonal psychology, cultic shunning of apostates, and modern social cancellations where individuals or controversial ideas are systematically boycotted.
