LINEN is a noun (historically also an adjective) denoting cloth woven from the bast fibres of the flax plant, Linum usitatissimum — the oldest textile fibre in human record, found in the mummy wrappings of predynastic Egypt (c. 5000 BCE) and in the Neolithic lake dwellings of Switzerland. In the source context (“He is wrapped in yellow ___, and embalmed with spices.”) it denotes the burial cloth of an embalmed body — an usage as old as the Egyptian embalmers and familiar from scripture, where Joseph wraps the body of Jesus “in a clean linen cloth” (Matthew 27:59) and the rich man of Luke 16 is “clothed in fine linen and purple”.


Primary senses



  • The fabric: a smooth, lustrous, strong, breathable textile spun and woven from flax; cooler in summer and crisper than cotton, it wrinkles readily and launders to a hard, clean finish; used for shirts, dresses, towels, and fine household textiles. The word denotes both the yarn and the woven cloth, and by extension the cultivated crop of flax itself.

  • Household linens: by metonymy, the articles made of linen — bed linen (sheets, pillowcases), table linen (tablecloths, napkins, runners), and towels; from the 17th century this collective sense (“the linen”) became the standard domestic meaning, and compounds like linen cupboard, linen basket, and linen room are built on it.

  • Underclothing (archaic and regional): from the 16th century “linen” stood for body linen — shirts, shifts, and chemises, the garments worn next to the skin — giving the transferred sense “underwear” and the old trade of the linen-draper.


Secondary, technical, and idiomatic senses



  • Linen paper: fine writing paper, and later banknote paper, made from linen rags (19th century); in stationery and printing, “linen” also names an embossed cloth-textured finish (linen-look cards, linen-textured covers), and linen content in banknote paper remains a security feature today.

  • Linen (colour): the natural, unbleached pale-cream tint of flax cloth, a standard tone in interior design and fashion palettes (“linen white”).

  • The idiom: to wash one's dirty linen in public — to air private quarrels publicly — a calque of French laver son linge sale en famille (“to wash one's dirty linen in the family”), current in English from the early 19th century.

  • Symbolic and ritual linen: fine linen (Greek byssos, Hebrew šēš) is the ritual textile of priesthood and burial across the ancient Near East and in Christian tradition — altar cloths, church vestments, burial shrouds, and the Holy Shroud of Turin are linen; the traditional gift for the twelfth wedding anniversary is silk and fine linen.

  • Trade and industry: linen names a global fibre economy — the Irish linen industry (from the 17th century), the Flemish and French linens, and Holland, the fine unbleached linen originally from Holland — and the crop itself, flax grown for fibre.


Register and usage notes: linen carries a clean, slightly formal, “good-housekeeping” register; in hotels “the linen” is the housekeeping stock of sheets and towels; in fashion it implies summer luxury, breathability, and casual elegance. Grammatically the word is a fossilised adjective: Old English līnen meant “made of flax”, and the noun use absorbed the fabric, then the goods, then the garments — a chain of metonymy from fibre to wardrobe that the modern word preserves unconsciously.
