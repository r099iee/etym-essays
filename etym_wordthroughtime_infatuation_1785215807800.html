- 15th-16th century foolishness - the word's early life centres on being made foolish or deluded: to infatuate is to deprive of judgment, whether by love, pride, or bad counsel; the tone is pitying and contemptuous.
- 17th-18th century delusion and transport - an infatuation is a blinding passion that overrides reason, often with a hint of diabolic or divine delusion; it is judged as folly in moral and conduct literature.
- 19th century Victorian censure - used, as in Mill's biography, for a passion the world judges unreasoning and disproportionate; the disapproval of society is written into the word.
- Contemporary use - an everyday word for an intense but short-lived romantic crush; it is idealizing, fantasy-fed, and expected to fade, and is still carefully contrasted with love.
