- Old English and Norse, 9th–11th c. - wæs hāl / ves heill is a solemn drinking salutation, a verbal pledge of health exchanged over a cup; the mood is ceremonial and communal, not yet festive in the modern sense
- Middle English, c. 1200–1400 - the loan wassail names both the cry and the spiced ale that accompanies it; the word gathers a Christmas and midwinter association and begins to imply generous hospitality
- Tudor and Stuart, 16th–17th c. - the wassail bowl becomes an emblem of old English good cheer and Twelfth Night misrule; the word develops a robust, slightly rowdy, distinctly seasonal colour
- 18th century - polite sobriety and the retreat of the custom leave wassail sounding antiquarian and rural; it survives chiefly in cider country and in antiquarian writing
- 19th century - Romantic and Victorian medievalism revive it as heritage vocabulary; the carol "Here We Come A-wassailing" fixes the word in the Christmas soundscape, and the tone becomes nostalgic and picturesque
- 20th century - wassail is archaic outside December and is often used jocularly or self-consciously for any warming drink, as in the smoking-room quip on this card
- Contemporary - orchard wassailing festivals, craft cider branding and pagan revival give the word a new connotation of authenticity, community and seasonal resistance to commercial Christmas; it now signals warm, handmade, pre-modern cheer
