- 14th to 15th Century: Borrowed into Middle English from Old French and Medieval Latin, initially signifying "pertaining to teaching, instruction, or edification" in general educational and moral contexts.
- 16th to 17th Century: Heavily focused and intensified by the Protestant Reformation and Catholic Counter-Reformation, narrowing to mean specifically "pertaining to religious dogma, church creeds, and theological articles of faith."
- 18th to 19th Century: Broadened beyond theology into political theory, legal jurisprudence, and military science, describing established strategic principles and codified legal doctrines (e.g., the Monroe Doctrine).
- 20th to 21st Century: Applied widely to artistic manifestos (such as theatrical methods and modernist architecture), corporate management theories, and political orthodoxy, frequently carrying a nuanced secondary implication of rigid, doctrinaire inflexibility when contrasted with practical adaptability.
