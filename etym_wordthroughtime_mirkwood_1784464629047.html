“Mirk” (or “murk”) entered English from northern stock meaning dark, gloomy, or obscured — a word of shadow and murkiness with no friendly colour at all.
“Mirk” attached itself to “wood” to name forests of darkness: Mirkwood is transparently “the dark wood,” a place-name built from living English elements and read at once as a warning label.
In the 19th–20th centuries the name Mirkwood was adopted by fantasy as a proper toponym for a shadowed, perilous forest — Tolkien made it the famous dark wood north of the river, where gloom is baked into the very name.
“Mirkwood” today is recognized as a fantasy place-name meaning literally “dark forest,” its archaic-northern ring carrying the dread that the plain phrase “dark forest” no longer does — the name itself is the threat.
