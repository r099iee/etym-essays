The word COUNSELLOR preserves within its spelling the memory of an ancient civic ritual: the summons. Behind counsel, council, and counsellor lies the Indo-European root *kel(h₁)-, “to call, to cry out, to summon,” which in Latin produced calāre, the verb of proclaiming, of summoning, and of calling an assembly together. When the Romans joined com- (“together”) to calāre they formed concilium, the assembly of citizens called together out of their houses to deliberate on public business; from this calling-together came consilium, the deliberation itself and the body that undertakes it. Scholars debate whether consilium is derived directly from concilium or from the related verb consulere, “to consult,” but both words cluster around the same image of a summons, and both belong to the public world of the Roman forum, where to be summoned was to be entrusted.

The medieval court. The medieval stage carried the word from Rome to the courts of Europe. In Old French, consiliārius hardened into conseiller, which was at once a verb, “to advise,” and a noun, “an adviser”; the older Latin sense of one summoned to deliberate was preserved in the feudal office of the king's counsellor, a fixed role in royal and noble government. Anglo-Norman brought conseiller across the Channel in the thirteenth century, and Middle English developed the spellings counseiler and counsellour, the doubled -l- marking the Romance double consonant that British spelling keeps to this day. In medieval English literature the counsellor is the wise figure who sits beside the ruler — the king's counsellors, the noble's counsellors — and the word's dignity was bound up with the act of deliberation itself, the giving of counsel in a formal gathering.

Modern professionalization. Modern English has widened and professionalized the word. With the growth of the welfare state and the helping professions, counsellor moved from the royal adviser to a host of specialized figures: the counsellor-at-law who pleads before the courts, the school counsellor, the marriage counsellor, the careers counsellor, the grief counsellor. In each case the word retains its root idea — one who stands within the deliberation and helps form the decision — while losing the courtly grandeur of the royal adviser. The American spelling counselor (one -l- before the suffix) runs parallel to the British double -ll-, a small orthographic fork in the same historical stream.

Mill and the intimate counsellor. It is the older, fuller sense of the word that Mill invokes in the Autobiography. His wife is the “almost infallible counsellor” — not a professional adviser but the companion who shared in every deliberation of the philosopher's mind, the one whose counsel he took as law. The word's courtly dignity and its image of shared judgment make it the perfect vehicle for Mill's portrait: the counsellor is summoned, trusted, and obeyed, yet remains human and intimate, a figure of the domestic circle rather than of the council chamber.

  • Sound and form. The English word descends through conseiller with the Anglo-Norman loss of the medial vowel and the retention of the double -ll- in British spelling; the American counselor regularizes the consonant.
  • Register drift. From a public office of state the word became a private relationship of trust, and finally a professional title of the helping professions — a steady widening from the court to the clinic.
  • Literary attestations. The counsellor appears throughout the medieval romances and chronicles, in Shakespeare's royal courts, in the political writings of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and in Victorian biography — culminating in the Mill context of this card.

The word thus carries within it the whole history of counsel: the Roman crier summoning the people, the feudal lord's trusted adviser, the monarch's privy counsellor, and the modern professional who sits beside the student, the couple, or the bereaved — and, in Mill, the beloved wife whose judgment stood beside the philosopher in every decision of his life.
