Tocharian is a modern scholarly name for an extinct branch of Indo-European, and its history is unusual because the word was not inherited from the people who spoke the language. It was coined by philologists and then attached, controversially, to an ancient ethnonym. The story therefore belongs as much to the history of scholarship as to the history of the language.

1. Discovery of the manuscripts

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, expeditions to the Tarim Basin in Chinese Central Asia — led by figures such as Aurel Stein, Albert Grünwedel, and Albert von Le Coq — recovered large numbers of manuscripts in an unknown language written in a north-Indian Brahmi script. In 1908 the German scholars Emil Sieg and Wilhelm Siegling published the decipherment and named the language Tocharisch. It soon became clear that there were two related languages, now called Tocharian A and Tocharian B, with B better attested and including secular documents.

2. The name and its ancient anchor

The name was borrowed from Greek Τοχάροι (Tokharoi), a people mentioned by Strabo and Ptolemy in Bactria, and Latin Tochari. The identification was attractive because it gave the new language a classical pedigree, but it is problematic: the manuscripts come from the Tarim Basin (Kucha, Turfan, and nearby sites), not from Bactria, and the speakers may have called themselves something else entirely. Scholars have therefore proposed alternatives such as Agnean and Kuchean for the individual languages, while retaining “Tocharian” as the branch name. The debate is a classic case of a label outrunning its evidence.

3. Linguistic significance

Tocharian is important precisely because it is unexpected. It shows centum-type treatment of the Proto-Indo-European palatovelars, although it is geographically surrounded by satem languages such as Indo-Iranian. This “Tocharian problem” complicated simple models of Indo-European dispersal and forced linguists to think about areal contact and parallel innovation. The branch also preserves archaic nominal morphology, including a dual, and a rich verbal system, while its phonology shows heavy reduction and palatalization. Its vocabulary includes many Buddhist loanwords from Sanskrit and Prakrit, plus Iranian and Chinese borrowings.

4. Sound changes and script

Tocharian merged the inherited voiced aspirates with voiced stops, reduced many unstressed vowels, and developed new palatal series. The writing system is not native: the scribes adapted Brahmi, so the spelling reflects Indian phonological habits rather than a Tocharian orthographic tradition. This complicates reconstruction, because the script does not mark all distinctions clearly. The languages died out by roughly the ninth century, displaced by Turkic and later Mongolian speech and by the decline of the oasis monastic economy.

5. Semantic and cultural expansion

  • Philology: “Tocharian” becomes a standard branch label in comparative Indo-European grammars and etymological dictionaries.
  • Archaeology: the term extends to the manuscripts, murals, and monastic sites of the region.
  • Genetics and migration debates: “Tocharian” is invoked in discussions of the Tarim mummies and Bronze Age movements, though linking genes to language is contested.
  • Public culture: Silk Road exhibitions and popular history use the word as a emblem of lost Central Asian cosmopolitanism.

6. Literary and scholarly attestations

The primary “literature” is Buddhist: sutras, monastic rules, and devotional verse translated or adapted from Indian models, alongside pragmatic documents such as caravan permits and medical recipes. In modern scholarship the word appears in the great comparative grammars and in specialist journals, where Tocharian is now inseparable from the wider story of Indo-European and the Silk Road.
