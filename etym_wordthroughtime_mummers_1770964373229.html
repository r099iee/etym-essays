- Middle English (from Old French "momer", to mask or disguise): "mummers" were masked, often silent performers of folk plays, chiefly at midwinter and Christmas — a grassroots, communal, ritual entertainment.
- 16th–17th c.: the practice of "mumming" becomes associated with festive disorder and was even regulated or frowned upon by authorities; the word carries a plebeian, unruly, carnivalesque colour.
- 18th–19th c.: with the antiquarian revival, "mummers" are romanticized as rustic folk tradition — the source of the Christmas pantomime and the seasonal Mummers' Play.
- Contemporary: distinctly archaic and historical; the word survives mainly in folk-lore scholarship and the Philadelphia Mummers Parade, while ordinary speech would say "masked performers" or "street actors".
