1. Indo-European foundations of strength and dominion. The deep etymological roots of valor extend back to the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂welh₁- (or *wal- in older reconstructions), signifying "to be strong, to possess dominion, to rule, or to prevail." This ancient root generated a widespread family of words across Indo-European daughter languages connected to sovereign authority, physical potency, and dynamic efficacy. In the Germanic branch, it produced Proto-Germanic *waldaną ("to rule, govern"), yielding Old English wealdan (source of modern English wield), German walten ("to rule"), and the second element in compound names like Oswald and Ronald. In the Slavic languages, it gave rise to Old Church Slavonic vlasti ("power, authority") and modern Russian vlast' (власть, "power, regime"). In the Baltic tongues, it emerged in Lithuanian valdyti ("to rule, manage"), while in Celtic it appeared in Old Irish flaith ("lordship, sovereignty").

2. The Italic branch: from bodily vigor to abstract worth. In the Italic branch, the root developed into Proto-Italic *walēō, which surfaced in Classical Latin as the central stative verb valēre, meaning "to be physically strong, vigorous, healthy, capable, or of sound bodily constitution." The Romans used valēre extensively in everyday life: the imperative form valē ("be strong, remain healthy!") was the standard parting formula in correspondence and conversation, while legal formulas employed valēre to denote that a contract or law "has validity or binding force."

  • The creation of Late Latin valor: In Classical Latin, abstract courage was expressed by fortitudo, virtus (literally "manliness," from vir), or animus. The nominal derivative valor did not appear in the golden or silver ages of Latin literature; rather, it was coined in Late Latin (4th–6th centuries CE) as an abstract third-declension noun built from the verbal stem val- with the suffix -or. Initially, Late Latin valor denoted "value, intrinsic worth, price, or legal validity," directly mirroring the financial and commercial vocabulary of late imperial administration.
  • Semantic crystallization in Medieval Romance and feudal chivalry: Following the collapse of the Western Roman Empire, Late Latin valor passed into Gallo-Romance and emerged in Old French (11th–12th centuries) as valour or valor. In the aristocratic crucible of high medieval feudalism and chivalric literature (such as the Chanson de Roland and Arthurian romances), the concept underwent an extraordinary semantic transformation. To possess "value" or "worth" as a medieval knight was not measured in coin, but in military prowess, aristocratic honor, loyalty to one's liege lord, and fearless conduct in hand-to-hand combat. Thus, Old French valour crystallized into the supreme chivalric virtue of martial courage.

3. Middle English transmission and the chivalric ideal. Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman French became the prestige language of the English court, military aristocracy, and legal system. The word entered Middle English around 1300 as valour, first attested in texts such as the monumental verse history Cursor Mundi. In Middle English, valour initially retained a dual significance: it was applied both to moral and spiritual excellence ("a man of gret valour") and to ferocious battlefield bravery ("doughty deeds of chivalric valour"). Writers like Geoffrey Chaucer and Sir Thomas Malory in Le Morte d'Arthur deployed valour alongside prowess and courtesy to define the complete character of an exemplary knight.

4. Early Modern English and the orthographic divergence. During the Renaissance and Early Modern English period (16th–17th centuries), the word maintained its high poetic and dramatic prestige. In the plays of William Shakespeare, valor is repeatedly invoked to distinguish true, disciplined martial virtue from reckless bravado or mere bloodlust (as when Falstaff in Henry IV famously opines that "the better part of valour is discretion").

  • The Great Spelling Split: In the 18th and 19th centuries, the orthography of the word became an emblem of national linguistic identity. While British English standardized the Anglo-Norman spelling with the diphthongal ending -our (valour, defended by Samuel Johnson in his 1755 Dictionary to honor French transmission), American lexicographer Noah Webster deliberately eliminated the superfluous "u" in his 1828 American Dictionary of the English Language, restoring the classical Latin spelling valor.
  • Institutionalization in military awards: In the 19th century, both Britain and the United States codified the term into their highest military honors. In 1856, Queen Victoria instituted the Victoria Cross, cast from the bronze of captured Crimean War cannons and bearing the sparse, iconic inscription "FOR VALOUR." In the United States, the Medal of Honor (established in 1861 during the Civil War) was created to reward soldiers who distinguished themselves by "conspicuous gallantry and intrepidity at the risk of life above and beyond the call of duty," formally termed acts of military valor.

5. Modern psychological and civic extensions. In the 20th and 21st centuries, while valor remains an indispensable fixture of military citations, it has also expanded into civic and moral discourse. Lexicographers observe that modern usage frequently distinguishes between physical valor (bravery in the face of bodily injury or death) and moral valor (the courage to withstand social ostracism, institutional retaliation, or political ruin to defend truth and ethical integrity). Today, valor stands at the pinnacle of bravery vocabulary, denoting an exalted, conscious transcendence of mortal fear in dedication to a transcendent cause.
