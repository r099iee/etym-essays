- 13th c. - loft enters English from Old Norse lopt, the word for air and for the upper room; English immediately puts it to work - hayloft, organ loft, sleeping loft - the enclosed air-chamber under the roof.
- 14th c. - the adjective lofty is coined: first "high in position", then "elevated in mind or spirit"; public perception splits, the noun sinking toward the dusty store-chamber while the adjective climbs to moral grandeur.
- 17th–19th c. - loft is the plain, functional word of farm, church, and workshop; a space below the dignity of "hall" or "room", associated with beams, hay, and disuse.
- 20th c. - the great inversion: urban artists move into abandoned industrial upper storeys and make them airy live-work studios; loft shifts from cramped and humble to fashionable and sought-after.
- 21st c. - "loft apartment" and "loft-style" become real-estate prestige terms; the word Peake used to demote a grand hall now advertises luxury - a total reversal of its earlier register.
