- [Late Middle English, imitative emergence] - Appears as 'whir' / 'whurr', an onomatopoeic word imitating the sound of rapid whirling or vibration — a spinning wheel, a bird's wings; purely descriptive.
- [16th–18th c., busy applications] - Applies to spinning wheels, mills, and flapping wings; the spelling 'whirr' settles in as the emphatic form of something revolving fast, and the word stays a neutral sound of activity.
- [19th c., the machine age] - As the industrial age fills rooms and streets with spinning, revolving things, 'whirr' becomes the signature word for machine-voices — clocks, looms, engines; it gains a busy, faintly relentless, sometimes menacing character.
- [20th c.–present, intimate vitality] - Now often describes small quick things — bat or hummingbird wings, fans, disks; a brief vibration that punctures silence rather than filling it. 'Whir' is the common spelling today; 'whirr' keeps a more emphatic, atmospheric ring.
