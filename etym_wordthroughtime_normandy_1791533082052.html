- 9th c. — Latin and Frankish chroniclers write of Northmannia, the raided lands of the lower Seine. The name is descriptive and threatening: it means the country of the Vikings, not a settled province.
- 911–1000 — After Rollo’s treaty the name attaches to a recognised territory and its ruling family. It begins to sound like a legitimate principality rather than a raid zone.
- 11th c. — The duchy is one of the best-administered states in Europe. “Normandy” now suggests knights, castles, Romanesque churches and a formidable duke.
- 1066–1204 — The name becomes half-English: the Norman dynasty rules England, and “Normandy” denotes the continental heartland of a cross-Channel empire.
- 1204–1450 — After the French conquest the name is a contested borderland; during the Hundred Years’ War it is occupied, liberated and fought over, and English kings still style themselves dukes of Normandy.
- 16th–18th c. — A prosperous provincial name: Rouen’s textile trade, the Norman parlements, the Grand Siècle writers. Abroad it is vaguely associated with seafaring and law.
- 19th c. — Romantic and Impressionist tourism turns Normandy into a landscape of cliffs, apple blossom and ruins; the name acquires a picturesque, slightly nostalgic colour.
- 1944 — The landings make “Normandy” a global synonym for liberation, sacrifice and the beginning of the end of the war in the west.
- 2016–present — The administrative merger revives the single name, while “the Normandy format” extends it into diplomacy. The word now carries simultaneously medieval, culinary, tourist and military connotations.
