• birds: Similar because finches are birds; differs because bird names the whole class of feathered vertebrates, whereas finch names one family with stout seed-cracking bills — narrow, precise, and diagnostic.
  • sparrows: Similar because they are small, brown, seed-eating birds of hedgerow and town; differs because they belong to another family (the Passeridae), take grit and insects far more readily, and lack the conical seed-specialist bill of the true finch.
  • buntings: Similar because they are small seed-eating songbirds once lumped with finches; differs because taxonomy has placed them in their own family (Emberizidae) — the classical case of the finch boundary being redrawn by science.
  • canaries: Similar because the canary is itself a finch (a domesticated Serinus); differs because “canary” names one specific species and its breeds, famous as a cage bird, and is a proper name in its own right.
  • linnets: Similar because the linnet is a cardueline finch; differs because its name (from flax, lin) records its taste for flaxseed and pins one species, where “finches” is the umbrella term.
  • siskins and redpolls: Similar because both are small cardueline finches; differs because they are genus- and species-specific names of the northern finch clan, with specialised tastes for alder and birch seed.
  • crossbills: Similar because they are finches with remarkable beaks; differs because their crossed mandibles are adapted to pry conifer seeds — the extreme of finch bill specialisation and a name that advertises the beak itself.
  • goldfinches / greenfinches / bullfinches / chaffinches: Similar because each is a species of true finch; differs because each name pins one identifiable bird with its own plumage, song, and habits — “finches” is the collective that holds them all.
  • songbirds (passerines): Similar because finches are passerines; differs because the passerines are the vast order of perching birds — finches are a single family inside it, so the word loses all finch-specificity.
  • seed-eaters: Similar because finches live chiefly on seeds; differs because it is an ecological description, not a common or taxonomic name, and covers finches, sparrows, doves, and many rodents alike.
