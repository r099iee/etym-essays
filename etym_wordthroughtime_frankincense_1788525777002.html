- [12th–13th c.] - Old French franc encens enters Anglo-Norman and Middle English as frankencens, denoting the supreme, unadulterated aromatic gum resin of Boswellia - [connotation: sacred, precious, aristocratic; distinct from cheap, adulterated domestic resins]
- [14th–16th c.] - Middle and Early Modern English establish frankincense as a fixed ecclesiastical and biblical noun in English scripture (Wycliffe, Tyndale, Great Bible, King James Version) - [connotation: divine tribute, holiness, one of the three Magi's gifts to the Christ child]
- [17th–18th c.] - Expanding global maritime trade and natural philosophy classify the resin pharmacologically as olibanum while retaining frankincense in high poetic diction - [connotation: exotic Arabian luxury, oriental splendor, medicinal balm]
- [19th c.] - Romantic and Victorian literature (Keats, Wilde, Tennyson) adopts frankincense as a sensory metaphor for transcendent beauty, perfumed atmospheres, and pure consecrated love - [connotation: aesthetic intoxication, decadent elegance, celestial sweetness]
- [20th–21st c.] - Contemporary usage encompasses luxury niche perfumery, holistic aromatherapy, and biomedical research on anti-inflammatory boswellic acids - [connotation: sophisticated fragrance note, mindful meditation, natural therapeutic remedy]
