The history of jungle-up is a story of two separate ancestral lines — one from the wild lands of India, one from the oldest Indo-European spatial particles — joining in the improvised camps of American hobos. Because the word is a compound, its evolution is told here in two complete essays, one for each component.

PART I — THE EVOLUTION OF "JUNGLE"

The component jungle begins far outside the Indo-European world, in the Sanskrit noun jaṅgala. In its earliest attested sense this word did not mean lush forest at all; it named arid, dry, uncultivated ground — a waterless waste, a desert or a barren tract. The deeper origin of jaṅgala is itself uncertain and is widely suspected to be a borrowing into Indo-Aryan from a non-Indo-European substrate language of ancient India (perhaps Dravidian), so the word is one of those terms that stands outside the regular Indo-European genealogies. What matters for our word is the dramatic semantic reversal that followed: as the Indian landscape was described from the cultivated lowlands outward, the old word for "dry waste" came to be applied to wild, unmanaged land covered with dense thorn and forest growth — the tangled "wilderness" that lay beyond the ploughed fields. In Hindi and Urdu the form jaṅgal took on exactly this meaning of "forest, wild wood, uncultivated overgrown country."

English met the word through the East India Company. British colonial officers and travellers in the late eighteenth century adopted jungle to describe the impenetrable tropical vegetation of Bengal and central India, and the word entered English around 1776, appearing in travel accounts, reports, and dictionaries of Indian vocabulary. From the start it carried the double meaning of wild vegetation and wild, lawless, unpoliced territory. During the nineteenth century the noun exploded into figurative use — "the law of the jungle" (a phrase borrowed from Rudyard Kipling's 1894 The Jungle Book) named brutal, amoral competition, and "the jungle" came to stand for any place governed by raw struggle rather than rule. By the twentieth century the word had produced a small family of compounds — concrete jungle, asphalt jungle, blackboard jungle — in which "jungle" means an environment as untamed and dangerous as a forest.

It was this secondary colouring — wild, untended, lawless land — that American hobos seized upon. From the 1870s onward, the itinerant labourers and railroad riders of the United States gathered in brushy, overgrown spots beside the tracks and rivers: out-of-the-way places where a man could light a fire, cook a meal, and sleep without being moved on. They called these rough encampments "jungles," and the "hobo jungle" became a fixture of the tramp's world — a communal fire, a shared pot of stew, a system of informal hospitality and signalling. The word kept its wildness: a hobo jungle was a lawless, unpoliced corner of the landscape, exactly the sense of "jungle" the colonists had borrowed from Hindi a century before. From the noun "jungle" the tramps built the verb "to jungle-up."

PART II — THE EVOLUTION OF "UP"

The second component, up, descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *upo, "under, up from under, from below upward." This tiny spatial root was extraordinarily widespread: it appears as Sanskrit úpa, Greek hypó, and Latin sub, all meaning "under, beneath, up from beneath." In the Germanic branch the root produced the particle *upp, "up, upward," which entered Old English as up or upp and is attested from the earliest English texts. In Old Norse the same form is upp, and Old High German gave ūf, the ancestor of modern German auf.

What makes English up special is its grammatical destiny. Unlike most other Indo-European languages, English evolved a vast system of phrasal verbs in which a simple verb is combined with a particle to make a new meaning — fix up, build up, rig up, light up, set up. In these constructions up does double duty: it keeps some sense of upward movement, but it also acts as an aspectual marker indicating that the action is completed, gathered together, or properly assembled — "set up" means fully assembled, "fix up" means thoroughly repaired, "rig up" means improvised into working order. This is the exact function in "jungle-up": the tramps did not merely camp; they gathered together the materials of a night's rest — fire, water, shelter, food — and "jungled it up," assembling a camp out of whatever the landscape offered. The formation of the phrasal verb is transparently English, built on the noun "jungle" and the aspectual particle "up" in a pattern that is as old as the language itself.

THE JOINING — "JUNGLE-UP" IN AMERICAN CULTURE

The compound jungle-up appears in American hobo and tramp slang in the early twentieth century, in the years when the railroad was the road of the unemployed and the dispossessed. To "jungle-up" was to perform the full ritual of the hobo camp: to leave the highway, find water, gather wood, build a fire, cook, and sleep in the open. The word appears in the tramp journalism, socialist labour writing, and folk material of the period, and it was given enduring literary life in John Steinbeck's fiction of the Depression — in Of Mice and Men (1937) the tramps "come wearily down from the highway in the evening to jungle-up near water." The verb captures the double attitude of the era: the economic shame and hardship of sleeping rough, and the quiet dignity and communal solidarity of the men who shared their fires.

As hobo culture faded with the passing of the rail-riding era, "jungle-up" itself became period slang — archaic, nostalgic, and faintly romantic. It survives in historical writing about tramp life, in the occasional reenactment or bushcraft revival, and as a literary marker of Depression-era America. Its two component lines — the Sanskrit-derived wilderness of "jungle" and the Germanic spatial particle "up" — met in a single vivid image of improvised outdoor shelter that still carries the whole history: a wild, lawless corner of the land, and the act of gathering oneself together for a night in the open.

SOUND AND FORM CHANGES

  • jaṅgala (Sanskrit) —> jaṅgal (Hindi) —> jungle (English): the syllable-final nasal cluster ṅg is retained, while the Indian long final vowel is lost in the Hindi form and the English spelling settled on -le, a reanalysis of the cluster.
  • *upo (PIE) —> *upp (Proto-Germanic) —> up (Old English): a shortening and loss of the final vowel, producing the stable one-syllable English particle.
  • The compound is written with a hyphen, jungle-up, reflecting its phrasal-verb origin; in prose it may appear unhyphenated.

SOCIO-CULTURAL TRAJECTORY

  • Ancient India: jaṅgala names dry, arid, uncultivated land — land beyond cultivation, and therefore wild and lawless.
  • Hindi/Urdu: the sense shifts to dense forest and overgrown wilderness, the untamed country beyond the fields.
  • Late 18th c. English: borrowed through colonial India for impenetrable tropical vegetation; quickly gains the figurative sense of lawless, dangerous territory.
  • 19th–20th c. America: hobos apply "jungle" to their rough, wild encampments by the tracks, and build the verb "jungle-up" on the old aspectual pattern of English phrasal verbs.
  • Contemporary: "jungle-up" is historical slang, nostalgic and literary, evoking the Depression-era road; its component "jungle" meanwhile lives on in vigorous figurative compounds.
