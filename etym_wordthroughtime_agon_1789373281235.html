- Classical Antiquity (5th–4th c. BCE) - Flourishes in ancient Athens as *ἀγών* (agṓn), denoting the sacred public assembly, athletic games at Olympia, and the climactic formal poetic debate between protagonist and antagonist in Attic drama.
- Hellenistic to Roman Era - Shifts gradually through Greek philosophy, rhetoric, and early Christian epistles (e.g., Saint Paul's "I have fought the good fight" / *tòn kalòn agōna ēgṓnismai*), evolving from civic athletic games into an interior metaphor for spiritual combat against sin.
- 19th Century - Revived in Western scholarship and philology (notably by Jacob Burckhardt in *The Greeks and Greek Civilization* and Friedrich Nietzsche in *Homer's Contest*, 1872) as a diagnostic concept defining the unique, agonistic spirit of classical civilization.
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Adopted into English literary criticism (Harold Bloom's *The Anxiety of Influence*) and narrative theory to describe the agonizing poetic drama of artistic creation, revision, and precursor rivalry.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Formalized in political philosophy (Chantal Mouffe's *The Democratic Paradox*) as "agonistic pluralism," redefining healthy democratic politics as perpetual, legitimate contestation between respectful adversaries rather than lethal warfare.
