Ornithology is a word with two separable histories: the Greek word-material it is built from (the bird-word ὄρνις and the speech-logic element λόγος) and the modern European science whose needs reshaped that material in the 17th century. Each root has its own Indo-European biography; together they produced one of the most transparently technical words in the scientific vocabulary — "bird" + "account of", literally bird-account.

1. The bird-root: PIE *h₂ér- and Greek ὄρνις. The Indo-European root *h₂ér- designated the large bird of prey, the eagle. In Greek it formed ὄρνις ("bird", but in Attic usage often specifically "hen, fowl"), already attested in Homer (8th century BCE) with its full inflectional stem ὀρνιθ- (genitive ὄρνιθος); the same root lives on in Hittite ḫaran- ("eagle"), in the Germanic element of English earn (the old word for eagle, preserved in names like Erne) and German Aar, and in Armenian oror ("seagull"). Greek ὄρνις was a central bird-word of antiquity: it recurs throughout Homer, the tragedians, and the historians, and above all in Aristotle's zoological books, whose History of Animals establishes the first systematic record of avian anatomy and behaviour — including his remarkable (if imperfect) observations of the cuckoo's brood parasitism and the seasonal habits of migrant birds. For the Greeks, however, birds were above all carriers of signs: the flight and cries of the ὄρνις were read by seers as omens, and the bird-word is from the beginning entangled with divination.

2. The divinatory Greek compound: ὀρνιθολογία as augury. Before any science, Greek had the compound ὀρνιθολογία meaning "bird-divination" — the reading of omens from the flight, cries, and feeding behaviour of birds. Greek and Roman public life institutionalised this: the ὀρνιθολόγος ("bird-reader") and the Latin augur interpreted the "auspices" (from avis, "bird"), deciding whether assemblies, wars, and marriages should proceed. Aristophanes' comedy The Birds (414 BCE) satirises the whole apparatus, staging a city of birds that sells oracles from its own shrine and burlesques the bird-seers who flock to it. This ancient meaning — divination, not science — is the semantic ancestor that the modern word would later invert, and the inversion is the single most important event in the word's history.

3. The speech-root: PIE *leǵ-, Greek λόγος and -λογία. The second element, -λογία, descends from the PIE root *leǵ- ("to gather, collect"), whose Greek verb λέγω (légein) moved from "collecting" to "counting" to "recounting, saying" — the same semantic path by which "to gather words" became "to speak". With it, Greek built λόγος ("word, speech, account, reason" — the "logos" of the philosophers and the New Testament) and the productive suffix -λογία: words for the giving of a reasoned account, such as φιλολογία, θεολογία, and ἀστρολογία ("star-accounting"). The same suffix powered the Hellenistic and Roman expansion of learning and was carried into Latin and then into the vernacular scientific languages of early modern Europe, where "-logy" became the universal badge of a discipline.

4. The modern re-coining: 17th-century Europe. The decisive event in the word's life was the Scientific Revolution's remaking of the -logy suffix. Between 1650 and 1750 European naturalists, writing in the international language of Latin, systematised nature into -logia and -graphia disciplines: ornithologia appears in Modern Latin works of the 1650s–70s; English "ornithology" is attested by the 1670s (the adjective "ornithological" from 1656), and "ornithologist" follows in the early 18th century. These coinings were self-consciously Greek: the naturalists chose ὄρνις + λόγος precisely to dignify bird-study beside the revived learning of Aristotle and Pliny. Francis Willughby, whose Ornithologia (published 1676 by his executor and collaborator John Ray) is the founding work of scientific ornithology, and Ray himself, who completed and published it, professionalised the old divinatory word into a discipline of description, classification, and anatomy — the bridge from omen-reading to observation crossed in a single generation.

5. The career of the science (18th–21st centuries). In the 18th century ornithology rode the Linnaean revolution: Linnaeus' Systema Naturae (10th edition, 1758) gave every bird a binomial name, and Buffon's Histoire naturelle des oiseaux (1770–86) made the field popular reading. The 19th century added world exploration, the collections of the great voyages, and Darwin: the Galápagos finches that John Gould identified from Beagle specimens, Darwin's own breeding of domestic pigeons (the birds he kept for his great book), and the splendour of Gould's bird folios — including his duck monograph of the 1840s — made ornithology a Victorian spectacle. Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) leaned heavily on bird evidence, and the 19th-century founding of the British Ornithologists' Union (1858) and the American Ornithologists' Union (1883) institutionalised the word. In the 20th century ornithology branched into behaviour (the ethology of Niko Tinbergen's gulls and sticklebacks, part of the 1973 Nobel Prize), migration science (radar, banding, satellite telemetry), and ecology; in the 21st it is arguably the most citizen-facing of the field sciences, with eBird gathering well over a hundred million new observations a year and the discipline's centres shifting toward conservation — the science of saving the birds themselves.

6. Summa. "Ornithology" thus fuses an eagle-root old as Homer, a speech-root old as the Greek pre-Socratics, a long aviary detour through the augur's reading of flight, and a 17th-century European decision to make bird-study a science in the classical mould. The modern word is a bridge built in one direction: from divination to data. Every ornithologist carries, in the name of the discipline, the ghost of the augur — and the eagle.
