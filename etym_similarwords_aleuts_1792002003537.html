• Inuit: Similar because both denote Indigenous Arctic and subarctic peoples belonging to the broader Eskimo-Aleut linguistic macro-family; differs because Inuit refers to the circumpolar peoples of northern Alaska, Canada, and Greenland who speak Inuit-Inupiaq dialects and traditionally adapted to sea-ice hunting and dog-sledding, whereas Aleuts inhabit a temperate-maritime, subarctic volcanic archipelago free of continuous pack ice and specialized exclusively in open-water kayak hunting.
  • Yupik: Similar because both represent Indigenous maritime populations of southwestern Alaska and the Beringian coast; differs because Yupik constitutes the western branch of the Eskimo family whose languages and ceremonial winter mask-dances differ markedly from the separate Unangan linguistic lineage of the Aleuts.
  • Alutiiq: Similar because the term is the Indigenous self-adaptation of the Russian word Aleut and historically caused severe ethnological confusion; differs because Alutiiq (or Sugpiaq) linguistically belongs to the Pacific Yupik branch native to Kodiak Island and Prince William Sound, whereas Aleuts (Unangan) form an entirely distinct language branch native to the Aleutian Island chain.
  • Chukchi: Similar because both are Indigenous maritime and coastal peoples of the North Pacific and Bering Sea rim; differs because the Chukchi are Siberian Paleo-Asiatic peoples native to the Chukotka Peninsula speaking a Chukotko-Kamchatkan tongue, split culturally between inland reindeer pastoralists and maritime walrus hunters, with no linguistic relationship to the Eskimo-Aleut Aleuts.
  • Koryak: Similar because both inhabit subarctic Pacific coastlines and contributed to early Russian fur-trade trade lexicons; differs because the Koryak are a Kamchatkan people belonging to the Chukotko-Kamchatkan linguistic family whose historical interactions with Russian explorers provided the probable linguistic source-word from which the Russian ethnonym Aleut was originally borrowed.
  • Inupiat: Similar because both represent Indigenous Alaskan native peoples with profound maritime hunting traditions; differs because the Inupiat inhabit the Arctic North Slope and northwestern Alaska, speaking an Inuit dialect and hunting bowhead whales from large open umiaks, whereas Aleuts occupied the subarctic maritime chain of the Aleutians.
  • Kamchadals: Similar because both were early targets of Russian fur-trade colonization along the North Pacific rim during the eighteenth century; differs because Kamchadals (Itelmens) are native to the Kamchatka Peninsula and speak an Itelmen language, culturally distinct from the maritime Unangan Aleuts.
