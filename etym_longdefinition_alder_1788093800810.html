ALDER is the common name of the tree genus Alnus (family Betulaceae, the birch family): roughly thirty to forty species of deciduous trees and shrubs that grow chiefly in damp ground — riverbanks, floodplains, fens and lake margins — across the temperate northern hemisphere, with outposts in the Andes and Central America. The word names a genus, a wood and a whole riverside ecology; it also names a handful of unrelated plants and objects that have inherited the name by association.

1. The tree proper.

  • Morphology: alders are small-to-medium trees (some are shrubs) with rounded to notched leaves — the European common alder, Alnus glutinosa, has famously sticky, notched leaves — separate male and female catkins on the same tree (male catkins long and dangling; female catkins developing into small, woody, cone-like fruits called strobiles), and smooth, greyish bark that is often cracked into square corky plates.
  • Habitat: wet and riparian sites; the English 'alder carr' is a fen woodland of alders on waterlogged peat. Alders tolerate flooding and oxygen-poor soils better than almost any broadleaf tree, which is why they dominate river corridors.
  • Ecology — the nitrogen factory: alder roots form nodules housing the actinomycete Frankia alni, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen into plant-usable ammonia; fallen alder leaves and roots thereby fertilize the surrounding soil and water, making the tree a natural pioneer that enriches land for later species. This twentieth-century discovery turned the alder from a 'common' tree into an ecological hero of wetland restoration and riverbank regeneration.

2. The wood and its uses.

  • Under water: alder wood is pale when freshly cut but quickly reddens to a salmon-orange; it is soft, light and easy to work, yet remarkably durable when permanently waterlogged — hence its classic role as piling wood. Tradition and engineering history alike report countless piles of alder (with elm and oak) underpinning buildings in waterlogged cities, most famously Venice.
  • On land: light, easily worked timber for clogs (standard in France and the Low Countries, and in English clog-making), broom handles, bowls, butts and boxes; it has even served for the bodies of electric guitars — Fender famously used alder for Stratocaster bodies from 1956 onward.
  • In fire, smoke and dye: alder charcoal was among the charcoals favoured for gunpowder; alder woodchips smoke fish and charcuterie slowly and sweetly; the bark yields red and yellow-brown dyes, is rich in tannin, and had a historic role in dyeing, leather-working and folk medicine (astringent gargles, wound washes).

3. Name-borrowings and near-homonyms.

  • Alder buckthorn (Frangula alnus) is a shrub of a different family that shares the epithet.
  • Black alder is applied in America to the winterberry holly (Ilex verticillata), and in Europe occasionally to Alnus glutinosa itself.
  • Alder fly is an angler's term for the insect Sialis lutaria, whose larvae live in mud, and for a fishing lure named after it.
  • Elder (the berry shrub, Sambucus) is not an alder, yet the two words are constantly confused: both developed an excrescent -d-, and both could appear as eller/aller in medieval spellings — a folk-etymology tangle that lexicographers still flag.
  • In names and places: Alder is a surname; English toponymy preserves the tree in Aldershot (usually explained as Old English alor + scēot, 'alder corner of land') and Alderley ('alder wood').

4. Folklore and literature. In medieval Irish tree-lore the ogam letter fearn is glossed 'alder,' and the tree carried a double reputation: its red sap was read as 'blood' of the tree, giving it sacred and slightly ominous glamour (Irish and Welsh lore warns that felling an alder could anger water-spirits), while practical tradition valued it for shields and spear-shafts. Norse creation myth made the first human pair from trees — Askr, 'ash,' and Embla, usually glossed 'elm' but with an etymology still debated — a reminder of how intimately the Germanic peoples tied tree-names to origins. In English verse the alder stands as the abiding companion of the willow along rivers and dykes — exactly the pairing the bare exclamation '_____ and Willow!' in the source card evokes, and one echoed in the waterside scenes of modern fantasy literature.

5. Summary of senses: (1) the tree itself; (2) its lightweight, reddening timber and the objects made of it; (3) several similarly named plants and insects (alder buckthorn, winterberry, alder fly); (4) a surname and place-name element; (5) the ecological and folkloric archetype of the riverbank — nitrogen-fixing pioneer, sacred red-bleeding wood, poetic companion of the willow.
