- Old English mattuc/mættoc: a plain word of the field and farm, recorded in glosses and estate documents as an unremarkable tool for breaking ground; connotation utterly humble and utilitarian, with no glamour of any kind.
- Middle Ages: the everyday implement of ditchers, hedgers, grave-diggers, and builders; listed in manorial inventories and labour accounts beside spades and axes; still low-register, but carries steady associations with skilled manual craft and hard masculine toil.
- 1500s–1700s: fixed in biblical English by the KJV (1611) at 1 Samuel 13:20 ('his axe, and his mattock'); the word takes on a solemn, homeland-pious colour, and Johnson's Dictionary (1755) records it as standard but plain.
- 1800s: resurfaces as the instrument of navvies, railway builders, settlers, and miners; in American catalogues it is often sold as a 'grub hoe'; perception: pre-industrial, durable, honest toil — the tool that beat the wilderness into fields.
- Modern: rare in speech, powerful in prose — fantasy and historical fiction use it to summon earthy, dwarfish, pre-mechanical labour; in real use it survives in archaeology, horticulture, and disaster rescue, and it still sounds unpretentious and ancient.
