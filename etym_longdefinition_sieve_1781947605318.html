In Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the noun sieve is an improbably comic prop: "MR. McGREGOR came up with a sieve, which he intended to pop upon the top of Peter." Mr. McGregor, the gardener, seizes the first household-and-garden tool to hand and turns it into a makeshift cage — a great wire-mesh strainer that he means to clap down over the little rabbit like a bowl over a mouse. Potter's word choice is exact in two ways. First, it is a genuinely rustic, workaday object: a sieve belongs to the kitchen, the dairy, and the garden — the world of sifting flour and riddling soil — and its very ordinariness makes the attempt both homely and faintly absurd. Second, the sieve is an open, latticed object: its mesh lets light through, so that, unlike a box or a basket, it would not have hidden Peter at all; the reader is made aware that Peter is visible, crouched and vulnerable, under the transparent net. The irony sharpens the suspense: the trap is see-through, and the chase is conducted in the open. The tone is brisk, breathless nursery comedy — McGregor "came up," the sieve is to be "popped," and Peter escapes "just in time" — and the sieve, as the instrument of the failed capture, is the little detail that makes the scene funny rather than frightening.

Core senses and polysemy:

  • Core sense (the utensil) — a sieve is a mesh, wire, or perforated plate held in a frame, used for straining solids from liquids or separating coarse particles from fine ones: a flour sieve, a garden sieve (a "riddle" in rural English). In the kitchen it strains stock and sifts flour; in the garden it riddles soil of stones; in the dairy it strained milk and curds.
  • Verb sense — to sieve is to pass through a sieve (to sift, strain, or riddle); the verb is ordinary in cooking and construction English (to sieve the flour).
  • Figurative and proverbial senses — the sieve is the universal emblem of inability to hold: a memory like a sieve (forgetful), water through a sieve (waste or futility; also a spendthrift's money), to be a sieve for secrets (someone who leaks information). In Greek myth the Danaids were condemned to fill a sieve in Hades, and the sieve was anciently an instrument of divination (coscinomancy) and of separating truth from falsehood.
  • Scientific and technical senses — the sieve of Eratosthenes (3rd century BC) is the ancient algorithm for finding prime numbers by "sieving" out composites; sieve analysis is the standard test for the particle-size distribution of soil, sand, and aggregates; molecular sieves are porous materials that separate molecules by size; and in genetics the phrase "genetic sieve" describes the filtering of mutations by selection.
  • Mining and industrial senses — sieving screens and riddles separate ore, coal, and gravel by size; a sieve in this context is any perforated screen used for grading.
  • Botany and zoology — the sieve tube names the conducting cells of plant phloem, and the sieve plate the perforated end-wall between them; in zoology a sieve of fine perforations names certain filtering structures.
  • Historical senses — a sieve (or riddle) was used in the dairy and brewery for centuries; in older English the word also meant a coarse basket or winnowing tray.

Near-synonyms and their boundaries:

  • Strainer: similar as a mesh utensil for separating; differs because a strainer is generally the kitchen tool that holds back solids while liquid passes, usually coarser and often bowl-shaped, while a sieve is more general and includes the dry sifting of powders and the riddling of soil.
  • Colander: similar as a perforated kitchen vessel; differs because a colander is a large bowl-shaped utensil with holes, used for draining and rinsing, with no fine mesh and no dry-sifting use.
  • Sifter: similar as a utensil that separates; differs because a sifter is specialised for dry powders (flour, sugar) and often has a crank or shaking mechanism, while a sieve covers both wet straining and dry sifting.
  • Riddle: similar as a coarse sieve; differs because riddle is the rural, dialectal term for a coarse garden or farm sieve for riddling soil or grain, and the word's other sense (a puzzling question) is a separate homonym.
  • Screen: similar as a mesh that separates by size; differs because a screen is the larger industrial or window mesh, used for grading aggregates or filtering air, not a handheld kitchen utensil.
  • Filter: similar as a device that separates; differs because a filter removes matter from a fluid by passing it through a porous medium, operating by fineness and often by adhesion, whereas a sieve separates by particle size and lets everything smaller pass through its holes.
  • Mesh: similar as the open network of a sieve; differs because mesh is the material (the netting itself), not the framed utensil that Potter's McGregor wields.
  • Tamis (or tammy): similar as a fine-mesh strainer used in cooking; differs because a tamis is the specialised fine drum-shaped cloth or hair sieve of the professional kitchen, a sub-type rather than the general word.

The sieve is thus at once the most ordinary object in the larder and one of the most metaphorically fertile words in the language — an open net that has come to stand for forgetfulness, futility, filtration, and the very algorithm that still finds primes today.
