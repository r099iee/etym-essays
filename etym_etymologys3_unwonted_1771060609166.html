The evolution of unwonted: from desire to dwelling to departure. 1. Proto-Indo-European beginnings (c. 4500–3000 BCE). The story of "unwonted" opens at the root *wenh₁-, which in reconstructed Proto-Indo-European meant "to desire, to strive after, to love, to win." The earliest speakers of the ancestor language used this root for wanting and striving — an active, outward-pointing force. Its reflexes are extraordinarily widespread: Latin "venus" (love, charm, desire) and "venia" (favour, grace), Sanskrit "vana-" (desire, pleasure; also the related 'wish' in 'vāñchā'), and, crucially for our word, a Germanic strand that moved the meaning of satisfied desire toward settled contentment. At this stage there is no trace of habit, custom, or dwelling; the semantic content is purely affective and volitional. 2. The Germanic shift: from satisfied desire to dwelling (c. 500 BCE – 500 CE). In the North, the root hardened into Proto-Germanic *wunēną "to be satisfied, to be content." The decisive change happened when the idea of being content merged with the act of staying put: a person who has found a satisfying place stops moving and resides there. Thus *wunēną acquired the concrete sense "to dwell, to remain." This is exactly the double meaning that survives across the Germanic family — Old High German "wonēn" (to dwell, to remain) giving modern German "wohnen" (to reside), Old Saxon "wonon" (to dwell), Dutch "wonen" (to live), and Old English "wunian" (to dwell, remain, continue, be accustomed). The bridge from dwelling to habit is a simple, powerful metaphor: to remain in a place repeatedly is to become used to it, and Old English "wunian" carried both meanings side by side, especially in its compounded form "gewunian," "to be accustomed to, to have the habit of." 3. Old and Middle English: habit takes the noun form (8th–15th c.). Old English regularly formed the past participle "gewunod," "accustomed," and the noun "gewuna," "custom, habit." By Middle English these collapsed into "wont," used both as a noun ("as is my wont" — my custom) and as an adjective meaning "accustomed, in the habit of" ("he is wont to rise early"). The attested continuum runs "wonen / wunien" (to dwell, be used to) → "wont" (accustomed). From "wont" the 14th century derived "wonted" ("habitual, usual," as in Shakespeare's "my wonted sleeps" and the stock phrase "wonted haunts"), an adjective built simply by adding -ed to the existing participial form. Literary attestations abound: Chaucer and the Gawain-poet use "wont," and "wonted" appears in Shakespeare, Milton, and throughout Renaissance prose with the settled meaning "customary." 4. The negative prefix and the birth of unwonted (15th–17th c.). The ancient PIE negative particle *ne- had become the Germanic prefix *un- and Old English "un-," the standard English way of negating adjectives. Joining "un-" to "wonted" produced "unwonted," first attested in the late fifteenth / early sixteenth century, with the transparent meaning "not habitual, not customary, unaccustomed." Early uses are both predicative of persons ("unwonted to hear such news" — not in the habit of) and attributive of things ("unwonted severity," "unwonted silence"). Crucially, the word quickly drifted from describing what a person is not in the habit of doing to describing what strikes the observer as remarkably out of the ordinary. Because the base "wont" retained a dignified, slightly archaic flavour, "unwonted" became a favoured intensifier in Elizabethan and Jacobean tragedy and in Renaissance sermons and essays, where "unwonted" energy, terror, or light signals something extraordinary breaking into the everyday order. 5. Eighteenth to nineteenth century: the psychological and Gothic turn. As the base word "wont" began to leave ordinary speech in the eighteenth century, "unwonted" was carried along into the elevated literary register, and its connotation shifted from neutral "unaccustomed" toward "uncanny, alarming, portentous." Gothic novelists (Walpole, Radcliffe, and their successors) discovered its power to describe the atmosphere of a scene — "an unwonted stillness," "unwonted gloom" — personifying places as having habits that could be violated. Romantic and Victorian prose then pushed the word into the psychological domain: an "unwonted" flush, "unwonted" tenderness, or "unwonted" energy signals a character momentarily displaced from their own temperament, a favourite device of Dickens, George Eliot, and the Brontës for registering emotional crisis without naming it directly. This is the exact reading required by our literary sentence, where "unwonted severity" marks the alarming, out-of-character hardening of a familiar figure. 6. Twentieth century to the present: the literary survivor. In the twentieth century "unwonted" effectively withdrew from spontaneous conversation and became a deliberate, self-aware literary and journalistic flourish. Its modern uses cluster in narrative fiction, historical writing, and mock-archaic or parodic prose, where it supplies period colour and a sense of gravitas. The sound shift to note is minimal: the word preserves the reflexes of its parts — the stressed "wont" (rhyming with "don't" in conservative RP) and the light suffix "‑ed." The semantic drift is the real story: a term that once meant simply "not in the habit of" now means "strikingly, almost disturbingly out of the ordinary," carrying a residue of the old psychological rupture. The mnemonic — "UN-WONT-ed: NOT what you are WONT / used to doing" — captures the morphology exactly, and it explains why an "unwonted" act always reads as a departure from a recognisable self.
