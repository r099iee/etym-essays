- [c. 1570–1620 Elizabethan & Jacobean Eras] — Coined in colloquial English cant and vernacular speech as noun trapes / traipse ("a slattern, a careless or slovenly woman who lets her skirts trail in the mud") and verb meaning "to walk untidily, trailing one's clothes through dirt" — [perception: strongly derogatory, misogynistic colloquial censure directed at untidy, vagrant, or lower-class women].
- [c. 1690–1780 Restoration & Augustan Eras] — Elevated into satirical literature and comedies of manners (used by Jonathan Swift, Alexander Pope, and John Gay), where it described fashionable or vulgar people trudging wearily through London's muddy streets or wandering impertinently through drawing rooms — [perception: comedic, satirical, and irritated appraisal of tedious urban walking and social vanity].
- [19th c. Victorian & Regional Dialectal Era] — Softened from a harsh moral slur into a vivid domestic and rustic verb (frequently spelled trapessing across Somerset, Devon, Yorkshire, and the Midlands) denoting weary foot-slogging, running endless domestic errands, or invading private garden spaces — [perception: exasperated, affectionate, or complaining domestic vernacular used by matrons and gardeners].
- [20th c. Literary & Dialectal Preservation] — Preserved in high modernist and fantasy prose (such as Virginia Woolf, D.H. Lawrence, and J.R.R. Tolkien), where it captures rural authentic speech (e.g., Gaffer Gamgee complaining about folk "trampling and trapessing all over the garden") — [perception: flavorful, evocative signifier of rural vernacular, garden sanctity, and weary human persistence].
- [21st c. Contemporary English] — Maintained across British, Irish, and Commonwealth English (primarily in variant traipsing, with trapessing preserved in dialect and historical prose) to describe exhaustingly tedious shopping trips, sight-seeing, or unwelcome household intrusions — [perception: humorous, mildly exasperated complaint against endless, tiresome walking].
