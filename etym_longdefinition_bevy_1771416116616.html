Bevy is a collective noun for a group of people or things of a particular kind, carrying a built-in implication of brightness, animation, and pleasing abundance. Historically it designated small groups of birds — above all a bevy of quails — and roe-deer; from the sixteenth century it broadened to a bevy of ladies or young women; and in modern journalese it can apply to any lively cluster: a bevy of officials, executives, celebrities, models, or proposals. In the Dream of the Red Chamber sentence, the beautiful young woman Wang Xi-feng “entered from the room behind the one they were sitting in, surrounded by a bevy of serving women and maids.” The noun stages her entrance as a fanfare: she does not simply walk in but arrives with a living train, and the word's hunting origin lends the scene a hint of natural abundance — a flock rising from cover — so that the young mistress is framed as a figure around whom energy itself flocks. The tone is vibrant and admiring, catching the exact mixture of theatricality and managerial power that defines Xi-feng from her first appearance: the woman who runs the Jia household the way a general runs an army, attended by her officers.

The full polysemy of the word:

  • Core sense — a group of quails or roe-deer: the original hunting (venery) sense. A bevy of quails is a small flock, a bevy of roes a small herd; this is the sense in which the word first appears in English in the fifteenth century.
  • Human sense — a company of young women: “a bevy of beauties,” “a bevy of girls,” “a bevy of debutantes”; the characteristic literary use, light, festive, and admiring rather than dense or impersonal. This is the sense that drives the Dream of the Red Chamber sentence.
  • Generalised sense — any bright animated cluster: a bevy of swans, of reporters, of new products, of proposals; the modern journalistic extension, still playful, still implying charm and countable abundance.
  • Terms-of-venery family: bevy belongs to the elaborate medieval vocabulary of collective nouns for game and birds — a gaggle of geese, a covey of partridges, an exaltation of larks, a murmuration of starlings — a branch of the lexicon that now survives mostly as playful trivia and hunting tradition.
  • Register and connotation: mildly archaic and literary. A writer chooses “bevy” to suggest grace, charm, and delightful abundance rather than mass or pressure; it is almost never neutral, and it is rarely used of anything solemn, hostile, or inert.
  • Etymological shadow: if the traditional etymology is right, the word descends from an Old French word for “a drink, a drinking-session” (bevee), so that a bevy may originally have meant “a company gathered for a drink.” The leap from drinking-party to flock is debated by modern handbooks, but the ghost of a convivial gathering lingers in the word's brightness.
  • Grammatical behaviour: bevy is a singular count noun governing a following “of”: a bevy of X; it takes singular verb agreement as a collective, and it is commonly used with an indefinite article (“a bevy of …”), rarely with “the.”

The near-synonyms each shift the picture in a characteristic direction. A group is flat and neutral; a crowd is dense, massed, and impersonal; a throng presses and jostles; a troop is organised and quasi-military; a flock applied to people suggests sheep-like following; a gaggle is noisy and faintly derisive; a swarm is insect-like and restless. The bevy alone combines lightness, elegance, femininity (in its classic human use), and delight — a cluster of beautiful things moving together, exactly the effect the novelist needs at Xi-feng's grand entrance.
