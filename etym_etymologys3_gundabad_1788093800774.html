1. A name with no real-world ancestry. Unlike the words of ordinary vocabulary, Gundabad cannot be traced to Latin, Germanic, or a PIE root, for it was deliberately invented by J.R.R. Tolkien as part of Khuzdul, the secret Dwarvish language that appears in the legendarium only in scattered names and phrases (Khazad-dûm, Zirak-zigil, Barazinbar, Gabilgathol, Tumunzahar, and the war-cry "Baruk Khazâd! Khazâd ai-mênu!"). Its "evolution" is therefore the evolution of a constructed artifact: invention, publication, philological glossing, and cultural afterlife - all of which this essay traces.

2. Invention: the design of Khuzdul (1910s-1930s). Tolkien began building Khuzdul in the decades before 1937, while his legendarium was still taking shape. He deliberately made it unlike his elegant Elvish tongues: it was harsh, consonantal, and guttural - heavy with k, g, z, r, kh, and dh - and, as he later stated, intentionally similar in character to the Semitic languages, with triliteral roots and a foreignness that would mirror how the Dwarves kept their language secret from other peoples. The conspicuous accumulation of gutturals and dental stops in Gundabad is exactly this design at work: a name that sounds underground, blocked, and hard - appropriate for a race that lives in halls cut from living rock.

3. First publication (1937). The name appears in The Hobbit, in the geography of the North that frames Thorin Oakenshield's quest: the goblin power of the Misty Mountains, against which the expedition must travel, has its northern root at Gundabad. Even at first appearance the name is already charged - an exotic, ominous toponym whose two heavy syllables the reader must simply accept as the name of a place of darkness. Tolkien gives it the formulaic tag "of the North", which in his style signals menace and distance: northern places in the legendarium are older, colder, less known, and home to the oldest evils.

4. The Lord of the Rings: strategic revelation (1954). The decisive textual moment is the Council of Elrond in The Fellowship of the Ring, where Gandalf's intelligence report (the source sentence of this card) reveals the mountain's modern role: "around and beneath the great mountain Gundabad of the North, where was their capital, a vast host was assembled ready to sweep down in time of storm unawares upon the South." The passage accomplishes several things at once: it confirms that the Orcs of the North are organised into a state with a capital; it locates that capital in the most unforgiving country of the range; and it sets a clock ticking - a storm-people massing under a mountain, ready to fall on the South. The revelation follows Gandalf's account of the Orcs' "forging and an arming" (the companion card in this batch), so Gundabad becomes the material centre of a hidden, underground war economy.

5. The Dwarvish past: Durin's awakening (1955 and later). Appendix A of The Return of the King, with Tolkien's later writings, binds Gundabad to the earliest Dwarf-lore: Durin the Deathless, eldest of the Seven Fathers of the Dwarves, first awoke beneath Mount Gundabad and thereafter wandered south to the Mirrormere, where he founded Khazad-dûm. The mountain thus functions as the origin-node of the Longbeards - their Eden, so to speak - which makes its occupation by Orcs a double violation: the enemy holds not merely a stronghold but the ancestral holy place of the race, and Durin's line (Thrór, Thráin, Thorin, and the Dwarves of Erebor and Moria) is defined by exile from, and memory of, the lost places of the North. Tolkien's posthumously published philological essays (in The Peoples of Middle-earth, 1996) continue to discuss the Dwarf-names as linguistic material, treating Gundabad as a Khuzdul formation; modern scholarship (e.g. David Salo's reconstruction work of 2004) accordingly analyses the first element as *gund(u), "underground hall, delve" - semantically perfect for a hollowed mountain - while the second element, -bad, remains unglossed and unpublished.

6. The accidental Persian twin. To the general reader the element -abad irresistibly suggests the Persian suffix of city-names (Hyderabad, Islamabad, Ahmedabad, "settled place"). This resemblance is coincidental: Tolkien designed Khuzdul on Semitic lines, and no Persian material entered the name's construction. The coincidence is nonetheless instructive, and it is routinely cited in discussions of invented etymology because it shows how plausible-sounding a constructed name can be - and how careful a philologist must be before "deriving" Tolkien's languages from real ones.

7. The name in fan culture and games (1980s-2020s). After the explosion of Tolkien fandom, Gundabad became a standard landmark of Middle-earth's North in atlases, genealogies, wikis, and encyclopedias of the legendarium; role-playing games - Middle-earth Role Playing and later The Lord of the Rings Online, whose 2021 expansion is titled Gundabad - rebuilt the mountain as explorable territory of tunnels, stairs, and war-camps; and the film adaptations, while not dwelling on the mountain, cemented the visual grammar of vast underground orc-armies that Gundabad epitomises in the books. Across all this the word's journey is complete: from a private invention of a philologist, through print (1937), strategic revelation (1954), ancestral sanctification (1955), linguistic scrutiny (1996), to a household name of fantasy geography - a proper noun whose evolution is a record of how invented words acquire, layer by layer, the weight of history.
