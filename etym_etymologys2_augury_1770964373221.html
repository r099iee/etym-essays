Single root word: augury (English noun, from the Latin line of the PIE root *h₂ewg-). Linear derivation chain (earliest attested/reconstructed to present; no fixed stage count, exact forms with dates): 1. Proto-Indo-European root *h₂ewg- "to increase, to prosper, to strengthen, to favour" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE). The root denotes growth and increase; it underlies Latin augēre and Greek aúxein "to increase." 2. → Proto-Italic *augē- "to increase, strengthen" (reconstructed intermediate stage of the Italic branch). 3. → Latin augēre "to increase, magnify, make grow, prosper" (attested 3rd c. BCE onward; cf. augmentum "increase," source of English augment). 4. → Latin augur "a religious official who interpreted omens, especially by observing the flight and cries of birds" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). The exact formation is DEBATED: most modern scholarship (following the connection to augēre) reads the augur as "one who increases or favours," i.e. one who procures divine favour and growth for the community; a widely repeated popular etymology instead links the word to avis "bird" (a genuinely PIE root *h₂éwis "bird," the source of auspex/auspicium and English avian), interpreting the augur as a "bird-watcher." The bird link is not supported by secure sound correspondences and is generally treated by modern etymologists as folk etymology, though it has ancient precedent and remains influential. 5. → Latin augurium "the office or practice of an augur; augury; a portent or omen" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE; formed from augur + abstract suffix -ium). 6. → Old French augurie "augury, divination, omen" (attested 12th–13th c.), learned and clerical transmission of Latin augurium into the Romance vernacular. 7. → Anglo-Norman augurie "augury, omen" (attested 13th–14th c.), the channel through which the word entered English. 8. → Middle English augurie / augurye "divination by omens; an omen, a prognostic" (attested 14th c., c. 1375 in Chaucer's usage and contemporary religious prose). 9. → Modern English augury "the art of divination by omens; an omen, portent, or sign of the future" (present day). Debate/nuance note: Stage 4 is the sole genuinely contested node. The avis-connection, although chronologically old (it appears in ancient Roman antiquarian writing and is repeated by later lexicographers), fails on strict phonological and morphological grounds — augur does not show the expected avi- base, and avis is not attested inside the word in Latin inscriptions. The connection to augēre "to increase" is favoured by most modern reference works (de Vaan, the OED), but even they flag the suffix -ur as opaque, so the final verdict is "probably from augēre, ultimately from PIE *h₂ewg-, but not certain." No other stage in the chain is seriously disputed.
