CHAIN 1 — THE ELEMENT "FIRST" (primus): Stage 1 — PIE *per- "forward, in front, through" (reconstructed root of forward position and movement; attested indirectly through its vast daughter family, e.g. Sanskrit purá "before", Greek pará "beside", Latin per "through"; sometimes also reconstructed as *perə- "to pass over" — see debate note). Stage 2 — Proto-Italic *pri- "before, in front" (the comparative base built on *per- with the *-i- formative; reflected in Latin prior "earlier, former" and the adverb prīmum "at first"). Stage 3 — Latin prīmus "first, foremost, chief" (superlative formed with the *-mo- superlative suffix on the *pri- base; attested from the earliest Latin literature of the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE, e.g. in Plautus and Ennius). CHAIN 2 — THE ELEMENT "TO TAKE" (capere): Stage 1 — PIE *kap- "to grasp, seize, take" (reconstructed root of taking and grasping; the source of Latin capiō, and cognate with English have, German haben, and Greek káptein "to gulp"). Stage 2 — Proto-Italic *kap-e- "to take, grasp" (the thematic present stem underlying the Latin verb). Stage 3 — Latin capere "to take, seize, grasp, catch, capture, contain" (attested from the 3rd century BCE; the regular source of English capture, captive, capacity). THE COMPOUND — THE FULL WORD: Stage 4 — Latin prīnceps (stem prīncip-) "first in rank, chief, leader, founder, originator" — a compound of prīmus ("first") + capere ("to take"), literally "one who takes the first part/place" (attested from the 3rd century BCE in Plautus; by the Augustan age the regular title of the emperor, prīnceps senātus "first of the senate"; in Late Latin and medieval usage the title of kings, rulers, and royal sons). Stage 5 — Old French prince "sovereign ruler, prince" (11th century, from Latin prīncip-, the oblique stem of prīnceps). Stage 6 — Middle English prince "sovereign, ruler, king's son" (attested from c. 1200, borrowed from Old French). Stage 7 — Modern English prince, plural PRINCES (the regular plural in -s). FINAL NODE: PRINCES. DEBATE NOTE: the analysis of prīnceps as "first-taker" is standard and secure — the Roman grammarian Varro already glossed it as quasi primum capiens, "as it were, taking first place". The only genuine uncertainty lies in the ultimate PIE root: whether *per- "forward" or a related *perə- "to pass over" underlies the *pri- of primus; scholars usually merge the two, and the difference is immaterial to the meaning of the chain.
