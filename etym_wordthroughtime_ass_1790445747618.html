- Beast of burden - in Old English assa names the humble he-ass, a farm animal with scriptural dignity and no insult attached.
- Emblem of folly - through medieval fable, proverb, and pulpit the donkey hardens into the type of clumsiness and stupidity, and by the 1500s calling a man an ass or asshead is standard abuse.
- Polite retreat - from the late 18th century respectable speakers switch to donkey, leaving ass to Bibles, natural histories, Ireland, and comic insults like Wodehouse's affectionate "silly ass".
- Vulgar twin arrives - across the 17th–20th centuries dialect arse loses its R to become American ass (buttocks), first in sailors' slang (1860), then mainstream (1930s), loading the same spelling with taboo force.
- Modern split personality - today British ears still hear mostly the lovable fool, American ears hear both the fool and the obscenity, and context alone decides which donkey is braying.
