ONSLAUGHT is the story of a single prehistoric word for striking that travelled from the earliest Indo-European world into the Germanic languages, across the North Sea into Dutch, and then into English in the 17th century, where it has served ever since as one of the most violent and most vivid words for an overwhelming attack. Its biography connects the smiting of ancient warriors to the crash of falling rocks in Middle-earth. THE INDO-EUROPEAN ROOT The whole family begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *slak- "to strike, smite, hit", reconstructed by Pokorny (some scholars prefer the variant *slā-). It is a western Indo-European root: it is fully attested only in the Germanic family, and no descendant of *slak- is securely recorded in Sanskrit, Hindi, Greek, or Latin. This is unusual and worth noting — most Indo-European roots of striking have a wide distribution, but *slak- stayed close to home in the northwest of the family, and the Indo-Aryan branch is honestly absent from its history. From *slak- the Germanic speech community formed the strong verb *slahan- "to strike, smite, kill", and from that single verb came one of the richest families of English words for violence. THE GERMANIC FAMILY In Old English, *slahan- became slēan, "to strike, to kill", the ancestor of English slay (past slain), a word that in Old English poetry named the greatest heroic action — the killing of monsters and enemies — and still carries an archaic, mighty resonance. In Old Norse the same verb slá "to strike" produced the deverbal noun slag "a blow, a stroke", and from slag English borrowed sledge, the heavy smiting hammer of the sledge-hammer, and, much later, the 19th-century slug, "to strike hard". Old Norse also formed slátr, "butchered meat, carcase" — literally what has been struck down — and English merged this with slay in the 13th century to produce slaughter, with its grim compounds slaughterhouse and slaughterman. On the continent the same root gave Old High German slahan, the ancestor of German schlagen "to strike, beat" and Schlag "a blow", a verb as everyday in German as hit is in English. The family is thus spread across the whole Germanic world, and every member keeps the core image of a blow. THE DUTCH COMPOUND The immediate ancestor of onslaught was formed in Middle Dutch. The Dutch verb slāen "to strike" (modern slaan) had the deverbal noun slag "a blow, a stroke, a smiting", and by prefixing the preposition aan (older aen), "on, upon", the language built aanslag, literally "a striking upon": an attack, an onset, an assault. The same Dutch word, in a revealing parallel, also meant a levy or tax assessment — a "striking on" of the roll — a sense English did not adopt. It is a compact, physical image: the blow falls upon something, and the compound names the moment of impact. In Dutch this family stayed alive, and modern Dutch aanslag still means "an attack, a (terrorist) attempt", alongside the fiscal sense of "a tax assessment". THE BORROWING INTO ENGLISH English adopted the word in the 17th century as onslaught, and from the first it meant a violent onset or fierce attack, chiefly of troops or weapons. The borrowing belongs to the age of the Dutch wars and the Dutch commercial and military prestige, when English took many sea and war words from Dutch (as with yacht, cruise, and many naval terms). The spelling, however, did not stay Dutch: under the pull of the unrelated-but-ultimately-related word slaughter — itself from the same ultimate root *slak- by a different route — the ending was reshaped to -aught, giving the modern form onslaught. The result is a curious, satisfying echo: the word for an overwhelming attack now visibly rhymes with the word for mass killing, even though the two arrived in English by separate paths, one through Dutch and one through Old Norse. WIDENING AND MODERN USE In the 18th and 19th centuries the target of onslaught widened from armies to the elements: an onslaught of wind, rain, or fire was an impersonal violence pressing down, still violent but no longer human. The century of imperial warfare and of great storms fixed this double life — the word could describe a cavalry charge or a hurricane with equal force. In the 20th and 21st centuries the word went fully metaphorical: an onslaught of criticism, of advertising, of email, of paperwork — the aggressor may be anything at all, and the victim is merely swept along. The press, advertising, and office life adopted the word because modern life is full of overwhelming quantities pressing in, and onslaught names the sensation precisely. Throughout, the word has insisted on mass and momentum: an onslaught is never a single blow; it is an overwhelming, unstoppable press of force, and even its most figurative uses keep the military image of an attack. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses onslaught at the moment the company is caught in the landslide: "some (like the little hobbit) got behind a tree to shelter from the onslaught of the rocks." The choice is deliberately martial. A landslide is a natural accident, but onslaught frames it as a deliberate assault, endowing the mountain itself with hostile intent: the rocks charge down upon the company like an enemy formation, and the hobbit shelters behind a tree as a soldier shelters behind a wall. The word carries the whole weight of the episode — the weight of a mass bearing down, the inevitability of the strike — and it shows how a 17th-century borrowing from a Dutch word for a blow struck upon something could still, three centuries later, make a mountain attack its readers, with the ancient PIE *slak- still audible in the crash.
