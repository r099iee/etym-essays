PIE *wer- 'to turn, bend, twist, coil' (c. 4500-3500 BCE) -> Proto-Germanic *wurmiz 'serpent, dragon, worm, creeping thing' (from *wr-m-, literally 'the twisting or coiling one'; c. 500 BCE) -> Old English wyrm 'serpent, dragon, reptile, worm, maggot' (attested c. 700-1000 CE; in the poem Beowulf the treasure-guarding dragon is a wyrm) -> Middle English worm 'a crawling creature, worm, serpent, maggot, parasite' (12th-15th c.) -> English worm (noun) 'a small creeping invertebrate; a caterpillar, maggot, or grub' (modern) -> English worm (verb) 'to move, crawl, or creep like a worm; to work one's way gradually and stealthily; to extract by persistent, devious means' (the verbal use is attested from the 16th c.; 'to worm oneself into' a position or confidence and 'to worm a secret out of' someone are recorded from the 16th-17th c.) -> WORMED (past tense and past participle of the verb) 'moved like a worm; insinuated; extracted deviously' (attested from the early 17th c.). Note on the chain: the derivation of Proto-Germanic *wurmiz from PIE *wer- is standard and uncontroversial - the worm is, etymologically, 'the turning or coiling creature'. The same PIE root *wer- produced Latin vermis 'worm' (the source of English vermin, vermicelli, and vermilion) as a parallel, independent branch, not as an ancestor of the English word. The semantic collapse from 'dragon, serpent' to 'earthworm' in English happened because Norman French dragon (and Latin serpens) displaced the grander senses of wyrm after the Norman Conquest of 1066, leaving the humble crawling creature in possession of the native word.
