DELLS is the plural of dell, a word for a small, secluded, and usually tree-screened valley or hollow in the ground. Where a valley is broad, open, and often river-drained, a dell is the diminutive of landscape: a modest depression scooped out of a hillside or among woods, sunken below the surrounding level, sheltered by trees, and intimate in scale. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the word appears in the topographical sentence that carries the company down toward the edge of Mirkwood: "He was on a stony path winding downwards with a rocky wall on the left hand; on the other side the ground sloped away and there were dells below the level of the path overhung with bushes and low trees." The picture is precise: the dells lie below the level of the path, on the sloping side, their mouths half-closed by overhanging bushes and low trees, so that each hollow reads as a cave-like pocket of green pressed into the hillside. The tone is one of enclosure and dimness, light blocked and view limited, the terrain folded and shadowed as the party descends into the dark wood.

Physical and topographical character. A dell differs from its neighbours by a combination of scale and enclosure. It is small enough to be walked across in moments, deep enough to be sunk below the level of the surrounding ground, and enclosed enough, usually by trees, undergrowth, or banks, to feel sheltered and hidden. It is not necessarily watered: a dell may be dry, whereas a dale or valley commonly carries a stream or river. In landscape terms it is a hollow, a basin, a scooped depression; the word stresses seclusion rather than drainage. This is why a dell, though a humble landform, has always been a place of the imagination: sunken and screened, it is out of sight, and what is out of sight can both harbour and hide.

  • Sense 1 (core geographical sense): "a small, deep, secluded valley or hollow, especially one among woods or hills," as in "the dells of the Pennines" or "a dell full of bluebells." This is the primary and oldest sense, native to English since the Old English period.
  • Sense 2 (plural, landscape in series): in the plural the word names a sequence or scatter of such hollows, the ground repeatedly folded into pockets, which is exactly how Tolkien deploys it. The plural makes the danger cumulative: not one hollow but a run of them, below the path like traps laid along the descent into the dark wood.
  • Sense 3 (literary and archaizing register): in poetry and stylised prose dell carries an archaic, pastoral, or fairytale colouring, the secluded retreat of the shepherd, the hermit, or the fairy. It is a word of the poets and the dialect map rather than of the surveyor's report, and its sweetness shades toward the eerie: a place to shelter in or to hide in.
  • Sense 4 (toponymy and scenic place-names): dell survives abundantly in place-names, often in the plural, "the Dells" designating scenic, sometimes spectacular, river-cut gorges and ravines (most famously the Wisconsin Dells, where the Wisconsin River has carved sandstone bluffs). Here the topographical meaning shifts toward a gorge or ravine, but the core idea of a sunken, enclosed hollow remains.
  • Sense 5 (figurative and metaphorical): in literary and rhetorical use a dell can stand for a hidden refuge, a retreat from the world, a secret or forgotten pocket of safety, or, conversely, a lurking place. The metaphor rests on the dell's defining property of enclosure: what is sunken and screened is both protected and concealing.

Register, distribution, and stylistic bridge. Dell is emphatically literary and dialectal in modern English. The OED marks it chiefly archaic or dialectal, and its modern life is largely confined to poetry, fantasy, historical fiction, and place-names. This is a consequence of its history: it was a plain native word of the shepherd and the ploughman in Old and Middle English, survived in the dialects, was revived by antiquarian and Romantic poets as a consciously literary word for native scenery, and was inherited by twentieth-century fantasy, Tolkien, C. S. Lewis, and their successors, as a word that sounds both ancient and homely. The semantic bridge between "a small valley" and "a place that hides or shelters" runs through the physical fact of enclosure: a hollow sunk below its surroundings is out of sight, and anything out of sight can both harbour and conceal. The plural form dells is no more than the ordinary plural, but in narrative it does special work, accumulating the landscape into a series of waiting pockets, each one a potential hiding-place as the travellers wind downward out of the daylight.
