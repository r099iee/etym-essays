- Late 14th c. — snar the single growl: Middle English holds the short imitative verb for an animal's angry sound.
- 1590s — snarls multiply it: the frequentative snarl with its -le of repetition makes the plural natural — a volley of growls, not one snap — and writers use it for scolding human voices too.
- 17th c. — faces join throats: snarls name the curled-lip expressions as well as the sounds, and pamphleteers hear snarls in every opponent's tract.
- 18th–19th c. — fiction's kennel chorus: snarling packs in novels and romances supply the audible wallpaper of danger, from guard dogs to goblins.
- 20th c. — metaphor takes the plural: press snarls, partisan snarls, tangled traffic snarls from the knot-twin — sustained hostilities of every kind.
- Today — the countable menace: snarls remain individual growls you can pick hints from between, exactly as the source narrator does.
