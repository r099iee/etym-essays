heredity (noun) — the biological passing on of physical and mental characteristics from one generation to another, and, more broadly, the transmission of any trait, endowment, or condition from predecessors to successors. In the literary context given, "It is a pity that none of you seem to understand this child's heredity," the word is used with fatalistic gravity: the speaker laments that an observer fails to grasp that the child's nature was fixed long before the child could act, that every gift and every flaw has been written into the child by forebears. The term here is not a clinical label but a philosophical verdict, suggesting that the present is a palimpsest of the past and that personal identity is largely a debt owed to ancestry. The tone is reflective, deterministic, and tinged with sorrow — the heredity of a child is presented as something that adults ignore at their peril.

Primary modern sense (biology)

In modern English, heredity names the process by which physical and behavioural characteristics are transmitted from parents to offspring through genes. It is the biological mechanism that explains why children resemble their parents in features such as eye colour, stature, blood type, skin tone, and a host of traits from disease susceptibility to aspects of temperament. The concept is inseparable from the history of biology: Gregor Mendel's experiments on pea plants in the 1860s established the units of inheritance later called genes; Francis Galton coined the related vocabulary of "nature and nurture"; and Darwinian evolution presupposes heredity, since natural selection can act only on traits that are reliably passed down. In this scientific register, heredity is frequently contrasted with environment — the "nature versus nurture" debate — and paired with the modern term genetics, which names the molecular science that explains how heredity actually operates. Heredity is thus the phenomenon; genetics is the study of its machinery.

Secondary historical sense (law and property)

Beneath the biological sense lies an older legal meaning. From its entry into English in the sixteenth century, heredity referred to the transmission of property, titles, rank, debts, and obligations from a deceased person to an heir. This is the sense preserved in the words hereditary and inheritance, and it is the literal root of the English word: Latin heres means "heir," the person who succeeds to what is left behind. The semantic bridge between the legal and the biological sense is striking and elegant: just as the heir receives the estate that the deceased leaves behind, so the child receives the bodily and mental endowment that the parents "leave behind" in the germ cells. When Victorian scientists needed a word for the transmission of traits, they reached for this ancient legal vocabulary, and heredity slipped from the courtroom into the laboratory. The old sense has not vanished — it survives in phrases such as hereditary monarchy, hereditary peer, and hereditary succession — but it has been largely overshadowed by the biological sense.

Tertiary and extended senses

  • Social and cultural heredity — In sociology and anthropology, "social heredity" denotes the transmission of customs, institutions, language, and knowledge from one generation to the next by teaching and imitation rather than by genes. The phrase is metaphorical but widely used in 19th- and 20th-century social science.
  • Figurative and literary sense — In literature, heredity is a favourite engine of tragedy and determinism: the hereditary curse, the inherited madness, the family failing that recurs inescapably in each generation. The Gothic novel, naturalist fiction (Émile Zola's Rougon-Macquart cycle), and modern psychological drama all exploit this sense. Here heredity means the inescapable moral or psychological legacy of one's ancestors.
  • Clinical and epidemiological sense — In medicine, one speaks of hereditary diseases, hereditary predispositions, and hereditary conditions — afflictions that run in families and are transmitted through genes, such as haemophilia, cystic fibrosis, or Huntington's disease.
  • Colloquial and idiomatic sense — In everyday speech, heredity is invoked loosely whenever a trait "runs in the family": a talent for music, a gift for languages, a strong jaw, a hot temper. This informal use blurs the scientific boundary and treats heredity as any family resemblance, whether genetic or environmental in origin.
  • Philosophical and theological sense — In philosophical writing, heredity can mean the transmission of original sin, of karma-like burden, or of a people's collective character; it is used to argue that individuals are shaped by forces beyond their will, a theme central to debates about free will and determinism.

Grammar, register, and collocations

Heredity is an abstract uncountable noun, normally singular, with no plural form in general use. It functions as the subject or object of clauses about transmission and influence, and appears in the fixed collocations "laws of heredity," "heredity and environment," "role of heredity," "transmission of heredity," and "heredity plays a part in." Its register ranges from the neutral scientific (genetics textbooks) through the elevated literary (naturalist fiction) to the homely conversational. The adjective is hereditary, and the related words heritage, heir, inherit, and inheritance all descend from the same Latin stem heres, "heir." In the context of the child's heredity, the word carries its full historical depth: it is simultaneously the biological fact of what the child is, the legal image of what is left behind, and the moral weight of what cannot be escaped.

Polysemy in summary

  • Biology: genetic transmission of traits (dominant modern sense).
  • Law: transmission of property, title, and obligation to an heir.
  • Sociology: transmission of culture and institutions ("social heredity").
  • Medicine: hereditary disease and predisposition.
  • Literature and philosophy: inescapable ancestral legacy, determinism, inherited burden.
  • Colloquial: anything that "runs in the family."

The historical bridge uniting all these senses is the image of what is left behind and received by those who come after: the heir receives the estate, the child receives the body and mind, the society receives its institutions, and the story receives its inherited doom. One root idea — succession — governs the whole family of meanings.
