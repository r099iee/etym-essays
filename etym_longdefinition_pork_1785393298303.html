PORK is the culinary name for the flesh of the domestic pig used as food — the meat as it appears in the kitchen, the butcher's shop, and at table, not the living animal in the sty. English, like the other post-Conquest varieties of Britain, splits the vocabulary of livestock and of dish: the peasant's pig, swine, and hog (native Germanic words) name the living animal, while pork (from French) names the dressed meat on the platter — exactly as cow/ox yields beef, sheep yields mutton, and calf yields veal. Pork is a mass noun: it has no plural in ordinary culinary use (we say some pork, never *porks), and it appears both alone and as the first element of dish-compounds such as pork pie, pork chop, pork belly, and pulled pork.

In the preserved context — "We packed a traditional pork-pie and a bottle of cider for our afternoon picnic" — the word is pure food-stuff. The pork pie is a classic cold dish of English origin, a raised pie of hot-water crust filled with chopped or minced pork, eaten cold with piccalilli and salad or at a picnic with cider; the word's bare culinary form as the first element of the compound is exactly its role here. The tone is hearty, traditional, slightly rustic-festive: pie and cider are portable British cold fare for an afternoon out of doors. The word does no metaphorical work in the sentence; it simply names the savoury filling and the cultural dish, and the combination of the mass noun with the compound marks the modern, everyday register of the kitchen.

  • Sense 1 — the culinary flesh of the pig (primary sense): the dressed meat of the domestic pig as food, in any cut — leg, loin, belly, shoulder, and the ground or minced meat of sausage and pie. This is the word's core and (in ordinary speech) sole sense.
  • Sense 2 — the animal in French and the livestock register: in the Anglo-Norman and Old French word porc, from which English took pork, the term meant both the living pig and its flesh; the restriction of the English word to the culinary sense is a sociolinguistic narrowing that happened after the Conquest, when the meat-names of the Norman kitchen were taken over for the table.
  • Sense 3 — "pork barrel" and political patronage (American political slang, mid-19th century onward): by metaphor, pork denotes government spending and favours directed to local constituency projects to secure votes — "pork-barrel spending," a "pork-barrel" bill. The bridge is the image of the household pork barrel, the cask of salted pork passed around and distributed to the workers or the household; the politician dispenses public funds the way the farmer distributes the salt meat, so "pork" came to mean the meat of patronage itself.
  • Sense 4 — informal/legal "pork" as flesh and the religious colouring: in some registers pork can stand for pig-meat generically with an eye to dietary law; in Jewish and Islamic dietary observance pork is forbidden (the pig is non-kosher and non-halal), so the word carries religious and cultural weight in discussions of diet, cuisine, and identity — a cultural resonance, not a distinct lexical sense.
  • Sense 5 — figurative "pork" as worthless matter (archaic/rare): in older or dialectal use, and in the modern idiom "pork barrel," the word can be stretched to mean something fat, gross, or to be distributed; there is also an archaic slang sense of "pork" as carnal, gross flesh. These are minor, secondary shadings built on the same image of animal flesh.

The semantic bridge in one paragraph: every secondary sense grows out of the primary culinary one. The word entered Middle English around 1300 as the Norman table-name for pig-flesh, and because the Norman lords ate the meat while the Saxon peasants kept the animals, English fixed a two-register system in which the animal word is native and the meat word is French. The political metaphor of the mid-19th-century United States then transferred the concrete image of the household pork barrel — a cask of preserved pork shared out — to the sharing out of public money, so that "pork" came to name patronage itself. The religious resonance is older than English: the dietary laws of the Hebrew Bible and the Qur'an taboo pig-flesh, and the English word inherits that cultural baggage. The word's modest modern shape, a short monosyllable of kitchen and idiom, conceals a history that runs from Indo-European pig-breeding, through the Latin farm, the Norman kitchen, and the American Congress.

Register, grammar, and collocations: pork is a standard, neutral mass noun in every register — cookery, butchery, journalism, and everyday speech. It is uncountable in the culinary sense, so that portions are named by cut or measure (a pound of pork, a pork chop), and the adjective of the dish-compounds is the bare noun (pork pie, pork chop, pork belly, pork sausage, roast pork). In the political idiom it appears in the fixed compounds pork barrel and pork-barrel spending. Regional British use adds gammon (the cured hind leg), bacon (cured sides), and ham (cured leg) as specific products; American use adds pork butt, pork shoulder, and baby back ribs. The word is never an insult in itself, though pig is; pork keeps its job of naming food.
