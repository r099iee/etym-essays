Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*mō- / *meh₂- (or *meh₁-)
"to exert will, strive, be energetic;"
"to measure, apportion"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*mōs-
"will, disposition, custom, habit"
↓
Old Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
mōs (genitive *mōsis)
"will, habitual conduct, custom"
↓
Classical Latin (rhotacism s > r) (c. 1st c. BCE)
mōs (nominative), mōrēs (plural)
"customs, habits, character, morals"
↓
Late Latin / Medieval Latin (c. 5th–14th c.)
mores
"traditional customs, moral behavior"
↓
Modern English (sociological borrowing) (c. 1898–1906)
mores
"vital moral conventions of a social group"

Note: Michiel de Vaan suggests Proto-Italic *mōs- ('custom, disposition') is cognate with Proto-Germanic *mōda- ('mood, courage, disposition', source of English mood), both tracing to PIE *mō- ('to strive, be in a strong mental state'). Intervocalic -s- regularly shifted to -r- in Latin (rhotacism), yielding oblique stem mōr- (plural mōrēs), which also gave rise to Latin morālis (Cicero's coinage translating Greek ēthikos), the ultimate source of English moral and morality.
