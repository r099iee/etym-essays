PIE *dem- “house, household; to build” (reconstructed root, c. 4000–3000 BCE; the ancestor of Latin domus “house” and English timber) → Latin domus “house, household, home” (attested 1st c. BCE) → Latin dominus “master, lord, the head of the household” (attested 1st c. BCE; formed from domus) → Vulgar Latin *dominiō / *dominiōnem “lordship; the lord's stronghold” (reconstructed Late Latin / early Romance; cf. classical dominium “lordship”) → Old French donjon “the great tower, keep of a castle” (attested 12th c.) → Anglo-French / Middle English dongeoun / dungeoun “the keep; then the underground prison-vault beneath it” (attested c. 1300) → Modern English dungeon “underground prison or strong-vault” → DUNGEONS “plural; underground prisons / vaults, Tolkien's form” (present day). Debated / nuanced note: The chain from Latin dominus through Gallo-Romance donjon is secure. Two nuances should be recorded. First, the form-history: Old French donjon descends from Vulgar Latin *domniōnem (accusative of *dominiō, “lordship”), a formation on dominus; the classical Latin word behind it is dominium “lordship, domain.” The spelling with -u- in English (dungeoun) arose in Middle English by scribal variation and was reinforced by the native word dung, with which dungeon is etymologically unrelated. Second, the famous semantic split: French kept donjon for the castle's great tower; English specialized the same word toward the dark underground vault beneath the keep, so modern English dungeon and modern French donjon mean different things — tower versus prison-vault. Neither nuance affects the solidity of the derivation chain.
