MISLIKE is a compound: the prefix mis- + the verb like. The two chains are given separately. Chain — the prefix mis- 1. Proto-Indo-European (uncertain) — the prefix has no secure PIE etymon; it is traditionally compared with Latin minus "less" and minor, and with Sanskrit mithu "wrongly, falsely" (from *mith- "to exchange, alter"), but all of these are distant and debated. 2. Proto-Germanic *missa- "badly, wrongly, amiss, apart" (prefixal element; the base of English miss "to fail to hit", Gothic missa- in compounds, German miss-). 3. Old English mis- "wrongly, badly, amiss" (used in many compounds: misbeodan, misgangan, misniman), attested from the earliest Old English records. Chain — the verb like 1. Proto-Indo-European *leyg- "body, shape, form" (the root underlying the entire Germanic *līk- family). 2. Proto-Germanic *līką "body, form, likeness" (whence English lich, -like, which, such). 3. Proto-Germanic *līkōjaną "to be like, to be pleasing, to please" (denominative verb). 4. Old English līcian "to please, to be agreeable to" (impersonal in early use: me līcað, "it pleases me"). 5. Old English mislīcian "to displease, to be displeasing, to offend" — the prefix reverses the verb's polarity (attested c. 1000). 6. Middle English misliken "to displease" (intransitive/impersonal) and, from the 14th century, "to be displeased with, to dislike" (transitive). 7. Modern English mislike "to dislike, disapprove of" (transitive verb; also noun "displeasure, aversion"), now archaic or literary. MISLIKE. Debated / nuanced note: the deeper PIE ancestry of the prefix mis- is uncertain; the comparison with Sanskrit mithu- and Latin minus is suggestive but not proven, and many dictionaries mark the prefix's ultimate origin as obscure. The līcian chain, by contrast, is secure; note that English like originally meant "to please" (as in "it likes me"), and only later shifted to "to be fond of", while mislike preserves the older polarity-reversal pattern.
