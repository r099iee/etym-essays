- Old English (8th–11th c.) - The ancestral noun *glēo* / *glīw* denoted musical entertainment, minstrelsy, singing, and communal mirth around the mead hall fire.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) - *Glee* broadened into general merriment, jesting, sport, and joyous play, often paired in poetic alliterative verses contrasting joy with sorrow (*gle and game* vs. *grief*).
- 16th–17th c. - The adjective *gleeful* and adverb *gleefully* arose in Early Modern English literature, initially describing heartfelt, innocent gladness and buoyant celebration.
- 18th–19th c. - While musical 'glee clubs' preserved the archaic choral sense of the noun, the adverb *gleefully* in literature acquired a subtle secondary edge of mischievous, triumphant, or roguish excitement.
- 20th–21st c. - Highly prominent in contemporary journalism, storytelling, and everyday speech for both uninhibited childlike delight and wicked, rubbing-one's-hands-together schadenfreude when an opponent stumbles.
