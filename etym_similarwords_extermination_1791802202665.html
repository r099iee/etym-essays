• annihilation: Similar because it denotes total destruction, reducing something to nothing; differs because it is more absolute and often physical or cosmic (“annihilation of matter”), and carries none of the legal or population-specific meaning of extermination.
  • eradication: Similar because it means rooting something out completely; differs because it is typically used of diseases, pests and problems, and stresses the removal of a cause rather than the killing of a people.
  • elimination: Similar as a general word for removal; differs because it is neutral and can mean simply excluding someone from a contest or deleting a possibility, with no implication of killing.
  • extinction: Similar because it describes the disappearance of a whole kind; differs because extinction is often impersonal and ecological, the endpoint of a process, while extermination names a deliberate human campaign.
  • genocide: Similar because both denote the destruction of a people; differs because genocide is a legal term requiring intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group, whereas extermination requires no protected group and may be committed by lethal conditions.
  • liquidation: Similar in its euphemistic force for mass killing; differs because it originated in commerce (winding up a company) and in Soviet usage became a bureaucratic cover word, so it is colder and more euphemistic than extermination.
  • slaughter: Similar because it denotes killing on a large scale; differs because it stresses bloodshed and butchery in a single episode, whereas extermination stresses completeness and system over time.
  • decimation: Similar in suggesting near-total loss; differs because historically it meant the killing of one in ten by lot, and today it is used loosely for severe attrition rather than complete destruction.
  • extirpation: Similar as a formal word for rooting out utterly; differs because it is archaic and technical, used of organs, weeds and vices, and lacks the modern association with mass atrocity.
  • massacre: Similar because it names large-scale killing; differs because a massacre is a bounded event with victims present, not a policy of destroying a category of people.
  • pogrom: Similar because it targets a community; differs because it denotes a violent riot against a specific minority, usually local and short-lived, not a systematic programme of total elimination.
