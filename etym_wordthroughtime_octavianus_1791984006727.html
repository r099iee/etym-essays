- 44 BCE — The will of Caesar creates Gaius Iulius Caesar Octavianus; contemporaries mostly say “Caesar” or “the young Caesar”, and the -anus form is a legal and family marker rather than a public name.
- 1st–2nd c. CE — Suetonius and Cassius Dio use the name to keep the pre-27 heir distinct from the emperor; it becomes a historians' label, not a style he himself used.
- 16th–17th c. — English readers meet “Octavius” in translations of Suetonius and in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar; “Octavian” begins to appear as the everyday English form.
- 18th–19th c. — “Octavian” is fixed in historiography for the period before 27 BCE, while “Augustan” becomes the adjective for the age; the pair is taught as a chronological rule.
- 20th–21st c. — The name is a technical term of classical scholarship and a rare given name; in popular retellings it signals the cold young politician of the proscriptions rather than the mature emperor.
