coastwise:
- Coast + the suffix of manner (17th-18th c.) - the word is built from 'coast' (English since the 14th c., 'the side or seashore') and the adverbial -wise of direction and manner (otherwise, clockwise, lengthwise), so that 'coastwise' reads almost like a course-description from a coast-pilot's log - moving along the line of the land rather than cutting across it
- Admiralty and maritime law (18th-19th c.) - 'coastwise' becomes a technical word of American navigation and shipping law: coastwise trade, coastwise commerce, coastwise shipping, and the coastwise laws restricting domestic carriage to U.S. vessels; the word's gravity is charted and legal
- The rare literary register (19th c. onward) - having receded from speech, coastwise survives in nautical prose and literary description; applied here to pelicans it deliberately borrows the formality of a vessel on a recorded course, and the rarity is exactly why the sentence feels precise and strange

swells:
- The verb of growth (Old English) - 'to swell' means to grow, rise, and inflate, from a root meaning 'to burn, to rise up'
- The deep-sea noun (16th-17th c.) - 'a swell' names the long, broad, unbroken undulation of the open ocean - the slow breathing of the water, the wave before it finds a shore to break upon
- Figurative gathering (19th-20th c.) - 'groundswell' extends into politics as a deep, slow, gathering movement of opinion rising from beneath the visible surface
- American colloquial twist (19th c.) - 'a swell' is a fashionable, dashing person and 'swell!' an exclamation of approval - the roundness of the rising wave turned into social polish
