Derivation chain for CLAIMANTS: 1. Proto-Indo-European *kel- / *klā- (to shout, call, cry out, summon) — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE; with many derivatives in Greek, Latin, and Germanic. 2. Latin clāmāre (to shout, cry out, proclaim, call upon) — attested from the 2nd c. BCE (Plautus, Terence). 3. Vulgar Latin *clamāre (same senses) — retained unchanged in the Romance dialects of Gaul. 4. Old French clamer / claamer (to call, proclaim; to assert a right, claim) — 9th–12th c. CE. 5. Anglo-Norman clamer (to claim, call, demand) — 11th–13th c. CE; transmitted to English legal usage after 1066. 6. Middle English claimen (to call out, assert a right, demand) — 13th c. CE (attested from c. 1300). 7. English claim (noun and verb; an assertion of right or title) — 13th–14th c. CE. 8. English claimant (one who claims; formed with the agent suffix -ant, from Latin present-participial -antem, as in defendant, assistant) — 18th c. CE (first cited c. 1740). 9. English CLAIMANTS (plural of claimant) — modern standard form. Note on debate: the agent suffix -ant entered English through French and Latin legal vocabulary and is the same suffix found in defendant, assistant, and servant; the plural claimants is a regular English inflection. The PIE root *kel- / *klā- "to shout, cry" is uncontroversially the ancestor of Latin clāmāre, though the exact vowel grade of the root (klā- vs. kle- vs. kel-) has been debated; this does not affect the direct Latin → French → English line. The Middle English verb was spelled claimen and shows the regular Old French palatalization of /k/ before /l/, which produced the modern "cl" pronunciation preserved in claim and claimant.
