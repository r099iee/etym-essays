DERISION is contemptuous ridicule, mockery, or scornful laughter directed at a person, idea, proposal, or object judged absurd, worthless, or beneath serious attention. In the reading passage the word completes a crescendo of wild, high-pitched cries — "shouts or cries of excitement, triumph, or derision" — and it names the mockery that rises into noise: the collective jeer of a crowd turning laughter into a weapon. Derision is laughter with a sting: it is not the affectionate laughter of shared amusement but the pointed, often public laughter that dismisses its target, reducing a person or an idea to a laughing-stock, an object of universal contempt. The word is distinguished from mere "mocking" by its formality and its implied social weight: derision is the weapon of a crowd, an institution, or a contemptuous establishment, and to be met with derision is to be cut down publicly.

SENSES AND USES

  • Core sense: contemptuous ridicule delivered with laughter and scorn — "his proposal was met with derision", "a howl of derision". The word names the attitude and its audible expression together: the jeering laughter that belittles.
  • "To hold in derision": the formal, chiefly biblical idiom meaning to treat with contemptuous mockery — "the Lord shall have them in derision" (Psalm 2:4, King James Bible). The phrase preserves the older, more solemn register of the word.
  • "An object of derision": a person or thing universally and contemptuously mocked — a failed politician, a discredited theory, a social climber; the phrase marks the target as having lost all standing.
  • Deride (verb): to subject someone or something to derision — "he derided the proposal as nonsense". The verb is the active face of the noun and is the more common form in everyday elevated use.
  • Derisive / derisory (adjectives): "derisive laughter" is laughter expressing contempt; "a derisory offer" (chiefly British) is an offer so small or insulting as to be laughable — an amount that invites mockery by its absurdity.
  • Law and politics: in public debate an argument, policy, or figure is described as "an object of derision" when it has lost all credibility and is dismissed without serious consideration — derision as the rhetorical death of an idea.
  • Literary criticism: "derisive humour" and derision figure centrally in satire; critics distinguish the derision that aims to destroy a target's status from the genial comedy that merely entertains — satire uses derision as its sword.
  • Psychology and social science: modern research treats derision as a mechanism of social exclusion and status-lowering — laughter used to humiliate, bully, or police group boundaries; public derision on social media is described as a "pile-on" or "mockery cascade".
  • Register: the word is elevated and literary, standing apart from casual terms like "mocking", "taking the mickey", "slagging off", or "jeering"; it carries the weight of institutional or collective contempt rather than private teasing.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: Latin derīdēre "to laugh down, mock" is composed of de-, "down, completely", and rīdēre, "to laugh". The core image is of laughter directed downward at an inferior — the powerful laughing at the powerless, the crowd laughing at the outsider. The noun derīsiō turned this action into the abstract mockery; Old French carried it into derision; Middle English took it in the late fourteenth century as derisioun. Over the centuries the physical picture of downward laughter has been steadily abstracted into an institutional and rhetorical tool — the total social dismissal of a person or idea — which is why the word still describes not mere amusement but the deliberate public destruction of standing. The modern pronunciation /dɪˈrɪʒ.ən/, with its soft French-influenced /ʒ/, preserves the long journey through French that the word took on its way to English.
