- In Old English (gerecenian) it meant "to narrate, recount, enumerate, tell" — first of all a word about speech and story, not arithmetic: to reckon was to give an account of events.
- By Middle English (rekenen, c. 1300) the accounting sense took the lead: merchants reckoned their money, "reckoning" meant settling a bill, and the word was the property of shopkeepers, sailors, and stewards.
- In the age of sail it became navigation: sailors "reckoned" their position at sea, and "dead reckoning" was the captain's arithmetic of course and speed — a proud, practical sense the word still owns.
- In the 19th century it stepped indoors: speakers "reckoned" their opinions ("I reckon it'll rain"), and novelists, especially American and South-West English writers, made it the signature of a plain-speaking, self-taught voice.
- Today it is a two-faced word: in educated speech it survives mainly in "reckon with" and "to be reckoned with" (i.e., formidable), while in the mouths of farmers, sailors, and Westerns it remains the everyday word for "suppose" — ancient arithmetic and modern guesswork rolled into one.
