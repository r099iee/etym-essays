The chain that produces repasts begins at the deepest level of Indo-European with the root *peh₂-, "to protect, guard, tend" — the root of the shepherd's care, whose semantic core is the guarding of flocks, and by extension the feeding of them. This single root split into two great branches that still serve English today. In the Germanic branch it yielded the noun family of food (Old English fōda), fodder (Old English fōdor, the feed of animals), feed (Old English fēdan), and foster (Old English fōstrian, "to nourish, rear") — an entire vocabulary of nourishment that runs from the barn to the hearth, from the feed-trough to the foster-mother. In the Hellenic branch, Greek patéomai, "to eat, feed upon", shows the same root extending into the act of eating itself. It is the Italic branch, however, that gives our word directly.

Latin pāscō (pāscere) meant "to feed, graze, pasture" — literally to put animals out to graze, an inchoative verb centred on the herder's daily task, and a word whose root meaning is the protection of life by feeding it. Its past participle pastus ("fed, grazed") generated a remarkable family. Pāstor, "feeder, herdsman, shepherd", was borrowed into English first for the literal shepherd and then, through the Christian metaphor of the soul's shepherd, for the minister of a church (pastor); pāstūra, "pasture-land", gave the grazing ground itself; pābulum, "fodder", entered English as pabulum and was then extended to "intellectual fodder". All these words carry the root's original sense of protective feeding — of sustaining and guarding life by providing food. The same base underlies the English pasture and the pastoral tradition of literature and art, which idealises the shepherd's life and, through it, the root's ancient identity of feeding and guarding.

Our own word arises in Late Latin, when the prefix re- ("again") was added to pāscere to form repāscere, "to feed again, to nourish anew, to restore with food". The past participle repastus — literally "refed" — was substantivised as a noun meaning "a feeding, a meal, a refection", and from this the Old French noun repast (and the verb repaistre, "to feed") developed. English took repast into Middle English in the fourteenth century by way of Anglo-Norman. The semantic arc is instructive: where Latin pascere described the repetitive, pastoral act of feeding grazing animals, repascere added the notion of restoring — of feeding again, of taking food to renew one's strength — and the noun that emerged designated the regular occasions of taking food, the meal as a daily act of restoration. The prefix re- is the same one that appears in refresh, repair, and restore: the meal is the body's refreshment, taken again and again, day after day.

In Modern French repas simply means "meal", the ordinary word, with no elevation at all. In English, by contrast, the loanword kept a bookish dignity: adopted through Anglo-Norman and used in literary prose, repast never became the everyday word — meal, a native Germanic word, held that place — and so repast preserved a formal, ceremonious colouring that French never knew. It is that register Macaulay exploits. By writing of "the revolutions which have taken place in dress, furniture, repasts, and public amusements", he takes a word of the dining room and makes it serve the history of civilisation: every dinner table in English history is, etymologically, a pasture renewed, and the humble act of eating becomes a chapter in the progress of a people.

  • Sound change. The chain Latin pāscere → repāscere → repastus → Old French repast → Middle English repast shows the regular loss of the verbal endings and the passage of the Latin -sc- verb into a past-participle noun; no Germanic sound laws are involved because repast is a loanword.
  • Sibling words. Food, fodder, feed, foster (Germanic branch); pasture, pastor, pastoral, pabulum (Latin branch); and repast itself — all from the single root *peh₂-.
  • False-friend warning. Do not confuse repast (re- "again" + pascere "to feed") with repaste or repasture (rare); the simple form is the standard word, and the accent falls on the second syllable: re-PAST.
  • Literary attestations. Middle English religious and courtly prose; the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century essayists and novelists; Macaulay's History of England for the plural; and modern ceremonial or jocular prose where the word is used for deliberate effect.
  • Contemporary note. In modern use repast is consciously formal or old-fashioned, chosen for elegance or wit; the plural repasts, rare even in Macaulay's day, retains its power to dignify the ordinary — a word that refuses to be plain about the humblest, most human of acts, the taking of food.
