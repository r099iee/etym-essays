**GONDOLIN** (proper noun) — in the legendarium of J. R. R. Tolkien, the secret, hidden rock-city of the Noldorin High Elves of the First Age, built by King Turgon within the encircling mountain wall of the Echoriath and concealed for centuries in the hidden valley of Tumladen. The name is Sindarin for 'the Hidden Rock,' and it is the supreme byword in Tolkien's world for doomed beauty, supreme smithcraft, and the prestige of the Elder Days. **1. The city and its history.** Gondolin was founded when Turgon, guided by the Vala Ulmo, discovered the hidden valley of Tumladen in the heart of the Echoriath and raised there a city of white stone, with fountains, towers, and gleaming streets, built in secret to withstand the power of Morgoth. It stood hidden for nearly four centuries of the First Age, and its smiths forged weapons and works of art unmatched anywhere in Middle-earth. It fell at last through the betrayal of Maeglin, Turgon's nephew, and was sacked by Morgoth's dragons, balrogs, and armies — a catastrophe that Tolkien treats, in the legendarium, as one of the great tragedies of the First Age. **2. In The Hobbit (Ch. III, 'A Short Rest').** In the passage where Elrond examines the swords taken from the trolls' hoard, the name rings through the scene with elegiac reverence. Elrond reads the runes: the blades 'were made in Gondolin for the Goblin-wars,' and he names them — Orcrist, the Goblin-cleaver, 'in the ancient tongue of Gondolin,' and Glamdring, the Foe-hammer, 'that the king of Gondolin once wore.' Gondolin was destroyed 'many ages ago' by dragons and goblins, yet the mere mention of its name transforms the travellers' looted blades into heirlooms of a lost golden age. The tone is one of relic-veneration and sorrowful nostalgia: Elrond's 'my kin' makes the ruined city personal, and naming it consecrates the swords as blades of legend, fated companions of the quest. The word thus carries weight far beyond geography: it is a byword for doomed beauty, supreme craft, and the melancholy prestige of the Elder Days. **3. Polysemy and usage — the name's roles.**
  • Proper noun, singular, capitalized. Gondolin is a place-name with no plural and no common-noun use. It cannot be translated and it cannot be genericized: it names one irreplaceable city.
  • Metonym for Noldorin craft. In Tolkien scholarship and fan usage, Gondolin stands for the highest Elvish smithcraft of the First Age — the standard by which other weapons and works are judged. 'Gondolin-work' is a byword for workmanship that can never be equalled.
  • Emblem of the doomed golden age. The city's fall is so central to the First Age that Gondolin functions as a type of the lost civilization — beauty that could be lost, and therefore remembered with grief. It stands beside Nargothrond, Doriath, and the fallen realms of Beleriand as a name of elegiac power.
**4. Distinctions from related terms.**
  • Nargothrond: the other great hidden Elven realm of the First Age, hollowed beneath the hills by Finrod and destroyed by the dragon Glaurung; Gondolin was built above ground, a city of white stone within encircling mountains.
  • Khazad-dûm (Moria): the underground Dwarven realm, dug for treasure rather than for secrecy; Gondolin was raised for concealment and craft.
  • Valinor: the immortal Undying Lands across the sea, never destroyed; Gondolin was mortal and doomed — precisely the source of its pathos.
  • Stronghold / fortress / citadel: generic functional terms naming a defended place; Gondolin names a single, irreplaceable, legend-freighted city.
**5. Register and connotation.** In the wider legendarium, Gondolin is the standard emblem of the Elder Days — supreme Elvish craft, hidden glory, tragic fall. Its mere mention consecrates the swords that came from it, as Tolkien's prose makes explicit: Orcrist and Glamdring are not merely well-made blades but relics of a lost world, and their naming sets the tone of wonder and sorrow that pervades the scene at Rivendell.
