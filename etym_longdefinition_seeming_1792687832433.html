seeming (adjective, verbal noun, and archaic adverb) — Giving an appearance or impression of being true, genuine, or actual, though often possessing that character only ostensibly, conditionally, or in contrast to hidden reality; apparent, outward, ostensible. As a noun, it designates an outward appearance, external show, semblance, facade, or superficial guise, particularly one that conceals or diverges from internal truth or underlying substance. In archaic and literary English, it historically denoted personal demeanor, comportment, decorum, or propriety of conduct.

In literary and Romantic epistemological verse—exemplified with consummate subtlety in William Wordsworth's The Prelude ("the life / In common things—the endless store of things, / Rare, or at least so seeming, every day / Found all about me in one neighbourhood")—the word performs a delicate, philosophically sophisticated role. Wordsworth does not deploy so seeming with cynical skepticism to dismiss rural sights as fraudulent illusions. Rather, he celebrates the cooperative relationship between the human mind and external nature. The phrase acknowledges that while common hedgerows, cottages, and stones might appear unremarkable to an unawakened observer, they become endowed with rare, sacred wonder through the transformative "seeming" cast upon them by the poet's awakened imagination. Here, seeming represents subjective aesthetic enchantment—the profound power of human consciousness to illuminate reality.

The polysemous branches of seeming extend across multiple essential epistemological, dramatic, psychological, and linguistic domains:

  • Epistemological Appearance vs. Ontological Reality: Throughout Western philosophy, from Plato's allegory of the cave to Immanuel Kant's distinction between phenomena and noumena, seeming represents the foundational boundary of human sensory experience. It encapsulates the perennial philosophical dilemma: how does an observer know whether an object's sensory appearance (its seeming) accurately reflects its real, objective essence (its being)? A proposition may possess an immediate, compelling "seeming" of truth while remaining scientifically or ontologically false.
  • Theatrical Masking, Hypocrisy, and Renaissance Drama: In Elizabethan, Jacobean, and Renaissance dramatic literature, seeming served as the central dramatic trope of courtly deceit, duplicity, and hypocrisy. Characters frequently disguise villainy behind an outward "show of seeming". In Shakespeare's Hamlet, the prince famously repudiates the superficial rituals of mourning with the exclamation: "Seems, madam? Nay, it is; I know not 'seems'." Similarly, in Measure for Measure and Othello, the tragic tension pivots entirely upon villains whose spotless external "seeming" conceals inner malice.
  • Contemporary Epistemology (Phenomenal Conservatism): In 21st-century analytic epistemology, seeming has been formalized into a rigorous technical concept through the doctrine of "Phenomenal Conservatism" (pioneered by philosophers such as Michael Huemer). In this framework, a seeming (often divided into perceptual seemings, memory seemings, and intellectual seemings) is a conscious, non-inferential mental state wherein a proposition presents itself to the mind as true. Epistemologists argue that seemings provide prima facie foundational justification for human beliefs: if it seems to an agent that p, the agent is rationally justified in believing that p in the absence of defeaters.
  • Romantic Subjectivity and Imaginative Transfiguration: In the Romantic poetics of Wordsworth, Coleridge, and Keats, seeming is emancipated from moral condemnation. It is recognized as the necessary perceptual medium through which human beings experience emotional truth. What seems rare or beautiful to a lover or a poet possesses authentic psychological reality, demonstrating that human perception is an active creative act rather than a passive camera.
  • Archaic Demeanor, Comportment, and Propriety: In Middle English and Early Modern English, seeming retained its ancient connection to seemly ("fitting, becoming, decorous"). In this historical usage, a person's seeming referred to their personal carriage, dignity, modesty, and outward conformity to social manners (as seen in Shakespeare's As You Like It: "bear your body more seeming, Audrey").
