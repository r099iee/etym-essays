UNWONTED — "unusual, unaccustomed, not habitual; out of the ordinary for a given person or situation." In the supplied literary context, a character's face assumes an expression of "unwonted severity": the adjective signals that this severity is not part of the person's established behavioural repertoire, so the shift reads as jarring, ominous and psychologically disruptive. The word does the work of alerting the reader that a familiar temperament has been visibly displaced by something alien to it, and the emphasis falls on the breach of personal continuity rather than on mere statistical rarity.
  • Sense 1 — not habitual for a person (predicative): "unwonted to labour," "unwonted to praise" — the subject is not in the habit of something; this is the etymological core built on "wont" = custom/habit.
  • Sense 2 — unusual, unaccustomed, not usual (of things, events, moods): "unwonted severity," "an unwonted silence," "with unwonted energy" — the attribute departs from what is normally expected, in or of a particular subject; the focus is on deviation from the usual rather than on frequency.
  • Sense 3 — rare/archaic "unused, unfamiliar, unaccustomed (of a thing to an agent):" a thing described as unwonted to a person is unfamiliar to that person, e.g. an object "unwonted to his eye"; this object-taking sense is largely confined to older literary usage.
  • Sense 4 — occasionally, "without wont or custom," i.e. unprecedented, without precedent: a weakened, near-archaic extension where the word describes something with no established pattern behind it at all.
Polysemy, jargon and metaphoric shifts. Unwonted is essentially a literary and formal adjective; it has no technical or trade jargon senses, but its figurative career is rich. The literal, physical core is "not customary behaviour." From that base the word performs three documented metaphorical extensions. First, an internal displacement: because "wont" is the habitual self, "unwonted" describes the moment a person acts outside their own known identity — the semantic bridge is that of a person "out of habit," as a thing is "out of place." Second, an atmospheric transfer: from persons the word shifts to scenes and moods, so that a silence, a stillness, or a noise can be "unwonted" when it breaks the established tenor of a place (e.g. "an unwonted stillness hung over the city"); the bridge is the personification of environments as having their own habitual character. Third, a degree intensifier: in Victorian and Edwardian prose "unwonted" is frequently pressed into service as a dignified intensifier meaning "remarkable, striking, unusual in a conspicuous way," e.g. "unwonted splendour," "unwonted vigour" — the bridge is the slide from "not habitual" to "standing out from the usual." Historical bridges and register. The word was built in late Middle English / early Modern English from the negative prefix "un-" plus "wonted," and for centuries it belonged to ordinary written English meaning "unaccustomed, unusual." Its closest formal ally is the phrase "not wont to" (e.g. "as I am not wont to judge hastily"). As everyday speech lost the base word "wont," "unwonted" receded into a consciously literary, slightly archaic register, where it acquired the dramatic weight of a sudden rupture. It is now rare in conversation but common in narrative fiction, historical prose, and mock-archaic humour. Register is formal-to-literary; connotation is neutral-to-ominous depending on the noun it modifies. It is a grammatically normal adjective — attributive ("an unwonted calm"), predicative ("he was unwonted to flattery," now rare), and modifiable by adverbs ("highly unwonted" is marginal; "quite unwonted" is idiomatic). Related words. "Wont" (noun: custom, habit; adjective: accustomed — "as is my wont," "he is wont to grumble"), "wonted" (habitual, usual — "wonted haunts"), "unwonted" (the negated form), and the obsolete "wontlessness." Distinguish carefully from "unwanted" (not desired), "unwitting" (not knowing), and "unwounded" (not hurt) — all phonetically near but semantically unrelated. The mnemonic "UN-WONT-ed = NOT what you are WONT/used to doing" is exactly right and anchors the correct reading of the literary sentence above: the severity is not what the character is accustomed to exhibiting, which is precisely why the change alarms the observer.
