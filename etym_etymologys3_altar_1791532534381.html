The history of altar is a history of elevation. Latin altus meant "high" or "deep" — the same word, because a tall thing and a deep thing are both measured from a baseline — and it was the past participle of alere "to nourish", so that a high thing was literally a thing that had grown up. From altus Latin formed altare, the raised place of sacrifice, and from altare English took the word that now names the holiest object in a church.

The Latin background: ara and altare

Roman religion had two words for an altar. Ara was the ordinary term, used of the modest stone or turf altar of domestic and public cult, and it survives in English only in learned or poetic contexts. Altare (neuter, plural altaria) was the high altar, the great structure of official sacrifice, and it is the form that passed into the languages of Christian Europe. The distinction is not absolute — the words are sometimes used interchangeably — but it explains why Christian Latin preferred altare: the new religion wanted the solemn, elevated word rather than the everyday one.

The etymological debate

The origin of altare has been disputed since antiquity. The majority modern view derives it from altus "high", which fits the archaeology of the raised altar and the semantics of the altaria as a high place. A rival ancient and medieval etymology connected it with adolere "to burn up, consume by fire", the verb of burnt offering, which would make altare mean "place of burning" rather than "high place". The two explanations are not mutually exclusive in practice: an altar was both raised and burning. Modern dictionaries usually prefer the altus derivation but flag the uncertainty, and the honest position is that the word's prehistory is not fully settled.

  • Height hypothesis — altare from altus "high", naming the raised platform of sacrifice.
  • Burning hypothesis — altare from adolere "to burn up", naming the place of burnt offering.
  • Plural hypothesis — altaria may have been reinterpreted as a feminine singular in Late Latin, which would explain some Romance forms.

Christian Latin and the Vulgate

When the Bible was translated into Latin, altare became the standard rendering of Hebrew mizbeaḥ and Greek thysiastērion, the altar of the Temple and of the patriarchs. The Vulgate fixed the word in the liturgy, and the Latin fathers used it for the altar of the Eucharist as well as for the altars of the Old Testament. This double inheritance — Jewish sacrifice and Christian sacrament — gave altar its peculiar density: it is at once a place of blood and a place of bread and wine.

Old English and Middle English

Old English borrowed altar (also spelled alter) from Latin, probably through the Church, and the word appears in homilies, glosses and the translations associated with Ælfric. After the Norman Conquest, Old French autel introduced the form auter, which competed with the Latin-derived altar for centuries. Middle English texts use both, often in the same manuscript. The sixteenth-century revival of classical learning restored the Latin spelling altar, and it is that spelling which prevails today, while auter survives only in antiquarian and dialect use.

  • Old English — altar, alter, learned and ecclesiastical.
  • Middle English — auter from Old French alongside altar from Latin.
  • Early Modern — Latinising altar wins; the spelling becomes fixed.
  • Modern — altar is the only standard form, with auter archaic.

Sound history

The phonological development is straightforward but reveals a characteristic English change. Latin altāre had /al/ followed by a consonant. In Middle English this sequence underwent a change in which /al/ before a consonant (especially /l/ plus another consonant) became /au/, and later /ɔː/ — the same change seen in salt, talk, walk and falcon. The result is the modern RP vowel /ɔː/ in the first syllable. The second syllable weakened to /ə/, and the final -r is pronounced only before a vowel in non-rhotic accents.

  • Latin /alˈtaː.re/ — full vowels, rolled /r/.
  • Old English /ˈal.tar/ — initial stress, short final vowel.
  • Middle English /ˈau.tər/ — the /al/ to /au/ change begins.
  • Modern RP /ˈɔːl.tə/ — the modern long vowel and non-rhotic ending.

The Reformation and after

The sixteenth-century Reformation made the altar a theological flashpoint. Reformers attacked the stone altar as a symbol of the sacrifice of the Mass and replaced it with a wooden communion table, often placed in the nave rather than at the east end. Catholics defended the altar as the place of the real sacrifice, and the word itself became a marker of confessional identity: to say "altar" rather than "table" was to signal a belief about the Eucharist. The Anglican settlement oscillated, and the nineteenth-century Oxford Movement restored altar ritual and vestments, giving the word a renewed ceremonial charge.

Literary and cultural attestations

George Herbert's poem "The Altar" (1633) literally shapes its stanza like an altar, making the poem an offering. Milton uses altars in Paradise Lost for the false worship of the fallen angels. Nineteenth-century hymnody and Gothic revival architecture filled English churches with altars, and missionary expansion carried the word around the world. In the twentieth century the Second Vatican Council reordered Catholic churches so that the priest celebrated facing the congregation, and the altar became a more visible, communal table; at the same time the word entered secular rhetoric, where "the altar of" any cause names the thing to which other goods are sacrificed.

Modern usage

Today altar is simultaneously archaic and alive. It is archaic in its liturgical and architectural sense, which most speakers meet only in church or in historical writing. It is alive in metaphor, where it names any site of costly commitment, and in the comparative study of religion, where it is applied to the shrines of many traditions. The word has also acquired a domestic and therapeutic life: a "wellness altar" of crystals and candles is a small secular ritual space, and the phrase "altar" lends it seriousness. The old idea of elevation persists in all of these uses: an altar is still a place set apart, raised above the ordinary, where something is offered.
