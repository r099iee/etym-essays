ROOT WORD: recesses — the plural of the noun recess, “a small hollowed-out space, niche, or alcove; the innermost hidden part of a place or of the mind.” Single root: Latin recessus, with the prefix re- analysed as a separable morpheme but the word treated as one inherited item.
Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ked- “to go, to yield, to give way” (reconstructed; reflected in Greek khézein “to go, to ease oneself” and Latin cēdere): the root is widely reconstructed but rests on few cognates, chiefly Latin and Greek.
Stage 2 — Latin cēdere “to go, to move, to withdraw, to yield, to give way” (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE; Plautus, Caesar): the great Latin root of motion and concession, ancestor of English cede, concede, recede, proceed, succeed, secede, exceed.
Stage 3 — Latin recēdere “to go back, to retire, to withdraw” (attested 1st c. BCE; Cicero, Caesar): re- “back” + cēdere, a military and political verb of retreating and retiring.
Stage 4 — Latin recessus “a going back, a retreat, a retired or secret place” (attested from the 1st c. BCE; Pliny the Elder uses it of the hidden places of nature): the past participle of recēdere used as a noun — “a going back” becomes “a place to which one goes back,” a retreat, a hidden corner.
Stage 5 — English recess “a withdrawing, a retirement; a secret or retired place; a niche or set-back in a wall” (borrowed directly from Latin, first attested 1617): a learned borrowing preserving the Latin form.
Stage 6 — Modern English plural RECESSES (the form on this card): the plural specialised for hidden, deep, often shadowy hollows and for the innermost parts of a place or mind.
Notes on debate and nuance: The PIE root *ked- is widely reconstructed for cēdere, but its wider Indo-European cognates are thin, and some handbooks (de Vaan) prefer to anchor cēdere within Italic, flagging the Indo-European connections as less than certain; the root should not be asserted with false confidence. The “break / adjournment” senses of recess (school recess, parliamentary recess) developed in English from the “retirement, withdrawal, cessation” meaning of Latin recessus and diverged from the physical “niche” sense in the 17th–18th c.; both branches descend from the same chain.”
