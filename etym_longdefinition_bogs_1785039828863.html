BOGS is the plural of the noun BOG: a tract of wet, spongy, waterlogged ground where poor drainage keeps the soil saturated, so that water-loving mosses and plants grow and die in place, accumulating as layer upon layer of acidic, oxygen-poor peat. The defining paradox of a bog is that its surface lies — a bog can look like solid, level, sunlit ground, green and even gay with tall bright flowers, while beneath the living crust lies a deep, semi-liquid mire of partly decomposed sphagnum. Because the peat below is waterlogged and nearly free of oxygen, decomposition is glacial; a bog is not simply a pool of mud but a slow, ancient accumulation, growing perhaps a millimetre a year, that preserves whatever falls into it. This is why bogs feel both ancient and treacherous: they are archives of the past — preserving pollen, wood, and even human bodies for millennia — and traps for the present, swallowing whatever is heavy enough to break the floating crust. In The Hobbit the word appears in the wild, desolate country the Company crosses in the days after the trolls' camp, on the road between the Misty Mountains and the Last Homely House: "There were bogs, some of them green pleasant places to look at, with flowers growing bright and tall; but a pony that walked there with a pack on its back would never have come out again." The sentence is a small masterpiece of menace: it concedes the beauty first — the bright, tall flowers, the green that invites the eye — and then delivers the cold fact that this lovely ground will swallow a laden pony. The narrator's flat, homely detail ('a pack on its back') makes the horror concrete and personal, and the whole passage warns the reader that in Middle-earth beauty is not evidence of safety. The bogs belong to the same family of deceptive landscape as the trolls' friendly fire: inviting surfaces, fatal depths. Against its neighbours the word is exact — a swamp is a wetland often wooded and fed by rivers; a marsh is open, grassy, often tidal and firmer; a fen is alkaline peatland of sedges, drained with ditches and rich with reeds; a bog is the acid, moss-built, quaking kind, the least trustworthy underfoot of them all. Polysemy & secondary senses:
  • Ecological and scientific: the word anchors modern peatland science — blanket bog, raised bog, valley bog, quaking bog (a floating mat that rocks underfoot), bog garden (a deliberately acid, peaty border for insectivorous and bog-loving plants), cranberry and blueberry bogs (commercial wet fields), bog myrtle, bog cotton, sphagnum moss. Bog iron is the impure iron ore precipitated in boggy ground, worked since antiquity; bog oak is ancient oak darkened and preserved by centuries in the peat, prized for carving; a bog body is a human corpse preserved by the peat's acid and lack of oxygen, such as the celebrated Iron Age Tollund Man.
  • Everyday verb and adjective: to bog down means to sink, or figuratively to become stuck and unable to progress — a vehicle bogged down in mud, negotiations bogged down in detail; to bog is used for the act of sinking; bogged is the state of being mired. 'Bogged up' and 'bogged down' are routine in report and prose.
  • Slang: bog is British and Commonwealth slang for a lavatory or toilet, probably from bog-house, a privy built over a pit, with the mire-sense transferred to the humble convenience; bog standard (also 'bog-basic') is British informal for plain, unexceptional, run-of-the-mill; to bog off is rude slang for 'go away'.
  • Metaphor and idiom: a 'bog' of anything is a mire in which effort is wasted — a bog of paperwork, a bog of litigation, a bog of corruption; the image is always of sinking without progress, of ground that accepts weight and gives no firm answer. The verb form keeps the old physical truth: what is bogged cannot advance.
  • Geographical reach: the word appears across the place-names and landscapes of the British Isles, Ireland and North America (the bogs of Connemara, the Dismal Swamp country, the muskegs of Canada, which are sphagnum bogs under another name); in Scotland and the north of England a moss or a flow may be the local word for the same thing, and in German the corresponding word is Moor.
In short, BOGS names a specific and scientific kind of wetland — acid, peat-forming, waterlogged, quaking — and, by extension, any situation or substance that swallows effort and gives back nothing firm. The plural form is the ordinary one: bogs exist as scattered, treacherous patches of landscape, and the word's power in the novel lies in the gap between their flowery surface and their fatal depth.
