The biography of indulgently is the biography of a Latin verb whose own past is a blank. The starting point is Latin indulgēre, "to be kind, to be complaisant, to concede, to yield," attested from the early comedies of Plautus onwards, always in this warm, granting sense. Yet the verb's structure refuses to explain itself. It looks analysable as in-, "on, toward," plus a base *dulgē-, but that base never existed as a free word in Latin, and no convincing cognate has been found anywhere in the Indo-European family. The great dictionaries are unanimous: the OED calls its origin unknown, Ernout–Meillet declare the étymologie inconnue, and de Vaan lists it without an etymology. Over the centuries scholars have floated guesses — a connection to the PIE root *dʰerǵʰ-, "to make firm," which produced Sanskrit dṛṃhati (दृंहति) and the Hindi adjective dṛḍh (दृढ़), "firm," so that to be indulgent would be to "fix" one's favour on someone; a link to Greek θέλγω thélgō, "to charm"; a tie to Latin longus, "long" — but none has survived scrutiny, and the phonetics of each proposal stand against it. Indulgēre therefore remains a Latin-only root: a word whose family tree runs back to a single, unexplained branch.

That single Latin branch, however, grew into a dense thicket. The present participle indulgēns, "kind, forbearing," produced the noun indulgēntia, "kindness, leniency," and from the early Church came a momentous semantic extension: in ecclesiastical Latin indulgēntia came to mean the remission of temporal punishment for sin. It is this sense that carried the word across Europe — Old French indulgence, English indulgence (fourteenth century) — and that exploded into history in 1517, when the sale of indulgences to fund the rebuilding of St Peter's drove Martin Luther to his Ninety-five Theses. So while indulgent grandmothers smile, the same word family also funded cathedrals and triggered the Reformation; the modern phrase "to indulge oneself" descends from the same verb that the medieval Church used for pardons. The Romance languages spread the word still further — Italian indulgente, French indulgent, Spanish indulgencia — and the Germanic and Slavic languages took it only as church-loans, German Indulgenz and Russian индульгенция, which is exactly the pattern of a genuine loanword, not a cognate.

English received the word in three instalments. The noun indulgence arrived in the fourteenth century through Old French; the adjective indulgent followed around 1600, borrowed from French and ultimately from Latin indulgēns; and the verb indulge appeared in the 1630s with the sense "to yield to a desire, to gratify, to give free course to." The adverb indulgently was then formed natively in the seventeenth century by the ordinary English machinery of adjective plus the suffix -ly — the same pattern as kindly or softly. By the eighteenth century indulgence had acquired its modern moral profile: to indulge a whim, to be self-indulgent, to spoil a favourite with patient, smiling, yielding love. That is exactly the register the translator of Dream of the Red Chamber needs when Grandmother Jia "chided indulgently." The adverb says that her scolding is not enforcement but endowment — she is, in the old Latin sense, indulging the boy, conceding him his nature, yielding to him with a fixed, doting favour. A word of unknown origin has become the perfect name for the most recognisable dynamic of the novel: the grandmother who rules, not by fear, but by the infinite, granting patience of indulgence. The adverb's career thus passes from the unknowable Latin seed, through the Church's pardons and the Reformation, into the domestic comedy of a nineteenth-century Chinese great house — a four-syllable word carrying the whole history of mercy, from papal indulgence to a grandmother's smile.
