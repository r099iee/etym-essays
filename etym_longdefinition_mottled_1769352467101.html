mottled (adjective): marked with irregular spots, patches, smears, or blotches of a different color or colors; variegated, blotchy, or dappled in appearance, rather than uniform, smooth, or single-colored. Primary sense: describes any surface that displays uneven patches of different shades — an animal's coat or plumage, a snake's skin, a bird's egg, the peeling bark of a plane or sycamore tree, a stone, a leaf, or skin. It also describes the mottled skin of fruit, the wings of certain moths and butterflies, the weathered surface of lichen-covered walls, and the marbled paper of decorative crafts. In Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, "mottled, white, recumbent limbs" pictures the sycamore branches whose bark flakes away in patches, leaving a mottled white-and-cream surface — the word is chosen precisely to suggest organic, weathered variety rather than a smooth, solid color. Secondary and specialized senses:
  • Medical: mottled skin is skin showing blotchy, marbled, purplish, or patchy discoloration, typically from poor circulation, cold, shock, or certain conditions; the technical name for the netlike pattern is "livedo reticularis." Mottled enamel describes teeth with brown or chalky patches from flawed development.
  • Metallurgy: mottled iron (or mottled cast iron) is iron whose fractured surface shows a mixed, mottled pattern of gray and white regions, produced by partial graphitization during casting.
  • Light and shadow: mottled light, mottled shade, and mottled shadows describe the patchy alternation of light and dark, as of sunlight filtering through a leafy canopy onto the ground — closely allied to "dappled."
  • Biology and zoology: mottled coloration is a widespread camouflage strategy in animals and eggs; many species carry "mottled" as a formal part of their English names (mottled owl, mottled duck, mottled sculpin, mottled woodcreeper).
  • Figurative (rare but real): a "mottled reputation" or "mottled history" means a mixed, checkered, uneven record — partly good and partly bad, patchy in quality, just as a mottled surface mixes colors.
  • Textiles and crafts: mottled fabrics, papers, and dyed goods are deliberately produced with uneven color for a natural, rustic, or weathered look — the deliberate opposite of the accidental mottling found in nature, and a direct descendant of the original "motley" cloth.
Historical and semantic bridge: the word is a back-formation from "motley," the parti-colored, patchwork garment of the medieval court jester or fool. English speakers took the image of a surface stitched together from contrasting patches of cloth and, in the 1670s, created the verb "to mottle" (to mark with such patches) and then the participle adjective "mottled." A fool's costume became the general language of natural variegation: bark, skin, stone, light, and metal are all now "mottled" in the sense that they look as if assembled from irregular patches of different colors. Register: plain, observational, and descriptive; neutral in nature writing, though in medical contexts "mottled skin" carries a clinical and slightly worrying tone.
