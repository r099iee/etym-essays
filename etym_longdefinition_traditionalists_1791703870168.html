Traditionalists (plural noun; singular traditionalist) refers to individuals, theorists, cultural critics, or factional adherents who vigorously uphold, defend, and preserve established traditions, ancestral customs, institutional orthodoxies, historical doctrines, or classical canons against modernizing reforms, progressive innovations, or avant-garde disruptions. Far from representing passive nostalgia, the stance of traditionalists is typically conscious, philosophical, and militant, viewing accumulated historical practices as repositories of inherited wisdom, order, and spiritual coherence that must be defended against reckless experimentation.

The term operates across diverse intellectual, aesthetic, ecclesiastical, and political domains, demonstrating significant polysemic richness:

  • Academic and Literary Sense (Curricular and Canonical Conservatism): In the humanities, higher education, and literary theory—the precise domain referenced in the source text ("vex English studies")—traditionalists denotes scholars and educators who champion the traditional Western canon (e.g., Shakespeare, Milton, Austen), formal philological rigor, close reading, biographical-historical context, and standard linguistic grammar. They stand in adversarial opposition to avant-gardists, postmodernists, deconstructionists, and cultural-studies theorists who seek to deconstruct the canon, dismantle Eurocentric paradigms, or prioritize theory over textual appreciation.
  • Ecclesiastical and Theological Sense (Liturgical and Doctrinal Orthodoxy): In religious history and church politics, traditionalists refers to devout factions that resist theological revisionism or liturgical vernacularization. Within the Roman Catholic Church, Catholic traditionalists (such as the Society of Saint Pius X or proponents of the Latin Tridentine Mass) reject or heavily scrutinize the reforms of the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), insisting upon ancient Latin liturgy, scholastic theology, and strict adherence to historical dogmas. Parallel movements exist within Eastern Orthodoxy (Old Calendarists), Judaism (Orthodox and Haredi communities), and Islam (Salafi and traditionalist schools).
  • Political and Sociological Sense (Philosophical Conservatism): In political philosophy, traditionalists (often associated with thinkers such as Edmund Burke, Russell Kirk, and Michael Oakeshott) prioritize organic social evolution, intermediary civil institutions, generational continuity, and moral prescription over abstract ideological schemas, utopian social engineering, or radical revolution. They contend that customs embody the pooled, unarticulated wisdom of countless generations, which no single contemporary intellectual class can replicate.
  • Artistic, Musical, and Architectural Sense: In the visual arts, classical music, and architecture, traditionalists champions figurative realism over abstraction, tonal harmony over twelve-tone serialism or atonality, and classical proportion (Gothic, Renaissance, Neoclassical) over modernist brutalism or deconstructivist architecture.
  • Philosophical and Perennialist Sense (The Traditionalist School): In 20th-century metaphysics and comparative religion, the "Traditionalist School" (associated with René Guénon, Ananda Coomaraswamy, Frithjof Schuon, and Julius Evola) refers to a distinct esoteric movement positing the existence of a primordial, universal metaphysical truth (sophia perennis) that was once shared by ancient civilizations but has been catastrophically obscured by modern secularism and materialism.

The semantic bridge linking these various facets derives from Latin traditio, from the verb tradere ("to hand over, deliver across, pass down from hand to hand"). Traditionalists are literally those who position themselves as the faithful custodians of that which has been handed down, treating the heritage of the past as a sacred trust that cannot be compromised or bargained away to suit contemporary fashion.
