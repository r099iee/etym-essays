The lineage of unawares begins with the Indo-European root *wer-, one of the great roots of vigilance: it meant to perceive, to watch out for, to keep guard over. Its children are remarkably diverse. Latin turned the "watch out for" sense into verērī, to hold in awe and fear — the source of reverent and irreverent — while Greek built horā́n, "to see," from the same material. The Germanic branch took the root into Proto-Germanic as *waraz, an adjective meaning cautious and watchful, which produced Old English wær "aware, wary." Old English intensified it with the prefix ge- to form gewær, and the same formation runs through the sister tongues — Dutch gewaar, German gewahr — the word, in every Germanic language, for the state of having one's wits about one. 1. From perception to vigilance: the root's branching The root *wer- is one of the most productive in Indo-European, and the differences among its branches are instructive. The Greek horā́n built "seeing," the purest form of perception; the Latin verērī hardened perception into fear — to watch out for something is to dread it, and from that dread come reverent, irreverent, and the family of awe — and the Germanic branch built the state of being on guard, *waraz, the adjective from which English wary, aware, beware, and the loan-words guard, ward and warden ultimately descend. No direct Sanskrit reflex is securely attested for this particular sense of the root, and none is claimed; the Germanic, Italic and Greek branches are the firm cognates, and together they prove the deep Indo-European pedigree of a word that now survives in a small fossilised English idiom. 2. Old and Middle English: from gewær to unaware In Middle English, gewær had worn down to iwar and then aware, and the negative prefix un- was applied to it to make unwar, unware, unaware: the state of not being watchful, of having no warning. From the adjective came the adverb unware, meaning "without warning," and then, in the sixteenth century, English speakers added the genitive adverbial -s that had already hardened in always, needs and towards, producing unawares — "in an unsuspecting manner." The word was a favourite of the Elizabethans, used of ambushes, of falls, of death itself arriving without notice; Shakespeare writes of being taken unawares, and the idiom has never left the language. 3. The eighteenth-century re-formation The modern shape of the family was fixed in the eighteenth century, when the bare adjective unaware was re-formed from the adverb and settled into its modern role as a common predicate adjective ("I was unaware of that"). This created the clean modern division: unaware for the static state of ignorance, unawares for the sudden event of being caught in it. The two forms, which once overlapped freely, became specialised, and unawares retreated into the fossilised phrase catch / take / be caught unawares — a small, elegant survivor of sixteenth-century adverb-formation. 4. The senses in summary
  • PIE *wer- "to perceive, watch out for, guard" → the root of vigilance.
  • Proto-Germanic *waraz "cautious, watchful" → Old English wær, gewær "aware, wary."
  • Middle English iwar / aware, with un- giving unware / unaware "ignorant, off one's guard."
  • 16th century: genitive -s gives unawares "in an unsuspecting manner" — the Elizabethan word of ambush and mishap.
  • 18th century: unaware re-formed as a predicate adjective; unawares survives in the fixed phrase catch unawares.
5. Literary life The word is perfectly placed in Tolkien's sentence. The whole comedy and horror of the troll episode consists in the dwarves being taken unawares, one by one, in a rhyme of identical ambushes; Thorin, alone, is not. The ancient root of vigilance — the same root that gave Latin its fear, Greek its sight, and Germanic its wariness — is paid off in that single clause: the one dwarf who keeps his wits is the one who is not caught unawares. A word formed in the sixteenth century from an adjective rooted in Proto-Indo-European thus carries the whole moral of the scene — vigilance rewarded, inattention punished — in a syllable count of three.
