- [15th century: the imitative noise] - To rattle enters English as an imitative word for a rapid succession of short, sharp, clattering sounds — hard objects striking one another again and again, such as stones, shields, or chains.
- [16th-17th century: machines and the throat] - The verb attaches to rattling carts, chariots, and looms — the clatter of wheels and machinery — and appears in the fixed phrase the death-rattle, the final rattling sound in the throat.
- [18th-19th century: from sound to nerves] - Two extensions take shape: rattling as an adjective means brisk and energetic ("a rattling pace"), while "to rattle someone" comes to mean to disconcert or unnerve — the noise is transferred from objects to the nerves, giving "don't let it rattle you".
- [Contemporary sense] - Both senses stay alive: rattled still names the rapid, brittle clatter of metal or hard objects, while the colloquial rattled describes a person shaken, disconcerted, and thrown off balance.
