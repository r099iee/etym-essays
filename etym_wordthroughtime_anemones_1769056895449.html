- Late 16th century - 'anemone' enters English as the name of the windflower, a delicate garden bloom of the genus Anemone that shakes and opens in the wind.
- 18th century - naturalists extend the word across the boundary of kingdoms: the 'sea anemone' is named for the marine polyp whose crown of stinging tentacles mirrors flower petals, so the same word now names both a plant and an animal.
- 19th century - with the vogue for seaside collecting and rock-pools, the animal sense spreads into ordinary speech; 'anemones' in the plural evokes jewelled tide-pool treasure, nature's ornaments laid out for the visitor's eye.
- Contemporary - usage splits by habitat: 'anemone' alone usually means the flower, 'sea anemone' the animal, and both carry a connotation of ornamental, easily-tired-of beauty - admired, counted, and found wanting on a thirsty day.
