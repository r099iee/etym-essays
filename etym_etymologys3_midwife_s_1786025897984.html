ROOT ESSAY 1: MID ("with")

The first element of midwife is the Germanic preposition mid, "with," and its story is one of a once-ordinary little word that survives today chiefly as a fossil inside a handful of compounds. At the deepest reconstructable level, the preposition belongs to the Indo-European family of "togetherness" and "midness." The reconstructed root *medhi- or *me- (with, among, in the middle of) underlies forms across the family: Greek meta "with, among," and the "middle" root *medhyo- that produced Latin medius, Greek mesos, Sanskrit madhya-, and English mid, middle, and amid. Whether the preposition and the "middle" adjective are ultimately one root or two is debated in the handbooks, but the semantic neighbourhood is the same: being "with" people is being "among" them, in their midst.

In Proto-Germanic the form is reconstructed as *midi, "with, together with," attested across the family: Gothic miþ, Old Norse með (modern Norwegian med), Old Saxon mid, Old High German mit (modern German mit). English's mid is a direct continuation of this Germanic word. In Old English it was an everyday preposition meaning "with, in company of, among"—mid Godes gife "with God's grace," mid his gefēran "with his companions"—used in prose, law, and verse, and appearing in scores of manuscript texts from the Alfredian period to Ælfric and Wulfstan. Alongside it stood the related midd "middle" and mid as an adjective or preposition of "amidness," so that "with" and "in the middle" continued to shadow each other, exactly as in the parent language.

The revolution came in Middle English. As English shed its Old English inflectional system under the influence of Norse settlement and Norman French, the prepositional system was reorganised, and with—which in Old English had meant "against, opposite" (as in wiþ, modern withstand)—gradually absorbed the "accompanying" sense of mid. By the 13th and 14th centuries mid had become archaic in ordinary speech, surviving in dialect and, crucially, frozen inside compounds where its meaning was no longer felt as a separate word. That is how midwife preserved it: speakers had already forgotten that the first syllable meant "with," and the compound was reanalysed as a single word. The same fossilisation preserved mid- in midland (land in the middle), midriff (the "mid-belly"), and midstream, where the prefix behaves as "middle" rather than "with"—evidence that the two senses had merged again in the minds of speakers.

Sound changes along the chain are mild. PIE *medhi- passes to Germanic *midi by the regular Germanic treatment of voiced stops (PIE *dʰ > Germanic *d; PIE *m stable), and the short i of mid survives unchanged into modern English, immune to the Great Vowel Shift precisely because it is short and checked. The word's literary life in English runs from Beowulf's formulaic mid Ecgþēowe through the homilies and laws, Chaucer's faint uses, and on into modern poetic archaism—mid today reads as deliberately archaic, as in Keatsian diction. But its most important legacy is the compound midwife itself, which carries a whole prehistoric phrase—"the woman with (the mother)"—untouched inside an English word of the 14th century. In social terms, the fossilisation of mid is a small monument to the upheavals of the Norman period: a native preposition driven out of free use by a native rival, yet kept alive precisely where tradition matters most, in the naming of one of society's oldest female roles.

Etymological note: the traditional connection of "with" mid to the PIE "middle" root is generally accepted but occasionally questioned; if rejected, the "with" preposition would stand as an independent *me- root with an extended -d-. No secure alternative has been proposed, and most handbooks (OED, e.g.) treat the link to *medhyo- as probable.

ROOT ESSAY 2: WIFE ("woman")

The second element of midwife is wife, and in the compound it does not mean "married woman" at all: it means "woman," the old, broad sense of the word. Reconstructing the prehistory of wife is genuinely difficult. The Proto-Germanic form is *wībą, "woman, female person," attested all over the family: Old Norse víf, Old Saxon and Old Frisian wīf, Old High German wīb (modern German Weib, though now coarse in register); Gothic, remarkably, does not use it, preferring qino for "woman." Beyond Germanic the word has no certain Indo-European cognates: the older handbooks proposed a PIE *gʯībʰ- with the meaning "shame" or "pudenda"—the woman as "the veiled one" or "the shameful one"—but modern scholarship generally treats the origin as unknown, and the OED and de Vaan alike mark it obscure. This is the honest state of the evidence, and it is worth stating plainly: for all its antiquity, the word wife has no securely reconstructable Indo-European pedigree, and any claim of a specific PIE root must be flagged as speculative.

What is certain is the history within English. Old English wīf (neuter noun) meant "woman" or "female person," with no necessary implication of marriage: the Bible glosses wīf for "woman," and compounds like wīfmann "woman-person" (the source of woman) show that "woman" was the base sense. Beowulf, the Exeter Book, and the Anglo-Saxon law-codes use wīf freely for women both married and unmarried. Yet even in Old English the meaning was already narrowing: wīf frequently rendered "wife, married woman" in legal and social contexts, and by Middle English the "married woman" sense had become dominant, so that wife came to contrast with maiden and widow. The process is a classic narrowing (semantic specialisation): a general word for "woman" became the specific word for "one's own woman, the married woman."

Compounds froze both senses in place. The broad sense "woman (of a certain trade or type)" survives in fishwife (a coarse, loud woman—originally the women who sold fish), alewife, housewife (where the meaning hovers between "woman of the house" and "married woman"), and above all midwife, where wife unambiguously means "woman": the midwife is "the woman who is with (the mother)," named for her role at the birth, not for her marital state. The compound is first recorded in Middle English in the 14th century (midwif, c. 1300; midewif in later forms), and it is a superb piece of transparent naming: Anglo-Saxon speakers needed a name for the community's birth attendant and simply described her literally. That the modern English word still contains the old, broad sense of wife is a linguistic time-capsule of early Germanic social structure, in which a woman was a person of her own before she was someone's spouse.

From the compound noun came the possessive MIDWIFE'S, formed with the ordinary English enclitic genitive. The possessive sits naturally in fixed phrases—"a midwife's tale"—and here the semantic history doubles back: because the midwife was the custodian of traditional birth knowledge, "the midwife's lore" came to mean inherited, unverified folk wisdom, and with the professionalisation of obstetrics in the 18th century the phrase hardened into the dismissive "old wives' tale." The literary trail runs from Chaucer's Wife of Bath (a wordplay on the two senses of the word) through Shakespeare's use of "wife" and "women," and into the modern idiom where "midwife" has regained prestige as a medical and feminist term. Socially, the narrowing of wife tracks the changing place of women in English law and marriage—from the Anglo-Saxon woman who could hold property, to the married woman of the common law whose legal identity was subsumed in her husband's—and the word midwife stands as one of the few everyday relics of the older, wider female world.
