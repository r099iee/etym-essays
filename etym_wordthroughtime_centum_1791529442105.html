- 1890s — English comparative philology borrows centum from Latin as a diagnostic label for the western languages that keep a velar in the word for "hundred".
- Early 20th c. — "centum language" becomes a textbook category, often presented as one half of the oldest split in the Indo-European family.
- Mid-20th c. — the decipherment of Hittite and the discovery of Tocharian complicate the label, and its genetic status begins to be questioned.
- Late 20th c. — the division is reclassified as an areal isogloss, and centum survives as a descriptive convenience rather than a family-tree node.
- 21st c. — the word is confined to historical linguistics; a separate South Asian English centum means a set of a hundred verses or a perfect examination score.
