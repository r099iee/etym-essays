The history of vacillating begins with a Latin verb of the body. Vacillāre is attested in the Latin of the late Republic with the plain sense "to sway to and fro, to totter, to be ready to fall" — said of drunks, of old men, of buildings, of a flame in a draught. Even at this stage, however, the verb was already doing metaphorical work: Latin writers use it of a state that is coming apart, of a judgement that will not hold, and of a mind that cannot keep its footing. The physical and the psychological senses are not successive layers in Latin but twins, which is why English could borrow both at once.

The missing Indo-European root. Vacillāre is a lexical orphan. It has no convincing cognates in Oscan, Umbrian, Greek or the Celtic and Germanic branches, and no secure Proto-Indo-European reconstruction. Older etymological dictionaries occasionally linked it to vagus "wandering, unsettled" or to a putative root meaning "to sway, bend", but neither proposal accounts for the phonology, and modern authorities such as de Vaan record the origin as unknown. The reader should therefore treat any PIE root offered for this word with suspicion: the honest chain starts in Latin.

Into English. The verb vacillate is first recorded in English in the 1590s, in the physical sense "to sway unsteadily". It was one of the many Latin verbs that flooded educated English in the late sixteenth century, and it arrived with its past participle vacillatus and its present participle vacillāns attached. The spelling is conservative: the double ll of the Latin stem is kept in every English form, and the c before i is pronounced /s/, giving the modern British /ˈvæs.ɪ.leɪt/. The adjective vacillating is a straightforward English participle formation, the final -e of the verb dropped before -ing, and it is first found in the seventeenth century.

The seventeenth-century moral turn. The crucial semantic shift is not from Latin to English but from physics to ethics inside English. Once vacillating could modify mind, purpose, judgement and resolve, it stopped describing a body and started describing a will. Seventeenth-century divines and moralists found the image irresistible: the man who cannot stand upright is the man who cannot stand by anything. From there the word acquired its lasting associations — weakness rather than uncertainty, cowardice rather than caution, and a suggestion that the wavering is culpable because it damages those who depend on the waverer.

  • 1590s: vacillate borrowed as a verb of physical swaying.
  • 17th c.: vacillating established as a character adjective; the moral sense becomes dominant.
  • 18th c.: naturalised in political and diplomatic prose, where policies and ministries "vacillate".
  • 19th c.: a fixture of parliamentary abuse and of biography; vacillation becomes an abstract noun of character judgment.
  • 20th–21st c.: restricted to formal registers, increasingly felt as slightly archaic and therefore emphatic.

Literary and political life. The word's centre of gravity has always been public life. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it was used of cabinets, alliances and commanders, and it carried the specific accusation that a statesman had sacrificed a consistent policy to momentary advantage. In the twentieth century it migrated into journalism about leaders who reverse themselves under pressure, and into biography, where it remains a standard charge against figures such as Cicero, whose career — exile, recall, shifting alliances, and a final war to the death with Antony — offers unusually rich material for it.

Why it stings. Vacillating is more injurious than its synonyms because it fuses two accusations: that the person could not decide, and that the person had no core to decide from. It says, in effect, that the oscillation is not between two goods but between two expedients, and that there is nothing underneath. That is why the source passage can put it first in a list of insults and let spineless follow as an explanation rather than a repetition.

Modern usage. The word has no technical or scientific life; where precision about alternating motion is needed, English uses oscillating, fluctuating or intermittent. Vacillating survives as a high-register term of political and moral abuse, common in editorials, biographies and formal commentary, and rare in speech. Its force now depends on that very formality: to call a modern leader vacillating is to reach deliberately for the language of the eighteenth-century dispatch box.
