1. Imitative / onomatopoeic formation in early English: creak was created by sound symbolism, echoing the harsh, dry, grating cry it names. No single PIE root is securely established for it; the word belongs to a Germanic clan of /kr-/ + velar echo-words (crack, croak, crow, cark, screech) built on the same acoustic template. Possible kinship with Old English ċearcian "to creak, jar, gnash (the teeth)" (attested c. 10th century) is probable but the intermediate stages are not fully attested — see note below. 2. Old English ċearcian "to creak, to jar, to gnash (the teeth)" (attested c. 1000) — a member of the same onomatopoeic family, used of the grating of teeth; relationship to creken is debated. 3. Middle English creken "to utter a harsh cry" (attested c. 1300, of birds — crows, rooks) — the earliest recorded form of the modern verb. 4. Middle English creken widens: by the mid-15th century it is applied to objects — doors, hinges, floorboards, timber, ships — "to make a harsh grating or squeaking sound." 5. Early Modern English creake / creak, with the regular past tense creaked, attested from the 16th century onward; the spelling creak stabilizes in the 17th century. 6. Modern English CREAKED (the past tense of creak, whose headword form is creak) — present day; the concrete sense is unchanged, with figurative extensions (a creaking institution, creaky joints, creak through). Debated / nuanced note: creak is fundamentally onomatopoeic, so no discrete PIE root is agreed; the connection with Old English ċearcian (and with Middle English craken "to crack, resound") is highly plausible, but because echo-words arise independently in every era, the exact branching of the imitative family cannot be reconstructed with certainty. What is attested and certain is the form creken (c. 1300) and its uninterrupted continuation to modern creaked.
