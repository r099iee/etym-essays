AGROCHEMICALS is a young, fully transparent 20th-century compound, and its evolution must therefore be told as two converging histories — AGRO- and CHEMICAL — that fuse only after the Second World War. Both halves are ancient, but their union belongs to industrial agriculture, Rachel Carson, and the peregrine crisis named in the source context.

The first half begins with PIE *h₂eǵ- “to drive, draw, lead”, reconstructed by Pokorny and confirmed in LIV. The suffixed noun *h₂eǵros meant literally “driven ground”: pasture where cattle are driven, then any open field. It is one of the best-attested PIE landscape words. Sanskrit ajra- “field, plain”, Greek agros “field, countryside”, Latin ager “field, farm, territory”, Gothic akrs, Old English æcer “acre, field” all descend regularly from it. Latin ager is central: agrī cultūra gives agriculture, agrestis gives agrestic, agrarius gives agrarian. De Vaan stresses the Latin paradigm ager / agrī / agrum and its derivatives agricola “farmer”, agrimensores “land-surveyors”. Through Old French and learned borrowing, English acquires agriculture (15th c.), agrarian (1610s), agronomy (1814, from French agronomie ← Greek agronomos), agrostology, and finally the productive combining form agro-, used from the 19th century in agro-industry, agrobiology, agroforestry.

The second half has a more exotic passage. Greek Hellenistic texts use khēmeia / khymeia for the Egyptian art of metal-transmutation and dyeing. Medieval Arabic writers, translating and expanding Alexandrian lore in Baghdad and Harran in the 8th–9th centuries (Jābir ibn Ḥayyān corpus), call it al-kīmiyāʾ, with the definite article al- prefixed. This is borrowed into Medieval Latin as alchimia / alchemia in 12th-century Toledo translations (Robert of Chester, Gerard of Cremona). Middle English has alkamye, alchemy. In the 17th century, chymistry splits: chymic / chemical at first means simply “alchemical”, then, after Boyle, Stahl, Lavoisier and Dalton, “of the science of matter”. Etymonline dates chemical (adj.) to the 1640s, chemical (n.) to 1620s–1710s. The 19th century industrialises it: petrochemical (1880s), biochemical (1840s–1900s), electrochemical.

  • 1940s–1950s: agro- + chemical(s) coined in British and American agricultural journals to group synthetic nitrogen fertilisers (Haber–Bosch ammonia), organochlorine insecticides (DDT, dieldrin, aldrin), phenoxy herbicides (2,4-D) and fungicides. OED first citations cluster c. 1946–1954. The plural agrochemicals dominates because the term denotes a class of products.
  • 1960s: J. A. Baker’s peregrines collapse. Organochlorine agrochemicals biomagnify: sprayed on fields, concentrated in seed-eating birds, magnified in raptors, thinning eggshells via DDE interference with calcium metabolism. The Peregrine (1967) documents this. Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962) makes agrochemicals a moral word.
  • 1970s–1980s: DDT banned (US 1972, UK 1980s), dieldrin / aldrin withdrawn. Agrochemical acquires a pejorative tinge in public discourse while remaining neutral-technical in industry (“crop-protection products”). Companies — ICI, Monsanto, Syngenta, Bayer — are called agrochemical companies.
  • 1990s–present: semantic broadening and euphemism cycling. Technical sense: any chemical used in agriculture including fertilisers, pesticides, adjuvants, plant-growth regulators. Polemical sense: shorthand for toxic industrial farming versus organic. Regulatory language prefers “plant protection products” or “pesticides and fertilisers”, but agrochemicals persists in journalism, ecology and GCSE / A-level textbooks.

Phonetically nothing irregular happens: agro- /æɡrəʊ-/ + chemical /kemɪkəl/ with main stress on -chem- and plural /-z/. Orthographically the word is sometimes hyphenated (agro-chemicals) in early attestations, then solid. The organochlorine-based collocation in the context is diagnostic: it points to DDT-type compounds containing chlorine-carbon bonds, persistent organic pollutants, exactly the class that nearly extinguished the peregrine.

Literary attestation is thus unusually precise: Baker, Carson, and later Mark Cocker and Robert Macfarlane use agrochemicals to mark the hinge between post-war productivism and conservation consciousness. To understand The Peregrine, as the context says, one must understand that a word coined for progress came within a decade to signify global extinction risk.
