- c. 1300 — a twisted stick of flame: Middle English borrows Old French torche, literally ‘twisted thing’, for resin-soaked brands carried for light.
- Medieval — the castle and cathedral light: torches in sconces along stone walls; practical, smoky, essential — the pre-candle workhorse of night.
- Early modern — ceremony and menace: torchlight processions for kings and weddings, but also the night-time mob; Shakespeare's revellers and villains alike carry them.
- 19th c. — romance and empire: Liberty's torch (1886) and explorers' brands fix the torch as the emblem of enlightenment carried into darkness.
- 20th c. — metaphor takes over: Olympic relays, ‘passing the torch’, torch songs of unrequited love, and the electric torch (flashlight) that keeps the name while killing the flame.
- Today — mostly symbolic or recreational: tiki torches, Olympic flames, and metaphors of tradition — real flaming brands survive at ceremonies, campsites, and in fantasy films.
