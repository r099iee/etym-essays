- [Old English heafodland] - Compound of "head" (heafod) + "land", first used both for a strip of unploughed land at the end of a field and for a projecting end or point of land; a plain agricultural and topographic term with no special emotional colour.
- [Maritime sense (16th–17th c.)] - Shifts decisively to the nautical meaning: a bold promontory or cape of high land jutting into the sea; becomes a standard word in sailing logs and navigation, tied to coasting, seamanship, and the landmarks of inshore waters.
- [Geological & scenic usage (19th c. onward)] - Adopted into geography and geology as the technical term for an erosion-resistant coastal cliff bounding a bay; in scenic and literary prose (as in Prince Caspian) it carries a rugged, atmospheric register of sea-beaten elevation and the dramatic threshold between land and open ocean.
- [Contemporary sense] - Primarily a coastal-geography term for a high rocky promontory between bays; in everyday speech slightly formal and scenic, often used with a nostalgic or majestic tone in coastal, travel, and maritime writing.
