- 1870s–1880s Anglo-Indian military cantonment slang - borrowed directly from Hindustani phaṭnā ("to burst, split, snap"); originally an echoic exclamation for a muffled rifle shot, a misfire, or a bursting boiler.
- 1880s–1890s Colonial literary popularisation - introduced into general English print through Rudyard Kipling's tales of British soldiers in India; used for sudden deaths, silent gunshots, and mechanical failures.
- 1900s–1930s Golden Age idiomatic peak - firmly absorbed into upper-middle-class British colloquial speech; popularised by P. G. Wodehouse, Agatha Christie, and interwar humorists as to go phut, the definitive humorous term for broken engagements, defunct magazines, and failed schemes.
- Mid-to-late 20th c. Stylistic British badge - established across Commonwealth English as a nostalgic, mildly genteel idiom conveying comic understatement and fatalistic acceptance of mechanical breakdowns.
- 21st c. Contemporary literary currency - recognized primarily as a vintage stylistic flourish in British journalism and period drama, applied wryly to bursting tech bubbles, stalled political initiatives, and sudden appliance deaths.
