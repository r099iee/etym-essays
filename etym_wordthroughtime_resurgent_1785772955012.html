- Pre-English / Latin substrate - resurgere and resurgens name literal rising again and, in Christian Latin, resurrection; English readers later meet the root mainly through resurrection and surge
- Late 18th–early 19th century English - learned adjective resurgent enters elevated prose for phenomena, passions, or powers that rise again after decline
- Mid-to-late 19th century - political and national discourse (revolutions, restorations, 'risorgimento'-colored journalism) normalizes resurgent for parties, peoples, and movements
- Early–mid 20th century - economic recovery, military rebound, and ideological comebacks broaden the collocations; medical and biological writing adopt it for returning infections and populations
- Late 20th century - cultural criticism applies it to styles, genres, and subcultures thought dead; slightly formulaic news style begins
- Contemporary usage - default journalistic label for any forceful return (markets, nationalism, pathogens, sports franchises); perception is formal, analytical, and dynamic rather than intimate or colloquial; positive when describing 'our' revival, ominous when describing threats
