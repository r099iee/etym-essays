- 13th to 15th Century (Holy Roman Empire) - Originated as an occupational surname in Middle High German (*wagener*), designating a cartwright, wheelwright, or carriage builder across German-speaking territories.
- 16th to 18th Century - Established as a ubiquitous, ordinary family name in Germany, Austria, and Switzerland without any musical or theatrical connotations.
- Mid-19th Century (1850s–1883) - Wilhelm Richard Wagner transforms the surname into an international cultural lightning rod; his revolutionary operas (*The Flying Dutchman*, *Lohengrin*, *Tristan und Isolde*, *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, *Parsifal*) polarize the European musical world between fanatic disciples and fierce detractors.
- Late 19th Century (Fin-de-Siècle & Symbolism) - "Wagnerism" sweeps European literature, visual art, and philosophy; French Symbolists (Baudelaire, Mallarmé) celebrate Wagner as the prophet of synesthesia, while Friedrich Nietzsche moves from ecstatic veneration (*The Birth of Tragedy*) to caustic disillusionment (*The Case of Wagner*).
- Early 20th Century (Modernism) - Modernist literary titans (Marcel Proust, James Joyce, Thomas Mann, T. S. Eliot) absorb Wagner's structural leitmotif technique to build multi-layered polyphonic novels and poetry.
- Mid-20th Century (Post-WWII Reckoning) - The name undergoes severe moral and political re-examination due to Wagner's anti-Semitic writings and the exploitation of his music by the Third Reich.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Universally recognized as the benchmark of colossal artistic ambition and epic scale; his leitmotif system becomes the foundational grammar of modern Hollywood film scoring (John Williams, Howard Shore).
