Old English Possessive Construction - "Have to do" descends from the Old English habban tō dōnne, literally "to have [something] to do," meaning to possess business or tasks to perform — a possessive idiom of held obligations.
Middle English Dealings Sense - Middle English haven to don (with) extends the phrase from "tasks to perform" to "to have business or dealings with," so that what one "has to do with" is what one is concerned with.
Early Modern Relational Idiom - By the early modern period "have to do with" is fully grammaticalized as the relational idiom meaning "to be concerned with, to relate to," while the bare "have to do" keeps its older business-sense.
Contemporary Sense - Today "have to do with" is a standard, neutral idiom of relation, scope, and involvement ("what does that have to do with this?"), distinct from the unrelated obligative "have to" meaning "must."
