• Wolfgang Köhler: Similar because both were co-founders of the Berlin School of Gestalt psychology; differs because Köhler is most famous for his pioneering research on animal insight and problem-solving in chimpanzees (*The Mentality of Apes*) and physical Gestalten, whereas Wertheimer was the philosophical originator who discovered the phi phenomenon and formulated the laws of perceptual grouping.
  • Kurt Koffka: Similar because both were the primary triumvirate of Gestalt psychology; differs because Koffka served as the chief international evangelist and systematizer of Gestalt theory, publishing the comprehensive English-language textbook *Principles of Gestalt Psychology* (1935), whereas Wertheimer focused on visual psychophysics and creative thinking.
  • Christian von Ehrenfels: Similar because both explored the psychology of holistic form; differs because Ehrenfels was the nineteenth-century philosopher who coined the term *Gestaltqualität* (1890) while still believing sensations were secondarily bound together, whereas Wertheimer radically broke from associationism by proving Gestalten are primary and immediate.
  • Wilhelm Wundt: Similar because both were giant figures in the history of German experimental psychology; differs because Wundt was the founder of structuralist and associationist psychology whose atomistic approach (breaking consciousness into elemental sensations) Wertheimer's Gestalt theory was specifically designed to overthrow.
  • Kurt Lewin: Similar because Lewin was an early student and close associate of Wertheimer in Berlin; differs because Lewin extended Gestalt principles outward into social psychology, group dynamics, organizational behavior, and field theory, whereas Wertheimer remained centered on perception and cognitive problem-solving.
  • Rudolf Arnheim: Similar because Arnheim was a direct student and disciple of Wertheimer at the University of Berlin; differs because Arnheim applied Wertheimer's Gestalt perceptual laws specifically to film theory and visual art history (*Art and Visual Perception*), bridging Wertheimer's laboratory psychology with studio aesthetics.
