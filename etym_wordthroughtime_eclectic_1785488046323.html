- [17th century (1683)] - Borrowed from French as a narrow philosophical label for the 2nd-century AD Eclectics, who selected doctrines from many schools; strictly sectarian and learned.
- [18th–19th century] - Widens from philosophy to art, architecture, and taste: any principled borrowing across styles; approving in reformers, faintly reproachful in critics who saw it as lack of commitment.
- [19th–20th century] - Becomes a standard, neutral-to-approving descriptor of mixed but deliberate collections, music, reading, and methods.
- [Modern register] - Approving when self-applied ("eclectic taste"), faintly critical when it implies flitting without depth; still implies choice guided by taste, not randomness.
