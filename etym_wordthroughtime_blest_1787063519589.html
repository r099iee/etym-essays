- 8th–11th c. (Old English *blēdsian / blētsod*) - Pagan blood consecration to Christian hallowing: Derived from *blōd* (blood) to mean sprinkling with sacrificial blood; adopted by Christian missionaries to translate Latin *benedicere*.
- 12th–14th c. (Middle English *blessen / blest*) - Semantic fusion with joy (*blithe*): Influenced by *blithe* to mean endowed with divine grace and joyful earthly prosperity; past participle contracted to *blest*.
- 16th–18th c. (Early Modern & Augustan Era) - Poetic monosyllable: Spelled *blest* by Milton, Dryden, Pope, and Blake to enforce single-syllable iambic meter (/blɛst/) against liturgical *blessed* (/ˈblɛs.ɪd/).
- 19th c. (Victorian Era) - Colloquial idiom of astonishment: Adopted in British vernacular as a mild exclamation of bewilderment and surprise ("Well, I'm blest!").
- 20th–21st c. - Literary fantasy & modern hymnody: Preserved in classical literature (Tolkien's Gollum) and traditional hymns, while *blessed* remains standard for prose.
