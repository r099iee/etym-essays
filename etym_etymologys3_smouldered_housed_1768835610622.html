smouldered — the fire that refuses to blaze. The honest starting point for this word is a confession of ignorance, because its ultimate origin is unknown. The OED records no secure Indo-European etymon for smoulder; the reconstruction *smel-, 'to burn for a long time, to smoulder', proposed by Pokorny, is plausible but not accepted by all scholars, and no stage earlier than Middle English can be securely reconstructed. What is certain is the word's native Germanic texture: it belongs to the vocabulary of smoke and suffocation, the family that also produced smother (from Old English smorian, 'to suffocate') and, probably, smell. Because the earlier stages are unattested, they must be left open rather than guessed — this is a case where the attested record itself sets the length of the chain. What is securely attested begins in the fourteenth century with the noun smolder, 'smoky vapour, stifling smoke'. From the noun came, around 1300, the verb smolderen, and its first meaning was exactly what the noun implies: 'to smother or suffocate', to kill or choke with smoke — the transitive act of what a damp fire does to itself. Only around 1520 did the verb turn intransitive and acquire its modern sense: 'to burn and smoke without flame'. The semantic path is beautifully logical: smolder the noun named the stifling vapour; smolderen the verb named the action of stifling; and a fire that is stifled in its own vapour is precisely a fire that smoulders — burning, smoking, but never achieving flame. The word's own etymology is the physics of its meaning: a smouldering fire is one that suffocates. The word then had a curious career of disappearance and revival. It fell out of common use in the seventeenth century and survived mainly in poetry; it was revived in the nineteenth century — a revival that was itself a kind of literary smouldering, a return from beneath the surface. The figurative sense, 'to exist in a suppressed state; to burn inwardly', is dated to 1810, and it is the figurative sense that carries the word's modern weight. Anger smoulders, resentment smoulders, desire smoulders; disputes and feuds smoulder beneath the surface before breaking into open conflict. The metaphor is exact because it preserves the physics: a smouldering emotion, like a smouldering fire, is heat without vent, waiting fuel, potential flame. It is precisely this suppressed quality that Gormenghast exploits: when the carvings 'smoulder to ashes in obedience to the law', the language supplies the nuance of a fire stifled, choked, obedient — heat without flame, destruction without glory, a burning that is itself a form of silence.
housed — the dwelling that shelters. English house descends from Proto-Germanic *hūsą, 'dwelling, shelter', a word attested in every Germanic dialect — Old Norse hús, Old Frisian hūs, Old Saxon hūs, Dutch huis, German Haus — but of unknown ultimate origin: no certain cognate exists outside Germanic. The OED notes that a connection to the Indo-European root *(s)keu-, 'to cover, hide', has been suggested — the family that would also yield English hide and hut — but the link remains uncertain, and it must be stated honestly that the pre-Germanic ancestry of *hūsą is unknown. The Germanic record itself, however, is unusually secure, because the word is one of the most stable and central in the vocabulary of the family. In Old English, hūs meant 'dwelling, house, shelter', a word of the deepest domestic gravity: the house was the fundamental unit of protection and belonging in Anglo-Saxon society, the place of the hearth, of kin, of safety. From the noun, Old English formed the verb hūsian, 'to take into a house, to shelter, to lodge, to provide with a dwelling', attested from the ninth and tenth centuries. The earliest verbal sense was of persons: to house people was to put them under a roof, to give them the shelter that the noun named. Middle English housen continued the verb, and the extended senses grew naturally: from sheltering persons, the word widened to goods, seed-corn, tools and stores placed under cover for the season — the agricultural and industrial sense of protection against weather. By the early modern period the verb had acquired its institutional and architectural reach: to house a collection, an engine, an institution's possessions. In modern English the word spans the whole range — to house refugees, to house a museum's collections, to house a cable in a casing — but the core is constant: to give something a protective dwelling. The verb's distinction from its near-synonyms lies in that root idea of a home. To store is functional; to keep is colourless; to shelter emphasises protection against danger; but to house is to give a dwelling, with all the permanence, care and belonging the noun carries. Peake's sentence turns on exactly that distinction: 'the choicest were still housed in the Hall of the Bright Carvings.' The rejected carvings 'smoulder to ashes in obedience to the law' — they have no house, no protection, and their destruction is a stifled, honourable-less burning. The chosen ones are 'housed': they have been given the Hall as their home, taken into the keeping of stone walls, made inhabitants rather than objects. The two verbs are the sentence's antithesis — ash and sanctuary, obedience and honour, smoke and brightness.
Together — destruction and sanctuary.
  • Antithesis of outcome: smouldered = annihilation without flame, ash; housed = preservation with care, brightness.
  • Antithesis of agent: the carvings smoulder 'in obedience to the law' (external command); the choicest are housed in the Hall (external care) — both passive, both acted upon.
  • Antithesis of image: smoke and stifling heat versus stone walls and safe keeping; the law's fire and the Hall's shelter.
  • Etymology in service of theme: smoulder's buried sense of suffocation makes the obedient burning almost a silence; house's buried sense of the hearth makes the Hall a true home for art — the two words carry the whole moral geometry of the passage.
