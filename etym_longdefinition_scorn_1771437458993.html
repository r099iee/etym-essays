Scorn is the contemptuous dismissal of a person or thing as worthless, a feeling of derisive contempt that combines intellectual judgment (this is beneath me) with an emotional charge of disgust and mockery. In the literary context of this card, the word appears in the archaic idiom to laugh to scorn, describing the fate the young woman fears if she makes a social blunder: "She feared that if she put a foot wrong, the high-society ladies would surely laugh her to __________." The scene is built on anxiety. She dreads that public laughter will be converted into a verdict of worthlessness, a ceremonial derision that strips the victim of dignity before the assembled company. The tone is one of tremulous, self-conscious social dread, and scorn supplies the note of finality: scorn is a judgment, not a jest. It is the weapon of a group that considers itself superior, and the very formulation laugh to scorn — an old, formulaic expression — suggests a ritualised, almost theatrical humiliation, as though the ladies would not merely laugh at her but laugh her out of the circle of respectability altogether. In a world where reputation is everything, to be laughed to scorn is a social death sentence.

Scorn is a richly polysemous word, and its senses fall into several distinct groups:

  • The feeling of contempt — the core noun sense: a strong negative attitude toward someone or something judged worthless or despicable. It is stronger and more emotional than mere disapproval; it carries an active desire to belittle.
  • The act of derision — the outward expression of that feeling: mockery, jeering, open contemptuous laughter. In the idiom to laugh to scorn the act is public and theatrical.
  • As a verb: to regard as worthless — "to scorn" means to feel or express contempt for someone or something, e.g. "she scorned his advice."
  • As a verb: to reject or refuse with contempt — "he scorned the offer" means he rejected it disdainfully, refusing to accept something he regards as beneath him.
  • As a verb: to be too proud to do something — "she scorned to beg" means she considered begging beneath her dignity. This sense, common in older and literary English, sits between refusal and pride.
  • Figurative and idiomatic senses — "to pour scorn on" an idea or person (to heap contempt upon it), "to think scorn of" (archaic, to regard with contempt), "a scorn" as a person or thing that is an object of contempt (now rare).
  • Derived forms — scornful (feeling or showing scorn), scornfully (adverb), scorner (a person who scorns, especially in biblical diction: "the scorner" as one who mocks wisdom).

The semantic history explains why the word carries such heat. Its ultimate ancestor is an Indo-European root meaning "to cut," and the oldest Germanic stage of the word is reconstructed as "to cut at, to mock" — the idea being that mockery cuts into a person's dignity like a blade. The word travelled from Germanic into Old French (escarn, escharnir, "mockery, to mock"), and English borrowed it from French around 1200. From the start English scorn meant public derision, but the word gradually internalised the feeling: by the Renaissance it names both the sneer on the face and the contempt in the heart, and it acquires its moral register — the scorn of the righteous for the base, the hero's scorn for the coward. In Shakespeare, scorn is a staple of tragic and comic dialogue alike; in the Authorized Version of the Bible, "scorner" is a fixed character type. Modern English keeps all of this alive: the word remains vigorous in formal prose, in journalism ("to pour scorn on"), and in the idiom preserved by this card.

Modern technical and specialised senses are modest but real. In rhetoric and literary criticism, "scorn" names a tone of lofty contempt that distances the speaker from the object; in psychology, contempt is studied as one of the basic moral emotions, and scorn is its most theatrical, publicly performed form. In social commentary, "class scorn" or "intellectual scorn" describes the contempt that groups with pretensions to superiority direct at those they judge beneath them — exactly the social mechanism dramatised by the ladies in the sentence of this card.
