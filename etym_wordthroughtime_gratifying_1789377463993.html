- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Classical & Late Latin) - Classical adjective *grātus* meant pleasing, dear, acceptable, or thankful; developed in Late Latin into the compound verb *grātificārī*, meaning to do a favor, oblige, or please someone.
- Mid-16th Century (c. 1540–1560) - Borrowed into Tudor English via Middle French *gratifier*, originally meaning to bestow a gift, grant a favor, or remunerate someone with a gratuity.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Restoration Eras) - Semantic shift from physical gift-giving to psychological pleasure; *to gratify* meant to indulge a desire, appetite, or whim, or to give mental pleasure.
- 18th to 19th Century (Georgian & Victorian Eras) - The participial adjective *gratifying* achieves widespread standard usage, characterizing events, honors, or compliments that bring deep satisfaction to one's dignity or pride (Oscar Wilde, Jane Austen).
- 20th to 21st Century - Cemented in psychological science (e.g., *instant vs. delayed gratification*) and professional discourse as the premier adjective for validating, fulfilling accomplishments and deeply pleasing outcomes.
