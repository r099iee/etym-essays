The story of candelabra begins with white heat. The PIE root *kand- signified intense, white-hot shining — not ordinary daylight but the blaze of fire at full brilliance or the gleam of a polished surface — and from it two great branches of Indo-European built their languages of light.

Stage one — the root and its Indo-Iranian moon: In Indo-Iranian the root went to the night sky: Sanskrit candrá- means “shining” and became the word for the moon itself, and from it descend Hindi chand and Odia candra, the moon-names of the modern subcontinent — the white-hot body of the night. This branch matters to the European story only as a distant cousin, but it shows the root's tendency to name the brightest shining thing in a given landscape, whether the moon above or the flame below. The semantic principle is the same in both branches: *kand- names whatever shines at full whiteness, and each culture applied it to the brightest body in its world.

Stage two — Latin and the family of whiteness: In the Italic branch the same root produced Latin candēre, “to be white-hot, to glow with whiteness”, and its family grew luxuriantly. From candēre came candor, “whiteness, radiance”, which English took for moral brightness, candor; and candidus, “pure white”, which gave English candid, “frank”, because what is white is unstained — and candidate, since the Roman seeker of office wore a bleached white toga, candidātus, “clothed in white”. The moralising of whiteness is a constant in this family: Roman culture prized the unblemished white as the colour of truth and of office, and the shine-root carried its radiance into ethics. The root's most material descendant is candēla, “a candle”, the small body of white flame; the word travelled through church Latin into every European language — Old English candel, English candle, French chandelle — and for a millennium the candle was the unit of human light, the standard by which hours were told and rooms were lit.

Stage three — the candelabrum: From candēla, Latin formed candelabrum with the instrumental suffix -brum (as in arātrum, “plough”, and labrum, “basin”), meaning literally “instrument for candles”: at first a simple stick or holder for a single flame, then, under the influence of Hellenistic luxury and Roman banqueting culture, a branched pedestal or hanging frame for many flames at once. The branched form was a material statement of wealth and ceremony — the more flames, the more light, the more power — and the Roman candelabrum, standing in the atrium or hanging in the triclinium, was the ancestor of every chandelier and candelabra in the Western world. Roman inventories and Pompeian wall-paintings attest the branched form as a standard item of elite domestic furniture; Pliny and Martial mention the costly candelabra of the empire. This is the very object Peake suspends from his ceiling, and the classical pedigree of the word is part of its tone: a candelabra is a Roman inheritance, not a modern convenience.

Stage four — the English borrowing and the grammatical accident: English took the word in the sixteenth century, and then a grammatical accident occurred that is the word's most famous feature. Candelabra is the Latin neuter plural of candelabrum, and English first used it as a plural; but because the form ended in -a, like feminine singulars (umbrella, arena), speakers reanalysed it — candelabra became a singular noun, and candelabras its new plural, a back-formation preserved in the dictionaries to this day. The strict singular candelabrum remains the term of art, so modern English holds three forms in uneasy coexistence: the Latinate plural candelabra, the singular candelabrum, and the vernacular singular candelabra with its new plural candelabras. Peake exploits exactly this instability when he writes of “seven great candelabra suspended from the ceiling”: the form is classically plural, ceremonially Latinate, and yet reads naturally as a modern singular collective — the seven fixtures become almost one great hanging constellation.

Stage five — the modern re-registering: In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, electric light stripped the candelabra of utility and left it pure atmosphere: candlelit rituals, film-noir menace, festive grandeur, the silver table centrepiece of dinners and weddings. The word now conjures an antique, ritual, slightly Byronic romance, and it has also entered technical registers — in electrical engineering, a “candelabra base” is a small screw fitting for chandelier bulbs, preserving the object's name in the industry that replaced it. In anatomy, “candelabra” names branching structures of vessels and nerve-cells, extending the branched-stand image into the very small. The word's long arc thus runs from the white-hot PIE blaze, through the Roman banquet, into the church and the ballroom, and finally into the museum-case of period prose — where, in Gormenghast, it lights the eternal vigilance of a single dusty curator, and where its plural form carries the whole Roman, ceremonial, architectural weight of the history that built it.
