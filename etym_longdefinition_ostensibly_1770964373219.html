The adverb ostensibly means "on the face of it; apparently; as far as appearances show" — and, crucially, it is almost always used with the implication that the appearance may be misleading. When someone does something ostensibly for one reason, the reader or hearer is invited to suspect that the real motive lies elsewhere. It is the adverb of the pretext and the polite fiction: the declaration, cover story, or stated purpose that may not be the true one. In the source sentence — "ostensibly on the subject of the moon, but really about the ambition he had been at some pains to conceal" — the word draws a precise line between the public surface and the private truth: the quatrain appears to praise the moon while actually voicing Yu-cun's concealed ambition.

The word's anatomy reveals the metaphor inside it. Ostensible is formed on the Latin verb ostendere, "to stretch out in front of, show, display" — literally, to hold something forward where it can be seen. Something ostensible is therefore "that which can be shown", the surface layer presented to view, as opposed to whatever may lie behind it. The adverb simply applies that idea to manner or purpose: an action done ostensibly in one way is done "in a showable manner", on the surface, for the appearance. The suffix -ly is the ordinary English adverb-forming suffix attached to ostensible.

Core senses:
  • Seemingly, apparently, to all appearances.
  • Professedly, allegedly: as publicly declared or claimed, often with doubt implied.
Exhaustive polysemy and modern senses:
  • Neutral descriptive use: "as appears to be the case", without necessarily implying deception (e.g. "the room was ostensibly empty"). In this reading the word simply reports the surface.
  • Skeptical, journalistic use — the dominant modern sense: "purportedly, under the pretext of", signalling that the stated reason is probably a cover for a real one. This is standard in news reporting, legal writing, and political commentary, where "ostensibly" flags the gap between official story and probable truth.
  • Formal-corporate and diplomatic use: in official documents and communiqués, ostensibly separates declared intent from actual practice; it is the adverb of the unreliable official narrative, used with studied courtesy.
  • Scholarly and historiographical use: employed to separate the professed meaning of a text, custom, or policy from its function — e.g. "a ritual ostensibly about harvest, actually about social hierarchy".
Historical bridge: the whole family goes back to Latin ostendere "to stretch out in front of, show, display", compounded of obs-/ob- "towards, in front of" and tendere "to stretch", from PIE *ten- "to stretch". From the participle ostēnsus, Latin formed ostēnsibilis "that may be shown", and French built ostensible (seventeenth century), which English borrowed in the eighteenth century with the meaning "apparent, professed, put forward for show". The adverbial form inherited the idea that what is shown may differ from what is real, and by the nineteenth century ostensibly had crystallised as the standard word for "apparently but not necessarily really". The mnemonic — "osten-sibly: giving an ostentatious appearance" — captures the show-and-display idea at the root, linking the word to ostentatious through the same ostendere.

Register and usage:
  • Formal; extremely common in journalism, law, and academic prose; rare in casual speech.
  • Roughly interchangeable with apparently, seemingly, allegedly, purportedly, professedly, though each has its own nuance of doubt or neutrality; ostensibly is the most "diplomatic" of the set, implying doubt without accusing.
