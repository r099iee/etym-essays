• Cuirass: Similar because both refer to rigid body armor shielding the torso; differs because a cuirass strictly denotes the complete two-piece defensive harness consisting of BOTH the front breastplate and the matching backplate buckled together, whereas a breastplate refers specifically to the front pectoral plate.
  • Plastron: Similar because both protect the front thoracic area and denote ventral armor; differs because plastron is primarily used in modern fencing as a padded underarm chest protector, or in zoology for the ventral shell of a turtle, whereas breastplate carries heavy historical, medieval, and cavalry armor associations.
  • Gorget: Similar because both are pieces of medieval steel armor defending the upper torso; differs because a gorget is a curved collar protecting the throat, neck, and clavicle region, sitting just above the breastplate rather than shielding the chest and abdomen.
  • Pectoral: Similar because both designate ornamental, protective, or ceremonial plates worn over the breast; differs because pectoral is a broader Latinate term frequently used for ancient Egyptian and pre-Columbian gold chest ornaments or religious vestments, lacking the martial steel-plate combat focus of breastplate.
  • Body armor: Similar because both provide defensive ballistic or melee trauma protection to the torso; differs because body armor is a generic, modern category encompassing Kevlar vests, tactical flak jackets, and composite ceramic strike plates, whereas breastplate evokes traditional metallic or leather rigid plates.
  • Hauberk: Similar because both protected the knight's torso in combat; differs because a hauberk is a flexible, long tunic constructed of interlinked chainmail rings extending to the thighs, whereas a breastplate is a rigid, shaped solid plate of hammered steel.
  • Carapace: Similar because both represent a hard, unyielding protective shell over vital organs; differs because carapace is primarily a biological term for the dorsal exoskeleton of crustaceans or chelonians (turtles), while breastplate is an engineered human garment or ventral plate.
  • Brigandine: Similar because both were medieval chest armors worn by soldiers; differs because a brigandine consists of hundreds of small overlapping steel plates riveted inside a heavy canvas or velvet jacket, whereas a breastplate is a single (or few) large, solid contoured plate.
  • Aegis: Similar because both represent defensive protection for the chest and carry rich mythological/moral symbolism; differs because the aegis is a legendary divine animal-hide cape or shield associated with Athena and Zeus, functioning today almost purely as a metaphor for divine or institutional patronage.
  • Corselet: Similar because both refer to light body armor protecting the torso; differs because a corselet was a lightweight 16th-century infantry armor worn by pikemen, often encompassing light breast, back, and tassets as a set.
