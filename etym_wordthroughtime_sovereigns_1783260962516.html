- [Medieval 'supreme'] - In its current form since Middle English, 'sovereign' meant supreme - a ruler whose authority stood above the law, at first of God, then of kings.
- [17th-century constitutional contest] - In the 1600s the plural 'sovereigns' became the charged word for kings who claimed prerogative above parliament - the very term over which the constitutional struggle was fought.
- [National sovereignty] - From the 18th century the word expanded from persons to the state: national sovereignty, popular sovereignty - the people, not the prince, as the ultimate authority.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'sovereigns' still names monarchs in formal historical prose, while 'sovereign' chiefly denotes the independent authority of a nation or the state itself.
