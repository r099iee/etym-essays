Gazette is a noun and transitive verb situated at the intersection of official state governance, public legal record-keeping, and the historical development of print journalism. In its primary modern institutional sense, a gazette is an official newspaper or statutory journal issued by a monarch, government department, parliament, or public authority to record legal enactments, royal proclamations, civil appointments, military promotions, state honours, public notices, and declarations of insolvency. Beyond this strictly sovereign and bureaucratic sphere, the term also functions as a common proper or generic title for independent commercial newspapers, literary reviews, and institutional academic circulars, carrying an aura of traditional prestige, civic consequence, and journalistic integrity.

In Oscar Wilde's satirical fairy tale "The Remarkable Rocket"—from which the source passage is drawn—the "Court Gazette" serves as the official organ through which royal appointments and ceremonial honours are publicly proclaimed to the realm. The page boy receives a nominal court elevation with great solemnity; yet because it confers zero salary, Wilde employs the formal announcement in the Court Gazette to satirize the empty pageantry of aristocratic institutions, where hollow public prestige in an official bulletin is substituted for material compensation.

The term displays extensive polysemy, subtle distinctions of register, and specialized technical applications across varied domains:

  • Official State Journal of Record (Governmental & Legal Sense): The official statutory bulletin published by a government or crown authority to communicate binding public announcements. In the United Kingdom and Commonwealth realms, The London Gazette (the oldest continuously published English newspaper, established in 1665), The Edinburgh Gazette, The Belfast Gazette, and The Gazette of India serve as the primary legal medium for publishing statutory instruments, insolvency orders, winding-up petitions, royal warrants, changes of name, and honours lists. What is published in the gazette carries official evidentiary status in courts of law.
  • Official Military and Civil Promotion / Notification (Verbal Sense): As a transitive verb (predominantly in British and Commonwealth English, almost invariably encountered in the passive voice: to be gazetted), it signifies to formally announce or record an officer's military commission, promotion, decoration, or retirement in an official gazette (e.g., "he was gazetted to the rank of Captain in the Royal Fusiliers"). By extension, it historically applied to legal bankruptcy: to be "gazetted as a bankrupt" was to have one's financial ruin published in the official register for creditors to inspect.
  • Commercial and Metropolitan Newspaper Title: A title adopted by numerous independent newspapers, magazines, and periodicals worldwide, signifying an informative, reliable chronicler of daily or weekly occurrences (e.g., The Montreal Gazette, The Harvard Gazette, The Arkansas Gazette, and The Literary Gazette).
  • Institutional and Academic Bulletin: In collegiate and corporate contexts, a regular internal publication recording university senate proceedings, faculty appointments, curriculum changes, and major administrative decrees.
  • Historical Venetian News-Sheet (Archaic Sense): The sixteenth-century handwritten notices of political, military, and commercial intelligence distributed in Venice, known as avvisi or fogli d'informazione, sold or made accessible to the public for the price of a small coin called a gazeta.

The semantic bridge linking an Italian Renaissance coin to modern statutory government records constitutes one of the most celebrated metonymic shifts in European lexicography. In mid-sixteenth-century Venice, handwritten news summaries regarding the Ottoman wars in Dalmatia and Cyprus were offered to curious citizens for the charge of one Venetian copper coin (a gazeta). Through metonymy, the payment transferred to the object purchased: citizens began referring to the news-sheet itself as a gazeta or gazzetta. When Cardinal Richelieu and Théophraste Renaudot established France's first weekly political journal in 1631 under the title La Gazette, they codified the term as a state-sanctioned medium of public communication. In 1665, when King Charles II relocated his court to Oxford during the Great Plague of London, the founding of The Oxford Gazette (later The London Gazette) permanently cemented the term in English as the sovereign's authoritative instrument of legal record and royal publication.
