- Middle English (c. 1400–1450) - Formed as a compound noun watercours (combining native Germanic water with Old French cours), appearing in municipal property deeds, monastic drainage records, and agricultural land surveys to designate boundary streams and mill races.
- 16th to 17th Century - Established as a formal legal term in English common law under riparian doctrine, designating a recognized flowing body of water that landowners could not unlawfully obstruct, divert, or foul.
- 18th to 19th Century - Expanded into civil engineering, hydrology, and geography during the Industrial Revolution and canal-building era, denoting both natural riverine systems and constructed drainage canals, aqueducts, and industrial sluices.
- 20th Century to Present - Codified in environmental law, urban hydrology, and ecological conservation (e.g. Clean Water Acts) to define protected aquatic habitats, runoff conduits, and subterranean karst drainage channels.
