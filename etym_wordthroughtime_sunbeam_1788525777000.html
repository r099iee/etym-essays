- [Old English: c. 800–1000] - Old English *sunnebēam* appears in poetry and biblical glosses, denoting a literal ray of sunlight or the miraculous pillar of light guiding the Israelites - [connotation: sacred divine majesty, celestial radiance, and spiritual salvation]
- [Middle English: c. 1150–1450] - Middle English *sunnebem* / *sonne-beme* transitions into naturalistic and love poetry, describing morning light streaming through windows and the beauty of courtly ladies - [connotation: romantic freshness, spring renewal, and natural illumination]
- [Early Modern English: 1500–1750] - Standardized as *sunbeam* in Renaissance and Enlightenment literature; utilized by Shakespeare, Milton, and Swift (who satirizes extracting sunbeams from cucumbers) - [connotation: vivid natural brilliance, divine geometry, and playful philosophical metaphors]
- [19th c.–Present] - Victorian culture popularizes the figurative sense of a cheerful child ("little sunbeam") in hymns and domestic fiction; Oscar Wilde uses it in fairy tales for transient beauty; modern science and daily speech retain it as the quintessential image of atmospheric light - [connotation: cheerful warmth, nostalgic innocence, and ethereal beauty]
