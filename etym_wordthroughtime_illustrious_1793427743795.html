- 1560s — Humanist Renaissance borrowing: adopted into Early Modern English from Latin illūstris via French illustre, initially celebrating the luminous brilliance of ancient Roman heroes, sovereigns, and celestial bodies.
- 17th c. — Baroque titles and metaphysical luster: applied as a formal titular epithet (Most Illustrious Prince) in diplomatic protocol and royal charters, while poets used it to link divine light with earthly majesty.
- 18th c. — Augustan stature and Swiftian satire: became the standard Enlightenment accolade for statesmen, scientists, and philosophers, famously subverted by Jonathan Swift in Gulliver’s Travels when Gulliver reverently dubs his equine Houyhnhnm host his ‘illustrious master.’
- 19th c. — Victorian meritocratic canonization: broadened beyond aristocratic birth to honor titans of science, literature, and social reform, establishing the concept of an ‘illustrious career’ or an ‘illustrious roster’ of national heroes.
- 20th–21st c. — Formal retrospective accolade: predominantly used today retrospectively to honor venerable institutions, lifetime achievements, and legendary legacies (an illustrious history, an illustrious heritage), retaining an unmistakable tone of dignified, classical admiration.
