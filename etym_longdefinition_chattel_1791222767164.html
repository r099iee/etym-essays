Chattel is a noun meaning a movable item of personal property — furniture, livestock, goods, or historically a human being treated as property — distinct in law from land and buildings.

In modern everyday use chattel most often appears in the fixed phrase goods and chattels (all one's movable belongings) and in legal-historical discussion of slavery (chattel slavery, where people are legally classified as movable property). In property law it denotes personalty as opposed to realty: anything ownable that is not real estate — from cattle and farm implements to leases, bonds and (in older law) enslaved persons.

The word looks humble but encodes a vast history: Latin caput (head) → capitale (property counted by heads of cattle) → Old French chatel, catel (goods, livestock, money) → English cattle (livestock) and chattel (movable goods) as a doublet split. Cattle and chattel are the same word twice borrowed.

  • Core legal sense — movable personal property: Chattel personal (tangible movables: furniture, tools, animals) versus chattel real (leasehold interests, a historical hybrid). Probate inventories, insurance policies and deeds still list chattels. The semantic logic is head-counting: wealth you can drive, carry or lead away, as opposed to land you cannot move.
  • Historical slavery sense — chattel slavery: From the 17th century, Atlantic plantation systems defined enslaved Africans as chattels — heritable, saleable movables with no legal personhood. Chattel slavery is distinguished from other coerced labour (serfdom, debt bondage, indenture) precisely by this absolute property status. The term today carries intense moral weight and is central to historiography, reparations debate and human-rights law (modern chattel-like slavery).
  • Archaic general sense — livestock, goods, wealth: In Middle English chatel meant cattle, money and goods indifferently (Chaucer: catel). Gradually cattle specialised to livestock and chattel to household goods and legal movables, but early wills use catel for oxen, sheep, pots and clothes together — the whole farm's movable stock.
  • Figurative and pejorative extension — person treated as thing: Beyond law, chattel is used rhetorically for anyone treated as disposable property: treated as a mere chattel, political chattel, wives as chattels in feminist historiography. The bridge is dehumanisation metaphor: to call a person chattel is to accuse the user of counting heads as capital.
  • Technical survivals — chattel mortgage, chattel paper: In commercial law a chattel mortgage (now largely security interest in personal property under UCC Article 9) pledges movables as loan collateral; chattel paper is a record evidencing a monetary obligation secured by goods. These jargon senses preserve the medieval link between cattle, capital and credit.
  • Register and connotation: Chattel is formal, legalistic or historical; one does not normally call one's sofa a chattel except in a will or exam. Cattle is agricultural; capital is economic; chattel is juridical. Its emotional charge is cold and objectifying — precisely why chattel slavery remains the starkest term for commodified humanity.

Polysemy therefore resolves into one image: heads counted on a hillside becoming entries counted in a ledger. Whether the heads are oxen, chairs or human beings, chattel names them insofar as law allows them to be moved, sold and inherited apart from the land they stand on.
