Lip names, in its first and most familiar sense, the fleshy rim of the mouth — the paired upper and lower folds that seal, shape, and sound our speech, eating, kissing, and expression — and, by one of English's most natural extensions, the rim or edge of anything hollow: a cup, a crater, a wound, or a valley.

In the source sentence the word wears this landscape sense: the travellers follow the Hedge to where it bends inwards, running along the lip of a hollow — the brink where level ground tips into declivity, the rim of a valley like the rim of a bowl. Tolkien the philologist chooses the homely body-word over the technical brink or verge, keeping his walkers inside a world scaled to flesh and touch.

Core meaning in detail: Anatomically the lips are the orbicularis oris and its covering tissue — among the most sensitive surfaces of the body, dense with nerve endings, central to suckling, tasting, articulating labial sounds, and the whole theatre of the smile. Poets have therefore loaded them with every charge from tenderness to scorn: lips that pray, lips that lie, lips sealed, lips loosened. The topographical sense follows the same curve in stone and soil: the crater's lip, the pit's lip, the hollow's lip — each a mouth without a face, each brink shaped like the mouth's own rim, concave below and open to the sky.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The fleshy folds of the mouth (primary sense, from Old English): her lip trembled, he bit his lip. The bridge is the body itself, the unmediated original every speaker carries; all other senses borrow from it.
  • The rim or edge of a hollow, vessel, or opening (transferred, medieval onward, the source sense): the lip of the crater, the cup's chipped lip, the lip of the hollow. The bridge is pure shape analogy: a hollow's brink curves like a mouth's rim, so the mouth lends its name — just as valleys get mouths and hills get shoulders and feet.
  • Speech itself, especially its manner (metonymic): praise on every lip, not a word passed his lips. The bridge runs from organ to product: the lips stand for the utterance they shape, so that silence becomes sealed lips and gossip becomes loose lips.
  • Impudence, backchat (slang, 19th century American): none of your lip, don't give me lip. The bridge narrows speech to its most unwelcome kind: the mouth that answers back earns the organ's name as a scolding, and the scolding — keep a civil lip — polices the very flesh it names.
  • A small projecting edge in craft and engineering (technical): the lip of a jug's spout, a ski's upturned lip, the lip of a wound in surgery. The bridge carries the rim-sense into the workshop and the operating theatre, where potters, carpenters, and surgeons all speak of lips with professional exactness.
  • A kiss or the offer of one (tender, colloquial): give us a kiss of your lip in folk song. The bridge condenses the whole courtship of mouths into the organ itself, so that one syllable carries the softest act the mouth performs.

Register and connotation: Lip is intimate, tactile, and vivid — a poet's word and a potter's word at once. The mouth-sense runs the full scale from devotion to derision, while the edge-sense stays cool and descriptive, the surveyor's friend. Together they make lip one of the rare body-words equally at home in love songs, geology textbooks, and bar-room warnings — always curved, always on the brink, always about to speak or spill.
