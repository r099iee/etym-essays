- 1640s — the verb appears in learned English as an astronomical term meaning "to be on the meridian"; confined to star tables and astrology, with no figurative force.
- Late 17th c. — the image of the meridian is transferred to human affairs, and culminate begins to mean "reach the highest point"; still bookish and comparatively rare.
- 18th c. — used of careers, fortunes and empires in historiography and moral essays; the participial adjective culminating appears in the sense "crowning, final".
- 19th c. — becomes a standard narrative verb in historical and biographical prose, used to give events a providential shape; Carlyle's generation employs it freely.
- 20th c. — settles into journalism and academic writing as a neutral marker of climax and outcome, and is increasingly criticised as a cliché when it means no more than "ended".
- 21st c. — dominant in news analysis and reports ("a dispute culminating in…"), while astronomers retain the strict meridian sense and style guides continue to warn against using it for mere termination.
