Single linear chain from the earliest reconstructable form to the present rootWord jiggered: 1. Imitative Germanic base *gig- / *jig- (sound-symbolic; no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed) — "quick, jerky, bouncing motion or sound." The family also yields German Geige, "fiddle," and the English verbs jiggle and jitter. 2. Middle English gigge / gige (14th century) — "a giddy, frivolous girl; a whipping-top (a top spun by a whip)." The top shows the core meaning: rapid, jerky spinning motion. 3. English gig (16th century) — "a light two-wheeled carriage," so called from its jerky, bouncing ride. 4. English jig, noun (c. 1560) — "a lively, jerky dance," probably supported by Middle French gigue, "a dance, a fiddle," from the same imitative family. The verb to jig, "to dance a jig; to move with quick jerky motions," derives from it. 5. English jigger, noun and verb (19th century, from jig + the agentive suffix -er) — (n.) "a person who jigs; a small device, gadget, or thing; a small measure for spirits (chiefly US);" (v.) "to jerk or shake; to tamper with, to spoil." 6. English jiggered, adjective/participle (19th–20th centuries) — with three derived senses: (a) "astonished, confounded," in the exclamatory "I'll be jiggered!" (1830s), a minced oath widely read as a euphemistic softening of "I'll be buggered"; (b) "broken, ruined, spoilt," chiefly British, from the verb; (c) "slightly intoxicated," chiefly American, from the measure of spirits. This is the exact word form of this card. Note on nuance/debate: the exclamatory sense's link to "I'll be buggered!" is the standard lexicographic reading (OED, Etymonline) but remains an inference, since minced oaths are by nature unrecorded at their birth; the link to the jig family and the derivative sequence jig → jigger → jiggered are securely attested. All three senses are documented separately in 19th-century sources.
