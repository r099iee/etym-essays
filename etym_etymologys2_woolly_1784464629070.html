Derivation chain for WOOLLY. The word has two building blocks — the noun wool and the adjectival suffix -y — so two chains are given, one for each root word, followed by the final derivation. CHAIN 1: WOOL (first root word) [1] PIE root *h2wlh1neh2 (also written *wlh1neh2) "wool" — one of the most widely attested roots in the entire family: Sanskrit urnā "wool", Latin lāna "wool" (whence lanate, lanolin), Greek lēnos "wool, fleece", Lithuanian vilna "wool", Old Irish olann, and the Germanic forms; the meaning "fleece of the sheep" is constant everywhere. [2] Proto-Germanic *wullō "wool" (the regular Germanic resolution of the PIE syllabic l-cluster). [3] Old English wull (also woll) "wool" (attested from the 8th c. onward in the vocabulary of the rural economy and the wool trade). [4] Middle English wolle, wole, wool "wool" (12th–15th c.; spelling regularization). [5] Modern English wool "the soft curly hair of sheep" (16th c. onward). CHAIN 2: the adjectival suffix -Y (second root word / formative) [1] Proto-Germanic *-ig(a)- "adjective-forming suffix meaning having, full of, characterized by" (reconstructed at the Germanic level; not securely traced into PIE). [2] Old English -ig "having, full of" (as in halig "holy", literally "having godliness"). [3] Middle English -y / -i / -ie "the regular spelling outcome of -ig". [4] Modern English -y "characterized by, full of, like" — the standard way of making "like X" adjectives. FINAL DERIVATION: wool + -y -> Middle English / Early Modern English woolly "made of, covered with, or resembling wool" (first attested in the 16th c.); figurative sense "vague, confused (of thought)" develops in the 18th–19th c. Final node: Modern English woolly (adjective). Note on the chain: the two chains cover the two native building blocks; both are inherited Germanic material, and the final union (wool + -y) is a native English morphological act, not a loan. The etymology is fully attested with no invented stages; the only reconstructed items are the PIE wool-root (securely attested by its daughters) and the Proto-Germanic suffix.
