Waterlogged is a genuinely English compound — water + logged — and its history is unusually datable, because the word was coined in the technical language of the sailing navy. The Oxford English Dictionary records water-logged from 1729 in the sense "rendered log-like, so full of water as to be heavy, sluggish, and unmanageable", applied to ships that had shipped heavy seas or been holed and filled with water until they floated like inert logs, unable to make way or answer their helm. The governing image is precise and concrete: a "log" in sailors’ speech was a length of tree-trunk floating in water, and a ship reduced to the state of a log — floating but powerless — was "logged". The verb to log, "to reduce to a log-like condition" or "to make heavy and inert", is recorded in the same nautical context, and the participle logged soon combined with water to form the compound we know.

Root 1: water. The first element is the ordinary English word water, which descends directly from Old English wæter, recorded from the earliest texts of the Anglo-Saxon period. Old English wæter is the exact reflex of Proto-Germanic *watōr, and the Germanic word descends from the Indo-European water-words *wéd-, *wód-, and *ud- ("water, wet"), one of the oldest and most stable vocabulary items of the Indo-European family: cognates include Latin unda (wave), Greek hýdōr (water), Sanskrit udán, Hittite watā-r, and Old Irish uisce (water, whence the word whiskey). The Germanic branch of the word has been the everyday English word for the substance since before the written record, entirely continuous from the early Middle Ages to the present. In the compound waterlogged it supplies the cause: it is the water that does the soaking.

Root 2: log. The second element is more exotic. The noun log, "a felled tree-trunk", appears in Middle English around 1300 as logge, of disputed origin: the standard view is that it was borrowed from the Scandinavian languages (compare Old Norse lág, "a fallen tree, log", and Norwegian lagg), though the precise Scandinavian source form is not attested and a native formation is not impossible. There is no reliable Indo-European chain for log; it belongs to the small class of English words — mostly from the everyday world of woods, ships, and farming — whose origin lies in the North Germanic dialects and cannot be traced further back with certainty. The verb to log, "to cut into logs" and hence "to fell timber", follows in the early 17th century, and from it comes the participle logged. A curious modern afterlife of the same stem: the nautical log-line (originally a floating timber drogue used to measure a ship’s speed) gives the log as the ship’s record, whence "to log" = "to record", and the "logbook". The heavy, sluggish logy of American speech is a sibling formation from the same tree-trunk image.

Semantic career. From ships, waterlogged spread naturally to the things a sailor handled: spars, timbers, wreckage, and cargo all became "waterlogged" when soaked beyond use. In the 19th century the word moved ashore to land and soil — "waterlogged ground" enters the vocabulary of drainage, agriculture, and civil engineering as a precise term for earth so saturated that its air-spaces are filled and plants suffocate. The 20th century widened the word still further: waterlogged clothing, books, boots, cameras, phones, and above all people. In The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the word of the dwarves with deliberate physicality: Bombur, asleep or senseless, and his companions, waterlogged and "only half alive", are reduced to heavy, sodden, helpless bundles — the modern human sense that descends directly from that first 1729 description of a ship that could no longer move. The word has also taken on a mild figurative life — a waterlogged mind, schedule, or organisation — but it always retains the concrete image of being so filled with water that one is made heavy, slow, and inert.
