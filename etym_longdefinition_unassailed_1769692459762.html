UNASSAILED (adjective): not attacked; not subjected to assault; hence, never questioned, challenged, or defeated; standing entirely without hostile contact, in both physical and figurative senses. Sense 1 — Physical and military (literal). At its root, unassailed describes a person, position, fortress, army, or country that has never come under armed attack. A citadel that has never been besieged, a flank that has never been turned, a coastline that has never seen an invader, is unassailed. In this sense the word is the perfect negative participle of assail ("to attack violently"), and it carries the clear implication of safety through absence of contact rather than through invincible strength. It is fact-reporting: no assault has occurred. Sense 2 — Figurative warfare of words and ideas (the core modern use). Since the 16th-17th centuries, assail was extended from battering rams and swords to rhetoric: arguments, accusations, doubts, temptations, calumnies, and flattery all "assail" a reputation, a conscience, a faith, or a mind. Unassailed therefore describes a judgment, a belief, an honour, or a record that has come through untouched because nothing has attacked it - not because it repelled an attack. The shade of meaning is passive security: immunity by non-occurrence. Sense 3 — Rhetorical, legal, and journalistic register. In formal prose, unassailed commonly means "unchallenged, uncontested, standing without opposition": an unassailed majority, an unassailed verdict, an unassailed position in a debate, an unassailed assumption. It implies that no one has bothered to question or refute it, and therefore that it simply stands. Sense 4 — Psychological and moral (Austen's usage). In Pride and Prejudice (1813, Chapter 4), Austen applies the word to Elizabeth Bennet's judgement: "a judgement too unassailed by any attention to herself." Here attention - flattery, admiration, social courting - is conceived as an attack, a siege upon a young woman's discernment. Elizabeth's judgement has never been breached because her vanity has never been played upon. The word thus carries cool, ironic praise: her perception is intact precisely because she has never been "got at" by self-regarding attentions. This use displays the full historical bridge: physical assault (breaching a fortress) leads to rhetorical assault (arguments, flattery) and then to social assault (courting attentions that corrupt judgement). Grammar and formation. un- (Old English un-, privative "not") + assailed (past participle of assail, from Old French assaillir "to leap upon", from Latin assalire). It is a participial adjective, typically predicative (a position stood unassailed) or attributive (an unassailed judgement). Related: assail, assailant, assault; adverb unassailably. Register and nuance. Unassailed is formal, chiefly literary and rhetorical; it sounds stately and slightly archaic. It differs crucially from invulnerable (innate capacity to resist), unscathed (survived an attack), and unimpeached (legal or moral standing). Unassailed simply records that the assault never arrived. Common collocations. an unassailed reputation/record/position/thesis/assumption; to stand, remain, or emerge unassailed; a judgement or conscience unassailed by doubt. Secondary and technical usages.
  • In logic and argumentation theory, an unassailed premise is one no interlocutor has refuted - the default status of an argument accepted without objection.
  • In finance and business writing, an unassailed valuation or forecast is one not yet challenged by analysts or competitors.
  • In sport, an unassailed record is one that no contender has yet threatened.
  • In military historiography, "unassailed" may describe a position never tested in battle, stressing that its strength is unproven rather than proven.
Metaphoric logic. The word's power rests on the siege metaphor: the mind or reputation is a fortress, and hostile forces (criticism, flattery, temptation) are besiegers. To be unassailed is to be a fortress no army has even reached - an image that stresses untouchedness, virgin integrity, and the absence of the enemy rather than the height of the walls.
