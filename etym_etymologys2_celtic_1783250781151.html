The English adjective Celtic descends from the ancient Greek name for the Celts, whose own ultimate origin is uncertain but debated; the attested chain runs: 1. (Uncertain Celtic source) *kelto- — the self-designation of the ancient Celts, of uncertain etymology; the traditional theory derives it from the Proto-Indo-European root *ḱel- ‘to hide, conceal’ (the Celts as ‘the hidden ones’), while a rival theory derives it from *kel- ‘to strike, cut’ (the Celts as ‘the warriors’). The ultimate PIE source is UNCERTAIN. 2. Ancient Greek Κελτοί (Keltoí) ‘the Celts’, attested from c. 500 BC (Hecataeus of Miletus; Herodotus, 5th century BC, places the Keltoi on the upper Danube) — the earliest attested form of the name. 3. Ancient Greek Κελτικός (Keltikós) ‘Celtic’ (adjective), from Keltoí + -ikos; also Κελτική (Keltikḗ) ‘the Celtic land’. 4. Latin Celtae ‘the Celts’ and Celticus ‘Celtic’ — the Roman forms, attested from the 1st century BC (Caesar, De Bello Gallico: “qui ipsorum lingua Celtae, nostra Galli appellantur” — “who in their own language are called Celtae, in ours Galli”). 5. French Celtique / Latin Celticus → English Celtic, attested from the early 17th century (1607) — the adjective for the Celtic peoples and, after Edward Lhuyd's 1707 classification, for the Celtic language family. 6. Celtic — the modern English adjective and noun. Debated points: the deepest root of the name is genuinely uncertain — whether *kelto- means ‘hidden’ (from *ḱel- ‘hide’) or ‘warrior’ (from *kel- ‘strike’) cannot be decided with certainty; and whether the Greeks received the name from the Gauls' own self-designation (as Caesar suggests) or from another channel is likewise not fully certain. The English chain from Greek via Latin and French, however, is securely attested stage by stage.
