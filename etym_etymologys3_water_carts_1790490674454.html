WATER — from PIE flood to London main: water continues one of the most archaic and suppletive nouns in Indo-European. The reconstructed paradigm alternated nominative *wodr with genitive *udnes, a heteroclitic r/n-stem preserved in Hittite watar / wedenas, Greek hydor / hydatos, Latin unda (wave, from the same family by different suffix), Old Church Slavonic voda, Russian voda, Sanskrit udan, and Germanic *watar. Proto-Germanic regularised the stem to *watar- (Gothic wato, Old Norse vatn, Old High German wazzar, Old English wæter), with Grimm's law turning PIE d to Germanic t and Verner's law explaining voicing variants. Old English wæter (neuter, strong) already meant rain-water, spring-water, sea, and the element itself; compounds like wæter-seoc (dropsical) and wæter-scype (water-vessel) show its productivity. Middle English water keeps the spelling while the vowel shortens and the final -er vocalises to modern /ˈwɔːtə/. The plural was historically waters for bodies and kinds (the waters of Bath, still waters), a usage the compound water-carts presupposes: carts for waters in bulk.

  • Phonetic stability: initial /w/ retained from PIE through Germanic to modern English; medial /t/ from Grimm; loss of grammatical gender and case by Middle English. Pronunciation shifts only in vowel length and rhoticity (RP non-rhotic /ˈwɔːtə/ versus American /ˈwɔːtər/).
  • Semantic constancy with technical layering: the core — the transparent drinkable liquid — never changed, but successive societies added mains water, hard versus soft water, mineral waters, holy water, and chemical H2O (1810s). Victorian London added water-rate, water-plug, water-cart, water-company as administrative vocabulary for the New River, Chelsea, and Grand Junction monopolies.
  • Literary attestation: from Beowulf's wæter to the Authorised Version's living waters to Wordsworth and Ruskin, water remains elemental and moral. Ruskin's delicious dripping is characteristic: water as delight, leakage, and providence, not as chemistry.

CARTS — from Norse kartr to parish dust-cart: cart (plural carts) enters Middle English as cart, kert from Old Norse kartr, a waggon, set beside native Old English cræt, a chariot, cart, with identical meaning. Both continue Proto-Germanic *krattaz or *kart-, a strong carrier, whose deeper Indo-European origin is uncertain and must be honestly marked unknown. Older dictionaries proposed PIE *gers- or *ger-, to turn, wind, bend — on the idea that a cart is a turning-wheeled thing — or linked it to crate as a woven box-body, but de Vries, OED, and Etymonline agree there is no secure PIE root; the Germanic forms stand isolated except for Welsh cart (borrowed back from English) and possible Middle Dutch cratte. The responsible chain therefore starts at Proto-Germanic, not PIE, with an explicit unknown earlier.

  • Old Norse versus Old English doublet: cræt in Beowulf and charters is already a farm and war vehicle; Viking settlement reinforces it as kartr in the Danelaw, with hard /k/ and trilled /r/. Northern and eastern dialects prefer cart, southern texts cræt until cart wins generally by 1400. Diminutive cartel gives modern cartel? No — it gives go-cart and push-cart.
  • Form and function (medieval to Victorian): two-wheeled tip-carts for dung and harvest, four-wheeled waggons for road haulage, spring-carts for market, night-soil carts, dust-carts, and water-carts. Parish accounts distinguish them by load, not shape: a cart is defined by what it carts. Wheelwrights standardise the box-body, iron tyres, and sprinkler rose for water work by the 1820s.
  • Morphology of the plural -s: Old English crætas and Old Norse kartar both level to Middle English cartes and modern carts /kɑːts/ with regular voiceless cluster. The hyphen in water-carts is 19th-century compositor habit for transparent compounds, later often closed (watercarts) or opened (water carts).
  • Idiom and survival: to put the cart before the horse (1340s), in the cart (in trouble, 1800s), American cart for shopping trolley and golf cart, computer cart. The horse-drawn sense survives in heritage streets and farm museums, while the word lives on in motorised water-bowsers that do exactly what Ruskin's carts did.

Recombined, water-carts is first attested in vestry and newspaper English in the late 18th century alongside dust-carts, dung-carts and night-carts, part of the parish vocabulary of urban housekeeping. Its Victorian heyday (1820s–1890s) coincides with macadam roads, cholera debates, and the move from private wells to company mains: the cart both lays dust and advertises the new water economy of plugs, trapdoors, and rates. Contemporary prints show high-ribbed wheels, barrel staves, and a rear rose throwing a fan of spray across the flags, while contractors' day-books list carters, horses, and gallons per turn. Motor tankers after 1910 replace horses but keep the compound logic in water-tanker, water-bowser, water-truck. Ruskin's 1820s memory thus captures the type at its most picturesque — leather hose, iron trap, dripping flags — just before it became invisible infrastructure buried in mains and sewers.
