- [19th-century entry (c. 1840s)] - Coined in scientific English from Latin auris "ear" as a clinical adjective (aural medicine, aural nerve) alongside the older auricular; purely technical and neutral.
- [Late 19th–20th century: the educational sense] - Aural spreads into teaching — aural training, aural comprehension — paired against oral (of the mouth/speech) in language exams; the near-homophony makes the aural/oral confusion a notorious, permanent classroom and clerical joke.
- [Modern register] - A standard, unmarked technical adjective meaning "of the ear or hearing," used across medicine, linguistics, and criticism; it carries no emotional charge but is forever shadowed by its homophonic rival oral and kept distinct from acoustic, which belongs to the physics of sound.
