Derivation chain for FLUNKEYS (single root word; the origin is officially uncertain, so the chain below is the traditional reconstruction, with the uncertainty stated at each doubtful step):

1. Proto-Indo-European *klei- (reconstructed) — "to lean, incline; side", the traditional equation for the flank-family; the primitive image is the body propped against something. Handbooks debate whether the flank-words derive from *klei- or from a distinct base *kleng-; the equation is traditional rather than proven.

2. Germanic *hlanka- (reconstructed; Frankish *hlanka) — "hip, side, flank", a West Germanic body-part word that passed into the Romance languages as a loanword; the source of Old French flanc.

3. Old French flanc — "side, flank" (attested from the 12th century); the loan from Frankish *hlanka, carrying the sense "the side of the body, the side of a troop or building".

4. English flank (16th century, via French) — "the side of the body between the ribs and the hip; the side of a military formation"; from it the verb to flank and the military flanqueur "one posted at the side of a line of battle".

5. English flunkey / flunky — first attested 1782 in Scotland, "a liveried footman, one who runs at the flank or side of a master's carriage or horse"; traditionally derived from flank with a diminutive or associative suffix -key. The OED, however, marks the origin as unknown, and the flank-derivation, though traditional, is not proven.

6. Modern English plural FLUNKEYS (also flunkies) — the card's inflected form, "liveried servants; footmen".

Note on the debate: because the word appears suddenly in Scottish sources of the 1780s with no clear attested intermediate, the precise route from flank to flunkey remains conjectural; some lexicographers have sought the source elsewhere (dialectal or cant formations), but no rival etymology is secure, and "origin uncertain" remains the scholarly verdict.
