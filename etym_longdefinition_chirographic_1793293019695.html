Chirographic is the adjective of chirography: pertaining to handwriting, to characters traced by the hand, and by extension to entire cultures organised around the technology of writing. Built from Greek kheir, hand, plus graphein, to write, it literally means hand-written — and in literacy studies it names the whole order of thought that handwriting makes possible.

In the source sentence the word stands opposite the oral: scholars compare oral cultures with chirographic, that is, writing, cultures coexisting at one period. The gloss in parentheses shows the author knows the term is rare and learned — he translates it on the spot — and the point is typological: societies with writing think, remember, and govern differently from societies without it, and the adjective gives that difference a name.

Core meaning in detail: At its narrowest chirographic describes anything produced by handwriting as opposed to print, typing, or speech: chirographic manuscripts, chirographic errors where a scribe misreads a letterform. At its widest, following the literacy theorists, it describes the mindset of writing cultures — list-making, archiving, codifying law, composing sentences meant to be re-read rather than heard. The hand that holds the pen reshapes the mind that dictates to it.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Pertaining to handwriting as a physical act (primary dictionary sense, from 17th c.): chirographic skill, chirographic specimens in copybooks. The bridge is the compound itself: hand plus writing equals what the hand writes.
  • Written by hand as opposed to printed (bibliographical sense): chirographic versus typographic transmission of a text. The bridge runs through book history: before print every book was chirographic, and textual critics still distinguish hand-copy errors from compositor errors.
  • Belonging to writing cultures (literacy-studies sense, the source sense, 20th c.): chirographic thought, chirographic memory. The bridge is technological determinism made adjective: the medium of the hand reorganises cognition, so the culture it founds takes its name.
  • Of legal instruments executed by hand (historical legal sense): the medieval chirograph, a charter written twice on one sheet then cut through wavy writing so the halves could be matched. The bridge is institutional: handwriting was authentication itself, and the divided charter is the ancestor of the indenture.
  • Of handwriting expertise and forensics (modern technical sense): chirographic analysis in questioned-document examination. The bridge carries the copybook into the laboratory: individual hands leave measurable traces, and experts read them.
  • Manuscript-era mentality in media theory (extended figurative sense): a chirographic bias toward fixity, authorship, and private reading. The bridge maps the technology onto values: what writing rewards — originality, consistency, silent study — becomes the implicit standard by which oral performance is judged and found wanting.

Register and connotation: chirographic is unapologetically learned — seminar rooms, footnotes, and media-theory monographs, almost never conversation. It flatters its user with classical precision while quietly asserting a grand thesis: that the humble act of forming letters re-plumbed the human sensorium. The source sentence wields it exactly so, as a crisp antithesis to oral, with the kindly aside translating for newcomers.
