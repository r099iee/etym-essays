SCORNED descends from a Germanic source of the verb "to mock," ultimately connected with a PIE root meaning "to cut." The chain: 1) PIE *(s)ker- "to cut, shear" (reconstructed; the root of shear, share, shard, sharp; its specific connection to the Germanic "mock" family is probable but not securely proven — see note) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *skarną "mockery, derision" (reconstructed) — from the image of "cutting to pieces," shredding a person's standing with words. 3) Frankish *skern "mockery, jest, derision" (West Germanic, reconstructed) — the form borrowed into Gallo-Romance speech. 4) Old French escarn "mockery, derision, insult, contempt" (noun, attested from the 11th c.) and escarnir "to mock, deride, insult" (verb) — borrowed from the Germanic base into French. 5) Anglo-Norman escarnir / escarner "to mock, despise" — the Channel form. 6) Middle English scornen (verb, c. 1200) "to mock, insult, hold in contempt"; scorne (noun, c. 1200) "insult, contempt, derision." 7) English scorn (verb and noun) — modern form, with the participle. 8) SCORNED (past participle and past tense) "rejected or treated with contempt." NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The ultimate PIE connection of the Germanic "mockery" family to *(s)ker- "to cut" is widely assumed but not universally accepted; some scholars prefer to treat the Germanic forms as isolated, without a secure PIE equation. (2) The popular etymology linking scorn to Latin cornu "horn" via Old French escorner "to cut off the horns" is NOT the accepted derivation and should be rejected. (3) The spelling shows the regular development: Old French escarn → Anglo-Norman escarn → Middle English scorne, with the earlier form scarn surviving in dialect.
