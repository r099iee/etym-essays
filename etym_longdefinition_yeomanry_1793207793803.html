Yeomanry names yeomen taken together: the class, the corps, and the legend of the English yeoman — the sturdy freeholding farmer standing between the gentry above and the labourers below, celebrated for four centuries as the backbone of the nation. In the source sentence Cobbett sneers at a Surrey landlord who approved of yeomanry cavalry to keep down the Jacobins and Levellers: the volunteer horse regiments raised from 1794, officered by squires and filled with tenant farmers, armed by the government to crush domestic radicalism in the shadow of the French Revolution.

Core meaning in detail: The medieval yeoman was a cut above the common: a free man holding his own land, serving in a great household, or drawing the longbow that won Crécy and Agincourt. The collective yeomanry gathered that whole middling rank into one proud noun — the yeomanry of England, invoked by statesmen from Cromwell to Churchill as the moral ballast of the kingdom. From 1794 the word gained its military capital letter: the Yeomanry Cavalry, county troops of volunteer horsemen, uniformed, sabred, and mustered on village greens to overawe reform meetings — never sent abroad, always available at home, which is exactly what made radicals like Cobbett despise them as the landlords’ private dragoons.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Yeomen collectively as a social class (primary historical sense, from the 16th c.): the yeomanry of Kent, the sturdy yeomanry. The bridge is the collective suffix -ry, as in peasantry and gentry: many yeomen fused into one order of society, the freeholders between squire and cottager.
  • The Yeomanry Cavalry — volunteer home-defence horse (Cobbett’s sense, 1794–1908): the Surrey Yeomanry, to serve in the yeomanry. The bridge is recruitment: the new volunteer regiments enlisted precisely the farming class, so the class-name became the corps-name, farmer-soldiers on their own hunters.
  • Later yeomanry regiments — Territorials and reservists (20th c.): the county yeomanry regiments of the two World Wars, many converted to artillery and armour. The bridge is institutional descent: the volunteer tradition survived mechanisation, and the old county titles — the Queen’s Own, the Sherwood Rangers — rode tanks instead of horses.
  • The ceremonial yeomen — Guard and Warders (picturesque survival): the Yeomen of the Guard and the Yeomen Warders of the Tower, the Beefeaters. The bridge is pageantry: the Tudor bodyguard fossilised into scarlet uniform and raven-keeping, the yeoman as living museum.
  • The moral idea — sturdy independence (figurative): the yeomanry spirit, a nation of yeomen. The bridge is four centuries of praise-literature: writers from Latimer to Orwell made the yeoman the emblem of plain English freedom — neither servile nor proud, rooted in his acres and answerable to none.

Register and connotation: yeomanry sounds of oak, acres, and anthems — warm, patriotic, faintly rousing — until Cobbett gets hold of it. In loyalist mouths it is a toast: the gallant yeomanry, England’s safeguard. In radical mouths it is an indictment: farmers tricked into saddling against their starving neighbours, the sabres of Peterloo (1819) still remembered when Cobbett rode Surrey. Both musics play in the word today: the heritage bodies parade it with pride, while historians hear, underneath the hooves, the argument about whom volunteer soldiers really serve.
