allusions is the plural noun form of allusion: implied or indirect references to a person, event, place, text, myth or cultural idea, made without explicit naming, which enrich meaning by invoking knowledge assumed to be shared between author and audience. In the source sentence — a mixture of Edwardian slang, quotations and allusions to numerous poets producing a comic-poetic prose style — it denotes literary borrowing by hint, the classic T. S. Eliot / P. G. Wodehouse technique of letting Virgil, Shakespeare or Tennyson hover behind modern comic prose.

Core literary sense: an allusion is never an outright mention. To say "like Romeo" alludes to Shakespeare's lover without quoting him; to quote "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks" cites him. Critics therefore distinguish:

  • Biblical allusions: "a prodigal son", "a Good Samaritan", "the writing on the wall" — invoking Scripture for moral weight.
  • Classical allusions: "an Achilles' heel", "a Sisyphean task", "crossing the Rubicon" — invoking Greece and Rome for grandeur or irony.
  • Literary allusions: echoing phrases, plots or characters from earlier authors, as Eliot echoes Webster, Dante and Baudelaire.
  • Historical / topical allusions: hinting at contemporary politics or scandal without libellous explicitness.
  • Personal allusions: private jokes intelligible only to an inner circle.

Polysemy and secondary meanings today:

  • Rhetorical hint in ordinary speech: "Are you making an allusion to my age?" — any veiled insinuation. The semantic bridge is direct: from literary device to everyday indirection. It retains playfulness; an allusion teases rather than asserts.
  • Art-historical and musical allusion: a painting alluding to Titian's Venus, a film score alluding to Wagner. The bridge is intermedial transfer: the same logic of quotation-without-quotation applied beyond words.
  • Obsolete senses preserved in dictionaries: 1540s "metaphor, parable" and 1550s "word-play, joke". These explain why early texts use allusion where modern readers expect allegory or pun. The bridge is Latin alludere "to play": all senses involve verbal play, only the rules changed from open jest to covert hint.
  • Allusion vs illusion vs delusion: a crucial modern triangulation. Illusion is false perception; delusion is false belief; allusion is true but indirect reference. Confusion arises purely from sound. Teaching the ludere family (prelude, collude, elude, ludicrous) cures it: all contain play.
  • Internet-meme allusion: a GIF, still or catchphrase that evokes a whole film or text. This is structurally identical to classical allusion — economy through shared archive — only the archive is now audiovisual and global.

Usage notes: allusion to is standard ("an allusion to Homer"); allusion on is obsolete. The verb is allude, the adjective allusive ("a highly allusive style"), the adverb allusively. Register is neutral-literary: at home in criticism, journalism and educated conversation, never slang. Connotation is positive — erudite, witty, connective — unless allusions are so obscure they exclude, when critics call them showy or elitist. To master allusions is to master cultural literacy itself: recognising that no text stands alone but plays, jests and converses with all prior texts.
