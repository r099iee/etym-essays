Derivation chains for SWINBURNE — a compound habitational surname of two independent elements, Old English swīn "swine" + burne "stream". Each element has its own separate chain. === CHAIN 1: the first element, swīn "swine, pig" === 1. Proto-Indo-European *sū- "pig" (with the adjectival/derived form *sū-ro-, *sū-k- etc.) — the ancient word for the pig, widely held to be imitative of the snorting or snuffling of a pig's snout. Attested across the family: Sanskrit sū-kara- "boar, pig," Latin sūs "pig, hog," Greek hŷs "pig," Avestan hū "boar," Old Irish socc "pig, ploughshare." 2. Proto-Germanic *swiną "pig, swine" (neuter) — formed on the same root, commonly analysed as the adjective *swī-na- "porcine, of the pig" substantivised, i.e., "the porcine animal." (The derivational detail is debated: some derive *swiną from a root adjective, others from the root with a -no- suffix.) 3. Old English swīn "swine, pig" (attested throughout the Old English period) — the ordinary Germanic word for the animal; survives in modern English as swine, now used for the animal collectively and, by insult, for a contemptible person, and in compounds such as swineherd and swine flu. 4. Old English place-name and personal-name element swīn- (as in Swindon "swine hill," Swinford, Swineshead) — used to name settlements and, in compound personal/surnames, to identify a person from such a place. This element, joined to burne, forms the first half of SWINBURNE. === CHAIN 2: the second element, burne "stream, brook" === 1. Proto-Indo-European *bʰreu- "to boil, bubble, well up" — the root of bubbling and welling water, whence Latin fervēre "to be hot, to boil" (English fervent, ferment) and Germanic forms meaning "to brew, to seethe." 2. Proto-Germanic *brunnōn (masculine, "spring, well, fountain"; also *brunnaz) — from *bʰreu- with the -nn- expressive gemination typical of Germanic; the word for a welling spring of water. 3. Old English burne / burna "spring, stream, brook" (attested in Old English; also bryne in some dialects) — the Anglian and Northumbrian word for a running stream, surviving to this day in Northern English and Scots as burn ("a stream"). The southern Old English form of the same etymon underlies the many -bourne place-names. 4. Old English place-name element -burne / -burn (as in Blackburn, Whitburn, and the southern -bourne names) — joined to swīn it forms the second half of SWINBURNE. === FINAL COMPOUND === The two elements combine in a medieval habitational surname: swīn + burne → SWINBURNE, "the swine-stream," the stream (or settlement by the stream) where swine were kept. The surname is recorded from the medieval period in Northern England, and the poet Algernon Charles Swinburne inherited it from his Northumbrian gentry family. === DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS === - The exact derivational relation of Proto-Germanic *swiną to PIE *sū- is debated: the standard account makes it an adjectival substantivisation (*swī-na-, "porcine"), but the intermediate steps are reconstructed, not directly attested. - The southern English place-name form -bourne (e.g., Bournemouth) and the northern -burn / -burne are the same Old English word in different dialectal form, so Swinburne, Blackburn, and Bournemouth share the same second element despite their different spellings. - The surname's two elements are independent English words: neither swīn nor burne is a "root" of the other, and the compound is a genuine two-root word — hence the two separate chains above.
