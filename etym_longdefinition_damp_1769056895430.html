damp (adjective and noun) — slightly wet; moist; and, as a noun, the moisture or humidity in the air, on walls, or in the ground, especially when cool, stale, and uncomfortable. In the line from The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, "I don't want an open door at my back... besides the draft and the ________," the word does atmospheric work: it names the physical signature of the underground — the chilled, unventilated wetness that clings to stone passages and buried vaults, the stale breath of a place no sun has reached. The nuance is one of sensory discomfort rather than disaster: damp is not soaked, not flooded — it is the sullen, lingering wetness that makes you aware of the cold and the dark, the wetness that seeps. It belongs with "draft" in the sentence as a paired discomfort, each word a small assault on comfort from a different direction: the draft moves, the damp hangs. In context the line is spoken by a character reluctant to have the door at her back, so "damp" is not just a weather report but part of the vocabulary of unease — the physical world conspiring with the psychological. It also carries faintly the word's old sense of noxious, chill vapour, which fits a chamber connected to the legendary past: the damp of Cair Paravel is the cold exhalation of history. The synonym set for wetness is a graduated scale, and "damp" sits on the low, unpleasant end. Wet is the unmarked word: saturated, covered or soaked with water, with no emotional colouring. Moist is damp's neutral, often pleasant twin: it describes cake, earth, and skin with a hint of freshness and life — the difference between a "moist sponge cake" and a "damp towel" is almost entirely attitudinal. Humid is climatic and air-borne: the tropical or summer mugginess of an atmosphere, usually warm, whereas damp is cool. Clammy is damp on the skin plus sticky-cold, the physical revulsion of a handshake that is moist and cold — a subjective touch-sensation rather than a property of surfaces. Soggy and sodden are waterlogged and heavy, the wetness of drowned ground or soaked bread. Dank is damp plus darkness, cold, and staleness — the word for dungeons and cellars — and is damp's closest literary cousin; but damp alone can be a neutral property (a damp cloth, damp grass), whereas dank is always unpleasant. Misty, drizzly, and dewy describe airborne or superficial wetness from weather and dawn. The noun "the damp" — British in flavour — is what the sentence exploits: it turns the adjective into a presence, a thing that occupies a place, so that "the draft and the damp" reads as two inhabitants of the darkness. The word's peculiarity is precisely this range: at once a modest technical adjective and a small engine of Gothic atmosphere. Collocationally, damp pairs with surfaces, weather, and the body's discomforts: "damp walls/air/towels/clothes/earth/grass," "a damp chill," "damp and cold," "cold and damp," "the damp" (noun: "the house smells of damp," "to keep out the damp"), and in technical UK usage "rising damp" and "a damp-proof course" (from the builder's vocabulary of wall moisture). The verb and its derivatives are equally rich: "to damp (down) a fire" (to choke and slow it), "to dampen" (to moisten, and figuratively to dull or depress: "dampen one's spirits," "dampen enthusiasm"), and the nouns "dampness" and "damper." The register is everyday and concrete, at home in weather talk, building reports, and descriptive prose, while retaining a literary undertow — "damp" is one of the stock atmospheric words of English ghost and adventure fiction. In Lewis's line its work is to make the reader feel the vault's chill as an almost living presence, part of the resistance the children face while the door at their backs stays open. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:
  • Slightly wet; moist (adjective, primary) — the everyday sense: the cool, clingy, uncomfortable wetness of walls, air, earth, and fabric, without soaking or flooding.
  • The damp (noun) — moisture or humidity as a presence in a place: "the house smells of damp," "to keep out the damp"; in British building usage, "rising damp" (moisture drawn up from the ground) and "damp-proof course" (a barrier layer in walls).
  • Noxious mine-gases: firedamp and chokedamp (noun, historical-technical) — from the word's original English sense of "a noxious, suffocating exhalation," the miners' names for methane (firedamp, which explodes near a flame) and carbon dioxide (chokedamp, which suffocates); a fossil of the word's old "bad air" meaning.
  • Verb "to damp" / "to dampen" — to moisten slightly; to choke or deaden (a fire); and figuratively to dull or depress: "to dampen someone's spirits," "a damper on the party."
  • Technical "damper" — a device that deadens vibration or sound (a piano damper, a chimney damper), or in engineering a shock-absorber; from the verb's "to deaden" sense.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge — the word was borrowed into English in the fourteenth century from Middle Low German damp "vapour, steam, smoke," from a Germanic root connected (plausibly, though not certainly) to the Indo-European root *dʰewh₂- "to fume, smoke, raise vapour" — the root of Sanskrit धूम (dhūmá-) "smoke," Latin fūmus, and Greek θυμός "breath, spirit." The whole history is a cooling: a root that begins as the word for hot smoke ends, three thousand years later, as the English word for cold clinging wetness. The first English sense was not moisture but bad air — a "damp" was a noxious, suffocating exhalation, remembered today in the miners' terms firedamp and chokedamp. As the vapour-image condensed, the noun broadened to mist and fog and then humidity, the adjective settled on "slightly wet" (sixteenth century), and the verb travelled from "to choke, stifle" (damp a fire) to "to dull, depress" (dampen spirits). Every modern sense, from the building's damp-proof course to the piano's damper, is a stage of that long cooling of vapour into wetness.
