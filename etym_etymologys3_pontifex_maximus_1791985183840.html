A title in two words

Pontifex Maximus is not a single lexical item but a Latin noun phrase, and English has kept it intact, in Latin word order, as the official name of an office. The grammar is simple: pontifex is the head noun, maximus the superlative adjective agreeing with it, so the phrase means “the greatest pontiff”. Latin inscriptions abbreviate it PONT MAX, the form in which it appears on countless imperial coins and stones. Because the phrase is a title, it behaves in English like a proper name: it is capitalised, it takes the definite article, and it is not normally pluralised.

Pontifex: the word and the debate

The old and still standard explanation, already given by Varro, derives pontifex from pons, pontis “bridge” and the combining element -fex “maker”, so “bridge-maker”. The -fex part is secure: it is the regular compound form of facere “to make, do”, seen in artifex “craftsman”, aurifex “goldsmith”, carnifex “executioner” and opifex “workman”. The pons part is less secure. Latin pons is usually connected with the Indo-European root *pent- “to tread, go, pass”, source also of Sanskrit pánthāḥ “path” and Greek póntos “sea”; on that account the original sense would be “way, passage”, and “bridge” would be a later specialisation. An Etruscan origin for pons has also been suggested. The semantic objection to “bridge-maker” is that the pontiffs were not, in historical times, a guild of engineers; the reply is that the sacred bridge over the Tiber, the Pons Sublicius, was maintained with ritual and that the college had ancient responsibilities for the river crossing. The debate is unresolved, and a careful dictionary says so.

  • Varro: De lingua Latina 5.83 derives pontifices from pons, connecting the priests with the bridge and its rites.
  • The Pons Sublicius: the wooden bridge below the Aventine, rebuilt in wood by tradition and associated with the Argei ceremony, in which straw figures were thrown into the Tiber.
  • The -fex series: artifex, aurifex, carnifex and opifex confirm the pattern; pontifex fits it formally.
  • The uncertainty: neither the meaning of pons at the time of formation nor the original duties of the college can be recovered with confidence.

Maximus: the superlative of greatness

Maximus is the superlative of magnus “great, large”, in the irregular series magnus — maior — maximus. The superlative is formed with the suffix -imus on the stem of magis “more”, from an earlier *mag-s-imus; the x is the regular result of gs. Republican inscriptions often spell it maxumus, with u for the later i, a variation shared by optumus/optimus and minumus/minimus. The root is Indo-European *méǵh₂- “great”, which also yields Greek mégas, Sanskrit mahā- and Armenian mec. In the title, maximus does not mean “very large” but “greatest in rank”: the pontifex maximus is the first among the pontiffs, not a physically enormous one.

The institution

  • Numa and the Regia: Roman tradition ascribed the founding of the pontificate to Numa Pompilius; the Regia in the Forum remained its cult centre.
  • The college: the pontiffs were originally few, increased to eight by the lex Ogulnia of 300 BCE, to fifteen under Sulla and to sixteen under Caesar.
  • The Vestals: the pontifex maximus had authority over the Vestal Virgins, including the power to punish breaches of their vow.
  • Law and time: the college kept the calendar, the annals and the rules of adoption, marriage and burial; control of intercalation gave the pontiff leverage over every public deadline.
  • Election: co-option by the college gave way in 104 BCE to election by the tribal assembly, a change that made the office openly political.

Caesar, Augustus and the imperial title

Caesar's election in 63 BCE was a turning point in his career and in the history of the office. He defeated two senior aristocrats, Catulus and Servilius Isauricus, and the campaign was notorious for its expense; the pontificate gave him a public house, a sacred inviolability and a platform from which to pursue the consulate. As pontifex maximus he later reformed the calendar, replacing the chaotic Republican system with the Julian year. Augustus, who understood the value of religious office, waited until Lepidus died in 12 BCE before assuming the title, and thereafter every emperor held it. Its presence in the imperial titulature is one reason the title survived the fall of the Republic: it had become part of the vocabulary of legitimate authority.

Gratian, the popes and the modern title

The Christian emperors kept the title until Gratian, probably in 382 CE, declined it under the influence of Ambrose and the Roman senatorial aristocracy. In the Latin West pontifex remained a word for a bishop, and summus pontifex became the ordinary Latin designation of the pope. The fuller Pontifex Maximus appears in papal coins and inscriptions from the Renaissance onward and is listed among the pope's titles; English “supreme pontiff” calques it. Protestant controversialists, from Luther's circle onward, cited the title as proof that the papacy had taken over a pagan imperial priesthood — a polemical simplification, since the Christian use of pontifex grew out of late-antique episcopal language rather than a single act of transfer.

English usage and connotation

English uses the phrase in three registers. In classical scholarship it is the neutral name of the office. In Catholic and ecumenical writing it is honorific and technical. In journalism it is often playful, applied to the senior figure of a profession — “the Pontifex Maximus of British theatre”. The derived words have drifted further: pontifical can mean “papal” but also “pompous”, and pontificate now usually means to hold forth dogmatically rather than to celebrate a pontifical rite. That drift is a neat illustration of how a religious title can become a comment on manner.
