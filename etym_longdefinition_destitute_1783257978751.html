In this sentence — from Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, describing a city whose public buildings "were still adorned with the sculptures of Polycletus and the paintings of Apelles" while its scholars toiled — destitute is used in its abstract, Augustan construction destitute of taste: the "laborious pedants" are utterly devoid of aesthetic taste and judgment. The force of the word is absolute and damning. Gibbon's irony is layered: these pedants are laborious, industrious, diligent — they study the masterpieces of Polycletus and Apelles with enormous effort — yet they are entirely without the one quality that would let them truly understand what they labour over. Destitute here does not mean poor; it means emptied, lacking to the very last degree. The tone is elegant, withering, and lapidary — the vocabulary of a historian who condemns by understatement, using a formal, almost legal word to deliver a devastating judgment on pedantry.

The senses of destitute in English. The word has a small, well-defined family of senses, and knowing the family is the key to the card.

  • 1. Destitute of + noun — 'entirely devoid of' (16th–17th century construction, still alive in formal prose). This is the card's sense: destitute of taste, destitute of merit, destitute of the common feelings of humanity. The construction is absolute: not a deficiency but an emptiness.
  • 2. Of persons — 'without means, extremely poor, penniless' (the modern primary sense, 19th century onwards). A destitute family, destitute refugees, a destitute mother: here the word means having no money, food, shelter or support at all — the language of charity, census, and social policy.
  • 3. Obsolete — 'abandoned, deserted, forsaken' (the literal Latin sense, English 15th century). A destitute friend or a destitute widow, 'left alone' in the strict Latin sense of dēstitūtus. Now obsolete, but it is the historical bridge between 'set down / abandoned' and 'lacking'.
  • 4. Grammar and derivatives. Today destitute is an adjective; the abstract noun is destitution ('extreme want'), and the adverb destitutely is rare. Older English also used destitute as a verb ('to forsake') and as a noun ('a person abandoned'), both now obsolete.

Semantic bridge. The Latin past participle dēstitūtus meant 'set down, put away, abandoned'. From 'abandoned' English built, in the 16th–17th centuries, the abstract absolute 'destitute of X = entirely without X', and from that abstract emptiness the 19th century built the concrete social sense 'utterly without means'. One could say the word travelled from a verb's passive ('left behind') to a negation of possession ('without') to a social category ('poor'). Each new sense rested on the previous one, and the absolute force of the word never weakened.

Boundaries among near-synonyms. Devoid of is the near-twin of sense 1, equally absolute but more neutral and cerebral. Bereft of implies sorrowful or violent loss (one is bereft of a loved one, rarely of taste). Lacking and wanting allow degree ('lacking in taste' suggests a deficiency, not necessarily an emptiness). Deficient in is the clinical comparative term, admitting that some is present but not enough. Impoverished and penniless are the economic neighbours of sense 2, but impoverished can be relative ('an impoverished country') while destitute insists on the absolute bottom. Poor is the general word of limited means; destitute is poor at the extreme.

Collocations and register. Destitute of taste / merit / feeling / scruple; utterly destitute; a destitute family; destitute refugees; reduced to destitution; legal and philanthropic destitution (the condition, often defined in law). Register is formal to solemn; in the sentence of the card the word is doing its most Augustan work, converting a mere absence of refinement into a complete vacancy — the pedants are not merely poor in taste, they are emptied of it.
