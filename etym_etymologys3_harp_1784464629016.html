The word harp is one of the oldest instrument-names in English and in the Germanic languages, and its biography is unusually stable on the surface — the noun has meant “the framed, plucked string instrument” for over a thousand years — while hiding a genuinely mysterious deep origin and a lively figurative career in the verb.

The Proto-Germanic layer and its uncertainty

All the early Germanic languages attest the word, which points to a common Proto-Germanic form reconstructed as *harpǽ or *harpō. The reflexes are: Old English hearpe, Old Norse harpa, Old High German harpha (modern German Harfe), and Middle Dutch harpe. Because the word is present across West and North Germanic, it must have existed in Proto-Germanic well before the branches separated, by roughly the early first millennium CE. Beyond that the trail fails. Handbooks mark the ultimate origin as unknown: the word may be a Germanic culture-word of substrate (pre-Indo-European) origin, and older speculative derivations — connecting it to an Indo-European idea of “hook, curve, pluck” or to a verbal sense of gripping strings — have never won consensus. No accepted cognate set exists outside Germanic, and the word is emphatically not a borrowing from Latin or Greek: the classical cithara / kithara line (which yields zither and guitar in modern English) is a separate word-history entirely. An honest etymology starts at Proto-Germanic and says “uncertain deeper origin,” rather than inventing a false PIE tree.

Old English: the harp of the hall

In Old English, hearpe names the instrument of the Anglo-Saxon hall — the poet's and nobleman's companion. The great vernacular poem Beowulf (8th–11th c.) pictures Heorot, the mead-hall, where the scop (poet) sings to the hearpe; the harp is the emblem of song, gift-giving, and the social order of the hall. It is precisely this bardic, royal association — the harp as the voice of a people's story — that the word carried into Middle English and that Tolkien draws on centuries later.

Middle English and the medieval harp

Middle English harpe continues unchanged in sense. The medieval English harp was a triangular frame instrument, often carried and played standing or walking by minstrels; the word appears in romances, lyrics, and ballads, and in the Arthurian and Celtic traditions the harp is the instrument of the great bardic figures (Taliesin in Wales, the harp at the feasts of Arthur). It is in this period that the harp's association with Wales and Ireland, already old in Celtic culture, becomes visible in English texts — the Welsh and Irish harpers were famous throughout Britain.

Early Modern: the figurative verb is born

  • The noun stays rock-stable: “harp” means the framed plucked instrument, now increasingly the elegant court harp.
  • The verb “to harp” (play the harp) coexists, and out of it grows the great figurative idiom “to harp on” — to dwell repeatedly, tiresomely on one matter. Shakespeare uses it in Hamlet (“harping on my daughter”), and “to harp on a string” is proverbial in 16th–17th century English.
  • The transfer is purely metaphorical: as the harper's fingers return again and again to the same strings, so a person “harps on” the same grievance or theme. This figurative branch, born in the Early Modern period, has outlived the plain verb in ordinary speech — most modern speakers meet “harp” first as “harping on about something.”

18th–19th centuries: the national and orchestral harp

In the 18th century the concert harp (with its growing system of hooks and later pedals) became an orchestral and salon instrument, and the word acquired its technical compounds: pedal harp, lever harp, double-action harp. Meanwhile the Celtic harp became a national emblem: the harp on Irish coinage and state arms (Ireland's symbol since the Middle Ages, standardized in the 20th century), and the Welsh triple-harp tradition. The instrument thus carried both a high-art European career and a powerful national-symbolic one.

Modern senses and the 20th century

  • The noun remains the everyday name of the instrument; the harp seal and other shape-metaphors show the word's continued productivity by analogy.
  • The idiom “harp on” remains the dominant verbal sense in modern English, with “harp on about” common in colloquial speech.
  • The early-music and folk revivals restored the older Celtic and frame harps to public performance, so “harp” spans from folk instrument to concert grand.

In The Hobbit

Thorin's harp, wrapped in green cloth, is the most deliberate musical symbol in the Unexpected Party. The harp is the instrument of the northern bardic-royal tradition — the instrument of kings, poets, and the lost halls of the Mountain. By giving the exiled prince the harp, and by wrapping it like treasure, Tolkien makes Thorin's identity visible before he ever plays: he is the heir of a royal line, and the harp is his voice of claim and loss. The word's long Germanic and bardic history — from the harps of Heorot to the harps of Wales and Ireland — is compressed into that green cloth, and when the dwarves' song swells, the harp speaks for the Mountain itself.
