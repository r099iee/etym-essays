Rune is a character of the old Germanic alphabets — chiefly the futharks (the Elder Futhark of twenty-four runes and the Younger Futhark of sixteen) — and, by extension, any mark felt to be cryptic, magical, or charged with hidden meaning. Historically runes were letters designed for carving into wood, bone, and stone: angular, vertical-stroke shapes that could be cut with a knife along the grain. Culturally they collected associations of secrecy, counsel, and spell, because the very word-family from which "rune" comes meant "secret" and "whisper" as well as "written character". A rune is, in its old double life, a letter that is also a secret — writing as exclusive lore, the possession of those who can read it. On Thrór's Map in The Hobbit, Gandalf points to "that rune on the West side" and "the other runes" in red, and the narrator adds that the runes are in red on the map: here the sense is concrete cartographic letters of the dwarven mode of writing that Tolkien uses for dwarf-maps — secret writing that marks a hidden door. The plural is used for the set of marks; the word is treated as a technical, antiquarian, map-room term with a magical charge. The tone is neither vague mysticism nor dry scholarship but the precise meeting of both: readable letters (to those who know them) that keep a secret. Primary senses
  • A letter of the ancient Germanic runic alphabets (Elder and Younger Futhark, Anglo-Frisian futhorc): a carved character of wood, stone, bone, or metal, attested in inscriptions from the early centuries CE.
  • By extension, any mark or symbol felt to be mysterious, cryptic, or charged with occult or magical significance — "the runes of the door", "reading the runes".
  • The old abstract sense, preserved in archaic and poetic use: a secret, a mystery, a confidential counsel or whisper (the ancient meaning of the word-family before it specialized to the letter).
Polysemy and secondary modern senses
  • Programming: in the Go programming language, a "rune" is a single Unicode code point — an integer representing one character — a modern technical sense of the word as the atomic unit of written text.
  • Computing and interface culture, colloquially: "reading the runes" means trying to discern hidden meaning from obscure signs (data, tea-leaves, omens) — a metaphor built on the ancient divinatory association.
  • Esoteric and neo-pagan practice: runes are used as a system of divination and ritual writing (rune-casting, rune-magic), continuing the Germanic belief that the letters carried power.
  • Fantasy and gaming: runes are carved symbols of power, magic, and ancient knowledge — the sense popularized by fantasy fiction, where map-runes mark hidden doors, as here.
  • Linguistics and epigraphy: "runic" is the adjective of the script; runic inscriptions are a major source for the early history of the Germanic languages.
The semantic history of rune is a single elegant arc: from a word meaning secret counsel and whisper, to the carved letters that kept secrets, to any mark charged with hidden meaning — and finally to the modern programmer's term for a character, the atomic unit of text. Every modern sense, from Go's rune to the fantasy sigil, carries the ancient double meaning of secrecy and writing.
