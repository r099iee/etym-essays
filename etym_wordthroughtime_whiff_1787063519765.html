- 1580s: literal physical sense — “a sudden gust of wind; a puff of air or smoke”; purely a weather and atmosphere word.
- 1590s: extends to the scent carried by the air — “a faint odour”; perception shifts from the air itself to what the air delivers to the nose.
- 17th–19th c.: everyday use for tobacco smoke, sea air and perfume; then the figurative “trace/hint” sense emerges in prose (a whiff of danger), shifting perception from literal smell to atmospheric suggestion.
- 19th-c. America: baseball slang — “to whiff” = to strike out, the bat meeting only air; the word becomes part of sporting colour.
- Modern English: both literal (faint smell/air) and figurative (a whiff of scandal) senses are equally alive; the word retains its essential meaning of faintness and brevity, and now sounds lively and colloquial.
