• Knowledge: Similar because both denote awareness and understanding; differs because knowledge is static, passive, and vast — the possession of facts — while cognizance is narrower and more active, the deliberate taking of notice directed at something specific.
  • Awareness: Similar because both mean being conscious of something; differs because awareness is the everyday, perceptual, bodily counterpart — one is aware of a noise or a danger — while cognizance is formal and institutional, awareness raised to the level of an official act.
  • Recognition: Similar because both denote acknowledgment of a fact or quality; differs because recognition can be instantaneous, perceptual, and personal (recognizing a face or a voice), while cognizance specifically means the deliberate, official notice taken of a fact, often in law or scholarship.
  • Notice: Similar because both mean attention paid to something; differs because notice is general and can be casual — one notices a stain or a glance — while cognizance implies the formal, deliberate acknowledgment of a fact by a person or authority, as in ‘judicial cognizance’.
  • Perception: Similar because both concern the mind taking in something; differs because perception is primarily sensory and immediate — seeing, hearing, feeling — while cognizance is intellectual and formal, the acknowledgment rather than the raw sensing.
  • Cognition: Similar because both derive from the same Latin root of knowing; differs because cognition is the technical psychological term for all mental processes of knowing, while cognizance is the practical, formal word for taking notice of a particular thing.
  • Apprehension: Similar because both mean the grasping or understanding of something; differs because apprehension is either abstract comprehension or anxious fear, and its ‘understanding’ sense is literary and formal, while cognizance is the working word of courts and records.
  • Discernment: Similar because both involve perceiving clearly; differs because discernment stresses the acuteness or judgment in perceiving — seeing what is not obvious — while cognizance stresses the mere formal acknowledgment of a fact, without any demand of insight.
  • Consciousness: Similar because both concern awareness; differs because consciousness is the general state of being awake and aware, or the mind itself, while cognizance is directed, specific knowledge of a particular matter, often official.
  • Ken: Similar because both mean the range of what one knows; differs because ken is a poetic or dialectal noun (‘beyond one’s ken’) and informal, while cognizance is formal and legal-administrative in flavour.
