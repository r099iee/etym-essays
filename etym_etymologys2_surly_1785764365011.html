SURLY descends, by a chain of social pejoration, from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “old”, through Latin, Old French, and Middle English. The chain: 1) PIE *sen- “old” (reconstructed; a root of age with reflexes in Latin senex “old man”, Greek hénos “old”, Sanskrit sána- “old, ancient”, Old Irish sen “old”, Welsh hen “old”, and Lithuanian sėnas “old”) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin senex “old, aged; an old man” (adjective and noun, genitive senis; the root’s ordinary Latin form, also in senātus “council of elders” and senēscere “to grow old”) — attested from the 3rd c. BCE. 3) Latin senior “older, elder; of higher rank by age” (comparative of senex, formed with the suffix -ior; the comparative became a noun of rank and a title) — attested in Classical Latin. 4) Vulgar Latin *seior / senior “the elder, the master” (the comparative contracted in speech and became a title of respect; the source of the Romance titles) — Late Latin / early Romance. 5) Old French sire “lord, master” (vocative and nominative title of address, developed from senior and used of feudal lords and royalty; the base of sieur, monsieur) — attested from the 9th–11th c. 6) Anglo-Norman / Middle English sir “lord; knightly or polite title of address” (the title of address borrowed into English, first of lords and knights, later of ordinary courtesy) — attested from c. 13th c. 7) Middle English sirly / syrly “lordly, majestic, befitting a sir” (adjective formed from the noun sir with the suffix -ly; the root form of modern surly) — attested c. 14th c. 8) Early Modern English surly “harrogant, haughty, churlish, bad-tempered” (the spelling and pronunciation settled from sirly to surly in the 16th century; the sense pejorated from “lordly” to “harrogant” to “bad-tempered”) — attested 16th c. 9) Modern English surly “bad-tempered, churlish, unfriendly in manner or appearance” — SURLY. NOTE ON DEBATE: The chain itself is uncontroversial and well documented. The only points of nuance are (1) the Romance contraction of senior — the exact shape of the intermediate form between Latin senior and Old French sire (commonly written *seior) is reconstructed, not directly attested, though the steps are secure; and (2) the spelling shift from sirly to surly, which is a documented early-modern development rather than an etymological change — the modern form obscures the link to sir, but the historical connection is certain.
