adorned (v., past tense and past participle of adorn; also used adjectivally) — decorated or beautified with ornaments; fitted out with something that enhances appearance, dignity, or value. The word carries a formal, literary colouring: to adorn is to add ornament not merely for utility but for beauty and honour.

Primary sense (concrete decoration). To decorate a person, building, surface, or object with fine things — jewels, flowers, paintings, sculpture, embroidery. In this card's context, the public buildings of Constantinople "were still adorned with the sculptures of Polycletus and the paintings of Apelles": the participle registers the persistence of decorative splendour even as the civilisation that produced it fades. The concrete sense presupposes an agent who places ornament upon a recipient, hence the regular preposition with.

Polysemy & secondary modern meanings:

  • Moral and spiritual adornment — "adorned with virtues," "adorned with grace": character, conduct, or reputation is said to be adorned when enhanced by admirable qualities. The semantic bridge is metaphor: the ornaments of the soul are its excellences, exactly as physical ornaments beautify the body (cf. 1 Peter 3:5, "the holy women... who trusted in God, adorned themselves").
  • Rhetorical and stylistic adornment — "adorned prose" or "adorned speech": writing and oratory are embellished with figures, elegant diction, and literary ornament. The semantic bridge runs through classical rhetoric, where ornatus (Latin "equipment, embellishment") named the ornaments of style; "adorned" thus describes language dressed in the finery of rhetoric.
  • Architectural and monumental sense — "an adorned façade," "adorned with pilasters and friezes": the participle is standard in art and architectural history for decorative enrichment, carrying a technical neutrality absent from everyday "decorated."
  • Ceremonial and honourific sense — "adorned with medals," "adorned with laurels": persons are adorned by the honours they wear or receive; the ornament is the badge of distinction, and the word shades into "honoured, distinguished."
  • Furnishing / equipping (archaic-lexical trace) — Latin adornāre meant "to furnish, fit out, equip" as well as "to decorate"; English adorned occasionally preserves a faint trace of "furnished, supplied" in older or figurative prose, though the equip-sense is now obsolete and the decor-sense has swallowed it.
  • Adorned as adjective vs. participle — as an adjective it is stative ("an adorned room" = a room that is beautiful); as a participle it is eventive ("the hall, adorned by the craftsmen, dazzled the guests"). English does not formally distinguish the two, so context decides.

Etymological bridge and register. Latin adornāre combined ad- "to, toward" with ōrnāre "to fit out, equip, decorate"; ōrnāre itself is traditionally connected with the PIE root *h₂er- "to fit together, to join" (whence Greek harmonia "fitting, harmony" and Latin ars "art," artus "joint"). The word therefore carries within it the idea of adding, fitting, and arranging — a subtle etymological sense that "decorated," its more common synonym, entirely lacks. Register-wise, adorned is literary and dignified, the word of poetry, scripture, and formal prose, where "decorated" is the neutral everyday term and "decked out" is colloquial.

Sense development summary. From "furnished, equipped" (Latin) through Old French adorner to Middle English adornen (attested from c. 1300), the word's centre of gravity moved steadily toward the beautiful: to fit out a house with what it needs became to fit it out with what delights. The participle adorned crystallises the finished state — the result of having been beautified — and is the natural form for describing enduring objects (buildings, monuments, brides, books) that remain ornamented over time, exactly as Macaulay uses it of the still-gleaming public buildings of a dying empire.
