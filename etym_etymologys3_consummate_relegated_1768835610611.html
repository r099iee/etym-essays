Consummate: from the summit to the sum

The biography of consummate begins not with completion but with height. Its deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *upér, 'over, above,' a root of great procreative power.

  • Worldwide cognates: in Indo-Iranian it issued in Sanskrit upári and its Hindi descendant ūpar (ऊपर), 'above'; in Greek hypér, 'over, beyond,' and the English prefix hyper-; in the Germanic branch it produced English over itself, through Proto-Germanic *uber and Old English ofer; and in Latin it generated super, 'above, over.'
  • The Latin ascent: from super came the adjective superus, 'that which is above,' and from its superlative summus, 'highest, utmost,' came summa — the 'top' of a column of figures, and hence, by a beautiful metonymy, the total itself, the whole amount gathered to its peak. English adopted sum directly (13th century), and from the same summus sprang summit (via Old French som, 'the top') and supreme (supremus, 'highest'), while plain super and its comparative superior entered English in the 14th–17th centuries.
The final stage is compounding: Latin joined the completive prefix con-, 'together, wholly,' to summa to form consummāre, 'to add up all the parts, to bring to a total,' which in its participle consummātus meant 'finished, perfected.' English took consummate in the 15th century, first as a verb ('to complete, to finish') and then as an adjective ('complete in the highest degree').

  • Religious gravity: the word is famous as the last utterance of Christ on the cross in the Vulgate — consummatum est, 'it is finished' — and that sacramental finality still clings to it: a consummate thing is not merely done but totalled, every element summed to its top, nothing left over.
  • Semantic career: from 'to bring to a total' (arithmetic) the verb widened to 'to finish' (any process), then narrowed again into the ritual and legal registers — consummating a marriage, an agreement — while the adjective rose through artistic and social judgement until it crowned persons and works with terminal perfection: a consummate craftsman, consummate skill, perfection not merely flawless but summed to its ceiling.
  • In the passage: the judges' 'most consummate' carvings are those whose skill has been summed to its highest point, exhausting the possibilities of the medium; and the Vulgate's shadow gives the phrase its severity — the annual judgement is a kind of terminal verdict, a perfection pronounced and then, in the next clause, dispatched to the Hall of the Bright Carvings.
Sound changes along the chain are modest but real: PIE *upér → Latin super (the initial sibilant is a Latin development); super → summus (superlative suffix *-is-mo-, with loss of the medial -p-); summus → summa (neuter plural substantivised); summa + con- → consummāre (the -n- of the prefix assimilating before -s-). English borrowed the participle form directly, giving the single spelling consummate for both verb and adjective — one of the few English words in which stress alone distinguishes the parts of speech, the verb stressing the final -ate and the adjective the middle syllable. The word entered literary and legal English fully formed, and five centuries of use have only deepened the double image it carries: the summit and the sum, the highest point and the finished total, perfection as a quantity added up to its ceiling.


Relegated: the envoy sent back

The story of relegate begins with gathering. Its deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *leg-, 'to collect, to gather' — a root whose children show a remarkable split: in Greek it became λέγειν (légein), 'to gather, then to speak,' giving lógos, 'word, reason,' and English logic, dialogue, prologue, geology; in Latin it became legere, 'to gather, choose, read.'

  • From gathering to choosing to reading: legere began as 'to gather' (a harvest, an army), moved to 'to choose' (to gather out, to select), and then to 'to read' — because reading was conceived as 'gathering' letters from the page. The same verb underlies collect, elect, select, intelligent ('able to choose between'), diligent ('singling out'), colleague (a fellow 'chosen together'), legion (a 'levy,' a gathered army), and lecture (a 'reading').
  • The legal turn: from legere (or, as modern scholarship prefers, from lēx, lēgis, 'law' — itself of debated origin but frequently connected to legere, a law being something 'read out' to the people) Latin derived lēgāre, 'to send with a commission, to dispatch as an envoy.' A lēgātus, 'legate,' is one sent with legal standing; the same verb gives legate, legation, delegate, and the Roman cousin of colleague.
The crucial compound is relegāre: the prefix re-, 'back, away,' joined to lēgāre produced 'to send back or away,' and in classical Latin the verb carried a double force — 'to banish, to send into exile,' and 'to send on a mission.' English took the past participle relegātus as relegate around 1500.

  • Semantic career: from the legal-administrative dispatch of Roman public life, the word entered English as a formal act of sending away — banishment with paperwork. In the 18th–19th centuries it spread from persons to things: furniture relegated to the attic, matters relegated to the footnotes, nations relegated to the margins of history — always a removal from the centre to the periphery. In the 20th century the sporting sense fixed the word in public speech: a club 'relegated to a lower division' — the standard term for official demotion. Throughout, the word retains its two Roman memories at once: the formality of the envoy (a dispatch, not a fall) and the loss of exile (a sending away, often for ever).
  • In the passage: the carvings judged 'most consummate' are 'subsequently relegated to the Hall of the Bright Carvings' — dispatched officially from the scene of judgement to a secondary hall; the bureaucratic shade of relegate makes the removal feel recorded, deliberate, irreversible, the ceremonial complement of the verb consummate that precedes it.
Sound changes: PIE *leg- → Latin legere; legere → lēgāre (the denominative -ā- suffix and lengthened vowel); lēgāre + re- → relegāre; participle relegātus → English relegate (borrowed direct, the final -us dropped). The modern English pronunciation reduces the penultimate syllable — the word has lost the full weight of its Roman disyllabic participle and stresses the first syllable, so that 'relegated' carries its four English syllables lightly even as it describes the heaviest of administrative acts. From Roman law to the football league table, the word has never stopped sending things away — and every modern use still sounds the old double note of the commission and the exile.
