A cravat is a band or scarf of cloth worn around the neck, tucked into or tied at the throat, and historically the direct ancestor of the modern necktie, bow tie, and ascot. In its classic 17th- to 19th-century form it was a long linen, muslin, or silk cloth wrapped around the collar, knotted in front, with the ends tucked into the waistcoat or left to hang. To "adjust the cravat" — as in the source sentence, where a speaker slips into a shirt and starts adjusting it — is to arrange that knot and drape so it sits correctly: a small, intimate, gentlemanly gesture that signals dressing, self-presentation, and social readiness.

The garment's cultural meaning far exceeds its fabric. Introduced to Paris by Croatian mercenaries in the 1630s whose knotted neckerchiefs caught the eye of Louis XIV's court, the cravate became aModesymbol of elegance, masculinity, and attention to detail. By the Regency and Victorian eras in Britain, how a man tied his cravat — the Oriental, the Osbaldeston, the Byron, Beau Brummell's starched masterpiece — announced taste, rank, profession, and even political attitude. The 19th-century dandy's morning ritual at the mirror, struggling with starch and folds, is a staple of fiction precisely because the object concentrates anxiety about appearance into one adjustable knot. The quoted scene replays that ritual in miniature.

Today the word survives in three overlapping uses. In fashion history and formal wear it denotes the historical neckcloth itself. In modern retail it often denotes a looser, softer descendant: a silk pouch-like tie worn with morning dress or wedding outfits, sometimes called a wedding cravat or ascot cravat, distinct from a stiff long tie. In military and medical contexts a triangular cravat bandage is a folded cloth sling for the arm or head, named for its resemblance to the neckcloth. The through-line is a folded cloth band whose function is to bind, dress, or support the neck region.

  • Historical neckcloth (17th-19th c.): the primary sense — a wide linen or muslin cloth wound around the neck, ancestor of all modern ties. Example: "Beau Brummell spent an hour tying his cravat." The semantic bridge is direct continuity of dress: the same object evolves in width, stiffness, and knot.
  • Modern wedding / formal ascot cravat: a soft gathered silk neckerchief worn with a wing collar and morning coat, fastened with a pin or tucked. Example: "The groom wore a silver cravat." This preserves the prestige association while adapting to contemporary formal codes where a day tie would be underdressed.
  • Military stock / steinkirk: 18th-century variants — a stiff leather or linen stock for soldiers, or a loosely knotted lace cravat (steinkirk) after the 1692 Battle of Steenkerque. These show how one garment split into functional (military uniformity) and fashionable (aristocratic negligence) branches.
  • Triangular cravat bandage: in first aid, a large triangular cloth folded into a band for slings, head dressings, or splint ties. Example: "Immobilize the forearm with a cravat bandage." The bridge is purely formal resemblance: any folded neckerchief-like band borrowed the fashionable name.
  • Figurative / metonymic dandyism: in literature, cravat can stand for foppishness, fastidiousness, or old-fashioned gentlemanliness itself. "A cravat-and-top-hat sort of man" means a traditionalist. The part stands for the persona because the object was the most labor-intensive part of the toilette.

For learners, register matters. Cravat is now slightly archaic and markedly British-formal; Americans more often say ascot for the modern version. Using it of an ordinary long tie would sound quaint or erroneous. Collocations are telling: adjust / tie / knot / arrange one's cravat; silk / linen / lace cravat; cravat pin / cravat bandage. The verb of handling is almost always careful manipulation, reinforcing the idea of dress as performance.
