The history of the word solidarities (plural of solidarity) is one of the most intellectually fascinating semantic journeys in modern social philosophy, tracing a trajectory from a technical Roman legal concept of shared financial debt to a universal rallying cry of labor democracy, sociological theory, and multifaceted political alliance.

The ultimate root of the word is the Proto-Indo-European root *solh₂- (or *solwos), signifying "whole, intact, well-kept, undivided, or healthy." This productive root yielded cognates across many Indo-European branches, including Sanskrit sarva- ("all, every, whole"), Ancient Greek hólos (ὅλος, "whole, entire" > English holistic, holocaust), and Old Latin sollus ("whole").

In the Italic branch, the root gave rise to the Proto-Italic adjective *solidus, which in classical Latin emerged as solidus, meaning "firm, dense, unyielding, whole, undivided, complete, and sound." From solidus, Roman currency derived the name of its gold coin (the solidus, introduced by Constantine), which subsequently gave rise to Italian soldo, French solde, and English soldier (literally, one who is paid in solid coin).

In classical Roman jurisprudence, solidus developed a precise legal application through the phrase in solidum (literally, "for the whole"):

  • In the Roman law of contracts and torts, an obligatio in solidum designated joint and several liability, where multiple co-debtors were each bound to pay the undivided total debt, or where multiple creditors were each entitled to claim the entire sum.
  • This Roman legal formula persisted throughout medieval civil law and was codified into the French Code Civil (Napoleonic Code) under the adjective solidaire and the noun solidarité.

During the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, French political thinkers and early socialists effected a dramatic semantic revolution:

  • Writers such as Pierre Leroux (who claimed to have introduced solidarité into political philosophy in the 1830s), Auguste Comte, and Charles Fourier seized upon the legal metaphor of solidarité. They argued that just as co-debtors in law are collectively bound to the whole debt, all human beings in a society are organically and morally bound in a web of mutual interdependence and shared responsibility.
  • Solidarité thus emerged as the secular, scientific counterpart to Christian charity (charité) and republican fraternity (fraternité).

The word was borrowed into English in the 1840s (initially italicized as a French term in socialist and reformist publications). Over the next century, it became thoroughly anglicized as solidarity, serving as a defining rallying cry for the international trade union movement ("Solidarity Forever").

The plural form solidarities developed in twentieth-century sociology, political philosophy, and critical theory:

  • Sociologists following Émile Durkheim used solidarities to describe the competing, plural systems of cohesion (mechanical versus organic) operating simultaneously within diverse social orders.
  • In post-colonial, feminist, and poststructuralist theory (such as Roland Barthes's textual critiques), solidarities captured the multiple, intersecting, and sometimes contradictory networks of intellectual, cultural, and political consensus that bind human groups together or protect institutional orthodoxies.
