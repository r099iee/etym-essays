• Aeschylus: Similar because both are towering founders of ancient Greek tragedy; differs because Aeschylus is earlier (c. 525–456 BCE), wrote interconnected trilogies, used two actors, and emphasized grand theological and cosmic themes, whereas Sophocles introduced the third actor, expanded scenic realism, and focused on individual human psychology and ethical choices.
  • Euripides: Similar because both were master dramatists of 5th-century BCE Athens; differs because Euripides is more skeptical, iconoclastic, and psychological, portraying human passions and societal hypocrisies rawly, while Sophocles presents idealized, noble characters confronting divine destiny.
  • Aristophanes: Similar because both were contemporary 5th-century Athenian playwrights; differs because Aristophanes was the master of Old Comedy (satirizing political leaders and philosophers), whereas Sophocles wrote serious high tragedy.
  • Seneca: Similar because both wrote influential classical tragedies on Greek mythological themes (e.g. Oedipus, Phaedra); differs because Seneca was a 1st-century CE Roman Stoic philosopher whose plays were written for recitation and emphasized graphic violence and philosophical stoicism.
  • Shakespeare: Similar because both represent the pinnacle of high tragedy in their respective civilizations; differs because Shakespeare wrote in Early Modern English across expansive Elizabethan theatrical stages, whereas Sophocles worked within the strict formal constraints of classical Athenian civic drama.
