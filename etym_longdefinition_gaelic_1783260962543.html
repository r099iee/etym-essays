Gaelic (adj. / n.) — relating to the Gaels, the Goidelic branch of the Celtic peoples, or their languages, specifically the speech of Ireland and of the Scottish Highlands. On this card the word appears in the sentence "In our island the Latin appears never to have superseded the old Gaelic speech": here it names the indigenous Celtic tongue of the island, the language that Latin failed to displace and that later held its ground against the incoming Germanic speech.

Sense 1 — The linguistic sense (the primary sense). Gaelic denotes the Goidelic (Q-Celtic) branch of the Insular Celtic languages: Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig), and the closely related Manx (Gaelg). In British usage "Gaelic" most often means Scottish Gaelic specifically; in Ireland the same language is normally called "Irish." Together these languages are set against the Brythonic branch (Welsh, Cornish, Breton).

Sense 2 — The ethnic sense. As an adjective, Gaelic describes what belongs to the Gaels — their language, music, song, dance, dress, and inherited culture. The noun "the Gaels" is the people themselves: the Irish, the Scottish Highlanders, and (linguistically) the Manx.

Sense 3 — Sports and institutional branding. "Gaelic games," "Gaelic football," and hurling are the traditional Irish national sports organized by the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA), founded in 1884. Here the word has become a badge of national identity and revival, deliberately set against English and international sporting codes.

Sense 4 — Cultural revivalism and heritage. Since the 18th–19th-century Highland revival and the Ossianic vogue, "Gaelic" has been freighted with a wistful, folkloric aura of ancientness — Highland music, tartan, song-collecting, and endangered-language advocacy. In popular writing the word is sometimes used loosely for any surviving Celtic tongue, though specialists apply it precisely to the Goidelic branch.

  • Register: standard in modern English; neutral and precise in Scotland ("Scottish Gaelic"), while in Ireland the language is normally called "Irish" or "Gaeilge."
  • Collocations: Gaelic speech/language, Scottish Gaelic, Irish Gaelic, Gaelic manuscripts, Gaelic revival, Gaelic football, the Gaelic Athletic Association, Gaelic song.
  • Historical bridge: the English word was formed in the 17th century from the ethnic name Gael (ultimately from Old Irish Goídel, itself borrowed from Welsh) plus the suffix -ic, on the model of names of languages and peoples such as Arabic or Celtic. The shift from "language of the Gaels" to "national sports and cultural identity" is a late, ideological extension of the same ethnic name.
  • Pronunciation note: the card's mnemonic gives "GAY-lick"; the final -ic is unstressed, and the word is never pronounced with a hard second g ("ga-LEEK").
  • Demographic note: all three Goidelic languages are minority tongues today — Irish is the first official language of the Republic of Ireland but daily-spoken by a small percentage; Scottish Gaelic and Manx survive in small, revitalizing communities after centuries of decline — so the word is inseparable from modern endangered-language advocacy.
