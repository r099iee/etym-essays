An expressive word from the north. Gaping reaches English from Old Norse gapa, "to open the mouth wide," a word of the Viking north recorded from about the ninth century and clearly expressive in origin: the open a vowels seem to imitate the very act of dropping the jaw. Beneath Old Norse lies a Proto-Germanic verb *gapōn with the same sense, attested by its family in Middle Dutch gapen, Old High German gaffōn (which gives modern German gaffen, "to stare nosily"), and Old Norse gap, the noun for an open mouth or chasm. Beyond Germanic there is nothing solid: the root is classed as onomatopoeic or expressive, the kind of word languages coin because the sound fits the action, and no confident Indo-European etymon exists.

Middle English: a body word with a wonder-struck face. English took the verb in with the Norse borrowings of the 13th–14th centuries as gapen. From the start it had a double life: physical (to open the mouth wide, to yawn) and psychological (to stare with open mouth — first in surprise, then in wonder or idiocy). Chaucer uses it for gaping crowds and gaping clowns; the animal world too, as gaping jaws and gaping beaks appear in bestiaries and natural history. The noun gap also arrived from Old Norse gap "chasm, open mouth," and the two words have grown up together, gap supplying the opening itself and gape the act and the state.

Early Modern English: wonder, craving, and the gaping wound. In the 16th–17th centuries the verb flowered. "To gape at" meant to stare in astonished admiration or stupid credulity — crowds gape at processions, men gape at wonders — while "to gape for" or "to gape after" meant to crave greedily, to long for something with an open mouth, as a bird gapes for food; Shakespeare works both strands, and satirists make gaping fools of the gullible. At the same time the participle gaping began its long career as an adjective of dramatic openness: gaping wounds appear in medical and martial writing, gaping mouths in sermons, gaping graves in elegy — always with the sense of an opening too wide and too exposed for comfort.

Nineteenth century: the Gothic and the geological gap. The Romantic and Gothic imagination took gaping to heart: gaping chasms, gaping abysses, gaping graves, and gaping doors populate the poetry and fiction of Shelley, Poe, and the Victorian sensation novel. The same era pressed the noun gap into landscape, industry, and political writing — a gap in the hedge, a gap in the market — and "gaping" ran alongside as its vivid partner, describing the dramatic emptiness left by what had been removed.

Modern English: from mouth to metaphor. In the 20th century gap became one of English's most productive abstract nouns (the gender gap, the generation gap, a gap in the market, gap years), and gaping kept its full force as the dramatic adjective for any opening, physical or figurative: a gaping wound, a gaping hole in the argument, a gaping absence. The old wonder-struck sense survives (an audience left gaping), and "gape" is still the exact word for an open mouth, whether of a sleeping commuter, a hungry fledgling, or a baffled tourist.

  • Sound history. ON gapa → ME gapen → E gape /ɡeɪp/ shows the regular lengthening of the open vowel and its modern diphthongisation; the participle gaping /ˈɡeɪpɪŋ/ adds -ing to the long-vowel stem.
  • Attestations. Chaucer's Canterbury Tales; Shakespeare's Julius Caesar ("gaping graves") and elsewhere; Milton's Paradise Lost; Shelley and the Romantics for gaping chasms; Victorian fiction for gaping doors.
  • Cultural note. Because the word has always sat between the body (the mouth) and the world (the hole), it moves with unusual ease between horror, comedy, and bureaucratic metaphor — a genuinely irreplaceable adjective.
