• Squire: Similar because both refer to young aristocratic attendants in the medieval hierarchy of chivalric education; differs because a page was a younger boy (ages 7 to 14) performing light domestic service and courtly errands, whereas a squire was an adolescent youth (ages 14 to 21) who acted as a knight's personal armor-bearer, rode into battle, and underwent direct martial combat training.
  • Attendant: Similar because both denote persons employed to accompany, assist, or wait upon a person of higher status; differs because attendant is a generic, functional term without age, class, or historical connotations, whereas page historically implies noble youth, ceremonial livery, or legislative tradition.
  • Lackey: Similar because both describe domestic servants attending upon wealthy masters; differs because lackey carries a distinctly derogatory, servile connotation implying obsequiousness and menial status, whereas a medieval page was a youth of noble birth destined for knighthood.
  • Footman: Similar because both describe liveried male servants in aristocratic households; differs because a footman was an adult male servant employed for heavy domestic duties (such as opening carriage doors, serving dinner, and running ahead of coaches), whereas a page was an adolescent youth in ceremonial or training roles.
  • Folio: Similar because both designate paper units within books and documents; differs because folio refers to a large sheet of paper folded once to create two leaves (four pages) or a large-format book size, whereas page refers strictly to a single side of any leaf of paper regardless of sheet format.
  • Leaf: Similar because both refer to paper components of a bound manuscript or printed volume; differs because a leaf comprises the entire physical sheet of paper, consisting of two distinct sides (the recto and the verso), whereas a page is strictly one single side of that leaf.
