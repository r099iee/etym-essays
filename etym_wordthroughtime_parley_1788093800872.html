- 1550s: borrowed from French parlée "a talk"; early English sense is broad — any conversation or discussion — and the verb parley means simply "to speak"; an earlier short form "parle" is attested from the 15th century
- late 16th–17th c.: military narrowing — a parley is a conference with an enemy, usually under flag of truce; "to beat/sound a parley" becomes the fixed phrase for the drum or trumpet signal requesting one; drama and war memoirs fill the age with "hold parley"/"call a parley"
- 17th–18th c.: military manuals regulate the signal, the safe conduct and the punishment for abusing a parley; the word is at home in every soldiers' narrative of sieges — the Age of the Musket claims it forever
- 19th c.: the general sense fades; "parley" is now the historian's and novelist's word for enemy conferences under truce
- 20th c.: persists in literary, historical and adventure prose; Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) — the very passage of this card — shows the word at home in epic fantasy
- 2003: Pirates of the Caribbean reintroduces "parley" worldwide as the pirate's right to negotiate under safe conduct — the word is reborn as cinematic vocabulary for any truce-talk
- Today: jocular or faux-antique for any earnest discussion between rivals ("a parley over the merger"); its true home remains the boundary between enemies who have agreed, for a moment, to speak
