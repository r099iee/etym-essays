SCIONS is the plural of scion, a word borrowed from Old French in the fourteenth century; the French word was in turn borrowed from a Germanic (Frankish) source. The chain: 1) (PIE stage, debated) — A deeper connection to a PIE root *ǵey- "to sprout, shoot" has been proposed for the Germanic "sprout" family, but it is not securely established, and the attested record begins with the Germanic forms; see the note below. 2) Proto-Germanic *kīþą / *kīþō "sprout, shoot, germ, young shoot" (reconstructed; attested in the daughters: Old English cīþ "sprout, germ, shoot," Old High German kīdi "sprout," Middle Low German kīt "sprout," Middle Dutch kiede "sprout") — the base of the whole family. 3) Frankish *kīþō "sprout, shoot" (West Germanic, reconstructed) — the form borrowed into Gallo-Romance speech, carried by the Frankish settlement of northern Gaul. 4) Old French cion / sion "shoot, twig, scion (of a tree)" (noun, attested from the 12th–13th c.) — the immediate source of the English word, with only the horticultural sense. 5) Middle English sioun / cioun "a shoot, twig, scion for grafting" (borrowed 14th c.), soon extended figuratively to "a descendant, heir, offspring of a family" (attested from the 14th c.) — the word's double life begins in English. 6) English scion (modern form; the s- spelling, from earlier cion and sion, established in the 17th c.) "a shoot for grafting; a descendant or heir of a prominent family," with the regular plural scions — SCIONS. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The Germanic chain *kīþą → Frankish *kīþō → Old French cion → Middle English sioun → scion is secure and well documented. (2) The deeper PIE connection is uncertain: the Germanic "sprout" family is often linked to a proposed root *ǵey- "to sprout," but the correspondence is not established with certainty and no agreed non-Germanic cognates are attested, so the standard handbooks do not assert a PIE etymon. (3) The spelling history is the word's chief curiosity: French cion/sion was borrowed into English as cion and sion in the 14th century, and the now-universal form scion, with an intrusive s-, is attested from the 17th century; the reason for the added s- is not securely explained.
