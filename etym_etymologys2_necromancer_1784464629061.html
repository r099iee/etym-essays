This compound word has two root elements; each is traced separately. CHAIN 1 — the "necro-" element (death, corpse): Uncertain deep stage: Greek nekrós "corpse, dead body" is derived from the Indo-European root *nek-, "death, to perish," one of the best-attested death-roots of the family (Latin nex "violent death," Sanskrit naśyati "perishes," Avestan nasi- "corpse"); the -ro- suffix formation of nekrós beside nékys (*nék-u-) is clear, but the details of the PIE ablaut are debated. [DEBATED] → Greek nékys (corpse, dead body; from *nék-u-), attested from the 8th century BCE (Homer) → Greek nekrós (corpse, dead body, dead person; -ro- adjective/noun), attested from the 8th century BCE (Homer) → Greek combining form nekro- / necro- "of the dead," used in compounds such as nekromanteía "divination by the dead" (5th century BCE) and in later Latin and modern learned English compounds (necropolis, necrosis) CHAIN 2 — the "-mancer / -mancy" element (diviner, divination): PIE *men- "to think, to be mentally active" (the thinking-root; also source of Greek ménos "mind, force, passion," Latin mēns "mind," English mind) → Greek maínesthai (to be mad, inspired, in a god-driven state; nasal-present verb on *men-), attested from the 8th century BCE → Greek mántis (seer, diviner, prophet; one whose mind is filled by the god), attested from the 8th century BCE (Homer) → Greek mantéia / manteía (divination, prophetic power, oracle), attested from the 5th century BCE → Greek nekromanteía (divination by consulting the dead), the compound of nekro- + -manteia, attested from the 5th century BCE → Latin / Medieval Latin necromantia (the same art), transmitted through the Middle Ages → Old French nigromancie (popular form, reshaped by folk etymology toward Latin niger "black," i.e. "black magic"), attested from the 12th century CE → Middle English nigromancie / necromancie (divination by the dead; dark sorcery), attested from the 13th century CE → Modern English necromancy (the art) and the agent noun necromancer (one who practises it; -er/-mancer pattern), attested from the 16th century CE → NECROMANCER — the card's target form. NOTE on debate: the derivation of Greek mántis from PIE *men- is standard but involves debated morphological details (the -ti-/-t- derivatives and the nasal present); the medieval French reshaping of the word through niger "black" is a genuine and famous folk etymology that permanently altered both the spelling and the connotation of the transmitted word.
