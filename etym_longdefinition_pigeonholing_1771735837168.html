PIGEONHOLING, noun (gerund of the verb pigeonhole). In the Gibbon passage the word names the very thing Gibbon is said not to have done: imposing a novel conceptual framework or a new way of filing the human past into neat categories. "He has not taken a place as the originator of any large new conceptual framework, or any novel way of pigeonholing the human past." The author's point is that Gibbon did not offer a new system that would sort history into tidy, rigid compartments — he is a great narrative historian, not a taxonomist or systematizer. Pigeonholing here carries a slightly dismissive connotation: it is what lesser, more mechanical minds do, fitting complex reality into small fixed boxes. The core senses of PIGEONHOLING / PIGEONHOLE:
  • Assigning something or someone to a category, especially one that is too restrictive or rigid. The central modern sense: to pigeonhole a person or thing is to file them under a fixed label — the actress pigeonholed as a "girl next door," a writer pigeonholed as a genre author — ignoring what does not fit. The gerund names the act itself.
  • Literal sense: to place papers in the pigeonholes of a desk. The original, now rare concrete sense: a pigeonhole was a small compartment or recess, usually in a writing desk or cabinet, for sorting and storing letters and papers. To pigeonhole a document was simply to file it.
  • To set aside and ignore; to shelve. By extension from filing papers away, to pigeonhole a proposal, request, or matter is to put it aside so that it is effectively dropped: "the committee pigeonholed the bill" — set it aside without action.
Secondary and extended senses, with historical bridges:
  • Classification as restriction. The bridge from the desk to the person is the image of a small, fixed compartment: both a paper and a person can be "filed" into a pigeonhole that does not fit them. What begins as neutral filing becomes pejorative when applied to people or complex realities.
  • Social stereotyping. Pigeonholing overlaps with stereotyping and typecasting: assigning individuals to fixed social roles — by gender, class, nationality, or profession — and refusing to see beyond the label. The word is now used critically of exactly such reduction.
  • In scholarship. In the passage, pigeonholing the human past means imposing an overly neat periodization or taxonomy on history — dividing the past into rigid frameworks that distort its fluid reality. The word implies a mechanical, schematizing habit of mind.
The historical bridge that explains the polysemy: the word is a compound of pigeon + hole. The literal pigeonhole is early modern: from the late 17th century it meant a small compartment in a desk, so called because the compartments, arranged in rows, resembled the niches in a dovecote in which pigeons nest — or, by another account, because papers in them were "pigeon-holed" like birds in a roost. The noun is attested from the 1680s; the verb "to pigeonhole" in the literal filing sense from the 1840s; and the figurative sense of "classify restrictively" developed in the later 19th century as bureaucratic filing culture made the desk image universal. The shift from filing to labeling people is complete by the early 20th century. Usage and collocations:
  • Common collocations: pigeonhole someone as, pigeonholing into categories, pigeonhole a bill (set aside), pigeonholed as, a pigeonhole compartment, pigeonholing the past.
  • Register: neutral to informal; the figurative sense is mildly critical or dismissive.
  • Family words: pigeonhole (noun and verb), pigeonholed, pigeonholing; related forms pigeon (bird), and the idiom "pigeonhole principle" in mathematics (if n items are placed in m boxes and n > m, some box holds more than one item).
  • Form: usually hyphenated or written as one word (pigeon-hole / pigeonhole); the gerund form used in the passage is pigeonholing.
