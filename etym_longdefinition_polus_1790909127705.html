Polus (Ancient Greek: Πῶλος, Pôlos; fl. 4th century BCE) was a celebrated ancient Greek tragic actor from the island of Aegina, revered throughout classical antiquity as one of the supreme virtuosos of the Athenian dramatic stage and recognized in theater history as the foundational historical archetype for total emotional immersion (*perezhivanie*) in performance.

In the historical development of dramatic theory, Polus achieved immortal fame through a celebrated historical account recorded by the Roman writer Aulus Gellius in his Attic Nights (Noctes Atticae, VI.5) and referenced in the moral treatises of Plutarch. According to Gellius, while performing the title role of Electra in Sophocles' eponymous masterpiece at the Theatre of Dionysus in Athens, Polus had to perform the climactic scene in which Electra receives the bronze funerary urn containing what she believes to be the cremated remains of her beloved brother Orestes, unleashing a heart-rending lamentation. Having recently suffered the real-world death of his own dearly beloved son, Polus retrieved the actual funerary urn and bones of his child from the family sepulcher and brought them concealed onto the stage. When he embraced the urn and delivered Electra's mournful verses, he wept real tears of profound parental agony, filling the Athenian theater not with fabricated theatrical imitations, but with genuine, unfeigned human sorrow.

Key Historical, Theoretical & Philosophical Dimensions:

  • The Historical Prototype of Emotional Immersion (*Perezhivanie*): Polus' performance stands as the earliest recorded historical instance of what 20th-century Russian director Konstantin Stanislavski conceptualized as perezhivanie ("living the experience") and what Lee Strasberg codified into the "affective memory" technique of the American Method. Rather than mimicking external symptoms of grief, Polus accessed a real, internal emotional trauma to animate his character's tragic reality.
  • The Central Debate of Acting Aesthetics (Denis Diderot): In 18th-century Enlightenment philosophy, Denis Diderot made the story of Polus the foundational counterpoint of his influential dialogue The Paradox of Acting (Paradoxe sur le comédien). Diderot argued vigorously against Polus' method, asserting that an actor who genuinely experiences raw personal agony loses control over vocal modulation, breath, and stage movement, producing erratic, unrepeatable, and exhausted performances, whereas a truly sublime actor relies on cold, calculated psychological mimicry, emotional detachment, and rigorous technical mastery.
  • Polus of Agrigento (Platonic Sophist & Rhetorician): In the history of classical philosophy, Polus also refers to an important contemporary figure: a Sicilian sophist and devoted disciple of Gorgias who appears as an aggressive interlocutor in Plato's dialogue Gorgias, contending that despotic power and unjust wealth represent the highest human happiness until Socrates demonstrates that doing injustice is worse than suffering it.
  • Scientific & Astronomical Terminology: As a Latin common noun, polus (derived from Greek pólos, "pivot, axis, celestial sphere") refers to the celestial pole, the celestial dome, or the geographic poles of the Earth.
