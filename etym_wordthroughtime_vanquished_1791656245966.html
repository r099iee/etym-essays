- 14th c. (Middle English) — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman as an elevated chivalric and martial verb (*venquisshen*); carried noble, romance-epic connotations of knighthood, judicial combat, and chivalric conquest.
- 15th–16th c. (Early Modern English) — Expanded into theological and biblical literature; used to describe Christ vanquishing sin, death, and Satan, imbuing the term with profound spiritual and cosmic victory.
- 17th–18th c. (Renaissance and Enlightenment) — Firmly established in classical historical accounts, tragedy, and political philosophy; used both for military empires crushing rivals and metaphorically for reason vanquishing superstition or passions vanquishing virtue.
- 19th c. (Victorian Era) — Retained high literary, poetic, and sociological prestige (as in discussions of "victors and vanquished" in state formation); frequently employed in psychological novels for characters overcome by emotion or moral crisis.
- 20th–21st c. — Largely retired from mundane conversational English in favor of "defeated" or "beaten"; preserved in elevated prose, historical analyses, epic fantasy literature, gaming lore, and formal philosophical discourse.
