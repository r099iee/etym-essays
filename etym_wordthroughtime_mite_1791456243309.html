Old English — mīte names a tiny insect or arachnid, a literal crawling creature rather than a term of affection.
Middle English — The word continues as the ordinary name for a mite, and the diminutive creature sense remains the core.
Late 14th c. — A second sense names a small Flemish coin; Wyclif uses mite for the widow’s two small coins in the Gospel.
16th c. — The coin sense generates the “small amount” sense: a mite becomes a unit of smallness, and “a mite” can mean “slightly”.
19th c. — “Poor little mite” becomes a common British term of pity for a small child or animal; the word acquires a warm, sentimental colour.
20th c. — The arachnid sense becomes medically important with the discovery of dust mites and scabies; the affectionate sense becomes increasingly informal and old-fashioned.
21st c. — A technical term in acarology and allergy, a biblical echo in “the widow’s mite”, and a slightly dated British term of endearment.
