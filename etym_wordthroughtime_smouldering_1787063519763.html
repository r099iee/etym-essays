- smouldering (present participle / adjective) — late 13th century: the earliest verb form smolderen means "to suffocate, smother, choke" — related to smother; the participle at this stage would have meant stifled or suffocated, with no fire implied.
- 16th century: dramatic shift — "smoulder" now means to burn slowly without flame, giving off smoke; the participial adjective "smouldering" applies to embers, fires, and ashes; the old suffocation sense fades away.
- 18th–19th century: the figurative turn — resentment, anger, hatred, and rebellion "smoulder"; a "smouldering" grievance is a discontent that burns inwardly, not yet blazing into revolt; highly productive in political and revolutionary prose.
- 19th–20th century romantic register: "smouldering" describes suppressed desire and passion — a "smouldering glance", "smouldering eyes"; the word gains a seductive, intense, dangerous undertone.
- 20th–21st century: both literal and figurative, often simultaneously in news and fiction — "smouldering ruins" after a fire, "smouldering conflict" that has not yet erupted, "smouldering anger"; the participle now connotes dangerous containment, whether of embers or of emotions.
