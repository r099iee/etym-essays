- 15th c. (Middle English) — Borrowed from Old French (*pacifique*) and Latin (*pacificus*); carried biblical and theological reverence as "peacemaking," directly echoing the Beatitudes of Christ (*Beati pacifici*).
- 16th c. — Dramatically thrust into global geography when Ferdinand Magellan named the *Mar Pacífico* (Pacific Ocean) in 1520; simultaneously adopted by Renaissance monarchs (such as King James I, who took the personal motto *Beati pacifici*) to project diplomacy over costly continental warfare.
- 17th–18th c. — Integrated into Enlightenment international law and diplomatic treaties (Grotius, Vattel), designating treaties, negotiations, and policies aimed at the avoidance of armed conflict.
- 19th c. — Used extensively in anthropology, philosophy, and history (e.g., Herbert Spencer, Auguste Comte, Will Durant) to characterize the transition from predatory "militant" societies to cooperative "pacific" commercial and industrial orders.
- Early 20th c. — Provided the linguistic foundation for the international peace movement, directly giving birth to the terms *pacifisme* and *pacifist* in 1901–1902.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Retains its dual identity: universally recognized as the primary proper noun for the world's largest ocean, while remaining an elevated adjective in diplomatic law ("pacific dispute resolution") and academic prose.
