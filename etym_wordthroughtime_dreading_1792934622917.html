- Old English — a fear without a father: ondraedan and draedan mean to fear, though no certain Germanic ancestor explains them.
- Middle English — awe enters: dreden covers both terror and reverence — men dread God and kings the way they dread wolves, with worship inside the trembling.
- 14th–16th c. — the noun and adjective bloom: dread becomes a thing (a great dread) and a quality (a dread sovereign, a dread disease), fear given titles.
- 17th–18th c. — awe drains away: polite and enlightened ages keep the terror but lose the worship; to dread is now simply to fear beforehand.
- 19th–20th c. — the everyday participle: novels and letters are full of dreading visits, Mondays, and examinations — fear domesticated into the calendar.
- Today — anticipation itself: dreading names the mind's preview of suffering, from the comic to the mortal, with the hobbits' hoofbeats as its perfect emblem — the feared sound arriving on schedule.
