1. Indo-European Origins and the Germanic Semantic Pejorization

The word stinking is the present participle and participial adjective of the strong verb stink, descending from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *stengʷ- (or *steng-), meaning "to strike, push, thrust, or prick." In the early Indo-European conception, sharp sensory impressions—particularly pungent smells and stinging tastes—were perceived through the physical metaphor of a strike or sharp prick against the sensory organs (a conceptual metaphor that also produced English sting and stang).

In Proto-Germanic, this root developed into the Class III strong verb *stinkwaną, carrying a fascinating and remarkably broad range of meanings: "to strike against, to leap or bound, to scatter like dust, and to emit an odor or scent." Intriguingly, in Proto-Germanic and throughout early Germanic dialects, the olfactory sense of *stinkwaną was originally sensorially neutral. It simply meant to give off a smell, whether sweet and fragrant or foul and offensive. This neutral state produced cognates across the Germanic family:

  • Old High German stinkan ("to emit an odor," whether pleasant or foul, before shifting to Modern German stinken).
  • Old Saxon stincan ("to smell, emit a fragrance or stench").
  • Old Norse støkkva ("to spring, leap, burst forth," preserving the original physical dynamic sense of striking/leaping).

2. Old English Development and the Shift to Uncleanliness

In Old English, the verb was recorded as stincan (past tense stanc, past participle gestuncen). In early Anglo-Saxon manuscripts, the verb could still be modified by adverbs of sweetness to describe delightful fragrances: texts explicitly spoke of substances that were swēte stincende ("sweet-smelling") or described the holy odor of sanctity rising from the relics of saints as a stenc (scent).

However, during the late Anglo-Saxon period (9th to 11th centuries), the word underwent a classic linguistic process known as semantic pejorization (or pejoration), in which a neutral or positive term becomes permanently restricted to a negative meaning. Because humans have a far more urgent biological and communicative need to warn others about noxious, dangerous, and toxic smells than to remark on pleasant ones, the unmarked verb stincan became monopolized by offensive odors. The present participle stincende (the direct ancestor of stinking) crystallized in Old English homilies, medical collections, and biblical translations as the standard designator for foul, rotting, and pestilential stenches.

3. Middle English Religious Literature and Demonology

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, the phonetic system of English shifted: the Old English participial suffix -ende merged with the verbal noun suffix -inge, producing the modern participial form -ing. By the 13th century, Middle English manuscripts wrote the word as stynkynge (or stinkende / stinking).

In Middle English literature, stinking became an indispensable theological and moral keyword. In the vivid religious imagination of medieval Christendom, physical stench was the outward, sensible manifestation of moral sin, spiritual death, and the demonic realm. The Blickling Homilies, the Pricke of Conscience, and Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales are replete with references to "the stinking pit of hell," the "stinking breath of Satan," and the "stinking pride" of the damned. In The Parson's Tale, Chaucer repeatedly employs stynkynge to denounce sins of the flesh, binding physical revulsion inextricably to ethical condemnation.

4. Early Modern Natural History and Vernacular Taxonomy

During the 16th and 17th centuries, as the Renaissance fostered a vigorous interest in empirical botany, herbal medicine, and natural history, English herbalists faced the challenge of classifying hundreds of native wild plants for popular use. Pioneering botanists such as William Turner (A New Herball, 1551) and John Gerard (Herball, 1597) systematically incorporated stinking into vernacular botanical nomenclature to describe species with pungent, acrid, or protective chemical emissions:

  • Stinking chamomile (Anthemis cotula, also known as mayweed), noted for its acrid scent that repels insects.
  • Stinking hellebore (Helleborus foetidus, literally translated from its Latin binomial).
  • Stinking iris or stinking gladwin (Iris foetidissima), famous for the pungent odor released when its leaves are bruised.
  • Stinking horehound (Ballota nigra).

This botanical tradition firmly embedded stinking as an objective, descriptive technical adjective in scientific and horticultural registers.

5. The Rise of Colloquial Intensifiers and Modern Slang

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, stinking underwent an extraordinary pragmatic expansion in vernacular English. Working-class speech and transatlantic popular slang seized upon the word's aggressive acoustic punch to forge vivid, hyperbolic intensifiers:

  • The phrase stinking rich appeared in American and British print around the 1920s, capturing the populist sentiment that colossal, ostentatious wealth is so excessive that it creates a metaphorical foul stench in a democratic society.
  • The phrase stinking drunk emerged even earlier in the 19th century, vividly combining the literal olfactory fumes of stale liquor and vomit with an emphatic description of complete physical collapse.
  • During the mid-20th century, stinking became an all-purpose emphatic pre-modifier expressing contemptuous dismissal, as popularized in the famous cinematic phrase from The Treasure of the Sierra Madre (1948): "Badges? We ain't got no badges! We don't need no badges! I don't have to show you any stinking badges!"

Today, stinking thrives across an unmatched spectrum of English discourse: functioning as a literal sensory descriptor of malodor, a formal botanical classification term, a devastating moral indictment, and a vibrant colloquial intensifier.
