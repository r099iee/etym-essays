PIE: *bʰleg- "to shine, burn, blaze" (attested: Greek phlégō "to burn", Latin flagrāre "to blaze", Sanskrit bhrājate "he gleams"; the root also yields English black, bleak).
Proto-Germanic: *blik- / *blēk- "pale, shining, scorched" — with the semantic pole of "burned pale/blackened" prominent in some derivatives.
Old Norse: blika "to gleam" and blikna "to become pale" — Scandinavian reflexes of the gleam/bleach pole; Old English blǣc "pale, shining" (ancestor of bleak) belongs to the same family.
Uncertain stage — English blight (16th c.): the plant disease that blasts and blackens crops; the immediate source is DEBATED — possibly from Old English blæce "scab, rash, skin eruption" (a blistered, blackened condition), possibly from a Scandinavian or Low German form related to blikna; OED marks the origin "of obscure/unknown origin."
17th century: blight — standard term for crop diseases that wither, blast, or blacken plants.
18th century: blight (figurative) — "anything that ruins, destroys, or checks growth," e.g. the blight of poverty, of war.
Late 19th century (British slang): blight + -er — blighter "one who blights," at first a genuinely contemptible fellow.
Late 19th–20th century: blighter — softening to "a fellow, a chap, a pitiable creature"; "poor blighter" becomes a stock phrase of affectionate sympathy.
Modern English: BLIGHTER — colloquial British noun for a contemptible, troublesome, or pitiable person.
NOTE ON DEBATE: the link between PIE *bʰleg- and blight is indirect and passes through a contested sixteenth-century step; the immediate etymon of blight is unknown, and both the Old English blæce derivation and a Scandinavian/Low German derivation have supporters. The word blighter itself, however, is a straightforward, well-documented derivation from blight + the agent suffix -er.
