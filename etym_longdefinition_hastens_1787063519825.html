Core meaning. Hastens is the third-person singular present tense of the verb to hasten (as in “he hastens”, “it hastens”). To hasten is to move or act with speed, to make haste, or to hurry. The verb has two complementary faces: it is intransitive — to go quickly, to rush to a place or task (“he hastens to the gate”) — and transitive — to cause something to happen sooner or to speed an event along (“the news hastened the departure”).

Primary senses.

  • Intransitive: to hurry, to move quickly. The subject moves at speed towards a goal: “they hastened home”, “the river hastens to the sea”. The register is formal or literary; in ordinary speech English prefers hurry, rush, or speed.
  • Transitive: to cause to occur sooner. The subject accelerates an event or process: “stress hastens ageing”, “the doctor hastened the patient’s recovery”, “they hastened his end”. This is the older transitive use, now common in medical, legal, and technical prose.

Secondary and extended senses.

  • “Hasten to add / emphasise / point out”: a rhetorical formula meaning “I make a point of saying this quickly and emphatically”, used to pre-empt misunderstanding: “I hasten to add that I meant no offence.” Here the verb means “to be eager, to make a point of”, with speed as a metaphor for eagerness.
  • Literary and archaic flavour: because of its formal register, hasten is frequent in older literature, the Bible, fantasy, and epic narrative; it carries a slightly elevated, purposeful tone.
  • The object of the hurry may be implicit: “to hasten away”, “to hasten to do something”, “to hasten to the rescue”.

Grammar notes. As a regular verb, hasten forms hastened and hastening; the form hastens is used with third-person singular subjects. It takes infinitive complements (“hastens to say”), prepositional objects (“hastens to the gate”), or direct objects (“hastens the process”). The noun is haste (“make haste” = hurry), the adjective hasty (“done with excessive speed, rash”), and the adverb hastily.

Derivatives. The family is compact: noun haste, adjectives hasty and overhasty, adverb hastily, verbs hasten, and the proverb “haste makes waste”. A related formula is hasten to add, a journalistic and academic device that signals a quick, emphatic qualification.

Usage notes. In careful prose, hasten is the formal counterpart of hurry: official reports and histories say “the government hastened to respond”, not “hurried to respond”. The transitive sense is frequent in medical and scientific writing (“the treatment hastens recovery”), and in euphemistic contexts (“hastened his end”) where urgency conceals agency. Because of its register, hastens is rarely used in casual speech except inside the fixed formula.

In the source context. The sentence “From here it hastens to the Gate” uses the intransitive sense of the river — swift, purposeful movement toward the gate — and the word’s formal, epic flavour suits the narrative register of the passage.

Semantic bridge. Hastens inherits all its senses from the noun haste, which itself developed from a Germanic word meaning “strife, contention, violence”: the effort of fighting became, metaphorically, the effort of hurrying. The transitive sense (“to cause sooner”) arose from the notion of “putting haste into” an event; the intransitive sense (“to make haste oneself”) is the reflex of the same idea applied to the mover. The modern “hasten to add” formula exploits the meaning “to act quickly and eagerly in speaking”.
