FAMISHED is the past participle and participial adjective of the verb to famish: reduced to the point of starvation, suffering extreme hunger, or - in modern colloquial use - extremely hungry, whether genuinely or as comic exaggeration. The word carries the severity of its Latin ancestor fames, 'hunger', and in its full strength it describes a hunger that has emptied and wasted the body, the want of a person who has had next to nothing to eat for days. In The Hobbit, Gandalf, speaking for the exhausted company to the Eagles of the Misty Mountains, pleads 'we are famished with hunger', and Bilbo's 'weak little voice' adds 'I am nearly dead of it': the word carries the whole weight of the moment. It is not the polite hunger of a missed meal but a gnawing, all-consuming want that has hollowed the travellers out after their dreadful adventures with next to nothing to eat. Its tone is intensely literary, old-fashioned, faintly dramatic - the language of romance and epic, where hunger is a force of nature that hollows a man out - and Tolkien lets the archaic gravity of the word dignify the travellers' plea. In the modern period the word runs on two poles at once: comic hyperbole before dinner ('I'm absolutely famished!') and genuine extremity in serious prose, and it is precisely this double life that makes it so useful in writing: it can be jokey and yet always keeps a kernel of real severity. CORE SENSES AND POLYSEMY
  • Primary sense (extremity): reduced to starvation; dying or nearly dying of hunger - a famished traveller; the famished refugees; famished with hunger, the fixed phrase used by Gandalf.
  • Comic / hyperbolic sense: extremely hungry, used of an ordinary missed meal - I'm absolutely famished, let's eat; famished after the long walk; the sense recorded from the 18th-19th centuries as the word cooled toward exaggeration.
  • Sense of desperate craving (extended): famished for something - famished for affection, famished for news - a hunger extended from food to anything keenly wanted, recorded from the 19th century.
  • Rare verbal senses of the base verb famish: (intransitive) to die of hunger, to starve; (transitive) to cause to starve, to reduce to hunger - now chiefly archaic or literary, the sense in which the word appears in medieval and early modern chronicles.
  • As noun: a famished person (rare, literary).
  • Register: literary, formal, faintly archaic; the word is felt as more intense than hungry and more elevated than starving, though its hyperbole has domestic, jokey uses.
POLYSEMY - THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE FROM STARVATION TO HYPERBOLE The two poles of the word - real extremity and jokey hunger - are not two unrelated senses but one sense that has been relaxed by everyday use. In its first English life, from around 1400, the verb famish meant literally to die of hunger or to bring someone to death by hunger: it was the language of famine, siege, and mass want, and it appears in medieval chronicles with deadly seriousness. The participle famished hardened into an adjective meaning 'reduced to starvation', and this is the sense in which the word enters the great literary tradition of the 16th and 17th centuries, where hunger hollows men out and famished heroes stagger through wildernesses. From the 18th and 19th centuries the word began to cool toward exaggeration: used of a missed dinner rather than a real famine, famished became a lively and affectionate hyperbole, and 'I'm famished!' became the ordinary exclamation of a hungry person before a meal. The two senses have coexisted ever since - the archaic gravity of the literary word and the comic lightness of the domestic one - and a skilled writer can sound either pole, or both at once, with the same word. Tolkien's use is the literary pole: the company really is famished, and the archaic formality of the word dignifies their hunger, while Bilbo's weak little voice gives the extremity a pathos the grander speakers do not reach. DISTINCTIONS AND COLLOCATION Hungry is the neutral, everyday state of appetite, entirely without intensity or drama; starving is now the ordinary blunt word for the same extremity but has lost its archaic colour; famished keeps the full severity of 'reduced to starvation' while also serving the comic register. Ravenous stresses furious, wolfish appetite - the desire to devour - rather than the pangs of want; peckish is a mild, slightly fussy hunger; voracious describes appetite, not need. Famished collocates with the preposition with (famished with hunger), with absolutely and positively as intensifiers (absolutely famished), and with the figurative for (famished for company). It is a word that rewards the writer who wants either epic gravity or affectionate comedy, and it is exactly the double life that makes it so useful in prose.
