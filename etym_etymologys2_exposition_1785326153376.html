EXPOSITION is a compound noun: its first element comes from the Latin preposition ex-, ‘out of, from,’ and its second from the Latin verb pōnere, ‘to put, place.’ The two etyma have separate chains, and the compound has its own chain. CHAIN 1 — ‘out’ element (ex-): - PIE *h₁eǥʷs / *h₁eḱs ‘out, out of, away’ (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Latin ex (preposition) ‘out of, from, away’ (attested from the earliest Latin records, 3rd c. BCE) — used as a prefix in expōnō CHAIN 2 — ‘put / place’ element (pōnere): - PIE *apo- ‘off, away’ — appearing in Latin compounds in the reduced form *po- (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Latin sinō ‘to let, allow, put down, set’ — from a PIE base *siH- ‘to let, leave, set’ (attested; the precise PIE etymon is debated) → Latin pōnō, pōnere ‘to put, place, set, lay down’ — from an older *po-sinō, a compound of *po- + sinō ‘to set down’ (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE) CHAIN 3 — the compound ‘EXPOSITION’ itself: - Latin expōnō, expōnere ‘to set forth, put out, display, expose; to explain’ — ex- + pōnere (attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Latin expositio (genitive expositio-nis) ‘a setting forth, a display, an exposition, an explanation’ — a derivative noun built on the past-participle stem exposit- of expōnere (attested in classical Latin) → Old French esposicion / exposicion ‘exhibition, explanation’ (attested in medieval French) → Middle English exposicioun / exposiciun ‘the explanation of a text, esp. of scripture’ (attested from the late 14th c.) → Modern English exposition (from the 15th–16th c. onward, with the modern range of senses: explanation, narrative opening, musical section, public exhibition) → EXPOSITION NOTE ON DEBATE: the standard account derives Latin pōnere from an earlier *po-sinō, a compound of *po- (from PIE *apo-, ‘off, away’) and sinō, ‘to let, to set,’ but the internal detail is debated: the precise PIE etymon of sinō (commonly reconstructed as *siH-, ‘to let, leave, set’) is uncertain, and a minority of scholars prefer a different reconstruction. The stages from Latin through Old French to English are securely attested: Middle English exposicioun (late 14th c.) meant above all the explanation of scripture, and the modern ‘public exhibition’ sense is a development of the nineteenth century, most famously associated with the Great Exhibition of 1851.
