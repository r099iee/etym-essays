Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*hei / *hai
"natural vocal exclamation of joy, calling, or surprise"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*hai / *hei
"shout to attract attention or express joy"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
hey! / hei!
"exclamation of hailing, joy, or hunting"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1520s)
hey-da! / hey-day!
"interjection of frolic, wonder, excitement"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1600)
hey-day (noun)
"youthful frolic, wild high spirits,"
"impetuous blood"
↓
Modern English (c. 1750)
heyday
"period of greatest vigor, prime, zenith"

Note: Heyday originated as an expressive interjection hey-da! formed by compounding the shout hey with da (or borrowed from Middle Dutch hei daar or Middle Low German heida). In the 18th century, English speakers reanalyzed the terminal syllable -day by folk etymology as the noun day, heavily influenced by high day ('festival, triumph'), transforming the term into a temporal noun for a golden age.
