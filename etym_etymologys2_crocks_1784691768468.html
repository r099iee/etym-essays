Origin below Proto-Germanic unknown / debated: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon is attested for the word-family; the cluster is native Germanic, built on the reduplicated expressive base *krukk-, typical of vocabulary for round, hollow vessels. Proto-Germanic (reconstructed; before the migration period, pre-5th c. CE): *krukkō- / *kruk-jō- "earthenware pot, pitcher, vessel" — a reduplicated expressive formation of the pottery lexicon; no exact date is attestable because it is a reconstruction. Old English (c. 700–1100 CE): crocc, crocca "earthen pot, pitcher, vessel" — attested in early glossaries and records; the geminate consonant and rounded vowel continue PGmc *krukk-. Middle English (c. 1150–1500): crokke, crocke "earthen pot, cooking vessel, jar"; by transfer also "the sooty iron crook on which pots hung over the hearth". Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): crock "earthenware pot or dish" — the coarse domestic sense is fixed; dialect and slang begin transferring the noun to a broken-down horse or a sickly, useless person ("old crock"). Modern English (18th–20th c.): crock "earthenware vessel"; the plural crocks "earthenware pots, jars, dishes, especially unwashed kitchenware" — the form used by J. R. R. Tolkien in The Hobbit (1937). American slang (20th c.) extends the singular to "a crock" = nonsense, a falsehood. NOTE ON DEBATE: The chain cannot be carried below Proto-Germanic with certainty. Some scholars propose a connection to PIE *greg- / *kreg- "to gather, press together" (cf. Latin grex, gregis "flock, herd") on the semantic ground of a clay mass gathered or coiled into a pot, but standard dictionaries mark the deeper origin as unknown, and no PIE root should be asserted as secure for this family.
