- [15th-century loan] - Entering English in the 1400s (from Latin destitutus, 'left alone, forsaken'), 'destitute' first meant abandoned or forsaken, of persons left without help.
- [Abstract absolute sense] - In 17th and 18th century prose the construction 'destitute of' hardened into a powerful abstract of total absence - a man 'destitute of taste' - the vocabulary of statute, sermon and Augustan satire.
- [Material poverty] - In the 19th century, as social questions rose, 'destitute' was applied overwhelmingly to persons utterly without means - the destitute poor - a word of philanthropy, censuses and public conscience.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today it chiefly means completely impoverished, while the older abstract 'destitute of' remains a formal, emphatic way of saying utterly lacking something.
