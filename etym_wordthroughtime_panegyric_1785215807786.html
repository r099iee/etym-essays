- 17th century - Enters English from the Greek for a festival assembly oration, naming formal, public speech of praise — an ancient genre reborn in Renaissance rhetoric.
- 17th–18th century - A standard term for set-piece literary or courtly praise: panegyrics on monarchs, victories, and worthies, written in grand, deliberately elevated style.
- 19th century (Victorian prose) - Still current as a sharp critical label: the biographer calls Mill's dedication to Harriet Taylor “the most extraordinary panegyric on a woman that any philosopher has ever penned” — praise so unreserved it invites both awe and worldly disbelief.
- Contemporary - Rare in speech, kept in literary and critical writing; it contrasts with eulogy (funeral-specific) and flattery (insincere), retaining the sense of a formal, presumptively sincere encomium.
