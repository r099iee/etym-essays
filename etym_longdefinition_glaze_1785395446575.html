To glaze is, in its core domestic sense, to cover a surface with a smooth, glassy, light-reflecting coating, and in the goblins' roasting-song — “till beards blaze, and eyes glaze” — the verb performs its most chilling figurative trick. While the beards of the dwarves blaze with fierce flame, their eyes glaze: they become fixed, bright, and unseeing, as though the living eye had been swapped for a disc of glass. The horror lies in the frozen brightness — the eye does not dim so much as polish itself into a hard, reflective, jewel-like vacancy, glossy precisely because the life has withdrawn from it. The word is drawn from the familiar crafts of pottery and confectionery, where a glaze is a smooth, vitreous, light-reflecting finish, and Tolkien's transposition makes the cruelty almost cosy: death is described in the language of a potter finishing a bowl or a cook finishing a pastry. The tone is gleefully macabre — grand, singing rhythm applied to the domestic verb, so that the fire that burns the beards and the cold shine that overtakes the eyes are delivered with the same festive relish.

1. The primary senses of the verb.

The oldest and most concrete meaning of to glaze is technical and craftsmanlike: to fit or set with glass — a window, a frame, a greenhouse — the work of the glazier, whose very name is the verb's offspring. From this, in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, grew the sense that dominates modern use: to give a smooth, glossy, vitreous coating to a surface, especially to pottery and ceramics. A potter glazes a bowl by applying a thin mixture of minerals (ground glass, feldspar, silica, and metallic oxides) that fuses in the kiln into a hard, impermeable, often coloured skin; the same process gives tiles, sanitary ware, and architectural bricks their durable, waterproof shine. The noun glaze names both the liquid coating before firing and the fused glassy layer that results. In cookery the verb transfers to food: a pastry is glazed with beaten egg or sugar syrup to produce a golden, glossy crust; a ham is glazed with honey and mustard; a fruit tart is glazed with warm jam or jelly. In wood-finishing and painting, to glaze is to apply a thin, usually translucent coat of colour over a dry ground — a technique of the old masters for building depth of tone — and glazed paper is the smooth, shiny coated stock used for printing photographs and magazines.

2. The figurative and modern senses.

From the middle of the nineteenth century the verb made its great leap from materials to the human body. To say that the eyes glaze — or glaze over — is to describe the eyes becoming fixed, glassy, and unseeing: the bright, reflective stare of shock, of dying, of utter boredom, or of drug-induced detachment, in which the attention has withdrawn and only a polished blank remains. The semantic bridge is the analogy of the material: as a potter's clay takes on a hard, shiny, lifeless skin, so a living eye takes on a shiny, reflective film as life or interest leaves it. This sense is the one Tolkien exploits, and it is now fully established in everyday English (“her eyes glazed over during the lecture”). Two further modern extensions deserve note. First, glaze over in a colloquial or business register can mean to gloss over, to cover up, or to skate over a difficulty or detail, exactly as a glaze covers a surface. Second, in meteorology and civil engineering, glaze (or glaze ice) names the smooth, transparent coating of ice formed when freezing rain falls on a cold surface — a natural, deadly counterpart to the potter's coat.

3. Noun senses, jargon, and technical vocabulary.

  • Ceramics: a vitreous coating fused to clay in a kiln; subdivided into lead glaze, tin glaze, salt glaze, and frit glaze, each named for its chief ingredient or firing method; the raw liquid mixture is also called a glaze or a slip.
  • Cookery: a glossy finishing coating of syrup, egg, jam, stock, or fat applied to pastry, meat, fruit, or vegetables; a meat glaze is a concentrated stock reduced to a shiny jelly.
  • Painting and print: a thin translucent layer of oil colour applied over dried paint to modify tone; the smooth coated finish of glazed paper, card, or photographic prints.
  • Meteorology and engineering: glaze ice — the transparent ice deposit from freezing rain on roads, wires, and aircraft.
  • Dialectal and old use: a glassy or lustrous look; in older texts, to glaze could still mean simply to furnish with glass.

4. Why the senses cohere.

Every sense of glaze hangs from a single image: a hard, smooth, shiny surface laid over something else, through which the light is reflected rather than absorbed. Whether the surface is glass in a window, vitrified clay on a pot, syrup on a tart, paint on canvas, ice on a road, or the film over a dying eye, the word always points to the same optics of reflected light and the same function of sealing a surface. That is why, uniquely among its near-synonyms, glaze can describe what happens to a human eye at the moment life or attention withdraws — the eye does not simply stop looking, it is finished, polished into a bright unseeing thing. The historical bridge runs from the craft of the glazier (fourteenth century), through the potter's and cook's coating (sixteenth to seventeenth centuries), to the figurative body-language of the nineteenth century and the modern idiom to glaze over meaning both to become glassy-eyed and to gloss over a subject — a single thread of reflected light uniting the workshop and the waking nightmare.
