- c. 1300: Middle English “goune” — a loose outer garment for men or women, an Anglo-French import; worn over other clothes, the medieval sense is completely general.
- 14th–16th c.: the gown of office — academic, legal, civic, clerical; “gown” becomes institutional dress, and the collocation “town and gown” later freezes the university-versus-city division (18th c.).
- 16th–18th c.: the woman’s gown — “gown” becomes the standard word for a woman’s principal dress; the mantua gown and sack gown of the 1700s; formal grandeur accrues as the male gown retreats to office.
- 19th c.: specialisation — nightgown, dressing gown, morning gown; the white wedding gown becomes an institution after Victoria’s wedding (1840); the satin court gown of Wilde’s story belongs exactly here.
- 20th–21st c.: narrowing — “gown” settles on formal feminine dress (evening gown, bridal gown) and institutional robes (graduation, judicial, clerical), plus the functional hospital gown; casual one-piece dresses are always “dresses”, never “gowns”.
- Present: the word carries ceremonial, formal or clinical overtones; the old neutral sense survives only in idioms (“town and gown”, “gowned surgeons”) and in the fashion and academic industries that keep the word in full vigour.
