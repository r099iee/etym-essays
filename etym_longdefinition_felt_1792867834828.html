Felt in the source sentence names a humble textile: a dense non-woven cloth produced by matting, wetting, heating, and pressing animal fibres, chiefly wool, until they lock into a firm mat that needs no spinning or weaving.

In the source sentence Sam has put on his head a tall shapeless felt bag, which he calls a hat: a coarse bag of this matted wool-cloth pulled over his head as a makeshift travelling hat, comic and endearing, the gardener's improvisation for a wet road. The joke lands because felt hats proper are dignified things, judges' hats and bowlers, while Sam wears the raw stuff, an unshaped bag still announcing its sack origins.

Core meaning in detail: True felt is among humanity's oldest textiles, older than the loom: moisture, heat, pressure, and the microscopic scales on wool fibres combine so that the fibres migrate, entangle, and shrink into a single sheet. Nomads roofed yurts with it, armies padded armour with it, hatters blocked it into bowlers and top hats, and pianists still quiet their hammers with it. The adjective felt then marks anything made of this cloth: felt hats, felt slippers, felt-tip pens whose nibs wick ink like matted fibres wick dye.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Matted woollen cloth (primary noun sense, the source meaning, from Old English): the felt bag on Sam's head, a yurt wall, a billiard table's baize cousin. The bridge is the craft itself: beaten wool becomes cloth without thread.
  • A hat or headcovering of such cloth (metonymic extension): judges in their felts, cowboys in ten-gallon felts. The bridge is matter for object: the cloth lends its name to the shaped thing, exactly as a glass names the vessel.
  • Past tense and participle of feel (twin homonym, universally known): he felt the rain, she felt afraid, the loss was deeply felt. The bridge here belongs to a wholly separate history, touch and emotion, joined to the cloth only by spelling and sound.
  • To make into felt or cover with felt (verb from the noun): to felt wool by beating, a roof felted against the weather. The bridge turns the noun back into action: working fibres until they mat.
  • Beading in paper and industry (technical extension): papermakers' felts carry wet sheets through the rollers, engineers felt joints against vibration. The bridge is functional analogy: any matted mat doing felt's work of cushioning, filtering, or carrying earns the name.
  • Felt-tip and felt-tip pen (modern commercial sense): the marker whose porous nib behaves like miniature matting. The bridge is texture: the nib's pressed fibres drink and deliver ink the way fulling-wool drinks dye.

Register and connotation: As cloth, felt is warm, lowly, and honest: shepherds, yurts, slippers, and school craft-tables, with one elegant exception in the blocked felt hat of judges and gentlemen. As past of feel it is intimate and inward, the verb of sympathy itself. Tolkien enjoys both resonances at once: Sam's felt bag is funny because the cloth is humble, and touching because the lad feels, deeply and loyally, everything his master faces.
