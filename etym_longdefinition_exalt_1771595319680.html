EXALT, verb. To raise someone or something to a higher rank, position, or degree of glory; to praise intensely; to glorify or magnify. The word is the language of worship, devotion, and royal elevation — the verb used of kings and of God — but it also carries a literal, physical sense: to lift up, to raise aloft. Its distinguishing feature, against its many synonyms, is intensity and height: to exalt is not merely to raise or praise, but to raise to a very great height, attended by glory, reverence, or intense acclamation. In John Ruskin’s Preface to Modern Painters (1843) the sentence runs: “To pour our flattery into the ear of Death, and exalt, with vain acclamation, the names of those who neither demand our praise, nor regard our gratitude.” Here the verb means “raise to the height of glory,” but Ruskin deploys it with bitter irony. The word, normally the language of worship and devotion — the psalmist’s “exalt his name” — is spent upon the dead, who “neither demand our praise, nor regard our gratitude.” The higher the register of the praise, the more damning its misdirection: a solemn, honorific, almost liturgical verb is emptied of its proper object. Ruskin’s tone is therefore oratorical and contemptuous beneath its dignity; to exalt the dead is to perform a ceremony of homage that is wholly unreciprocated and, because it blinds the public to living merit, actively harmful. The reader should feel the clash of registers: biblical elevation meets Victorian vanity, and the mismatch is the point. The primary senses of EXALT:
  • To raise in rank, position, or power. To elevate a person to a higher station — ‘exalt a minister to the highest office’; to raise a thing to a higher condition. This is the secular, political sense, from the Latin literal meaning ‘to raise up high.’
  • To glorify, magnify, or praise intensely. The devotional and rhetorical sense: ‘to exalt the name of God,’ ‘to exalt virtue.’ This is the psalmist’s sense — Latin exaltare, ‘to raise up,’ used in the Vulgate for the glorification of God — and it is the sense Ruskin poisons with irony.
  • To raise up physically; to lift aloft. The older literal sense — ‘exalt the banner,’ ‘the hills exalt their heads.’ In modern use this is rare and poetic, but it underlies all the figurative senses.
Secondary and transferred senses:
  • In alchemy and science (historical). To refine, subtilise, or ‘raise’ a substance — the alchemical sense of exaltation, the purification of a metal or essence to a higher state. This technical sense is now chiefly historical.
  • In rhetoric and preaching. To elevate a theme or subject in discourse; to dignify or ennoble — ‘the poet exalts the ordinary.’
  • Psychological and emotional transferred uses. To raise the spirits or elate — to fill with intense joy or rapture (cf. the related exaltation, a state of extreme happiness or elevation of mind). In this sense the word shades toward elate and uplift.
The semantic history of exalt is a rise from the barnyard to the throne. Etymologically the word’s ultimate ancestor is the Indo-European root meaning ‘to grow, to nourish, to feed’ — the verb of mothering and feeding — which in Latin produced alere, ‘to nourish,’ and the participle altus, ‘grown, hence high.’ From altus Latin built exaltare, ‘to raise up on high,’ and in Christian Latin the Vulgate turned it into a verb of worship: ‘exaltabo te, Domine — I will exalt thee, O Lord.’ Old French exalter and Middle English exalten carried this double nature — physical raising and devotional glorifying — into English in the fourteenth century. The word therefore entered English already split between the throne room and the psalm, and it has kept both faces ever since. Among its near-synonyms, exalt must be carefully distinguished. Elevate is the most neutral member of the family: it names any raising, physical or figurative, with no implication of worthiness, intensity, or worship — one may elevate a button, a platform, or a standard. Raise is the plainest and most physical verb of all; a hand, a roof, a flag is raised, and the word is emotionally flat. Exalt differs from both in degree and quality: it implies elevation to a very great height, attended by glory, reverence, or intense praise, and it gravitates to religious and rhetorical contexts. Glorify overlaps heavily — both describe honorific praise — but glorify centres on making something radiant or divine, whereas exalt keeps the specific sense of raising in rank or height. Magnify enlarges a reputation through words; exalt raises the subject itself. In Ruskin’s sentence the word’s liturgical weight is the engine of the irony: only a word so solemn can make the acclamation of the dead seem so vain.
