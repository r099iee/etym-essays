Archibald is a masculine Christian name of Old German origin meaning truly brave or precious-bold, borne in Scotland above all by the Douglas earls of Angus. In the source sentence — Ruskin in Praeterita on his disenchanted castle-tour — Tantallon was there; but no Archibald of Angus means the headland fortress stood empty of its historic lord: Archibald Douglas, Earl of Angus, the Bell-the-Cat, the great councillor of James III, or his namesake Archibald Douglas, 6th Earl of Angus, who held the boy-king James V in tutelage and was besieged in Tantallon in 1528. Scott's Marmion stages an Archibald at Tantallon receiving the English envoy; the child Ruskin expected to meet him on the wall-walk and met only wind. The capital A marks a proper noun; the word has no common-noun sense.

As a name in the world Archibald belongs to the Norman-German stock brought to Britain after 1066. Old High German Erchanbald (genuine-bold) passes through Norman French Archambault, Archimbaud to Middle English and Scots Archibald. Medieval Scotland adopts it enthusiastically: the Black Douglases and Red Douglases christen heir after heir Archibald — Archibald the Grim, 3rd Earl of Douglas (c. 1328-1400), Archibald, 4th Earl killed at Verneuil (1424), Archibald, 5th Earl called Bell-the-Cat who hanged Cochrane over Lauder Bridge (1482) — until the name itself sounds like a border trumpet. The Campbells rival them with Archibalds of Argyll; the Scots ballads ring with Archie, Archy, Baldie as pet-forms. Dr Johnson's friend Archibald Campbell, the architect Robert Adam's brother-in-law Archibald Alison, the footballers and admirals of the 19th century, all carry a name that signals Scottish, Presbyterian, and stalwart before any deed is done.

  • Head sense: Archibald of Angus (source sense): the Douglas earls who held Tantallon — above all Archibald, 5th Earl (1449-1513, Bell-the-Cat, who defied James III and fell at Flodden's eve) and Archibald, 6th Earl (1489-1557, who married Margaret Tudor, ruled for the boy James V, and was forfeited). To say no Archibald of Angus is to say the lordship is vacant, the age of magnates past.
  • General onomastic sense: the Christian name: e.g. my uncle Archibald, Archibald Prize, Archibald Cochrane. Ranked among the top Scottish boys' names from the 15th to the early 20th century, now rare for babies but common for grandfathers, clubs, prizes, streets, and ships. Diminutives are Archie, Archy, Baldie; surname derivatives are Archibaldson, Archbold, Archbell.
  • Scott sense: Archibald in Marmion and Lady of the Lake: Scott peoples his poems with historical Archibalds and invented ones, always as honourable, formidable elders who rebuke young hotheads. The Archibald of Marmion warns Marmion against invasion; the name guarantees counsel and courage.
  • Caricature sense: fusty Archibald: 20th-century English comedy uses Archibald for pompous, old-fashioned respectability — Archibald the butler, old Archibald at the club — much as Cecil or Reginald does. The tone is affectionate-mocking: tweeds, whiskers, and disapproval of modernity.
  • Global echo: Archibald abroad: the Sydney Archibald Prize for portraiture (bequeathed by J. F. Archibald), Archibald, Pennsylvania, Archibald's restaurants, and the Archibald crab-apple cultivar all borrow Scottish prestige without Douglas blood.

Grammatically Archibald is a proper noun, countable only as different bearers (two Archibalds in the regiment), with possessive Archibald's. Pronunciation in RP is /ˈɑːtʃɪbɔːld/ with ch as in church, stress on the first syllable; Scots keeps a harder /ˈartʃɪbald/. Spelling is stable; medieval variants Archambald, Archebald, Archembald survive only in charters. Register is formal-given-name: to call a child Archibald today is a deliberate revival, to call a man Archie is familiar affection.
