gulf (noun): A gulf is, first and literally, a deep inlet of the sea almost surrounded by land — a large bay so enclosed that it forms a basin of water with shores, a body of sea that reaches far into the land and is nearly or partly cut off from the open ocean. The Persian Gulf, the Gulf of Mexico, and the Gulf of Bothnia are the classic instances, each a great hollow of water bounded on most sides by coast. The word's second concrete sense is a deep ravine or chasm, an abyss or deep hollow in the earth — a gulf in the ground, a pit that swallows — and in older English this was often the primary sense: the "gulf" of hell or of the sea that swallows the drowned, the wide-open mouth of destruction. In the source passage — "The beam also failed to reach the further shore of the gulf, which might have been many leagues in extent" — the word is stretched between its senses: the explorers face an immense underground hollow, a subterranean sea of shadow, one with "shores" but no water, and the beam of a torch cannot cross it; "many leagues in extent" — leagues, the ancient unit of walking, of human scale, held up against a void that is inhumanly wide. The gulf here is simultaneously a geography and a measure of separation.

Polysemy and modern extensions: (i) the primary geographic sense — a deep inlet of the sea almost surrounded by land (Gulf of Mexico, Persian Gulf); (ii) the older concrete sense — a deep ravine, chasm, or abyss in the earth, and by extension the pit of destruction or of hell in religious and literary use ("the gulf that swallowed the dead"); (iii) the dominant modern figurative sense — an impassable gap or wide separation between people, classes, opinions, or generations: "the gulf between rich and poor," "the gulf between the generations," "a gulf of misunderstanding," "the gulf between theory and practice." This figurative sense, fully developed by the 18th–19th centuries, is now so strong that gulf is often used with no sea in sight at all, purely as the word for the widest kind of difference — a distance so great it cannot be crossed, exactly as a torchbeam cannot cross the underground waterless sea of the passage; (iv) in finance and business jargon, a "gulf" (or "valuation gulf") is an enormous disparity — in price, expectations, or valuation — between what two sides believe or offer; (v) in geology and planetary science, the word is used loosely of major basins and depressions, terrestrial or extraterrestrial (a crater gulf, a rift gulf); (vi) dialectal and archaic senses — the throat or gullet (gulf is cognate with the family of "gulp" and Old French gole "throat"), the wide-open mouth of an animal or monster, and a whirlpool or engulfing current; (vii) the verb to gulf, "to swallow up, engulf," is archaic and mostly obsolete but confirms the deep etymological link to "swallowing." The historical semantic bridge: the Greek root kolpos meant "bosom, lap, fold, hollow," and the image of a fold of cloth or the lap of a garment — a hollow that holds and contains — was transferred first to a hollow of the sea (a bay) and to a hollow of the earth (a chasm), and then, by the metaphor of distance, to any hollow or gap that holds two things apart. The gulf, in all its senses, is a hollow that separates: land from sea, earth from depth, one person from another.
