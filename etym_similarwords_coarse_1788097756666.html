• Rough: Similar in the texture sense (rough hands, rough cloth); differs because rough stays purely physical and even positive (rough terrain, rough diamond), while coarse also carries the quality and vulgarity senses.
  • Harsh: Similar in describing an unpleasant surface or manner; differs because harsh is sharper — grating, severe, cruel — while coarse implies thickness and dullness rather than severity.
  • Crude: Similar in “unrefined, vulgar”; differs because crude names raw, unprocessed matter and blunt tactlessness, whereas coarse emphasises roughness of grain or manners.
  • Rude: Similar in vulgarity; differs because rude is purely a behavioural failing (impolite, offensive) and never describes texture.
  • Vulgar: Similar in indecency and low taste; differs because vulgar implies the commonness and bad taste of the crowd, while coarse implies roughness and unrefinement, and keeps its innocent texture senses.
  • Gross: Similar in the older senses “coarse, thick, indelicate”; differs because gross has swung to “total/whole” (gross weight) and to a much stronger “disgusting, obscene” force, and no longer contrasts with “fine” in ordinary usage.
  • Unrefined: Similar in lacking processing or polish; differs because unrefined is clinically neutral (unrefined sugar, oil) and never means vulgar or rude.
  • Gritty: Similar in rough texture; differs because gritty implies loose particles or sand, and its figurative sense is toughness and realism, not vulgarity.
  • Rugged: Similar in physical roughness; differs because rugged is admiring (rugged coastline, rugged individual), never moral or vulgar.
  • Abrasive: Similar in roughness that wears or grates; differs because abrasive is mechanical (friction) and, of people, means grating or confrontational rather than vulgar.
  • Indelicate: Similar in lacking refinement; differs because indelicate is a polite euphemism for tactless or off-colour, far weaker and narrower than coarse.
  • Boorish: Similar in describing a rude, unrefined person; differs because boorish is exclusively social clumsiness and never a texture word.
  • Common: Similar in the old “ordinary, low-class” sense; differs because common has lost the texture sense entirely and is now purely social or statistical.
