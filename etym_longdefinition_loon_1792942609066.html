Loon is one spelling holding two birds and one fool. In its everyday human sense it means a silly, daft, or crazy fellow — a clown, a simpleton, a madcap whose behaviour has slipped its moorings — exactly the bawdy ballad sense of the source line, where a loon is he that will not sing: the dull or surly companion who refuses to join the round, mocked as witless for sitting out the merriment. In its naturalist's sense it is the great northern diving bird, the common loon of Canada and Scandinavia, famous for its tremolo wail rolling across night water. The two senses come from wholly different nests and only converged on one spelling by accident and by ear.

Core meaning in detail: The fool-loon descends from Middle English and Scots loun, a lazy rascal, idle boy, or low wench — a scolding word for worthless youth — sharpened in modern slang through association with lunatic and the cartoon loony into the meaning crazy person. The bird-loon descends from Old Norse lómr, the diver, named for its lameness on land: a loon walks as if crippled because its legs sit so far aft for swimming, and the Norse name records that hobbling gait. English sailors and settlers in northern waters borrowed the bird's name with the bird, first as loom and loon side by side, before ornithology fixed loon in America and diver in Britain for the same genus Gavia.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Foolish or crazy person (primary modern human sense; the source sense): you silly loon, the old loon next door. The bridge runs from Scots loun, idle rascal, through nineteenth-century loon, lout or simpleton, reinforced by the pull of lunatic, loony, and the bird's own mad laughter of a cry: the fool and the diver's wail now confirm each other in the popular ear.
  • Great northern diving bird (primary ornithological sense, from the 17th c.): the common loon, red-throated loon, and their kin — heavy fish-divers with dagger bills and night-piercing calls. The bridge is borrowing with the bird: Norse fishermen named the lame-walking diver lómr, and English-speaking settlers kept the name where the bird outnumbered the books, in Canada and the northern States.
  • Idle rogue or lout (older Scots and northern sense, 15th–19th c.): a loun about the town, a servant-girl loun in Burns. The bridge is the human root itself: before it meant mad, it meant worthless and work-shy, the parish scold-word for youth hanging about with nothing to do.
  • Slang lunatic, loony (20th c. overlap): acting like a total loon, the cartoon loons of comedy. The bridge is contamination by sound: loon and the clipped loon- of lunatic fell together, and the bird's famously unhinged night chorus sealed the merger — to cry like a loon is, acoustically, to sound unhinged.
  • Simple festive refuser (ballad and convivial use, as in source): he that will not sing, drink, or dance when the round demands it. The bridge is social rather than clinical: the loon here need not be mad, only churlish, and the singers' insult works by equating killjoy silence with witlessness.
  • Place-name and surname residue: the bird survives in lake names (Loon Lake in half a dozen states and provinces) and the fool in surnames like Loon and Scots Loun. The bridge is commemoration: landscapes keep the bird where it bred, families keep the nickname where it stuck.

Register and connotation: said of a person, loon is affectionate more often than cruel — the daft uncle, the lovable eccentric, the mate who jumped in the harbour on a dare — closer to character than to lunatic, though it can sharpen when aimed at strangers. Said of the bird, it is pure wilderness: solitude, black water, pine shores, and that long quavering cry which travellers describe as the loneliest sound in nature. The ballad line keeps the human sense at its most communal and teasing: sing, or be laughed at as the loon of the company.
