bung-oh is a small word with a short, thickly documented life. It appears in print only at the beginning of the 1920s and is already marked as fashionable slang by the middle of that decade. Its history therefore belongs less to the longue durée of Indo-European than to the social history of interwar Britain: public schools, Oxford and Cambridge, the cavalry, the country house, the cocktail bar and the music hall. The word is a compound of two elements, but neither element can be traced with confidence, and its earliest meaning may have been a toast rather than a farewell.

Components: two bungs and a ho. English has two quite different nouns bung. The first, “a stopper for a cask”, is recorded from the late Middle Ages and comes from Middle Dutch bonghe, perhaps ultimately from Latin puncta “a puncture”. The second, “a purse” or “a bribe”, is a sixteenth-century word of uncertain origin. Neither is the source of the verb bung “to throw”, which is a Scots and northern English variant of bang and is recorded from the early nineteenth century. Green’s Dictionary of Slang derives bung-ho! from this verb, with the image of “throwing” a drink down the throat. The derivation is plausible because it accounts for the toast sense and for the word’s hearty, physical flavour; it is not proved, because slang compounds are often formed by punning and analogy rather than by strict semantic composition. The second element, ho, is an old call or exclamation, familiar from heave-ho, tally-ho and westward ho. It contributes emphasis and sociability, not a detachable meaning.

  • 1921: D. L. Sayers, The Unpleasantness at the Bellona Club, records “Bung-ho!” as a toast over a dry martini.
  • 1925: An American newspaper notices “Bungo!” as a fashionable substitute for “Here’s how!”
  • 1926: Hemingway, The Sun Also Rises, uses “Bung-o!” in a drinking scene.
  • 1934: Wodehouse, Right Ho, Jeeves, uses “Well, bung-ho!” as a cheerful farewell.
  • 1948: Betjeman’s “Beside the Seaside” lists “Bunghosky” among fashionable parting formulas.
  • 1955: Donleavy plays with the phrase as an adjective meaning “proper, right, good”.

From toast to farewell. The semantic path from drinking to parting is easy to reconstruct because both are ritual moments at which people raise something: a glass, a hat, a hand. A toast is a blessing uttered at a shared occasion; a farewell is a blessing uttered at the end of an occasion. The word’s cheerful, slightly aggressive heartiness suits both. The earliest citations cluster around drinking, but by 1923 it is already a goodbye, and by the 1930s Wodehouse can use it as the natural thing a young man says when a girl turns him down. The shift is not a “corruption” of meaning; it is an extension of a social gesture from one kind of leave-taking to another.

Sound and spelling. The pronunciation /ˌbʌŋˈəʊ/ preserves the velar nasal of bung and a long open back vowel in the second syllable. When the h is dropped, bung-ho and bung-oh become homophonous; when it is retained, the word sounds more deliberately archaic. The spelling variants are typical of twentieth-century colloquial English, which had no single authority for slang. Bungo looks like an Italianate proper name; bungho looks like a transliteration; bung-ho looks like a music-hall catchphrase. The hyphen in modern dictionaries is a convention for legibility, not evidence of an Old English compound.

Social register. The word is a class marker. It belongs to the speech of the British upper middle class between the wars, and its companions are old fruit, old bean, old thing, right-ho, pip-pip, toodle-oo and what-ho. These expressions were not simply “posh”; they were the self-conscious idiom of a generation that had been to the same schools and fought in the same war. Their heartiness was partly a defence against earnestness. Bung-oh carries that whole atmosphere in two syllables, which is why Wodehouse uses it and why later writers use it to signal that they are writing about Wodehouse’s world.

Decline and survival. After 1945 the idiom aged quickly. The social world that sustained it shrank, and the rise of a more democratic public speech made its hearty upper-class manner sound stagey. By the 1970s dictionaries labelled it “dated” or “archaic”. It survived because Wodehouse remained popular, because Sayers’s Lord Peter Wimsey remained popular, and because television adaptations of both kept the word in circulation as a period flavour. In the twenty-first century it is almost never used spontaneously; it is used to quote, to parody or to evoke. That is a common fate for slang that is tied to a vanished class culture: it becomes a quotation.

Literary attestations and afterlife. The word’s documentary trail is unusually clear for a slang item. Sayers gives it to Wimsey, Hemingway gives it to expatriate drinkers, Wodehouse gives it to Bertie Wooster, Betjeman gives it to a seaside crowd, le Carré gives it to a journalist. Each use is a small social portrait. In Wodehouse it is part of the comic machinery by which a trivial phrase deflates a serious emotion. In Hemingway it is part of the laconic drinking code of the Lost Generation. In Betjeman it is part of the affectionate satire of English seaside manners. The word has no grand etymology, but it has a precise cultural biography, and that biography is the best guide to its meaning.

Conclusion. Bung-oh is a twentieth-century British interjection, probably formed on the slang verb bung “to throw” and the exclamatory particle ho, first used as a drinking toast and soon extended to a farewell. Its spelling is fluid, its register is upper-middle-class and dated, and its modern life is almost entirely literary and nostalgic. It is not related to bunghole, and it does not mean “good luck” in any technical sense; it means “cheers” or “so long”, delivered with a hearty slap on the back.
