DESPAIRED — the biography of a lost hope.
The story begins in a PIE root *speh₁- meaning "to prosper, to thrive, to grow fat," a word of swelling and success. Its daughters span the family: Vedic Sanskrit sphāyate "it swells, it increases," Lithuanian spėti "to manage, to be in time," Slavic *spěti "to ripen, to succeed" (whence Russian uspekh "success"), and the Germanic pair Old English spōwan "to prosper" and spēd "prosperity, riches, success." That last word, spēd, is the ancestor of English speed — a beautiful example of semantic drift, since the word slid from "prosperity, wealth, success" to "haste, velocity" over the centuries, so that "God speed" literally prays "may God prosper you," while a speeding car has long forgotten the root's meaning. The drift matters for despair because it shows how a single root can split into opposite emotional temperatures: the "swelling" that means thriving in one language becomes, when negated, the "shrinking" of hope in another.

In Latin the same root took a turn toward expectation: spēs "hope," spērāre "to hope, to expect," formed on the nominal stem. The privative prefix dē- — "down from, away, without" — produced dēspērāre, "to be without hope, to abandon hope": not the dimming of hope but its deletion, the grammatical cancelling of the verb "to hope" by its negative prefix. Classical writers used it freely — Cicero reports despairing in defeat, Vergil lets despair colour his epic, and Latin rhetoricians catalogued despair as the emotion that follows the loss of every expectation. Romance inherited the verb whole: Old French desperer, Modern French désespérer, with the noun désespoir carrying the burden of romantic and tragic feeling in French literature.

English took the word through the Anglo-Norman intermediary despeirer in the thirteenth century, the noun despair (from Old French desespoir, Anglo-Norman despeir) arriving alongside it. From that moment the word carried a religious as well as an emotional charge: the Church ranked despair among the most dangerous spiritual conditions — the refusal to trust in God's mercy that was folded into the sin of acedia, or spiritual sloth — and pastoral writers warned that despair was the one sin that could not be repented, because it cut off the very channel of repentance. This theological gravity saturated English literature: Spenser's Despair, in Book I of The Faerie Queene (1590), is an allegorical tempter who argues the Redcrosse Knight into contemplating suicide; Milton weighs despair in Paradise Lost; and Shakespeare builds whole scenes on the word's tragic music. The secular literature of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries gradually loosened the theological knot, and despair became the name of a universal human emotion — the felt, utter absence of hope in any worldly matter.

The nineteenth and twentieth centuries gave the word a philosophical and clinical afterlife. Kierkegaard's The Sickness unto Death (1849) defined despair as the condition of the self that will not be itself, an existential malaise rather than a mere sadness; existentialist writers after him used despair for the felt meaninglessness of existence; and modern psychology split the state from the feeling, studying "hopelessness" as an objective expectation and "despair" as the agonised experience of it, alongside Seligman's "learned helplessness," the behavioural shutting-down that despair produces. The verb meanwhile turned chiefly literary: "to despair of" a person or outcome grew common in the nineteenth century and then receded, leaving the bare intransitive "I despaired" as the form with the strongest literary ring.

The form despaired adds a second, Germanic etymology onto the Romance body: the dental preterite -ed, descended from the PIE root *dʰeh₁- "to put, do" that underlies the Greek passive suffix -thē- and the Sanskrit imperative -dhi-, so that the English past tense is, at root, a periphrasis — "I did despair." In this passage the whole layered history is present at once: the old root's "prosperity" denied by dē-, the medieval gravity of the deadly sin, the existential weight of the modern philosophers, and the plain English past tense — "I have often despaired; but something always happens to start me hoping again."

  • The root: PIE *speh₁- "to prosper" — Sanskrit sphāyate, Lithuanian spėti, Slavic spěti, Old English spōwan / spēd (whence speed).
  • The Latin turn: spēs "hope," spērāre "to hope," and the privative dē- in dēspērāre "to un-hope."
  • The Romance path: Vulgar Latin *dēspērāre → Old French desperer → Anglo-Norman despeirer → Middle English despeiren (13th c.).
  • The theology: acedia, the sin of despair, "the one sin that cannot be repented"; Spenser's Despair, Milton, Shakespeare.
  • The modern arc: Kierkegaard's "sickness unto death," existential despair, psychology's hopelessness and learned helplessness.
  • The -ed suffix: PIE *dʰeh₁- "to do" → Germanic dental preterite → English past tense, "I did despair."
