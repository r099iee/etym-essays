LINEAR CHAIN FOR "droned" (past tense of "to drone", "to make a low continuous hum or to speak monotonously")

Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *dhren- (c. 4500–2500 BCE) — "to hum, buzz, murmur". A root reconstructed as echoic (sound-imitating), describing low continuous murmuring or buzzing sounds. Its reflexes include Greek thrēnos "a lament, dirge, wailing song" (a vocal hum or mourning chant), Sanskrit dhvanati "sounds, roars", and the Germanic stem that produces our word. The root is plausible but, being echoic, is reconstructed with caution; the Germanic and English stages below are the firmly attested part of the chain.

Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic: *dran- / *drēn- (c. 500 BCE – 1st millennium CE) — "male bee, drone; a hum". The low continuous buzz of the male honeybee gives the Germanic stem its noun, which also carries the general idea of humming. Cognate with Old Saxon drān, Middle Dutch drāne, Old High German treno "drone, male bee".

Stage 3 — Old English: drān (before 12th century) — "male honeybee, drone". The word names the male bee, whose continuous low buzz is its defining feature, and whose idleness (it gathers no honey) makes it the type of an unproductive person.

Stage 4 — Middle English: drane / drone (12th–15th centuries) — "male honeybee; idler". The noun continues with both its entomological and figurative senses.

Stage 5 — English noun: drone (16th century onward) — "a male bee; an idler; a continuous low humming sound; a sustained bass note or pipe of a bagpipe".

Stage 6 — English verb: drone (16th–17th centuries) — "to make a low continuous humming or buzzing sound; to speak in a monotonous, flat, unvarying tone". The verb is denominal — formed from the noun — and by the 17th century is applied to dull speech ("to drone on").

Stage 7 — English: droned (17th century onward) — the regular past tense and past participle of drone. DRONED ◄ TARGET

NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: (1) The PIE stage *dhren- is a reconstruction based on the echoic nature of the word family; because sound-symbolic vocabulary is notoriously slippery, some scholars are reluctant to assign it a fixed PIE form, though the Greek and Germanic reflexes make a common echoic origin very likely. (2) The modern noun "drone" = unmanned aircraft (20th century) is a recent technical sense named for the monotonous engine hum of early target drones; it is a new application of the existing word, not a new etymology. (3) The bagpipe "drone" pipe (16th century) is a specialist transfer of the "continuous bass note" sense and follows the same acoustic logic.
