• Ethiopians: Similar because it denotes the people of the same country and is the standard modern equivalent; differs because Ethiopians is the inclusive citizenship term for all of Ethiopia's peoples, whereas Abyssinians historically names only the Christian highland core.
  • Habesha: Similar because it is the self-designation closely associated with the same highland, Amharic- and Tigrinya-speaking Christian culture; differs because Habesha is an insider cultural identity, not a translation of the foreign place-name, and its boundaries are debated among Ethiopians themselves.
  • Aksumites: Similar because they were the ancient inhabitants of the northern highland state that is the ancestor of Abyssinia; differs because Aksumites is strictly chronological, referring to the kingdom of Aksum (c. 1st–7th c. CE), not to later Ethiopian society.
  • Amharas: Similar because the Amhara are the largest component of the historical Abyssinian elite; differs because Amharas names one ethnolinguistic group, while Abyssinians also conventionally includes Tigrayans and other highland peoples.
  • Tigrayans: Similar because they share the same highland Christian heritage and language family; differs because Tigrayans is a single ethnic label, and their political relationship with the Ethiopian state has its own modern history.
  • Highland Ethiopians: Similar because the phrase describes the same upland population in neutral geographic terms; differs because it is a modern descriptive category that avoids the imperial and colonial associations of Abyssinians.
  • Ethio-Semitic peoples: Similar because the historical Abyssinians were chiefly speakers of Ethio-Semitic languages such as Amharic, Tigrinya and Ge'ez; differs because it is a linguistic classification, not a demonym, and excludes Cushitic-speaking highlanders who lived within the same polity.
