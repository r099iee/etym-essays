The biography of accursed is almost entirely a story of English, because the word's foundation, curse, has no known ancestor. Old English curs, attested before the year 1000, is a genuine English word — but it has no cognates in the other Germanic languages, no accepted Indo-European root, and no agreed origin. Etymologists have floated three main ideas: that it may owe something to ecclesiastical Latin (the fanciful old derivation from cursus, 'course,' reading a curse as a sentence or course of fate); that it may reflect contact with Celtic (comparisons with Old Irish cúrsagim, 'I reprove,' have been drawn); or that it is a native Germanic creation whose early development is simply opaque. The OED records the whole question under 'origin unknown.' No honest derivation tree, therefore, begins below Old English, and any chain that asserts a PIE root for curse is inventing what the record does not contain. What the record does contain is a productive Old English verb. From the noun curs, Old English formed cursian, 'to curse, to call down evil upon,' and from cursian it formed acursian by prefixing a-, the native Germanic intensifier meaning 'thoroughly, completely.' The prefix is the whole point: where cursian was a simple malediction, acursian was to put someone or something fully and solemnly under a curse — to anathematise, to condemn utterly. The same a- prefix appears in a wide Old English family of thoroughness (adreccan 'to weary utterly,' aswencan 'to vex'), so accursed enters English as the participle of a strengthened verb, and the extra intensity is built into the word's structure. Middle English carried the formation forward as acursen and the participial adjective acursed, and the word lived almost entirely inside a religious and legal world. To be acursed was to be under the church's ban: excommunicated, anathematised, cut off from the sacraments and from the community of the faithful. The sense of divine condemnation is the oldest and strongest layer of the word, and it gave accursed a gravity that plain cursed never quite had — the grammar of the intensive prefix was still faintly audible behind it, so that even in secular use the word kept an echo of solemn imprecation. The broadening from damnation to loathing was a natural slide. If the worst thing the church could say of a person was that they were accursed, then any detestable thing — a person, a place, an object, an event — could be called accursed as a term of intense dislike. By the early modern period the adjective was doing regular service as a strong intensifier of anger and contempt, equivalent to 'damned' or 'confounded' but with an archaic, literary dignity of its own: 'that accursed noise,' 'the accursed thing.' The verb accurse shrank to rarity, but the adjective flourished precisely because it could fuse moral outrage with the old supernatural charge. In modern English accursed reads as consciously literary or biblical. It survives in elevated and melodramatic prose, where it brands a misfortune as personally, even supernaturally, directed — the exact note struck in the Red Chamber sentence, where a stroke of bad luck is 'accursed' because it is so cruelly and unforeseeably timed. The participial spelling carries a three-syllable pronunciation, /əˈkɜːsɪd/, which older and rhetorical speakers preserve (as in 'the ac-CURS-ed'), though in ordinary use many now say two syllables like cursed. The unprefixed sister cursed remains the everyday base, and damned the everyday intensifier; accursed keeps the highest register of the family, the word a novelist reaches for when mere bad luck is not bad enough — when what the narrator means is that fate itself seems to have pronounced a curse.
