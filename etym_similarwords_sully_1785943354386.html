• SOIL: Similar because both mean to make dirty and are the closest ordinary equivalents; differs because soil is the everyday, neutral verb for physical dirt (soil your clothes, soil the carpet) with no strong moral weight, while sully is the formal, literary verb whose main modern force is the figurative tarnishing of honour and reputation.
  • STAIN: Similar because both denote marking or discolouring a surface and can be used figuratively of reputation; differs because stain names a persistent discoloration (a coffee stain) and, figuratively, a lasting disgrace (the stain of betrayal), whereas sully emphasizes the act of soiling or tarnishing a previously pure thing rather than the resulting permanent mark.
  • TARNISH: Similar because both mean to dim or spoil the brightness of something, physically or morally; differs because tarnish derives from the dulling of metal (a tarnished silver cup) and therefore stresses loss of lustre, while sully stresses the addition of dirt to a pure surface; tarnish is also common of reputations and legacies, like sully, but lacks the visceral mud imagery.
  • DEFILE: Similar because both mean to make impure or dirty, morally and religiously; differs because defile is the graver, more archaic word of ritual pollution and violation (defile a temple, defile a maiden, defile the dead), with sacred overtones, whereas sully is the more ordinary literary word for tarnishing honour.
  • BESMIRCH: Similar because both mean to tarnish a reputation; differs because besmirch (from "smirch," to smear with soot) is more rhetorical and slightly more archaic, used almost exclusively of reputations, while sully can still be used literally of surfaces and keeps its physical base visible.
  • SMEAR: Similar because both mean to spread dirt across a surface, literally or metaphorically; differs because smear suggests smearing a substance (or metaphorically, a slander) over a surface, with a sloppy, irregular result, while sully suggests a general soiling and, metaphorically, a grave moral defilement rather than a political smear campaign.
  • SMUDGE: Similar because both mean to mark with dirt; differs because smudge is the light, everyday verb for a smear or rubbed stain (smudged ink, a smudged window) with no moral meaning, while sully carries dignity and the figurative sense of dishonour.
  • BLEMISH: Similar because both can mean to damage the purity or perfection of something; differs because a blemish is a small flaw or spot, and to blemish is to mar with minor defects, whereas sully suggests a defiling dirt or a serious tarnishing of honour.
  • TAINT: Similar because both mean to contaminate or corrupt something previously pure; differs because taint stresses contamination, often by corruption or poison (tainted meat, a tainted reputation), entering from outside, while sully stresses surface soiling and the moral verb of scandal.
  • POLLUTE: Similar because both mean to make impure; differs because pollute is the strong technical word for contaminating the environment, water, or air, and, figuratively, the mind or morals, whereas sully is the lighter, more literary verb of tarnishing honour and purity.
  • DEBASE: Similar because both mean to lower the moral quality of something; differs because debase means to reduce in worth, dignity, or value (debased coinage, debased taste), while sully means to dirty or stain something that was pure, without necessarily lowering its worth.
  • DIRTY: Similar because both mean to make unclean; differs because dirty is the plain, everyday verb and adjective, while sully is the formal, literary word whose main force is figurative defilement of reputation and honour.
  • BEGRIME: Similar because both mean to make dirty; differs because begrime is a heavier, earthier word for covering with grime or soot, chiefly literal, while sully is the elegant verb of both literal soiling and moral tarnishing.
  • BEFOUL: Similar because both mean to make dirty or filthy; differs because befoul is stronger and more literal, suggesting filth or excrement, while sully is milder and predominantly figurative, tarnishing rather than befouling.
