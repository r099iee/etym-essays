The strange hyphenated string death-where-is-thy-sting-fullness is not a standard dictionary word but a literary nonce-compound in the manner of P. G. Wodehouse, built out of one of the most famous biblical taunts in English: St Paul's O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? (1 Corinthians 15:55, King James Version, 1611). To understand it we must trace each component separately and then explain how they were fused into a Wodehousean abstraction in -ness denoting a manner or aura.

Death: From PIE *dheu- to die, pass away, extended with a -th- abstract suffix in Proto-Germanic *d Guthuz death, the act or state of dying. Old English dead already covered both the event and the personified power, Death. Middle English deth shows familiar vowel shortening. The sense has remained remarkably stable: cessation of life, then by extension cause of death, personified Death, extinction or ruin. English multiplied synonyms (demise, decease, passing) around it while death remained the blunt core term, never displaced.

Where, is, thy: All three are ancient grammatical function words. Where continues PIE interrogative *kwo- seen in Latin quis, Greek tis, Sanskrit kas. Germanic *hwar gave Old English hwaer in what place. The hw- to wh- simplification is routine. Is continues PIE *h1es- to be, one of the most conservative verbs: Sanskrit asti, Greek esti, Latin est, German ist, English is are transparently the same word after 5,000 years. Thy continues PIE *te- second singular pronoun: Latin tu, French tu, German du, English thou, thy, thine. Old English thin declined for case; Middle English reduced thi before consonants versus thine before vowels, and Early Modern English replaced thou-forms with you except in liturgy and fixed biblical echoes. Retaining thy instantly signals archaism and scriptural solemnity, which is precisely the joke when grafted onto a contemporary hostess.

Sting: From PIE *stegh- to prick, sting, via Proto-Germanic *stingan to thrust, pierce, sting. Old English stingan meant both to stab with a weapon and to be stung by insect or nettle. The noun sting developed from the verb. In Paul, kentron in Greek meant a goad, sting, or sharp point used to prod oxen - hence Latin stimulus - and English translators chose sting to keep both the insect image and the moral image of sin's sharpness. Theologically, sting became shorthand for death's power to hurt, poisoned by sin. Wodehouse's audience knew the phrase from Easter liturgy and funeral sermons.

Fullness (full + -ness): Full from PIE *pelh1- to fill (Latin plenus, Greek pleres, Sanskrit purna). Proto-Germanic *fullaz, Old English full. The suffix -ness from Proto-Germanic *-nassus, Old English -nes, forms abstract nouns of quality (goodness, darkness, fullness). Fullness is attested since Old English fulnes in both concrete sense state of being filled and spiritual sense plenitude, perfection, divine abundance (the fullness of time, Galatians 4:4). By Middle English it also meant completeness of manner and expression.

  • Biblical fixation (1611): The KJV wording fixed death, where is thy sting in Anglophone memory. Handel set it in Messiah (1741) as a bass recitative, embedding it further in popular culture.
  • Victorian sermon and parody: Preachers used it triumphantly at Easter; humorists began to borrow it ironically to describe smug comebacks from illness, debt or social eclipse.
  • Wodehousean nonce-formation (1920s-1930s): Wodehouse loved to turn whole clauses into hyphenated nouns: in-the-soup, heart-bowed-downness. The device mocks both pedantic abstraction and the character's inflated self-satisfaction by granting a passing smirk the dignity of doctrine.
  • Modern perception: Outside Wodehouse the form has no currency; readers parse it as the aura of someone acting as if she has abolished death itself. The distaste in context comes from that gloating triumphalism.

Phonetically nothing irregular happens: each element keeps its normal pronunciation; hyphens merely signal read this whole clause as one noun. Semantically the drift is pragmatic: from eschatological victory over literal death to social gloating, from cosmic to comic. That bathos - Paul on the Resurrection applied to a dislikeable manner - is the entire point, and why the narrator finds it distasteful. The fullness suffix completes the joke by turning a taunt into a stable trait, as if triumph were a perfume she wore.
