rapped — Rapped belongs to the oldest stratum of English sound-words: verbs that imitate the noise they describe. The base verb rap appears in English around 1300, in Middle English, meaning "to strike a sharp, quick blow" — and it is almost certainly onomatopoeic, coined to match the crisp, dry sound of one hard thing meeting another. The word sits in a dense English echo-cluster — tap, clap, slap, snap, flap, whap — all built on the same short-vowel + stop pattern that imitates percussive impact. There is no Proto-Indo-European root behind it and no Latin or French ancestor; like twang, it is a word that every language invents for itself, and English invented it in the fourteenth century. The noun rap ("a sharp blow") appears at the same time, and the verb and noun have fed each other for seven centuries — the noun names the event, the verb performs it. The word's history is a story of the blow turning into the message. From the physical strike English developed rap out, "to utter sharply" — the eighteenth century has officers rapping out orders, and the crispness of the command borrows the crispness of the blow. From "sharp speech" came, in American slang around the early twentieth century, the sense "to talk, converse" — first in the phrase to rap, to chat or banter — and then, in the 1960s and 1970s, in African-American vernacular, "to speak rhythmically over a beat". Rap music, born in the Bronx around 1979, made the word global: rapper, rap battle, rap sheet — and yet the musical rap is still recognisably the old percussive word, transferring the sharpness of the blow to the sharpness of voice and rhythm. A rapper "hits" the beat, "drops" rhymes, and "snaps" the flow; the percussive ancestor is never far away. Alongside this chain, the idiom take the rap (1904) shows the noun passing from "a blow" to "a punishment" to "the blame for a crime", and a "rap sheet" is literally the written record of the blows of the law. Two false friends must be kept out of the family. Old Norse hrapa "to fall, to hurry" gave English a second, homophonous rap meaning "to hasten, to seize, to snatch" (as in the archaic rap and rend, to tear and snatch) — a Scandinavian haste-word that coincides with the strike-rap in form but not in origin. And English rapid descends from Latin rapidus, from rapere "to seize, to tear" — again unrelated, despite the tempting sound-alike rap-id that the mnemonic on this card plays on. The strike-rap of this card is thus an island word: purely English, purely imitative, with a family only among the other percussive echo-words of the language. In C. S. Lewis's "Prince Caspian", Peter raps the shield and sword on the floor to knock off the dust, and the word does its ancient work perfectly: quick, sharp, dry, and audible — the brisk, matter-of-fact sound of a boy handling royal weapons as his own. The word's seven centuries of meaning — from the blow, to the reprimand, to the command, to the rhythm of music — all begin in that single crisp impact on a hard floor.
  • Sound and form: the echo-cluster tap, clap, slap, snap, flap, whap, rap shares the pattern of a short vowel followed by a stop + labial or nasal; rap's final -p captures the dry closure of the blow.
  • Morphology: rapped is rap + the weak past-tense suffix -ed; the word generates rapping, rapper, rappy, and the compounds rap sheet, rap battle, rap music.
  • Chronology: c. 1300 first recorded; the figurative reprimand in the 16th–17th c.; rap out in the 18th; take the rap (1904); to rap "to talk" in early 20th; rap music c. 1979.
  • Literary and musical attestation: from medieval proverbs ("a rap on the knuckles") through Shakespeare's era to the Bronx block parties of 1979, the word has travelled from the blow to the beat without ever losing the sound of the blow.
  • Socio-cultural context: the 20th-century rise of rap music is a textbook case of semantic widening — the percussive blow became the percussive word, and the sharpness of the strike became the sharpness of the rhyme.
