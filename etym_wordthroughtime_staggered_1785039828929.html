- 13th-14th centuries - Stagger enters English from Norse for the reeling, unsteady walk of one losing command of the legs - the tottering of the drunk, the wounded, or the exhausted.
- 16th-17th centuries - The word goes figurative: to stagger a person is to shock or bewilder them so thoroughly that their mental footing gives way, and to stagger is also to make blows or plans fall unevenly.
- Contemporary sense - Staggered still describes unsteady, near-falling motion and overwhelming shock, and it has added a precise technical sense - staggered rows or payments arranged in alternation; in every use it keeps the image of losing one's footing.
