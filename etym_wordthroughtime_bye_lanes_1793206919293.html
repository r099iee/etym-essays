- Old English — the parts exist: bi (near, aside) and lanu (narrow way) live side by side for centuries before ever joining.
- 14th c. — the cousin is born: byway appears for side roads and (figuratively) devious courses; the by- family of minor ways is established.
- 1500s — by-lane enters: writers needing a word for the smallest public lanes compound it plainly; spelling wobbles for centuries between by-lane, bye-lane, and by lane.
- 1820s — Cobbett's instrument: the bye-lanes become the travelling radical's roads — the only ways that show the real producing England the turnpikes hide.
- Motoring age — romance and rescue: numbered B-roads and tarmac demote real bye-lanes to leisure — walkers' guides, cycling clubs, and preservationists celebrate what the car cannot use.
- Colonial export — the Indian byelane: British address habits root the word in South Asian postal language, where it regularises and thrives in thousands of addresses.
- Today — heritage and metaphor: surviving bye-lanes are walked, ridden, and photographed as old England, while historians happily wander the bye-lanes of their subjects.
