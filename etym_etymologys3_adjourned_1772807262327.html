The biography of adjourn begins with time and sky. Its deepest root is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European base *dyew-, which names the bright sky and daylight and underlies a whole constellation of day-words and sky-god names: Latin diēs "day", Greek Zeus, Sanskrit dyáuḥ "sky", and Latin Iuppiter, the sky-father. From *dyew- Latin derived diēs "day", with the further sense of an appointed day, a date set in the calendar; and from diēs it built the adjective diurnus "of a day, daily". It is this Latinate day-word — not the Germanic day (English day < Germanic *dagaz) — that eventually reaches English inside adjourn. The crucial step happens in Old French. Latin diurnum (the neuter, "daily matter, day") developed into the Romance word for "day": Old French jorn / jour, with the characteristic palatalisation of Latin d- before the following vowel. To this the language added the prefix a- (< Latin ad, "to, toward"), producing ajorner / ajourner: literally "to put toward a day", that is, to fix a day. In medieval legal and administrative usage the verb had two faces, both institutional. To ajourner someone could mean to summon them to appear in court on a specified day (to give them their day), and it could equally mean to put a matter off to a day (to defer it). Both senses run on the same engine: authority controls the calendar; whoever fixes the day controls when justice resumes. This is the semantic core that later English adjourn inherits, and it explains why the word has always lived in courtrooms and assemblies. Entering English. In the thirteenth century, through Anglo-French and Old French, the verb appears in Middle English as ajornen / adjournen. Early English usage still shows the double face of the French original: the verb could summon ("adjourned the parties to appear") and could defer ("adjourned the matter to the next term"). During the later medieval and early modern periods the second face dominates, and the sense settles into the procedural suspension we know: a court, parliament, or assembly breaks off its sitting with the intention (or formal possibility) of resuming. The word becomes a workhorse of legal and parliamentary prose — one of the technical verbs by which public bodies manage their own time. Related English words from the same Latin day-root show how "day" radiated across the lexicon: journal, originally a daily record (French journal), journey, originally a day's travel (Old French jornée), and the later loan diurnal in astronomy and zoology. Sound and form. The change from Latin diēs to French jour involves the palatalisation of Latin d- and the reduction of Latin diurnum; Old French jour (earlier jor) drops the Latin vocalism almost entirely. English adjourn also shows the etymological d- reintroduced in spelling: the word's written form was influenced by Latin ad + diurnus, so Middle English ajournen later became adjourn, restoring a written d that French had never pronounced and English never sounded. Pronunciation follows the French: the d is silent, and the word is pronounced /əˈdʒɜːn/ with a soft g. Spelling and pronunciation thus preserve two different moments of the word's history. Semantic drift and idiom. In modern English the verb slightly broadens. A meeting may adjourn for lunch or until next week; courts adjourn a hearing to a later date. The marked formula adjourn sine die (Latin, "without a day") ends a sitting without fixing a resumption date, effectively an indefinite or final suspension — "day" still at the heart of the idiom. Literary and informal English also use the prepositional adjourn to: a group adjourns to the dining-room, the pub, or the garden, carrying the old sense of formally removing the company to another place. It is precisely this "adjourn to" that appears in the novel, where Yu-cun adjourns to his private chambers. The verb dignifies his withdrawal with procedural formality: what looks like a mere exit is staged as an official act, the court's sitting suspended and relocated, the usher kept behind. The historical thread — fixing a day, controlling the calendar of justice — still shows through: by adjourning to his chambers, Yu-cun takes control of when and where the case will next be heard, turning public procedure into private power. Sociocultural factors. As a legal and parliamentary loan, adjourn belongs to the institutional vocabulary of the common law and of assemblies — the language of sessions, terms, sine die, and reconvening. Its social meaning tracks the growth of formal institutions that manage time publicly: parliaments, courts, committees. In everyday speech it remains recognizably formal, which is why using it of a trivial removal is comic or ironic; its natural home is still the courtroom and the chamber. From *dyew- "bright sky" to Yu-cun's private chambers is a long path, but the chain is continuous: sky → day → fixed day → summoned day → deferred day → suspended sitting → "adjourned to his chambers".
