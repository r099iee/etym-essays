Linear chain from the reconstructed Indo-European base to the modern word. 1. Proto-Indo-European *h₁eys- (traditionally reconstructed as *eis-) 'to move violently, to be in violent motion, to urge on' (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; Pokorny; see debate note below). 2. Latin īra 'anger, wrath, passion, passionate hostility' (Classical Latin, attested from Ennius and Plautus, 3rd–2nd centuries BCE); the family includes īrāscī 'to grow angry', īrātus 'angered', īrācundus 'quick-tempered'. 3. Old French ire 'anger, wrath' (10th–13th century), the epic anger-word of the chansons de geste, e.g. the wrath of Roland and Charlemagne. 4. Middle English ire / yre 'anger, wrath, violent displeasure' (borrowed 13th century, e.g. in religious and legal texts). 5. Modern English IRE (first attested in its modern form from the late Middle English period onward). Notes on debate and nuance: The deeper Indo-European ancestry of Latin īra is debated. The traditional reconstruction links it to a root *eis- 'to move violently, to hasten' (Pokorny), with alleged cognates including Sanskrit eṣati (एषति) 'he hastens, drives, urges' (Rigveda) and Greek oistros 'gadfly; frenzy'. Modern scholarship (notably de Vaan) is cautious: īra may stand on a base that has left no other secure relatives, and the Sanskrit and Greek connections are plausible but not universally accepted. The Latin and Romance history (īra → Old French ire → Middle English ire) is, by contrast, fully documented and not in dispute.
