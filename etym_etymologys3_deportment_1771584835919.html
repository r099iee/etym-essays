The biography of deportment begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *per-, "to lead, to pass over, to carry." This was one of the most travelled roots of the family: it is the ancestor of the Sanskrit verb pṛ- / par-, "to bring over, carry across," with píparti, "he brings across," and the noun pārá, "the far side, the opposite shore," which survives in Hindi as pār, "across, beyond." In the Germanic branch the same root became Proto-Germanic *faraną, "to travel, to go," producing English fare (both the verb, "to get on, proceed," and the noun, the price of a journey) and, via *ferjaną, "to carry across," the word ferry — a vessel that conveys passengers across a stretch of water. The whole family is built on the image of movement from one side to another: the traveller who fares, the ferryman who carries, and the harbour that receives. That image of carrying and passing is the deep background of our word.

The route into English passes through the Italic branch, where the root produced Latin portāre, "to carry, to convey." From it came portus, "a harbour" (the place of passage — English port), porta, "a gate" (English portal, porch), and portātor, "a carrier" (English porter). Most important for us, Latin combined the verb with the prefix de-, "away, down," to form deportāre, "to carry away, to carry off." Here the semantic history performs its decisive turn. In classical Latin the verb meant physical removal — carrying a prisoner away, transporting goods. But Old French, which inherited deporter, developed a reflexive sense: se deporter came to mean "to conduct oneself," literally "to carry oneself," and alongside it deporter also meant "to divert, to amuse." The noun déportement, formed with the suffix -ment, therefore meant "behaviour, conduct, manner of carrying oneself." English borrowed this noun around the turn of the seventeenth century as deportment, and the word settled precisely there: a person's "deportment" is the way they carry themselves through the world.

The socio-cultural history of the word is bound up with the rise of polite society. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, as the courts and then the middle classes codified good behaviour, deportment named the observable, trainable side of that code — the posture, walk, bow, and bearing that advertised breeding. By the nineteenth century it had become the great Victorian instrument of class discipline: the "deportment class" and the "deportment manual" of the finishing school taught girls and young men to carry themselves according to rank, and conduct books graded "correct deportment" alongside grammar and needlework. This is why the word still smells of the schoolroom: deportment is not a natural gift but an acquired, examinable skill, and to be judged "on deportment" is to be judged on the evidence your own body gives of your upbringing. In the twentieth century the word narrowed and receded from daily speech, displaced in conversation by bearing, demeanor, and posture, while deport and deportation — the other, punitive children of the same Latin verb — moved into law and current affairs; a striking reminder that "deportment" and "deportation" are, etymologically, one word split between the way we carry ourselves and the way the state carries us away.

Two further notes complete the picture. First, the sibling verb comport — from Latin comportāre, "to carry together" — gave English comport and comportment, which now compete with deportment for the same ground, though with a more behavioural and moral emphasis. Second, the same Latin deportāre fed the modern verb deport, "to banish, to transport away from a country," which preserves the classical sense of physical removal that Old French added a behavioural meaning to. English thus holds two descendants of one Latin verb, split along the same fault line that appeared in medieval French: deportment is the way one carries oneself; deportation is the way the state carries one away. In the novel, the maids' "deportment" is the visible, trained carriage that distinguishes the inner apartments — the body itself "carried" according to the household's exacting rules, a small, moving proof that in the Jia mansion even a servant's way of carrying a teacup is a statement of rank. A word born of a root meaning "to carry across" ends by describing the quietest form of carrying there is: the way a trained body carries its own breeding through the world.
