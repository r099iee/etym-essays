Wee is a small word with a surprisingly concrete history. It did not begin as an adjective for smallness at all: it began as a noun for weight and measured quantity. The modern sense "little" is the end of a long semantic journey through weighing, measuring and diminution.

Indo-European and Germanic background. The ultimate root is the reconstructed PIE *weǵʰ-, "to move, transport" (the same root that gives Latin vehere "to carry" and English way, wagon and weigh). In Proto-Germanic the root produced *weganą, "to carry, move, weigh". The original sense involved motion and lifting; from lifting an object to test its heft came the idea of measuring weight.

Old English wǣge. Old English had the noun wǣge (Anglian wēg), "weight, a unit of weight, scales". It belonged to the same family as wegan, "to carry, weigh", and it was a thoroughly practical, commercial word. A wǣge was what you put on a balance; it could also mean a specific quantity of a commodity. There was nothing affectionate or diminutive about it.

Middle English wei. In Middle English the form wei (also wey, wegh) came to mean "a little bit, a small quantity", especially in the phrase a littel wei, "a little amount". The shift from "a measured quantity" to "a small quantity" is easy to imagine: a "weigh" of something was a portion, and a small weigh was a little bit. The noun then began to function adjectivally in northern dialects.

The adjective emerges. By the fifteenth century, northern English and Scottish usage had wee as an adjective meaning "small, little". The earliest evidence is often dated to before 1400 in the northern poem Cursor Mundi, and the word is well established in Scots by the sixteenth century. The old noun sense survives in the phrase a wee, "a short time" (as in bide a wee), and in the fixed expressions a wee bit and a wee while.

Scottish literary career. Robert Burns and the eighteenth-century Scottish revival made wee a badge of national voice. Burns's "wee, sleekit, cow'rin, tim'rous beastie" in "To a Mouse" (1785) is perhaps the most famous literary use; it fixed the word in the English imagination as warm, homely and Scottish. Walter Scott, Robert Louis Stevenson and later Scottish writers used it freely, and it passed into general British literary English as a marker of Celtic or northern speech.

Semantic spread. From "small in size" the word extended to "small in amount" (a wee dram), "short in duration" (a wee while), "young" (a wee child) and "early" (the wee hours). The last phrase is interesting because it is now standard in English worldwide, even for speakers who would never say a wee house. It probably spread through literary and journalistic use in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Register and identity. Wee is a shibboleth of Scottish and Irish English. In England it can sound either cosy or affected, depending on context; in Scotland it is simply normal. In New Zealand it is also common, partly through Scottish immigration. The word's affective force — tender, modest, slightly self-deprecating — makes it useful in advertising, children's books and political rhetoric, where it can soften a claim or create a sense of shared homeliness.

Homographs and caution. The adjective wee is not related to the nursery verb wee meaning "to urinate", which is probably imitative and unrelated, nor to the pronoun we. The homophony is a source of puns and occasional embarrassment. Etymologically, the adjective is a descendant of the weight noun, not of a word for urine or for infancy.

Modern usage. Today wee is best described as colloquial, regional and affectionate. It is common in Scotland, Ireland, Northern England and New Zealand, familiar throughout the English-speaking world through literature and film, and fixed in the international phrase the wee hours. Its history shows how a technical word for a measured weight could shrink, by easy metaphor, into one of the warmest little adjectives in English.
