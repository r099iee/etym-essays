The Indo-European root. The history of reckon leads back to one of the most important roots of the Indo-European language family: the root *h₃reǵ- (traditionally written *reg-), meaning "to straighten, stretch out, direct, rule". This root is the source of Latin rex "king" and regere "to rule", of English "right", "rule", "regal", "regulate", and "correct", and of Sanskrit rājan "king". The original image is physical: a line stretched straight, a ruler drawing a straight edge, and from that the idea of directing, governing, and setting things in order. Counting, on this ancient view, is the same gesture applied to numbers — putting them in a straight line, in order, in their proper places.

The Germanic development. The Germanic branch of the family produced a dense cluster of related verbs: Old English reccan and recenian, Old High German rehhanōn, Gothic rahnjan "to reckon, count", all built on the same "straighten, direct" root. The relevant ancestor of "reckon" is the West Germanic verb family behind Old English (ge)recenian, reconstructed as *rekanōną "to count, enumerate, give an account". The precise morphological path from *h₃reǵ- to *rekanōną involves proposed intermediate verbs meaning "to stretch, direct" and an ablaut-graded noun of "reckoning, account"; the details are debated among historical linguists, but the family relationship is not in doubt.

Old English. In Old English the word is (ge)recenian, and its meaning is broader than modern counting: it means "to recount, narrate, tell, enumerate". Reckoning and storytelling are the same verb, because to give an account of something was literally to "recount" it — to count it out in order. This union of arithmetic and narrative is the oldest and most interesting feature of the word's life: a reckoning was both a bill and a story.

Middle English: the age of money. In Middle English (from about 1300) the verb settles as rekenen (with many spellings: reken, rekne, recon), and the commercial revolution of medieval towns pushes the word hard toward money. Merchants "rekened" their stock, innkeepers presented their "rekeninges" (bills), and to "reckon" became, above all, to settle accounts. The word entered the world of trade and law, and its noun "reckoning" took on the sense of an itemized bill and, by extension, a settlement or judgment. "The day of reckoning", in which a person's deeds are finally counted and judged, grew naturally out of this merchant's metaphor.

Early modern English: opinion and idiom. The 16th and 17th centuries extended the word from arithmetic to opinion. "To reckon" came to mean "to estimate, consider, regard": "I reckon him among my friends", "they reckoned the distance too great". From "estimate" it was a short step to "suppose, think" — "I reckon he will come" — a sense recorded from the 16th century onward and given enormous popular life in the speech of the American South, the English West Country, and colonial and maritime English generally. The age of sail also produced "dead reckoning": navigating by calculated course and distance from a last known position, without observing the stars — a sense in which the word's ancient "counting" meaning is preserved in technical use to this day.

The 19th century and the modern word. In the 19th century, as standard education spread, the plain sense "to count, calculate" came to sound old-fashioned next to "calculate", "compute", and "count", and "reckon" began its retreat into dialect and idiom. The colloquial "I reckon" flourished in dialect writing and in American English, while the idioms "reckon with" and "to be reckoned with" rose to prominence in the 19th and 20th centuries as the word's standard, prestigious form. "To be reckoned with" means to be taken into account as a serious force, and it is the sense at work in the source sentence: Smaug was still to be reckoned with — a dragon could not be left out of any calculation, however the dwarves wished it otherwise.

Sound and form. The word's form has been remarkably stable: Old English recenian → Middle English rekenen → modern reckon, pronounced with a short /e/ and a silent k and o swallowed into the final syllable ("reck-un"). The spelling with ck settled in early modern English, and the inflected form reckoned (past tense and participle) has been regular throughout. Seven hundred years of use have changed the meaning much more than the sound: a word that once told stories and counted coins now mostly tells us who must be taken seriously — and still, in dialect speech, what we think.
