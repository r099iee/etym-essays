The biography of amiable begins not with a learned root but with a baby. Before grammar, before nations, the first syllables any human infant produces — ma, am, na, ba — cluster around the mother, and from them every language on earth builds its nursery words for “mother,” “breast,” and “nurse.” Latin had amma (“wet-nurse, dear one”), Greek had ammá (a childish word for mother), and Sanskrit had ambā; German still keeps Amme, “wet-nurse,” from the same infantile store. These are Lallwörter, reduplicated baby-talk, and they are not words of Proto-Indo-European in the strict philological sense — no ordinary PIE root can be reconstructed behind them. Yet Latin turned one of them into one of the most humane verbs in the language: amāre, “to love.” De Vaan and the great dictionary of Ernout–Meillet agree that no convincing Indo-European etymology exists; the root am- is simply the maternal syllable extended into adult affection — love understood as the warmth a child feels at its mother's breast. That is the deep meaning coiled inside the English word: to love as a loved child loves.

From amāre the Romans built a whole galaxy of affection. Amor gave us amour, amorous, and enamoured; amātor, “one who loves,” travelled through French amateur into English amateur — still literally “one who loves a pursuit for its own sake.” Amīcus, “friend,” is the same word in relational dress, and its negative inimīcus, “not-friend,” hardened through Old French enemi into English enemy: the darkest sister of amity. Two adjectives then diverged and merged their fates. Amābilis meant “worthy of love,” while amicābilis meant “friendly, of a friend.” Old French amiable inherited the amicābilis form but absorbed the warmer sense of amābilis, and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as amyable. For centuries the English word meant both “worthy of love” and “kindly disposed”; the modern sense — pleasantly good-natured, easy to love — crystallised in the early modern period and hardened into the dispositional adjective Austen uses of Bingley.

The word’s later career is a study in specialisation. As amiable settled on the person — the lovable, sunny-tempered soul — its near twin amicable was re-borrowed in the seventeenth century from Late Latin and given a narrower, more impersonal job: the goodwill that exists between parties in a settlement or a dispute. Enemy kept its thousand-year-old shape from inimīcus, and amity stayed a literary word for friendship among nations. When Jane Austen writes “such amiable qualities must speak for themselves,” she is deploying the full inheritance of the nursery root: Bingley is not merely pleasant — he is, in the oldest sense, easy to love, the human equivalent of a child turning naturally toward its mother. The warmth of amiable in her sentence is thus not decoration but the word’s whole two-thousand-year history condensed into a single adjective.
