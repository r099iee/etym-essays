hydra in the source passage — Our good and sublime emperor has the greatest part in the world to play, and he is so virtuous and noble that God will not desert him, and he will fulfil his mission—to strangle the hydra of revolution, which is more horrible than ever now in the person of this murderer and miscreant.… Whom can we reckon on, I ask you?… England with her commercial spirit will not comprehend and cannot comprehend all the loftiness of soul of the Emperor Alexander (Leo Tolstoy, War and Peace, Book 1, Chapter 1, in the royalist drawing room of Anna Pavlovna Scherer) — is employed as a powerful classical metaphor for the French Revolution and its Napoleonic embodiment. In Greek myth, the Lernaean Hydra was a terrifying multi-headed water serpent that sprouted two new heads whenever one was cut off. In monarchist political polemics of the early 19th century, the revolution was viewed precisely as a monstrous, multi-headed, self-regenerating beast of regicide, jacobinism, and military conquest that could not be killed by ordinary military victories, but required heroic, providential annihilation.

In standard literary, mythological, political, biological, and astronomical English, hydra refers primarily to the legendary multi-headed serpent of Greek myth, and by extension to any multifaceted, deeply entrenched, and self-regenerating evil, threat, problem, conspiracy, or systemic corruption that is exceptionally difficult to eradicate because attempting to solve or eliminate one part immediately creates multiple new complications in its place. In the natural sciences, it designates both a genus of freshwater polyps renowned for their biological immortality and the largest constellation in the southern celestial hemisphere.

  • Mythological sense (the Lernaean beast of Greek lore): The venomous, serpent-like monster inhabiting the marshes of Lerna in Argolis, possessing numerous heads (often nine, fifty, or one hundred in classical accounts), of which the central head was immortal. Slain by Heracles as his Second Labour with the assistance of Iolaus, who cauterized the severed necks with firebrands. For example: Heracles faced the deadly venom and regenerating heads of the Lernaean hydra; ancient vases depicted the hero battling the coiled hydra.
  • Political, societal, and metaphorical sense (source sense): A persistent, self-perpetuating danger, bureaucracy, or illicit network where suppressing one faction or symptom causes others to multiply. For example: the hydra of international narcotics trafficking; trying to reform the sprawling ministry was like fighting a hydra; the hydra of political extremism reared its head once more. It frequently appears in the compound adjective hydra-headed (e.g. a hydra-headed crisis).
  • Biological and zoological sense (genus Hydra): A genus of small, freshwater cnidarians (polyps) discovered by Antoni van Leeuwenhoek (1702), experimentally investigated by Abraham Trembley (1740), and formally named by Carl Linnaeus in 1758. These organisms possess remarkable regenerative capabilities: an entire individual can regenerate from a tiny fragment of tissue, rendering them biologically non-senescent (immortal) under ideal conditions. For example: students observed the tentacles of a budding green hydra under the microscope; researchers study Hydra vulgaris for clues to stem cell immortality.
  • Astronomical sense (constellation Hydra): The largest of the 88 modern constellations, spanning over 1,303 square degrees across the celestial equator and southern sky, traditionally depicted as a long, winding water snake. For example: the constellation Hydra stretches below Cancer, Leo, and Virgo in the spring night sky; the spiral galaxy M83 lies within the borders of Hydra.
  • Collocations and stylistic register: Commonly paired with verbs of slaying, fighting, and proliferation: strangle the hydra, battle the hydra, sever the heads of the hydra, spawn a new hydra. Its register is educated, rhetorical, and classical, carrying rich literary weight from antiquity through Renaissance political theory to modern journalism.

The timeless symbolic power of hydra lies in the terrifying paradox of self-regeneration: a threat that feeds on the very attacks meant to destroy it, demanding not mere resistance, but complete, radical cauterization at the root.
