- 1650s–1700s: Physical and material non-adhesion - Used in natural philosophy and chemistry to describe matter, granular substances, or particles that do not physically stick together or form a solid mass.
- 1700s–1780s: Extension to rhetoric, logic, and philosophy - Broadened during the Enlightenment to denote arguments, treatises, or discourses marred by internal contradictions, disjointed logic, or non sequiturs.
- 1790s–1900s: Medical, psychiatric, and emotional delirium - Heavily specialized in clinical psychology and literature to characterize the broken, raving speech of fever, madness, intense trauma, or delirium.
- 20th Century: Formalization in physics and wave mechanics - Codified in optics, laser physics, and quantum theory as a precise mathematical descriptor for electromagnetic waves with randomized, uncorrelated phases.
- 21st Century: Political and cultural discourse - Widely applied to describe fractured public policies, fragmented geopolitical alliances, or chaotic media ecosystems incapable of sustaining cohesive narratives.
