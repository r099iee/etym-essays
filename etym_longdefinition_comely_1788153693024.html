comely (adjective) — Pleasing and attractive in appearance, person, or demeanour; physically handsome or pretty, and by extension seemly, decorous, and fitting in behaviour, speech, and conduct. In the passage on this card, Walter Pater uses the word of a "liberal and comely way of conceiving life": a manner of envisioning experience that is beautiful and seemly at once — attractive to the senses and morally appropriate together — exactly the Renaissance conviction that aesthetic grace and ethical fitness belong to one another.

Primary sense — physical attractiveness. Since its Old English origins comely has referred first of all to pleasing appearance: a comely face, a comely figure, a comely person is one who is well-proportioned, agreeable to look upon, and possessed of a wholesome, orderly beauty. Unlike words that stress glamour or splendour, comely emphasises a modest, balanced, healthful kind of attractiveness — beauty without excess, charm without ostentation. Older literature applies it freely to young women, but from the beginning it was also used of men, of dress, of buildings, and of scenes in nature.

Secondary sense — seemliness, decency, propriety. A natural bridge runs from "pleasing to the eye" to "pleasing to the moral or social sense": what is comely is what is fitting, proper, and decorous. A comely action, a comely answer, a comely silence: the word came to mean "becoming, seemly, appropriate to the occasion, in good taste". This is the sense at work in Pater's phrase — a comely way of conceiving life is dignified, harmonious, and becoming to a human being, as opposed to a base, squalid, or belittling conception of existence. The aesthetic and the moral senses overlapped for centuries, and in many contexts they cannot be cleanly separated: comely behaviour is perceived as attractive precisely because it is fitting.

  • Of objects and arrangements: comely may describe pleasing proportions rather than human beauty — a comely dwelling, a comely garden, a comely multitude — with the sense "well-proportioned, shapely, agreeable to the eye".
  • Of speech and style: in rhetorical use a comely style is elegant, well-ordered, and suited to its matter; the word aligns with classical ideals of decorum.
  • Obsolete and archaic senses: early English could use comely of persons meaning "kindly, gentle, gracious" in character; dictionaries mark this sense as obsolete or rare, and it survives mainly in older religious writing.
  • Modern register: in present-day English comely is uncommon in everyday speech; it carries a distinctly literary, slightly archaic or poetic flavour, and typically signals warmth and wholesomeness rather than passion or exotic beauty. It persists in formal phrases such as "comely and decent behaviour".

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The whole family of senses hangs together by a single logic: comely names beauty of a kind that is also right. The aesthetic sense ("handsome") is primary; the ethical sense ("seemly") arises because what is fitting is felt to be beautiful in conduct, and what is beautiful in form is felt to be fitting. The word never acquired the pejorative or ironic energy that attaches, for example, to pretty (which can trivialise) or good-looking (which is neutral and bare); comely keeps an old-fashioned conviction that visible beauty and moral grace belong together — precisely the Renaissance ideal that Pater repeatedly voices: the good is comely, and the comely is good.
