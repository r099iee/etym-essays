Onomatopoeic Germanic formation imitating a hollow shout or cry (no recoverable PIE etymon; the word is imitative / non-PIE in origin) → attested continental cognates: Middle Dutch huten "to hoot" and Middle High German hūzen / hūchen "to cry out, shout" (medieval attestations); Old Norse hóta "to threaten" is sometimes compared, though this link is debated → Middle English houten "to shout, call out," first attested c. 1200 → Modern English hoot (verb), continuous in the shout sense; from the mid-15th century applied to bird cries, above all the owl’s deep, wavering call; from c. 1600 the sense "to shout in derision or disapproval" (to hoot at someone); noun senses follow — "the sound itself" (16th c.), "a hoot" = something funny (1926), "not worth a hoot" / "don’t give a hoot" (early 19th c.) → HOOTING, the present participle, formed by adding the participial suffix -ing (from Old English -ende, the present-participle ending, which in Middle English converged with the gerund ending -ing(e)); the participial form is attested from the 15th–16th century. NOTE ON THE DEBATE: Old Norse hóta "to threaten" is often cited as a cognate, but some scholars separate it as an independent formation, so the external Germanic family of "hoot" is not fully settled; the English word itself is securely imitative.
