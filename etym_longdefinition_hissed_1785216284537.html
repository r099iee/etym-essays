Hissed is the past tense and past participle of the verb hiss: to force a stream of breath through nearly closed teeth or a narrow aperture so that it produces a sharp, prolonged, unvoiced sibilant sound — the sound written s prolonged and intensified, the natural hissing of a serpent, of escaping steam, or of an angry crowd. In The Hobbit the word opens the sentence of Gollum's mounting fury: "He hissed to himself, and still he did not answer; he whispered and spluttered." The order of the three verbs is a descending ladder of composure: the hiss is the loudest and most animal — a fierce, breathy expulsion through shut teeth; the whisper that follows is quieter but still deliberate; the splutter is broken, choked, chaotic. To read "hissed" here is to hear the last civil surface of Gollum's voice tearing: a creature of the dark whose speech slips into a serpent's hiss under strain. The word's power is that it is simultaneously animal, mechanical, and human — a sound that does not need language to carry menace.

Core meaning and its near relatives. The central sense is to make the sharp sibilant sound. It is usefully separated from its neighbours: a whisper is breathy but articulate quiet speech, still forming words; a mutter is low, closed-mouth, barely intelligible mumbling; to sibilate (rare and technical) is simply to articulate an s-sound; sizzle and fizz are the hiss of heat and of gas bubbles; a rasp is a dry, rough grating. The hiss alone is pure unvoiced friction shaped only by tongue and teeth, and for that reason it is the one speech-noise that animals and machines share with people.

Exhaustive polysemy.

  • Of serpents and animals (oldest sense, 14th c.). Serpents hiss; also geese, cats in warning, and angry or cornered beasts. The snake's hiss is the archetype — a warning made of breath, without a larynx's voice. This sense is the root of all others, because the word is onomatopoeic, invented to imitate the sound.
  • Of steam, gas, and pressure (17th–19th c.). Escaping steam, compressed air, a boiling kettle, a radiator, a pressure valve all hiss as they vent. The mechanical sense grows with the industrial age, so that a locomotive, a steam pipe, or a deflating tyre "hisses" exactly as a serpent does. The bridge is the identical acoustics of friction and pressure.
  • Of a crowd — derision and catcalls (Early Modern theatre). Audiences hiss to express contempt: actors, orators, and opponents are "hissed off the stage". This is a socialized hiss — a crowd turning a sound of warning into an instrument of public shame. Related transitive uses: "hissed his displeasure", "hissed the performance", "hissed the speaker down".
  • Of a person speaking (intransitive and transitive). A person "hisses" when speaking in a fierce, breathy undertone of anger, secrecy, or urgency — "hissed a warning", "hissed her name", "hissed with impatience". The bridge from the snake is the breathy sibilance of suppressed emotion: fury squeezed through the teeth.
  • Of fire, fat, and friction (domestic, 18th c.). A hot iron hisses on damp cloth, fat in a pan hisses and spits, a damp match hisses and goes out. The sound of sudden evaporation and of contact heat.
  • Of radio, audio, and electronics (technical jargon, 20th c.). "Hiss" is the technical word for the steady high-frequency background noise of an amplifier, tape, or radio — the hiss on an old recording. Sound engineers distinguish hiss (broadband noise) from hum (mains hum) and crackle (impulses). The bridge is again pure acoustics: electrical hiss is literally hissing friction in the signal.
  • In phonetics. The hissing sounds are the sibilants — /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /ʒ/ — named precisely because they hiss. "Sibilant" derives from Latin sībilāre "to hiss", and a linguist may describe a lisp as a defective sibilant hiss.
  • Figurative and metaphoric shifts. "Hissed" extends metaphorically to any sound of angry or derisive disapproval that resembles the noise — the hiss of a metaphorically deflating argument, a "hissing" critique; and to express contempt generally: "the play was hissed". It can also describe the malicious whispering of rumour and scandal, which "hisses" through a room or a town (compare "whispering campaign").
  • As a noun. The same word is a noun — "a hiss" — covering the same senses: the hiss of the serpent, of steam, of the crowd, of the radio; and figuratively, the contemptuous rejection itself.

Historical semantic bridge in summary. Every modern sense is an echo of the first: the word began (14th-century Middle English hissen, an onomatopoeic creation) as the sound of the serpent and of any forced-breath friction. Each later domain — steam, theatre, radio, phonetics — simply borrowed the acoustically identical noise and gave it a name already waiting. The emotional core (warning, menace, contempt) has never been lost: to be hissed is still to be rejected, and to hiss is still to warn.
