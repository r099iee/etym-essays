- 13th–14th centuries: Norse loan "thrusten" arrives as a plain, vigorous verb of physical pushing — the word of labourers, sailors and fighters, rougher in tone than the French "push" that arrived beside it.
- 14th–16th centuries: sword and fencing vocabulary — "thrust" versus "cut" in the duelling schools and manuals (George Silver's Paradoxes of Defence, 1599); "a thrust" is the lunge home with the point.
- 16th–17th centuries: figurative extension — to thrust oneself forward (intrude), to thrust something upon someone (impose); the Authorised Version uses the plain physical verb with memorable force.
- 19th–20th centuries: military "thrust" becomes the concentrated offensive push of modern warfare (constant in First and Second World War reporting); engineering adopts the word for the axial force of engines, propellers and bearings; geology takes it for thrust faults.
- 20th–21st centuries: "the thrust of the argument" is now a standard analytical phrase; rocket thrust and thrust-to-weight ratio are household jargon of spaceflight. THRUST today moves from the fence to the report to the launch pad — one invariant verb spanning them all.
