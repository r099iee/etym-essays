The English name Macedonia descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *meh₂k- ‘long, tall, pointed’ through an unbroken Greek chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European *meh₂k- ‘long, tall, pointed’ (an adjectival root of height and length; also reflected in Latin macer ‘thin, lean’ and Greek makrós ‘long’). 2. Ancient Greek μακεδνός (makednós) ‘tall, slender, tapering’, attested as an adjective of height and length (Homeric usage of high, slender things) — the o-grade formation on the same root. 3. Ancient Greek Μακεδόνες (Makedónes) ‘the Macedonians’, the ethnonym understood as ‘the tall ones’ or ‘the highlanders’ (attested from the 5th century BC, Herodotus and Thucydides); singular Μακεδών (Makedōn). 4. Ancient Greek Μακεδονία (Makedonía) ‘the land of the Macedonians’, the territorial noun in -ια (-ia), attested by the 4th century BC (the country of Philip II and Alexander). 5. Latin Macedonia ‘Macedonia’, the Roman name for the region and (after 146 BC) the Roman province, taken directly from Greek. 6. English Macedonia, attested from the 14th–15th centuries via Latin (with Old French mediation in some spellings) — the modern proper noun for the region, the ancient kingdom, and the modern contested territory. 7. Macedonia — the modern English toponym. Debated points: the connection of the ethnonym to makednós ‘tall’ (the Macedonians as ‘the tall ones / highlanders’) is traditional and widely accepted but not universally so; a rival theory derives the name from a pre-Greek or Illyrian substrate, in which case the PIE chain above would be a folk-etymological overlay rather than the true origin. The root *meh₂k- itself is secure through its Greek (makrós, mēkos ‘length’) and Latin (macer ‘thin, lean’) reflexes; Sanskrit mahánt ‘great’ belongs to a different root, *meǵh₂- ‘great’, and is not a cognate here.
