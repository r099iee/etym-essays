• Mixed: Similar because both mean elements joined into one mass; differs because mixed is the plain, unmarked everyday word with no stylistic elevation, while mingled is literary and suggests a gentler, more intimate interpenetration.
  • Blended: Similar because both describe combination into one, especially of liquids or colours; differs because blended implies a smooth, homogeneous result, whereas mingled allows the parts to remain co-present and visible.
  • Combined: Similar because both mean united or joined; differs because combined is neutral and factual and can mean mere juxtaposition without true interpenetration.
  • Intermixed: Similar because both describe mutual interpenetration; differs because intermixed stresses the reciprocal distribution of parts among one another, while mingled stresses the unity of the resulting mass.
  • Intermingled: Similar because it shares the same root and core meaning; differs only in formality — intermingled is more formal and stresses the mutual reciprocity of the mixing.
  • Commingled: Similar because both mean mixed together; differs because commingled is a formal, even legal term implying merger into a common stock (as of funds or goods), while mingled is the warm, general word.
  • Amalgamated: Similar because both describe union into one body; differs because amalgamated implies complete merger or alloying (metals, corporations) in which elements lose their identity — far more drastic than mingled.
  • Fused: Similar because both describe joining into one; differs because fused implies union by heat or intense pressure with total loss of the parts' separate identity; mingled is far milder.
  • Merged: Similar because both describe things becoming one; differs because merged implies one entity absorbed into another (an asymmetric union), while mingling is mutual between equals.
  • Coalesced: Similar because both describe coming together into one; differs because coalesced is scientific and formal, describing gradual growing-together of particles, droplets or opinions rather than a simple stirring together.
  • Woven together: Similar for textures and narratives (threads or storylines mingling); differs because it presupposes strands or threads and an interlacing pattern, which mingled does not require.
  • Socialised with / mixed with (people): Similar for the social sense of mingled (as in "mingled with the guests"); differs in register — these are neutral mundane words, while mingled is polished and faintly old-fashioned.
  • Fraternised (with): Similar because both describe social association; differs because fraternised implies deliberate familiarity between particular people (often taboo contact in wartime), whereas mingling is anonymous circulation within a crowd.
  • Hobnobbed: Similar because both describe social mixing; differs in register — hobnobbed is informal and implies cultivating the great or the powerful, without the neutral, democratic colour of mingled.
