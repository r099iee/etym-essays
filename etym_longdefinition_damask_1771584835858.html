Damask is a rich textile — originally silk, later also linen and cotton — in which the pattern is formed not by printing or embroidery but by the structure of the weave itself, so that a single colour of fabric carries a design in contrasting light and shadow, glossy against matt. The word is also an adjective meaning "made of or patterned like such a fabric." In the literary context of this card the word appears in a passage of careful costume description: the gentleman wears "a jacket of slate-blue Japanese silk __________ with a raised pattern of eight large medallions on the front and with tasselled borders." Every element of the phrase — the rare fabric, the distinctive colour, the medallions, the tassels — is chosen to mark the wearer as a person of wealth and taste, and damask is the keystone of that impression. The word carries within it the name of a great city, and its tone is therefore exotic, luxurious, and faintly oriental: damask is not simply a patterned cloth but the very idea of woven richness, the fabric a narrator reaches for when he wishes to signal opulence without further explanation. Because the pattern here is described as "raised" — a textured, three-dimensional effect achieved in the weave — the word also suggests craftsmanship of a high order, a garment that is not printed with ornament but woven out of it, as though the luxury were part of the material's very nature rather than applied to its surface.

Damask is a thoroughly polysemous word, and its senses radiate outward from the city of Damascus:

  • The fabric (primary sense) — a heavy, figured silk, linen, or cotton cloth with a pattern woven into it, reversible, and produced by contrasting weave structures in a single colour. Used for table linen, drapery, upholstery, and dress.
  • The adjective — "damask silk," "damask tablecloth": made of or resembling this fabric; by extension, figuratively "damask" can describe anything whose pattern is subtle, woven-in, and luxurious.
  • Damask steel / Damascus steel — a famous type of high-carbon steel with a visible watered pattern, used for blades; so called because the medieval European blade-smiths associated the patterned steel with Damascus.
  • Damask rose — a rose variety (Rosa damascena) with an intense fragrance, used in perfumery, named after Damascus.
  • Damson — the small dark plum is etymologically a "plum of Damascus" (Latin damascenum), a second, disguised descendant of the same city-name.
  • Damascene / damaskeen — the verb and adjective for decorating metal, especially steel, with inlaid gold or silver, or with watered patterns; the craft is damascening, associated with the metalwork of Damascus.
  • In textiles terminology — modern technical usage distinguishes the "damask weave" (figured, reversible) from "brocade" (with added surface threads), "tapestry," and "jacquard," which refers to the loom mechanism that can produce damask patterns.

The semantic history is a chain of metonymies — the name of a city transferred to the goods it traded. Damascus was for millennia a crossroads of the silk and spice routes between the East and the Mediterranean, and European merchants labelled the finest eastern products by their city of origin. The word damask itself came into English in the 16th century from French damas, from Medieval Latin damascus, "of Damascus." Once the fabric had taken the city's name, the same name was pressed into service for the rose, the plum, the steel, and the metalworking technique — each a separate loan-word or semantic extension from the same toponym. This is why the word carries such an aura of the East even today: it is literally the name of an ancient city worn as a textile.

In modern usage the word keeps three distinct lives. In the furnishing trades, damask is a precise technical term for a reversible figured fabric, typically of linen or cotton for tablecloths and drapery. In literary and historical writing, it retains its old glamour as the rich silk of princes. And in the compounds damask rose and Damascus steel (often spelled out in full), it survives as a fragment of a medieval trade geography — a reminder that a word can be a map, carrying within it the memory of the city where the silk was sold.
