tenour: the general course, drift, or purport of something — the sustained character, sense, or direction of a written work, a life, or a statement; also, a steady, continuous course of conduct, action, or being. The word is simply the British -our spelling of tenor, and in Ruskin's Preface the two spellings are interchangeable, the -our form being the older and more literary one. He writes: "Whatever may seem partial in my task is dependent not so much on the tenour of the work, as on its incompleteness." He means that any appearance of bias in Modern Painters flows not from the settled character or intention of the book — its tenour — but from the fact that it is unfinished. The nuance is apologetic and self-deprecating in the scrupulous Ruskin manner: he concedes a visible defect (partiality) and reassigns its cause from design to accident.

Primary senses

  • Of a document or discourse — the legal/purport sense: the exact wording, the drift, the substance, or the general purport of a writing or statement. In law and formal usage "the tenor of the document" means what the instrument actually says, considered as a whole, often with an emphasis on exact verbal content: the tenour of a will, of a deed, of a proclamation. This is the sense that governs Ruskin's sentence.
  • Of conduct or life — the steady course sense: a continuous, unbroken course or way of proceeding; "the even tenour of his life" is a familiar phrase meaning a life that runs smoothly and consistently, without violent changes of direction. Here the word carries the feel of a thread running through a fabric — a line of consistent direction against which individual episodes may be measured.
  • Of the general drift of argument or feeling: the prevailing tendency, tone, or import of a series of remarks or of a period of time; "the tenour of the conversation," "the tenour of public opinion." This sense shades into tone and tendency.
  • Musical sense (tenor, not tenour): the highest of the ordinary adult male voices, and in medieval music the voice that holds the main plainchant melody. Etymologically the same word — the voice that "holds on" — but in English the musical term is almost always spelled tenor; the -our spelling is confined to the non-musical senses.

The semantic bridge

All the senses grow from one Latin root: tenor is a noun formed from tenēre, "to hold." The core image is of something held steady — a continuous, unbroken stretch. From "a holding on" the word moves naturally to "the way something continues," and hence to the sustained direction or drift of a discourse, a life, or a period. The musical sense is the same image transferred to sound: the tenor voice is the one that holds the melody in a steady line while other voices weave around it. Whether in law, in ethics, or in music, tenour always names the holding quality — the steady inner bearing that persists beneath surface variation.

Near-synonyms and boundaries

  • Tenor: identical in meaning; differs only in spelling — tenor is the standard modern form, tenour the older, more literary British variant, which Ruskin and nineteenth-century prose deliberately retain.
  • Gist: similar in being what a text means at bottom; differs because gist is the colloquial word for the essential core one extracts after reading — a summary — whereas tenour is a quality of the whole, the sustained direction itself rather than the takeaway.
  • Drift: similar in general direction of meaning; differs because drift suggests a slow, possibly accidental or even aimless movement, whereas tenour implies a consistent, intended course against which individual passages are measured.
  • Purport: similar in apparent meaning or import; differs because purport concerns the outward sense a reader takes from the words, what they convey or seem to intend, whereas tenour is the settled inner character that governs the whole.
  • Tone: similar in being an underlying quality of a work or remark; differs because tone is the emotional or stylistic colouring (solemn, ironic, light), while tenour is the direction and substance — tone is how it feels, tenour is where it is going.
  • Thrust: similar in being the main line of an argument; differs because thrust is the point pushed forward with energy and insistence, whereas tenour is the steady, even character of the whole, without the aggressive connotation.
  • Trend: similar in naming a prevailing direction; differs because trend is statistical and temporal — the direction of change over time — while tenour is the settled character of a thing, work, or course at a given moment.
  • Course: similar in being a continuous progression; differs because course is the path or track along which a thing moves, whereas tenour is the manner and direction of that movement — its consistent bearing.

The tone of the word in Ruskin's Preface is formal, legalistic, and gently defensive — the language of a writer explaining that the plan is sounder than the incomplete execution. To speak of "the tenour of the work" is to invoke the book's steady inner bearing, the continuity of its purpose, as distinct from any single passage or from the accident of incompletion.
