PIE base *grem- / *krem- "to break off, cut into bits" (PROPOSED, unconfirmed — the Germanic root's ultimate origin is obscure; Beekes compares Greek grumea 'bag/chest of old clothes' (small things of little value) and Albanian grime 'crumb, tiny piece') → Proto-Germanic *krumōn "small broken-off piece" (c. 5th c. BCE–1st c. CE) → Old English cruma "crumb, fragment, morsel" (c. 8th–11th c.) → Old English gecrymman "to break into crumbs" (verb) → Middle English kremelen (late 15th c.) "to break into small fragments" (frequentative in -el) → Modern English crumble (16th c., with intrusive -b-; intransitive 'to fall into pieces' from 1570) → present participle crumbling. Note on debate: the PIE stage *grem-/*krem- is a proposal, not an established root; the word is securely attested only from Proto-Germanic *krumōn onward, and its connection to the wider Indo-European family remains speculative (Beekes) rather than proven. The -b- in crumb/crumble is unetymological, added in the mid-15th century by analogy with words like dumb and thumb.
