The etymological evolution of the verb abstain showcases how a concrete Proto-Indo-European physical root signifying tension and stretching evolved through classical Latin morphology into a sophisticated term for moral fortitude, parliamentary procedure, and scientific skepticism.

Indo-European Roots and Proto-Italic Heritage:

  • The Base Root *ten-: At the deepest reconstructed layer, abstain derives from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ten-, signifying "to stretch, extend, or pull tight." This exceptionally productive root generated cognates across the Indo-European languages, emphasizing tension, extension, and duration:
    • Sanskrit tanóti (तनोति), meaning "he stretches, extends, lasts."
    • Ancient Greek teinō (τείνω, "I stretch"), yielding tonos ("tension, tone"), tetanos, and hypotenuse.
    • Proto-Germanic *þunjaz ("thin", stretched out), giving English thin, and German dünn.
    • Latin tendere ("to stretch, aim toward") and tenēre ("to hold, keep hold of").
  • The Italic Shift from Stretching to Holding: In Proto-Italic, the stative verb *tenē- developed the specialized meaning of maintaining tension—hence "to hold, grasp, or keep in one's possession." In Classical Latin, tenēre became the primary verb for physical grasping and conceptual comprehension (yielding English tenable, tenant, tenacious, and contain).

Latin Composition and Phonetic Apophony:

  • Prefixation with ab-: During the early Roman Republic, Latin formed the compound verb abstinēre by prefixing the prepositional particle ab / abs ("from, away from, off") to tenēre. The morphological union triggered regular Latin vowel apophony (vowel weakening): when a short, unaccented root vowel [e] occurred in a medial closed syllable following a prefix, it regularly shifted to a short high front vowel [i] (thus abs- + tenēre $ightarrow$ abstinēre; compare re- + teneō $ightarrow$ retineō, con- + teneō $ightarrow$ contineō).
  • Classical Usages in Roman Society: In Plautus, Terence, Cicero, and Livy, abstinēre carried both transitive and reflexive-intransitive syntax. Transitively, one could abstinere manus ab alieno ("to keep one's hands off another's property"). Reflexively, a person could se abstinere ("to hold oneself back") from wine, anger, bribery, or war. Cicero famously praised magistrates who were able abstinere a vitiis ("to refrain from vices"), establishing the term as a hallmark of Stoic self-regulation and republican civic virtue.

Gallo-Romance and Anglo-Norman Transmission:

  • Phonetic Evolution in Old French: With the transition into Vulgar Latin and Old French (c. 11th–12th centuries), the Latin consonant cluster -bs- was assimilated and simplified into an aspirated sibilant, yielding astenir (alongside learned Latinate spellings like abstenir). The reflexive verb s'astenir was conjugated with an epenthetic diphthong in tonic stems (e.g., je m'astiein).
  • Anglo-Norman Channel Crossing: Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman legal and ecclesiastical scribes imported abstener into England. In medieval monastic houses and church courts, the term was heavily reinforced by Vulgate biblical texts, notably 1 Peter 2:11 ("abstinete vos a carnalibus desideriis"—"abstain from fleshly lusts").

Middle English Adoption and Sound Shifts:

  • Fourteenth-Century Integration: The word was naturalized into Middle English around 1380 as absteinen or asteynen, adopting the characteristic Middle English diphthong -ei- / -ey- from Old French tonic forms. John Wycliffe's 1382 Bible translation and Geoffrey Chaucer's The Parson's Tale (c. 1390) cemented the word in English vernacular moral theology. In Chaucer's treatise on the Seven Deadly Sins, the Parson proclaims that the remedy against gluttony and lechery is "abstinence" and to "absteine him from delicat metes and drynkes."
  • Great Vowel Shift: During the 15th and 16th centuries, Middle English long [æːi] underwent the Great Vowel Shift, fronting and raising to the modern diphthong /eɪ/ in Modern English abstain, while the Latinate spelling with -b- was firmly restored by Renaissance humanist printers seeking classical etymological fidelity.

Modern Institutional and Intellectual Broadening:

  • The Political and Parliamentary Sphere: In the 18th and 19th centuries, as representative constitutional governance flourished in Westminster and Washington, abstain acquired its technical legislative application. Members who were present in the chamber but refused to vote for or against a motion were formally recorded as choosing to abstain, giving birth to the modern parliamentary noun abstention.
  • The Victorian Temperance Movement: Throughout the 19th century in Britain and North America, abstain became the rallying cry of the temperance leagues. To "take the pledge" meant to abstain totally from intoxicating spirits, leading to the coining of teetotal abstainer.
  • Scientific and Philosophical Discourse: Concurrently, economists and philosophers such as John Stuart Mill, Carl Menger, and William James adopted the verb for scientific methodology. In scholarly critique, to abstain from examining an ancestor's doctrine was cast not as courteous restraint, but as dogmatic cowardice. Thus, the verb completed its circle: from monastic withdrawal from food, to parliamentary neutrality, to fearless intellectual confrontation.
