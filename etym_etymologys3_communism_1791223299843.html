Communism enters English in the 1840s as a borrowing of French communisme, itself coined around 1840 by followers of Gracchus Babeuf and Étienne Cabet to name the doctrine of abolishing private property in favour of common ownership. French communisme joins commun (common) from Latin communis plus -isme from Latin -ismus from Greek -ismos denoting doctrine or practice. Latin communis itself joins com- "together, with" + munis "bound to service, obliged," from munus "duty, gift, public office." Munus traces to Proto-Italic *moinos and ultimately PIE *mey- / *mei- "to change, exchange," the idea of reciprocal exchange of duties. Thus etymologically communism literally means "the doctrine of holding in common through mutual obligation."

Before 1840 the adjective communist / communiste already existed: in 1780s revolutionary France, and in English as early as 1841 in Robert Owenite and Chartist press. The Manifesto of the Communist Party (Manifest der Kommunistischen Partei, 1848) by Marx and Engels canonized both Kommunismus / communism and gave it a scientific-socialist program: class struggle, dictatorship of the proletariat, withering of the state. Early English attestations: John Goodwyn Barmby's Communist Chronicle (1841), the New York Tribune debates of the 1840s. Initially it overlapped with socialism, communalism, Owenism.

  • Utopian phase (1840-1871): Cabet's Icaria, Owenite communes, German Handwerker communism. Small-scale intentional communities; word evokes idealistic sharing, still largely interchangeable with socialism.
  • Marxist codification (1848-1917): Marx distinguishes communism (stateless classless end-goal) from socialism (transitional stage). Sound shift is nil; semantic narrowing begins. Capital C Communism starts to denote party affiliation.
  • Bolshevik institutionalization (1917-1991): After October Revolution, Communist Party (Bolsheviks), Third International, USSR, People's Republic of China (1949). Meaning expands to actually existing state system: one-party rule, central planning, collectivization. In the West during Cold War, communism becomes a fear-word synonymous with Soviet threat, red scare, McCarthyism.
  • Post-Cold War fragmentation (1991-present): Collapse of USSR shrinks referent to China, Vietnam, Laos, Cuba, North Korea, each market-reformed to varying degrees. In academia communism retains Marxian theoretical sense; in popular U.S. discourse it often blurs loosely to mean any state intervention, while anthropologists also use "primitive communism" or "communism in food" to mean pre-state sharing economies with no private accumulation.

Phonetically communism shows the regular French-to-English adaptation: French /kɔmynism/ → English /ˈkɒmjʊnɪzəm/ with stress retraction to first syllable, yod-coalescence and voicing of s to z in -ism. Orthographically the double-m preserves Latin communis. The suffix -ism productive in English generates anti-communism, anarcho-communism, Eurocommunism, neo-communism.

The specific phrase "communism in food" in early anthropology (Lewis Henry Morgan, Engels' Origin of the Family, Kropotkin's Mutual Aid) uses communism in its oldest literal sense of communal sharing before Marxist state theory — food held in common, distributed by need, no hoarding. This usage persists in hunter-gatherer studies: "food communism" or "demand sharing." It is crucial not to anachronistically read Cold War state-communism into such passages; they mean primordial communalism. Debate: scholars dispute whether Marx himself distinguished socialism vs communism consistently, and whether PIE *mey- vs *mew- is the correct root for munus (de Vaan prefers *mey- with -n- suffix; some prefer *mew- "to move"). The French coinage priority (Cabet vs Dezamy vs Reybaud's 1840 report) is also debated, but 1840 as birth year is consensus.
