In the Gibbonian and eighteenth-century literary setting, sycophants are not merely polite admirers but the professional flatterers and courtly dependents who thrived in a culture of patronage. The passage stresses that Gibbon refused the dedication-and-flattery economy of his age: he would not join the crowd of writers who bought favour with exaggerated praise. Calling them sycophants is a moral judgment as much as a social description. The word brands a whole class of literary and social climbers as parasitically obsequious — people whose praise serves advantage rather than truth — and thereby elevates Gibbon's independence as a rare integrity in a corrupt marketplace of compliments.

Primary modern sense. A sycophant is a person who acts obsequiously toward someone important in order to gain advantage. The core semantic ingredients are: (1) a powerful target; (2) flattery or servile deference as the instrument; (3) a self-interested motive (promotion, favour, money, safety). The word is always pejorative; there is no neutral or complimentary use in standard English.

Historical and extended senses.

  • The ancient sense (historical only): in classical Athens a sykophántēs was a professional or habitual informer — one who brought accusations (traditionally against exporters of figs) in the courts, frequently for blackmail or personal gain; a type proverbial for malicious litigiousness. This sense survives only in historical discussions of Greek law and in etymological notes.
  • Court and patronage flattery (the dominant historical sense): from the Renaissance onward, sycophant names the courtier who fawns on a prince, patron, or great man for advancement — the figure Gibbon refused to become.
  • Modern institutional and corporate flattery: the yes-man or brown-noser in offices, boardrooms, and media; flattery as a career strategy. The word remains formal and literary in tone but is applied freely to modern settings.
  • Political and journalistic usage: "a court of sycophants" around a leader; sycophantic media or applause. The noun builds the adjective sycophantic and the abstract noun sycophancy.
  • Literary-critical and moral usage: sycophancy as a vice of corrupt cultures of patronage — a term of high moral reproach in biography, satire, and history.

Semantic bridge. The word travelled an astonishing reversal: Greek sykophántēs, literally "fig-shower," first meant an informer or denouncer; Latin sycophanta added "trickster, parasite"; French sycophante and English sycophant (16th century) still meant "slanderer, false accuser"; only later did English specialize the sense to the servile flatterer. The bridge is shared self-interest: both the ancient informer and the modern toady use words as weapons of advantage — the one to accuse, the other to praise, both to gain.

Boundaries and register. A flatterer praises without necessarily living off a patron; a sycophant's flattery is instrumental and systemic. A toady or yes-man is the colloquial peer — same subservience, less classical sting. An obsequious person is marked by deferential manner; sycophancy names the role and the motive. A parasite lives off a host's resources; the sycophant's currency is verbal service. Typical collocations: surrounded by sycophants; a court of sycophants; sycophantic praise / applause; refuse to play the sycophant. Register is formal to literary; the tone is always pejorative.
