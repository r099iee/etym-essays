Prostrate describes the body laid flat and face-down upon the ground — stretched out full length, forehead to the earth — in worship, submission, supplication, or utter exhaustion. It is the posture of the penitent before the altar, the defeated before the conqueror, the invalid felled by fever: the human form making itself as low, as defenceless, and as earnest as a body can be.

In the source sentence the hobbits are found prostrate — flung face-down by the pain and alarm of the dragon’s passing — and then, the terror lifting, they leap to their feet. Tolkien stages a tiny resurrection: the flat prone heap becomes upright hobbits in a single verb. The contrast is the whole point — prostrate is the zero of posture, the ground state from which standing is a miracle, and the leap that follows it measures exactly how complete the deliverance feels.

Core meaning in detail: As an adjective prostrate is pronounced with stress on the first syllable, while the verb to prostrate (to throw oneself down, or to lay someone low) stresses the second — one spelling, two musics, divided by grammar. The adjective covers three great conditions: (a) deliberately prone in reverence or surrender — pilgrims prostrate before shrines, ambassadors prostrate before thrones; (b) felled by force — armies, forests, and patients prostrate by sword, storm, or sickness; and (c) botanically creeping — prostrate stems that trail along the soil instead of rising. All three share the geometry of lowness: what stands has been brought, or has brought itself, to the ground.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Lying face-down in worship or submission (primary sense, from the 15th c.): the prostrate pilgrims, the prostrate ambassadors. The bridge is the body’s oldest argument: to lower oneself is to declare the other higher — before gods, kings, and judges, flatness flatters.
  • Overcome, exhausted, incapacitated (figurative, 17th c.): prostrate with grief, prostrate after influenza, the nation prostrate after war. The bridge runs from the chosen posture to the imposed condition: sorrow and sickness lay a body as flat as devotion does, and the same adjective serves the mourner and the monk.
  • Thrown down or laid low by force (literary): the prostrate city, the prostrate forest after the gale. The bridge extends the fallen body to anything felled — walls, woods, economies — keeping the pathos of something built upright now levelled.
  • Creeping along the ground, not upright (botany): prostrate thyme, prostrate rosemary, the prostrate stems of the partridge berry. The bridge is pure geometry borrowed for plants: the stem that refuses to rise is described in the language of the worshipper who refuses to stand.
  • The verb to prostrate oneself (reflexive, formal): she prostrated herself before the throne to beg mercy. The bridge returns the adjective to action: the state of lowness made into a deliberate deed, the courtier’s full-length bow.
  • The verb to prostrate meaning to exhaust or subdue (transitive): prostrated by heat, a prostrating illness. The bridge carries the battlefield into the sickroom: fever lays siege to the body exactly as armies lay cities low, and medicine kept the martial verb for the crisis.

Register and connotation: Prostrate is solemn, literary, and weighty — the vocabulary of liturgy, diplomacy, tragedy, and the sickbed, never of the playground. It dignifies even collapse: to call the hobbits prostrate rather than merely flattened lends their terror ceremony, as if the dragon’s passing were a dark god’s visitation. The botanical sense alone is neutral and technical, trailing harmlessly along garden walls while its grander siblings kneel in cathedrals and fall on battlefields.
