1. The debated root. The councillor's pedigree begins in the grammar of Indo-European gathering. The prefix con- is Latin cum, "together, with", from the PIE adverb *kom, "beside, near, together" — the same root that produced English com- loans and the Germanic ge- collectives. The second element, the base of consulere, "to consult, deliberate", is one of the celebrated cruxes of Latin etymology. The Roman grammarians claimed, with visible etymological enthusiasm, that consulere and consul came from salire, "to leap", because the consuls and consultants "leapt together" in assembly — a beautiful story in which no modern scholar believes. The serious candidates are a root meaning "to take, to grasp" (PIE *sel-, seen in Greek heleîn); one theory reads the compound as "to take to oneself", that is, to seize advice into the mind. De Vaan's comparative dictionary of Latin records the matter as unresolved: the formation is old enough to sit on a genuinely prehistoric root, but the evidence does not permit certainty. The honest chain therefore marks *sel- as tentative.


2. Roman deliberation: consulere, consilium, consiliarius. Whatever its root, consulere is attested from the earliest Latin literature — Plautus and Terence, c. 200–160 BCE, use it constantly for "to ask advice, to deliberate" — and beside it grew the noun consilium, "deliberation, advice, a deliberative assembly". In the Roman republic consilium was both an act and an institution: the senate's formal opinion was the senatus consultum, "the senate's deliberation", and generals consulted their consilium of senior officers before battle. The same root built consul, the supreme magistrate whose very name commemorates the act of consulting the senate and the people. From consilium the late republican and imperial language derived consiliarius, "a counsellor, one who sits in deliberation" — the direct ancestor of our word. It was a staff title: the emperor's consiliarii were the jurists and ministers who advised him, and provincial governors travelled with their own consilium of advisers.


3. The two Latin words and the medieval channel. Here the history forks into two streams that only the spelling of modern English rejoins. On one side, consilium (advice, deliberation) passed into Old French as conseil "advice" and gave conseillier/conseillour "counsellor"; this line produced English counsel (advice) and counsellor (adviser). On the other side, a different Latin word, concilium — "assembly, meeting", from com- + calare, "to call together" (the root of calendar, the called days) — passed into Old French as concile and gave English council: the body that is called together, not the advice it gives. Medieval English initially kept the two families apart: a counseillour was a person, counsel was advice, councile was the assembly. But the forms were too close, and from the 13th century the person-word is found doing duty for membership of assemblies: the "counseillours" of a king, of a city, of a guild were those who sat and deliberated in council.


4. The spelling fusion and the Privy Councillor. The decisive event was orthographic. In the 16th century, writers began spelling the office-holder councillor, with the -cil- of council, to distinguish him from the pure adviser, the counsellor. The spelling reform institutionalised a semantic distinction that had been creeping in for three centuries: a councillor is defined by the chamber he sits in (the assembly, concilium), a counsellor by the advice he gives (the deliberation, consilium). The 16th century also created its grandest title: the Privy Councillor, member of the Privy Council, the crown's inner circle of advisers — an office of enormous prestige, its holders addressed as "Right Honourable". Meanwhile the -our spelling of the suffix, inherited from Old French, wavered and settled as -or in Britain while American English took one l as well (councilor), following Noah Webster's 1828 dictionary.


5. Municipalisation and the modern councillor. The modern sense is a 19th-century creation. Before 1835, English boroughs were governed by self-perpetuating corporations — closed bodies whose members, called councillors or burgesses, were rarely elected and often corrupt; the age of the "rotten borough" and the borough-monger darkened the word. The Municipal Corporations Act of 1835 swept the closed corporations away and created elected town councils of councillors chosen by the ratepayers, with aldermen elected by the councillors themselves. Local government expanded through the county councils of 1888 and took its present shape with the Local Government Act 1972. In Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888) the Town Councillors are characters in this reformed municipal world, and their author treats them with the affectionate contempt the Victorians reserved for the committee man: they crave artistic reputations, reject the useless statue, and later propose a practical monument — every step a satire of the councillor's instinct to measure worth by utility and reputation. The word itself, though, has completed its own quiet journey: from "he who takes counsel together with others" to "he who sits on the council of the town" — from the personal wisdom of the Roman emperor's jurist to the ward surgery of the modern elected official, carrying in its double l the ghost of the two Latin words that history fused.
