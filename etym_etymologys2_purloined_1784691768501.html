PIE: *delh₁gʰ- "long" (attested in Greek dolikhós "long", Sanskrit dīrghá- "long", Avestan darəga- "long"; the shape *dl̥h₁gʰós underlies the Italic adjective).
Proto-Italic: *dlongos "long" — zero-grade of the root with the adjectival ending *-os.
Latin (c. 1st c. BCE): longus "long; far, distant" — the adjective attested in classical literature; the root vocalism *dlo- leveled to *lo-.
Late Latin: longē "far off, at a distance" — the regular adverb formed from longus.
Old French (c. 9th–14th c.): loign / luign "far, distant" — descended from Latin longē by regular Romance sound change (the adverb became an adjective/adverb of distance).
Old French (c. 12th c.): porloignier / purloignier "to put far off, remove to a distance, postpone" — compound of por-/pur- (from Latin pro- "forth, away") + a stem based on loign; literally "to put far away."
Anglo-Norman: purluigner — the insular French form that crossed the Channel with the Norman ruling class.
Middle English (14th c.): purloinen "to put aside, remove, set far away" — the sense then narrows on English soil to "to remove secretly, to steal."
Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): purloin "to steal, pilfer furtively" — a settled literary word.
Modern English: PURLOINED — the past participle, meaning "stolen secretly or artfully."
NOTE ON DEBATE: The prefix is Latin pro- "forth, away"; the sense-development from "put far away" to "steal secretly" took place in Anglo-Norman and English, where "to remove to a distance" was understood euphemistically as "to remove (another's property) for oneself." The PIE root *delh₁gʰ- "long" is securely attested, though its exact grade in Italic (*dl̥h₁gʰó- > *dlongos) is reconstructed on comparative evidence rather than directly attested.
