- [early 15th century: the sound enters English] - Middle English gurgelen records the imitative bubbling noise of liquid churning in a throat or a narrow channel; the word is echoic from its birth.
- [late 16th century: the modern verb settles] - Gurgle is established, straddling the two poles of its meaning: the human gullet and swirling water.
- [17th-19th century: the pleasant pole grows] - Applied to streams, brooks, and drains, gurgling becomes a gentle, musical, almost affectionate sound; later, happy babies gurgle in their cribs, cementing the merry register.
- [19th-20th century: the dark pole stays] - The ominous side never fades: a gurgling throat, a gurgling drain, the wet bubbling of a body half-choked with liquid — the two registers coexist, one playful, one horrible.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still split between a cheerful baby's gurgle and the wet, throat-choked gurgle of distress; it is precisely this dual life that lets Tolkien's 'whistling and gurgling sound horrible to listen to' land so hard.
