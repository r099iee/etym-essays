- c. 1400 — disloggen enters Middle English from Old French deslogier ‘to break camp, to evict’: the earliest sense is military-housing — forcing an enemy (or anyone) OUT of their quarters or logement; to dislodge is to evict from shelter.
- 15th–16th centuries — the word leaves the campsite behind: anything fixed may now be dislodged (stoppages, stones, men from office); the chronicles use it for driving garrisons from castles and towns, and the tactical sense crystallises into ‘drive from a position’.
- 17th–18th centuries — classical tactics make ‘dislodging the enemy from the heights, the wood, the redoubt’ a standard phrase of drill manuals and dispatches; the word is now solidly martial and positional, with lodgement as its paired noun (the foothold seized).
- 19th century — science, engineering and medicine domesticate it: surgeons dislodge obstructions and foreign bodies; geologists write of dislodged boulders and strata; engineers dislodge blockages — the word loses its purely military colour and becomes the everyday verb for knocking things out of their sockets.
- 20th century — politics borrows the tactics: regimes, incumbents and monopolies are dislodged from power; psychology and rhetoric dislodge fixed ideas, prejudices and habits; the everyday physical sense (dislodged stones, teeth, corks) is fully neutral and common.
- 21st century — dislodged is standard in medicine (dislodged lines, catheters, clots, airway obstructions), in geology and engineering, and in rescue reporting — while the old camp-eviction echo still faintly powers every use: what was dislodged was lodged, settled, entrenched — and had to be driven out.
