The evolution of puff: a word that is its own sound 1. An imitative beginning: the word as sound The story of puff is essentially the story of an onomatopoeia. The word's shape — the labial p-, the short vowel, and the fricative -ff — mimics a short, sharp expulsion of air through the lips; pronounce it and you physically perform the thing it names. Old English already had the verb pyffan, 'to blow in puffs', and the same expressive formation appears across the Germanic languages — Middle Low German puffen, Dutch poffen, German puffen — as well as beyond the family, in French pouf and Italian buffare. This is the signature of an imitative or echoic word: it is coined afresh in language after language from the same imitation of a sound, and for that reason it has no Indo-European root and no reconstructable proto-form. Dictionaries class puff as echoic, and they note that its Old English attestation, though early, is sparse — the word was surely spoken long before it was written. 2. Old and Middle English: from blowing to breathing In its earliest English life, puff was a verb of the breath and the wind: to blow out air in short clouds, as the mouth does on a cold morning, as the bellows do, as a gust does from the chimney. By Middle English, puffen had already broadened along two lines. First, the physiological line: a person who puffs is short of breath — panting, working hard, overheated — so the word moved from blowing to breathing. Second, the inflationary line: anything filled with air 'puffs', so the word came to describe swelling — cheeks puffed, sails puffed, pastry puffs in the oven. Both lines coexist in the modern word and both descend from the same concrete breath-image; there was never a moment when the word stopped meaning 'a burst of air', and every later sense is that burst of air extended one step further. 3. The noun: a puff of wind, smoke, and vapour Alongside the verb grew the noun: a puff, a short emission of air, smoke, or vapour, and by metonymy the visible cloud itself — a puff of smoke from a pipe, a puff of wind, a puff of steam. The noun appears in Middle English and quickly became the standard word for the small clouds of smoke that tobacco and steam produce, and for the small gusts that air makes. The 'puff' of a locomotive, chuffing black smoke up a gradient, is one of the most vivid modern examples — a mechanical panting that English speakers instinctively describe with the same breath-word they use of themselves. 4. The inflationary family: pastry, sleeves, and powder The swelling-with-air branch produced the word's most famous concrete applications. Puff pastry, so called because it puffs up in the oven with layers of trapped steam, is attested from the mid-18th century, and a cream puff — the light choux pastry — follows from the 19th. Garments puffed with air gave 'puffed sleeves', a fashion recurring from the 16th century onward. The powder puff, the soft pad that puffs powder onto the skin, is attested from the 17th century and remains the everyday name for a cosmetic tool. In every case the image is identical: a soft thing inflated with air, light, rounded, and delicate — the word's breathiness turned into texture. 5. The figurative branch: puffery and the puffed-up reputation The most productive figurative extension of puff is promotional. To 'puff' something is to inflate its worth with empty praise, exactly as air inflates a balloon or a pastry — swelling without substance. The usage is old: to puff a book, to puff a performance, to puff a candidate, all from the 17th century onward, and from it came the noun 'puffery', hollow or exaggerated promotional praise, and the modern journalistic 'puff piece', a story written to inflate a subject rather than inform about it. The same image produced the moral senses: to be 'puffed up' is to be swollen with pride or self-importance, and to be 'puffed' simply means out of breath. The whole cluster — promotion, pride, breathlessness — is one image: air, inflation, and its cost. 6. The word in everyday life and in The Hobbit In ordinary usage puff is vivid and slightly comic: people 'huff and puff', trains 'puff along', wind comes 'in puffs', and smokers 'puff' their pipes. The word has never lost its acoustic root — you can always hear the breath in it. In Tolkien's sentence — "'Gandalf for certain this time,' he thought as he puffed along the passage" — that acoustic root does the characterization. Bilbo, a well-fed householder forced into repeated trips to the door, moves in pants rather than strides; the word marks the cost in air and dignity of his domestic adventure, and it does so with a word as old as English blowing itself. The same family that gives soft pastry, powder puffs, and puffed-up reputations here gives a hobbit's winded progress toward yet another knock at the door.
