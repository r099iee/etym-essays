Dreary is an adjective meaning dull, bleak, cheerless and depressing, applied above all to weather, landscapes, moods and the general tone of places and lives. Its characteristic note is a heavy, weary, colourless melancholy, the greyness of things rather than their blackness. It is stronger than "unpleasant" and less intense than "horrifying"; a dreary thing does not terrify, it drains. Senses and usages:
  • Of weather. A dreary day is grey, overcast, drizzly, damp and sunless; the word is almost the standard English adjective for the long, dripping, lightless days of a northern winter, and it commonly collocates with grey, wet, damp, dull, November, afternoon, day and sky.
  • Of landscapes and places. Dreary hills, moors, roads, streets, towns and rooms are places drained of colour, warmth and life: barren, exposed, monotonous, or simply lightless and empty. The context sentence, "Not far ahead were dreary hills, rising higher and higher, dark with trees", shows the landscape sense doing its first darkening work in The Hobbit.
  • Of mood, temperament and life. A dreary mood is joyless and heavy; a dreary person is without liveliness; a dreary life or existence is monotonous and wearisome, one long grey stretch with no colour or incident.
  • Of sounds, events and things. A dreary drone, a dreary lecture, a dreary meeting, a dreary room: anything so tediously dull or monotonous that it depresses the spirits. In this sense dreary approaches boring but keeps a melancholy weight that boring lacks.
  • The clipped poetic form drear. A back-formation of the seventeenth century (1620s), drear is used by poets as a one-syllable adjective (and occasionally noun) meaning the same as dreary, valued for its brevity in verse; it survives in poetry and archaizing prose.
  • The noun dreariness. The state or quality of being dreary — the dreariness of a long winter, of a dead town, of a loveless routine.
Historical semantic bridge: the word descends from Old English drēorig, which literally meant "gory, blood-stained" and secondarily "sad, sorrowful", both built on the noun drēor "gore, spilt blood, that which falls or drips" from the verb drēosan "to fall". In Anglo-Saxon poetry the grief of a lord who has watched his retainers fall in battle was felt as a kind of bleeding of the heart; the two senses, physical gore and emotional grief, were never far apart. By Middle English (drery, c. 1300) the bloody sense had faded and the sorrowful sense remained, now detached from the battlefield. The great modern shift came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when dreary transferred from a person's grief to a place's atmosphere: a landscape, road or sky could now be dreary, and the word settled into its modern career as the grey, wearying adjective of draining weather, scenes and lives. Dreary in the family of gloom: it differs from gloomy (darkness and shadow), bleak (exposure, cold, barrenness), dismal (ill omen, from the medieval Latin dies mali "evil days"), desolate (emptiness and abandonment) and forlorn (lost and pitiable) precisely in its dullness: a dreary day is not a terrible day but a wearying, colourless one.
