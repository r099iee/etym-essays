In the preface to his study of English metre, the author writes that his concentration on metre 'has also meant giving scant attention to other varieties of sound patterning like alliteration or rhyme.' The adverb of the sentence is deliberate understatement: scant attention is not merely little attention but attention deliberately kept short — a measured insufficiency, a portion consciously rationed. The author admits that alliteration, rhyme, and the wider rhythms of syntax and meaning have been set aside, not through carelessness but by the discipline of a book that must draw its central network before exploring byways. The nuance is courteous, almost apologetic: the word concedes the shortfall while implying it was chosen. Its slightly formal, bookish tone is exactly right for a scholar's preface.

Polysemy: the senses of SCANT
  • Sense 1 — barely or less than sufficient (primary): 'Barely sufficient or less than sufficient; meagre, limited, deficient in quantity or amount.' Scant rations, a scant supply, scant thanks — the amount is small and is felt to be short of what is wanted.
  • Sense 2 — short of the full measure (measuring): Of weights and measures, scant means short of the full or honest amount: a scant cup, a scant hour, scant measure. The bridge: from 'short' (Old Norse skammr) to 'short of measure'.
  • Sense 3 — barely, only (attributive with number/time): Used before a measure to mean 'barely, hardly': 'scant two hours,' 'a scant mile away,' 'scant hope of success.' Here the word means 'at most, no more than.'
  • Sense 4 — adverb (now chiefly literary): As an adverb, scant means 'scarcely, barely' — 'scant better than a guess' — surviving in formal and literary use.
  • Sense 5 — verb (to scant): To scant is to limit, stint, or withhold proper measure: 'to scant a labourer his wage,' 'to scant one's attention.' The bridge: from 'short in measure' to 'to make short, to skimp.'
  • Sense 6 — figurative and abstract (modern strong use): In modern writing scant is most powerful in abstract collocations: scant attention, scant regard, scant consolation, scant mention — an amount deliberately kept short, with a hint of disappointment or reproach.

The semantic boundary: Scant must be set against its near-synonyms. Scanty stresses barely sufficient or skimpy quantity — a scanty meal — and carries a mildly apologetic colour; scant itself has the flavour of a portion consciously rationed. Sparse describes things thinly scattered over a space — sparse hair, a sparse population — rather than a shortfall in amount. Meagre suggests thinness and poverty, often of food or income, with a note of pity. Minimal is the neutral, technical word for the least possible; limited states a bound without emotional colour; skimpy is colloquial and mildly dismissive; insufficient and deficient are plain statements of shortfall; slight and negligible scale the degree down, with negligible meaning 'too small to matter'; scarce means hard to find or obtain, not merely little; short is the ordinary blunt word. Only scant combines measured insufficiency with a deliberate, almost courteous rationing of amount — the word for attention that is kept short on purpose.
