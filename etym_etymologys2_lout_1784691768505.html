PIE: *leud- "to bend, shrink, bow down" — NOT to be confused with the separate root *leudʰ- "to rise, grow; the people" (German Leute, Old English lēod "people"). The two roots are distinct; the confusion is a well-known trap in etymological handbooks.
Proto-Germanic: *lūtaną "to stoop, bow down, incline" — from *leud- with the thematic verb formation and lengthened *ū.
Old English (c. 700–1100): lūtan "to bow down, incline, stoop" — a strong verb (past lēat, past participle loten); beside it the derived weak verb lūtian "to lurk, skulk, lie hidden."
Old Norse: lúta "to bow, stoop" — the Scandinavian cognate, which reinforced the native verb across the Danelaw in northern and eastern Middle English dialects.
Middle English (c. 1150–1500): louten "to bow, crouch, cringe, act submissively" — the regular descendant of Old English lūtan.
16th century (English): lout (noun) "an awkward, clumsy, ill-mannered fellow" — derived from the verb "to lout," i.e. one who stoops or cringes; recorded from the mid-1500s.
16th century: loutish (adj.) "clumsy, boorish" and loutishness (n.) — the derived adjective and abstract noun appear in the same century.
Archaic verb: lout (to bow, stoop) survives in dialect into the 19th century.
Modern English: LOUT — an uncouth, clumsy, aggressive, or ill-mannered person.
NOTE ON DISTRIBUTION: no certain cognates of the root *leud- "to bend/stoop" are attested outside Germanic, and the reconstruction of the PIE root rests on the Germanic family alone; the root is therefore sometimes treated as Germanic-internal. The Old Norse form lúta reinforced, rather than created, the English verb.
