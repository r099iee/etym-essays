- Old English — reinforced negation: nāwiht means not-anything, an emphatic negative built from ne plus āwiht, ordinary prose vocabulary.
- Middle English — everyday and moral: naught and nought spellings mix freely for nothing, zero, and worthlessness in Chaucer and devotional writing.
- 16th–17th c. — the high literary age: Shakespeare and the King James Bible fix its solemn music — set at naught, come to naught — and coin naughty for the worthless person.
- 18th–19th c. — retreat into idiom: plain nothing takes over ordinary speech while naught survives in proverbs, pulpits, and poetry, and nought settles as the cipher spelling.
- Victorian playroom — the game piece: noughts and crosses names the O with the cipher word, planting the old nothing in every nursery.
- Today — fossil with sparkle: rare outside come to naught and set at naught, but favoured by fantasy, fable, and period pastiche for instant antiquity.
