Vernal comes straight from Latin vērnālis “of spring”, built on vēr “spring” — one of the most securely reconstructed words in the Indo-European lexicon. The name of the season belongs to the parent language: PIE *wésr̥ / *wesr- “spring” is attested by cognates across the family — Sanskrit vasantá- “spring”, Greek ἤαρ / ἔαρ (ear) “spring”, Latin vēr, Lithuanian vasarà “summer”, and the Slavic vesna “spring” (Russian весна). The season-word is thus thousands of years older than the English adjective, and it was already common vocabulary at the time the parent language was spoken.

In Latin

Latin vēr “spring” (with the combining stem vern-) produced a small family of adjectives. The classical adjective is vērnus “of spring, vernal”; the longer form vērnālis, which is the direct source of English vernal, circulated in later literary Latin and in the learned Latin of the mediaeval period — the -alis suffix (as in autumnālis, hiemālis) was the standard way of forming relational adjectives. It is from this Latin adjective that the Renaissance humanists took the English word: the first English attestations date from the 1530s, in the same decades that borrowed autumnal, estival, and hiemal — of which only autumnal and vernal ever fully naturalised in English.

Into English: almanacs and the vernal equinox

In English, vernal's first home was the almanac and the astronomical calendar: “the vernal equinox” appears in 16th-century English almanacs, bound up with the reckoning of Easter and with the “first point of Aries” — the reference point for the equinoxes since Hellenistic astronomy (Hipparchus discovered the precession of the equinoxes around 130 BCE, and the vernal equinox has been the zero-point of celestial coordinates ever since). For the humanist writers of the period the word had an attractive scholarly pedigree, and it spread quickly through natural description: “vernal flowers”, “vernal weather”, and the seasonal migrations of birds all appear by the early 17th century.

The poetic century: Milton, and the Romantics

Renaissance and Augustan verse turned “vernal bloom” and “vernal shades” into stock diction — Milton uses vernal in Paradise Lost for the freshness of Paradise itself — and the 18th century's georgic poetry kept the word busy with the seasons. The Romantics then renewed it as an emblem of hope and renewal rather than decoration: Wordsworth writes in The Prelude (the source passage) of days that “bring with them vernal promises, the hope / Of active days”, and in The Tables Turned gives the language one of its best-known seasonal lines, “One impulse from a vernal wood / Shall teach you more of man”. Keats's To Autumn is the autumnal counterpart; between them, the two season-words frame the Romantic idea of nature's teaching. For the Victorians “vernal” became elegant period-colour, used with a certain deliberate literary distance.

Modern register: the technical word

In the 20th century vernal receded from poetry into fixed technical uses — and in doing so it gained new precision. Vernalization, the agricultural technique of cold-treating seeds or seedlings to induce early flowering, was named in the inter-war years from the Latin adjective (translating the Russian jarovizacija, from jarovoj “spring”); it became a fixture of Soviet and then international agronomy and plant physiology. Ecology contributed the vernal pool, the seasonal wetland of Mediterranean-type climates, and botany long had its sweet vernal grass. Astronomy keeps the vernal equinox as the fundamental reference point of coordinate systems. In conversation the word is extinct — a speaker who says “vernal” is deliberately literary or technical. Its career — from the common Indo-European word for spring, through Latin adjective-formation, English Renaissance learning and Romantic nature poetry, to the jargon of astronomy, agronomy, and ecology — is a small map of how a word can be mainstream, then elegant, then specialised without ever changing its meaning.
