In the literary context of this maxim, Li Shou-zhong founding his educational policy for girls on the saying 'a stupid woman is a virtuous one', virtuous does not praise active moral excellence or public heroism. It names a patriarchal ideal of female 'virtue' defined as silence, ignorance, and submissive domestic utility. Intellectual growth is cast as a threat to moral purity; the word's glow of ethical approval is twisted so that enforced stupidity becomes the guarantee of a woman's supposed goodness. The tone is bitterly ironic to a modern reader: virtue is hollowed out into social control rather than character. DEFINITION, PRIMARY SENSE. In its standard modern meaning, virtuous is an adjective meaning having or showing high moral standards; morally excellent, upright, and good. It describes a person or an action that conforms to a moral code, whether religious or secular. The noun is virtue, and the adverb is virtuously. In everyday use it is a formal or literary word, often replaced in speech by good or decent. POLYSEMY. Virtuous is polysemous, and its senses trace the whole career of the Latin virtus, 'manliness, excellence, power.' The full inventory:
  • Moral excellence in general (the primary modern sense): a virtuous life, virtuous conduct, a virtuous ruler. The sense covers honesty, justice, kindness, and integrity.
  • Chastity and feminine purity (historically prominent, and the sense weaponized in the card): a virtuous woman, virtuous wife, virtuous maiden. From the early modern period onward, 'a virtuous woman' came to mean one who is chaste, modest, and domestically obedient, a didactic ideal with strong class overtones.
  • Efficacy or power of a substance (older, now mostly within the noun virtue): 'the virtue of a herb' meant its medicinal power or potency; the adjective in this sense survives in phrases like 'by virtue of' (by the power or authority of).
  • Active valor and manliness (the classical Roman residue, now only historical or literary): virtuous once carried the sense of brave, manly excellence inherited from Latin virtus, 'manliness, courage.' This is the sense lost first as the word became moralized and feminized.
  • Self-reinforcing positive feedback (modern technical, chiefly through the noun): a virtuous circle is a beneficial cycle in which each good result produces another, common in economics, politics, and systems analysis; the adjective also appears as a modifier (a virtuous cycle).
  • Ironic and critical uses (modern): in feminist and literary criticism, 'virtuous' is often used under quotation marks to expose imposed ideals of female excellence; virtue signaling names public displays of moral stance, and is almost always pejorative.
SEMANTIC BRIDGE. The bridge from classical excellence to domestic chastity is the double career of virtus. In Rome virtus meant the excellence proper to a vir, a man: courage in battle, vigor, and public worth. Christianity moralized virtus into the general 'goodness' of the soul, listed as the cardinal and theological virtues. As that moralized virtue came to be applied to women, social practice redefined it: a woman's virtue was not battlefield courage but bodily purity and obedience. Hence virtuous acquires both the general moral sense and the specifically gendered sense, and the maxim in the text exploits exactly that double life, turning 'moral excellence' into a label for ignorance and submission. COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER. Common collocations include virtuous life, virtuous woman/wife, virtuous conduct, virtuous character, and the older virtuous virgin/maiden. Register is formal to literary; in everyday speech good or decent usually replace it. In moral philosophy and religious discourse it remains standard; in feminist and literary criticism it frequently appears under ironic or critical quotation marks. Avoid using it as a bland synonym for 'nice': its historical gender load and high moral tone make it sharper and more specific than casual near-equivalents. In this maxim the word is doing ideological work, and a reader who misses the gender history of virtuous misses the sentence's bitter point.
