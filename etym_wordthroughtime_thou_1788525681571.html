- [Old English (c. 700–1100)] - þū was the universal, standard nominative second-person singular pronoun across all societal contexts - [connotation: completely neutral grammatical singular subject]
- [Middle English (c. 1100–1500)] - thou developed the T/V politeness distinction alongside plural ye/you under French court influence - [connotation: intimate, familial, affectionate, or condescending toward inferiors]
- [Early Modern English (c. 1500–1650)] - thou became socially risky in polite society; using thou to an equal or superior became a severe insult - [connotation: rustic, uncouth, highly offensive, or deeply intimate]
- [Late 17th–18th c.] - standard spoken English abandoned thou entirely in favour of universal you; Quakers adopted it for egalitarian principle - [connotation: sectarian plainness or liturgical prayer]
- [19th c.] - Victorian literature and Romantic poetry (Wilde, Keats, Tennyson) embraced thou for elevated aesthetic, sublime, and fairy-tale moods - [connotation: sacred, grand, reverent, and poetically exalted]
- [20th–21st c.] - fully archaic in standard English; surviving in the Authorized Version, church liturgy, Shakespearean staging, and Northern English dialects - [connotation: liturgical, historical, majestic, and nostalgic]
