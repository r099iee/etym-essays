PIE *dek- "to take, accept; to perceive or judge as fitting" (Pokorny: *dek- "to take, accept, to seem worthy"). The root yields independent Greek and Latin branches; the Greek branch (dokeîn, dogma, doxa) is a genuine sister and is not part of doctrinaire's own line. → Latin docēre "to show, teach" — the Latin development of *dek- in the "teach" direction; the verb is a causative, literally "to cause to take (into the mind), to make acceptable" (attested). → Latin doctus "taught, learned, skilled" (past participle of docēre). → Latin doctrīna "teaching, instruction, learning; a body of doctrine" (from doctor "teacher", itself from doctus). → Old French doctrine → English doctrine (noun, recorded 1380s) "teaching; a body of principles". → French doctrinaire (adjective and noun, coined 1815) — first applied as a nickname to the French post-Restoration constitutionalist group (Royer-Collard, Guizot), derided for ruling by abstract principle; formed from doctrine + the person-marking suffix -aire (as in millionaire). → English doctrinaire (adjective and noun, borrowed c. 1840) "seeking to impose a doctrine in all circumstances without regard to practical considerations" — DOCTRINAIRE (our card). NOTE on the debate: the derivation of Latin docēre from PIE *dek- is the standard scholarly account. The Greek branch of the same root (dokeîn "to seem, think", dógma, dóxa) is a true sibling; the anti-merging guardrail keeps the DOGMA and DOCTRINAIRE lines distinct even though they are distant cousins of the same root. The French political origin of the English word in 1815 and its borrowing around 1840 are securely documented.
