• Tomorrow: Similar because it is the everyday descendant of the same word — literally "to the morrow" — and denotes the next day; differs because tomorrow is the unmarked, current word (adverb and noun), while morrow is archaic/literary and can still echo "morning."
  • Morning: Similar because both descend from the Germanic "morning" word and both can mean the early part of the day; differs because morning names a portion of any day and never by itself means "the following day," the primary modern sense of morrow.
  • Morn: Similar because it is a poetic clipping of morning sharing the archaic literary register; differs because morn usually means "morning" only, while morrow specializes in "the next day" after a given night.
  • Dawn / daybreak: Similar because both reach toward the beginning of day and both occur in literary English; differs because they pinpoint first light as a moment, not the whole ensuing daylight unit that morrow denotes.
  • The following day: Similar because it is the neutral paraphrase of morrow's main sense; differs because it is unmarked, prosaic, and carries none of the archaic glow or residual dawn imagery of morrow.
  • Eventide / evening: Similar because both are archaic-marked "part of day" nouns with a literary flavor; differs because they name the opposite pole (end of day), throwing morrow's position at the start of the day into relief.
  • Hereafter / time to come: Similar because both can gesture vaguely at "the future"; differs because hereafter is abstract and general ("after this"), while morrow names a concrete, dated next day.
