Derivation chain for damp — a single linear chain from the Indo-European root to the present English word: 1. Proto-Indo-European *dʰewh₂- (also written *dʰeu-) "to fume, smoke, raise dust or vapour" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the great "smoke/dust/vapour" root, cognate across the family: Sanskrit धूम (dhūmá-) "smoke," Greek θυμός (thūmós) "breath, spirit," Latin fūmus "smoke," Old Church Slavonic дымъ (dymŭ) "smoke," Lithuanian dū́mai "smoke." 2. Proto-Germanic *dampaz "vapour, steam, smoke" (c. 500 BCE–500 CE) — the connection to *dʰewh₂- is the standard scholarly hypothesis but is flagged as plausible rather than certain (OED: "of unknown origin, perhaps from the PIE root *dheu- 'to rise in a cloud'"). 3. Middle Low German damp "steam, vapour, fume, smoke" (attested 13th–14th c.) — the immediate source of the English word. 4. Middle English damp "a noxious vapour, fume, exhalation" (borrowed 14th c.). 5. English damp (noun "a fog/mist; a noxious mine-gas; humidity"; adjective "slightly wet, moist" from the 16th c., from the condensing-vapour image; verb "to choke, stifle, deaden" — whence "to damp a fire," "to dampen," "damper"). Note on debate and nuance: the pre-Germanic stage linking Germanic *dampaz to PIE *dʰewh₂- is uncertain; OED and Etymonline treat the connection as plausible rather than certain. The word is recorded across West Germanic — Middle Dutch damp "vapour, mist" (modern Dutch damp "vapour, steam"), Old High German damph → modern German Dampf "steam" — and English received its form as a loan from Middle Low German while German and Dutch descend natively from the same Germanic base.
