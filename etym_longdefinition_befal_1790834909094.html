befal (verb; modern standard variant: befall; past tense: befell; past participle: befallen; third-person present: befalls) means to happen, occur, or come to pass, particularly referring to events that affect someone as their lot, destiny, or unforeseen fortune. While the verb can theoretically apply to neutral or fortunate occurrences, historically and in contemporary usage it carries a strong tilt toward grave, momentous, ominous, or adverse events—such as disasters, accidents, death, or severe trials.

The verb exhibits distinct syntactic structures and semantic dimensions:

  • Transitive Construction with an Affected Object: Functioning transitively, it takes a direct personal or collective object denoting the entity upon whom an event or fate descends ("whatever may befal him", "a terrible calamity befell the ancient city", "no harm shall befal thee"). In this construction, the subject is typically the occurrence, fate, or danger, while the object is the recipient of that destiny.
  • Intransitive and Impersonal Constructions: Used intransitively, often in archaic, poetic, or narrative discourse, to mean simply to occur or take place ("as it befell in days of old", "let come what may befal"). It frequently appears with introductory dummy pronouns ("it befell that the king died").
  • Momentous and Ominous Gravitas: Unlike neutral verbs of occurrence such as happen or take place, befal carries an inherent weight of consequence, inexorability, or moral significance. Events that befal someone are rarely trivial; they alter lives, test character, or fulfill a tragic trajectory.
  • Spelling and Lexicographical Nuance: The single-l spelling befal represents the standard early modern orthographic variant common in the 17th and 18th centuries (as seen in the prose of John Milton, Joseph Addison, and Alexander Pope). In modern English, this has been largely regularized to befall, though befal remains an authentic historical form in classical texts.

Historical Semantic Bridges and Metaphoric Shifts:

The verb is formed from the Germanic prefix be- (an intensive and applicative prefix derived from Proto-Germanic *bi-, "around, about, upon") combined with the base verb fall (from Proto-Germanic *fallaną, "to fall down, drop").

  • From Physical Descent to Existential Eventuation: In early Germanic, the compound befeallan possessed literal physical meanings, such as falling upon something, collapsing around someone, or being engulfed by physical debris. Over time, physical descent was metaphorically mapped onto the psychological concept of fate. Just as physical rain, nightfall, or a falling tree descends from above onto an unsuspecting traveler, events, fortunes, and divine decrees descend from heaven or destiny onto human beings.
  • The Concept of "Falling" as Chance and Fate: Across Indo-European and Germanic languages, concepts of luck, chance, and happening are intimately tied to the motion of falling (compare English fall out, German zufallen / beifallen, Latin accidere from ad- + cadere "to fall upon", and Greek $\pi\tilde{\imath}\pi\tau\omega$). What "befalls" a person is literally what "falls upon" them from the wheel of fortune.
  • Contemporary Register: In present-day English, befall is reserved for literary, elevated, tragic, or philosophical registers. It imparts a sense of timeless narrative weight, frequently used in epic storytelling, high journalism, or solemn reflections on human vulnerability.
