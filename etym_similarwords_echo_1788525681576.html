• reverberation: Similar because both involve the reflection of sound waves within an enclosed or semi-enclosed space; differs because reverberation is a continuous, dense blend of overlapping reflections arriving in rapid succession (< 50 ms) creating acoustic fullness, whereas an echo is a distinct, perceptibly separated repetition of the original sound.
  • resonance: Similar because both describe sound persisting or being amplified through an environment; differs because resonance involves a system vibrating at its natural sympathetic frequency in response to an external stimulus, whereas an echo is a straightforward reflection off a boundary surface without sympathetic oscillation.
  • reflection: Similar because acoustic echoes are a specific type of wave reflection; differs because reflection is the broad physical principle governing all wave phenomena (light, sound, radio waves, water), whereas echo specifically names the distinct returned auditory sensation or mythological figure.
  • repetition: Similar because both involve an event or statement occurring again; differs because repetition is general and intentional (a deliberate restatement or mechanical loop), whereas an echo is an environmental, passive, or secondary reflection of an existing sound.
  • imitation: Similar because both produce a copy of an original model; differs because imitation implies conscious or purposeful emulation of behavior, art, or manners, whereas an echo is an involuntary, natural, or acoustic rebound.
  • mimicry: Similar because both repeat or mirror another's vocalization or form; differs because mimicry carries strong connotations of mockery, performance, caricature, or biological camouflage, whereas an echo is disembodied, neutral, or tragic.
  • repercussion: Similar because both derive from the physical concept of striking back or rebounding from an impact; differs because repercussion has shifted almost entirely into a figurative noun meaning an indirect, often unwelcome long-term consequence of an action.
  • aftersound: Similar because both describe a sound heard after the original emission has ceased; differs because aftersound is a rare, poetic term for residual ringing or fading tone, lacking the discrete articulatory precision of an echo.
  • parroting: Similar because both involve unoriginal vocal repetition; differs because parroting is a derogatory colloquial term implying thoughtless, mechanical repetition without comprehension, whereas echo retains poetic elegance and acoustic accuracy.
  • allusion: Similar because in literary criticism an echo is often an allusion to an earlier text; differs because an allusion is an explicit or implicit intellectual reference, whereas a literary echo may be atmospheric, cadence-based, or intuitive.
