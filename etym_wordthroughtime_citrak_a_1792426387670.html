- [Late Vedic & Early Epic Era (c. 6th–4th c. BCE)] — identified in early oral transmissions and Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa as a picturesque, wooded sanctuary along the Mandākinī River, celebrated as an idyllic paradise for forest ascetics and the stage for Bharata's reunion with Rāma — [perception: venerated as a secluded, sublime refuge of pure nature and filial righteousness]
- [Classical Sanskrit Golden Age (c. 4th–8th c. CE)] — immortalized by Kālidāsa in the Meghadūta and Raghuvaṃśa as a lush mountain laden with blossoms and peacocks whose rocks bear the eternal footprints of Rāma and Sītā, while its name was borrowed to designate hill-fortresses like Citrakūṭa-durga (Chittorgarh) — [perception: celebrated as the pinnacle of classical pastoral beauty and romantic landscape poetry]
- [Medieval Bhakti Period (c. 14th–17th c. CE)] — transformed into one of North India's preeminent pilgrimage centers under the influence of Rāmānandī ascetics and the saint-poet Tulsīdās, who composed portions of the Rāmacaritamānasa on its riverbanks after experiencing divine visions — [perception: sanctified as an emotionally charged holy ground where devotees experience direct divine intimacy with Rāma]
- [Colonial & British Indological Era (19th c.)] — surveyed by British geographers and philologists (such as Alexander Cunningham) who mapped its ancient caves, inscriptions, and geological features straddling the Bundelkhand plateau — [perception: studied as an essential topographical anchor confirming the geography of the ancient Indian epics]
- [Modern Contemporary Era (20th–21st c.)] — established as a bustling religious pilgrimage hub and administrative district in Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh, attracting millions annually for the Kāmadgiri Parikramā and Amāvasyā festivals, while remaining a national literary symbol of uncompromised moral duty — [perception: universally honored as the holy landscape of sacred exile, filial love, and rural serenity]
