Compound word: triennial is composed of two root elements — tri- "three" and the annus "year" stem — each with its own independent linear derivation chain. Complete chains for each element, in isolation, are given below; the two chains converge in Latin and then travel to English together. CHAIN A — the element tri- "three": 1. Proto-Indo-European root *tréyes "three" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE). One of the most stable and widely attested roots in the family, surviving in almost every Indo-European branch. 2. → Proto-Italic *trēs "three" (reconstructed intermediate Italic form). 3. → Latin trēs / tria "three" (attested from the earliest Latin inscriptions, 7th–3rd c. BCE). From the oblique stem comes the combining form tri- "three," used in Latin compounds such as trifur, triangularis, triennium. 4. → Latin triennium "a period of three years" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE; tri- "three" + annus "year" with the -ium suffix). The tri- element is the first component of this compound. 5. → first element tri- "three" of English triennial (attested 17th c.). CHAIN B — the element annus "year": 1. Proto-Indo-European root *h₂et- "to go" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE); from it the derivative *h₂et-no- "year, the going around of time" — the year conceived as the circuit that time completes (cognate with Gothic aþnam "years" and Sanskrit átati "he goes"). The reconstructed intermediate form of the root with the -no- suffix is *h₂et-no-. 2. → Proto-Italic *atnos "year" (reconstructed intermediate Italic form, showing the assimilation of the sequence to -nn-). 3. → Latin annus "year" (attested 3rd c. BCE onward). From the nominal stem Latin forms the adjectival stem -ennis "of years" (as in biennis "of two years," triennis "of three years") and the adjective annuus "yearly." 4. → Latin triennium "a period of three years" (tri- + annus, Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) and the later adjectival triennalis "of or occurring every three years" (post-classical / medieval Latin, attested in ecclesiastical and administrative usage). 5. → English triennial "occurring every three years; lasting three years" (formed in English on Latin triennium with the suffix -al, alongside the parallel French triennal; attested in English from the 17th c., c. 1650s). Debate/nuance note: No stage in either chain is seriously contested; *tréyes "three" and annus "year" (from PIE *h₂et-no-) are both securely reconstructed. The only nuance concerns the intermediate -ennis / -triennalis steps: English "triennial" was re-formed directly from Latin triennium + -al (matching French triennal) rather than inherited through French, so the direct French word triennal is a parallel borrowing, not the immediate source. The annus → *h₂et-no- connection is standard but relies on the assumption that the year was conceptualized as "the going round"; this motivating metaphor is plausible rather than strictly provable.
