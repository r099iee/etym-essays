The biography of "cot" begins far from English soil, in the domestic furniture of the Indian subcontinent, where a light bedstead made of a wooden frame strung with rope or woven cotton webbing has been in continuous use for millennia. The Sanskrit noun khaṭvā (खट्वा) — "bedstead, cot, couch on a frame" — is already a fully settled domestic term in epic and classical literature, and it passes into the Buddhist canon through Pali, where the khaṭvā appears among the simple furnishings permitted to monks. There is no Indo-European ancestor for this root: linguists regard *khaṭṭ- as a substrate word, most plausibly taken into Indo-Aryan from Dravidian or Munda stock, or else as an ancient cultural Wanderwort that traveled across the subcontinent with the object itself. The point matters because it means that "cot" is a loan from the very beginning of its attested history — a word borrowed along with its thing, like "curry" or "khaki," rather than inherited through regular sound-change from a reconstructed proto-form.

From Sanskrit the word moved down through the Middle Indo-Aryan (Prakrit) stage into the modern vernaculars: Hindi–Urdu khaṭ (खाट), Odia khāṭa (ଖାଟ), Bengali khāṭ (খাট), Gujarati khāṭ (ખાટ). At this stage the semantic center is stable — a light, collapsible frame-bed strung with webbing, the furniture of warm climates where a woven bed lets the air pass beneath the sleeper. The colonial encounter of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries carried the word westward twice over: Portuguese took it as cata or catre (hence Brazilian catre, a poor man's bed), and English, in the Anglo-Indian speech of the East India Company's world, took the Hindi form as "cot" in the seventeenth century. In English the primary sense remained the light portable bed of camp and barracks, but in British usage a striking secondary shift occurred in the nineteenth century: "cot" came to mean a baby's crib, so that the same word that named the soldier's folding bed named the infant's enclosed bed — and from that sense came the grim modern compound "cot death" for Sudden Infant Death Syndrome. American English, by contrast, kept the portable sense and made "cot" the standard word for the folding field or army bed. The word has also spun a set of institutional compounds — camp cot, hospital cot, folding cot, cot bed, cot sheet — and, in slang, a "cot" can be anywhere one lays one's head.

Standing beside the Indian loan is a native English homonym that shares the spelling but not the history: the Old English word cot (also cote), "a small hut, cottage, or shelter," from Proto-Germanic *kutan-. This is the cot of the sheepcot and the dovecote, the second element of cottage (the little house of a cotter), and it survives in dialect and place-names — and it is emphatically not the word on this card, though readers regularly confuse the two. The Indian cot is a bed; the native cot is a shelter. The two words collided in English after the colonial borrowing and have lived as a happy orthographic accident ever since.

In the passage, the Indian cot does exactly what its deep history says it should. The word arrives already meaning portability and transience — a light, woven, collapsible frame that can be folded up and carried away, the furniture of travellers, servants, and soldiers rather than of settled householders. Laid in it for two weeks while the tavernkeeper's wife attends him, the child is lodged briefly, at minimal cost, without belonging; the cot is a bed of utility lent to a body that will vanish in the night, leaving the cot behind as anonymous as the room. The single clipped syllable, /kɒt/, is itself the sound of something insubstantial — one beat, easily folded, easily forgotten — which is precisely the note the story needs for a wounded boy healing in borrowed space before returning to the road.

  • c. 1st millennium BCE: Sanskrit khaṭvā "bedstead, cot" — a settled domestic word; Pali khaṭvā in the Buddhist canon.
  • Prakrit → modern vernaculars: Hindi–Urdu khaṭ (खाट), Bengali khāṭ, Odia khāṭa — the light woven frame-bed.
  • 16th c.: Portuguese cata/catre; Brazilian catre "a rough poor bed."
  • 17th c.: English cot borrowed from Hindi khaṭ in Anglo-Indian usage — a light portable bed.
  • 19th c.: British transferred sense "baby's crib" → cot death (SIDS); American "folding army/field bed."
  • Contemporary: the light portable bed of camps, hospitals, and babies — furniture of transience, exactly right for the wounded child of the story.
