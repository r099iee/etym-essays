ribaldry: coarse, irreverent, and jeering talk or behaviour; low and vulgar mockery; indecent or obscene jesting of a scoffing sort. In Ruskin's sentence — "While the press vents its ribaldry on the most exalted truth, it becomes the duty of all who know what is great to come forward" — the word describes the mockery that the Victorian press directed at the highest subjects (specifically the abuse heaped on Turner and on high art). Its nuance is crucial: this is not good-natured raillery or intellectual satire but contemptuous, gutter-level derision, speech that drags what is elevated down to the level of the tavern. Ruskin counterposes ribaldry to "what is great" and to the duty of the knowing few to defend it, so the word carries a class judgement as well as a moral one: ribaldry is the language of the ribald, the worthless scoundrel, vented by a press that has abandoned taste and truth together.

Primary senses

  • Coarse, irreverent speech: the plainest sense — talk that is grossly indecent, mocking, and free of all respect, often with a sexual tinge but not necessarily so; the speech of the tavern floor, the barracks, the gutter.
  • Vulgar mockery / jeering: derision deliberately aimed at what is held sacred or elevated, carried on in a low, sneering register; mockery that degrades its object. This is the sense Ruskin uses of the press.
  • Obscene or licentious jesting: in older usage, ribaldry is close to bawdry — indecent talk, scurrilous humour, jesting on sexual and bodily subjects.
  • Ribald behaviour (now chiefly historical): the conduct of a ribald — dissolute, licentious, disorderly living; the word once meant "debauchery, licentiousness" as much as "coarse speech."

The semantic bridge

The word descends from the noun ribald, "a low, worthless fellow, a scoundrel, a lewd person," which came into English from Old French ribaut / ribalt in the thirteenth century. The Old French noun itself grew from the verb riber, "to be wanton, to live licentiously, to dally amorously," which the Franks bequeathed to French from a Germanic verb meaning "to rub" — with the euphemistic "rub" → "copulate" transfer that appears in many languages. The chain runs from "to rub," through "to be wanton," to "a wanton, worthless fellow," and finally to "the coarse, jeering speech of such a fellow." The history of the word is thus a steady descent from a body-metaphor into a class insult, and then into a name for a style of speech — the scoundrel's tongue.

Near-synonyms and boundaries

  • Bawdry: similar in being sexually explicit humour; differs because bawdry is often playful, earthy, and even affectionate — Chaucer's fabliaux are bawdy — whereas ribaldry is deliberately irreverent and jeering, contemptuous rather than fond.
  • Vulgarity: similar in being coarseness and bad taste; differs because vulgarity is the broadest term and may be wholly unconscious, whereas ribaldry is a deliberate, contemptuous debasement of speech aimed at something.
  • Profanity: similar in being improper speech; differs because profanity concerns forbidden words as such — swearing, taking holy things in vain — while ribaldry is not a matter of vocabulary but of a debased, mocking attitude.
  • Obscenity: similar in being indecent speech; differs because obscenity is defined by indecent or sexually explicit content, whereas ribaldry is defined by jeering irreverence, which may or may not be sexual.
  • Derision: similar in being contemptuous mockery; differs because derision names targeted ridicule generally, without the defining low-life register that makes ribaldry the scoundrel's tongue.
  • Mockery: similar in being ridicule of something; differs because mockery is neutral as to register — it can be witty or gentle — whereas ribaldry is always coarse and low.
  • Sarcasm: similar in being cutting, scornful speech; differs because sarcasm is verbal irony with a bite, often clever and pointed, whereas ribaldry is blunt, coarse, gutter-level derision without irony's refinement.
  • Satire: similar in attacking follies and vices; differs because satire corrects through wit and has a moral aim, whereas ribaldry merely degrades — satire is ribaldry's respectable cousin, and the two are often opposed.
  • Raillery: similar in being playful teasing; differs because raillery is good-humoured, jesting mockery among equals, whereas ribaldry is contemptuous and degrading, aimed at dragging its object down.
  • Scurrility: similar in being foul-mouthed abuse; differs because scurrility is abusive or defamatory language in general — the language of the scurra, the buffoon — while ribaldry emphasises coarse, irreverent jesting and mockery.

The word thus occupies a precise niche: obscene-and-scornful humour of the sort ascribed to rogues, with a social register as important as its indecency. Ruskin's use captures the full force — a press that has abandoned taste and truth, venting the language of the ribald on the most exalted subjects.
