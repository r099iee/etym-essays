nasturtians (noun, plural; singular: nasturtian) — An archaic, dialectal, and rustic English variant of nasturtiums, denoting trailing, climbing, or sprawling herbaceous annual plants of the genus Tropaeolum (most commonly Tropaeolum majus, native to the Andes of South America). The plant is celebrated for its lush, circular, peltate leaves with central petioles, and its brilliant spurred flowers that bloom in fiery shades of crimson, scarlet, copper, gold, and sunny yellow. Historically and culturally, the term encapsulates not merely a biological specimen, but a distinctive fixture of the traditional British cottage garden, known for cascading over dry stone dykes, rambling along turf embankments, and climbing rustic trellises.

Morphology, Growth Habit, and Botanical Context:

  • Vegetative Characteristics: The plants produce tender, succulent, trailing stems that can extend from one to three metres across open ground or climb upwards when supported by brushwood or masonry. The leaves are orbicular and peltate (umbrella-like), meaning the stem connects directly to the underside of the leaf blade rather than the margin, creating a striking lily-pad silhouette that repels water via a pronounced hydrophobic lotus effect.
  • Floral Structure: The flowers are zygomorphic, boasting five open petals with fringed claws and a long, nectar-filled backward spur that extends from the uppermost sepal. This nectar tube attracts long-tongued pollinators, primarily bumblebees and hummingbirds in their native Andean habitat.
  • Taxonomic Bifurcation: In formal taxonomy, the name Nasturtium designates a genus of the mustard family Brassicaceae (specifically watercress, Nasturtium officinale), whereas the ornamental garden flower belongs to the entirely unrelated family Tropaeolaceae. The shared popular name arose purely through culinary equivalence, as European botanists found the taste of the Andean plant indistinguishable from European watercress.

Culinary, Medicinal, and Practical Uses:

  • Gastronomic Utility: Every aerial part of the nasturtian is completely edible, distinguished by a lively, peppery, mustard-like sharpness caused by glucosinolates (mustard oil glycosides). The tender young leaves and vibrant petals are tossed into salads for visual splendor and piquant flavor, while the firm, unripened green three-lobed seed pods are traditionally brined or pickled in spiced malt vinegar as an economical domestic substitute for Mediterranean capers ("poor man's capers").
  • Herbal and Pharmacological Lore: Historically valued in folk medicine for high concentrations of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and sulfur compounds, the plant was utilized as an antiscorbutic against scurvy by sailors and as a natural antibacterial dressing for respiratory and urinary tract complaints.
  • Companion Planting in Permaculture: In organic horticulture, nasturtians are revered as exemplary trap crops. They attract hungry garden pests such as the caterpillars of the large white butterfly (Pieris brassicae) and black bean aphids away from prize brassicas and legumes, while their pungent root exudates are believed to deter subterranean nematodes.

Dialectal Evolution and Literary Nuance in Tolkien's Shire:

  • The Philological Shift from -ium to -ian: In standard British English of the 18th and 19th centuries, classical Latin plant endings in -ium were frequently naturalized by rural dialect speakers into -ian (or -ion), by analogy with words like gentian, valerian, and carnation. Country gardeners who did not possess classical Latin schooling readily adopted nasturtian as a native-sounding, fluid spoken term.
  • Tolkien's Deliberate Stylistic Choice: In The Fellowship of the Ring (1954), when describing Bag End on the eve of Bilbo Baggins's farewell banquet, J.R.R. Tolkien writes of "nasturtians trailing all over the turf walls and peeping in at the round windows." As an Oxford professor of English Language and philology, Tolkien deliberately opted for the dialectal spelling nasturtians over the formal Latinate nasturtiums. This subtle lexical choice instantly grounds the Shire in the cozy, unhurried, pastoral domesticity of rural Victorian and Edwardian England, characterizing hobbit culture as unpretentious, deeply rooted in the soil, and delightfully indifferent to pedantic academic Latinity.
