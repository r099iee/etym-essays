Dart names, in its oldest and most concrete sense, a small sharp missile meant to be thrown by hand: a light spear, a javelin, a shortened arrow. From that weapon the word grew a second life as a verb and noun of motion, and it is in that kinetic sense that the source sentence uses it: with a dart he sprang to the sill. Here dart is the sudden compact dash itself, a burst of directed speed, the body behaving like the missile, covering the distance to the window in one launched, unhesitating movement. The image fuses intention and velocity: no wandering, no preparation, just release and arrival.

The power of the word lies in that fusion of lightness and aim. Heavy things charge, lumber, and trundle; a dart does none of those. It is small mass at high speed on a straight line, and every figurative use preserves those three features. Eyes dart across a room, thoughts dart toward escape, swallows dart through evening air, and in each case the motion is quick, light, and pointed. To understand dart is to understand the poetics of the small and sudden: the mouse against the cat, the minnow against the pike, the messenger against the crowd.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A small thrown missile (oldest sense, medieval): a light spear or javelin hurled by hand, the ancestor of the pub game’s arrows and the blowpipe’s darts. This is the literal foundation: a pointed thing given speed by the arm, designed to pierce at a distance.
  • A sudden swift dash (the source sense, verb and noun from the 16th c.): to dart across the room, to make a dart for the door. The bridge is the missile’s flight transferred to the whole body: the runner becomes the projectile, the dash borrows the weapon’s straightness and surprise.
  • A quick directed glance or movement of the eyes (figurative, 17th c. onward): eyes darting nervously, a darting look. The bridge is speed plus aim: the gaze behaves like a missile, striking one point then another, too fast to intercept, betraying alertness or guilt.
  • A tapering fold stitched into clothing (dressmaking sense, 19th c.): darts shape a bodice by pinching fabric to a point. The bridge is visual: the stitched fold narrows to a sharp tip exactly like a missile head, a triangle of cloth echoing a triangle of steel.
  • The game of darts (modern sporting sense): small finned missiles thrown at a numbered board in pubs worldwide. The bridge is direct continuity: the medieval hand-missile domesticated into pastime, its name unchanged across six centuries.
  • A piercing sting of feeling (literary sense): darts of pain, darts of love, Cupid’s darts. The bridge runs through classical imagery: emotions strike from afar without warning, and the wound they leave is localised and sharp, like a puncture rather than a bruise. Renaissance sonneteers made this the standard figure of sudden passion.
  • A small fast fish or insect motion (natural-history extension): minnows darting in shallows, dragonflies darting over ponds. The bridge is observational: small creatures move exactly as missiles fly, in straight economical bursts, and the verb fits them better than run or swim.

Semantic bridges and register: the missile-to-motion metaphor that made the verb has never died because it encodes a truth about scale: small agents survive by suddenness, and English reaches for dart whenever the small outmanoeuvres the large. Register-wise the word is vivid but not slangy; it belongs to narrative prose at every level, from chronicles of battle to children’s adventure tales like the source sentence, where a dart to the window tells us the character is alert, slight, and quick. Contrast its near-synonyms: dash is heedless and may be heavy, rush suggests mass and noise, scurry implies undignified haste, while dart alone keeps the clean line of the arrow, purposeful, economical, and exact.
