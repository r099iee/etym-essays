• pierced: Similar because both are puncturings with a point; differs because “pierced” is broader and deeper — it can describe ears, hearts and armour, with no implication of smallness or speed.

  • stabbed: Similar because both are point-entry wounds; differs because stabbing is violent, deep and intent-driven (knife or sword), while pricking is minimal, precise and often accidental.

  • punctured: Similar because both make a hole with a point; differs because puncture is technical and describes the result (a punctured tyre, bladder), while prick stresses the act and the sensation.

  • jabbed: Similar because both are quick thrusts of a point; differs because a jab is heavier and more forceful (an elbow, a needle in injection), while a prick is the lightest touch of a point.

  • poked: Similar because both are small contacts with a point; differs because poking is a firm nudge for attention (poking a fire, poking a bear), without the sharp sting that pricking carries.

  • stung: Similar because both name a sharp, sudden pain; differs because stinging comes from a venomous barb (bee, nettle) and leaves a burning after-sensation; pricking is the clean entry of a dry point.

  • nicked: Similar because both are tiny injuries; differs because a nick is a small cut (blade, razor) that bleeds along a line, whereas a prick is a puncture that barely breaks the surface.

  • scratched: Similar because both are minor surface injuries; differs because scratching is a drawn-out abrasive graze, while pricking is a single instantaneous point-contact.

  • goaded: Similar because both urge or spur (goaded a horse, pricked a horse); differs because “goaded” is figurative and psychological today (goaded into action), while the equestrian prick is archaic and physical.

  • spurred: Similar because both derive from the image of the riding spur and mean “urged on”; differs because “spurred” is the common modern word for motivation (spurred by ambition), while “pricked” in this sense is obsolete and its surviving moral use is the conscience, not the motive.

  • wounded: Similar because both can describe hurt feelings; differs because a wound is deep, lasting and serious (physical or emotional), whereas a pricked feeling is a quick superficial sting that passes.
