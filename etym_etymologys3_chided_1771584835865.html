Chide is a word with a short but eventful biography - an island word, one whose recorded history begins already in English and nowhere else. The Old English verb cīdan appears in texts from the eighth century with the senses "to strive, contend, quarrel," and from there "to complain, to murmur, to find fault with." What is remarkable is that no cognate survives in Old Saxon, Old Norse, Old High German, Gothic, or any other Germanic language, and no Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed. The word seems to have been born - or at least to have survived - only on English soil; whether it was once wider and simply lost, or whether it was a dialectal creation, the record simply does not say. The most honest scholarly position is that given by the Oxford English Dictionary: cīdan is "of unknown origin." The boldest guess comes from Anatoly Liberman, who proposed a link to the Old High German *kîdal "wedge," imagining the sense evolution "brandishing sticks at someone" to "to scold, reprove." It is an attractive picture - the quarrel as a swinging of staves - but the comparative evidence is too thin to call it a true etymology, and so the word must still be classified as an isolate. The historical path is therefore internal to English, a genealogy written within a single island language. The recorded path shows a verb that survived conquest and settled into modern shape. Old English cīdan was a weak verb - inflected cīde, cīdde, cīdod - and it carried through the Norman Conquest to re-emerge in Middle English as chiden, first attested around 1175, at first intransitive ("to rail, to scold in the abstract") and only in the fourteenth century fully transitive ("to scold a particular person"). Two important things happen in this Middle English stage. First, the meaning narrows from "quarrel, contend" (a two-sided fight) to "scold, reprove" (a one-sided correction): the word loses its sense of mutual strife and becomes the voice of an authority addressing the erring. Second, the verb begins to drift into the strong conjugation, attracted by the pattern of ride / rode / ridden: alongside the regular weak forms appear the analogical past chid and participle chidden. These strong forms flourished in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries - Shakespeare writes "the air did chide" and uses the participial and past forms with freedom - and they survive today only as archaisms in literary style, while the regular chided became the standard modern form, the very form this card's rootWord takes. The social and literary fortunes of the word are as interesting as its form. In the medieval and Tudor periods chide is a vigorous, everyday verb with a wide emotional range: it can reprove a servant, a child, a lover, a wind, or a sea, and the "chiding" of natural forces is a favourite poetic figure - the chiding wind, the chiding wave, the chiding conscience. By the sixteenth century it is prominent in the Bible translations and in the drama, where it covers everything from a monarch's rebuke to a nurse's scolding. In the eighteenth century the verb begins its long softening. As scold, berate, and rebuke claimed the loud, long, and formal ends of the family, chide retreated to the gentle end: a brief, loving, corrective word, most naturally spoken by an elder to a child, increasingly bookish as the nineteenth century progressed. It is exactly this late softness that David Hawkes draws on in his translation of The Story of the Stone: when Grandmother Jia "chides indulgently" the beloved Bao-yu, the word carries a whole family dynamic - authority, affection, forgiveness, and special standing - that the stronger verbs of scolding could never supply. In modern English chide is literary and slightly old-fashioned, kept alive in prose and poetry precisely because of its mildness; it remains the word for the grandmother's tap, the gentle reproof that both corrects and cherishes, and it has been that word - narrowing, softening, and never quite dying - for twelve hundred years.
