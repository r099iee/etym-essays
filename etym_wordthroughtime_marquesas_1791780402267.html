- 1595–1770s: Spanish Imperial Toponym - Coined by Spanish navigator Álvaro de Mendaña de Neira as Las Marquesas de Mendoza to flatter his patron, the Viceroy of Peru; existed on early European maritime charts as an elusive, legendary Pacific sighting.
- 1774–1830s: Enlightenment Cartography & Oceanic Exploration - Re-entered European consciousness through Captain James Cook's second voyage (1774); perceived across the West as an untamed, perilous Polynesian wilderness inhabited by formidable, elaborately tattooed warrior societies.
- 1840s–1880s: Romantic Paradise & Cannibal Tropes - Sensationalized globally through Herman Melville's bestselling debut novel Typee (1846); viewed through the dual Victorian lenses of an uncorrupted Rousseauian Eden and a terrifying haunt of cannibal warriors, accompanied by French colonial annexation (1842) and catastrophic demographic collapse from introduced diseases.
- Late 19th–Mid 20th Century: Artists' Sanctuary & Elegiac Ruin - Romanticized as the ultimate remote refuge from Western industrial alienation by figures like painter Paul Gauguin, writer Robert Louis Stevenson, and singer Jacques Brel, while often elegized as a tragic, vanishing civilization.
- Contemporary Era: Cultural Renaissance & UNESCO World Heritage - Reclaimed by the indigenous population through linguistic revival, the revitalization of traditional patutiki tattooing and woodcarving, and formal inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage site honoring both its unique biodiversity and ancestral Polynesian culture.
