The form condemning is the present participle of condemn, and its story is the story of Roman loss becoming moral doom. Latin damnum meant loss, damage, expense, and fine; damnare meant to inflict such loss by judicial sentence, hence to condemn, convict, and doom. Prefixing intensive togetherness gave condemnare, to sentence together, to pronounce guilty as a body, to convict fully. Roman courts damnabant and condemnābant thieves, debtors, and enemies to fines, mines, beasts, and crosses; early Christians then turned the verb toward divine judgment, damnation as eternal loss.

As with its cousin indemnity, the ultimate root is plausibly the notion of cutting and allotting, whose cost becomes loss. What is cut away is gone; what is adjudged to be cut away is penalty. Roman law made this concrete: damnum iniuria datum, loss wrongfully caused, gave rise to action; damni infecti, threatened loss, required security; damnatus, condemned, meant one whose property and caput were diminished. To condemn was therefore originally to damage by decree.

  • From Rome through the Church to France: Ecclesiastical Latin spread condemnare for heresy and sin: condemnare haeresim, to condemn a heresy, condemnare ad inferna, to doom to hell. Old French took it as condemner in the 13th century for both courts and conscience: to sentence a thief, to blame a vice. Middle English borrowed condemnen at the same time in legal and religious prose, with spellings condemnen, condempnen influenced by damn. The double -mn- spelling preserves its Latin dignity against phonetic simplification.
  • Phonetic shaping: Latin condemnare with stress on -dem- became French condamner with nasalized vowel and silent -m-, then English condemn with silent -n- in the second syllable and stress on the second syllable. The present participle adds regular -ing with loss of final e where applicable, though condemn already ends in -mn. Received Pronunciation pronounces the first vowel weakly, the second with full /em/, and the -ing with velar nasal. The silent n is a learned spelling-pronunciation puzzle for learners.
  • Semantic broadening in English: Medieval condemn is overwhelmingly judicial and theological: condemned to death, condemned to hell, condemned cells, condemned souls. Early modern England extends it to official disapproval of things: to condemn houses as unfit, to condemn meat as unwholesome, to condemn books as dangerous. Eighteenth-century moral essayists complete the move to general censure: to condemn conduct, manners, extravagance. By the 19th century one can condemn without any court: critics condemn, conscience condemns, history condemns.
  • Grammar of condemning: As a participle and gerund, condemning inherits this full range. Condemning to slavery in the source sentence uses the oldest judicial sense: sentencing defaulting debtors and criminals to the status of slaves by legal decree. Modern condemning eyes, condemning report, condemning evidence use the newer censuring sense. The form can be progressive verb, adjective, or noun: they are condemning the policy, a condemning glance, condemning is easy but forgiving is hard.
  • Literary and moral weight: The King James Bible fixes phrases like condemned already and self-condemned. Milton, Bunyan, and Victorian novelists use condemned cell pathos. Modern human-rights discourse condemns torture, apartheid, and aggression, giving the word international legal force in United Nations resolutions. Its gravity comes from its Roman origin: to condemn is not merely to dislike but to inflict loss by authority, whether of life, liberty, property, or reputation.

Thus condemning in the source sentence is chillingly precise: debtors and criminals were not merely disapproved but sentenced, damaged by decree, their freedom cut away like damnum. Slavery extended not by accident but by judgment, and the participle captures the ongoing legal machinery that kept producing slaves.
