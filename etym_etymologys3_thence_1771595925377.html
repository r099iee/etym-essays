From pointing to concluding: the evolution of thence The biography of thence begins in the oldest machinery of language: the pointing word. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European demonstrative root *to-, a bare gesture of deixis meaning simply "that" — the far thing, the thing over there — as opposed to *so-, "this," the thing near the speaker. In the parent language the two stems were not separate words but one suppletive paradigm, exactly as in modern English this/that. From this single pointing root descends an astonishing share of our most basic grammar. The Greek definite article ho, hē, to is the *to- stem wearing a case-ending; Latin iste, "that one of yours," carries it fused inside; Sanskrit tat, "that," is it in its nakedest form; Russian tot and Polish ten are it again; and Hindi vah and Odia tā still point with it. In every branch of the family the same t- consonant marks the far demonstrative — the most persistent sound in Indo-European morphology, a consonant that has been pointing at distant things for five thousand years. In Germanic the *to- stem grew into the demonstrative and the definite article, and from it Germanic built a family of spatial adverbs on the pattern of "there-ness." The ancestor of our word was Proto-Germanic *þanana, an ablative adverb meaning "from there," formed from the stem *þan-, "that," with an ending of motion-away. Old English preserved it as þanon (also þanone), meaning "from that place, from that source" — already fully at home in the oldest English prose, where Alfred and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle use it of journeys and of consequences: the ships sail thence, the armies march thence, and from a king's death thence come troubles. Middle English added a final -s, the old adverbial genitive ending seen in once, twice, hence, whence, and thus, giving thennes; and by the fourteenth century the modern form thence was fixed. The word is thus a small museum of ancient grammar — a demonstrative, an ablative of motion, and a dead case-ending fused into one unanalysable particle. Its semantic career runs from space to time to logic. In the beginning thence was purely spatial: it answered "from where?" with a pointing gesture. The extension to time came early and naturally — "from that time" is "from that place in the course of events," and the compounds thenceforth and thenceforward, "from that time forward," are attested in Middle English and remain the standard elevated forms. The extension to cause and logic came as the word moved into the language of argument: if an effect comes from a cause, then it comes "thence"; if a conclusion follows from a premise, it follows "thence." By early modern English the word had settled into the full modern polysemy — place, source, time, and consequence — and the Renaissance grammarians recognised hence, thence, and whence as a tidy trio of "from-this, from-that, from-which" adverbs, the demonstrative and interrogative members of a single pointing system.
  • Sound changes: the Proto-Germanic cluster *þan- passed into Old English as þan-; the -n- of the stem and the nasal of the ending produced the -nn- of Middle English thennes; the final -s is the adverbial genitive, the same ending that gives English once, twice, hence, whence, and thus their forms.
  • Semantic drift: from pure spatial deixis ("from that place") to temporal ("from that time") to causal and logical ("from that source, therefore") — a classic migration from the concrete to the abstract, in which the grammar of space becomes the grammar of argument.
  • Socio-cultural context: the legal and clerical register of the word kept it alive in formal prose long after ordinary speech had abandoned it; lawbooks, wills, and Acts of Parliament use thence and its compounds to this day, so that the word has become a deliberate archaism signalling judicial precision.
  • Literary attestations: the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle uses þanon of real journeys; the King James Bible and Shakespeare use thence of both places and consequences; and Ruskin's Preface gives the word its most delicate modern service, making it the pivot of a logical disclaimer — "from that claim, you may infer."
In contemporary English thence survives as a bookish, formal, and faintly archaic word, most at home in legal instruments, antiquarian prose, and sentences written to sound scrupulous. But its grammatical bones are among the most ancient in the language: the same demonstrative pointing gesture that a Proto-Indo-European speaker used to indicate a distant object still structures the word when a modern writer says "thence, to infer." The word is a fossil that still points — and in Ruskin's hands it points with courtroom exactness at the clause from which his reader must not too hastily depart.
