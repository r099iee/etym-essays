Linear derivation chain for SWOOPED (root word: swoop): 1. Proto-Germanic *swaipaną "to sweep, to swing, to move with a driving swishing motion" (c. 2nd half of the 1st millennium BCE to early 1st millennium CE; the root survives in Gothic *sweipan, Old Norse sveipa "to sweep, to wrap", Old Saxon swepan, Old High German sweifan). 2. Old English swāpan (class VII strong verb, past sweop) "to sweep, to rush, to sweep along", attested from the 8th c.; in Beowulf the past form sweop is used of a flame sweeping over a hall, already carrying the sense of a rapid, engulfing motion. 3. Middle English swopen/soupen "to sweep, to swing, to move in a curve; to rush down upon", attested from the 12th–15th c. (e.g., in the Ormulum and Lagamon's Brut), with the prepositional construction "soupen upon" marking the transition to the attacking sense. 4. Modern English swoop (16th c.) — the hawk's verb: "to descend swiftly and steeply from above upon prey", first fully established in falconry contexts; the past-tense form swooped is regular in Modern English from the 17th c. onward. Final node: SWOOPED. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGE: no PIE etymon can be securely reconstructed for *swaipaną; the root is usually treated as an isolated West/North Germanic form with possible but uncertain connections to Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic roots meaning "to turn, to swing" (cf. Sanskrit svápate? — rejected; the standard handbooks instead compare Old Norse sveipa and, more distantly, Greek sobes "to drive, to chase"). The relationship between swoop, sweep, swipe, and swoon is a genuine family: all go back to the same Germanic base *swīp-/swaip- with vowel alternation, but the exact derivational steps between them are not fully recoverable. The OED treats "swoop" as historically the same word as "sweep", split by a vowel difference and by the specialization of meaning — a nuance worth noting rather than a clean split.
