heaths is the plural of heath, a noun naming an area of open, uncultivated land, especially in Britain, covered with low, hardy vegetation — heather, gorse, bracken, and coarse grasses — rather than trees. In the passage, the young rider "saw on every side unknown woods, wild heaths, and blue mountains": the plural pictures, in one word, the open, windswept, trackless scrubland that covers great stretches of the British landscape. The nuance is spaciousness and exposure: heaths are open to the sky, swept by wind, often high and dry, with thin acidic soils in which only tough, low-growing plants thrive. They are the country of the shepherd, the traveller, the outlaw, and the solitary walker.

Primary sense (landscape): a heath is treeless, exposed, and largely unenclosed — the wild margin of settled country, land too poor or too rough to plow. It is distinguished from forest and field alike:

  • Vegetation: the classic heath is clothed in heather (Calluna and Erica), gorse (furze), bracken, bilberry, and wiry grasses, studded with the gold of gorse flowers in spring.
  • Place-names: the word survives massively in English place-names — Hampstead Heath, Blackheath, Putney Heath — where the name often outlives the wilderness it commemorates.
  • Ecology: modern ecology distinguishes lowland heath (heathland) as a semi-natural habitat of high conservation value, maintained by grazing and periodic burning against the encroachment of birch and pine.

Secondary sense (the plant): by metonymy, "heath" is also the name of the plants themselves — the heaths (genus Erica, and related heathers) — low evergreen shrubs of the heath family (Ericaceae). Gardeners speak of "heaths and heathers" as ornamental, acid-loving shrubs. The bridge from land to plant is natural: the land is named for its characteristic vegetation, and then the plant takes the land's name.

Literary and figurative uses:

  • Wildness and solitude: the heath is the great English symbol of exposed, untamed nature — the setting of Thomas Hardy's Egdon Heath in The Return of the Native (1878), "a tract of unenclosed wild" where the prehistoric past still breathes.
  • Social margin: historically the heath was the place of the outlaw, the vagrant, and the gathering of the disaffected — outside the law's enclosure and the settled church.
  • Figurative barrenness: "heath" and its kin can stand for bareness, desolation, or spiritual wilderness — "a heath of the heart," in poetic and rhetorical writing.
  • Heathen link: the very word heathen ("dweller on the heath") was built from heath — the church's term for those who lived beyond the settled, Christianized fields, on the wild waste.

Register and nuance: heath is a word of the English landscape, concrete and topographical, with a strong Romantic and antiquarian glow. The plural heaths (as here) stresses multiplicity and extent — not one wild place but wild country everywhere the rider looks. The word is unmistakably British in atmosphere: an American would more naturally say scrubland or moor, and the British heath carries with it a particular weather — thin rain, gorse-gold, and the cry of birds over open ground. It is at once a precise ecological term and a literary emblem: it names a real piece of England and a state of the soul.

Historical semantic bridge: the word has meant "untilled waste land" since Old English hǣð (9th century), and it has never shifted its core: it is the ancient, everyday name for the open scrub beyond the plow. Its durability lies in that double identity — an ecosystem and an image of wild exposure — and in the thousand-year chain of place-names, poems, and novels that have kept the word rooted in the actual ground of the British Isles.
