The story of assail begins with a leap. The reconstructed PIE root *sel-, meaning to leap, spring, or dance, runs beneath a family of lively Latin words: salire, to leap, saltare, to dance, saliens, leaping, and the compound assilire, formed with the prefix ad-, toward, meaning to leap upon or spring at someone. Roman writers used assilire both for soldiers flinging themselves at the enemy and, in a transferred sense already classical, for speakers launching into vehement argument, so the physical-to-verbal bridge that still defines the English word was poured by Latin hands two thousand years ago.

As Vulgar Latin softened consonants and refashioned verbs, assilire was reshaped on the model of verbs in -aillir into Old French assaillir, to attack or storm, a favourite of chansons de geste in which knights assail castles, giants, and each other's honour with metronomic regularity. The Norman Conquest carried it across the Channel around 1200 as Middle English asailen, and since the Normans supplied England's military vocabulary, asailen arrived with full knightly prestige, storming chronicles, romances, and statutes alike.

Centuries of assault:

  • Medieval England — the siege engine of words: alongside battering walls, writers set the devil assailing souls and litigants assailing verdicts, proving how fast the leap metaphor colonized mind and courtroom.
  • Phonetic smoothing — consonants settle: the Old French double -ss- and the ending -aillir simplified to the clean English assail, stressed on the second syllable, while the noun assault, borrowed separately from French assaut, kept its own terminal -t.
  • Reformation and civil war — polemic's heyday: pamphleteers assailed popes, kings, and doctrines in tracts whose very titles declared war, and Milton's devils assail heaven's gates, fixing the word in the high style forever.
  • Enlightenment inward turn — assailed nerves: Richardson, Sterne, and the Romantics let fears, doubts, and blushes assail their characters, mapping the fortress onto the self and giving psychology its favourite siege verb.
  • Victorian empire — despatches and novels: war correspondents reported columns assailed on the march while Dickensian odours assailed nostrils, the sensory sense now fully naturalized.
  • Modern usage — the literary reserve: plain attack dominates speech, law, and medicine, leaving assail to fantasy quests, solemn journalism, and the inward life, where to be assailed still dignifies suffering with courage.

Socio-culturally the word preserves the aristocratic memory of warfare as personal daring, the single champion's leap, inside an age of distant impersonal violence. Its etymology is therefore a small monument: every modern doubt that assails a sleeper descends, leap by leap, from a Roman soldier springing at the foe.
