- Medieval sense - despair names the sin of hopelessness, the abandonment of hope in divine salvation; a spiritual condition with heavy religious weight, not merely an emotion
- 16th–18th c. - the word secularises: to despair is to lose all hope in any worldly matter, and despair becomes the felt, utter absence of hope rather than a low hope
- 19th–20th c. - "to despair of" a person or outcome grows common, then recedes; the verb turns chiefly literary, while medicine and psychology split "hopelessness" (the state) from "despair" (the felt agony)
- Contemporary - "I have often despaired" is used bare and intransitive, naming recurring troughs in a life's emotional rhythm; its gravity still faintly echoes the old theological sin, which is exactly why recovery from it feels redemptive
