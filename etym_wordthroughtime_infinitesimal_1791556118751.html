- 17th c. — Coining in mathematical Latin: Formed in Modern Latin as infinitesimus (by mathematician Nicholas Mercator in 1668, modeled on ordinals like centesimus) and popularized across Europe by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz to designate differential increments in calculus.
- 18th c. — Philosophical controversy and literary adoption: Entering English in the early 1700s, the term sparked famous philosophical and theological controversy (Bishop George Berkeley mocked infinitesimals as 'the ghosts of departed quantities'); concurrently began filtering into philosophical prose as a descriptor for boundless minuteness.
- 19th c. — Epsilon-delta revolution: Banished from foundational mathematical calculus by Augustin-Louis Cauchy, Karl Weierstrass, and Richard Dedekind in favor of rigorous limit definitions ($\epsilon$-$\delta$), while simultaneously exploding in general literature and journalism as an indispensable word for negligible, microscopic quantities.
- 20th c. — Logical vindication and colloquial entrenchment: Vindicated within pure mathematics by Abraham Robinson's 1960 development of non-standard analysis; firmly established in modern English as both a precise mathematical concept and standard figurative vocabulary for vanishingly tiny fractions of space, time, or probability.
