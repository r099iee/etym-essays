sieges (n., plural of siege) — In Macaulay's History of England (1848–61), the word opens the catalogue of conventional military-political history that he disclaims: he would "very imperfectly execute the task" he has undertaken, he says, if he "were merely to treat of battles and sieges, of the rise and fall of administrations, of intrigues in the palace, and of debates in the parliament." The pairing "battles and sieges" is a set phrase of old-fashioned chronicle history: sieges were the great set-pieces of pre-modern warfare, standing for the patient, methodical reduction of fortresses that dominated campaigns. Core sense. A siege is the formal military operation of surrounding and blockading a fortified place — a town, castle, city, or fortified building — cutting off its supply of food, water, men, and information, and compelling surrender through starvation, bombardment, mining, sapping, and assault. It is distinguished from a pitched battle (an open-field engagement) by its duration, its methodical investment, and its aim: not to destroy an army in the field but to reduce a place. The plural sieges denotes such operations collectively — the sum of a war's fortress-campaigns. All primary senses.
  • (1) Military operation (count noun). The investment of a fortified place by an army that surrounds it and cuts it off. "The siege of Vienna," "the siege of Leningrad."
  • (2) Figurative: relentless pressure or harassment (count noun). From the 17th century, any prolonged encircling pressure: "a city under siege by disease," "under siege from reporters," "the company is under siege from competitors."
  • (3) Prolonged distressing period of illness (count noun, now chiefly dated/colloquial). "A siege of the flu," "a siege of gout" — a long bout of illness that "lays siege" to the body. Attested from the 18th century.
  • (4) Obsolete/archaic: a seat, throne, or position. The original English sense (13th–17th c.), from Old French sege "seat": "the king's siege" = the king's throne; also a fixed seat in a church or a position of dignity.
  • (5) A seat in the body — historical jocular sense. The old sense of "seat" also produced the now-facetious "siege" for the backside ("a siege of ease" = a privy seat); this etymologically literal meaning survives only in old texts.
Modern jargon and metaphoric shifts.
  • Law enforcement and counter-terrorism. "Siege" is standard for operations in which armed suspects barricade themselves in a building ("a hostage siege," "a seven-hour siege"): SWAT operations and barricade situations.
  • Sport and competition. A team "lays siege" to the opponent's goal — sustained, unrelenting pressure, not a single attack.
  • Medicine and public health. "A body under siege" from infection; the metaphor of a city "under siege" from an epidemic.
  • Computer security. A network "under siege" from cyberattack; "siege warfare" borrowed as a metaphor for prolonged coordinated attacks.
  • Games and strategy. Chess and war-game language uses "siege" (and "siege engines") for the reduction of a fortified position.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The entire semantic career is one image: an army that sits down before a town. The root is Latin sedere "to sit," via Vulgar Latin *sedicum "seat" and Old French sege/siege "seat, throne." The military meaning arose in Old French when "to sit before a place" (assiégier, "to besiege") described an army that took up a fixed position — a "seat" — in front of a town and refused to move until the town capitulated. The shift from throne to blockade is therefore not a metaphor of violence but of patient occupation: to besiege is to settle in and wait. All the later senses — harassment, illness, pressure — extend that image of implacable, encircling persistence to non-military besiegers. Usage notes.
  • Collocations: "to lay siege to," "to withstand a siege," "to raise a siege," "under siege," "siege works," "siege engines," "state of siege" (= martial law in some jurisdictions).
  • Register: military-historical core; figurative uses are common in journalism; "state of siege" is a technical legal phrase (French état de siège).
  • Related forms: besiege (verb, from Old French assiegier), besieger, besiegement, siegecraft.
  • Distinguish from blockade (cutting off, usually naval, without reduction) and assault (the violent storming, the climax, not the investment) — see similarWords.
