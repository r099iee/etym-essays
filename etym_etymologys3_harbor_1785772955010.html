The story of harbor begins as a compound of war and protection. Proto-Germanic speakers could form nouns built on *harjaz "army, host" and the verb *bergan "to protect, shelter, keep safe," producing something like *harja-bergō: literally a "protection-place for a host," the quarters where an army or a company of travelers found shelter on the march. The two elements reach back into Proto-Indo-European — *koro- "war, army, host" (seen in Greek koiranos "commander" and the Baltic word for war) and *bhergh- "to hide, protect, preserve" (seen in the Germanic family of beorgan/bergen, "to save, hide, recover"). Neither reconstruction is beyond dispute, but the Germanic compound and its daughters are solidly attested.

Old English had herebeorg "lodging, shelter, inn"; Old Norse had herbergi "inn, room, lodging"; and the cognates survive across the Germanic world to this day — German Herberge "inn, hostel" (as in Jugendherberge, "youth hostel"), Dutch herberg "inn," Swedish härbärge and Danish herberge "hostel, shelter." All of them preserve the ancient core: a place that takes people in and keeps them safe. Middle English inherited the word as herberwe (also herberwe, herberge, harborwe) from the twelfth century, meaning "shelter, lodging, inn, refuge," and formed a verb herberwen "to lodge, to give shelter to" in the thirteenth century. In Chaucer's English one could be "herberwed" — housed, sheltered, given lodging.

The decisive specialization came with England's maritime expansion. As the noun sense settled on the sheltered water where ships lie safe from storm — the harbor as a haven from the open sea — the maritime image became the dominant meaning in Modern English by around 1500. But the verb remained far wider: one harbors ships in a port, but also harbors guests, fugitives, or animals, and in the early modern period writers freely extended the verb inward. In the Bible and devotional prose, and then in Shakespeare and his contemporaries, the breast or mind "harbors" thoughts, fears, treasonous designs, or tender hopes. The container is no longer only a building or a bay; it is the self. This psychological extension is the single most important semantic event in the word's career after the maritime specialization.

Modern usage keeps all the layers active. Legal statutes criminalize "harboring" fugitives and criminals; humanitarian language speaks of harboring refugees; epidemiology says reservoir hosts harbor pathogens; political rhetoric accuses states of harboring militants; ordinary psychology speaks of harboring a grudge or ill will. American harbor and British harbour differ only in spelling (the -our spelling is the French-influenced norm in Britain; -or became standard in the United States through Noah Webster's reforms). Throughout the biography the deep structure is constant: an enclosed interior that protects and sustains what it holds — fleet, refugee, microbe, or unspoken resentment — against a more dangerous outside.

  • Compound origin: PGmc *harja-bergō "shelter for a host/army" from *harjaz "army" + *bergan "to protect, save" — a place that takes people in.
  • PIE inputs: *koro- "war, army, host" (debated) and *bhergh- "hide, protect, preserve" (secure in Germanic); both reconstructions noted as debated.
  • Germanic daughters: OE herebeorg, ON herbergi, German Herberge, Dutch herberg, Scandinavian härbärge/herberge — all preserve "inn/hostel/refuge."
  • Middle English: herberwe (12th c.) "lodging, refuge"; verb herberwen (13th c.) "to lodge, shelter"; the form shortens toward harbor.
  • Maritime specialization (c. 1500): the noun shifts to sheltered water for ships; the verb keeps the wider sheltering sense.
  • Psychological extension (early modern): the mind or breast "harbors" thoughts, fears, and desires — the lodging metaphor turned inward.
  • Modern layers: legal harboring of fugitives, humanitarian harboring of refugees, medical harboring of pathogens, political harboring of militants; American harbor vs. British harbour.
