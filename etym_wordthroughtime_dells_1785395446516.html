- [Old English period: a native hollow] - dell (OE dell, kin to dale) is plain native English for a small hollow or depression in the ground — a word of the shepherd and the ploughman for sunken land, without refinement or literary air.
- [Middle English: the poets' diminutive] - alongside dale, dell takes the small end of the scale — a little hollow, often among trees — and, small and sheltered, drifts into alliterative verse and early English poetry, taking a gentle, homely, almost domestic colouring.
- [18th–19th century: the antiquarian revival] - as dialect and archaizing poets reach back for native landscape words, dell is revived as a consciously literary word for a secluded, tree-locked hollow; the OED marks it chiefly archaic or dialectal, and its sweetness shades toward the eerie — a place to hide in.
- [20th century: fantasy and place-names] - Tolkien (1937) presses the plural into the descending topographical sentence of Mirkwood, where dells below the path's level are overhung with bushes and low trees — secretive, shadowed pockets; meanwhile “the Dells” survive as scenic place-names (as in Wisconsin), fixing the word's picture of natural scenery.
- [Contemporary sense] - dell is emphatically literary, a poet's and place-namer's word for a small, wooded, secluded hollow — intimate, sheltering, and faintly ominous, a valley so small it can both harbour and hide.
