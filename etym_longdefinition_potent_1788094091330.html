Potent (adjective) means “having great power, influence, or effect; strong, mighty, forceful”. A potent ruler commands; a potent argument persuades; a potent drug or draught acts strongly; a potent smell overwhelms. The word measures concentrated effectiveness: it is used of anything whose power is great in proportion to its size, dose, or surface. In the source context — “It must be potent wine to make a wood-elf drowsy” — the wine’s power is shown by its effect: ordinary wine would not touch an elf, so the wine that does must be exceptionally strong; the word quantifies efficacy by result.

Primary senses

  • Of persons and power — “a potent monarch”, “a potent warlord”: mighty, powerful, able to command; now slightly archaic or literary, replaced in ordinary speech by powerful or influential, but alive in formal and historical prose.
  • Of arguments, forces, and influences — “a potent argument”, “a potent force”, “potent symbolism”: effective, compelling, able to produce a strong effect on minds or events.
  • Of drugs, poisons, medicines, and drinks — “a potent poison”, “potent wine”, “a potent remedy”: strong in effect per dose; the most common modern use, and the sense in Tolkien’s “It must be potent wine to make a wood-elf drowsy”.
  • Of smells, tastes, and sensory effects — “a potent aroma”, “potent spices”: intense, concentrated, overwhelming to the senses.

Secondary and specialised senses

  • Physiology and medicine — of a male: capable of sexual intercourse or reproduction; the opposite impotent names erectile dysfunction; the noun potency measures the strength of a drug per unit dose (pharmacology) and of sperm (fertility medicine).
  • Heraldry — potent is a heraldic fur: a repeating pattern of T-shaped (crutch-shaped) figures; from Old French potence “crutch” (Latin potentia “power”), because a crutch is a staff of support; the related noun potent (now obsolete) meant “a crutch or staff”.
  • Mathematics and logic (via potential) — potential, from Latin potentia “power, capacity”, means “existing in possibility, not yet actual”; in physics, potential energy and electric potential quantify stored or available power. The contrast “potent (actual) vs potential (possible)” runs through the whole word family.
  • Semantics of degree — potent is a degree word: it admits comparison (“more potent”, “most potent”) and intensification (“highly potent”, “extremely potent”), and it pairs with potency (noun) and potently (adverb).

Semantic bridge: Latin potens is the present participle of posse “to be able”, itself a contraction of potis esse “to be master of, to be able” — from potis “able, powerful, masterful”, cognate with Sanskrit páti- “master, lord” and Greek pósis “husband, master”. The word family’s core idea is mastery: the ability to do something because one is in control of it. From “being able”, English derived “being effective”: what is potent is what can actually do something — a potent wine can put an elf to sleep, a potent poison can kill, a potent argument can change a mind. The modern shift is from personal mastery (the potent ruler) to impersonal efficacy (the potent drug), but the underlying idea — power that shows itself in results — is unchanged.

Register and nuance

  • Potent is formal-to-neutral; in speech, strong or powerful usually replaces it, but in writing about drinks, drugs, and forces it is the precise idiomatic choice.
  • It differs from powerful by suggesting concentration and efficacy rather than sheer size or capacity; from strong by suggesting directed, effective power rather than mere resistance; from forceful by being statable of things as much as persons.
  • Word family: potency, potentate (a ruler, from Late Latin potentātus “dominion”), omnipotent (all-powerful), potential, impotent, puissant (via French from the same Latin stem).
