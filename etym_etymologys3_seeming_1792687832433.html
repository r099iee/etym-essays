1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and the Concept of Unity. The remote linguistic ancestor of seeming is the Proto-Indo-European root *sem-, meaning "one; as one, together with". This prolific root is the ultimate parent of an extraordinary constellation of words across the Indo-European family expressing sameness, unity, resemblance, and fitness. In Sanskrit, it yielded sama- ("same, equal, level") and sam- ("together with"); in Ancient Greek, it gave hama ("at the same time, together with") and homós ("one and the same"); in Classical Latin, it produced similis ("like, resembling"), semper ("always, continuously"), and simul ("at the same time"); and in Proto-Germanic, it generated *samaz ("same", whence modern English same).

2. Germanic Evolution and the Notion of "Fittingness". In the Germanic branch, the root developed a specialized adjectival derivative in Proto-Germanic: *sōmiz or *sōmija-, literally signifying "bringing together as one; agreeing with; fitting, suitable, becoming, or agreeable". In Gothic, this appears as sams ("agreeing"), and in Old English, it produced the noun sōm ("agreement, reconciliation, concord") and the derivative adjective gesōm ("unanimous, reconciled").

However, modern English seem and its participle seeming do not descend directly from Old English sōm. Rather, they entered the English linguistic bloodstream through intense contact with the North Germanic branch following the Viking invasions. In Old Norse, the Germanic base *sōmija- developed into the adjective sœmr ("fitting, becoming, handsome, suitable") and its primary derived weak causative verb sœma, meaning "to befit, honor, conform to, or be suitable for" (cognate with modern Icelandic sæma and Swedish söma).

3. The Scandinavian Danelaw Influx and Middle English Adoption. During the 9th, 10th, and 11th centuries, large areas of northern and eastern England were settled by Danish and Norwegian Vikings under the legal jurisdiction known as the Danelaw. The intimate bilingual coexistence between Old English and Old Norse speakers led to the absorption of hundreds of core Scandinavian words into everyday speech. Around the year 1200, the Old Norse verb sœma was adopted into the northern and East Midland dialects of Early Middle English as semen.

Crucially, when semen first appeared in early Middle English texts (such as the *Ormulum*, c. 1200), its primary meaning was identical to its Old Norse parent: "to be suitable, proper, becoming, or befitting". One spoke of an action that "seemed a king" (meaning it was appropriate or becoming for a monarch). This original sense is preserved in modern English in the adjectives seemly ("appropriate, decorous") and unseemly ("indecorous, inappropriate").

4. The Extraordinary Semantic Shift: From "Befitting" to "Appearing". During the course of the 13th century, Middle English semen underwent one of the most remarkable semantic shifts in English lexicography. The transition followed a natural psychological bridge:

  • If a garment or a behavior befits or suits a person, it presents an agreeable, becoming external appearance to the eye of the beholder.
  • From "presenting a becoming appearance", the verb shifted naturally toward "presenting a particular appearance or impression to the observer" (c. 1250–1300).
  • By the early 14th century, the impersonal construction it seemeth me ("it appears to me") became widespread, evolving into the general cognitive sense of "to appear to be, to look like, to give the outward impression of".

By the late 14th century, the present participle and verbal noun seminge or seemynge had become fully established in the literary works of Geoffrey Chaucer (The Canterbury Tales, Troilus and Criseyde) and John Gower (Confessio Amantis). In Chaucer, seeming already carried its modern dual character: both an objective sensory impression and a potentially deceptive outward show (*"in my seemynge"* meaning "according to my judgment or observation").

5. Renaissance Drama and the Great Elizabethan Crisis of Appearance. In the 16th and early 17th centuries, the noun and adjective seeming became central to Renaissance literature. The Reformation, the rise of Machiavellian political theory, and the emergence of professional theater in London ignited deep anxieties about the discrepancy between inward conscience and outward performance. William Shakespeare made seeming a recurring thematic obsession. In Hamlet (Act I, Scene 2), when Queen Gertrude asks why the death of his father "seems... so particular" to him, Hamlet indignantly retorts:

"Seems, madam? Nay, it is; I know not 'seems.'
'Tis not alone my inky cloak, good mother...
Together with all forms, moods, shapes of grief,
That can denote me truly: these indeed 'seem,'
For they are actions that a man might play."

Here, Shakespeare explicitly polarizes seeming against authentic ontological truth, branding it as the theatrical mask of courtly dissimulation.

6. Romantic Transfiguration: Wordsworth and the Poetry of Perception. Two centuries later, William Wordsworth rescued seeming from Shakespearean cynicism. In Book I of The Prelude, describing the serene joy of his rural life at Grasmere, Wordsworth marvels at the "endless store of things, / Rare, or at least so seeming, every day / Found all about me in one neighbourhood". Wordsworth recognizes that the "seeming" is not a dishonest counterfeit, but the very mechanism of poetic epiphany. To the sympathetic imagination, everyday stones, clouds, and peasant cottages "seem" rare, holy, and eternal. In Wordsworth's hands, the word completed a full philosophical circle: originating from an ancient root of oneness (*sem-), evolving into Scandinavian fitness (*sœma*), transforming into the English appearance of things, challenged as a deceptive theatrical mask in the Renaissance, and finally crowned in Romantic poetry as the blessed lens through which the human mind communes with the divine in nature.
