- [Vedic Era (c. 1500–800 BCE)] - celebrated in the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda as an animate, consecrated weapon of divine power and the sacred voice of Indra; struck in royal sacrifices (Rājasūya, Mahāvrata) to sanctify the realm and terrify hostile tribes - [perception: revered as a holy, divine acoustic entity possessing autonomous protective magic]
- [Epic & Classical Sanskrit Period (c. 500 BCE–500 CE)] - codified in the Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa as the quintessential battlefield instrument alongside conches and bherīs; concurrently idealized as celestial drums (deva-dundubhis) sounding spontaneously in heaven to mark divine interventions - [perception: recognized as the universal symbol of heroic valor (vīrya), martial majesty, and divine celebration]
- [Classical Musicological Codification (c. 200 BCE–1000 CE)] - classified in Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra and later Sangīta texts under avanaddha-vādya (percussion membranophones) as the ancient standard of heavy, solemn, and theatrical heroic rhythm - [perception: admired by courtly dramatists and musicians as the venerable ancestor of dramatic percussion]
- [Modern Indological & Contemporary Epic English (19th–21st c.)] - anglicized with plural -s in English translations of the Sanskrit epics, and studied by ethnomusicologists as the ancestral forebear of traditional South Asian martial percussion like the nagada - [perception: celebrated in world literature as the iconic voice of ancient Indian epic warfare and sacred ceremony]
