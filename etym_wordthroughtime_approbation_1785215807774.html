- 14th–16th century - Enters English from church and legal Latin as “proved good, sanctioned”; carries a tested, authoritative flavour — approval after judgement.
- 17th–19th century - Becomes the dignified word for formal approval; widespread in moral, theological, and parliamentary prose, where “with the approbation of…” signals weighty, considered endorsement.
- 20th century - Retreats from ordinary speech as plain “approval” takes over; “approbation” acquires a literary, slightly archaic elevation.
- Contemporary - Survives in formal and academic registers, where its tested, weighty connotation is precisely what separates it from casual “approval”.
