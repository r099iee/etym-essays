Legislated is a Renaissance and Enlightenment word in its ancestry, though its immediate English form is surprisingly recent. Its root lies in Latin lēx (genitive lēgis), "law, statute, contract", the word that gives English legal, legitimate, privilege and legislation. The Romans did not use a single verb meaning "to legislate"; law-making was described through phrases such as legem ferre, "to carry a law", and the person who proposed a bill was a legis lātor, literally a "bearer of the law". In that compound, lātor is an agent noun from lātus, the suppletive past participle of ferre "to carry" — the same irregular verb that supplies English transfer, refer and relate.

Late Latin formed legislātor, "lawgiver", by treating legis lātor as a single noun; Medieval Latin added the verb legislāre, "to make laws". This is a characteristic medieval pattern: an ancient phrase is resegmented, a new denominative verb is coined, and the whole family is then borrowed wholesale by the modern languages. French produced législation in the seventeenth century; English took it in the 1650s, at first as a learned term for the body of enacted law and the activity of enacting it.

The missing verb

  • 1650s: English legislation is attested, but there is no matching verb. Speakers say "to make laws", "to enact", "to pass a statute".
  • 1719: Legislate appears, almost certainly a back-formation from legislation — the noun was stripped of its suffix to produce a verb, a common pattern in English (edit from editor, diagnose from diagnosis). An alternative explanation derives it directly from Latin legis lātor, but the timing and morphology favour the back-formation.
  • 18th–19th c.: The verb becomes standard in parliamentary and constitutional writing. "To legislate for" a colony, a trade or a class is a phrase of empire and reform; "legislation" and "legislature" supply the institutional nouns.
  • 20th c.: The verb acquires its critical idioms. "You cannot legislate morality" is an old idea given new currency in the debates between Lord Devlin and H. L. A. Hart over the Wolfenden Report (1957); "legislating from the bench" becomes a staple of American judicial politics.
  • 21st c.: Legislated is most often met as a participle-adjective — legislated rates, legislated mandates, legislated targets — especially in climate, labour and health policy.

Semantic structure

The verb's meaning has remained remarkably stable: it denotes the exercise of the legislative power. What has changed is the range of subjects and the evaluative charge. In the eighteenth century, to legislate was a dignified act of sovereign or parliamentary will. In the twentieth, it could be a term of reproach — for moral paternalism or for judicial activism. The participle legislated adds a further nuance: it describes a state of affairs that exists because a statute created it, as opposed to one produced by custom, contract or market. Hence "a legislated monopoly" is a monopoly created by law, and "legislated equality" is a sceptical phrase implying that equality exists on paper only.

Grammar and derivatives

Because legislate is a back-formation, its derivatives are unevenly distributed. Legislator and legislature are older, borrowed directly from Latin; legislation is seventeenth-century French; legislative is an English adjective from the same family. The verb itself is weak and regular, so legislated never alternates in form. Its syntax is flexible: intransitive with on, for or against; transitive in American legal usage; and passive in formulations such as "the standard was legislated into existence".

Why the word matters here

The reading contrasts two ways of making law: the slow growth of custom and the deliberate act of a lawgiver. Legislated names the second. When the text says Hammurabi legislated "in such terms", it attributes to him both the authority to fix a tariff and the intention to publish it as law. The word thus carries the passage's central argument — that law begins when compensation is scheduled, written down and enforced by a sovereign rather than left to the vendetta.
