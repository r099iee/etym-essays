Amicable is an adjective meaning “friendly, peaceable, marked by goodwill”, and it is used almost exclusively of relations, arrangements, and outcomes between parties rather than of individual temperament. An amicable settlement, divorce, parting, or discussion is one conducted without hostility and usually without litigation. The word is formal, slightly cool, and carries an implicit contrast: relations are called amicable precisely when hostility was a real possibility.

In the source context the phrase is “some amicable settlement of the dispute”, describing what boards of voluntary conciliation arranged. The adjective modifies the settlement, not the disputants: it tells us that the outcome was reached by consent and goodwill rather than imposed by a judgment. This is the word's modern centre of gravity — the peaceful, agreed resolution of a difference — and it already implies the existence of the difference it resolves.

Core senses

  • Peaceable, non-hostile (of relations or proceedings). “The two states maintain amicable relations”; “the negotiations were amicable throughout”. The emphasis is on the absence of animosity, not on warmth.
  • Reached by consent rather than coercion (of settlements and agreements). An amicable settlement is one the parties agree to, typically avoiding or ending litigation. In law this is close to a term of art.
  • Friendly in manner, but in a restrained, formal register. “An amicable exchange of views” suggests courtesy and willingness to cooperate, not intimacy or affection.

Legal and diplomatic uses

  • Amicable settlement. In English practice, a settlement agreed between parties, often before or during proceedings, as opposed to a judgment. Family lawyers advertise “amicable divorce” and “amicable separation” to promise a process without adversarial combat.
  • International law. Article 33 of the UN Charter speaks of the “peaceful settlement of disputes” and lists negotiation, enquiry, mediation, conciliation, arbitration, and judicial settlement. Older treaty language and diplomatic commentary routinely call these “amicable” means of settlement, meaning those short of hostilities.
  • Amicable composition. In arbitration, a tribunal may be empowered to decide ex aequo et bono, by equity rather than strict law. The French term is amiable compositeur, and English texts sometimes render it “amicable compositeur” — a common minor error, since the fixed French phrase uses amiable, not amicable.
  • Diplomatic formula. “Amicable relations”, “an amicable understanding”, and “an amicable solution” are stock phrases in communiqués, chosen to signal that a quarrel has been contained without conceding anything substantive.

Technical sense: amicable numbers

In number theory, two positive integers form an amicable pair when each is the sum of the proper divisors of the other. The classic example is 220 and 284: the divisors of 220 sum to 284, and the divisors of 284 sum to 220. The metaphor is ancient — the Pythagoreans regarded such pairs as symbols of friendship — and the English term calques the Latin notion of numbers that are “friendly” to one another. This is a genuine technical sense, not a poetic flourish, and it shows how far the word's core notion of reciprocal goodwill can be abstracted.

Amicable versus amiable: the boundary

The two words are doublets, both descending from Latin amīcus, “friend”, but they have divided the territory between them, and the division is worth stating precisely.

  • Amiable describes a person's disposition: pleasant, good-natured, easy to like. “An amiable host”, “an amiable eccentric”. It can also describe a mood or atmosphere, and in older English it meant “lovable, worthy of love”.
  • Amicable describes a relationship, transaction, or outcome: free of hostility, conducted with goodwill. “An amicable agreement”, “an amicable split”.
  • An amicable divorce may unite two thoroughly unamiable people; an amiable person may nonetheless wage a bitter feud. The adjectives answer different questions: what is this person like? versus how are these parties behaving toward each other?
  • “An amicable man” is archaic or erroneous in modern English; “an amiable settlement” is likewise abnormal today, though the two words were interchangeable in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.
  • The adverb forms diverge in the same way: “they parted amicably” (without a quarrel) versus “he smiled amiably” (pleasantly).

Semantic reasoning behind the split

Latin amīcābilis meant “worthy of being loved” as well as “friendly”: the suffix -ābilis marks capacity or worthiness. French inherited it as amiable and pushed it toward the lovable, pleasing, genial sense, which is the sense English took over in the fourteenth century. English then borrowed the Latin word again, in the fifteenth century, as amicable, and this learned doublet kept the older, more relational meaning of friendliness between parties. The result is a rare case where a Latin adjective was borrowed twice and the two copies divided the semantic field neatly: one for persons, one for dealings.

Register, intensity, and collocations

  • More formal than friendly, less warm. Friendly can describe genuine affection; amicable describes cooperative civility.
  • Stronger than civil. Civility is the minimum of politeness; amicability implies active goodwill and a wish to avoid conflict.
  • Common collocations: amicable settlement, amicable agreement, amicable divorce, amicable separation, amicable relations, amicable parting, amicable resolution, amicable solution, amicable discussion, “in an amicable manner”.
  • Typical grammar: attributive before the noun (“an amicable settlement”), or predicative of an event or relationship (“the split was amicable”). It is not used of physical objects or of sensory impressions.
  • Connotation: mildly positive, but often faintly managerial or public-relations flavoured. Calling a breakup “amicable” can be a claim about good behaviour rather than a description of feeling.
