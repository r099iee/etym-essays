The evolution of blurb is the shortest in this batch and the most honestly documented, because the word was born in living memory and its birth certificate survives. Promotional praise on books is ancient - publishers have puffed their wares since print began - but the name for it dates to 1906-1907 in America. U.S. scholar Brander Matthews used blurb in print in 1906 (in American Character), and the humorist Frank Gelett Burgess - San Francisco bohemian, author of the Purple Cow nonsense verse and editor of the magazine The Lark - popularised it in May 1907 at a dinner given by the American Booksellers' Association. Exhibiting his new book Are You a Bromide?, Burgess displayed a mock dust jacket bearing the image (lifted, by his own account, from a dental advertisement) of a young woman labelled Miss Belinda Blurb, shown "blurbing" - i.e. uttering fulsome praise. The trade laughed, the Publishers' Weekly reported it ("A blurb is a check drawn on Fame, and it is seldom honored"), and the joke stuck.

Burgess himself codified the joke in Burgess Unabridged: A New Dictionary of Words You Have Always Needed (1914): "Blurb, n. 1. A flamboyant advertisement; an inspired testimonial. 2. Fulsome praise; a sound like a publisher. Blurb, v. To flatter from interested motives; to compliment oneself." The definition is half satire, half lexicography - and entirely accurate about the object's function. The jacket of the latest fiction, Burgess wrote, abounds "in agile adjectives and adverbs, attesting that this book is the sensation of the year", telling of "thrills" and "heart-throbs", of "vital importance" and "soul-satisfying revelation". A century later the adjectives have changed ("unputdownable", "visceral", "luminous") but the machinery is identical.

  • 1906 first print: Matthews's use shows the word already circulating as witty slang before Burgess's stunt.
  • 1907 popularisation: the booksellers' dinner, the Miss Belinda Blurb jacket, the Publishers' Weekly notice - the moment a joke becomes trade vocabulary.
  • 1914 definition: Burgess Unabridged fixes noun and verb senses ("to flatter from interested motives").
  • 1920s-1950s normalisation: dust jackets standardise front, back and flap blurbs; the word loses its quotation marks and enters general dictionaries without apology.
  • Late 20th century: the verb spreads ("she blurbed his novel"), and the noun escapes books for films, records and festivals.

Phonologically there is nothing to explain beyond English itself: a monosyllabic bl- onset with a short vowel and final -b, rhyming with curb, herb, verb and echoing expressive neighbours like blurt (to utter suddenly), blubber (to sob, to portray thickly) and babble. Etymonline's verdict - "probably derisively imitative" - captures this halo without claiming a derivation: Burgess likely chose a sound that already suggested spluttering, effusive speech. No Latin, French or Germanic ancestor exists, and the scholarly rule against guesswork forbids inventing one. Semantically the drift has been from mockery to neutrality to mild cynicism: what began as a joke about hype became the standard name for hype, and readers now decode blurbs with the same practised irony Burgess brought to inventing them. The source sentence - a factual claim ("he rewrote it five times") encountered inside a blurb - shows the end state: the blurb as a citable, if suspect, historical source.
