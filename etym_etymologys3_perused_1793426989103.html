The historical evolution of perused is one of the most intriguing semantic journeys in the English language, tracing a trajectory that begins with Indo-European concepts of motion through a medium, passes through medieval notions of material exhaustion, achieves a golden age of scholarly scrutiny in the Renaissance and Enlightenment, and finally arrives at modern lexical contrariety. At the structural root of the word stands the Proto-Indo-European prepositional base *per-, which signified ‘forward, through, across, or beyond’. In Latin, this root evolved into the highly productive prefix per-, which possessed two related functions: a spatial meaning of ‘through’ (as in perforāre, to pierce through) and an intensive meaning signifying ‘thoroughly, completely, to the utmost degree’ (as seen in perfectus, thoroughly made, or perlegere, to read through to the end).

The verbal component of the word derives from Latin ūtor (participle ūsus), meaning ‘to make use of, employ, or consume’. When borrowed into Old French as user and subsequently imported across the English Channel into Anglo-Norman and Middle English as usen, it carried the practical senses of utilizing, wearing out, or expending resources. In the late fifteenth century (attested in records from roughly 1475 to 1500), English speakers combined the Latin intensive prefix per- with the verb use to form perusen. In its earliest documented occurrences, the word had nothing whatsoever to do with books or reading. Instead, it meant literally to ‘use up completely’, to wear out by continuous handling, or to go through an entire inventory or property boundary piece by piece until nothing remained unaddressed.

From Material Consumption to Intellectual Inspection: During the explosive intellectual expansion of the Tudor Renaissance in the early sixteenth century, the word underwent a profound metaphoric shift from the material sphere to the cognitive domain:

  • The Cognitive Bridge (c. 1500–1530): If one could ‘peruse’ an estate by physically walking every foot of its boundaries or ‘peruse’ goods by inspecting every item in an account book, it was a short and natural conceptual leap to ‘peruse’ an idea, problem, or argument by examining every facet of it in the mind. The verb quickly came to signify thorough investigation, mental survey, and rigorous inspection.
  • The Literary and Bibliographical Anchor (c. 1530–1600): By the mid-sixteenth century, peruse had found its true calling in the world of letters. With the rapid spread of the printing press and the rise of legal and theological humanism, scholars and jurists needed a dignified term to describe the linear, uninterrupted reading of a complete text from title page to colophon. To peruse a manuscript meant not to dip into it erratically, but to read every line with analytical seriousness. Shakespeare made frequent and celebrated use of the verb in this exact register: in Hamlet (Act II, Scene 1), Ophelia recounts how the prince scrutinized her countenance—‘he falls to such perusal of my face / As he would draw it’—transferring the intense textual scrutiny of a reader directly to the human features.
  • Enlightenment Precision and Swiftian Satire (17th–18th c.): During the Augustan age of English literature, perused became an indispensable term of intellectual validation. In Jonathan Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726), when Gulliver's cousin Richard Sympson introduces the travel narratives to the public, he declares: ‘I have carefully perused them three times.’ Sympson's claim is designed to establish the veracity and textual integrity of the accounts: the papers were not glanced at; they were subjected to three full cycles of comprehensive inspection. The word functioned as a guarantee of editorial and scholarly diligence.
  • The Twentieth-Century Contronymic Drift: In the middle decades of the twentieth century, peruse began to enter everyday, unacademic speech. In casual social settings, people looking over magazines in waiting rooms or skimming the morning paper began using peruse as a decorative, high-sounding synonym for looking around or browsing. Through widespread conversational adoption, the intensive semantic feature (‘thoroughly, completely’) became bleached out, replaced by the casual visual aspect of the activity (‘looking through’). By the turn of the twenty-first century, peruse had evolved into a classic contronym: depending on whether the speaker is a High Court judge reviewing evidence or a customer browsing a bookstore rack, the past participle perused can indicate either painstaking, exhaustive scrutiny or light, superficial browsing.

Phonetically, perused reflects the standard English stress shift to the verbal root, pronounced in standard Received Pronunciation as /pəˈruːzd/, with a reduced initial schwa /pə-/, a lengthened high back rounded vowel /uː/, and a voiced alveolar fricative assimilation resulting in the /zd/ coda. Historically, the word stands as a testament to the dynamic interplay between Latinate morphological compounding and the living, unpredictable currents of English semantic change.
