English mill is a Roman loanword twice over. Latin mola meant a millstone and by extension a mill; from it came the late Latin molina, “a mill,” which passed into the Germanic languages at the end of antiquity. Old English has mylen and myln, and the form is recognisably the same word as Dutch molen, German Mühle, and Old Norse mylna. The ultimate root is Indo-European *melh₂-, “to grind,” which also gives English meal in the sense of ground grain and Latin molitio, “a grinding.”

The mill in the medieval economy

The mill was one of the few machines in the medieval world, and control of it was a source of power. Lords held the right of multure or soke: tenants were obliged to bring their grain to the manorial mill and to surrender a portion of the flour as payment. Watermills appear in England by the late Saxon period, and the Domesday Book records thousands of them. Windmills arrive later, in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and the familiar types — post mill, smock mill, tower mill — belong to the later medieval and early modern centuries. Place names and surnames preserve the word everywhere: Mill Lane, Milford, Milton, Miller, Milne, Mills.

From grain to goods

Because the mill was the model of powered processing, the word widened to any building where machinery transformed a raw material. A fulling mill thickened cloth; a sawmill cut timber; a paper mill reduced rags to pulp; a cotton mill spun and wove; a steel mill rolled ingots. By the nineteenth century mill had become a general term for the factory, and mill town, mill hand, mill owner, and mill girl named the social world of industrial capitalism. This is the sense that makes milled mean “factory-processed” as well as “ground.”

Milling cloth and soap

Textile milling is one of the oldest finishing trades. Wet wool is agitated so that the scales on the fibres interlock; the cloth shrinks, thickens, and becomes weatherproof. The process is also called fulling, and the two words coexisted for centuries, with mill naming the building and the action alike. Soap milling is a much later application of the same idea: passing soap through rollers removes air and water, producing a dense, translucent bar, which is why “triple-milled” appears on modern packaging.

Coins and machines

The monetary sense is a separate but related development. Hammered coinage was made by placing a blank between dies and striking it; the edges were irregular and easy to clip. From the sixteenth century European mints experimented with screw presses and rolling mills, and in 1662 Charles II's mint adopted the process, producing coins with a raised rim and a milled, lettered, or grained edge. Milled therefore became a technical antonym of hammered, and the word still carries that meaning for collectors. In engineering, the milling machine — a rotating cutter fed against a workpiece — was developed in the early nineteenth century and became indispensable in arms manufacture and general machining; milled then came to describe any surface so produced.

Why a crowd mills

  • The crowd sense is first recorded in the 1870s, applied to cattle and then to people.
  • The likely image is livestock circling a mill or tramping in a pen, combined with the rotary motion of the mill wheel itself.
  • The semantic payload is motion without progress, which is why the verb so often takes about or around: the crowd turns on the spot.
  • It is now the most familiar use of milled in ordinary prose, even though it is the youngest.

Idioms and cultural residue

Run of the mill comes from ungraded output, before mills sorted their production into qualities. Grist to the mill is something that can be turned to advantage. To put someone through the mill is to subject them to a punishing process, and a millstone round the neck is a burden that drowns its bearer. These idioms keep alive the pre-industrial sense of the mill as a place of relentless, impersonal grinding.

Uncertainty

The origin of mill about is not documented. The two explanations usually offered — cattle circling a mill, and the churning motion of grain between stones — are plausible inferences from the word's earlier senses, not attested derivations, and dictionaries generally record the phrase without committing to either. The slang sense “to beat, defeat” is likewise poorly recorded and regionally variable.

The Germanic noun is a late borrowing from Latin rather than an inherited word, and the proto-form *mulīn- in the chain above is reconstructed from the daughter languages. Because the borrowing may have happened separately in several branches, the single common Germanic stage should be treated as a convenient shorthand rather than a securely attested form.
