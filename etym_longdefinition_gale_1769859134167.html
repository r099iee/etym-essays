gale: a very strong wind; in meteorology, a wind of Beaufort force 7 to 10 (32 to 63 knots); figuratively, any powerful, sustained rush or loud outburst of sound or emotion. Primary sense — a very strong wind. In modern English a gale is a powerful, sustained wind, far stronger than a breeze but not necessarily a full-scale storm or hurricane. Meteorological convention fixes it precisely on the Beaufort scale: Force 7 is a "near gale" (32–38 knots), Force 8 a "gale" (39–46 knots), Force 9 a "strong gale" (47–54 knots), and Force 10 a "storm" (55–63 knots). A gale is long-lived and broad — it covers a wide area and persists for hours rather than striking in a single sharp blast. It can be an onshore wind, a sea wind that drives and endangers ships, or a howling mountain wind. The word always carries an element of violence and hazard: gales tear at trees, whip the sea into white foam, and strain rigging and roofs. As a mass noun it is usually uncountable ("there was a gale blowing", "the gale died down"), though "a gale" as a countable unit of weather is standard. Historical nuance — the sense strengthened over time. The word entered English in the 16th century meaning simply a wind or a breeze, without any necessary violence; the earliest records (c. 1540s) show it as a moderate current of air. Over the 17th and 18th centuries, in nautical usage, it strengthened steadily until it designated a strong, sustained wind, and by the 19th–20th centuries the Beaufort scale fixed it as a wind of force 7–10 — a properly violent and hazardous one. The career of the word is therefore one of gradual intensification from "a wind" to "a violent wind", driven largely by the sailors who needed a precise vocabulary for dangerous weather. Figurative extensions. Because a gale is powerful, loud, and hard to resist, the word lends itself readily to metaphor:
  • "Gales of laughter" — a sudden, overwhelming, gusting outburst of loud laughter; today the most common figurative use.
  • "A gale of applause / a gale of rage" — a violent surge of sound or emotion, as if blown by wind.
  • "In full gale" — at maximum intensity, of any force that sweeps everything before it.
  • Attributive uses: "gale-force winds", "a gale warning", "gale damage", "gale day" — meteorological and journalistic terminology.
  • Nautical slang (historical): a "merry gale" or simply "a gale" could mean a spree or a good time, as in the sea songs — weather language blended with high spirits.
Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • A strong, sustained wind of Beaufort force 7–10 — modern dominant meteorological sense.
  • A moderate breeze or wind — 16th-century sense, now obsolete.
  • Figuratively, a loud, overwhelming outburst of sound or emotion ("gales of laughter") — modern.
  • Attributively, "gale-force", "gale warning", "gale day" — nautical-journalistic terminology.
  • A spree, a good time, a frolic ("merry gale") — 17th–19th-century nautical slang, now archaic.
  • Distinct but unrelated homonym: gale as a bog shrub (sweet gale, Myrica gale), from Middle Dutch gaghel — a botanical word with no connection to the wind.
Near-synonym differentiation:
  • Gale vs breeze: a breeze is mild, pleasant, and gentle; a gale is strong, sustained, and dangerous.
  • Gale vs gust: a gust is a sudden, momentary blast of limited duration; a gale persists for hours and covers a wide area.
  • Gale vs squall: a squall erupts suddenly and briefly, usually accompanied by rain, snow, or thunder; a gale is prolonged and need not carry precipitation.
  • Gale vs tempest: tempest is the grand, literary, mythic word for a violent storm (often oceanic and emotional); gale is the precise, practical nautical-meteorological term.
  • Gale vs storm: storm is the general word for violent weather; a gale is specifically wind-driven, and on the Beaufort scale a storm (Force 11) is actually stronger than a gale.
  • Gale vs wind: wind is the generic term for any moving air; gale names a particular, powerful strength band within it.

The metaphor makes the gallop's force, its duration, and the rider's helplessness all vivid — a gale, unlike a gust, keeps battering, and a sapling, unlike an old tree, cannot stand firm.
