Provençal is an adjective and a noun whose centre of gravity is Provence, the historical region of south-eastern France stretching from the Rhône to the Italian border and from the Alps to the Mediterranean. In its broadest English use the word names anything belonging to that region: its people, landscape, speech, cooking, architecture and distinctive way of life. Because Provence was the first part of Gaul to be organised as a Roman province, the name carries a deep political layer: Provençal descends ultimately from Latin prōvincia, "province", and the territory was known simply as Provincia — "the province" — long before it became modern Provence.

Primary sense: the region and its people. As a noun, a Provençal is a native or inhabitant of Provence; as an adjective, the word describes whatever belongs to the region — Provençal wine, Provençal markets, Provençal sunlight, a Provençal accent. This is the oldest and most transparent layer of meaning, and it remains the default sense in ordinary English. The adjective is not merely geographic: it implies a cluster of associations that travel with the place — warmth, stone farmhouses, lavender, olive groves, cicadas, a slower Mediterranean rhythm, and a culture that has been consciously defended against the centralising pressure of Paris.

Linguistic sense: the language of the troubadours and of Mistral. The most important specialised meaning is linguistic. Provençal names the Romance variety spoken in Provence, traditionally classified as a dialect of Occitan (the langue d'oc), the southern Gallo-Romance language that once stretched from the Atlantic to the Alps. In older scholarship the term was used much more widely for the whole Occitan literary language, especially the medieval language of the troubadours; modern linguists usually restrict it to the Provençal variety proper, while acknowledging that the wider usage survives in literary history and in library catalogues.

  • Old Provençal (or Old Occitan) is the language of the troubadours, the courtly lyric poets of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Its prestige was European: Dante admired it, and its conventions of fin' amor shaped the love poetry of Italy, Catalonia, Castile and Germany.
  • Modern Provençal is the descendant spoken in Provence and parts of Drôme and Gard. It is written in two competing orthographies: the classical Occitan norm and the Mistralian or Félibrige norm, which uses a more French-like spelling.
  • ISO classification: Provençal formerly had the ISO 639-3 code prv; in 2007 that code and the other Occitan dialect codes were retired and merged into oci (Occitan). The older code is no longer active, though it still appears in legacy records.
  • Political semantics: calling the language "Provençal" rather than "Occitan" can be a deliberate assertion of regional rather than pan-southern identity; the choice of label is therefore never purely descriptive.

Literary and cultural sense. "Provençal literature" denotes the body of verse and prose composed in the language, above all the troubadour corpus but also the nineteenth-century revival led by Frédéric Mistral, Joseph Roumanille and the Félibrige movement founded in 1854. Mistral's Mirèio (1859) and his dictionary, Lou Trésor dóu Félibrige, gave the language a modern literary dignity and helped win him the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904. In English, "Provençal" in this sense often functions as a metonym for courtly love, sunlit lyricism and the medieval South.

Culinary and lifestyle sense. À la provençale is a recognised culinary formula: cooked with tomatoes, garlic, olives, olive oil and herbs — the herbes de Provence blend of thyme, rosemary, savory, oregano and marjoram. Dishes described as Provençal tend to be robust, sun-driven and unpretentious, and the label now sells everything from table linen to soap. The semantic bridge from region to cuisine is straightforward metonymy: the land and its produce stand for a way of eating and living.

Art, architecture and design. In interior design, "Provençal style" means pale limed wood, faded blues and ochres, printed cottons, terracotta, wrought iron and rustic farmhouse furniture. In painting, "Provençal" evokes the light that attracted Cézanne, Van Gogh, Matisse and Picasso; the label has become a commercial shorthand for warm, sun-bleached Mediterranean colour. Here the geographic adjective has drifted into an aesthetic category that can be applied far from France.

Extended, transferred and contested senses. English also uses "Provençal" loosely for the whole culture of the Midi, and occasionally for a supposed temperament — vivacious, rhetorical, hot-blooded — as in the old phrase "a speech à la provençale". This impressionistic usage is impressionistic and should be treated with caution. Historically, Provençal could also mean "of the Roman province" in general, a sense preserved in medieval Latin provincialis, which could designate people from a much wider southern area than modern Provence. The modern narrowing of the term is itself a story of semantic drift.

Technical and scholarly notes. In philology, "Provençal" contrasts with Languedocien, Gascon, Auvergnat, Limousin and Alpine (Vivaro-Alpine) within the Occitan continuum. The dialect boundary is gradual rather than sharp, and speakers themselves often say prouvençau or provençau. The English pronunciation /ˌprɒvɒ̃ˈsɑːl/ preserves the nasal vowel of French; anglicised /ˌprɒvənˈsɑːl/ is also heard. The spelling with the cedilla (ç) is essential: it marks the soft /s/ before a, and the form Provencal without it is a common typographic error.
