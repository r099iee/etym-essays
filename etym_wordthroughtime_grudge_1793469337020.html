- 13th to 14th c. — Vocal Murmuring and Secret Complaint: Borrowed into Middle English from Old French as grucchen; means literally to murmur, grumble in the throat, or complain secretly against God or one's master.
- 15th c. — From Vocal Grumbling to Reluctant Bestowal: Shifts phonetically from voiceless affricate /tʃ/ to voiced /dʒ/ (grudgen); develops the transitive sense of grumbling at a concession, meaning to grant or give unwillingly.
- 16th to 17th c. — The Crystallization of the Noun: The noun form solidifies into the concept of a smoldering, cherished resentment or ancient feud harbored within the heart against an enemy.
- 18th c. — Augustan Moral Critique: In the moral prose of Samuel Johnson, grudge becomes an analytical instrument to dissect human envy and the hypocritical parsimony of withholding praise from living contemporaries.
- Early 20th c. — The American Sporting Arena: American sports journalism coins the phrase grudge match (c. 1920s) for fierce boxing matches and baseball rivalries where personal bad blood elevates the stakes.
- Contemporary Era — Therapeutic Discourse and Psychology: Widely discussed in modern psychology and popular self-help culture, framing the 'holding of a grudge' as a destructive emotional fixation that must be released through cognitive forgiveness.
