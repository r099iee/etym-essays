PIE *bʰuH- (also written *bheuə-) 'to be, to become, to grow, to dwell' (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *būaną 'to dwell, to live, to cultivate' (pre-5th c. CE) → Old English bȳre (byre) 'cow-shed, cattle-stall' (attested Old English, c. 8th–11th c.) → Old English dative plural (æt) bȳrum 'at the cattle-sheds' (locative use in place-names, Old English) → place-names Byram / Byrom / Byrun / Byron 'homestead at the byres' (Yorkshire and northern England, recorded from the 11th–13th c.) → Middle English / Anglo-Norman hereditary surname de Biron, de Byrun, Byron 'one from Byron/Byrom' (recorded from the 13th c.) → baronial surname Byron, Lord Byron of Rochdale (peerage; 1st Baron created 1643) → George Gordon Byron, 6th Baron Byron (1788–1824), the poet → cultural derivatives Byronic, Byronism → BYRON. Note on the debate: The PIE reconstruction is written *bʰuH- in modern laryngeal notation but appears as *bheuə- in Pokorny; both denote the same 'be/become/grow/dwell' root, and the spelling difference is scholarly convention, not a different etymon. The surname's toponymic origin from Old English bȳre is the standard onomastic account; the similar-looking French noble house Biron (de Biron) belongs to a separate line and must not be merged with the English family.
