• Ravines: Similar because both are water-carved channels in the earth; differs because a ravine is far larger and steeper-walled — too wide to leap and too sheer to climb — the next scale up from a gully, and implies a major torrent.
  • Gulch: Similar because it denotes roughly the same landform, a narrow steep-sided water-worn channel; differs as a New World variant, more American-frontier in flavour than the older English gully.
  • Arroyo: Similar because both are channels worn by water; differs because an arroyo is a desert watercourse, usually dry except in storms, with Southwestern Spanish associations.
  • Wadi: Similar because both are water-cut channels in dry country; differs because a wadi is the Arabic/Middle Eastern term, usually a broad valley or ravine with a streambed that flows only after rain.
  • Gorge: Similar because both are steep-sided openings in the land; differs because a gorge is grander, wider, and usually carries a stream along its floor, rather than a narrow, often dry runoff-cut gully.
  • Canyon: Similar because both are valleys cut by water; differs because a canyon is far larger and deeper, with American Southwestern associations (from Spanish cañón, 'tube'), a scale wholly beyond the leapable gully.
  • Ditch: Similar because both are narrow channels; differs because a ditch is dug by human hands for drainage or defence, while a gully is carved naturally by running water.
  • Furrow: Similar because both are narrow channels in the ground; differs because a furrow is a plough-cut line in a field, shallow and man-made, not a steep-sided water-worn chasm.
  • Coulee: Similar because both are landforms carved by water; differs because a coulee (from French couler, 'to flow') is a Canadian/American term for a ravine or valley often formed by glacial meltwater, generally broader than a gully.
  • Watercourse: Similar because both name the path of running water; differs because 'watercourse' is the general, neutral term for any channel, stream, or bed, with none of gully's steepness or leapable-narrow danger.
