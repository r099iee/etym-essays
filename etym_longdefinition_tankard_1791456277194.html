tankard is a large drinking vessel, usually cylindrical or slightly tapering, with a handle and often a hinged lid, traditionally made of pewter, silver, copper, leather or wood and used for beer, ale and cider. In the Wodehouse passage a tankard of home-brewed is handed to the narrator, and the word immediately supplies a whole scene: a country house, a generous host, a frothy dark drink, a vessel heavy enough to be worth lifting with both hands. The word is old, its origin is disputed, and its history runs from coopered tubs to silver presentation pieces to modern craft-beer bars.

Primary sense. In current English a tankard is a large one-handled drinking mug, usually holding a pint or a quart, and often fitted with a hinged lid. The lid is the feature that most clearly distinguishes a tankard from an ordinary mug: it keeps the drink from spilling, preserves the head on beer and keeps dust and insects out. Tankards are commonly made of pewter or silver, but glass, ceramic, copper and leather examples also exist. The word can also denote the quantity contained in such a vessel: “he drank a tankard of ale” means not just that he used the vessel but that he consumed its contents.

Historical vessel. The word’s earliest English sense is not a drinking vessel at all. In the early fourteenth century a tankard was a large tub or cask, usually of wooden staves hooped with iron; the word is found in surnames from the late thirteenth century. By the late fifteenth century it had come to mean a large drinking vessel, and in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it could be applied to vessels of considerable size, including water carriers holding two gallons or more, used to bring water from the conduits of London. The modern association with beer and ale is a later narrowing of a much broader word for a large liquid container.

Materials and status. Pewter was the characteristic tankard metal of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: cheap enough for taverns, soft enough to be hammered and cast, and capable of taking a fine patina. Silver tankards were status objects, often engraved with initials, coats of arms or inscriptions, and given as christening gifts, wedding presents or presentation pieces. Glass tankards, sometimes mounted in metal, became common in the nineteenth century; ceramic and enamel versions served cheaper markets. Collectors today value the hallmarks, the shape of the handle and the quality of the hinge as much as the vessel itself.

Measure and metonymy. Because a tankard is a standard vessel, its name can stand for its contents. This is ordinary metonymy, but it is worth noticing because it explains many of the word’s literary uses: “a tankard of ale” is both a container and a drink, and “to drain a tankard” is to perform a social ritual rather than a merely physical act. The same pattern appears with glass, bottle and cup.

Tavern culture and literature. The tankard is an emblem of the tavern, the inn and the alehouse. It appears in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama whenever a scene moves to a drinking house, in eighteenth-century prints of merry company, in Dickens’s inns and in the country-house comedy of Wodehouse. Its connotations are warmth, hospitality, hearty appetite and a slightly old-fashioned masculinity. A tankard is not a delicate object; it is meant to be gripped, raised and emptied. That physicality is part of its literary value: a character holding a tankard is immediately placed in a tradition of convivial drinking.

Modern use. Tankards survive in several niches. They are collectible antiques, especially silver and pewter examples; they are used in historical re-enactment and in traditional pubs; they are popular as personalised gifts and trophies; and they appear in fantasy fiction and role-playing games as the standard drinking vessel of dwarves, innkeepers and adventurers. Craft-beer culture has revived the handled glass tankard, sometimes called a dimple mug or stein, though the terminology is loose. In all these uses the word carries a deliberate note of tradition and solidity.

Tankard, mug, stein, flagon. A mug is the generic term for a large handled cup; a tankard is usually larger, often lidded, and historically associated with beer and pewter or silver. A stein is a German beer mug, often stoneware with a pewter lid; the word is German for “stone”. A flagon is a large vessel for holding drink, often with a narrow neck and a stopper, used for serving rather than drinking. A goblet is a stemmed drinking cup without a handle; a beaker is a tall cup without a handle; a chalice is a ceremonial cup, especially in religious use. The tankard’s defining features are size, handle and lid.

Etymology. Middle English tan(c)kard(e) is recorded in the early fourteenth century in the sense “tub, cask”, and the word corresponds to Middle Dutch tanckaert, which means the same. Both are of unknown origin. The OED tentatively suggests that the English word may be a metathesis of an unattested Medieval Latin *kantard, from Latin cantharus “large drinking cup” (itself from Greek kantharos). Klein proposed French tant quart “as much as a quarter”, but that is a folk etymology. The Century Dictionary long ago pronounced the idea that the word is tank + -ard “wholly untenable”, since tank is not recorded in English until the seventeenth century and is not the source. The word may have been coined in England rather than borrowed; its earliest attestation is the Latinised tancardus of 1266. In short, the origin is genuinely obscure, and the various proposals should be labelled as hypotheses rather than facts.

  • Large vessel: a tub or cask, the earliest English sense.
  • Drinking vessel: a one-handled mug, often lidded, for beer or ale.
  • Measure: the quantity contained in a tankard.
  • Status object: a silver or pewter piece, engraved and collected.
  • Cultural symbol: an emblem of tavern hospitality and hearty drinking.
