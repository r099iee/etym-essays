INFALLIBLE is an adjective meaning incapable of making a mistake or of being wrong; unerring, and so completely dependable that it cannot fail. In the passage behind this card, Mill's Autobiography speaks of the “almost INFALLIBLE counsellor” — his wife, Harriet Taylor. The word here does not merely mean “very reliable”; it asserts a standard of judgment so free from error that it borders on the divine, while the modifying “almost” keeps the claim within human reach. Mill presents Harriet's advice as the one counsel he could trust without reserve, a trust his contemporaries regarded as the eccentric obsession of a brilliant man. The tone is reverent, earnest, and faintly hyperbolic, typical of Victorian hagiography of the beloved; the word carries an emotional weight of complete intellectual surrender, yet it never quite tips into blasphemy because of that saving “almost.” Because the adjective is applied to a person rather than to an institution or a doctrine, it acquires an intimate, almost domestic gravity: the infallible counsellor is the private oracle of the home, the one interlocutor whose judgment the philosopher takes as law.

Primary sense — freedom from error in judgment or truth. In its strict sense, infallible means incapable of error: infallible reasoning is reasoning that cannot go wrong; an infallible test, diagnosis, or proof is one that cannot fail to be correct. The word makes a categorical claim — it is not about high probability but about the impossibility of mistake.

  • Theological sense — the infallibility of the Church and the Pope. In Catholic theology, infallible describes the Church's teaching authority and, in the dogma proclaimed by the First Vatican Council (1870), the Pope's teaching on faith and morals when he speaks ex cathedra: such teaching cannot err. This is the historical heart of the word and the source of its sacred resonance.
  • Philosophical and logical sense. In logic and philosophy, an infallible method or criterion is one that never yields false results — an infallible sign, an infallible rule of inference. The word contrasts with fallible, “liable to error,” and with the modern technical vocabulary of reliability and certainty.
  • Practical sense — an infallible remedy or method. In everyday use the word is applied to remedies, recipes, techniques, and systems that never fail: “an infallible cure,” “an infallible way to get a seat.” Here the word has weakened into a hyperbolic guarantee — “infallible” means dependable to the point of never failing — though careful writers reserve it for the literal impossibility of error.
  • Personal sense — an infallible judge, taste, or instinct. Applied to a person, infallible means one whose judgment, taste, or instinct never misses: an infallible critic, an infallible judge of character, an infallible instinct for what sells. This is the sense of Mill's “almost infallible counsellor,” tempered by the adverb that keeps it human.

Register and collocations. INFALLIBLE is formal and carries a permanent echo of theology and logic. It collocates with nouns of authority, judgment, and method: “infallible authority,” “infallible rule,” “infallible instinct,” “infallible remedy,” “infallible logic,” and the set phrases “papal infallibility” and “infallible in matters of faith.” The abstract noun is infallibility, the negative is fallible, and the word is frequently hedged with “almost,” “practically,” or “not,” precisely because an unhedged claim to infallibility is a claim to the divine.

Distinctions and semantic boundaries. Unerring describes a faculty or aim that never misses its mark (an unerring aim) and stays close to practical performance; infallible makes the categorical claim that the person or principle cannot be wrong about truth and judgment. Impeccable belongs to manners, taste, and style and says nothing about cognitive error. Foolproof applies to mechanisms and procedures too simple to be misused, and rarely to persons. Omniscient is theologically reserved for a being who knows everything; infallible is narrower, meaning only that what is affirmed is never false. In Mill's sentence the adjective, with its saving “almost,” moves between these spheres: it gives Harriet Taylor the authority of an oracle while keeping her, explicitly, human.
