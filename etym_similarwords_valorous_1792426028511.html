• valiant: Similar because both words derive from the same Latin and Old French root (*valere* / *valoir*, "to be strong"), describing heroic bravery and fighting spirit; differs because valiant is an older present-participial formation (*vaillant*) that is much more common in everyday spoken English and literature, whereas valorous is formed with the Latinate abstract noun suffix (*-ous*), giving it an elevated, stately, and epic poetic register.
  • courageous: Similar because both denote possessing the mental or moral strength to face danger, fear, or difficulty; differs because courageous (from Old French corage < Latin cor, "heart") focuses primarily on internal, psychological resolve and moral conviction (e.g., "a courageous moral stand"), whereas valorous overwhelmingly highlights active, martial heroism, physical bravery, and chivalric action in combat.
  • intrepid: Similar because both describe unflinching bravery in dangerous situations; differs because intrepid (from Latin intrepidus, "not trembling/alarmed") emphasizes absolute coolness, unshakeable calm, and absence of fear during exploration or discovery (e.g., "an intrepid arctic explorer"), whereas valorous emphasizes active, passionate, and noble heroic exertion against mortal opposition.
  • heroic: Similar because both characterize actions or qualities worthy of a hero; differs because heroic (from Greek heros) suggests superhuman stature, epic scale, or mythic proportions (e.g., "heroic efforts to rebuild the city"), whereas valorous focuses specifically on the specific chivalric virtue of martial courage and honorable battle conduct.
  • gallant: Similar because both belong to the chivalric lexicon describing brave warriors; differs because gallant (from Old French galer, "to rejoice/make merry") combines bravery with dashing charm, courteous elegance, and polite chivalry toward women or comrades, whereas valorous focuses strictly on formidable martial bravery and stoic courage.
  • dauntless: Similar because both describe courage that refuses to be subdued; differs because dauntless is a negative formation (*daunt* + *-less*) highlighting the impossibility of being intimidated, tamed, or disheartened by terrifying threats, whereas valorous denotes the positive presence of radiant, active valor.
  • doughty: Similar because both describe formidable bravery in battle; differs because doughty is a native Germanic adjective (from Old English dohtig, "capable, worthy, strong") carrying an earthy, archaic, and sometimes faintly rustic or stubborn connotation of rugged persistence, whereas valorous is a high-chivalric Romance term.
  • lionhearted: Similar because both celebrate legendary, fearless courage in combat; differs because lionhearted is a vivid animalistic metaphor famously associated with King Richard I (*Cœur de Lion*), emphasizing ferocious, regal fearlessness, whereas valorous is a comprehensive ethical and martial descriptor.
  • stouthearted: Similar because both denote courageous resilience under trial; differs because stouthearted employs a homely Germanic anatomical metaphor (*stout* + *heart*) suggesting stubborn endurance and pluck in everyday adversity, lacking the grand epic stature of valorous.
  • audacious: Similar because both denote bold willingness to take extreme risks; differs because audacious frequently carries a critical connotation of reckless bravado, impudence, or insolence (crossing established boundaries), whereas valorous is exclusively positive, honoring noble, virtuous, and honorable bravery.
