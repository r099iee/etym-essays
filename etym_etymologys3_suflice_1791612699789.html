2. The Latin Synthesis: Sufficere

When Latin prefixed sub- to facere, two regular phonological changes occurred:

  • Consonantal Assimilation: The voiced bilabial stop -b- assimilated completely to the following voiceless labiodental fricative -f-, producing suf- (hence sub- + facere -> suffacere).
  • Vowel Reduction (Apophony): In accordance with Latin phonological rules for compound verbs, the short open root vowel -a- in an unaccented medial syllable underwent apophony (weakening) to short -i-, yielding the classical infinitive sufficere (present stem suffici-, perfect suffēci, supine suffectum).

In Classical Latin, sufficere initially carried concrete physical meanings: "to put under, to lay a foundation from beneath, to furnish as a substitute, to impregnate, or to dye with a base color from beneath." Roman writers quickly developed metaphorical extensions: to supply something from below meant to sustain, support, or furnish an ongoing supply so that a structure did not collapse or an army did not run out of rations. From "furnishing adequate supplies," the verb naturally shifted into an intransitive sense: "to be equal to the task, to meet the emergency, to be competent, to be enough." Cicero, Virgil, and Livy frequently used sufficere to describe troops having enough strength to withstand an assault or funds sufficing to meet state expenses.

3. Romance Transformation and the Anglo-Norman Inchoative Stem

As Vulgar Latin evolved into Old French, sufficere lost its unaccented syllables and became suffire. However, in its present participle (suffisant) and plural indicative conjugations (nous suffissons), Old French adopted the expanded inchoative suffix -iss- (derived from Latin -escere, originally denoting the beginning of an action). When Anglo-Norman and Old French speakers brought the language into post-Conquest England, English scribes and speakers adopted the verb based on this prominent inchoative stem.

In the mid-14th century, Middle English borrowed the word as suffisen or soffisen. Geoffrey Chaucer used it extensively in The Canterbury Tales (e.g., "suffiseth the, but if thy wittes madde"—let it suffice thee, unless thy wits be mad). The voiceless sibilant /s/ of the French inchoative stem was preserved in the English spelling with -ce, yielding modern suffice by the late 15th and early 16th centuries.
