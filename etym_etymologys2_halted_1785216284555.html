Proto-Indo-European *kel- “to drive, urge on” (reconstructed; the PIE stage is tentative and debated — some scholars prefer a connection to *kel- “to hide, conceal,” and the equation is not secure) │ └── Proto-Germanic *haldaną “to hold, keep, tend” (reconstructed; attested reflexes in Gothic haldan “to tend, keep, watch over”, Old Norse halda “to hold”, Old English healdan “to hold”) └── Old High German haltan “to hold; to stop” (8th c.; the sense “to come to a stop” develops in continental West Germanic) └── Middle High German halten “to hold, keep, stop” (11th–14th c.) └── German halten “to hold, keep; to stop” (16th c.) └── German imperative halt! “stop!” — the sixteenth-century military drill command for freezing a march └── English halt “to stop” (verb, borrowed as a military order c. 1659; noun and general verb from the 17th–18th c.) └── English halted = halt + regular past/participial suffix -ed (17th c. onward) └── Modern English: halted Debated stage: the PIE root is not securely established; the chain from *kel- to Proto-Germanic *haldaną is the standard but contested equation (Old English healdan “to hold”, Gothic haldan, Old Norse halda all point to *haldaną, but its pre-Germanic source is disputed). The later stages from Old High German to English are well attested. Important caution: English hold (from Old English healdan) is a sibling of German halten, not an ancestor of halt — the “stop” word reaches English via the German command, not via the native hold line. And the archaic English adjective halt “lame, crippled” (Old English healt, Proto-Germanic *haltaz) is a completely different word that happens to be spelled identically; it must not be merged into this chain.
