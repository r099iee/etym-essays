• Lombard Street: Similar because both are historic City of London thoroughfares named after foreign merchant and financial communities who settled under royal protection in the Middle Ages; differs because Lombard Street became the undisputed global capital of merchant banking, credit, and goldsmiths (named after Italian financiers from Lombardy), whereas Old Jewry is forever tied to medieval Anglo-Jewish settlement and the tragic 1290 expulsion, later diversifying into surgery, nonconformist preaching, and police administration.
  • Fleet Street: Similar because both are legendary London streets that served as residences for Lemuel Gulliver and developed powerful institutional metonymic identities; differs because Fleet Street lies further west outside the ancient Roman walls and became the world-renowned capital of printing, newspapers, and journalism, whereas Old Jewry lies deep inside the financial core of the City.
  • Petticoat Lane: Similar because both are London street names intimately associated with Jewish history and community life; differs because Petticoat Lane (Middlesex Street) in the East End represents the late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Ashkenazi immigrant working-class garment district and open-air Sunday market, whereas Old Jewry represents the medieval pre-expulsion Norman and Sephardic community living under direct feudal charters from the Crown.
  • Cheapside: Similar because both are ancient commercial thoroughfares in the heart of the City of London that physically connect at Poultry; differs because Cheapside was the grand central open-air market and retail promenade of medieval and early modern London, whereas Old Jewry is a narrower lateral street historically designated for segregated residential and financial enclaves.
  • Judengasse: Similar because both designate historic European urban quarters designated for Jewish residents under royal or imperial charters; differs because a Judengasse specifically refers to the walled, gate-locked ghettos of Germanic cities (such as Frankfurt), whereas Old Jewry, though socially distinct and containing the Great Synagogue, was not an enclosed, locked ghetto in the continental Italian or German sense until late thirteenth-century restrictions.
  • The Ghetto (Venice): Similar because both refer to historically enforced Jewish districts within major European commercial powers; differs because the Venetian Ghetto was physically enclosed on an island behind guarded gates and canals and gave its name as a universal noun for segregated quarters, whereas Old Jewry ceased to have Jewish inhabitants after the 1290 expulsion and evolved into an entirely Christian mercantile, medical, and civic street.
  • Threadneedle Street: Similar because both are venerable streets located in the financial core of the City of London near the Royal Exchange; differs because Threadneedle Street is famous worldwide as the institutional seat of the Bank of England (‘The Old Lady of Threadneedle Street’), whereas Old Jewry became renowned for municipal policing and dissenting academies.
