- [17th century: origin of 'wobble'/'wabble'] - The verb appears as a dialectal or nautical word for rocking, swaying motion of objects (cart wheels, jelly), a homely, onomatopoeic-sounding term; 'wobbly' the adjective names anything that rocks instead of staying put.
- [18th-19th century: bodily application] - Extends from objects to people: wobbly legs, wobbly knees — the physical signature of faintness, dizziness, or fear, used of children and the infirm in everyday speech.
- [20th century: figurative extension] - Moves onto non-physical subjects: wobbly plans, wobbly prices, a wobbly argument or performance; still informal and mildly comic.
- [Mid-20th century British idiom] - 'To throw a wobbly' (also 'a wobbler') enters slang for losing one's temper or panicking, sealing the word's homely, faintly ridiculous register.
- [Contemporary sense] - Informal, domestic word for unsteady swaying — a rickety chair, pudding knees, a table's rock — carrying comic warmth rather than clinical alarm, exactly the register Tolkien exploits for Bilbo's spent legs.
