Plumpish is a small, hedged word, but its family tree reaches back to one of the oldest physiological themes of Proto-Indo-European: the root *peyH-, “to be fat, to swell,” the primal image of fullness and increase. That root produced a constellation of words for the body in abundance across the ancient daughter languages. In Sanskrit it gave pīván- “fat, plump” and pīvas- “fatness, marrow,” together with the Vedic verb pyāyate “swells, increases,” used of rivers rising and bodies thickening; in Avestan it appears as pīva-, the sacrificial fat of Zoroastrian ritual; in Greek it yielded πίων (píōn), “fat, rich, fertile,” said of soil as much as of flesh, along with πιαίνω “to fatten” and the poetic noun πῖαρ “fatness, abundance”; and in Latin it became pinguis, “fat, plump, thick,” the everyday adjective that survived in Italian pingue and Spanish pingüe. In every branch the semantic field is the same: fatness as ripeness, as sufficiency, as life holding nothing back. The Germanic branch took a different path. The proto-form *plump- meant “massive, blunt, blocky”—a property of objects rather than of bodies, the coarseness of a lump of wood or a thick log. Handbooks debate whether *plump- descends directly from *peyH- or was contaminated by *pleu-, “to flow, to float,” but the semantic bridge is plain: a blunt, solid thing is full of matter, just as a plump person is full of flesh. Middle Dutch plomp and German plump preserved the sense of clumsiness and heaviness, and when English borrowed plump in the seventeenth century it first described blunt or massive objects. Only gradually, through the eighteenth century, did the word migrate from the object-world to the body-world, coming to mean “well-rounded, full, and fleshy in a pleasing way,” the sense in which we speak of plump cheeks or a plump harvest. Sound and form at each stage:
  • PIE *peyH- “to be fat, to swell” — the root of fullness and increase.
  • Proto-Germanic *plump- “massive, blunt, blocky” — a property of objects; the descent from *peyH- is debated, with a plausible contamination by *pleu- “to flow, to float.”
  • Middle Dutch plomp “blunt, massive, coarse”; German plump “clumsy, heavy, unwieldy.”
  • English plump (17th c.) “full and well-rounded,” first of blunt objects, then of bodies.
  • English plumpish (18th–19th c.) = plump + -ish, “somewhat plump,” recorded in descriptive prose and novels of manners; the card’s reading is “slightly plumpish.”
The final step is morphological. English added the Germanic suffix -ish—Old English -isc, from Proto-Germanic *-iska-, ultimately from PIE *-isko-, “pertaining to, rather like”—to make an approximating adjective: plumpish, “inclined to be plump, rather than truly plump.” The suffix dilutes the claim, turning a categorical statement into a hedging one, exactly as in greenish, oldish, and tallish. In the novel, that soft boundary is everything: the girl is not fat and not merely slim, but “slightly plumpish,” her cheeks “white and firm as a fresh lychee.” The word’s very vagueness is its meaning—it records a favourable, kindly appraisal of a body raised in comfort, the material proof of a great house’s prosperity. Across four thousand years, a root meaning “to swell” has become a word for a particular, affectionate way of seeing a healthy young woman. Socio-cultural trajectory:
  • 17th century: plump enters English for blunt, massive objects; the bluntness sense survives in German plump.
  • 18th century: plump migrates to bodies and harvests as a positive, pleasing fullness; plumpish appears as its soft-hewn variant in descriptive prose.
  • 19th century: in novels of manners, plumpish is the polite, fond word for a pleasingly rounded girl or child, a marker of health and good feeding.
  • 20th century onward: as slenderness becomes the beauty ideal, plumpish shifts toward tactful euphemism, a kind way of saying “slightly overweight,” yet it retains its courtly, affectionate warmth.
