Ortega primarily denotes José Ortega y Gasset (9 May 1883 – 18 October 1955), the preeminent 20th-century Spanish philosopher, cultural theorist, essayist, and statesman whose brilliant prose and profound philosophical innovations positioned him as the foremost Spanish intellectual of the modern era. Educated in Madrid and at the German universities of Leipzig, Berlin, and Marburg (where he studied under neo-Kantian masters Hermann Cohen and Paul Natorp), Ortega served as Professor of Metaphysics at the Complutense University of Madrid and founded the legendary journal and publishing house Revista de Occidente (1923), which served as the intellectual conduit connecting Spanish and Latin American intellectual life with avant-garde European philosophy and science.

Beyond the biographical figure, Ortega is invoked across contemporary philosophy, sociology, literary criticism, and aesthetics as the architect of pivotal theoretical frameworks. His major philosophical and socio-political contributions include:

  • Ratiovitalism (Vital Reason): Ortega formulated *ratiovitalism* (*razón vital*) as a critical synthesis overcoming the historical deadlock between dry, abstract rationalism (which ignores the pulsing reality of lived experience) and blind vitalism/irrationalism (which abandons intellect). For Ortega, reason is not an autonomous metaphysical entity floating above the world, but a fundamental biological and existential function of living human beings: "Life is the fundamental reality, and reason is a form of life."
  • Perspectivism and the Circumstance: Ortega is universally celebrated for his famous maxim articulated in Meditaciones del Quijote (1914): "Yo soy yo y mi circunstancia, y si no la salvo a ella no me salvo yo" ("I am I and my circumstance, and if I do not save it, I do not save myself"). Perspectivism posits that objective reality is inherently multi-faceted and can only be apprehended through the complementary sum of individual, historical, and cultural perspectives. Absolute truth is not a monolithic single vantage point, but the harmonious integration of distinct viewpoints.
  • The Revolt of the Masses (La rebelión de las masas, 1930): Ortega's most internationally celebrated socio-political treatise. He diagnosed the arrival of the "mass-man" (*hombre-masa*)—a psychological type, spanning all socioeconomic classes, characterized by self-satisfied mediocrity, intellectual complacency, and a sense of absolute entitlement to the fruits of industrial and scientific progress without any corresponding sense of duty, historical gratitude, or ethical discipline. Ortega warned that when the mass-man displaces cultivated, qualified minorities and captures political power, liberal democracy descends into hyper-conformism, direct action violence, and tyrannical totalitarianism.
  • The Dehumanization of Art (La deshumanización del arte, 1925): A foundational text of modernist aesthetics in which Ortega analyzed avant-garde movements (Cubism, modern poetry, atonal music). He argued that modern art deliberately eliminates ordinary human sentimentality, realism, and melodrama, choosing instead to explore pure aesthetic form, irony, and self-referential artistic language, thereby intentionally dividing the public into those who understand aesthetic form and those who merely seek emotional reflection.
  • Literary Criticism and the Novel: In works such as Ideas sobre la novela, Ortega offered incisive structural analysis of narrative evolution, demonstrating how the expansive, epic poetry of ancient myth inevitably collapsed into the psychological, realistic, and fragmented modern novel—a concept pivotal to formalist and thematic interpretations of Don Quixote.

In onomastics and daily usage, Ortega is also one of the most widespread surnames across Spain and Latin America, deriving historically from toponymic roots denoting places overgrown with stinging nettles (Latin urtica) or frequented by game birds, borne by numerous prominent cultural and political figures throughout the Hispanic world.
