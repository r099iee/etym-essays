- “vernal”, 1530s–1600s - a learned adoption from Latin; means simply “pertaining to spring”; used in almanacs, astronomy (vernal equinox), and natural description.
- “vernal”, 17th–18th c. - extended to figurative youth and freshness: “vernal years”, “vernal bloom”; the season-word becomes the metaphor for the springtime of life.
- “vernal”, Romantic era - a staple poetic word of renewal and hope: Wordsworth's “vernal promises” (the source passage) and “One impulse from a vernal wood”; “autumnal” gains its counterpart weight.
- “vernal”, 19th–20th c. - retreats from speech into literary and technical registers: vernalization (cold-treated seeds), vernal pools (seasonal wetlands), vernal equinox (astronomy).
- “vernal”, modern - perceived as elegant, old-fashioned, faintly poetic; fixed technical phrases keep it alive while conversation prefers “spring”, “springtime”, “fresh”.
