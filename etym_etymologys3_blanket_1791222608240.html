Blanket begins not in English but in the colour vocabulary of Frankish conquerors of Gaul. Proto-Indo-European *bhel- to shine, flash, burn is one of the great colour roots, giving Sanskrit bhala lustre, Greek phalos white, and Germanic *blankaz white, shining, specifically of horses (a blank horse is a white horse). Frankish *blank white enters Gallo-Romance as Old French blanc, blanche white, displacing Latin albus in many uses. Its diminutive blanchet meant a small white thing - a small white cloth, white woollen lining, or white wine.

Anglo-Norman brings blanket to England after 1066 as undyed woollen cloth. The 14th-century blanket in Middle English still means undyed, often white or grey, coarse woollen fabric for beds, exactly the staple traded on frontiers centuries later. Bristol became a centre of blanket weaving; blanket as bed cover is attested c. 1380-1400 in Chaucer-era inventories. Phonetics are straightforward: French nasalized blanc /blãk/ plus diminutive -et yields blanchet, borrowed with English stress shift to BLAN-ket and denasalization.

  • Sound shifts: PIE *bh- to Germanic *b- by Grimm, *-nk- preserved through Frankish to French; English keeps /blæŋkɪt/ RP.
  • Semantic drift: white to white cloth (metonymy) to any heavy bed cover regardless of colour (generalization) to covering action (verb 17th c.) to universal application (adjective 19th-20th c.).
  • Socio-cultural contexts: medieval undyed cloth for poor; early modern Witney blankets; Hudson Bay point blankets as currency for furs (points indicated size); military blankets in both World Wars; modern fleece and electric versions.
  • Literary attestations: Langland blankets for beds; Shakespeare wet blanket image in embryo (smothering); 19th-century American frontier blanket as money unit exactly as in the source passage; Peanuts security blanket (1950s) creating psychological sense.

The adjective sense blanket ban emerges from the verb image in 19th-century journalism: to throw a blanket over all cases. By the 20th century it is fully lexicalized in law and administration. Thus a Germanic colour word, via French textile trade, becomes an English household noun, then a global metaphor for uniformity. The money-history equation - three units equal a blanket - captures a moment when this manufactured textile was so standardized and desired that it functioned as a unit of account.

Later industrialization added new layers without erasing old ones. Steam-powered looms made coloured blankets cheap, electricity added heated blankets, synthetics added fleece throws, yet the Hudson Bay stripes and military olive drab remain icons of durability. Linguistically, verb and adjective uses now outnumber noun uses in news corpora, showing metaphorical covering overtaking literal bedding. The blank in blanket - empty white space - reinforces this: a blank page, blank cartridge, and blank cheque all share the same Frankish whiteness, an absence that covers uniformly.
