Larceny is a noun belonging to the technical vocabulary of criminal law. Its core meaning is the unlawful taking and carrying away of another person's personal property with the intention of permanently depriving the owner of it. In the reading — "petty larceny by a common man was considered a more heinous crime than murder by a chief" — the word names the minor grade of theft, the stealing of goods of small value, as opposed to grand larceny. The passage uses it generically, to contrast a small property crime by a low-status offender with a grave crime by a high-status one.

English law distinguishes larceny from several offences that ordinary speech lumps together as "stealing". The distinctions are ancient and technical, and they still shape American criminal codes, even though England abolished larceny in 1968 and replaced it with the broader statutory offence of theft.

Elements of the common-law offence

  • Trespassory taking. The property must be taken without the owner's consent. If the owner hands it over voluntarily, the taking is not trespassory, and the offence may instead be embezzlement or false pretenses.
  • Caption and asportation. The old formulae require both a "taking" (caption) and a "carrying away" (asportation). The slightest movement may suffice — lifting a wallet an inch can complete the offence.
  • Personal property. Larceny historically protects movable goods, money and choses in action, not land or fixtures. Modern statutes extend it to electricity, services and intangible property in various ways.
  • Property of another. The taker must not own the property. A co-owner can sometimes be guilty of larceny of partnership or joint property, depending on the jurisdiction.
  • Intent to permanently deprive. A temporary taking, however dishonest, is not larceny at common law; the intent must be to keep the property or dispose of it as one's own. Borrowing without permission, intending to return, is not larceny.
  • No claim of right. An honest belief that one has a right to the property negates the necessary intent, even if the belief is unreasonable in some jurisdictions.

Grand and petty larceny

The offence is traditionally divided by value. At common law, grand larceny was the theft of goods worth more than twelve pence, a threshold linked to the benefit of clergy; petty (or petit) larceny was the theft of goods below that value. The distinction survives in many American states, where grand larceny and petty larceny are separate grades with different maximum penalties and different rules of procedure. New York, for example, grades grand larceny in four degrees according to the value of the property taken. In England the terminology was abolished by the Theft Act 1968, which created a single offence of theft defined as the dishonest appropriation of property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it.

Related offences and the legal boundaries

  • Theft. The modern umbrella term. In England and Wales it has replaced larceny entirely; in ordinary American usage it is broader and less technical. Larceny is a species of theft, not its synonym.
  • Robbery. Larceny accomplished by force or the threat of force, or by putting the victim in fear. Robbery is larceny plus violence or intimidation; the taking must be from the person or in their presence.
  • Burglary. Unlawful entry into a building with intent to commit a crime inside. Burglary concerns the entry, not the taking; a burglar may be guilty even if nothing is stolen, and a thief who enters lawfully is not a burglar.
  • Embezzlement. The fraudulent conversion of property that the offender lawfully possesses, typically because it was entrusted to them. The embezzler does not take the property from the owner's possession; the larcenist does.
  • False pretenses. Obtaining ownership of property by deception. The victim intends to transfer title, which is why the offence is not larceny; the larceny victim does not consent at all.
  • Shoplifting. A lay and statutory term for larceny from a retail store, often defined separately with its own procedures and penalties.
  • Receiving stolen goods. Handling property known to be stolen. It is a separate offence from the taking itself and can be committed by someone who was not present at the theft.

Figurative and journalistic uses

  • Literary larceny. Plagiarism or the unacknowledged borrowing of another writer's work; the phrase treats intellectual property as stealable goods.
  • Petty larceny in politics. A dismissive label for small-scale corruption or minor misuse of public funds, chosen to suggest that the offence is real but beneath grand proportions.
  • Grand larceny of the public purse. Rhetorical inflation of the same metaphor, common in scandal reporting.

Register

Larceny is formal, legal and slightly archaic in British English, where theft is now the ordinary word. In American English it remains current in statutes, police reports and journalism, especially in the phrases grand larceny and petty larceny. Its use in the reading is of this formal, legal kind: the point is not simply that the man stole, but that his offence fell into the lower legal grade.
