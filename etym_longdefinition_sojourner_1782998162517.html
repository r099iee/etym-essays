SOJOURNER (noun; RP /s'əʊdʒɜːnə/). The word names a temporary resident — a person who stays in a place for a time without settling there permanently; a traveler who dwells for a season in a foreign or unfamiliar land. The suffix -er marks the agent ("one who sojourns"), and the whole word is built on the verb sojourn, "to stay temporarily," which descends from the Latin root meaning "to spend the day in a place." The sojourner is thus, at the word's etymological heart, someone who passes a day — or days, or months — in a place that is not their home, and who will move on. In the opening of Wordsworth's Prelude the poet, escaped from "the vast city," calls himself "A discontented SOJOURNER: now free, / Free as a bird to settle where I will." The phrase is precise: he has been a dweller in a place that never became his home, pining and restless, and the new freedom of the countryside releases him from that uneasy, temporary tenure. The word's whole weight falls on temporariness and homelessness — on being a guest in the world rather than an owner of it. The primary sense is clear and literal: a person who resides in a place temporarily. This covers the visitor staying for weeks, the exile lodged in a foreign city, the traveler resting in an inn before riding on, the refugee housed until return becomes possible. In older, biblical and theological use the word carries a profound extension: the sojourner (Hebrew ger, translated "stranger" or "sojourner" in the King James Bible) is the resident alien who dwells among a people without full belonging, and by metaphor the human soul is a sojourner on earth, only passing through toward its true home. That spiritual sense gives the word its characteristic dignity and music — the sojourner is a pilgrim, a guest of this world. The word's semantic range may be mapped as follows:
  • Temporary resident: one who stays in a place for a limited time without settling — "a sojourner in the city for a season," "the inn welcomed weary sojourners." This is the literal, core sense.
  • Traveler who dwells along the way: a person on a journey who pauses to stay somewhere — the sojourner as guest of an inn, court, or household. In medieval romance and chronicle this is the knight or pilgrim resting between stages.
  • Resident alien / stranger in a foreign land: one who lives among a people not their own, without citizenship or permanent ties — the biblical ger, the foreigner within the gate. This sense pairs sojourner with stranger in scripture and retains a legal-historical flavor.
  • Spiritual pilgrim: by metaphor, the human being considered as a temporary inhabitant of this world, en route to a heavenly home — "strangers and sojourners on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13), the classic devotional sense.
  • Figurative homelessness: in literary use, anyone who feels they belong nowhere — "a sojourner in the bustling metropolis, never truly at home" — the word for existential displacement rather than mere travel.
  • Modern specialized uses: in space exploration the word has been adopted for temporary inhabitants of alien environments (NASA's Sojourner rover, named for the abolitionist Sojourner Truth; astronauts as "sojourners" in space), and it survives in religious prose, historical fiction, and solemn rhetoric.
The semantic bridge that unites these senses is the idea of temporary tenure. A sojourner is never the owner, lord, or permanent dweller; they are always passing through, always a guest. This single idea explains both the word's neutral uses (the traveler, the visitor) and its exalted ones (the pilgrim, the exile), because the same temporariness can be read either as mere logistics or as the deepest fact about human life. Unlike "traveler," which emphasizes the motion of the journey, sojourner emphasizes the pause within it; unlike "resident," which implies belonging and permanence, sojourner implies the opposite. In register, sojourner is today a deliberately literary, archaic, and dignified word. Ordinary speech prefers temporary resident, visitor, or newcomer. But the word has never died because no ordinary synonym carries its freight: the sojourner's temporariness is not accidental but essential, and the word's biblical and poetic history gives it a solemn, almost sacred resonance that "temporary resident" entirely lacks. In the Wordsworth passage the choice is exact — "discontented sojourner" holds together the restlessness of the city years, the status of a guest who never became a citizen, and the hope of the freed bird that follows; no plainer word could bear that weight. For the learner the essential facts are these: a sojourner is a temporary resident or traveler staying for a time; the word descends from Latin for "spending the day" somewhere; and its special power lies in combining the practical fact of temporariness with the ancient, spiritual image of the pilgrim passing through an alien world.
