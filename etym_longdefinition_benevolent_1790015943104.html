benevolent means wishing well, intending kindness, and actively doing good to others. At its core it describes a person, disposition, action, institution, or power that is kindly, charitable, and disposed to promote happiness and welfare rather than harm: a benevolent grandfather, a benevolent smile, benevolent rule, a benevolent fund, a benevolent dictator, a benevolent universe. The word always joins inner goodwill with outward benefit — to be benevolent is both to mean well and, where possible, to do well. In your sentence — “I am just, and indeed benevolent,” he said — the speaker claims not only fairness but active kindness, justice tempered by generosity.

The historical bridge from wishing to giving is Roman moral vocabulary. Latin bene velle literally meant to wish well; benevolens was the wisher, benevolentia the wishing itself, goodwill. English narrowed and warmed this: benevolence became not only an attitude but organized charity, and benevolent became the adjective for persons and bodies whose settled character is to give, forgive, protect, and heal. Because wishing easily stays private, modern usage stresses proof — benevolence must show in gifts, care, tolerance, and mercy — while still honouring quiet goodwill that cannot act.

  • Kindly personal character (primary): of people who are warm-hearted, generous, tolerant, and helpful. A benevolent teacher, neighbour, doctor, employer, stranger. Implies steady disposition, not single act. Collocations: benevolent smile, eyes, manner, nature, presence. Opposite is malevolent, spiteful, cruel.
  • Charitable action and giving: benevolent acts, deeds, donations, works. To perform a benevolent act is to help without self-interest. Implies material aid, forgiveness of debts, volunteering, patronage. Near-synonym philanthropic but more personal and less institutional.
  • Institutional-philanthropic sense: a benevolent society, fund, association, trust, order. In Britain especially, benevolent societies are mutual-aid charities for workers, orphans, widows — e.g. Policemen’s Benevolent Association, Actors’ Benevolent Fund. Means non-profit, aid-distributing, membership-based. Legal flavour: benevolent purposes in charity law.
  • Power and governance: benevolent ruler, king, dictator, regime, empire, employer, deity. Means exercising authority for subjects’ good, merciful rather than oppressive. The famous paradox benevolent dictator means an absolute ruler who uses power kindly — efficient but undemocratic. Connotation depends on view: sincere praise or ironic scepticism.
  • Impersonal-favourable sense: benevolent climate, weather, environment, market, interpretation, fate. Means favourable to life, growth, or success, as if nature itself wished well. A benevolent spring allows crops; a benevolent reading of a rule favours leniency. Bridge by personification: what helps is as if kind.
  • Philosophical-theological sense: benevolence as moral virtue. In Hutcheson, Hume, Smith, benevolence is the natural sympathy that grounds morals. A benevolent God wills universal happiness. Implies disinterested love, universal goodwill, contrasted with self-love. Technical in ethics and theology.
  • Medical-astrological archaic nuance: older texts call tumours benevolent for benign, aspects benevolent for favourable. Now largely replaced by benign, but survives in history: benevolent stars, benevolent humours. Means not malignant, propitious.

Grammar and register: adjective, typically attributive or predicative — he is benevolent, a benevolent man. Comparative more benevolent, most benevolent. Noun benevolence, adverb benevolently. Register warm-formal, praising, literary-humanitarian. Stronger and more settled than kind, softer and more personal than philanthropic, less sentimental than tender-hearted. To call someone benevolent is to credit motive, not only result; even failed help can be benevolent if meant well, while successful help done for show is not truly benevolent.
