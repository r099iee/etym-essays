"Patriarchal" is an adjective describing a social organization, family form, culture, or attitude in which the oldest male — the patriarch — holds central authority, with power, property, name, and status transmitted primarily through men. In its narrowest anthropological sense it denotes a family that has taken on a father-ruled form, exactly as in the source sentence: authority centralized in the oldest male, property individualized, and bequest passing down the male line.

More broadly it denotes any system of male headship and male primacy: patriarchal societies, patriarchal religions, patriarchal values. In feminist theory since the 19th century it has become the standard term for systemic male dominance — not merely one stern father but an entire order in which political leadership, moral authority, economic control, and symbolic prestige are vested in men and transmitted father to son, while women are subordinated, secluded, or rendered legally dependent.

  • Family and kinship (core, source sense): A patriarchal family is headed by the father or grandfather, who controls property, arranges marriages, disciplines members, and represents the household to the outside world. Residence is typically patrilocal, descent patrilineal, inheritance primogenitary through sons. The Durant sentence captures the historical thesis: as families became patriarchal, collective tribal property gave way to individualized, heritable, male-transmitted property and personal bequest.
  • Societal system: A patriarchal society is one in which men hold most positions of power — chieftainship, priesthood, magistracy, military command, landownership — and in which law, custom, and religion affirm male authority. Classical Athens, ancient Rome under patria potestas, Confucian China, and Victorian England are routinely cited as patriarchal societies in different degrees.
  • Religious and symbolic: Patriarchal religions emphasize God as Father, male priesthood, male prophets and apostles, and scriptures commanding wifely obedience. Judaism, Christianity, and Islam each preserve patriarchal layers — the biblical Patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob give the word its sacred resonance — even as modern reform movements contest them.
  • Attitudinal / ideological: Patriarchal attitudes, assumptions, or bargains denote beliefs that men should lead and women follow: that the husband is head of household, that sons matter more than daughters, that public life belongs to men. Here the word is critical and pejorative, diagnosing bias rather than describing kinship neutrally.
  • Political theory: From Sir Robert Filmer's Patriarcha (1680) defending divine-right kingship as father-rule writ large, to Locke's refutation, to modern feminist uses, patriarchal names the analogy between family father-rule and state rule. To call monarchy patriarchal is to say the king rules as father of his people.
  • Extended / weakened use: Colloquially, a patriarchal figure or manner means an elderly man who is venerable, authoritative, kind but commanding — white-bearded, benevolent, laying down the law. This retains the dignity of the patriarch while softening domination into benevolence.
  • Register and antonyms: Formal, academic, and politically charged. Antonyms are matriarchal (ruled by mothers), matrilineal, egalitarian, feminist. Unlike paternal, which can mean merely fatherly or benevolent, patriarchal almost always implies structural power, tradition, and age-ranked male hierarchy.

The polysemy bridges easily: father-headship in the household extends outward to male headship in clan, church, and state, then inward to headship as a mindset. The historical bridge in the source — patriarchal form producing individualized property and bequest — explains why economists link patriarchy to private inheritance: when the oldest male embodies the family, he can alienate and bequeath its wealth as his own, dissolving communal tenure into personal ownership passed from father to son.
