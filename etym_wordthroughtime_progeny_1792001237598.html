- Classical Latin (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Attested as prōgeniēs, signifying descent, lineage, family stock, or heroic descendants, prominent in Virgil's Aeneid celebrating Rome's imperial destiny through Augustus.
- Late Latin & Medieval Latin (4th–13th c.) — Pervasive in the Vulgate Bible and ecclesiastical theology to denote holy generations of faith or condemned lineages (e.g. progenies viperarum, "generation of vipers").
- Middle English (c. late 14th c.) — Borrowed via Old French progenie into Middle English; featured in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and Trevisa's chronicles to denote noble family bloodlines, breeding, and legal inheritance.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) — Widely deployed in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (Shakespeare, Spenser) to dramatize dynastic legitimacy, royal succession, and tragic heirs.
- 19th c. (Victorian era) — Adopted into the lexicon of natural history and evolutionary theory by Charles Darwin in On the Origin of Species, establishing its modern scientific foundation in reproductive success, parental investment, and phenotypic variation.
- 20th–21st c. — Firmly anchored in genetics, ecology, and population demographics, while flourishing across literature, philosophy, and technology as a refined metaphor for intellectual and digital successors.
