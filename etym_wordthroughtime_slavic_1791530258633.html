- Sixth to ninth centuries - Byzantine and Latin writers record Sklabēnoi, Sclaveni, Sclavi: an ethnographic label for peoples expanding across the Danube frontier, tinged with alarm
- Ninth to eleventh centuries - Christianisation and the Cyrillo-Methodian mission give the Slavs a written language; "Sclavonic" becomes a bookish label for the liturgy and its people
- Medieval Mediterranean - Sclavus narrows in legal and commercial Latin to mean "slave", and the ethnic name becomes entangled with captivity and servitude
- Sixteenth to eighteenth centuries - English borrows "Slavonic" and "Sclavonian" for the eastern European peoples and their church; the tone is antiquarian and slightly remote
- Nineteenth century - nationalist philology and Pan-Slavism turn "Slavic" into a proud family name, while racial anthropology gives it a dubious pseudo-biological edge
- Twentieth century - the Cold War makes "Slavic" a geopolitical shorthand, and Slavic studies becomes a major academic field
- Twenty-first century - the word is primarily linguistic and cultural, used neutrally in scholarship and journalism; the memory of the "slave" etymology keeps it sensitive
