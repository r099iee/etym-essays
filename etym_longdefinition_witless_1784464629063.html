Witless is an adjective meaning lacking wit — lacking intelligence, sense, or the ordinary power to understand and respond. It is an older, stronger, more serious word than stupid: it describes not merely slow thinking but a mind emptied or disabled, whether by congenital folly, by temporary stupefaction (fear, shock, exhaustion), or by the wreck of trauma and enchantment. In Tolkien's The Hobbit, Gandalf reports Thorin's father Thráin: He was witless and wandering, and had forgotten almost everything except the map and the key. The adjective here is not comic but tragic — a once-great dwarf king reduced to aimless wandering, his mind shattered by years in the dungeons of the Necromancer, retaining only the two tokens of his ruined quest. The pairing with wandering fixes the sense: a witless person is not merely mistaken but unmoored, unable to think clearly enough to remember, to choose, or to find his way. The tone is archaic-serious, almost clinical or legal in older English (found witless), here darkened by captivity and despair; the word's old-fashioned dignity is part of its power.

Polysemy — the full range of senses. Though built on a single negation, witless has several distinguishable uses:

  • Intellectual defect: lacking intelligence or good sense; foolish, senseless — a witless idea, a witless decision. Here the standard of comparison is ordinary common sense or judgment.
  • Stupefaction / dazed state: temporarily robbed of the power to think by fear, shock, astonishment, or illness — witless with fright, witless and wandering. This is the sense in the card context.
  • Mental derangement: mad, insane, out of one's wits — the older, stronger sense used in legal and formal language (he was found witless in the sense of mentally incapable).
  • Resultative intensifier scared/bored witless: a modern fixed construction in which witless means 'to the point of losing one's wits' — scared witless, bored witless, worried witless. Here the adjective describes not the subject's permanent nature but the extreme degree of the emotion named.
  • Modern reading via wit = verbal humour: because wit since the seventeenth century has also meant clever, sparkling humour, a modern reader may hear witless as 'humourless, unfunny, lacking cleverness' — a secondary contemporary reading that the word's history did not originally intend but which is fully live today.
  • Wordplay with wits = faculties: phrases like at one's wits' end or to lose one's wits keep alive the underlying image of the wits as the mind's faculties, which witless simply empties out.

Morphology and semantic bridges. The word is a transparent compound of wit (mind, understanding, sense — and later, verbal cleverness) and the privative suffix -less (without). The historical bridge from 'foolish' to 'dazed' is continuity: the oldest sense was the absence of wit (sense/intelligence); from there it naturally extended to any state in which the mental faculties are absent, whether permanently (folly, madness) or temporarily (fear, shock). The modern scared witless construction re-animates the same image — fear so strong that it empties the mind of its wits. Distinguish from near-synonyms: stupid names a plain lack of intelligence without drama; foolish names bad judgment, not an emptied mind; senseless can mean either 'without sensation' (unconscious) or 'without sense', and is blunter; dazed is purely the stunned, temporary state and carries no moral or intellectual judgment; brainless and empty-headed are informal and dismissive; daft is dialectal-British and lighter; mad and insane name clinical or informal derangement and are far stronger and more stigmatised. Witless keeps the archaic gravity of wit: it is the word for a mind not just slow but empty, a mind that has lost itself — exactly the tragic, wandering emptiness of Thráin, who had forgotten almost everything except the map and the key.
