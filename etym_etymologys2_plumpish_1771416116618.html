[ROOT CORE (PIE)]: *peyH- (to be fat, swell) — the primal image of fullness and increase │ ├── Indo-Iranian branch │ ├── Sanskrit: pīván- पीवन् “fat, plump, swelling”; pīvas- पीवस् “fat, marrow, richness” │ │ └── pyāyate प्यायते “swells, increases, grows fat” (Vedic verb classed with the *peyH- family) │ └── Avestan: pīva- “fat” — the sacrificial fat of Zoroastrian ritual │ ├── Hellenic branch │ ├── Greek: πίων (píōn) “fat, rich, fertile” (of flesh, of soil) │ │ ├── πιαίνω (piaínō) “to fatten, to make fat” │ │ └── πῖαρ (pîar) “fat, richness, abundance” (as in the Homeric πῖαρ χθονός, “the fat of the earth”) │ └── (the Greek ple-/pleth- “full” family is a distinct though parallel root, not a direct cognate) │ ├── Italic branch │ └── Latin: pinguis “fat, plump, thick” (adjective) │ ├── Italian: pingue “fat, rich” (formal, learned register) │ ├── Spanish: pingüe “fat, juicy, profitable” (e.g., pingües beneficios “fat profits”) │ └── Late Latin: pinguedo “fatness” → English: pinguid “fat, greasy, unctuous” (rare literary word) │ └── Germanic branch (handbooks debate whether *plump- continues *peyH- directly or blends with *pleu- “to flow, to float”; the linkage via the zero-grade is plausible but not certain) ├── Proto-Germanic: *plump- “massive, blunt, blocky” (a property of objects) │ ├── Middle Dutch: plomp “blunt, massive, coarse” │ │ └── Modern Dutch: plomp “plump, chubby” │ └── German: plump “clumsy, heavy, unwieldy” — the bluntness sense surviving └── English: plump “full and well-rounded” (17th c., first of blunt/blocky objects, then of bodies) └── English: plumpish “somewhat plump” = plump + -ish ├── suffix -ish < Old English -isc < Proto-Germanic *-iska- < PIE *-isko- “pertaining to, rather like, somewhat” └── plumpish recorded in 18th–19th c. descriptive prose; the card’s exact reading is “slightly plumpish”
