“Obstinate” entered English from Latin meaning fixed firmly against — stubbornly holding one’s ground, refusing to move or give way despite opposition.
“Obstinate” through the 16th–18th centuries carried a double edge: the admirable firmness of a resolute mind and the blameworthy mulishness of one who refuses reason — the word balanced between these in sermons and moral writing.
“Obstinate” in the 19th–20th centuries settled into everyday description of stubborn people and animals, and the adverb “obstinately” came to colour speech and action with a slightly abrasive persistence.
“Obstinately” today is the adverb of fixed refusal: to say or act obstinately is to hold to one’s point with a Latinate, slightly formal stubbornness that is both comic and admirable — a hobbit obstinately demanding plain terms from a dragon.
