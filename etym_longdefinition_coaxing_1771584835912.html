To coax is to win someone over by gentle, persistent flattery, soothing talk, and tender fondling rather than by force, argument, or authority. In this scene from Chapter 3 of Dream of the Red Chamber, Grandmother Jia is coaxing Bao-yu "like a little child": she answers his anguished tantrum over the jade with a made-up story, sweet words, and tender fondling, steering his "unbounded" temperament back to calm. The word captures the intimate, nurturing, and faintly manipulative texture of her management — hers is not a stern scolding but a diplomatic performance of love, flattery, and small fictions deployed to restore order in his troubled spirit. The tone is warm, matriarchal, and quietly theatrical; "coaxing" implies that the speaker controls the situation from behind a screen of sweetness, guiding the other person without ever confronting them. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES "Coaxing" is the present participle of "coax", and its full range of senses extends well beyond the literal "gentle persuasion":
  • Core interpersonal sense: persuading someone gradually and gently, especially by soft words, flattery, or fondling — a mother coaxing a child, a person coaxing a shy animal, a lover coaxing a sulking partner. The word always implies patience, softness, and a goal to be reached without force.
  • Applied to animals: luring or gentling an animal into doing something — coaxing a cat out from under a bed, coaxing a horse forward. Here the word implies patience, treats, and a soothing voice; it is the standard verb for persuading creatures that cannot be reasoned with.
  • Mechanical and instrumental sense (modern, semi-figurative): persuading a machine or device to work — coaxing an old car to start, coaxing a fire into flame, coaxing the last drops from a bottle. The word personifies the object, treating it as something obstinate that must be soothed or persuaded; this sense is common in informal speech and implies effort and patience against resistance.
  • Figurative and technical extensions: coaxing signals or data out of a reluctant source — "coaxing an answer out of him", "coaxing a signal out of the wire" — where the shared idea is extracting something that does not easily give itself up.
  • Historical and dated senses: in the 16th century "to coax" first meant "to make a fool or dupe of someone" — the word began as a slangy verb meaning to cajole, wheedle, or cheat by flattery, built on the noun "cokes" ("a fool, simpleton"). By the 1660s the meaning had softened to "wheedle, persuade by flattery", and the older sense of outright trickery dropped away. A faint trace of the old slyness survives even today in the word's implication of strategy: coaxing is sweet but calculated.
  • Related word-forms: "coax" (verb), "coaxing" (present participle and adjective: a coaxing voice), "coaxingly" (adverb), "coaxer" (one who coaxes), and the noun "coaxing" (the act: "after much coaxing, he agreed").
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE The bridge from "to dupe" to "to soothe into agreement" is the word's most interesting feature. When "coax" entered the language around 1580 it meant "to make a fool of, to dupe", an action performed with flattery and false sweetness; the 17th century then transferred the means to the end: since duping was done by honeyed words, the word came to mean "to persuade by honeyed words" whether or not deceit was involved. Over the following centuries the negative charge faded almost entirely, leaving a warm, patient verb of gentle persuasion — though the faint trace of manipulation (sweetness used as an instrument of control) remains in the word to this day, which is exactly right for Grandmother Jia's management of Bao-yu. SYNONYM BOUNDARIES AND REGISTER Set alongside its neighbours, "coaxing" occupies the tender end of the persuasion spectrum. "Persuading" is the neutral, reasoned verb: you persuade by argument, and it carries little emotional charge. "Cajoling" uses flattery but with a hint of deceit or nagging pressure. "Wheedling" is sly and ingratiating, aimed at personal advantage. "Enticing" and "luring" work through the appeal of the goal, not the speaker's manner. "Urging" is forceful and insistent. "Soothing" calms distress without necessarily steering toward action. "Coaxing" alone combines tenderness with gentle direction: it is persuasion softened into nurture. Register-wise, "coaxing" is standard, warm, everyday English, at home in domestic narrative, journalism, and casual speech; it is affectionate without being sentimental and strategic without being cold. In the novel it names precisely the matriarchal art that keeps the household running: not orders, but sweet, persistent, lovingly manipulative persuasion.
