The word Romany carries a double history: the linguistic history of the Romani language, which reaches back to ancient India, and the social history of how English speakers came to use an endonym instead of the older exonym Gypsy. The form itself is transparent within Romani: rom means "man, husband, a Rom", and romani is the feminine or adjective form, "of the Roma". English Romany, first recorded in 1812, is a borrowing of that adjective, used both for the language and for a person. Its rise belongs to the period when comparative philology was establishing the Indian origin of the Roma and when older Egyptian-origin legends were losing scholarly credit.

Indian origins and the Sanskrit background. Romani is an Indo-Aryan language, most closely related to the Central Indo-Aryan languages of northern India, with early connections to northwestern and Dardic varieties. Its core vocabulary preserves Indo-Aryan forms, and its grammar shows the typical reshaping of Middle Indo-Aryan. The ethnonym rom is usually derived from Sanskrit ḍomba-, a member of a low caste of musicians, via Prakrit ḍoṁba-. The change of retroflex d to r is regular in Romani, so ḍomba- yields rom. The derivation was suggested by Hermann Brockhaus in a letter of 1841, adopted by August Friedrich Pott in 1844, and has been mainstream in Romani studies ever since. It is not universally certain: the semantic leap from a caste of musicians to a general ethnonym is plausible but debated, and some scholars have proposed other origins. The word's double life as "man" and "husband" is typical of ethnonyms that begin as ordinary person-terms.

Migration and language contact. The ancestors of the Roma probably left the Indian subcontinent in the first millennium CE, moving through Iran, Armenia, Anatolia, and the Byzantine world before entering Europe in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries. Romani shows loanword layers from Persian and Kurdish, Armenian, Byzantine Greek, South Slavic, Romanian, Hungarian, German, and other languages, which provide a rough itinerary of contact. The language split into dialects as communities moved and settled. The Greek layer is particularly important for the early Byzantine period; the Romanian and Slavic layers mark the Balkan and Danubian phases. Modern Romani is therefore not a fossil but a stratified record of a long migration.

From Gypsy to Romany. Before the nineteenth century, English and most European languages used exonyms based on the false belief that the Roma came from Egypt: Gypsy, Gitano, Gitan, Aigyptios. The discovery of the Indian origin by Johann Rüdiger in 1777 and the comparative work of Pott and Miklosich in the nineteenth century changed scholarly understanding. Romany entered English in 1812 as the people's own name, and George Borrow's Lavengro (1851) and The Romany Rye (1857) made it familiar to a literary public, though Borrow's portrayal was heavily romanticized. In the twentieth century, Romani became the preferred academic spelling and Roma the preferred name for the people; Romany remained common in British English and in older bibliographic headings.

Sound changes and dialect variation. Romani phonology preserves some Indo-Aryan features and innovates in others. The retroflex series was partly lost or shifted; aspiration is distinctive; word stress varies by dialect; and many varieties have developed large vowel inventories under European contact. The change of d to r in rom is one of the diagnostic correspondences. Dialects differ sharply in vocabulary and grammar: Vlax Romani has strong Romanian influence, Balkan Romani shows Greek and Slavic influence, and Sinti and Kale varieties have their own histories. The English word Romany does not distinguish these varieties; it is a broad label for the whole linguistic and ethnic complex.

Socio-cultural context and modern usage. The Roma have suffered centuries of persecution, enslavement in the Romanian principalities, forced assimilation, and, under Nazism, genocide (the Porajmos). These experiences shaped both the language and the politics of naming. After the first World Romani Congress in 1971, Roma and Romani became markers of self-determination, while Gypsy was increasingly rejected as a slur. Romany occupies an intermediate position: it is an endonym in origin, but it is also old-fashioned and associated with Romantic-era writing. Modern style guides generally prefer Roma for the people and Romani for the language.
