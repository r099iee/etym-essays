1. The Indo-European root

The chain begins with PIE *bʰewdʰ- “to be awake, observe, make aware”. The root is rich in Indo-European: Sanskrit bódhati “he wakes, perceives, understands”; Greek peúthesthai “to learn, find out” (the Homeric middle voice); and, decisively, the Germanic branch, which developed the meaning “to make (something) known, to announce” — the act of rousing awareness. From “announce” came both “proclaim/command” and “offer/present (what is announced as available)”, and those two branches run straight into modern English “bid” and “bade”.

2. Proto-Germanic and the strong verb

Proto-Germanic had the class II strong verb *beudaną “to offer, command, proclaim”, with past singular *baud, plural *budun, participle *budanaz. Its cognates are everywhere in Germanic: Gothic biudan / anabiudan “to command, order”; Old Norse bjóða “to offer, invite” (still Icelandic’s everyday verb for inviting); Old Saxon biodan; Old High German biotan, modern German bieten “to offer”, with Gebot “commandment” as its noun. The Germanic system was remarkably stable: a single announcement-verb sliding between “command”, “offer” and “invite” in every major branch.

3. Old English: bēodan and biddan

Old English had bēodan (class II), whose recorded senses are “to announce, proclaim, command, offer, present, give up, foretell”; its past singular is bēad, plural budon, participle boden. Bēodan is frequent in the Anglo-Saxon laws, charters and poetry, where kings and thanes “beodan” commands and services. Alongside it stood the class V verb biddan “to ask, entreat, pray” (PIE *gʷʰedʰ- “to ask, long for”; cognate with Gothic bidjan “to pray”), with past bæd. The two verbs collided in Middle English: spelling, morphology and senses fused into a single “bidden” that could mean “ask”, “pray”, “command” and “offer”, so that today’s English “bid” is really two ancestral words wearing one coat — and “bade” the past of the imperious half.

4. Middle English and the birth of “bade”

Middle English records the past tense of the merged verb as bad / bād / bade, with senses “commanded, bade, invited, said (a greeting), offered”. The spelling “bade” (with final -e) stabilised in the later medieval period; the traditional modern pronunciation /bæd/ descends from the short-vowel form bad, while /beɪd/ is a spelling-pronunciation from the -e spelling (or from the old long-vowel bād).

5. Early Modern apex: Shakespeare and the Bible

In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries bade is a completely ordinary past tense. Shakespeare uses it constantly: “he bade me, from him, call thee Thane of Cawdor” (Macbeth, I.iii); Lear “bade” his daughters; messengers and heroes “bade” defiance. The King James Bible employs it throughout for divine and royal commands. The formulas “bade farewell”, “bade good-night”, “bade welcome” hardened in this period and have never quite died.

6. The nineteenth-century retreat

During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the verb “bid” was regularised in speech: the past tense “bid” (already common in auction-room usage) spread to the command senses, and bade withdrew into printed, formal and rhetorical diction. Grammarians kept it alive as the “correct” past for the command sense; the spoken language largely forgot it. By the twentieth century bade was a deliberate archaism — the property of the Bible, poetry, historical fiction, and fantasy.

7. Modern afterlife and the phonetic note

  • Surviving idioms: “bade farewell”, “bade welcome”, “bade them enter/stay”, “bade fair to”.
  • Fantasy register: Tolkien uses it naturally — “even though the king bade them stay a while in his halls” (The Hobbit, 1937, of the Elvenking’s invitation) — where the archaic form lends an ancient courtesy to the scene.
  • Pronunciation: /bæd/ (traditional RP) vs /beɪd/ (spelling pronunciation, common in America); both tolerated.
  • Sense split finalised: bade = commanded/invited/said farewell; “bid” = auction-offer, and, in modern casual speech, “command” as well.

8. Summary of the drift

  • PIE “awaken, observe” → Germanic “announce” → “offer, command”.
  • Old English bēodan (command/offer) + biddan (ask/pray) merge in Middle English.
  • Early modern: standard past tense; Shakespeare and the KJV.
  • Modern: archaic fossil of command, invitation and farewell.
