Multiple root words: one chain for the verb BATTER (to beat), and a separate chain for the noun "batter" (the cooking paste). Both converge through Latin battuere but diverge at Old French. CHAIN 1 — the verb BATTER "to strike repeatedly": - PIE *bhau- "to strike, beat, knock" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Gaulish *battuere "to beat, strike" (Celtic; the probable source of the Latin verb) → Latin battuere / batuere "to beat, strike" (rare in literary Latin, common in Vulgar Latin; borrowed from Celtic, not inherited through sound-change) → Old French batre "to beat, strike" (attested 11th c.) → Anglo-Norman batter "to beat" → Middle English bateren / batteren "to strike repeatedly with heavy blows" (frequentative: Middle English batten "to beat" + iterative suffix -er, 13th–14th c.) → Modern English BATTER "to strike repeatedly with violent, heavy blows" (battering-ram 1610s; domestic-abuse sense 1962). CHAIN 2 — the noun BATTER "the beaten cooking mixture": - PIE *bhau- "to strike" → Latin battuere "to beat" → Old French batre "to beat" → Old French bateure "a beating" (the act or product of beating) → English batter "the beaten paste of flour and liquid" (cooking sense; the mixture is beaten into being). Note on debate: the ultimate provenance of Latin battuere is disputed. Most scholars treat it as a loan from Gaulish/Celtic *battuere (the striking family also appears in Welsh bathu "to beat, mint" and Gaelic bat / bata "staff, cudgel"); a smaller group argues for a native Italic formation from PIE *bhau-. Either way the PIE *bhau- root is widely accepted for the wider striking family. The English noun "bat" (the cudgel, c. 1200) belongs to the same Celtic striking family, and the sports "batter" (the player) is its agent noun — a separate but kindred word from the verb BATTER.
