- 1st Century BCE to 4th Century CE (Classical Latin) - The compound verb *complacēre* meant to be very pleasing, to give great satisfaction, or to find favor with others.
- Mid-17th Century (c. 1640–1660) - Emerges in English as an adverb (from *complacent* + *-ly*), initially meaning with agreeable contentment, pleasant tranquility, or courteous satisfaction.
- Late 17th to Early 18th Century - Begins diverging from its twin *complaisantly*; while *complaisantly* retained the social duty of pleasing others, *complacently* increasingly focused on being pleased with oneself.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Acquires its definitive modern critical nuance: characterizes smug, narrow-minded self-satisfaction and bourgeois indifference to social injustice or imminent peril (Oscar Wilde, George Eliot).
- 20th to 21st Century - Universally employed in political, military, and corporate critique to describe the dangerous, uncritical arrogance that precedes catastrophic institutional failure.
