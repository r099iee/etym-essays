Parchments, the plural of parchment, names in its primary material sense sheets of animal skin — usually sheep, goat, or calf — cleaned, stretched, scraped, and dried into a durable cream-coloured writing surface that carried the law, literature, and liturgy of Europe for a thousand years before paper displaced it. True parchment is not leather: it is untanned skin, limed to remove hair and flesh, tensioned on a frame, and polished with pumice until it takes ink with a crisp dark line. Its finer grades have their own names — vellum for calfskin, uterine vellum for the skins of stillborn calves, thin as tissue — and its defects too: the hair side darker than the flesh side, the cockled edges, the holes the scribe had to write around.

In the source sentence the word wears its great secondary sense: not the blank sheets but the laden documents — sales and conveyances, recoveries, deeds, and other parchments — the engrossed legal instruments by which estates changed hands. Here parchments is metonymy made flesh: the material standing for the writings it bears, the skins for the seals dangling from them, the lawyer craft for the fraud it could solemnize. The speaker insists that amid all this vellum ceremony the real transfer lay elsewhere — a characteristically radical stroke, pitting living possession against dead skins, and using parchments as the contemptuous shorthand for the whole machinery of conveyancing.

Core meaning in detail: As a material, parchment is manufactured by soaking skins in lime pits, stretching them wet on wooden frames, shaving them with crescent knives, and dressing them with chalk and pumice. The result is tough, long-lived, and expensive: a single Bible could consume two hundred sheep, which is why medieval books are small and abbreviations dense. Parchment survives where paper perishes — the Domesday Book, Magna Carta exemplifications, and the American Declaration are skins, not sheets — and British Acts of Parliament were still enrolled on vellum until 2016. As documents, parchments in the plural almost always means title-deeds and muniments: the family chest of parchments is the archive of ownership, wills, settlements, and mortgages, tied with tape, yellowed at the folds, smelling faintly of dust and sheep.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Prepared animal skins for writing (primary material sense, from the 13th c.): the limed, stretched, scraped writing surface of the medieval book and the modern calligrapher. The bridge from the Pergamene origin is direct: skins prepared in the Pergamene manner became the standard luxury support wherever the codex ruled.
  • Legal deeds and title-documents collectively (great secondary sense, the source sense): the family parchments, musty parchments — deeds, conveyances, recoveries, charters. The bridge is material metonymy: the deed is called by the skin it is written on, exactly as a sailor calls a ship a bottom or a reader calls news the papers.
  • Diplomas, commissions, and certificates (modern ceremonial sense): university degrees still arrive as rolled parchments, and officers hold the parchment of their commission. The bridge is solemnity: the archaic material dignifies the modern honour, lending the weight of centuries to printer ink.
  • The complexion or skin likened to parchment (figurative descriptive use): parchment skin, a parchment face — aged, dry, yellowed, finely wrinkled. The bridge is visual and tactile: old human skin takes on the colour, thinness, and creasing of the old writing surface, a favourite simile of novelists describing age.
  • Parchment as a colour and texture in art and cookery (extended attributive sense): parchment tones in painting, cuisson en parchemin — baking in parchment paper. The bridge generalizes the cream, matte, slightly translucent quality of the skin to any surface sharing it, including the modern silicone-coated baking parchment, which contains no animal at all.
  • The historical era and its learning — the age of parchment (historiographical shorthand): the manuscript centuries before print, with their scriptoria, palimpsests, and chained libraries. The bridge is synecdoche of period by medium: as we say the age of print, so historians say parchment culture for the world that wrote on skins.

Register and connotation: parchments is antiquarian and evocative — archives, solicitors strong-rooms, fantasy novels — carrying the smell of dust and the authority of seals. The material sense is respectful and craft-honouring (calligraphers, conservators, Jewish scribes writing Torah scrolls on klaf), while the documentary plural often tilts satirical: musty parchments are the establishment hoard, the paper fortress of landlords and lawyers, and radicals from the source sentence author onward have waved them as exhibits of mystification. To call deeds parchments is thus already half an argument — it reduces law to skin — which is precisely why the word survives in political rhetoric long after solicitors switched to paper.
