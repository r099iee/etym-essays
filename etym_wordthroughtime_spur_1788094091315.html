- The horseman's tool (OE–ME): 'spur' is the plain metal instrument for urging a horse — concrete, practical, equestrian
- The knight's badge (13th–14th c.): golden spurs are conferred at knighthood; 'to win one's spurs' makes the word a symbol of honour and achievement
- The abstract goad (16th c.): the instrument's function becomes a metaphor — anything that urges or incites is a 'spur'; 'the spur of necessity' enters the language
- The mountain's point (17th–18th c.): geography borrows the shape — a 'spur of the mountain' is a projecting ridge; the word gains a landscape sense that survives in adventure prose
- The industrial spike (19th c.): railway spur lines, botanical spurs (larkspur, 1570s), geological and anatomical spurs multiply the technical senses
- Today: mostly figurative in daily speech ('on the spur of the moment', first recorded 1801; 'a spur to action'), with the equestrian and geographic senses alive in their own domains
