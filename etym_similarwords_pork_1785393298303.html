• pig: Similar because both belong to the same family of animal-and-meat words for the same creature; differs because a pig is the living animal (or the carcass in farming talk), whereas pork is the culinary flesh at the kitchen table — the split is the classic English livestock-versus-dish division.
  • swine: Similar because both refer to the same creature; differs because swine is the older, collective, or pejorative word for the animals, whereas pork is the neutral, modern name of the meat.
  • hog: Similar because both name the same beast in its food context; differs because hog is an American-English animal word (often for a large mature pig, or the slaughter-weight animal), whereas pork is the dressed meat itself.
  • ham: Similar because both are food-words for pig-meat; differs because ham is the specific cured hind leg, a single product, whereas pork keeps the general culinary sense of the whole flesh, fresh or cured.
  • bacon: Similar because both are pig products on the breakfast table; differs because bacon is cured and often smoked side-meat, sliced for frying, whereas pork is the general meat-name covering fresh, cured, and processed forms alike.
  • gammon: Similar because both name pig-meat as food; differs because gammon is specifically the cured or smoked hind leg of the pig (and a British slang word for humbug), a narrow product-name rather than the general term.
  • porc: Similar because it is the French word from which pork derives, and still means both pig and its meat; differs because porc is French (and the source of English pork), whereas pork is the anglicised culinary noun restricted to the flesh.
  • charcuterie: Similar because both concern pig-meat products; differs because charcuterie is a French culinary category of prepared pork products (terrines, pâtés, sausages) and the shop that sells them, not the simple meat-name.
  • pork barrel: Similar because it is a fixed extension of the same word; differs because pork barrel is an American political idiom for patronage spending, a wholly metaphorical use, whereas pork in the kitchen keeps the literal culinary meaning.
  • meat: Similar because both are general food-words for animal flesh; differs because meat is the hypernym covering all flesh, whereas pork is the specific product of the pig — one species' flesh among many.
