- 12th-13th c. - Old French jouster entered Anglo-Norman as an aristocratic term for mounted combat, knightly tournaments, and meeting in battle.
- 14th c. - Middle English coined the frequentative verb jostelen / justlen (from jousten + frequentative -le), meaning to tilt repeatedly, knock against, or collide in motion.
- 16th c. - Early Modern English (Tudor & Elizabethan prose) expanded the word from martial tilting to the everyday physical shoving and elbowing of urban street life (Shakespeare, Marlowe).
- 17th-18th c. - Solidified in social commentary and satire (Jonathan Swift, Alexander Pope) as the standard metaphor for ambitious courtiers and merchants competing ruthlessly for royal patronage.
- 19th c. - Victorian urban literature (Charles Dickens, William Makepeace Thackeray) deployed jostling to capture the industrial congestion, packed streets, and market dynamics of 19th-century London.
- 20th-21st c. - Ubiquitous in modern descriptive prose, sports commentary, and geopolitical analysis to describe both dense physical crowds and fierce institutional rivalry.
