Linear derivation chain for CHIDED (single root word; a native Germanic isolate): 1. Old English cīdan "to strive, contend, quarrel; to complain, murmur; to blame, find fault with" (weak class I verb, inflected cīde / cīdde / cīdod; attested from the 8th c.). This is the earliest recorded ancestor. It has NO certain cognates in any other Germanic language and NO reconstructible PIE root: the word is a native isolate of unknown ancestry. 2. Middle English chiden / chide "to rail, scold, complain; to blame" (first attested c. 1175, at first intransitive; the fully transitive sense "to scold (a person)" is attested from the 14th c.). The verb also begins to attract analogical strong forms - past chid, past participle chidden - on the model of ride/rode/ridden. 3. Early Modern English chide (16th-17th c.): common and colourful, used of people and of nature alike; the strong forms chid (used by Shakespeare) and chidden remain available into the 18th c. 4. Modern English chide "to rebuke mildly, gently, or playfully; to find fault with" - the sense narrowed and softened in the 19th-20th c. 5. English chided, the regular weak past tense and past participle of to chide (the standard modern forms, alongside the archaic chid / chidden) - CHIDED. Note on the debated origin: the Oxford English Dictionary describes cīdan as "of unknown origin." Anatoly Liberman (2014) tentatively proposed a link to Old High German *kîdal "wedge" (a stick for splitting wood), imagining the semantic route "brandishing sticks at someone" -> "to scold, reprove"; but no cognate set has been proven and the suggestion is speculative. Because the route is unverifiable, the word must still be classified as an isolate, and no PIE root is reconstructed for it.
