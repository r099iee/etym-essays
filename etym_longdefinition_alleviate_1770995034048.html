alleviate: transitive verb (and the basis of the noun alleviation and the adjectives alleviative / alleviatory). Its core sense is "to make (pain, suffering, a problem, or a burden) less severe, intense, or oppressive" — to lighten or ease something that presses down heavily upon a person, a body, or an institution.

PRIMARY SENSE (relief of suffering). The central and overwhelmingly dominant modern sense is the reduction of pain or hardship without necessarily eliminating its cause. When a physician prescribes a drug "to alleviate the symptoms of migraine," the medicine is expected to soften the acute attack, not to cure the underlying disorder. This is the word's cardinal behaviour: alleviate promises a lessening, a lightening, a bringing of a crushing weight back down to a tolerable level, rather than a complete removal. It is fundamentally a word of degrees — of making something less — rather than of perfect restoration. In the literary context from which this card is drawn ("Hoping in this way somewhat to alleviate the sense of desolation left by the death of their only heir"), the parents do not seek to erase their grief but to make it bearable; the education of their remaining daughter is used as a lever to lift a portion of the weight of mourning, and the sentence's own adverb "somewhat" confirms the partial, graded nature of the action.

SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES.

  • Physical relief — historically the earliest English sense: to lighten a physical load or ease bodily pain and discomfort ("a salve to alleviate the ache in his joints," "to alleviate the pressure of a swollen limb").
  • Emotional and psychological relief — to ease grief, anxiety, loneliness, or fear ("alleviate his anxiety before the examination," "alleviate the boredom of a long journey").
  • Economic and social relief — to reduce poverty, debt, unemployment, or housing pressure ("programmes designed to alleviate rural poverty," "measures to alleviate the cost-of-living crisis"); in development discourse, poverty alleviation is a standard fixed collocation.
  • Environmental and scientific usage — to lessen the severity of climate impact, pollution, congestion, or flooding ("tree-planting schemes to alleviate urban heat," "reservoirs built to alleviate seasonal water shortage").
  • Legal and administrative usage — to reduce burdens imposed by regulation, taxation, or liability (tax-relief measures described as alleviating the burden on small businesses).

GRAMMAR, COLLOCATION, AND REGISTER. alleviate is a transitive verb requiring a direct object that names the thing lightened, and it is used almost exclusively of things perceived as burdens or pains: pain, suffering, distress, poverty, boredom, congestion, pressure, symptoms, hardship, misery, loneliness. It does not normally take a person as its object ("alleviate me" is ungrammatical; one alleviates the suffering of a person). It is a formal, bookish, carefully measured word, typical of medical reports, policy documents, journalism, and literary prose, and it rarely appears in casual conversation. The register is compassionate but restrained: alleviate signals concern and competence while honestly refusing to promise a cure.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE. The verb's deep history (from Latin alleviare, formed on ad- "to, toward" + levis "light in weight") explains everything about its modern behaviour. A Roman alleviator literally lifted a weight off something; when the word entered English in the sixteenth century it was imported with this tactile, mechanical core — to raise or lighten a physical burden — and the whole later history of the word is a progressive transfer of that "lifting" metaphor from the shoulders of the body to the shoulders of the mind, the household, and the economy. Every modern sense — easing pain, easing grief, easing poverty — is the same ancient gesture of taking part of the load. Its near-synonyms mark the same territory by contrast: mitigate softens the harshness of an impact or consequence, ameliorate actively improves a bad state toward something better, palliate masks a disease's symptoms without addressing its cause, relieve is more immediate and can be complete, assuage soothes an inner appetite or emotion, and ease is looser and more general. Alleviate sits among them as the precise verb for "lightening the load" — partial, gradual, and never falsely hopeful.
