- 16th century classical adoption - brought into English as a learned borrowing from Greek oratory, naming the formal hymn of praise sung at the Greek games; the word wears its classical origin like a laurel, signalling erudition rather than emotion.
- 17th-18th century rhetoric and letters - becomes the standard English term for a set-piece tribute in prose or verse, at home in dedications, reviews, and funeral verses; prized as ornament, yet already suspected of extravagance.
- 19th century Victorian irony - used of lavish personal tributes (as in Mill's praise of his wife) where the surplus of admiration begins to look disproportionate; the word acquires a faint undertone of suspicion that the praise outruns the occasion.
- Contemporary use - rare, formal, and almost always faintly ironic: an "encomium" today implies an ornate tribute, more museum-piece than live emotion, reserved for pompous or self-conscious praise.
