Pièce de résistance is three histories in a trench coat: a Celtic scrap of land, a Latin preposition of descent, and a Roman soldier standing his ground — assembled in a French kitchen and exported to the world’s auction catalogues.

  • Pièce: the Celtic morsel: French pièce does not descend from classical Latin at all. It comes from Late Latin pettia/petium (“piece, piece of land”), itself borrowed from Gaulish — the Celtic language of pre-Roman France — where a root *pett- meant a part, share, or lot (compare Welsh peth, “thing,” Breton pezh, “piece”). This makes pièce a substrate survivor: the conquered Gauls’ word for ‘a bit of something’ outlived their language inside the conquerors’ Latin. Old French piece (11th c.) already spans the modern range: a morsel of food, a bit of cloth, a coin, a room (a ‘piece’ of the house), a written work, a dish. The circumflex-and-grave spelling pièce records the lost pre-consonantal s (cf. bête from beste). English borrowed piece itself from Old French in the 13th century, so the first word of the phrase is the speaker’s own cousin twice over.
  • De: the hardest-working syllable in French: Latin dē (“down from, out of, concerning”), from PIE demonstrative-adverbial *de-, bleached over two millennia into the universal French genitive-partitive linker: de = of, from, with, by, than. In the phrase it simply genitivises: “piece OF resistance.” Nothing resists here politically; the grammar only says the piece belongs to staying-power.
  • Résistance: standing one’s ground: Latin resistere = re- (back) + sistere (to stand, set, reduplicated from PIE *steh₂-, “to stand” — the root behind stand, state, statue, stage, establish). To ‘re-stand’ is to stand firm against pressure. Old French resister (12th c.) kept the verb; the abstract noun résistance crystallised in the 16th century for withstanding in war, physics, and morals alike. The culinary compound plays on an older nuance: the dish with staying power — the substantial joint that ‘holds the line’ after the light courses, the edible centre that endures in memory. Later political capital (the Résistance of 1940–44) re-heroised the word, but the roast got there first.
  • Assembly and export (1700s–1800s): French service (à la française, then à la russe) needed a name for the main joint, and pièce de résistance — the piece that carries the meal — became standard kitchen French by the 18th century; figurative use (the gem of a cabinet, the triumph of a fête) follows within decades. English gourmets, grandees on the Grand Tour, and post-Waterloo Paris diners carried it home; by the 1830s–1850s it seasons English novels, menus, and exhibition reviews, always italicised, always savoured. Anglicisation has been phonetic but never complete: /piːˌɛs də rəˈzɪstɒns/ keeps a French pout around an English jaw.
  • Why the phrase never died: every language needs a word for ‘the main thing’ that also says ‘and isn’t it splendid’; English synonyms (showpiece, highlight, main event) each drop half the payload. Pièce de résistance keeps both — centrality AND celebration — plus a wink of irony whenever, as in the source passage, the ‘roast’ turns out to be lice.

A Gaulish scrap, a Roman preposition, and a soldier’s stance walk into a kitchen: the order that emerges feeds two hundred years of menus, museums, and — in Durant’s hands — one unforgettable pot of lice.
