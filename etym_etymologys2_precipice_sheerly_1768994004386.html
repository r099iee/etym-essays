PRECIPICE (four-stage Latin chain from PIE to the borrowed noun): 1. PIE *kaput- (also written *kauput-) "head" (reconstructed, ca. 4000–3000 BCE; the same root gives Sanskrit kapúčchala "tuft on the crown of the head" and Germanic *haubudą, ancestor of English head). 2. Latin caput, capitis "head" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; caput is a direct reflex of *kaput- with regular sound changes; it is also the root of English capital, captain, capitol, chapter). 3. Latin praeceps, genitive praecipitis "headlong, rushing headfirst, precipitous" — formed from prae "before, in front" + caput "head," literally "with the head forward" (attested 1st c. BCE; the abstract noun praecipitium "a steep place, a precipice; a headlong fall" is derived from the stem praecipit- and is attested in Late Latin; note also praecipitāre "to throw headlong, to hurl down," source of English precipitate). 4. French précipice "a very steep cliff" (12th–16th c., from Latin praecipitium) — English borrowed the word from French, not directly from Latin. 5. English precipice "a very steep or perpendicular rock face, a cliff" (first attested 1590s, via Middle French précipice) — the target word precipice. The figurative sense "imminent danger, the brink of ruin" develops within English in the 17th–18th centuries. SHEERLY (five-stage Germanic chain; the PIE stage is the standard *skei- "cut/split," with the semantic route explained below): 1. PIE *skei- "to cut, split, separate" (reconstructed, ca. 4000 BCE; the root yields a vast family: Latin scindere "to cut," scire "to know" — literally "to separate one thing from another," i.e. to distinguish; Greek skhizein "to split," source of English schism and schizoid; Germanic *skī-). 2. Proto-Germanic *skīriz "clear, pure, bright" (ca. 500 BCE–200 CE) — derived from the "cut/separate" root with the sense development "separated, unmixed, free from admixture" > "pure, clean, clear." (An alternative account derives it from a "shine" root; see the debate note in etymologyS3.) 3. Old English scīr "bright, clear, pure; transparent" (attested from the 8th c. CE; the same word survives in Modern English shire, originally "a district," from the "clear/separate" sense). 4. Middle English schere "clear, sheer, thin, transparent; pure, unmixed" (13th–15th c.); the sense "steep, perpendicular, unobstructed" develops in the 16th c. from the idea of a clean, clear, unbroken drop. 5. English sheer (adjective, adverb) "absolute, utter, complete; transparent; perpendicular, steep" + the suffix -ly (Old English -līce) — English sheerly (adverb, attested from the 16th c.) — the target word sheerly. DEBATED STAGE (stated explicitly): the "bright/shining" sense of Old English scīr is sometimes referred to a separate PIE root *skēi- "to shine" (cf. Greek skiā "shadow," with a peculiar opposite development), rather than to *skei- "to cut." Mainstream dictionaries (OED, Etymonline) prefer the "cut/separate" derivation — "pure" as "free from admixture, cleanly separated" — and it is the account followed here; the "shine" alternative, which would make sheer originally a word of brightness like clear, remains a live minority view. The vertical sense of sheer is not inherited but English-internal: a sheer cliff is one that falls away "clear," without break or obstruction, as if cleanly cut.
