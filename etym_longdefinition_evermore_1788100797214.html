Evermore (also spelled ever-more, and in older texts evermo, euermore) is an adverb meaning "for ever, always, at all future time, eternally" — the compound of ever ("at any time; always") and more ("further, in addition"), fused into a single word meaning literally "ever further on", and so "unceasingly onward in time". It is the grand, archaic-leaning member of the English family of eternal-duration words: forever is its plain everyday cousin, evermore its solemn, literary, and liturgical one. When Wilde's God promises that the little bird "shall sing for evermore" in the garden of Paradise, the word gives the sentence its eternity: not a long time, but time without end.

Primary sense: eternal or unending duration. The core meaning is duration without end, looking forward from the speaker's present: "for evermore" means "for all time to come". Historically the word also carried the sense "always, constantly, at every moment" — so Chaucer's narrators promise to remember evermore, and older writers say a thing is evermore true — and that habitual sense survives in literary English alongside the eternal one. In liturgy and hymnody the word is at home: "from this time forth and for evermore" is the English form of the doxological clause; "as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end" is its kin; and the marriage service, the psalter, and the prayers all ring with everlasting "evermore". The Bible's Authorized Version of 1611 uses it in its unforced natural voice: "Lord, evermore give us this bread" (John 6:34) — the crowd at Capernaum asking for the bread of heaven at every hour.

Secondary and stylistic senses.

  • Emphatic "for ever and ever" — "evermore" can double or intensify: "for ever and evermore", "now and evermore", heightening the solemnity; here the word is pure rhetoric of eternity.
  • The habitual sense (older literary use) — "always, without exception": "he was evermore at her side"; this sense, common in medieval and early modern prose, is now mostly poetic.
  • The negative twin, "nevermore" — the same formation with never: "at no time henceforth, never again", fixed forever in readers' minds by Poe's raven (1845), whose "Nevermore" is evermore's dark mirror — the difference of a single letter buys the whole difference between hope and despair.
  • The connective "ever more" — as two separate words, "ever more" means "increasingly" (ever more anxious), a modern construction distinct from the fused adverb; the confusion is common enough to need untangling in dictionaries.

Register and boundaries. Evermore is formal, literary, and faintly archaic in ordinary speech: no one says "I'll be evermore tired", but everyone recognises "for evermore" in vows, hymns, and poetry. It differs from forever (the everyday standard word), from eternally (which describes what exists outside time, or without beginning as well as end), from always (which includes the past and present — "always" is at all times, "evermore" is from now on without end), from everlastingly and perpetually (continuous, but formally drier), and from the poetic aye ("for aye"). In the story the word is the last word of hope: the bird that sang and died for love is promised song "for evermore" in Paradise — the compound's time-without-end is God's reply to the dust-heap.
