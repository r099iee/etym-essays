In Macaulay's sentence "and bound up together the rights of the people and the title of the __________ dynasty," the word reigning is a present participle used as an adjective, meaning "currently in possession of the throne." The referent is the dynasty established by the Glorious Revolution of 1688 — William III and Mary, and their successors — whose very title Macaulay is at pains to present as resting on, and inseparable from, the constitutional rights of the English people. The nuance of reigning as opposed to ruling is exactly the point: a reigning monarch is the present occupant of the royal office, the one who holds the title, whatever its legal source, while a ruling monarch actively exercises government. Macaulay's constitutional argument is that after 1688 the title to reign and the rights of the people were "bound up together" — one could no longer exist without the other. The tone is solemn, legal, and measured, the vocabulary of a parliamentary historian weighing the legitimacy of crowns.

At its core, reigning means "currently holding royal office, presently on the throne," and is formed from the verb reign "to hold royal office, to rule as a monarch" plus the present-participial suffix -ing. It is used almost exclusively of monarchs and dynasties, and it carries a specifically temporal sense: a reigning sovereign is the one who occupies the throne now, as opposed to a former, future, or nominal sovereign.

  • Royal and constitutional sense (the primary meaning): currently on the throne, holding royal office by title — "the reigning monarch," "the reigning queen," "the reigning dynasty," "the reigning house," "reigning sovereigns." This is the sense in Macaulay's sentence.
  • Distinction from "ruling": reigning stresses occupancy of the throne and possession of the title, whatever the actual exercise of government; a constitutional monarch may reign while ministers rule — a distinction familiar in the phrase "the queen reigns but does not rule."
  • Sporting metaphor (modern): "the reigning champion" — the current, undefeated title-holder in a sport or competition, by analogy with a monarch presently on the throne; also "reigning world champion," "reigning titleholder."
  • Figurative and social extensions: "reigning beauty" (the acknowledged queen of a season's society), "reigning fashion" (the dominant mode), "reigning opinion" — the current, acknowledged, pre-eminent holder of some position by common assent.
  • Verb and other forms: the verb reign ("to hold royal office; to prevail, be supreme"), the noun reign ("the period of a sovereign's rule"), the phrase reign supreme ("to be unchallenged").

In this constitutional sense reigning must be set against its near neighbours. Ruling stresses the active exercise of power over subjects or territories and can be used of any authority, from a monarch to a junta or a court; reigning stresses occupancy of the throne, often with a ceremonial or hereditary colour, and is properly reserved for royalty. Governing is the administrative side of rule, the conduct of affairs, applicable to presidents, ministers, and councils as much as to kings. On the throne is the periphrastic idiom for the same idea, more casual and narrative in flavour. Incumbent is the modern, bureaucratic word for whoever currently holds any office, and is far too colourless for royalty. Outside politics, reigning has a well-established metaphorical life: a reigning champion is the current, undefeated title-holder in sport, a reigning beauty the acknowledged queen of a season's society, the reigning fashion the dominant mode — in all of which the word carries a hint of acknowledged pre-eminence held by title, not merely by force.

Collocationally the word is found above all with royal and dynastic nouns: reigning monarch, reigning sovereign, reigning king or queen, reigning dynasty, reigning house, and the historical phrase reigning sovereigns. Its register is formal and, in the political sense, nearly technical, the natural language of constitutional history and public law. Note the homophone: reign "to rule" (from Latin rēgnāre) must not be confused with rein "a strap for guiding a horse" (from Latin retinēre, "to hold back"), with which it is etymologically unrelated despite identical pronunciation; to give free rein and to reign supreme belong to two different word-families.

Polysemy & Modern Usage. In sports and social life, reigning describes current championship title-holders ("reigning champion") or prevailing modes and moods. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: Latin rēgnāre "to be king, to reign" via Old French regner and Middle English reignen (13th c.) shifted from royal governance to sporting title-holding and dominant conditions: the image of the monarch currently occupying the throne was transferred to any current, acknowledged holder of pre-eminence — the champion who holds the title "now," whatever may come later.
