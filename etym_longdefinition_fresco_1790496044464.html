Fresco in English most precisely means a wall-painting executed on freshly laid wet lime plaster, so that the pigments bind chemically with the setting plaster, and by extension any painting in that technique, the wall or chapel containing it, and the method itself. In the source fragment, a truncated Ruskin note reading The head of the Emperor in the fresco in the Spanish Chapel, the word is used in this strict art-historical sense: a figure painted in buon fresco on the wall of the Cappellone degli Spagnoli (Spanish Chapel) in Santa Maria Novella, Florence, the great Dominican chapter-house frescoed by Andrea di Bonaiuto c. 1365-1367. Ruskin, who measured his life by such walls, singles out one imperial head among dozens of saints, allegories, and Dominicans.

Technically, true or buon fresco (Italian fresco buono, good fresh) works thus: the mason lays a rough arriccio, the painter transfers the cartoon by pouncing or incision, then lays each day's patch of smooth intonaco (lime putty plus sand), and paints swiftly while it is wet with pigments ground in water, without egg or oil binder. The lime carbonates as it dries, locking colour into crystalline calcium carbonate. The join of each day's patch remains visible as a giornata. Mezzo-fresco paints on nearly dry plaster, fresco secco paints with lime-water or casein on fully dry plaster for retouching blues and reds that alkali would burn. The result, when sound, is incomparably durable and matte, pale and luminous, part of the wall itself rather than a skin upon it. Pompeian walls, Giotto's Arena Chapel, Masaccio's Brancacci, Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling, and the Spanish Chapel are all substantially buon fresco.

As an Italian adjective, fresco simply means fresh, cool, new: acqua fresca (cool water), pane fresco (fresh bread), aria fresca (cool air), tener fresco (keep cool). English preserves this only in phrases like fresco room or al fresco (in the cool, outdoors, dining outside). The semantic bridge is direct: fresco painting is painting done on fresh, still-wet plaster (Italian pittura a fresco, painting on the fresh). What is fresh is cool, damp, and newly made; the wall is fresh for only eight to ten hours, so the painter must be swift, decisive, and master of the whole figure.

  • Core art-technical noun (countable): a single painting on wet plaster or a detail from one; for example the Spanish Chapel frescoes, the head of the Emperor in the fresco, Giotto's frescoes at Padua. Plural frescoes or frescos, also frescoes cycle. This is the source sense and dictionary head sense in English.
  • Uncountable method noun: the technique itself; for example to paint in fresco, master of fresco, the decay of fresco in damp churches. Opposed to oil, tempera, mosaic, and secco. Here fresco names a craft with apprenticeship, lime, scaffolds, and giornate.
  • Building or surface by metonymy: the wall, chapel, or room so painted; for example the fresco in the Spanish Chapel equals the wall-painting in that chapel. The bridge is container for content: the room is called by what covers it.
  • Italian fresh, cool, crisp (adjective, chiefly in Italian contexts): cool and refreshing, newly made, unspoiled; for example fresco wine, fresco shade. In English seen in al fresco dining (eating in the fresh cool air), fresco balconies, fresco linens. The bridge is temperature plus newness: what is fresh feels cool.
  • Figurative freshness (rare in English, common in Italian): a fresh complexion (viso fresco), fresh news (notizia fresca), a fresh recruit. English fresco does not normally carry this, but translators meet fresco di giornata (the day's fresh news) and must not render it as wall-painting. The bridge is metaphor: new information is fresh plaster.
  • Wine and food jargon (Italian borrowing): vino fresco (chilled light young wine), formaggio fresco (fresh uncured cheese). Again fresh equals young, cool, unaged. English menus keep the Italian to signal authenticity.

Grammatically fresco is a masculine Italian noun and adjective (plural freschi, feminine fresca, fresche) naturalised in English as a fully countable noun (a fresco, two frescoes) and as a combining element (fresco-painter, fresco-technique, frescoed adjective). Pronounced in Received Pronunciation as FRES-koh, in Italian as FRES-ko with trilled r. It must not be confused with fresco secco (dry-wall retouching), affresco (the full Italian phrase for the technique), or refreshing fresco air (the cool sense). In Ruskin the word is always reverent: fresco is the moral, public, architectural art of faith, opposed to private easel oil which he thought selfish and perishable.
