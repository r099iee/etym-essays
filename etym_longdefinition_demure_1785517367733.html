DEMURE (adjective). Reserved, modest, and shy in manner or behaviour — a composed quietness of bearing, typically regarded as proper, though sometimes affectedly so. The core of demureness is a deliberate, gentle self-containment: lowered eyes, soft voice, folded hands, a refusal of attention that is itself a kind of dignity. Etymologically the word descends from the Old French phrase de meur, 'of mature years, settled, grave', so that a demure person was originally a sober, settled one — and that ancestry explains why demureness always carries a hint of restraint and control rather than of mere timidity. The card's context sets the word at its most delicate: 'She entered the great hall with a demure poise that seemed almost out of place among the raucous banquet guests, her eyes lowered and her hands folded neatly before her.' Here demure does double work. On one reading it describes genuine quiet reserve — a dignified, old-fashioned propriety that contrasts sharply with the noise of the feast; on another, the phrase 'almost out of place' hints at the word's darker undertow, for demure can also describe reserve that is performed, modesty that advertises itself, a poise calculated to be noticed precisely because it refuses to court attention. The word hands the writer a knife-edge instrument: it can render a heroine genuinely modest or render a rival quietly strategic, and the surrounding text decides which. Against raucousness, demure poise is either a reproach to the feast or a performance for it — and the ambiguity is the whole point. Polysemy — the word's senses and their historical bridges:
  • Sense 1 — Sober, settled, grave (archaic). The earliest English sense (c. 1400), preserving the original 'mature, steady' meaning: a demure person was calm, composed, and serious, often of mature years. The bridge to the modern sense is the easy step from 'settled and grave' to 'quiet and modest in bearing'.
  • Sense 2 — Reserved, modest, and shy in manner; quiet and proper. The modern core sense, usually applied to women and girls but available for anyone: a demure manner is soft-spoken, unassuming, self-effacing. This is the sense the card's context exploits.
  • Sense 3 — Affectedly or ostentatiously modest; coy, prim. The pejorative edge: demure can describe modesty that is put on — a primness, a false shyness, a coyness designed to be admired. The bridge is that real and performed reserve look exactly alike from the outside, so the word easily slips into accusation. This is the sense in 'she gave a demure little smile'.
  • Sense 4 — Of things: quietly unassuming, understated, discreet. By extension from persons, demure is used of clothing, colours, decor: 'a demure grey dress', 'demure tailoring'. The bridge is personification — the garment behaves modestly. In fashion, demure means covering, sober, and elegant rather than bold or revealing.
  • Sense 5 — Modern ironic revival: 'very demure, very mindful' (2020s). The viral TikTok idiom of 2024 recast demure as playful, self-aware, gently parodic performance — 'I'm very demure, very mindful, very cutesy'. The bridge runs straight back to Sense 3: the performed-modesty reading, re-appropriated as comedy. The 2024 Word of the Year by several dictionaries is the newest chapter in a word that has always balanced sincerity against show.
Synonym boundaries — each near-synonym stresses a different component:
  • Modest: similar in denoting humility and lack of ostentation; differs because modest is the broadest term, an internal attitude of humility about oneself and one's achievements (one can be modest about a discovery without being demure in manner), whereas demure is a quality of outward bearing and quietness.
  • Coy: similar in denoting shyness or reserve; differs because coy foregrounds performance and usually carries a flirtatious, coquettish edge — coyness is demureness with an agenda, the pretence of shyness designed to attract; demure at its best is genuine.
  • Reserved: similar in denoting emotional restraint; differs because reserved stresses a reluctance to reveal oneself or one's feelings, without the specifically behavioural, decorous, and often feminine coding that demure carries.
  • Retiring: similar in suggesting shrinking from attention; differs because retiring implies a temperamental withdrawal, less poised, deliberate, and socially graceful than demure.
  • Prim: similar in suggesting proper, precise modesty; differs because prim stresses neat, precise, conventional correctness and a touch of disapproval or prudishness, whereas demure is softer and more gentle.
  • Shy: similar in suggesting timidity; differs because shy implies actual nervousness or fear of attention, while demure implies a composed, controlled quietness that may be entirely self-possessed.
  • Proper: similar in suggesting decorum; differs because proper is a broad judgement of conformity to social rule, while demure describes the specific quiet manner.
  • Sedate: similar in denoting calm, composed gravity; differs because sedate implies a placid, unhurried calm (perhaps dull), while demure adds modesty and self-effacement.
  • Staid: similar in denoting sober, settled gravity; differs because staid can imply dullness or stuffiness, while demure is gentler and carries the softness of shyness.
Demure is used almost exclusively of demeanour, appearance, and manner — not of actions performed but of the style in which they are performed. Its register is literary rather than technical, with a long history in fiction, where it has served novelists from the 18th century to the present as the exact instrument for describing quiet women — and for raising the question of whether their quietness is sincere.
