Curtseys is the plural of the noun curtsey (also spelled curtsy), and it names the gesture of social deference in which a woman or girl bends her knees and dips her body slightly, often gathering or spreading her skirt, as a formal mark of greeting, respect, or submission. In this scene from Dream of the Red Chamber, the newly arrived Dai-yu rises to meet her cousins and "exchanged curtseys and introductions." The plural form matters: a curtsey is here not an isolated act but a reciprocal ritual performed by both parties at once, so that the "exchange of curtseys" becomes a mutual recognition of rank. Each dip of the knee and inclination of the head is a small, choreographed statement about where the other person stands in the family's intricate web of seniority — the elder and the younger cousin, the hostess and the guest, the household insider and the newcomer. The translation renders an aristocratic Qing-dynasty greeting through a Western gesture because curtsey, too, belongs to a vanished etiquette of the great house: a vocabulary of the knee and the hem in which politeness was measured by the exact degree of the bend.

The word is richly polysemous, and every sense traces a historical bridge back to the courtly ideal of "courtesy":

  • Sense 1 — the formal greeting gesture (primary). The full, slow curtsey of polite society: knees bent, body lowered, head inclined, performed to a superior, a hostess, or a dignitary. This is the sense on the card, and it is the direct continuation of the 16th-century usage "to make a courtesy," in which the whole vocabulary of courtly virtue was compressed into a single graceful motion.
  • Sense 2 — court ceremonial and royal audiences. The curtsey before a monarch or at a presentation at court, still the living protocol for women in royal presence today; historically it was drilled into young ladies by dancing-masters and governesses, so that a girl's curtsey publicly marked her breeding and her family's standing.
  • Sense 3 — the quick and informal variants. The "bob" is a small, rapid, almost perfunctory curtsey, the servant's hurried version; a woman may also "drop" or "make" a curtsey. These lightened forms show the gesture relaxing from ritual into social reflex while retaining the same bodily grammar.
  • Sense 4 — the verb. To curtsey (or curtsy): "she curtsied to the Queen," "the company curtsied and bowed." The verb is a 16th–17th-century back-formation from the noun and shares its full range of register and collocation.
  • Sense 5 — metaphorical and mock-archaic uses. In modern prose and comedy, "to drop a curtsey" or "to make one's curtsey" is used for exaggerated, theatrical, or ironic deference — a verbal or behavioural "curtsey" of flattery — and in period fiction the word supplies deliberate period colour. The gender-marking of the gesture (female, in contrast to the male bow) is part of its meaning, and "curtsey" is sometimes used figuratively to signal old-fashioned, demure feminine politeness.

Boundaries against near neighbours are precise. A bow inclines the upper body from the waist, is used by men and women alike, and need not involve the knees; genuflection drops the performer to one knee and carries religious or quasi-sacred weight before an altar; obeisance is the broad umbrella term for any act of homage; and a kowtow touches the forehead to the ground, marking far deeper submission than the proud, self-possessed curtsey. In collocation, one makes, does, drops, or bobs a curtsey, and a company exchanges curtseys upon first meeting, exactly as on this card. In register the word is archaic-to-formal, surviving in royal ceremonial, historical fiction, and deliberate period effect; etymologically it is a late 16th-century specialization of courtesy, and its whole history, as the derivation chain below shows, runs from the enclosed court of a great house to the exact angle of a young lady's knee.
