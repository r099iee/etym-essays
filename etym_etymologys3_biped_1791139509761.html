The English word biped is a 17th-century learned borrowing from Latin bipes, bipedis, itself a transparent compound of bi- “twice, having two” and pēs, pedis “foot.” Both elements continue Proto-Indo-European roots: ped- from PIE *ped- “foot” (also seen in Greek pous, Sanskrit pád-, English foot via Grimm’s law) and bi- from PIE *dwóh₂ “two” (Latin duo, Greek dyo, English two), reduced in compounds to bi- before consonants. Latin bipes was already both adjective (“two-footed”) and substantive (“a two-footed creature”), used by Pliny and other naturalists to contrast men and birds with quadrupeds and serpents.

English naturalists anglicized the form directly as biped in the 1640s, during the great influx of Neo-Latin scientific vocabulary. Early attestations in Thomas Browne and the Royal Society circle use it adjectivally (“biped animals”) and nominally (“bipeds”). Phonologically nothing dramatic happened: Latin /biːpeːs/ was regularized to English /ˈbaɪpɛd/, with the Great Vowel Shift already having raised bi- to /baɪ/ and the final -es levelled to /ɛd/ on the analogy of quadruped, centipede. Orthographically the Latin stem biped- was retained whole, which is why the plural is bipeds rather than *bipedes.

  • 1640s–1700: anatomical coinage: physicians and virtuosi need a precise opposite to quadruped; biped fills the slot in bird and human anatomy.
  • 1700s–1850: Linnaean classification: Bipedes appears as an order or habit-group in systems of birds and mammals; the word is purely descriptive.
  • 1859–1900: Darwinian reframing: after the Origin and Descent of Man, being a biped links man to apes through locomotor evolution rather than separating him by soul; satirists seize on “featherless biped” from Plato via Diogenes.
  • 1900–1970: palaeoanthropology: Dart, Broom, the Leakeys, and Laetoli footprints make “obligate biped” and “bipedalism” central technical terms; bipedal overtakes biped as adjective.
  • 1970–present: robotics and ecology: humanoid engineers adopt “biped robot / biped locomotion,” while environmental writers revive the pejorative “marauding biped” to criticize humanity’s footprint — the exact tone of the source sentence.

Semantically the drift is from form (“having two feet”) to habit (“walking on two feet”) to identity (“man as animal”) to machine (“walking on two actuated legs”). Morphologically English derived a small family: bipedal (adjective, now dominant), bipedalism and bipedality (the condition), bipedally (adverb). The doublet bipod (two-legged gun rest) splits off in the 20th century for artefacts, leaving biped for animate or humanoid walkers. No serious etymological debate surrounds the Latin origin; the only nuance is whether English took it directly from Latin bipes or via French bipède (attested slightly earlier). The OED favours direct Latin borrowing with French reinforcement, which is typical for 17th-century science.
