Core definition. Scrutinized is the simple past and past participle of scrutinize, denoting a careful, minute, and searching examination — a looking that intends to see beneath the surface. To scrutinize is not merely to look but to sift, to probe, to test every part against expectation. The word's force lies in exhaustiveness plus judgment: the scrutinizer searches every element, assumes nothing, and holds what is found up to evaluation. Etymologically the word is rooted in Latin scrūtārī, "to search through rubbish," and that origin never quite leaves it — scrutiny implies that truth must be dug out of appearance, that the valuable thing may be hidden among broken bits. In the literary context. In Dream of the Red Chamber the word carries one of the novel's most charged moments of social reading: "Xi-feng took Dai-yu by the hand and for a few moments scrutinized her carefully from top to toe before conducting her back to her seat beside Grandmother Jia." Dai-yu, newly arrived from the south, is being appraised by the household's formidable manager — her health (she is frail), her dress, her beauty, her manners, her potential as a rival or ally. The verb's tone is therefore sharp and penetrating: it is not a warm look of welcome but an inventory, an evaluation carried out in full view. Because scrutiny etymologically means digging through rubbish for the truth, the word implies that Dai-yu's appearance must be read past — that her surface must be sifted for what she really is — and that is exactly what Xi-feng does, reading the girl "from top to toe" as an assessor reads a document. Exhaustive sense inventory. The word's senses form a tight cluster around minute examination, with significant extensions in political, legal, and social life:
  • Core — to examine minutely, searchingly, and carefully (since c. 1671): the general sense of looking at every part of a thing to discover its true nature; applies to documents, objects, faces, and scenes.
  • Legal and professional — to inspect a document or record with forensic attention: contracts, accounts, evidence, and financial statements are scrutinized for errors, irregularities, and concealed details; the sense of professional due diligence.
  • Political — the counting and checking of votes: from the noun scrutiny, the formal examination of a ballot or election result; in Britain and the Commonwealth a scrutineer is an official who checks votes, and in Australia and New Zealand the scrutineer is a party's appointed watcher at the polling station or count.
  • Social and interpersonal — to appraise a person closely, often uncomfortably: to scrutinize someone is to subject them to a searching, evaluative look that can feel invasive; the sense at work in the Xi-feng passage, where appraisal is both social ritual and power play.
  • Modern surveillance and public-life sense — to be under scrutiny: since the twentieth century the noun and verb increasingly describe being watched and judged by the media, institutions, and the public; "under scrutiny" now routinely means "subject to critical, potentially exposing oversight," with an adversarial or invasive connotation.
  • Ecclesiastical-historical — the scrutiny of catechumens: in the early church, the scrutinium was the formal investigation and examination of candidates for baptism; the word's technical religious sense, from which the general sense grew.
The semantic bridge. The bridge between the senses is the act of sifting. Latin scrūta, "rubbish, broken bits," produced scrūtārī, "to search through refuse," and from rummaging in junk the verb climbed to examining anything with the same exhaustive care — ballots, documents, catechumens, faces. The political sense comes from the scrutinium of the medieval church and the counting of votes; the social sense from the appraising gaze; and the modern surveillance sense from the extension of "examination" to "being examined by the world." Throughout, the word keeps its sharp edge: scrutiny is never idle looking, always the search for what may be hidden. Against near-synonyms. Among its near-synonyms the word marks the intense end of a spectrum. Examined is the neutral term — methodical, often professional, but without the sense of hunting for concealed detail. Inspected is more systematic and official, the word of auditors and building codes, and it is impersonal where scrutiny is intimate and evaluative. Studied describes a prolonged, absorbed, often appreciative gaze (to study a face) and lacks the critical edge. Surveyed is broad and panoramic — a survey takes in a whole room quickly but shallowly. Scanned is the quick, cursory opposite, barely touching the surface. Perused stays with documents and books. Analyzed emphasizes breaking a thing into parts but is intellectual rather than visual. And ogled is the vulgar, sexualized staring against which scrutiny's cool professionalism is defined. What distinguishes scrutinized from them all is exhaustiveness plus judgment — the sifting, evaluative search that no quick glance can accomplish.
