The English word Celtic is one of the oldest ethnonyms of Europe and also one of the most opaque. It enters written history not in a Celtic language but in Greek: the geographer Hecataeus of Miletus, writing about 500 BC, already uses Κελτοί (Keltoí) for the peoples of the upper Danube, and Herodotus in the fifth century BC places the Keltoi beyond the Pillars of Hercules and on the Danube, so that the name was firmly established in Mediterranean literature by the classical age. The people themselves almost certainly called themselves something like *kelto-, but the meaning of that self-designation is disputed. The traditional etymology, championed by generations of Indo-Europeanists, connects *kelto- with the Proto-Indo-European root *ḱel- ‘to hide, conceal,’ making the Celts “the hidden ones” — a withdrawn, mysterious people. A rival school prefers the homophonous root *kel- ‘to strike, cut,’ making them “the warriors, the smiters.” Both derivations are phonologically possible, and honest accounts simply state the uncertainty. What is certain is the history of the name in Greek and Latin. Greek formed the adjective Κελτικός (Keltikós) and the regional noun Κελτική (Keltikḗ), the ‘Celtic land.’ The Romans, who met the same peoples on the Po and in Gaul, adopted the Greek name as Celtae and Celticus; Julius Caesar, in the opening of the Gallic War, records that the people of central Gaul were “in their own language called Celtae, in ours Galli.” The Roman use was confined to ethnography: for most Latin writers the Galli (Gauls) were the practical name, and Celtae retained a Greek, learned colouring. English received the word through the humanist revival. The adjective Celtic is attested from 1607, taken from French Celtique and Latin Celticus, and for a century it remained a learned term for the ancient peoples described by Caesar and Strabo. The decisive semantic expansion came in 1707, when the Welsh antiquary Edward Lhuyd published Archaeologia Britannica and proposed “Celtic” as the family name for the related languages of Wales, Ireland, Scotland, Cornwall, and Brittany — a classification that proved correct and that gave the word its modern linguistic sense. From the mid-18th century, with the rise of comparative philology, “Celtic” became the standard label for the westernmost branch of the Indo-European family, the branch that once filled the map of western and central Europe and now survives at the Atlantic fringe. The nineteenth century added the romantic and national layers. As the Celtic languages retreated before English and French — the very retreat the source passage describes from the Latin side — writers, antiquarians, and nationalists revalued the Celtic past. The “Celtic Revival” in Ireland, the “Celtic Twilight” of Yeats and the Irish literary movement, the Welsh eisteddfodau, and the Breton and Scottish revivals made “Celtic” a badge of identity, poetry, and spiritual antiquity — a counter-tradition to industrial, classical, or English culture. Matthew Arnold's 1867 lectures on Celtic Literature crystallized the stereotype of the Celt as emotional, imaginative, and unsystematic, a trope that scholarship has since worked to dismantle. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries “Celtic” has become a fully modern identity term. The Celtic nations — Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, Brittany, the Isle of Man — maintain living Celtic languages with official status in Ireland and Wales; the pan-Celtic movement, the Celtic Congress, and the Festival Interceltique institutionalize the link; and the word extends into popular culture, sports, and economics. The famous pronunciation split is itself part of this modern history: the classical and learned pronunciation is /k/ (‘Keltic’), restored in the 19th century on the model of the Greek and Latin forms, while a soft /s/ (‘Seltic’) — derived from the French pronunciation of Celtique — survives in the names of the Boston Celtics basketball club and Glasgow Celtic Football Club. From the upper Danube of Herodotus to the football terraces of Glasgow, the word has carried the full arc of European identity — ancient ethnonym, scientific family label, romantic badge, and living modern name — and the source passage's calm philological use of it in the nineteenth century stands at exactly the hinge between the scientific and the romantic senses.
