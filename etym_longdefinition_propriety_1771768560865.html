PROPRIETY belongs to the same clause of Gibbon’s Memoirs as prudence: his amorous commitments, he writes, “also were dominated by prudence and propriety.” Where prudence governs the inner calculation of advantage, propriety governs the outer conformity to social rule — the etiquette, decorum, and moral expectations of the upper classes that a gentleman and his family are expected to observe. In this context propriety names the relentless pressure of respectable society: the demand that one do the right thing as the world defines it, whatever one’s private wishes. For Gibbon, propriety meant protecting his dignity and his family’s reputation — and, in the matter of his broken engagement, it meant submitting to his father’s authority precisely because dissolving an ill-approved match was more proper than insisting upon a sentimental one. The tone of the word here is cool, even merciless: propriety is the social code that quietly trumps personal happiness.

The word’s history explains why it carries this double charge — both “correct conduct” and “that which is one’s own.” Propriety entered English in the fifteenth century from Old French propriété, from Latin proprietās, which meant “ownership” and “a distinctive quality.” For its first two centuries in English, propriety meant chiefly “ownership” and “a peculiar characteristic” — the sense in which a thing’s “propriety” is its own nature. In the seventeenth century the word was applied to conduct: first in the rhetorical sense of the “propriety of words” (using exactly the right expression — proprietās verborum, in Cicero and Quintilian), and then, by extension, to the fitness of behaviour to one’s station and situation. The modern sense — conformity to conventionally accepted standards of behaviour and morals — is thus the last, social chapter of a word that began by meaning “ownership” and “essential nature”: what is proper is what belongs rightly, what is appropriate to the role.

The senses today:
  • Conformity to social convention — the dominant modern sense: correct, seemly behaviour as polite society defines it (“conduct oneself with propriety”).
  • The proprieties (plural) — the set of accepted rules and conventions of a society or situation (“to observe the proprieties”).
  • Fitness / appropriateness (of an action, word, or thing) — the general, less socialised sense: suitability to circumstance (“question the propriety of a decision”).
  • Distinctive quality / essential nature (archaic) — the older sense of proprietās, “that which is peculiar to a thing,” now rare.
  • Ownership (archaic / obsolete) — the earliest sense, shared with property, from which the modern word diverged in the seventeenth century.
  • Decency / modest respectability — a moral narrowing, especially of sexual modesty and blamelessness, in nineteenth-century usage.
The boundary words matter. Decorum is the closest neighbour — it emphasises outward fittingness, the seemliness of manner, dress, and gesture, and is closer to ceremonial beauty, while propriety is more purely ethical, the moral fitness of conduct to one’s station. Etiquette is far narrower: the codified rules of polite society, a mere list of manners, whereas propriety reaches into moral obligation. Decency names a moral baseline — what is fundamentally respectable or shame-free — often with a charge of sexual modesty; propriety is broader and more social. Seemliness and fitness are more archaic synonyms stressing aesthetic suitability. Protocol is procedural, official, even diplomatic — the rules of formal procedure — whereas propriety is diffuse and unwritten, a whole climate of accepted behaviour. Respectability stresses the standing one enjoys in the eyes of society; propriety is the conduct that earns it. Conventionality emphasises mere custom and habit, where propriety can imply a reasoned sense of what is fitting. Correctness is the neutral word for doing things the accepted way, without the moral weight that propriety carries in its strongest uses.
