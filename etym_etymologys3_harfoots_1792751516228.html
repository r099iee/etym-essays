1. Root Word 1 Evolution: Proto-Indo-European *kers- to Old English hǣr ("Hair"). The first lexical constituent of the compound Harfoot traces back to the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *kers- (or *ker-), which signified "to bristle, stand on end, be rough, or stiffen like hair." In the Proto-Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, this root underwent regular sound changes governed by Grimm's Law: the voiceless velar plosive [k] shifted to the voiceless glottal fricative [h], while the root vocalism shifted to an elongated vowel, yielding Proto-Germanic *hērą ("hair, fleece, coat of an animal"). In the West Germanic development leading to Old English, this developed into hǣr (in the West Saxon dialect) and hēr (in Anglian dialects), designating both human head hair and the thick fur of beasts. In historical compounding, Old English long vowels in the first element regularly underwent phonetic shortening before consonant clusters (the phenomenon known as trisyllabic or cluster shortening, which transformed wīf-man into woman and hālīġ-dæġ into holiday). Thus, in a compound like *hǣr-fōt, the long vowel shortened from [æː] to short [a], yielding the phonetic base Har-.

2. Root Word 2 Evolution: Proto-Indo-European *pōds to Old English fōt ("Foot"). The second constituent element originates from the ancient Proto-Indo-European anatomical noun *pōds (with its oblique root *ped-), meaning "foot, stepping limb." As this root transitioned into Proto-Germanic, Grimm's Law transformed the initial voiceless bilabial plosive [p] into the voiceless labiodental fricative [f], while the dental stop [d] shifted through regular unvoicing to [t], producing Proto-Germanic *fōts. In Old English, this became the core masculine noun fōt ("foot"). In the native Germanic nominal system, fōt belonged to the athematic root nouns that formed their plurals via i-mutation (umlaut) of the root vowel (Proto-Germanic *fōtiz > prehistoric Old English *fēt > Old English fēt > Modern English feet). However, when compounded into an exocentric proper ethnonym or family surname, the regular productive weak plural suffix -s attaches directly to the whole compound, yielding Harfoots rather than *Harfeet, a grammatical rule Tolkien rigorously enforced in the Shire's nomenclature (as seen in the proud insistence of the Proudfoots).

3. Internal Translation Architecture: From Westron Kûd-dûkan to English Harfoots. Within the internal fictional universe of Middle-earth, the Hobbits did not speak English; they spoke the Common Speech (Westron), in which their dialect preserved archaic northern idioms from their former residence along the Anduin. In Appendix F to The Lord of the Rings ("The Languages and Peoples of the Third Age"), Tolkien meticulously explained his translation methodology. The genuine Third Age Westron name for the Harfoots was Kûd-dûkan. This compound derived from the primitive northern Mannish base kûd- (related to kûd-dūkan "hole-dweller", which yielded the reduced form kuduk, the word the Hobbits used for themselves). To convey the subtle historical relationships of these languages to modern English readers, Tolkien established an elaborate translation matrix:

  • Westron was translated into Modern English.
  • The ancient, related language of the Rohirrim was translated into Old English (Anglo-Saxon).
  • The rustic, archaic elements preserved in Hobbit speech were translated into archaic, rural, or dialectal English compound forms.
Thus, Tolkien translated Kûd-dûkan as Harfoots, deriving it from Old English *hǣr-fōt, highlighting both their love of burrow-holes and their most celebrated anatomical feature: the thick, curling, warm hair covering their unshod feet.

4. External Philological and Mythological Context: Folklore of the Shaggy Folk. Externally, Tolkien's invention of the Harfoots tapped directly into deep European folkloric and mythological archetypes of the "hairy-footed" rustic creature. In Germanic, Celtic, and Slavic folklore, benign subterranean spirits, brownies, hobgoblins, and boggarts are frequently depicted with furry or hair-covered feet, symbolizing their primal, intimate connection to the soil and the earth. By describing the Harfoots as having tough leathery soles covered in thick brown curl, Tolkien rendered shoes an absurd, unnatural encumbrance to them, emphasizing their organic harmony with the natural environment.

Furthermore, by dividing the Hobbits into three distinct breeds with divergent physical, linguistic, and cultural affinities, Tolkien applied the rigorous methods of nineteenth-century comparative anthropology to his fantasy world. The Harfoots represent the pure, unadulterated agrarian archetype: conservative, gentle, deeply rooted in their subterranean smials, and suspicious of the outside world. Through the rigorous philological craft of Harfoots, Tolkien elevated what could have been a whimsical fairytale detail into an enduring masterpiece of literary ethnography, grounding the beloved heroes of the Shire in thousands of years of authentic linguistic and cultural evolution.
