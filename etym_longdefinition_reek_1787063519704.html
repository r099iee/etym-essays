Reek is both a noun and a verb, with a two-part history that makes it a remarkable case of semantic shift. In Tolkien's sentence, "his foul reek" is a noun meaning the noisome smoke, vapor, and stench of the dragon's lair — the word's oldest sense ("smoke, steam, vapor") combined with its modern sense ("a strong foul smell"). The double meaning is deliberate and traditional: the reek of a dragon is at once visible smoke and nauseating odor.

As a noun.

  • Smoke, steam, or vapor: the oldest sense. In Scots and Northern English the word survived in full force as the plain word for smoke — a chimney "sends up reek," and Edinburgh is affectionately called "Auld Reekie" ("Old Smoky") from the smoke that once hung over its Old Town.
  • A strong, disagreeable smell; a stench: the modern dominant sense. "The reek of stale tobacco," "a reek of sweat and diesel."
  • By extension, the emanation of a foul condition: "the reek of decay," "the reek of corruption," where the word slides between literal smell and figurative atmosphere.

As a verb.

  • To emit smoke or vapor (intransitive): "the chimney reeked," "the ground reeked with steam." Archaic-literal, still used in literary and regional English.
  • To smell strongly and unpleasantly of something: "the room reeked of garlic," "he reeked of cheap perfume." Now the dominant verbal sense.
  • Figurative: to be saturated with, to be strongly suggestive of: "the proposal reeks of hypocrisy," "the affair reeked of corruption." A modern, productive idiom meaning that something is permeated by a negative quality.
  • Transitive (regional and archaic): to expose to smoke, to cure by smoking — e.g., "reeked bacon" in some dialects.

The semantic shift in focus. The word's history is a famous example of a sense moving from one sense organ to another. For over a thousand years reek meant smoke — something seen. Then, because smoke in practice almost always smells, the word drifted from the visual to the olfactory: by the 19th century reek primarily named an unpleasant smell, and the "smoke" sense retreated to dialect, poetry, and Scotland. Tolkien, writing in the 1930s, could exploit both at once: a dragon's reek is smoke that is also a stench, and the word's doubleness reinforces the horror of the image.

Register and collocations.

  • Literary and regional: "the reek of a peat fire," "Auld Reekie," the historical word of Scottish homes and Northern kitchens.
  • Colloquial-negative: "it reeks in here," "what's that reek?" — informal but common, always of bad smells.
  • Journalistic-figurative: "the deal reeks of insider dealing," "the verdict reeked of injustice" — the modern flagship use, always of something reprehensible.
  • Fixed phrase: "reeking of" is by far the most common construction in current English, used both literally (a person reeking of smoke) and figuratively (a plan reeking of desperation).
  • Sound-symbolic value: the diphthong and final velar of /riːk/ give the word a long, grimy, throat-clearing weight; it is one of those English words whose sound seems to match its meaning, which is why writers choose it over blander "smell" or "odor."
