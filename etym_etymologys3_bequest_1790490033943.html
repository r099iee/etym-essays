Bequest looks like request with a be-, but the resemblance misleads. It is Middle English biqueste / bequeste (c. 1300), meaning act of bequeathing, built from prefix be- (about, thoroughly) plus a Germanic noun *cwis / *cwiss meaning saying, behest, will — the noun corresponding to the verb cwethan to say and its fossil past quoth. The final -t is unetymological, an excrescent stop as in behest (from behæs vow) and amongst, amidst: speakers added t after s for ease or analogy. Thus bequest is etymologically be-saying, the solemn saying-to that disposes goods.

Early attestations confirm the act-sense. Ancrene Riwle and chronicles speak of land held by bequeste, i.e. by disposition. Only in the late 15th century does the concrete thing-sense appear: a bequest is what is bequeathed, a legacy. The shift is ordinary metonymy, paralleled by gift (act of giving to thing given) and grant. Pronunciation follows: /bɪˈkwɛst/ with reduced be-, /kw/ from Germanic cw, short e, and /st/ cluster from the added t. Spelling stabilises as bequest after Caxton, with qu for /kw/ under French influence.

  • Act to thing (1300–1500): lawyers first plead the bequest meaning the devising; testators then list my bequests meaning items. The plural marks the change: acts are singular, things countable. By Malory and Paston letters, bequests of plate, gowns, and marks are inventory, not procedure.
  • Victorian moral load (1800s): wills become middle-class drama — Dickens, Trollope, Eliot turn on bequests — and Ruskin can call debts a bequest without strain: readers know bequests bind conscience. To accept is honourable, to contest is shameful, to squander is tragic. The granite epitaph an entirely honest merchant is the reward for accepting a burdensome bequest literally.
  • Philanthropic present (1900–now): death duties, probate reform, and charity law make charitable bequest a technical term with tax relief. Museums, in wall labels, shorten to Bequest of... plus date, turning law into provenance. Fundraisers soften to legacy gift, but deeds still say bequest. Figurative journalists extend to history: empires and cabinets leave bequests of debt and warming, echoing Ruskin's ironic debts.

Like bequeath, bequest rests on a Germanic say-root whose deeper Indo-European kinship is uncertain. Beyond Germanic the equations wobble, and Boutkan urges agnosticism. Within English, however, the development from saying to will to legacy to museum label is documented line by line, a textbook case of a speech act becoming property and then memory.

Phonetic and orthographic notes clinch the story: Middle English qu spells Germanic cw /kw/ under French scribal habit, be- unstresses to /bi/, and excrescent t hardens the ending to /st/, distinguishing bequest /biˈkwɛst/ from bequeath /biˈkwið/ in both ear and eye. Wills, plaques, and fundraising letters thus carry a tiny sound-change fossil in every use.
