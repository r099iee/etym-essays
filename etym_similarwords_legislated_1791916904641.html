• enacted: Similar because it means "made into law" and is the closest everyday equivalent; differs because enact foregrounds the specific bill or provision brought into force ("enacted the Criminal Code"), whereas legislated foregrounds the law-making activity or the resulting legal imposition.
  • passed: Similar because a legislature passes laws; differs because passed refers to the procedural vote in a chamber and says nothing about royal assent or legal effect, while legislated implies that law was actually made.
  • decreed: Similar because it denotes authoritative law-making; differs because a decree issues from a monarch, dictator or court, is often unilateral, and carries a stronger note of arbitrary power than the parliamentary, deliberative legislated.
  • ordained: Similar because it means "established by authority"; differs because ordained is archaic-religious or solemn, suggesting providential or ecclesiastical order rather than the ordinary work of a legislature.
  • prescribed: Similar because a statute prescribes what must be done; differs because prescribe names the content of a rule (the conduct required) rather than the act of making law, and is equally at home in medicine and contract.
  • codified: Similar because it refers to putting law into systematic written form; differs because codification arranges and consolidates existing rules, whereas legislation may create entirely new ones.
  • promulgated: Similar because it means "published or proclaimed as law"; differs because promulgation is the formal announcement that makes a law known, a step after enactment, not the enactment itself.
  • mandated: Similar because a legislated requirement is a mandate; differs because mandated stresses the obligation imposed on someone, often by an authority or contract, and need not involve a legislature at all.
  • sanctioned: Similar because a sanctioned rule has official force; differs because sanction can mean approval or a penalty, and its legal use often concerns enforcement rather than law-making.
  • ruled: Similar because a ruler or court may lay down a rule; differs because ruled typically describes a judicial decision in a case or a sovereign's governance, not the enactment of general statutes.
