• Cicero: Similar because both are Roman authorities on eloquence and the central figures of the classical rhetorical curriculum; differs because Cicero was the practising orator-statesman whose speeches and dialogues supply the model, while Quintilian was the professional teacher who turned that model into a twelve-book course of instruction.
  • Aristotle: Similar as a foundational theorist of rhetoric; differs because Aristotle’s Rhetoric is a philosophical analysis of persuasion (ethos, pathos, logos, the enthymeme), whereas Quintilian’s concern is the moral and practical education of a speaker from childhood.
  • Isocrates: Similar as a Greek educator who made rhetoric the centre of civic education; differs because Isocrates left speeches and letters rather than a systematic handbook, and his Panhellenic ideals differ from Quintilian’s Roman professional curriculum.
  • Hermagoras of Temnos: Similar as a technical authority on rhetorical invention whose doctrines shaped later teaching; differs because Hermagoras survives only in fragments and is known chiefly for stasis theory, while Quintilian’s complete treatise covers the whole art and its pedagogy.
  • Seneca the Elder: Similar as a Roman teacher and collector of declamations; differs because Seneca compiled model controversies and suasoriae for practice, whereas Quintilian built a systematic theory and criticised the declamatory excesses Seneca’s collections preserve.
  • Tacitus: Similar as a Roman writer concerned with the health of oratory; differs because Tacitus’s Dialogus is a literary dialogue on the decline of eloquence, not a schoolroom manual, and its author was a historian rather than a professor.
  • Longinus: Similar as an ancient critic of style and sublimity; differs because the treatise On the Sublime is aesthetic and literary criticism, while Quintilian’s subject is the complete training of the orator.
  • Erasmus: Similar as a humanist educator who made rhetoric central to schooling; differs because Erasmus was a Christian scholar and satirist working in print culture, not a Roman teacher, and his Ciceronianus attacked the very purism that Quintilian’s authority was often enlisted to support.
