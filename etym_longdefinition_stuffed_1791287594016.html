Stuffed is the past-participle adjective of to stuff, meaning filled, crammed, or packed tightly with material, and by specialised extension preserved by taxidermy with the interior replaced by padding so the outward shape remains lifelike but lifeless.

In the Wodehouse phrase a stuffed moose, the taxidermic sense is primary and comic: a real moose killed, mounted, glassy-eyed, and set up in a hall or museum, solemn, immobile, and faintly absurd. To compare a discomforted Englishman to it is to say he has gone rigid, expressionless, and overly dignified, preserving decorum like upholstery. The humour draws on the Edwardian country-house world where stuffed heads, antlers, and whole animals were standard décor, simultaneously prestigious and ridiculous.

More generally, stuffed describes anything whose cavity has been forcibly filled: a stuffed turkey filled with seasoning, a stuffed suitcase bursting shut, a nose stuffed up with a cold, a head stuffed with facts, a person stuffed after overeating. It always implies fullness to the point of tightness, often with loss of natural function — what is stuffed cannot take more, breathe freely, or move easily.

Polysemy today includes:
  • Filled with another edible material (culinary): stuffed chicken, stuffed olives, stuffed crust pizza. The bridge is direct from Old French estofer, to fill and equip — the cavity is equipped with flavour.
  • Crammed, overfull, congested: a stuffed drawer, a stuffed nose, a brain stuffed with trivia, stuffed full of cash. The bridge is generalisation from food to any overfilled container, including the body.
  • Preserved by taxidermy or as a soft toy: a stuffed moose head, stuffed animals, teddy bears. The bridge is technical: tow and straw (Latin stuppa) literally used to pack skins to keep shape.
  • Defeated hollowly / dismissed rudely (informal, chiefly British/Australian): we got stuffed 5–0, tell him to get stuffed. The bridge is metaphorical violence: to cram down, to treat as waste, to fill full of defeat.
  • Pompous and inflexible (in stuffed shirt): a self-important official. The bridge is visual: a shirt starched and padded stiff, like a mannequin, all exterior and no life.
Unlike full, which is neutral, stuffed stresses force, excess, and artificiality: something has been done to the thing to make it unnaturally full, whether by cook, taxidermist, cold virus, or pride.

Grammatically, stuffed functions both as participial adjective and as resultative state: a turkey is stuffed by a cook, a nose becomes stuffed by infection, a man becomes stuffed with pride by habit. Intensifiers such as completely stuffed, crammed stuffed, and densely stuffed underline excess beyond normal fullness, while compounds such as stuffing-box, stuffy, and stuffed-up extend the image to steam engines, rooms without air, and heads without clarity. The underlying metaphor is consistent across domains: air, thought, and movement have been displaced by packed matter, leaving a shape that looks complete but can no longer breathe, bend, or think freely.
