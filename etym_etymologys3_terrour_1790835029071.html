The noun Terrour (in modern standard English orthography terror) has its ultimate origins in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *ters- (with its metathesized variant *tres-), which primarily signified "to tremble, shake, quiver with fright", and transitively "to cause to tremble, to scare away".

Comparative Indo-European Lineage:

  • This root generated an extensive family of cognates across ancient Indo-European branches: in Ancient Greek, it yielded the verb $\tau\rho\acute{\epsilon}\omega$ (treō, "to flee in fear, tremble, quake") and the noun $\tau\rho\acute{\alpha}\sigma\sigma\omega$ / $\tau\rho\alpha\sigma\mu\acute{o}\varsigma$; in Sanskrit, it produced trasati ("he trembles, quakes") and trāsa- ("terror, fright"); and in Avestan, tərəsaiti ("he is afraid").
  • In Proto-Italic, the root developed the stative/causative verb *terzeō, where the intervocalic *-s- underwent rhotacism to *-r-, giving rise to classical Latin terrēre ("to frighten, scare, terrify, cause to shake").

Latin Classical Development:

  • From the verbal stem of terrēre, Latin formed the masculine abstract third-declension noun terror (genitive terrōris) utilizing the nominal suffix -or, designating physical or psychological states (compare horror, timor, pallor).
  • In classical Roman prose and epic poetry (Lucretius, Virgil, Cicero, Tacitus), terror was used to describe battlefield panic (terror panicus), martial alarms (terror belli), divine dread, and political intimidation.
  • The word also underwent an early metonymic shift to denote the instrument or agent of fear: a formidable conqueror or unassailable general was praised or feared as a terror hostibus ("a terror to the enemy").

Old French and Middle English Reception:

Following the decline of the Western Empire, Latin terror passed into Gallo-Romance, emerging in Old French during the 12th century as terrur, teror, and later terreur.

  • During the Anglo-Norman period following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman scribes routinely rendered Latin -or words with the diphthongal spelling -our (e.g., honour, valour, terrour).
  • The word was borrowed into Middle English in the late 14th century (c. 1375–1385), appearing prominently in the Wycliffite Bible translations, Geoffrey Chaucer's translation of Boethius's De Consolatione Philosophiae, and John Gower's Confessio Amantis, spelled terrour or terror.
  • In medieval English usage, the word retained a strong theological association with the Last Judgement, demonic visions, and the awesome Majesty of God.

Early Modern Orthography, Aesthetics, and Political Transformation:

  • During the Renaissance and 18th century, English lexicographers and printers established terrour as the prestigious orthographic form in Britain. Dr. Samuel Johnson, in his landmark Dictionary of the English Language (1755), insisted upon preserving the -our ending for words borrowed through French, making terrour the standard literary spelling throughout the Enlightenment.
  • In 1757, Edmund Burke's aesthetic treatise elevated terrour into a central category of British philosophical discourse, arguing that "terror is in all cases whatsoever, either more openly or latently, the ruling principle of the sublime."
  • In the late 18th century, the French Revolution introduced la Terreur (the Reign of Terror), cementing the word as a technical concept of state-sponsored political violence, which subsequently entered 19th-century English as reign of terror and eventually gave birth to terrorism.
  • In the 19th century, Noah Webster's American dictionary (1828) dropped the -u- to establish terror in the United States, and British usage gradually followed suit in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Phonetic and Contemporary Status:

Phonetically, the word evolved into Received Pronunciation /ˈter.ər/. Although modern publications use terror, the 18th-century spelling terrour remains a vital historical marker of Addisonian and Johnsonian prose.
