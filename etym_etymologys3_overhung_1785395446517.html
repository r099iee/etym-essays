Evolution of "overhung." The word is a compound of two of the plainest words in the language, over and hang, and its history is the history of both, plus the story of what happens when they are pressed together. It is a word of geometry, the canopy, and the brow, and it has spent a thousand years slowly learning to mean menace.

1. The ancestry of "over." The first element is the oldest and most stable. Its root, PIE *upér, "over, above, beyond," was already a word of superiority and excess in the prehistoric language, and it left descendants across the whole family: Greek hypér (our hyper), Latin super (our superior, supreme), Sanskrit upári. The Germanic branch gave *uber, which became Old English ofer, a preposition and adverb of the plainest cast, over the table, over the sea, over and above. In a thousand years the word has barely moved: the modern over is phonologically the direct descendant of the Old English form, its meaning unchanged. It brought into the compound the essential notion of a position above with something beneath, which is the entire geometry of overhung.

2. The ancestry of "hang." The second element has a much more eventful history. The PIE root, usually reconstructed *ḱenk-, "to hang, to be suspended," produced in Germanic the strong verb *hanhaną. Old English inherited a remarkable doublet: a strong verb hōn, "to hang (something), to crucify," and a weak verb hangian, "to be hanging, to be suspended." The pair, transitive and intransitive, coexisted for centuries and then merged into the single verb hangen in Middle English. The modern past and participle hung descends from the strong past plural, while hanged was quarantined in the execution sense; the older strong inflection survives only there, a fossil of the class VII verbs that once conjugated by vowel change (compare hang, hung with the older pattern).

3. The compound: from architecture to landscape. Old English already had oferhōn, "to hang over," and the compound has been available ever since. Its early life is architectural and physical: an upper storey overhangs a street, a rock overhangs a pool, trees overhang a path. The signature geometry, a projecting mass with space left beneath, made it the natural word for the canopy of a wood, the brow of a hill, the edge of a cliff. It is the word for the roofed-over hollow, which is exactly how Tolkien uses it: dells "overhung with bushes and low trees," each hollow a cave-like pocket with vegetation arched above it.

4. The figurative turn. From the sixteenth century the picture of something suspended above begins to point inward. A danger overhangs a life; fate hangs overhead; an overhung brow darkens into gloom, secrecy, or threat. The word family acquires its note of suspended menace, something arched above that has not yet fallen, and overhung becomes a favourite of gothic and romantic description for the threatened traveller, the overshadowed house, the brooding face. In the nineteenth century the idiom "overhung by" or "overhung with" carried both the physical shade and the emotional weight: a landscape overhung with cloud, a life overhung with sorrow.

5. The technical re-entry. In the twentieth century the noun overhang went professional. Mountaineers borrowed it for the outward-projecting rock face that defies climbing; architects and engineers use it for the projecting roof or storey; financiers speak of an overhang of unsold supply pressing down on a market. Each use preserves the same geometry, a mass above with vulnerable space beneath, now measured and priced rather than imagined. The older pictorial sense survives intact in literary prose, and Tolkien's overhung dells show the word doing in 1937 exactly what it did in Old English, shading a hollow with a canopy of growth.

  • Sound change: PIE *u- → Germanic *u- → Old English o (the standard rounding of /u/ before certain consonants in the early period, seen in ofer); PIE *k- → Germanic *h- (Grimm's Law) in hang; the Old English initial h- preserved to the present day.
  • Morphology: the strong class VII verb hōn and weak hangian merge; the past hung is generalised from the strong plural, while hanged is retained for executions.
  • Semantic drift: from physical canopy, to architectural projection, to the brow and menace, to suspended threat, to technical overhang; the figurative senses all preserve the core picture of a mass overhead with space beneath.
  • Literary attestations: Old English oferhōn; Middle English overhangen; Shakespearean and Elizabethan use of the projecting brow and threat; romantic and gothic prose of the 18th–19th centuries; Tolkien (1937); modern mountaineering and financial terminology.

Contemporary state. Today overhung still names the state of being covered, roofed, or shaded from above with space left beneath, a canopy that overarches rather than lies flush, and its figurative twin overhang keeps the picture of weight suspended above without contact. It is a word that has never needed to change its shape because its shape, the brow and the canopy, has never stopped meaning the same thing: something above, space below, and time still passing before the suspended thing falls.
