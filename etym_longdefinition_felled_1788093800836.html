Felled is the past tense and past participle of the verb fell, meaning "to cause to fall; to strike or knock down; to cut down (a tree)". The verb is the causative partner of fall: the tree falls by itself or by the wind, but the axe fells it; the warrior falls in battle, but the blow fells him. In the source sentence — Smaug's death-plunge in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit — "felled trees and split stone" is the participial aftermath of the dragon's crashing weight: the great body knocks grown trees flat and shatters stone in its fall, and the participle captures the instantaneous, violent transfer of force from the crashing mass to everything beneath it.

1. Primary senses.

  • Forestry: to cut down a tree — "felled timber", "felled oak", "a felling licence", "the felling season". This is the most frequent modern sense, and it is ancient: English woodsmen have felled trees since before the Conquest, and the verb remains the standard, unmarked word of the timber industry.
  • Combat and sport: to knock a person or animal down — "the blow felled him", "the tackle felled the runner", "he was felled by a single punch". Ranging from the battlefield to the boxing ring, this sense is vivid, physical and instantaneous: what is felled is vertical and becomes horizontal.
  • Hunting: to bring down game — "felled a stag with one arrow".

2. Extended and figurative senses.

  • Impersonal and medical forces: from the 19th century onward, disease, exhaustion, grief and shock "fell" people — "she was felled by pneumonia", "felled by a stroke", "felled by grief". The agent is impersonal; the victim is struck down without warning, like a tree at the axe. Obituary and news prose especially love "felled by".
  • Metaphors of collapse: a career, a regime, a champion or a company is "felled" by scandal, defeat or crisis — "he was felled by the revelation", "the empire was felled from within". The tree image persists: a reputation falls with a crash, and nothing rises from it quickly.
  • Tailoring (technical): a "felled seam" (or "flat-felled seam") is a seam whose edges are folded flat, one over the other, and stitched down — from the same verb in its old sense "to cause to lie flat"; "to fell a seam" is a technical term of needlework exactly parallel, historically, to felling a tree.

3. Grammar — the treacherous pair. Fell (weak verb: fell–felled–felled) and fell (irregular past tense of fall: fall–fell–fallen) are different words that happen to be identical in form. Thus "the tree fell" (intransitive, past of fall) and "he felled the tree" (transitive, present of fell) — and "the tree has fallen" versus "the tree has been felled". The participle felled is unambiguously the causative word, and it always takes an object in the active voice or appears in the passive ("were felled"). This grammatical polarity — causing a fall versus undergoing a fall — is the single most important fact for using the word correctly, and it is exactly what Tolkien exploits: Smaug does not fall quietly; his fall fells.

4. Register and nuance. Neutral in register but heavy in weight: felled is the plainest, most physical word for bringing something tall down. It is more direct than toppled (which suggests loss of balance), more brutal than cut down (which is slow and deliberate), and more native than the Latinate trio demolished, destroyed, razed (which are about buildings and abstractions). In modern prose the word's two great homes are the logging report and the sporting or medical news item, with the literary participle — "felled trees and split stone" — bridging them.

5. Summary. To be felled is to be struck down — a tree by the axe, a fighter by the blow, a life by disease, a reputation by scandal: the participial form of the ancient causative "to make fall", a word that has carried the same crisp image of vertical-to-horizontal for thirteen centuries of English.
