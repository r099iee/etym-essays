- [Vedic Period (c. 1500–1000 BCE)] — Emerged in the Ṛgveda as a prominent adjectival epithet of Indra (derived from the root *śak*, "to be able, capable, powerful"), celebrating the deity’s vital physical strength to slay the serpent-demon Vṛtra and release the cosmic waters - [perception: revered as the ultimate epithet of primal divine warrior potency, virility, and sacrificial generosity]
- [Epic & Classical Sanskrit Period (c. 500 BCE–500 CE)] — Standardized in the Rāmāyaṇa and Mahābhārata as a primary proper name for Indra, where human monarchs of the Solar and Lunar dynasties were routinely lauded with formulas like *Śakra-sama* ("Śakra’s equal") to sanctify their earthly legitimacy - [perception: celebrated as the universal golden benchmark of courtly royal majesty, chivalric kingship, and earthly dominion]
- [Early Buddhist & Jain Canon (c. 500 BCE–1st millennium CE)] — Reconceptualized in Pali canonical texts (as *Sakka*) and Jain scriptures; rather than a stormy warrior deity prone to pride, Śakra became an enlightened, humble devotee and cosmic protector of the Buddha and the Tīrthaṅkaras - [perception: moralized and elevated as the quintessential devout celestial guardian of righteous spiritual teachers]
- [East Asian Silk Road Transmission (c. 200–1200 CE)] — Adopted into Chinese as Dìshìtiān (帝釋天) and Japanese as Taishakuten, integrated into Buddhist temple pantheons as one of the Twelve Devas protecting the eastern cardinal direction and the state - [perception: venerated as a benevolent, stately guardian deity of monastic order, civic peace, and moral law]
- [Modern Indological & Western Reception (19th–21st c.)] — Adopted into English comparative mythology and academic translations of Sanskrit epics (such as Goldman’s Princeton Rāmāyaṇa translation); recognized by scholars and readers worldwide - [perception: appreciated as a classic emblem of ancient Indian celestial kingship and epic poetic hyperbole]
