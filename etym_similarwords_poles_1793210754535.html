• Stakes: Similar because both are sharpened timbers driven into the ground; differs because a stake is shorter and stouter, meant for anchoring or impaling, while a pole is long and slender, meant to rise and carry.
  • Posts: Similar because both are upright timbers set in earth; differs because a post is thick and load-bearing — gateposts, king-posts — while a pole is lighter and taller, valued for length over girth.
  • Rods: Similar because both are long straight lengths for training plants or measuring; differs because rod suggests thinness and flexibility or a unit of measure, while pole suggests a working timber with real girth.
  • Spars: Similar because both are stripped lengths of timber for rigging and support; differs because spar is the nautical and mining term — masts, booms, pit-props — while pole is the farm and garden word.
  • Masts: Similar because a mast is a great pole stepped in a ship or flying a flag; differs because mast implies rigging, sails, and stays, while pole stands alone or carries only wire and bine.
  • Piles: Similar because both are timbers driven into ground or water; differs because piles are foundation timbers hammered deep to bear buildings and bridges, while poles rise above ground to bear plants or wires.
  • Canes: Similar because both train climbing plants and furnish light straight sticks; differs because canes are bamboo or reed — hollow, jointed, garden-scale — while hop-poles are stout coppice timber, a dozen feet of ash or chestnut.
  • Staffs: Similar because both are hand-scale lengths of wood carried upright; differs because a staff is grasped and borne — shepherds, pilgrims, sergeants — while a pole is planted and left standing.
