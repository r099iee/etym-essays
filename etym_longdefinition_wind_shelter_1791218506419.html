Wind-shelter is a compound noun denoting a structure, screen or natural feature that provides protection from the wind, especially a rudimentary wall, hedge, fence, lean-to or arrangement of branches set up to break prevailing gusts. In its most literal sense it is any shelter whose defining function is to shelter from wind rather than from rain, sun or view. A wind-shelter of branches, as in the source passage, means a few boughs stuck in the ground or leaned together to make a break against draughts behind which people can sit or sleep.

The form varies in writing as wind-shelter with hyphen, wind shelter open, and occasionally windshelter solid, without change of meaning. Stress falls on wind, with secondary stress on shelter. The word is countable: one wind-shelter, several wind-shelters. It belongs to practical outdoor, archaeological and ethnographic vocabulary rather than to formal architecture, where windbreak, windscreen and shelterbelt are more technical.

  • Primitive architecture sense: a minimal screen of branches, skins, stones or snow built by hunter-gatherers or travellers, e.g. Bushman wind-shelters, Aboriginal windbreaks. This is the source sense and the classic anthropological use.
  • Rural and horticultural sense: a hedge, belt of trees, wall or fence that protects fields, gardens, stock or houses from wind, often called a windbreak in American English and a shelterbelt for extended plantings.
  • Recreational and urban sense: a glass, wooden or canvas screen on a promenade, viewpoint, bus stop, smoking area or sports terrace that lets people enjoy outlook while avoiding wind chill.
  • Nautical and military sense: any canvas, metal or snow wall rigged to give lee for cooking, observation or gun positions in exposed places.
  • Figurative extension: a person, rule or institution that shields another from harsh criticism or exposure, e.g. he acted as her wind-shelter in meetings, though windbreak is more common in this metaphor.

Polysemy is unified by function. The bridge from branch screen to planted hedge to glass screen is generalisation of material while retaining purpose, to block moving air. The bridge from ethnographic shelter to horticultural windbreak is scaling up from human body to crop or building, with the same aerodynamics of deflecting flow and creating a lee. The bridge from physical to figurative is metaphorical mapping of wind as hostile force and shelter as protection, parallel to shield and buffer. In each case wind specifies the threat and shelter the affordance, making the compound transparent even to first-time hearers.

Domain nuances clarify boundaries. In archaeology a wind-shelter or windbreak is distinguished from a hut or house by lack of roof and permanence; post-holes in an arc with hearth suggest a wind-shelter rather than a dwelling. In ecology a wind-shelter effect describes how boulders and shrubs create microclimates for seedlings. In building physics a wind shelter assessment measures gust reduction behind barriers. In everyday hiking a bothy, tent and bivouac all provide fuller enclosure, while a wind-shelter provides only lateral screening and is often open to the sky.

In the source context, built here and there a wind-shelter of branches, the word marks the first constructive step above sleeping in the open, in hollow trees or in caves. The qualifiers here and there and of branches stress improvisation and lightness, while the contrast with pile dwellings roofed with moss signals increasing labour and permanence. Grasping wind-shelter as purposeful but minimal architecture lets the reader feel the author's evolutionary ladder from earth-and-sky contentment to true house-building.
