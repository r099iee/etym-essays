- [13th to 14th c. / Middle English] - Sacred polyphony: entered English via Old French (*treble*) from Latin *triplus* / *triplum* to designate the third and highest singing part in early medieval polyphonic motets, sung high above the lower tenor and motetus voices.
- [Late 14th to 15th c. / Middle English] - Arithmetic multiplication and physical threefoldness: extended organically from vocal polyphony into general arithmetic, geometry, and material descriptions, meaning made threefold, multiplied by three, or consisting of three interwoven strands (*treble-fold*).
- [16th to 17th c. / Early Modern English] - Acoustic register and commercial contracts: crystallized in music theory as the high soprano range, boy treble voice, and the G-clef (*treble clef*), while the verb *to treble* became standard in commerce, legal contracts, and agriculture for tripling monetary yields, crop volumes, and property values.
- [18th to 19th c. / British Imperial Era] - Economic, statistical, and literary prominence: widely used in British literature, parliamentary debates, and historical treatises to document industrial expansion, colonial trade figures, and population growth multiplying threefold.
- [20th to 21st c. / Contemporary usage] - Dialectal divergence and specialized registers: in British and Commonwealth English, *trebled* remains thoroughly standard alongside *tripled* in financial and sporting reporting (such as winning a sporting 'treble'), while American English predominantly uses *tripled*, reserving *treble* for audio controls (treble vs. bass) and music notation.
