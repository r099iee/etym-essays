- Late 14th–15th Century: Biological and agricultural barrenness - introduced into Middle English from Old French to describe unfruitful wombs and blighted, unyielding soils, viewed with sorrow and religious anxiety as a denial of divine fecundity.
- 16th–17th Century: Rhetorical and intellectual aridity - extended during the Renaissance and early scientific revolution to characterize dry writing, uninventive minds, and empty philosophical disputations that bore no practical fruit.
- 18th–19th Century: Epistemological and economic futility - employed in Enlightenment philosophy, classical economics, and epistemology (as seen in Carl Menger) to denote theoretical frameworks that failed to produce empirical foundations or practical truths.
- Late 19th Century: Microbiological asepsis - revolutionized by the germ theory of Pasteur and Lister, dramatically inverting the word's connotation in medical science from a regrettable defect into the supreme clinical ideal of germ-free purity.
- 20th–21st Century: Modern architectural, artistic, and cultural austerity - maintains its vital clinical value in medicine and biotechnology while acquiring pejorative cultural connotations of soulless monotony, emotional coldness, and over-regulated bureaucratic blandness.
