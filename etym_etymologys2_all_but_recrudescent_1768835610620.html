all-but — a phrasal compound of two independent roots; each element is traced separately. Chain for the element 'all': - Proto-Indo-European *h₂el- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'beyond; other' (the derivation is probable but debated; an alternative connection to *h₂el- 'to grow, nourish' has been proposed and remains uncertain) - Proto-Germanic *allaz (c. 500 BCE–1st c. CE, reconstructed) — 'all, entire, whole' - Old English eall (c. 8th–10th c., attested) — 'all, whole, every' - Middle English al / all (c. 1150–1500) — 'all' - Modern English all (c. 1500–) — 'all' — first element of the compound all-but. Chain for the element 'but': - Proto-Indo-European *ud- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'up, out' - Proto-Germanic *ūt (c. 500 BCE–1st c. CE, reconstructed) — 'out' - Old English ūt (c. 8th–10th c., attested) — 'out' - Old English būtan (c. 9th–10th c., attested) — 'on the outside, without, except', built from be- 'by, at' + ūtan 'outside' - Middle English buten / but (c. 12th–15th c.) — 'except, unless, only' - Modern English but (c. 1500–) — 'except' — second element of the compound. - Phrase 'all but X' (Middle English, c. 13th c.) — 'all except X' - Elliptical sense 'almost' (c. 14th–15th c.) — 'everything short of', from the arithmetic of exclusion - Hyphenated attributive compound all-but (19th–20th c.), as in 'all-but forgotten'. Nuance / debate: the shift from 'all except' to 'almost' is a classic case of semantic bleaching and is securely attested, though the exact date of the ellipsis is debated. The fusion into a hyphenated prenominal adjective is clearly modern (19th–20th c.).
recrudescent — single linear chain, a direct learned borrowing (no Germanic or Romance vernacular intermediate stage): - Proto-Indo-European *kreuə- (c. 4500–3500 BCE, reconstructed) — 'raw flesh, gore, blood' (also the source of Greek kréas 'flesh, meat' and Sanskrit kravíṣ 'raw flesh') - Latin crūdus (3rd–1st c. BCE, attested) — 'raw, bloody, undressed' - Latin crūdēscere (1st c. BCE, attested; e.g. in the medical writers) — 'to grow raw, to become sore' (inchoative in -sc-) - Latin recrūdēscere (1st c. CE, attested — used of wounds and diseases breaking open again) — 'to become raw again', literally re- 'again' + crūdēscere - Latin recrūdēscēns, genitive recrūdēscentis (2nd c. CE, present participle) — 'becoming raw again' - English recrudescent (18th c., borrowed into scientific and medical English) — 'breaking out afresh' - Modern English recrudescent — with the derived verb recrudesce (19th c.) and noun recrudescence. Note: this is a direct learned borrowing from Latin; the English word skips the French vernacular stage that many Latinisms pass through, which is why the -ent participle ending was kept intact.
