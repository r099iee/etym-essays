• Ayodhyā: Similar because both are ancient, highly sacred North Indian capital cities featured prominently as royal centers in the Rāmāyaṇa; differs because Ayodhyā is the hereditary capital of the Solar dynasty of Kosala and the birthplace of Lord Rāma, characterized by martial sovereignty, whereas Mithilā is the capital of Videha and the birthplace of Sītā, characterized primarily by agricultural sanctity, philosophical inquiry, and scholastic dialectics.
  • Janakpur: Similar because Janakpur (located in modern Madhesh Province, Nepal) is the direct historical and geographic continuation/site of ancient Mithilā, housing the Janaki Mandir; differs because Janakpur is the modern town and pilgrimage destination, whereas Mithilā is the classical, literary, and regional civilizational denomination encompassing the entire cultural belt.
  • Videha: Similar because both refer to the same sovereign civilization and geography in the late Vedic and epic periods; differs because Videha designates the entire kingdom, territorial realm, or confederated state, whereas Mithilā specifies the metropolitan capital city and urban administrative core.
  • Kāśī (Varanasi): Similar because both are ancient Gangetic cities celebrated as premier centers of Vedic philosophy, debate, and spiritual liberation; differs because Kāśī is primarily dedicated to the ascetic worship of Lord Śiva and death rituals on the cremation ghāts, whereas Mithilā was an imperial court famous for royal patronage of logic (Navya-Nyāya) and the householder path to liberation.
  • Hastināpura: Similar because both are storied, epic capital cities of ancient North Indian kingdoms; differs because Hastināpura is the warlike capital of the Kuru dynasty in the Mahābhārata, dominated by succession crises and military catastrophe, whereas Mithilā represents peaceful philosophical contemplation and unswerving moral righteousness.
  • Takṣaśilā: Similar because both were celebrated ancient seats of high intellectual learning and pedagogy; differs because Takṣaśilā was an international university city on the northwestern frontier focusing on statecraft, medicine, and military strategy, whereas Mithilā was an eastern Brahmanical center specializing in Upanishadic metaphysics, Navya-Nyāya logic, and sacred literature.
  • Magadha: Similar because both were prominent ancient eastern Indian political entities located in the Gangetic basin; differs because Magadha (south of the Ganges) became a militaristic, imperial empire that fostered early Buddhism and Jainism, whereas Mithilā (north of the Ganges) remained deeply tied to classical Vedic orthodoxy, Brahminical scholasticism, and agricultural aristocracy.
  • Pañcāla: Similar because both were major late Vedic kingdoms that patronized metaphysical debates and preserved sacred hymns; differs because Pañcāla was located further west in the upper Doab region and represented the orthodox ritual core, whereas Mithilā represented the pioneering eastern frontier where novel non-dual doctrines were boldly explored.
