Crêpe (also spelled crepe in English) names, first and foremost, a thin fabric whose surface is deliberately puckered, crimped, or pebbled by the use of highly twisted yarns that “cockle” the weave when released, or by chemical or heat treatment of the cloth. The result is a matte, softly granular, light-absorbing texture — the opposite of the glassy shine of satin — that drapes in soft, flowing folds. In the literary context of this card (a translated scene of Dream of the Red Chamber), the word appears in the sentence “Her under-skirt was of a turquoise-coloured imported silk crêpe embroidered with flowers”: a hidden garment of costly foreign silk with a textured surface, embroidered with floral motifs, worn beneath brighter outer layers. The details matter: the crêpe is imported (a foreign luxury arriving through Qing-dynasty trade), silk (the most prestigious fibre), and an under-skirt (wealth worn quietly, visible only in movement and at the hem).

The word is famously polysemous, and its senses fan out over fabric, food, paper, and industry:

  • Textile (primary): the crimped, pebbled, or wrinkled fabric, in silk, wool, rayon, or synthetic fibre; used for dresses, blouses, scarves, linings, and mourning dress. Sub-types fill the trade vocabulary: crêpe de chine (light silk crêpe), crêpe georgette, crêpe-back satin, canton crêpe.
  • Food: a very thin pancake of French origin, made from a batter of flour, eggs, and milk, cooked on a flat griddle and usually rolled or folded around a filling (sweet or savoury); the same word in French, borrowed into English in the 19th–20th century.
  • Crêpe paper: thin, crinkled tissue paper used for streamers and decorations; the crinkle is imparted mechanically during manufacture.
  • Crêpe rubber: a form of raw rubber with a rough, crêpe-like surface, used for shoe soles (crêpe soles) and industrial products.
  • Mourning crêpe (historical): the black, crimped silk worn as the badge of deep mourning in 19th-century Western dress; the fabric’s dull, non-reflective surface was appropriate to grief, and “in crêpe” meant “in mourning.”
  • Metaphoric / adjectival use: “crêpe-y” describes any surface texture that resembles crêpe cloth (crêpey skin with age), and “crêping” appears in technical description of puckered surfaces.

Historically, the textile sense is the root. Latin crispus (“curled, wrinkled”) passed through Old French as the adjective crespe, and the feminine form crispa was used of a crimped, “frizzy” fabric — a cloth whose very structure is a deliberate wrinkle. The word’s prestige in the 19th century was enormous: black crêpe became the universal fabric of Western mourning, so much so that the garment and the word fused (wearing “crêpe” meant mourning), and the fashion vocabulary of the day added crêpe de chine and other varieties as the smooth satins’ elegant, textured counterpoint. The pancake sense is a parallel extension in French — the thin, crinkled-edged, flat cake — and entered English separately, so that a single form now straddles textiles and cuisine, an unusual degree of polysemy for a loanword.

Against related words the boundaries are clear. Chiffon is sheer, translucent, and smooth — transparency is its essence — whereas crêpe is opaque and texturally emphatic. Satin is glossy and smooth, built on long floats of thread that reflect light, while crêpe kills the shine with its pebbled matte surface. Georgette is a specific crêpe (a dull, crinkly silk or synthetic) — a species of the crêpe family rather than a rival. Muslin and gauze are plain, open weaves without the deliberate crimp. The core remains constant: crêpe is a fabric whose character is its controlled wrinkle — and a word whose family includes pancake, paper, and rubber.
