Northwest Semitic root *ṣ-y-w / Hebrew ṣ-y-h "to be dry, parched" (Hebrew noun ṣiyyā "drought, dry land"; Ugaritic verb ṣyw "to be dry," attested in the 14th-century BCE texts of Ras Shamra). → Biblical Hebrew proper noun Ṣiyyōn (צִיּוֹן) "dry height; the stronghold/fortress of Jerusalem" (2 Samuel 5:7, c. 10th c. BCE). → Ancient Greek Σιών Siōn (Septuagint transliteration, 3rd c. BCE; also in Hebrews 12:22). → Latin Sion (Jerome's Vulgate, c. 405 CE). → Early Modern English SION (Tyndale, Geneva Bible, King James 1611, Milton 1667), the Vulgate-derived form coexisting with the alternative Zion. Debated / nuanced: the derivation from the "dry" root is the standard scholarly account and fits the exposed, waterless ridge, but it is not the only one. A minority view, since the ridge was the seat of a pre-Israelite (Jebusite) settlement, proposes a non-Semitic, possibly Hurrian or Anatolian substratum origin for the name; this remains unverifiable with present evidence. A connection to Arabic ṣiyy "waterless ground" supports the Semitic account. State explicitly: the Semitic "dry height" etymology is favored by standard lexica but the ultimate origin of a toponym so old cannot be proven.
