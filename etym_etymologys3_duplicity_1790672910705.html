duplicity travels from arithmetic to ethics without changing its image: folded double. Its ultimate elements are two of the most durable Proto-Indo-European roots: *dwo- two, source of Latin duo, Greek dyo, Sanskrit dva, and English two, twice, twin, between, combine; and *plek- to plait, to fold, source of Latin plicare to fold, Greek plekein to weave, and English ply, plait, pleat, complex, perplex, explicate. Latin combined them as du- + plicare in duplex (genitive duplicis) twofold, double — applied to tablets folded double, to double rations, to two-faced arguments — and abstracted it as duplicitas doubleness, the state of being double.

  • Latin duplicitas: In classical Latin the noun is largely neutral and physical: doubleness of leaves, of tablets, of meanings. Moral colouring appears late and lightly, in Christian writers who contrast simplicitas singleness of heart with duplicitas doubleness of mind. The ethical polarity — single good, double bad — is biblical as much as Roman: a double minded man is unstable in all his ways.
  • Old French duplicité: By the 13th–14th centuries French duplicité already means both doubleness and guile, especially in theology and law. To plead with duplicité is to double one's plea deceitfully; to speak with duplicité is to speak with a forked tongue.
  • Middle English borrowing (15th century): English takes the word via Anglo-French as duplicite, duplicity, first in legal and Chaucerian moral senses. Lydgate and Hoccleve use it for double-dealing at court. The spelling stabilises as duplicity under Latin influence in the 16th century, matching simplicity, multiplicity, and complexity.

Phonetically the French borrowing was nativised to English stress: modern RP /djuːˈplɪsɪti/ keeps the Latinate du- as /djuː/ (yod-retaining RP; General American /duːˈplɪsəti/ without yod), stresses the second syllable by the -icity rule, and reduces the ending to /sɪti/. The given RP /djuːˈplɪsɪti/ is the conservative British form. The morphology remains transparent to the learned ear: du-plic-ity, state of being folded double — twin to simplicity (folded single), complexity (folded together), and multiplicity (folded many).

Semantically English quickly moralised what Latin left neutral. By Shakespeare's time duplicity is unambiguously deceitful: double-dealing, falsehood, fraud. The Authorised Version's translators prefer dissembling and hypocrisy, but divines like Tillotson thunder against duplicity of heart. Eighteenth-century politeness made sincerity a supreme virtue, which only darkened duplicity further: to be single-hearted was English and manly; to be double was French and courtly. Nineteenth-century empire writing racialised the charge, imputing Oriental duplicity to colonised peoples — a usage now recognised as slur, but evidence of how fully the word had become an ethical weapon.

On this card, moral duplicity paired against superficial ornament restates the oldest charge against rhetoric since Plato's Gorgias: that eloquence beautifies the outside while corrupting the inside. The phrase becomes a dirty word signals semantic contamination — rhetoric itself, once the art of civic speech, is soiled by association with deceit. Duplicity here does exactly what it names: it folds the aesthetic and the ethical together, accusing beautiful speech of hiding an ugly intent.
