1. (Deep layer, debated) PIE *skab- / *skebh- 'to cut, scratch, scrape' — the scraping / cutting family from which the Germanic scab-words descend; Latin scabere 'to scratch' and scabies 'itch, mange' (→ English scabies, a medical loan) are parallel Italic reflexes, not the source of the English word. NOTE: the exact PIE reconstruction and vowel grade are debated; not all authorities accept a clean PIE etymon for the scab family.
2. Proto-Germanic *skabb- / *skab- 'scab, mangy sore, scurf' (vowel grade varies by handbook; core image: a scraped, roughened, flaking surface).
3. Old English sceabb 'scab, itch, mange' (attested from the Old English period) → native English scab, scabby; Old Norse skabbi 'scab' is the North Germanic twin.
4. English dialect shab (noun, 16th c.) 'a scab; a scurfy or mangy condition; also a low, contemptible person' — a variant of scab with the /ʃ/ initial that gives shabby its shape (spelling with sh-).
5. shabby (adjective, 1660s) 'scurfy, shabby, mean, worn' — shab + adjectival -y; the sense widened from 'mangy, diseased-looking' to 'poorly dressed, worn, dilapidated' and then to 'mean, dishonourable' (of behaviour).
6. English shabby — the exact word on this card.

PARALLEL WITNESS (not an ancestor): German schäbig 'shabby, worn, mean, stingy', from Middle Low German schabbich 'scabby, shabby', shows the same West Germanic drift from the scab-family to 'mean and worn' — independent confirmation of the semantic path, but not the source of the English form.
