PIE *legwh- "light, having little weight; quick; easy to move" (reconstructed, c. 4500-3500 BCE) → Latin levis "light in weight, not heavy; trivial; nimble" (attested 2nd-1st c. BCE) → Latin alleviare "to lighten, lift up, relieve" (ad- "to, toward" + levis) (Late Latin, Christian texts, 3rd-5th c. CE) → Old French alegier / alleger "to lighten, relieve" (10th-13th c.) → English alleviate (borrowed c. 1530 directly from Latin alleviatus, past participle of alleviare, on the model of verbs in -ate) → alleviate (modern English, /əˈliːvieɪt/) Note: the inner derivation is debated in detail — whether the base of the compound is the adjective levis "light" or the verb levare "to lift" (allevare "to lift up" is a distinct attested formation). The two verb families became blurred in Romance. English borrowed the -ate form directly from the Latin participle rather than through the French oral line.
