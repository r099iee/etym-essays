Alaric (proper noun) — the name of Alaric I, king of the Visigoths (reigned 395–410 AD), the chieftain who led the first successful sack of Rome in eight hundred years on 24 August 410, and by extension a metonym in historical and literary prose for barbarian conquest, the fall of the ancient world, and the breaching of Rome's inviolable walls. THE HISTORICAL FIGURE Alaric I was born into the Gothic noble house of the Balti and spent part of his youth as a hostage and then a federate commander in Roman service, leading Gothic troops on imperial campaigns. When pay was withheld and his people were settled on unfulfilled terms, he turned against the empire, marched through Greece, invaded Italy, and besieged Rome three times, extorting a heavy ransom before finally entering the city in August 410. The shock was enormous: the city that had not fallen to a foreign enemy for nearly eight centuries, the symbol of the world's order, had been taken. Augustine of Hippo responded with The City of God, and contemporaries read the event as the end of the age. Yet the sack was, by the standards of such catastrophes, restrained: Alaric, who was a (Christian, if Arian) chieftain, ordered his men to spare those who took refuge in the churches of the Apostles and to restore the consecrated vessels of St Peter's. This double face — the destroyer of Rome who still honoured Rome's Christian God — is exactly what Macaulay seizes on when he names Alaric first among the 'zealous Christians' who founded Teutonic dynasties in the provinces of the empire. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Historical king sense — Alaric I (c. 370–410), king of the Visigoths, the sack of Rome and the ransom of the city; recorded by Jordanes and the late-antique chroniclers in the Latin form Alaricus.
  • Second king sense — Alaric II (reigned 484–507), king of the Visigoths in Gaul and Spain, who issued the Breviarium Alaricianum (the 'Breviary of Alaric', 506), a compendium of Roman law that preserved the Theodosian Code for the Roman subjects of his kingdom; he was defeated and slain by Clovis at the battle of Vouillé in 507. The name thus also denotes Roman-law preservation in the West.
  • Metonymic and symbolic sense — in historical and literary prose 'Alaric' stands for the barbarian who breached Rome's inviolability, the archetype of the anti-imperial conqueror and a fixed landmark in narratives of the fall of the ancient world.
  • Legendary and romantic sense — the legend that Alaric died at Consentia (Cosenza) in 410 and was buried in the bed of the river Busento, its course diverted and restored so that no one could find the grave, became a set-piece of romantic historiography and verse, most famously in August von Platen's ballad; 'Alaric' therefore also carries the colour of romantic Germanic mythography.
  • Modern name sense — Alaric survives as a (rare) modern given name, revived in the nineteenth century largely under the influence of romantic literature.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGES Etymologically the name means 'ruler of all', from a Germanic compound of *allaz 'all, whole' and *-rēks/-reiks 'ruler, king' (the Celtic-loaned Germanic royal element also found in Theodoric, Ermanaric, and the Gothic reiks of Wulfila's Bible). The minority reading takes the first element as Gothic aljis 'other', making the name 'ruler of others'. The semantic bridge from the two elements to the full cultural weight of the name runs through the man: because Alaric I really did rule 'all' his Goths and briefly held the whole world's attention at Rome, the name became a type of total conquest. Each later sense — the codifier of Roman law (Alaric II), the romantic hero of the Busento, the modern revival name — refracts that original claim to rulership through a different era's concerns, until in Macaulay the 'ruler of all' becomes the first witness in a list of Germanic dynasty-founders converted to Christianity.
