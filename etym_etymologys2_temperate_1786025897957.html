*PIE *temp-* —
to stretch, span, measure
(reconstructed; the traditional etymon of temperare, but DEBATED and uncertain — cf. Latin tempus “time”, of disputed derivation)

↓

Latin temperāre —
to mix in due proportion, blend, moderate, regulate, restrain
(classical; in the traditional view a derivative of tempus “time”, i.e. due measure, season)

↓

Latin temperātus —
mixed in due proportion; moderate, restrained
(past participle of temperāre; used by Cicero and others)

↓

Old French temperé / tempré —
tempered, moderate
(adopted into French from Latin)

↓

Middle English temperat —
moderate, self-restrained, temperate
(14th century; adopted from Old French)

↓

English temperate —
moderate, mild, self-restrained; of mild climate
(the climate sense extended from classical geography’s “tempered” middle zones)

↓

TEMPERATE —
the present word

Notes on debate: The Proto-Indo-European stage is reconstructed and debated; the precise derivation of temperāre — whether from tempus “time” in the sense of due measure or season, or from a separate root meaning “to stretch” — is not fully settled, and de Vaan treats the etymology as uncertain. The climate sense arose from the classical doctrine of “tempered” zones between the torrid and the frozen.
