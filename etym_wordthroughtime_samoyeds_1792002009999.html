- 11th c. — First recorded in Old East Slavic (the Primary Chronicle, 1096 CE) as Самоядь (Samoyad'), designating northern Arctic hunter-gatherer populations dwelling beyond the Pechora River.
- 16th–17th c. — Introduced to West European Renaissance audiences through diplomatic memoirs (Sigismund von Herberstein, 1549) and English maritime travel anthologies (Richard Hakluyt, 1589), contaminated by sensational folk-etymological claims of Arctic cannibalism ("self-eaters").
- 18th–19th c. — Codified as a formal taxonomic classification in comparative philology and imperial Russian geography by scholars like Matthias Castrén, designating the eastern branch of Uralic peoples and languages.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c. — Exported to Britain and Scandinavia by Arctic explorers (Fridtjof Nansen, Frederick Jackson), initiating the transformation of the term into the premier global brand for the white polar sled-dog breed.
- 1930s to Present — Officially retired as an ethnic classification within the Soviet Union and modern anthropology in favor of Indigenous autonyms (Nenets, Nganasan, Enets, Selkup), while flourishing globally as the standard name for the domestic dog breed.
