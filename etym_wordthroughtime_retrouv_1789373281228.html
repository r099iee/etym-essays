- 12th to 14th Century - In Old French chivalric poetry, romances, and chansons de geste, *retrové / retrouvé* appears as the past participle describing captured knights safely ransomed, lost heirs recognized, or sacred relics recovered.
- 16th to 18th Century - In classical French theatre (Racine, Corneille, Molière), *retrouvé* becomes the standard vocabulary of dramatic recognition (*anagnorisis*), where long-lost children, lovers, or estranged brothers find each other in dramatic reunions.
- Early 20th Century (1927) - Elevated into a permanent monument of world literature and philosophy upon the posthumous publication of Marcel Proust's *Le Temps retrouvé* (the final volume of *À la recherche du temps perdu*), establishing *retrouvé* as the definitive symbol of involuntary memory and artistic redemption over mortality.
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Adopted directly into Anglo-American literary criticism, aesthetics, and continental philosophy; critics routinely retain the original French *retrouvé* rather than English "regained" to preserve Proust's specific philosophical nuance of recognition, circularity, and aesthetic repossession.
- 21st Century - Flourishes globally in cultural journalism, art curation, and high-register literature to describe recovered masterworks, resurrected memories, and the triumph of human artistic consciousness over the ravages of linear time.
