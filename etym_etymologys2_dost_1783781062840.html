PIE: *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, set" — a root of enormous productivity across the family (Sanskrit dádhāti "places," Greek títhēmi "I put," Latin faciō "I make"; also English do, deed, -dom).
Proto-Germanic: *dōną "to do, put" — from *dʰeh₁- with the lengthened-grade present stem; the Germanic verb generalizes "put, place" to the broad sense "to do, act."
Old English (c. 700–1100): dōn "to do, put, perform"; second-person singular present dēst "thou doest" — the *-si second-person ending fused with the stem, with i-mutation of the vowel.
Middle English (c. 1150–1500): dōst / dost — the vowel leveled and the -st ending consolidated as the standard thou-form; the variant doest (< dōest) also survives for metrical needs.
Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700): dost "thou dost" — used as both the ordinary present of do and the emphatic auxiliary; standard in the Authorized Version (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton (Paradise Lost I.17: "that dost prefer").
Modern English (archaic): dost — survives only in scriptural, liturgical, and poetic diction; standard English uses do for all persons.
NOTE ON DETAIL: Old English second-person singular was dēst (West Saxon, with i-mutation), but Anglian texts show dōst; both fed into the Middle English variants dost/doest. The -st ending is the regular second-person singular present marker inherited from PIE *-si and is not an innovation of English alone.
