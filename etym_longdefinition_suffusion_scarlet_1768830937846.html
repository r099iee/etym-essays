suffusion: The act or process of spreading over, overspreading, or diffusing a fluid, light, or color through or beneath a surface, so that the result is not a surface coating but a coloring that appears to rise from within the substance itself. The word derives from Latin suffundere, "to pour beneath or over" (sub-, "under," + fundere, "to pour"), and that literal geology of the word is exactly what the context exploits: in the passage the flowers "deepened to copper, then with a suffusion of crimson, warmed through maroon to scarlet." The color is not applied, painted, or laid over the blooms; it is a suffusion — a crimson that seems to bleed upward from beneath the flower's skin, filling the tissue from within until the whole bloom is saturated, and then continuing to deepen into maroon. The tone is painterly and almost alchemical: the landscape behaves like living, breathing matter in which light and color circulate like blood or dye beneath the surface. Primary sense: A spreading of color, light, or fluid from within or beneath a surface.
  • Of the body: a face suffused with blood (blushing), eyes suffused with tears — the oldest and most physiological use, describing a fluid rising from beneath the skin to color the surface.
  • Of color and light: a suffusion of crimson, of rose, of gold — a color that seems to saturate a surface from within rather than coat it.
  • Of emotion (figurative): a heart suffused with joy, a scene suffused with light or tenderness — the transfer of the inward-rise image from the body to the spirit.
Secondary and technical senses:
  • Medicine and ophthalmology: a suffusion is a congestive redness or accumulation of blood beneath a surface, e.g. conjunctival suffusion (bloodshot eyes) or a suffusion of the brain; the word remains current in clinical description.
  • Pathology: suffusion of a membrane or of the skin is the diffuse spread of fluid or blood into a tissue, distinct from a confined hemorrhage or bruise.
  • Literary and painterly use: in criticism and descriptive writing, a suffusion is a diffuse, glowing wash of color or feeling that colors the whole of a surface or a passage without defined edges.
Semantic bridges: The Latin verb suffundere literally means "to pour beneath or over"; the participle's passive sense, "to be overspread," carried the image of liquid poured up from below. When English borrowed suffusion in the sixteenth century it was first a medical word for blood rising under the skin, and from that physiological core the meaning spread to any spreading of color, light, or feeling from within. The distinction from related words is directional: infusion pours something into a thing, diffusion spreads something apart through a medium, effusion pours something out, and suffusion pours something up from beneath the surface itself.
scarlet: A brilliant red color with a slightly orange or vermilion cast; also, historically, a rich red cloth of the highest quality, and today the brightest, most commanding of the reds. In the passage the flowers "warmed through maroon to scarlet": scarlet is the terminal, hottest hue of the scale, the red toward which all the deepening color has been climbing. Primary sense: A vivid red, brighter and more orange than crimson, lighter than maroon.
  • Of cloth and costume: the scarlet of cardinals' robes, of ceremonial dress, of the scarlet coat of the British soldier (the "redcoats") — the color carries rank, ceremony, and display.
  • Of natural things: scarlet flowers (scarlet pimpernel, scarlet runner bean), scarlet sunsets, scarlet blood — the vividest red found in nature.
  • Of manufactured things: scarlet cloth, scarlet dye, scarlet paint — the color as a standard commercial and artistic name.
Secondary and figurative senses:
  • Scarlet fever: the disease (named for its red rash) — a nineteenth-century medical name that entered common speech.
  • Scarlet letter: in Nathaniel Hawthorne's novel of 1850, the red letter "A" worn as a badge of shame; the phrase now denotes any public mark of disgrace.
  • Scarlet woman: a prostitute or a woman of brazen, shameless character — the color as a symbol of vivid, unashamed display.
  • Scarlet as intensity: "to blush scarlet" — to blush deeply and visibly, the color as the limit of embarrassment.
Historical development and semantic bridges: The word was not originally a color at all. It entered Middle English in the thirteenth century from Old French escarlate, naming a costly cloth of the finest quality; the fabric's prized dye — kermes, the crimson of the scale-insect of the kermes oak — was a brilliant red with an orange cast, and because the cloth was so expensive, the color sense inherited the cloth's aura of rank and luxury. By the fourteenth century scarlet could name the hue itself, and the textile sense gradually faded, leaving a color word wrapped in the memory of its cloth. That is why scarlet, alone among reds, still carries a residue of ceremony: cardinals wear scarlet because cardinals once wore scarlet cloth. Distinctions from neighboring reds:
  • Scarlet vs. crimson: scarlet is brighter with an orange cast; crimson is deeper, bluer, bloodier.
  • Scarlet vs. vermilion: vermilion is a mineral pigment (mercury sulfide), orange-red and opaque; scarlet is a dye-red, more fire-like and translucent.
  • Scarlet vs. maroon: maroon is a dark brownish-red; scarlet is the light, blazing end of the scale — exactly the relation the passage uses.
