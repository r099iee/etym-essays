Nine-tenths is a compound fractional expression meaning nine out of ten equal parts — 9/10, or ninety per cent — and, by extension, the great majority or nearly all of something. Literally it belongs to arithmetic: divide a whole into ten equal tenths and take nine of them. Figuratively it functions as a rhetorical quantifier of overwhelming proportion, the decimal system’s elegant way of saying ‘almost everything’ while sounding precisely measured.

Grammatically it behaves as both noun and quasi-adjectival quantifier. As a noun it takes of: ‘nine-tenths of the harvest was lost’, ‘nine-tenths of what we seek to explain’. As a predicate or adverbial it stands alone: ‘the work is nine-tenths finished’, ‘his account proved nine-tenths invention’. The hyphen, standard in attributive and nominal use (‘a nine-tenths share’), marks it as a single lexicalised fraction rather than a loose phrase, though open forms (‘nine tenths’) appear, especially in older or American prose. Pronunciation joins the cardinal nine to the plural partitive tenths (/tɛnθs/), a consonant cluster that keeps the fraction sounding exact and slightly technical.

The figurative extension is the sense that does most cultural work. English speakers reach for nine-tenths when they want hyperbole with a straight face: ‘nine-tenths of statistics is guesswork’, ‘writing is nine-tenths rewriting’, ‘possession is nine-tenths of the law’. The mathematics is almost never verified; the function is persuasive, converting an impression of preponderance into a quasi-statistical fact. Closely allied is the idiom of near-completion — ‘nine-tenths done’ — where the fraction dramatises how little remains, the last tenth looming as the hard remainder.

  • Literal fraction (arithmetic): the quantity 9/10; nine equal shares of a whole divided in ten, e.g. ‘nine-tenths of a second’, ‘a nine-tenths majority’.
  • Overwhelming majority (rhetorical): nearly all of a mass or category, e.g. ‘nine-tenths of the rumours were baseless’; the dominant everyday sense, rarely mathematically exact.
  • Near-completion: the state of being almost finished, e.g. ‘the bridge is nine-tenths built’; stresses the smallness of what is left undone.
  • Proverbial formula: fixed sayings using the fraction for worldly wisdom (‘possession is nine-tenths of the law’), where the number lends folk authority.
  • Statistical and technical use: in science, finance, and engineering, precise 90% thresholds — tolerances, confidence, yields — where nine-tenths is genuinely computed, not estimated.
  • Distributive share: in legal or commercial division, an allotted portion of nine parts in ten, e.g. profit shares, inheritance divisions.

The semantic bridge from arithmetic to rhetoric is the prestige of number. Tenths feel scientific because decimals feel scientific; to say ‘nine-tenths’ rather than ‘most’ borrows mathematics’ authority for what is usually judgment. That is precisely its force in the source sentence: to repudiate nine-tenths of the explanandum is not merely to neglect much but to discard a measured, damning proportion — nearly the whole of it.
