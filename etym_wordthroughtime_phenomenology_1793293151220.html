- 1764 — a new science of appearing: Lambert coins Phänomenologie for the doctrine of how things seem, a modest corner of optics and perspective.
- 1807 — Hegel electrifies it: the Phenomenology of Spirit turns the word toward the grand journey of consciousness toward absolute knowing, sweeping and dialectical.
- 1900 — Husserl refounds it: the Logical Investigations make phenomenology the rigorous science of intentional acts, patient, anti-psychologistic, quietly revolutionary.
- 1927 — existence breaks in: Heidegger and then Sartre and Merleau-Ponty bend the word toward anxiety, freedom, and the lived body, and lecture halls fill.
- 1960s — expansion and suspicion: religion scholars, architects, and therapists adopt it lovingly, while analytic critics hear it as soft introspection, the source-sentence sneer at instant phenomenology.
- Today — method and brand at once: revered in philosophy and qualitative research, borrowed by designers of places and technologies, still mocked when vivid seeming replaces evidence.
