- Late 14th c. — clumps of brush: brake first names patches of bushes and briars on waste and wood-edge ground.
- 15th–16th c. — fern and thicket merge: the fern-plant brake (bracken) and the thicket brake exchange meanings until fern-covered ground is simply a brake.
- Shakespeare’s age — the lovers’ bower: a brake becomes the pastoral hiding-place — Bottom, Titania, wild thyme — shady, flowery, enchanted.
- 18th–19th c. — the sportsman’s word: hunting writers beat, draw, and preserve brakes as game covert; the cane-brake crosses to America.
- 20th c. — Tolkien’s lane: deep brakes of hazel line the Shire’s approaches, and the mechanical homonym (car brakes) drowns the thicket in noise.
- Today — green and nearly forgotten: walkers still push through brakes, but most modern ears hear only stopping-devices.
