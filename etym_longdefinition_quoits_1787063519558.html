Quoits (pronounced /kɔɪts/ or /kwɔɪts/) is a traditional historic lawn game of skill, aiming, and throwing, as well as the plural noun designating the heavy flat rings made of forged iron, vulcanized rubber, or coiled rope used in play. The objective of the game is to pitch or hurl these rings from a set distance across a pitch toward an iron pin, spike, or peg (traditionally called a hob, pin, or peg) driven into the center of a square clay pit, scoring points by landing the ring closest to the hob or encircling it cleanly (a "ringer").

The polysemous, historical, cultural, and mechanical dimensions of quoits encompass:

  • 1. The Traditional Ring-Tossing Sport:
    • Historic English and Scottish Quoits: A major rural and working-class pub and village green sport in Britain and North America from the medieval era through the early 20th century. Traditional northern quoits used heavy forged iron rings weighing several pounds, pitched with immense spin into damp clay beds.
    • Deck Quoits: A maritime variant developed on ocean passenger liners during the 19th and 20th centuries, played on shipboard wooden decks using quoits fashioned from coiled, spliced Manila rope to prevent damaging the ship's timbers.
    • Modern Lawn and Rubber Variants: Contemporary recreational versions played in backyards, schools, and parks using lightweight rubber or plastic rings.
  • 2. The Physical Throwing Ring (The Quoit):
    • Iron Quoits: Heavy annular discs of iron or steel, slightly convex on one side and concave on the other, designed with a weighted rim to stabilize the spin in flight and bite into clay upon landing.
  • 3. Archaeological and Architectural Megaliths:
    • Cornish Dolmens (Quoits): In British archaeology (especially in Cornwall and Wales), quoit is the traditional Celtic regional term for a Neolithic portal dolmen or megalithic burial chamber, named for the enormous, flat, disc-shaped granite capstone balanced atop standing upright stones (e.g., Chûn Quoit, Lanyon Quoit, or Trevethy Quoit).
  • 4. Industrial, Mechanical, and Maritime Jargon:
    • Mechanical Bushings and Washers: In older British engineering and foundry work, heavy circular iron collars, washers, or flat gaskets were occasionally referred to as quoits due to their ring-like shape.
    • Nautical Mooring Rings: In older maritime rigging, heavy iron grommets or rings attached to docks or hulls for tying off lines.

In literature and folklore (such as in Tolkien's description of the Shire hobbies of young Bilbo Baggins), quoits epitomizes quintessential pastoral English leisurely pastimes requiring keen hand-eye coordination, patience, and steady aiming.
