The morphological and historical structure of befalling is deeply rooted in the Germanic branch of the Indo-European family, formed by the union of the intensive/transitivizing prefix be- with the strong primary verb fall, extended with the present participial suffix -ing.

The root verb traces to Proto-Germanic *fallaną ('to fall, drop from a height'), which scholars derive from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *pōl- or *pʰel- ('to fall, drop down'). In Proto-Germanic, prefixing the particle *bi- (from PIE *bʰi- / *h₁epi-, denoting proximity, surrounding, or direct application) created the compound verb *bifallaną. The morphological effect of *bi- was transformative: it converted an intransitive motion verb ('to fall downward') into a transitive verb of impact and existential occurrence ('to fall upon someone; to happen to someone; to overtake').

  • Old English Origins (*Befeallan*): In Anglo-Saxon England, Old English befeallan was recorded across prose and verse (including the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* and the homilies of Ælfric). In its earliest attestations, it retained physical senses—such as falling into a pit, dropping into sin, or collapsing in combat—while simultaneously establishing the metaphorical sense of sudden occurrence or divine visitation. The pagan Germanic worldview of *Wyrd* (Fate or Destiny) viewed life events as things cast or allotted from above, an ideological framework into which befeallan fit perfectly.
  • Middle English Syntactic Bloom: By the 12th and 13th centuries, Old English befeallan transitioned into Middle English bifallen / befallen. It became one of the defining verbs of Middle English storytelling. Geoffrey Chaucer opens the General Prologue of The Canterbury Tales with this exact verb: 'Bifil that in that seson on a day...' ('It befell that in that season on a day...'). In medieval romance literature, befalling introduced adventures, providential miracles, and tragic reversals with solemn authority.
  • Shift to Participle and Calamitous Specialization: During the transition from Middle English to Early Modern English, the participial form shifted from the northern Middle English suffix -and / southern -inde to the universal modern suffix -ing (from Old English nominal suffix -ung / -ing). As general vocabulary expanded with French and Latin loanwords (such as happen from Old Norse happ, and occur from Latin occurrere), the functional domain of befall and befalling narrowed. Rather than serving as an everyday synonym for 'taking place,' it specialized into serious, high-register contexts—specifically regarding tragedies, misfortunes, and solemn destinies.
  • Modern Stylistic Endurance: Throughout the 18th to 21st centuries, in the prose of John Milton, Samuel Johnson, and Victorian novelists such as Thomas Hardy and Leo Tolstoy (in translation), befalling retained its poignant, elevated dignity. It encapsulates the existential confrontation between human vulnerability and external cosmic occurrence, maintaining unbroken continuity across more than a millennium of English literature.
