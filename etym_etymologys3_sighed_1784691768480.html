The ancestry of sighed is short but remarkably revealing of how English builds its vocabulary. The word descends from the Proto-Germanic base *sīkaną, an imitative formation whose long sibilant beginning mirrors the very sound it names: the soft, hissing outflow of a deep breath. This echoic habit is the root of the word's emotional durability — a sigh sounds like a sigh in every age, because the sound itself is the meaning. Old English inherited the verb as sīcan, "to sigh, groan, draw a deep breath", recorded in the earliest poetic texts, with iterative formations such as sicettan "to sob, sigh repeatedly" suggesting how common the word was in the soundscape of daily life. Cognates cluster in the neighbouring West Germanic dialects — Middle Low German sīken "to groan", for instance — but the root did not spread beyond the Germanic family, and no Indo-European parent can be reconstructed; the word is, so to speak, a native-grown sound.

  • Sound shifts. The Middle English period reshaped the verb from the inside. The past tense of Old English sīcan, sīcte, developed a palatal fricative in forms like sīhte, and from that past stem English speakers back-formed a new infinitive: sihen, sighen, sygh. The -gh spelling recorded the now-silent consonant, a palatal fricative pronounced with the breath of the sigh itself. Then, in the sixteenth century, the fricative was lost in pronunciation — compare through, high, light — and the Great Vowel Shift raised and diphthongised the long vowel, so that sigh, formerly rhyming roughly with see, came to rhyme with buy. The spelling stayed fixed while the pronunciation drifted, leaving the modern reader to pronounce two silent letters and a vowel that no longer looks like the word's pedigree.
  • Semantic drift. From human grief the word spread, already in early modern poetry, to the natural world: winds sigh, leaves sigh, waves sigh, so that the word became the standard verb for the melancholy voice of the landscape. The bridge is the sound: the wind's passage through trees makes a long, soft, breathing noise indistinguishable from a human sigh, and poets exploited exactly that likeness. The yearning sense — to sigh for someone or something — grew directly from the human act, the sigh as the breath of desire, carried in the sighing lover of medieval and Renaissance verse.
  • Socio-cultural factors. The sigh carried heavy literary and cultural freight. In medieval courtly love poetry the sigh was the lover's characteristic act, evidence of longing; in the eighteenth-century novel of sensibility it was a token of refined feeling; in Romantic nature poetry it became the sound of the sympathetic landscape; and in Victorian verse it was the stock gesture of melancholy. Because the word is imitative, it remained immune to the class-marking that infected other emotional vocabulary, staying a plain word of every register from nursery to pulpit.
  • Literary attestations. Chaucer uses sīken for lovers' sighs in Troilus and Criseyde; Shakespeare's verse is full of sighs; Keats hears the sigh of the wind; and Tolkien, in the passage under discussion, gives the word back to the willows, closing the circle — the human sound returns to the natural world from which the metaphor first came.

The preterite form is itself a small history. In Old English the verb was strong (sīcan, sāc, sicen), but the modern English sigh took the weak ending -ed, giving sighed — a regularisation that completed the word's movement from strong verb to weak, from ancient Germanic patterning to the modern English default. The result is that sighed, though only six letters long, contains the whole story: an imitative Germanic root, a Middle English reshaping from its own past stem, the loss of a pronounced consonant, the drift of a vowel under the Great Vowel Shift, and the regularising hand of modern English grammar.
