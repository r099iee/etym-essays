- [Middle English - to flay or strip skin] - 'Scorch' descends from Old French 'escorchier' (to strip off skin, flay); its earliest recorded sense was brutal and surgical — tearing the covering off a body.
- [Shift to surface burning (16th c.)] - The sense migrated from flaying to searing: burning or singeing a surface with dry heat so the outer layer discolours, blackens, or blisters without the whole object being consumed — cloth, skin, vegetation, earth.
- [Parching and withering (17th-18th c.)] - Extended to dryness without fire: sun-scorched fields and lips, a scorched land robbed of moisture; the word fused withering dryness with dark discolouration.
- [Cooking and warfare (19th-20th c.)] - In kitchens 'scorching' meant browning or singeing a surface quickly; in warfare 'scorched earth' named the strategy of burning everything the enemy could use, giving the word a deliberate, totalizing cruelty.
- [Modern register] - Today 'scorch' holds a precise middle ground between 'singe' and 'char', still evoking apocalyptic, dry, blistering destruction — the natural twin of 'shrivel' in a curse like the goblins' 'Shrivel and scorch!'.
