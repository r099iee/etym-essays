Throb (verb and noun) names a steady, rhythmic pulsation that is felt or heard rather than merely seen. As a verb, to throb is to beat, pulse, or vibrate with a repeated, usually dull and heavy rhythm; as a noun, a throb is a single beat or the continuing pulsation itself. The word is deeply physical: it is used of the heart, of an artery, of pain, of sound, and, by extension, of anything that seems to pulse with energy or life.

In the source context — “A sound, too, began to __________ in his ears” — the verb describes a noise that does not simply ring or hum but beats with a slow, forceful rhythm inside the ears, as if the sound itself had a pulse. The sentence compares it to bubbling and to a giant cat's purr: both are rhythmic, liquid, and organic, and throb binds them together by stressing the pulse common to all three.

Primary senses

  • Of the heart, pulse, or a wound — to beat rhythmically: “his heart throbbed with fear”, “the vein in his temple throbbed”. Emphasises a strong, felt, often alarming beat.
  • Of pain — to ache with a rhythmic beat: “her head was throbbing”, “a throbbing toothache”. The pain appears to pulse in time with the heartbeat, each throb a wave of ache.
  • Of sound — to pulsate: “bass throbbing through the floor”, “a throb of distant drums”. The sound has a slow, felt beat, usually low and powerful.
  • Of machinery and engines: an engine “throbs” as it idles, its vibration carrying a regular rhythm.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • To be full of life or emotion: “the city throbbed with life”, “he throbbed with excitement or rage”. The pulse of the city or of feeling is imagined as a heartbeat; note the phrase is normally “throb with” an emotion.
  • Vibrate or quiver intensely: “the air throbbed with heat” — the heat itself seems to pulse in waves.
  • As a noun — a single beat or pulse: “the steady throb of the engine”, “a throb of pain”. Both countable and uncountable uses occur.
  • In medicine: throbbing is a canonical descriptor of inflammatory pain; a “throbbing headache” (e.g. migraine) is set against stabbing or burning pain in clinical vocabulary.
  • In music and audio: “throbbing bass”, the throb as the pulse of a track; the word carries connotations of physical, bodily engagement — music you feel in the chest.
  • Romance and slang: “my heart throbbed” in love writing; the 20th-century noun heartthrob (first attested 1927) for a celebrity whose presence sets hearts racing derives directly from this sense.

Semantic boundaries and register

Throb differs from its synonyms by its dullness, regularity, and physical depth. It is not sharp (that would be prick or stab); it is not quick and light (that would be flutter); it is not solely visible (that would be vibrate). A throb is felt as much as heard or seen — a beat you are inside. It is equally at home in medical prose, in engineering description, and in the most purple romantic fiction, and this breadth of register, together with its bodily immediacy, is what makes it so useful in literary writing such as the source sentence.

Etymology in brief

Throb appears in Middle English around the late 14th century as throbben. No earlier ancestor is attested, and the word is judged to be imitative (echoic): it mimics the sound of a heavy, regular pulse.
