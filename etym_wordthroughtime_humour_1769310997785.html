- Bodily fluid (14th c.) - Imported from Old French as a term of humoral medicine: one of the four fluids - blood, phlegm, and the two biles - whose balance was thought to govern health and character.
- Temperament and state of mind (16th c.) - The fluid becomes the disposition it was held to produce: good humour, ill humour, in and out of humour; to humour someone is to indulge their mood or whim.
- The comic quality (18th c.) - Broadens from temperament to the disposition of a mind that finds the world amusing; 'a sense of humour' fuses a cheerful temperament with a relish for comedy.
- Modern usage - The double career persists: everyday 'sense of humour' and literary dark and gallows humour, with British spelling humour kept beside American humor, and the word still able to mean both mood and mirth at once.
