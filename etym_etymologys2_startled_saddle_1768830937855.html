Chain for STARTLED: - PIE *ster- 'stiff, rigid' (with a debated secondary sense 'to move briskly, spring up'; some scholars instead posit a separate unattested root for 'to leap') — see note. - Proto-Germanic *sturtjaną 'to start up, leap up, spring' — the reconstructed verb of sudden movement. - Old English styrtan 'to leap up, spring up, start, jump' (attested from the Old English period). - Middle English sterten 'to leap, jump, start, rush, move suddenly' (13th-14th c.). - Modern English start 'to move suddenly; to begin' — the base verb, with the 'begin' sense developed from the 'start up' sense in the 16th c. - English startle (16th c.) — a frequentative formation: start + the suffix -le, marking repeated or sudden involuntary action; 'to cause to start; to start involuntarily.' - Modern English startled (past tense and past participle; also an adjective). Note on the root: The PIE ancestor is uncertain. Traditional dictionaries connect *ster- 'stiff, rigid' on the idea of a startled creature springing up stiff, or 'to move briskly'; modern handbooks are less certain, and some prefer an unattested root meaning 'to leap.' The Germanic intermediate *sturtjaną and the Old English form styrtan are secure; only the deepest layer is debated. Chain for SADDLE: - PIE *sed- 'to sit' — the root of English sit, set, seat and Latin sedere 'to sit.' - Proto-Germanic *sadulaz 'saddle' — a 'seat for riding,' built from the sitting root with an instrumental suffix (*sed- + *-tl-/*-dl-); the same formation gives Latin sella 'seat, saddle' and Old Church Slavonic sedlo 'saddle.' - Proto-West Germanic *sadul. - Old English sadol (Anglian sadul) 'saddle' — the word is native, attested from the Old English period. - Middle English sadel / saddel 'saddle' (13th-14th c.). - Modern English saddle (the modern spelling with -dd- settles in the 16th c.). Note on cognates: The Germanic *sadulaz is an inherited word, not a loan — its exact cognates appear in Old Norse sǫðull, Old High German satul, and Gothic saduls, and, more distantly, in Latin sella and Old Church Slavonic sedlo, all from the same sitting root. The reconstruction is secure; the only nuance is the exact suffix shape (*-tl- vs *-dl-), which does not affect the English form.
