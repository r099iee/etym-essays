When Gibbon calls his own Greek training desultory, he is not merely saying it was incomplete; he is confessing that it lacked a single continuous path. The word's buried image is of a circus rider vaulting from horse to horse: study that leaps from author to author, grammar to lexicon, without settling into systematic drill. Set against "the scrupulous ear of the well-flogged critic," desultory becomes a moral-intellectual self-indictment — fragmented enthusiasm versus institutional discipline. The tone is candid and slightly rueful, the mature memoirist measuring youthful curiosity against the standards of the professional classicist and finding a scatter of leaps where others had a course.

Core meaning: lacking a plan, purpose, or continuous course; occurring in an irregular, disconnected, or sporadic way; jumping or passing from one thing to another without settled method or connection. The word applies to activities, efforts, attention, conversation, reading, and (in military use) fire.

Primary senses:
  • Lacking a plan or method — without systematic order: "his desultory reading habits meant he knew a little about everything."
  • Irregular and discontinuous — occurring in fits and starts, with no sustained line: "desultory conversation," "desultory fire" (military: intermittent, scattered shooting).
  • Moving or passing from one thing to another — the literal "leaping about" sense, from the Roman circus rider (dēsultor) who jumped from one horse to another: "desultory remarks," "a desultory attempt."

Secondary and extended uses:
  • Academic and intellectual self-description — scholars and memoirists use "desultory" to confess scattered, unsystematic study, often with modest understatement ("my desultory acquaintance with…").
  • Military usage — "desultory fire" or "desultory bombardment" names scattered, uncoordinated shooting, in contrast to concentrated fire.
  • Social register — "desultory chat" or "desultory small talk" is conversation that drifts without direction, never settling on a topic.
  • Literary-critical usage — critics call narratives or arguments "desultory" when they wander from point to point without a main line or sustained structure.

Historical semantic bridge: the modern meanings all flow from the Latin circus rider. Latin dēsultor was the acrobat who leapt down from one galloping horse onto another, and the adjective dēsultōrius meant "of or like a desultor" — hence, by easy metaphor, "skipping about, inconstant, superficial." Early modern English borrowed the transferred sense: discourse or study that vaults from point to point without staying the course. The concrete rider fades; the pattern of motion remains. The word thus always implies not mere laziness but discontinuous attention — energy spent in jumps rather than in a single line of march.

Collocations and register: desultory conversation / reading / study / fire / remarks / attempts / work / attention. Register is educated and slightly formal; the word thrives in literary criticism, memoir, and historiography more than in casual speech, and it can carry a mildly self-deprecating or gently critical tone. Antonymic foils include systematic, methodical, continuous, sustained, concentrated, and regular.
