Etymology of English scot-free: a compound of two separate roots, scot (a tax) and free. Each root is given its own chain. The phrase has nothing to do with Scotland.

FIRST ELEMENT — scot 'tax, contribution'

1. Proto-Germanic *skuta- / *skot- 'shot; contribution, payment' [reconstructed] — related to the verb *skeutaną 'to shoot, cast, throw'; the underlying image is of money cast or 'shot' into a common pot or reckoning.
2. Old Norse skot 'tax, contribution, payment toward a reckoning' [attested in medieval Scandinavian records].
3. Old English sceot / scot 'payment, contribution, tax' [attested] — the native West Germanic cognate, surviving in the legal phrase 'scot and lot' (parish rates and dues).
4. Middle English scot 'municipal or parish tax; one's share of a bill' [attested, 12th–14th c.] — also the source of colloquial shot 'one's share of a tavern bill'.
5. In the compound scot-free: 'exempt from the scot', attested from late Middle English / early modern English, then generalized to 'without any penalty or consequence'.

SECOND ELEMENT — free

1. PIE *priH- / *preyH- 'to love, please' [reconstructed] — the semantic path runs 'dear, beloved' → 'not in bondage, at liberty'.
2. Proto-Germanic *frijaz 'beloved; free, not in thrall' [reconstructed] — cognates: Gothic freis, Old High German frī (German frei), Old Norse frír / frjáls, Dutch vrij.
3. Old English frēo / frīo 'free, noble, exempt' [attested from the earliest Old English records].
4. Modern English free — the second element of the compound scot-free.

COMPOUND
  • The full idiom scot-free = scot (tax, payment) + free (exempt): originally 'exempt from the levy', later 'free from any punishment or harm'.

WARNING
  • The folk etymology that connects scot-free to Scotland is historically false; the first element is the tax-word scot, not the ethnonym Scot.
