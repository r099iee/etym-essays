- Old English pattern - the name is built from the deep-rooted compound of 'wild' (Old English 'wilde,' of creatures not under man's control) + 'land'; it revives the shape of 'wilddeor' (wild beast) and the place-naming habit that characterized whole regions by their fierceness.
- Tolkien's coinage (1930s) - the philologist-author, steeped in Old English, invents 'Wilderland' as a deliberately archaic proper noun for the trackless country east of the Misty Mountains; capitalized and chronicle-like, it turns a landscape into a character and carries the ominous weight of the unknown.
- 20th century - through The Hobbit, the word passes from a private coinage into the shared vocabulary of fantasy, standing beside Mirkwood and the Waste as the name of untamed, dangerous country; it no longer reads as a common noun but as a realm.
- Contemporary - 'Wilderland' is recognized chiefly as Tolkien's term and a generic label for vast, indifferent, beast-haunted country; unlike 'wilderness' (from Old English 'wilddeornes,' place of wild beasts), it remains a named, geographical, mythic place rather than a moral emblem of desolation.
