lunged — past tense and past participle of the verb lunge: to make a sudden forward thrust or plunge of the body, especially a swift thrust with a sword in fencing or combat; to spring at something with the whole body extended to its full reach. In the card's context, 'when she lunged at him again' marks the escalation of the duel: the woman launches her entire frame forward in a single elongation, the blade leading, her body stretched to its full reach. The verb's root meaning, 'to lengthen,' is physically realized in the posture: the lunge is the body literally making itself longer to close the distance between fighter and target. The motion is linear, explosive, and committed — and because it overcommits, it is exactly the move a skilled opponent can punish, which is what Turjan does next.
  • Sense 1 — fencing: the deep forward thrust of the leading leg with the weight thrown behind the blade; the classic attacking technique.
  • Sense 2 — general attack: any sudden forward spring at a person or thing, of boxers, animals, and desperate people.
  • Sense 3 — horse-training (lunge / longe): the circular exercise in which a horse is worked on a long rope; a separate borrowing now merged in spelling.
The fencing sense is the historical heart of the word: 'lunge' entered English in the eighteenth century from French as the technical name for the long forward extension of the fencing attack. From the sword it spread in the nineteenth century to every creature that throws its whole body forward — a boxer lunges, a tiger lunges, a desperate man lunges — and the word kept its dark logic: because a lunge is all-in, it is the move that leaves a fighter exposed. In this passage the word does double work: it names the woman's commitment and, by its very meaning, predicts its own punishment. fended — past tense and past participle of the verb fend: to ward off, repel, or push away; to keep (a blow, a danger, a person) at a distance by an active, physical or verbal deflection; and, reflexively, to look after oneself ('to fend for oneself'). The verb is historically the clipped heart of 'defend' — the aphetic form that shed its first syllable in Middle English but kept its ancestor's full meaning, 'to strike a blow away.' In the card's context, 'he fended off the blow' describes a sharp, economical deflection: a single movement of the sword that turns an attack into an opening, so that the defender, 'leaning forward, touched the point to her arm.' Fending is active, immediate, and muscular — it is not passive guarding but the deliberate striking away of a threat.
  • to fend off — to deflect, repel, or beat back (blows, attacks, questions, reporters).
  • to fend for oneself — to provide for one's own needs; to look after oneself without help.
  • to fend against (archaic / regional) — to guard against, resist.
The reflexive sense is as old as the verb's modern life: from the sixteenth century, 'to fend for oneself' means to manage, to get along, to supply one's own needs — the everyday self-reliance of the capable poor. The phrasal 'fend off' keeps the martial core: a blow is fended off by one sharp movement, a question by a wave of the hand, an attack by the blade's quick turn. The word's brevity is its character — it is defend without the ceremony, the bare, muscular act of striking a threat away.
