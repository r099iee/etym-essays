embarked is the past tense and past participle of the verb to embark, meaning "to go on board a ship, aircraft, or other vehicle for a journey" and, by extension, "to begin a new and significant venture, enterprise, or course of action." The word's core is physical and nautical: it pictures a person stepping from the firm, known world of land into the moving, uncertain world of a vessel. Its root is a compound of en-/em- "into" and barque/bark "boat," so that to embark is literally "to get into a boat."

In the supplied context. "The preparations were finally complete, and he too now embarked as an escort for the young girl." The word marks the moment of committed departure: after long preparation, the character steps aboard and takes up his role — an escort — on a journey. The emphasis falls on the crossing of a threshold: from shore to ship, from readiness to action, from observer to participant. Embarked here fuses the literal boarding with the taking-on of a duty that will shape the journey to come.

Range of senses and uses.

  • To go on board a vessel — embark on a ship; the passengers embarked at noon; troops embark for overseas service. This is the literal nautical sense, current since the 16th century.
  • To begin a journey or venture (metaphorical) — embark on a career, embark on a project, embark on a new life: the dominant modern sense, always followed by "on" or "upon."
  • Transitive: to take passengers or cargo on board — "to embark troops," "the ship embarked its cargo": in this use the ship or its crew is the agent.
  • Opposite: disembark "to go ashore," with the noun disembarkation; embarkation is the noun for the act of boarding.
  • Extended to air and space travel — embark on a flight; astronauts embark on a mission: the same threshold-crossing idea, transferred from sea to any departure.

Register and nuance. Embarked is more formal and more weighted than "boarded": boarding is mechanical and routine (you board a bus), while embarking implies a significant, risky, or purpose-filled beginning. In its figurative sense the word almost always signals commitment to something consequential — a project, a journey, a stage of life — whose outcome is uncertain. The intransitive sense takes "on" or "upon," and the word is rarely used of trivial beginnings.

Usage notes. The past tense and past participle are both embarked, pronounced identically. The construction "embark on/upon + noun" is the standard figurative pattern; the bare transitive "embark someone/something" is reserved for loading. The noun pair embarkation (boarding) and disembarkation (landing) marks the beginning and end of the same journey.

Metaphoric shift and semantic bridge. The word's double life rests on a single physical image: the threshold of the gangplank. To board a vessel is to commit — the shore recedes, the ship moves, and turning back becomes costly or impossible. English borrowed that concrete act in the 1540s and within a generation was applying it to anything that required the same irreversible commitment: a war, a career, a reform, a marriage, a sea of troubles. The bridge is preserved in the grammar itself, for "embark on" (with the preposition echoing the boarding of a vessel) is exactly parallel to "board a ship": you enter something that will then carry you. Where "begin" merely marks a first moment, "embark" insists on the commitment and the risk, and that is why the present reading context is so apt — the escort embarks not merely because the voyage starts, but because his duty, his presence on the journey, is now irrevocably undertaken.

Distinctions worth remembering. Embarked always points forward to the journey that follows, not backward to the moment left behind. It is the word of the threshold itself — the last firm step on land, or the first committed step into an undertaking — and it keeps a faint nautical shimmer even in its most abstract uses, reminding the careful reader that every great beginning is, at root, a setting sail.
