- Old English (c. 8th–10th Century) - Attested as *specca*: A tiny natural spot, blemish, or mark on animal skin, cloth, or the human body.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1450) - Expansion to tiny particles: Deepened into general use for minute particles of dust, dirt, or ash, alongside physical blemishes on fruits and fabrics.
- Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700) - Metaphorical and astronomical scaling: Applied to distant objects viewed in perspective (e.g. distant ships or stars appearing as tiny specks) and abstract minimal quantities ('not a speck of doubt').
- 18th to 19th Century - Maritime and Whaling Loan: Interacted in coastal jargon with Dutch *speck* / *spek* ('blubber, fat of a whale'), forming a distinct nautical homonym.
- Contemporary Usage - Universal standard term across science, everyday description, and literature for microscopic particulate matter, immaculate cleanliness standards, or astronomical insignificance (e.g. Carl Sagan's 'pale blue dot' as a speck of dust in the cosmos).
