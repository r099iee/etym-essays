- Rome — paying peoples: tributarius describes persons and states liable to tributum, the war-tax levied tribe by tribe, an adjective of duty and subjection.
- Late antiquity — paying rivers: writers begin calling lesser streams tributarii, waters paying their flow into greater ones, and the metaphor quietly joins the money.
- Middle Ages — chronicles and charters: monks record tributary kingdoms on the frontiers, Welsh princes, Moorish taifas, crusader counties, while poets let rivers gather their tributary brooks.
- 16th–18th c. — the river sense rises: atlases and voyages make tributary the standard word for feeder streams, the Amazon's hundred tributaries amazing Europe, while empires still count tributary states from Algiers to Mysore.
- 19th c. — science generalises it: geologists map tributary valleys and glaciers, anatomists trace tributary veins, and the money-sense retreats to history books.
- Today — the honoured contributor: tributary paths, ideas, and memories pour into greater wholes, the source sentence's Road-philosophy made everyday, and only historians hear the tax-collector in it.
