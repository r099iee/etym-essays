TRAILING MY COAT is an English idiomatic phrase meaning deliberately provoking a quarrel, fight, or controversy by making aggressive, challenging, or ostentatiously provocative statements. In the supplied context — "Apart from TRAILING MY COAT about minor controversies" — the speaker admits, with a mixture of insolent confidence and theatrical light-heartedness, to baiting opponents over comparatively trivial matters; the phrase signals a habitual, performative combativeness rather than genuine malice. The nuance is crucial: unlike spontaneous anger, trailing one's coat implies a calculated, public performance designed to lure an adversary into argument, while the qualifier "minor controversies" reveals that the provocation is more about sport and ego than about substantive disagreement. The idiom's origin is literal and picturesque. In nineteenth-century Ireland (and to a lesser extent in England), a man who wished to pick a fight could drag or "trail" his coat along the ground behind him as he walked; the long skirt of the tailcoat, which swept the dust and mud of the street or fairground, was an open invitation for a passer-by to tread upon it. Stepping on the trailing garment was understood as the deliberate insult that entitled the owner to challenge the offender to a fight. The gesture therefore encoded the entire social logic of provocation in an object: the coat dragged on the ground was a dare issued indiscriminately to any comer, and to step on it was to accept the challenge. The phrase to trail one's coat (or to trail one's coattails) then travelled from the literal gesture into figurative use: to trail one's coat is to behave in a way that is calculated to pick a quarrel, to court controversy, or to invite hostile reaction by one's words and attitude, even where no physical challenge is intended. Polysemy and related senses. The phrase is fixed but admits a spectrum of emphasis. At its most literal, trailing one's coat refers to the actual historical gesture of dragging a garment to provoke a fight. In its central figurative sense, it names deliberate verbal provocation — making statements designed to rile, bait, or offend so as to draw an opponent into dispute. In a lighter, almost self-mocking use (as in the Empson context here), it describes the pleasure of intellectual provocation, "flying a kite" of an unpopular opinion simply to see who will bite, where the sport is in the reaction rather than in any genuine hostility. The individual elements keep their ordinary senses: trail means to drag or draw behind, and by extension to lag or follow; my is the first-person possessive; coat is the outer garment. None of these is altered in the idiom; what the idiom adds is the fixed image of the dragged garment as a dare. Semantic and stylistic boundaries:
  • Core meaning: deliberate, ostentatious provocation of a quarrel or controversy by challenging words or actions.
  • Literal historical sense: the actual nineteenth-century gesture of dragging a coat along the ground to invite a passer-by to step on it and so start a fight.
  • Figurative political / intellectual sense: courting controversy, baiting opponents, or adopting provocative positions for sport, attention, or rhetorical advantage.
  • Light-hearted / self-deprecating sense (this card): a confessed habit of picking fights over minor or trivial matters, with more ego and entertainment than malice.
  • Register: chiefly literary and colloquial; the full idiom is more common in British than American English, and is prized for its vivid, slightly archaic and theatrical flavour.
Distinguishing the phrase from its near-synonyms sharpens its meaning. Picking a fight implies direct, personal, often physical aggression toward a specific opponent, whereas trailing one's coat is a broader, more public and symbolic gesture that invites any passer-by to take offence. Playing devil's advocate involves arguing an unpopular position for intellectual or pedagogical reasons, without genuine hostility or desire to wound. Provoking or riling someone suggests goading a particular individual, often through taunts, whereas trailing the coat is an indiscriminate dare issued to a crowd. Courting controversy emphasises seeking public attention and debate, but without the belligerent, duelling connotation of a coat left literally dragging on the ground for someone to step upon — the traditional signal that a fight is being offered. Finally, the modern American to run for office or to float a trial balloon lacks the aggressive, adversarial edge: trailing one's coat is not merely testing opinion but daring someone to take offence.
