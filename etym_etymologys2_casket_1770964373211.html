PIE *kap- "to grasp, take, hold, contain" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin capere "to take, seize, grasp, contain" (attested from the earliest Latin, c. 7th–3rd c. BCE; the base of an enormous family including capsa, capacitas, captivus) → Latin capsa "box, chest, repository for scrolls or valuables" (a nominal derivative of capere, "container for holding"; attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE, e.g. the scroll-boxes of Roman libraries) → Latin capsula "small box, little chest" (diminutive of capsa; attested in Imperial Latin; the direct ancestor of French cassette and English capsule) → Vulgar Latin *capsa / *cassa "box, chest" (Late Latin; intervocalic -p- voiced to -b- and then simplified to -ss- in the Romance vernaculars) → Old French casse "box, chest, case" (attested from c. 10th–12th c. CE) → Anglo-Norman casse / caske "box, chest, barrel" (imported into England after the Norman Conquest, 11th–12th c. CE) → Middle English cask / caske "barrel, wooden container" + the diminutive suffix -et (or an Anglo-French diminutive formation on casse) → casket "little chest, jewel box" (first attested mid-15th c. CE) → Modern English casket "small ornamental box for jewels; (chiefly US, from 19th c.) a burial container" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The exact mechanism by which English acquired casket is slightly uncertain. The OED records it as a 15th-century formation, most plausibly an Anglo-French or English diminutive of casse/cask with the suffix -et ("little cask"), but a direct derivation from the French diminutive cassette is also possible and some authorities link the form to the influence of the unrelated word cask "barrel." The PIE root *kap- "to grasp, hold" behind Latin capere is secure; the Romance-to-English portion is securely attested. The funerary sense is a 19th-century American semantic extension, not part of the word's original meaning.
