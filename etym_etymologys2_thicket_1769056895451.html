Chain for "thicket" — the ancestry of thicket: 1. Proto-Indo-European *tegu- (also *tegus) — "thick, dense, fat". A root of density; a commonly cited cognate is Lithuanian tigulas "thick, dense". Its exact Indo-European spread is debated (some authorities are cautious), but the Germanic reflex is secure. 2. Proto-Germanic *þikkuz (also reconstructed *þik(k)uz) — "thick, dense". The inherited Germanic adjective; it underlies Old Norse þykkr, Old High German dicchi, Middle Dutch dic, Gothic *þiqqus, and Old English þicce. 3. Old English þicce (attested from the 8th century onward) — "thick, dense, crowded, close-set". The adjective from which the noun is built. (Old English þicce is the ancestor of modern English "thick".) 4. Old English þiccet (attested from the Old English period, c. 9th–11th century; also spelled þiccett, þicced) — "a thicket, a dense growth of bushes or shrubs". Formed transparently from þicce "thick" + the collective noun-suffix -et/-ot (a native Germanic suffix forming nouns from adjectives, as in "thicket" = "a place where things grow thick"). 5. Middle English thikket / thikkette (12th–15th century) — "a dense growth of bushes or small trees". Regular development of Old English þiccet; the sense is unchanged. 6. English thicket (16th century onward; modern form) — "a dense, tangled growth of shrubs, bushes, or small trees", and, since the 19th century, figuratively "a dense, confusing mass" ("a thicket of rules"). thicket IS OUR WORD. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: The formation of the noun from the adjective is transparent and secure. The only nuance concerns the suffix: although -et looks like the French diminutive ending (coronet, cigarette), it is a native Germanic collective suffix — the word never passed through French, and "thicket" is not a "little thick" but "a dense growth". The PIE root *tegu- is accepted by Pokorny and Etymonline, though some scholars treat the deeper Indo-European assignment with caution; the Germanic ancestry from *þikkuz is not in doubt.
