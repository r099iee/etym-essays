LINEAR CHAIN (VICISSITUDES, plural of VICISSITUDE) 1. PIE *weik- / *weyk- (to bend, wind, turn, yield) — reconstructed root; the "turning" idea behind a large family of words for giving way and for cyclical change. 2. Latin vicis (a turn, change, succession, reciprocal place; attested chiefly in oblique cases, e.g. the genitive vice) — classical Latin, 1st c. BCE onward. 3. Latin vicissim (in turn, by turns, alternately) — derived from vicis; classical Latin. 4. Latin vicissitūdō (change, alternation, mutual succession; abstract noun built on vicis with the suffix -tūdō) — classical Latin, attested from the 1st c. BCE. 5. Middle French / Old French vicissitude (change, alternation, vicissitude of fortune) — 14th century. 6. English vicissitude (change, alternation, successiveness; later, a change of fortune) — borrowed c. 1560s–1570s, first attested in the 16th century. 7. English vicissitudes (the plural: successive changes, reverses, and turns of fortune) — idiomatic in the plural from the 17th–18th century onward; the dominant modern form. SISTER ENGLISH FORMS from the same Latin root: vicar, vicarious, vice- (vice-president, viceroy) — all from vicis / vicarius "substitute, stand-in." NOTE: The PIE root *weik-/*weyk- is a standard reconstruction, but the exact set of derivatives assigned to it (Germanic week, weak, weichen) involves some debate; the Latin chain from vicis onward is solidly attested.
