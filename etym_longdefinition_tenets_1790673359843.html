tenets is the plural noun form of tenet, meaning a central doctrine, principle, dogma, or belief that is held to be true, especially one held in common by members of a church, school, philosophical movement, political party, or profession. To say “the tenets of Stoicism” or “the tenets of modern journalism” is to refer not to casual opinions but to the load-bearing propositions that define membership: deny them and you are, strictly speaking, no longer of that school. In the source sentence — “behaved according to these tenets” — the word carries its full normative force: tenets are not merely descriptions of the world but prescriptions for action, things one lives by, departs from at the cost of inconsistency, and can be accused of betraying.

The singular tenet is grammatically singular but pragmatically almost always plural, because belief systems travel in clusters. Dictionaries define it as “a principle, belief, or doctrine generally held to be true” (OED), with stress on held: a tenet is what a community holds. That holding metaphor matters. Unlike a “fact,” which claims to stand whether or not anyone endorses it, a tenet openly confesses its dependence on holders. Unlike a mere “opinion,” it claims authority, stability, and teachability; tenets are written into catechisms, manifestos, textbooks, mission statements, and codes of conduct. They are recited, examined, defended, and transmitted to novices.

Core modern senses and their bridges:

  • Religious / philosophical doctrine: the primary sense — e.g., the tenets of Buddhism (Four Noble Truths), the tenets of Christianity (Nicene Creed). Bridge: direct continuation of the Medieval Latin scholastic use, where tenet introduced a proposition a master “helds” in disputation.
  • Political / ideological principle: e.g., the tenets of liberalism, Marxism, feminism, free-market economics. Bridge: 17th–18th-century extension during confessional and party conflict, when each faction was understood to have its portable creed.
  • Professional / methodological creed: e.g., the tenets of evidence-based medicine, of modernist prose, of community policing. Bridge: 19th–20th-century broadening, where any disciplined practice is dignified with quasi-confessional language.
  • Personal moral code (looser, often ironic): e.g., “Smith lives by three simple tenets.” Bridge: individualization of a collective noun for rhetorical weight; calling a habit a “tenet” makes it sound principled rather than idiosyncratic.
  • Legal / logical tenet (rare): a proposition taken as a premise in argument. Bridge: survival of the scholastic disputation formula “tenet quod…” (“he holds that…”).

Register and collocation deserve attention. Tenets is formal, Latinate, and dignified; it elevates its referent. Newspapers speak of the “tenets” of a faith but of the “ideas” or “views” of a pundit. Common collocates are uphold, espouse, adhere to, betray, violate, expound, codify, central, core, basic, founding, democratic, religious. One holds, follows, lives by, acts according to, or departs from tenets; one does not merely “think” them. The word also carries a faint whiff of rigidity to skeptical ears — to call something a tenet can imply unexamined dogma — which is why critics say “challenging the tenets” as praise and defenders say “faithful to the tenets” as praise; the same noun serves both loyalties.

Do not confuse tenet with tenant (occupier of land, from French tenant “holding” in a different legal sense) or with tenor (general course or drift). The spelling tenent is a common error produced by analogy with Latin tenent (“they hold”); the correct English noun is tenet (“he holds”), plural tenets, pronounced /ˈtɛnɪts/. In exams and essays it functions as a precision upgrade over “beliefs” or “ideas” when you mean structural, identity-defining commitments rather than passing thoughts.
