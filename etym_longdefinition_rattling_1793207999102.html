Rattling is the present participle and verbal noun of rattle: sounding, shaking, or moving with a rapid succession of short hard knocks — the noise of loose things struck together in quick repetition. As an adverb and adjective of manner it describes anything done with that clattering energy: a rattling good story, a rattling pace, bones rattling in a box.

In the source passage Spicer the stock-broker comes ______ in with four horses and a couple of out-riders — driving into Guildford fast, loudly, and showily, harness jingling and wheels clattering over the road. Cobbett sets the participle against the lord's modest one-horse gig: where his Lordship retrenches, Spicer rattles. The word carries the whole contrast — haste, noise, and vulgar display in a single clatter — and the reader hears the four-horse team before seeing it.

Core meaning in detail: At its heart rattling is imitative: the syllables themselves perform the knocks they name. From that acoustic core three uses fan out. First, the literal noise: windows rattling in wind, dice rattling in a cup, a kettle rattling on the hob. Second, the manner of motion: to go rattling along is to travel fast and noisily, shaking as one goes — coaches, carts, and later trains and lorries. Third, the colloquial intensifier: a rattling pace, a rattling success — where the noise has faded and only the speed and gusto remain.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Driving or moving fast with noise (Cobbett's sense, from 17th c.): to come rattling in with horses, to go rattling down the road. The bridge joins sound to speed: a fast coach on a hard road cannot help clattering, so the noise becomes the name of the pace.
  • Making a rapid clatter of knocks (primary literal sense, Middle English): loose objects shaken together — chains, windows, teeth in cold. The bridge is pure imitation: the word sounds like its referent, which is why every generation understands it without teaching.
  • Shaking or jolting roughly (transitive and intransitive): the cart rattled the passengers; the train rattled over the points. The bridge extends the noise to the motion that causes it: to rattle a thing is to subject it to the knocks, whether of a road or of fear.
  • Chattering volubly, gabbling (figurative, from 16th c.): to rattle on, to rattle off names or verses. The bridge runs from clattering objects to clattering tongues: rapid speech is heard as a kind of mouth-noise, empty and percussive, like dice in a cup.
  • Disturbing, unnerving (modern colloquial): to be rattled by bad news or by a hostile crowd. The bridge passes through the shaken nerves: as a window is shaken in its frame, so composure is shaken by shock — and the victim rattles on, talking too fast.
  • A lively intensifier (colloquial adjective and adverb, 19th c.): a rattling good tale, a rattling pace, to get along rattlingly. The bridge keeps the energy while dropping the sound: rattling becomes pure gusto, the verbal equivalent of cracking pace.

Register and connotation: rattling is cheerful vigorous English, at home in narrative and conversation rather than in statutes or sermons. It likes speed and dislikes ceremony: things that rattle are unpretentious, physical, and slightly out of control. Cobbett exploits exactly this: Spicer's rattling arrival convicts him of push and ostentation before a word of accusation is spoken. Even the intensifier keeps a wink — a rattling good fellow is admirable precisely because he is not solemn.
