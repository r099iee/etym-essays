- [Early English: full of one's own will] - 'Wilful' first describes a person abundant in will - determined and self-directed; connotation is neutral or even admirable, the mark of a strong character who follows their own course.
- [17th-19th century: the turn to disapproval] - The word darkens: a wilful person is headstrong, obstinate, disobedient to advice, rules, and convention; in law 'wilful misconduct' names harm done on purpose - connotation becomes firmly pejorative, the flaw of the self-willed child who insists on having his own way.
- [Contemporary sense] - Still carries deliberate obstinacy, with the British spelling 'wilful' and American 'willful'; connotation is disapproving but double-edged - it condemns the defiance even as it concedes the strength of will behind it, and in legal English it denotes precise, knowing intentionality.
