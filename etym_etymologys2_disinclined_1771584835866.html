Linear derivation chain for DISINCLINED (morphologically dis- + inclin(e) + -ed; the chain for the root word): 1. PIE *klei- "to lean, slope, incline, bend" (reconstructed root, with variant *klēy-; attested by Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, and Germanic descendants independently - one of the classic "lean" roots of Indo-European). 2. Latin clīnāre "to bend, incline, slope" (first-conjugation verb, Classical Latin). 3. Latin inclīnāre "to cause to lean, to bend toward; to be disposed or inclined toward" (in- "into, toward" + clīnāre) - the verb in which the physical leaning begins to acquire a mental sense. 4. Latin inclīnātus (past participle "bent, inclined") and inclīnātiō (noun "a leaning, a tendency, a disposition"): the bridge from physical posture to mental tendency is already complete in Classical Latin. 5. Old French encliner "to bend, bow, incline" -> Anglo-French / Middle English inclinen "to bend, bow, be disposed toward" (English verb attested c. 1300). 6. English inclined (adjective and past participle, 16th c.) "disposed, willing, having a tendency toward." 7. English disinclined (adjective, 17th c., first attested in the 1640s): the negative prefix dis- (Latin dis-, "apart, in different directions, reversal of") + inclined = "leaning away, not disposed, unwilling" - DISINCLINED. Note: the root *klei- is among the best-attested of Indo-European roots, with Sanskrit śri- (श्रि) "to lean, resort to," Greek κλίνω (klī́nō) "to lean," Latin clīnāre, and Germanic hlin- (Old English hlinian "to lean," German lehnen) all descended from it independently; there is no serious debate about the chain. The English compound form is transparent: dis- (negative/reversive prefix, from Latin) + inclined (from inclīnen, from Old French encliner, from Latin inclīnāre). The un-prefixed inclined and the negative disinclined entered English within a century of each other and have been a symmetrical pair ever since.
