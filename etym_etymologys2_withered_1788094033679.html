PIE (c. 4000–3500 BCE)
*we- / *h₂weh₁-
"to blow"
(Watkins gives *we- 'to blow'; the
laryngeal form *h₂weh₁- 'to blow'
is the debated fuller reconstruction)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE–200 CE)
*wedra-
"wind, weather"
↓
Old English (8th c.)
weder
"air, sky, wind, weather, storm"
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
widren / witheren
"to dry up, shrivel"
(from wederen 'to expose to
the weather'; vowel e → i)
↓
Modern English (15th c.)
wither
"to dry up, decay, decline"
↓
Modern English (c. 1400, adjectival)
withered
"dried up, shrivelled"
(past participle used as adjective)
↓
Modern English
WITHERED

Note: the exact PIE etymon of the 'weather' word is debated — Pokorny groups it under a root '*wē-/*wə-' 'to blow', while a laryngeal reconstruction *h₂weh₁- is preferred by some; the Germanic form *wedra- and its Old English reflex weder are secure.
