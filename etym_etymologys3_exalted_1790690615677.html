Exalted is the past participle of exalt turned fully adjectival, and its elevation from “lifted physically high” to “noble in character, lofty in mood, sublime in rank” recapitulates two millennia of Roman, Christian, and Romantic height metaphors in which up means good.

The Latin base is altus “high, lofty” (also “deep” — height measured both ways, as in altum mare “deep sea”), from PIE *al- “to grow, nourish, become large” (source also of adult, altimeter, altitude, alderman via “grown, elder”). Prefixed with intensive-perfective ex- “up, out, thoroughly” (PIE *eghs), Late Latin exaltare meant “to raise high, elevate, extol” — physically lifting (exalt a throne), socially promoting (exalt to office), and rhetorically praising (exalt God’s name). The Vulgate made it ubiquitous: exaltavit humiles (“he hath exalted the humble,” Luke 1:52), exaltate Dominum (“exalt the Lord”). The participle exaltatus thus carried both literal and doxological weight.

Key stages:

  • Late Latin exaltare / exaltatus (2nd–6th c.): Christian elevation language. To be exalted is to be lifted by God or to lift God in praise — active and passive at once. Connotation is reverent-cosmic: heights are holy, lowliness is sinful humility awaiting lifting.
  • Old French exalter / Middle English exalten (13th–14th c.): borrowed as a prestige verb of feudal and devotional promotion. Chaucer and the Wycliffite Bible use exalten for raising kings and praising heaven: “exalted be thy name.” The adjective exalted appears as participial modifier (“an exalted throne”). Register is high-poetic, never colloquial.
  • Early Modern moralization (16th–17th c.): Shakespeare, Milton, and the King James Bible fix the moral sense: an exalted person, mind, or station is noble, eminent, raised above the common. Milton’s Satan aspires to an “exalted seat”; his God sits “high exalted.” Simultaneously, mystics speak of “exalted states” of rapture — elevation as emotion. The physical sense (exalted platform) persists but is overtaken by the ethical.
  • Enlightenment and Romantic sublimity (18th–19th c.): philosophers oppose the exalted (lofty, sublime, heroic) to the low, mean, or common. Burke’s Sublime, Wordsworth’s “exalted mood,” Shelley’s “exalted imagination” make exaltation an aesthetic psychology: to feel exalted is to be transported. Irony becomes available:exalted can tip into self-important (“exalted opinion of himself”) or overblown (“exalted language”). The source sentence — “On a less exalted level” — depends on this scale: there are higher (play, mystical participation) and lower (practical sense) levels of stylistic function.
  • Modern stratified use (20th–21st c.): three layers coexist. Formal: exalted office, rank, position (king, judge, office still “exalted”). Emotional: exalted mood (elated, euphoric, sometimes manic in psychiatry). Stylistic: exalted prose, diction (lofty, elevated, bordering on grandiose). Chemistry borrows it playfully (exalted state is not standard; excited is), while everyday speech uses it with gentle irony (“our exalted leaders”). Theological use remains (“the Exalted” as divine epithet in Islam and Christianity).

Phonetics reflects learned French mediation: Latin exaltare /eksalˈtaːre/ → Old French /egzalte/ (voicing of x intervocalically) → Middle English re-latinized to /eksˈɔːlt/ with restored /ks/, preserved in RP /ɪɡˈzɔːltɪd/ with voicing of x to /ɡz/ before a stressed vowel and the regular participial -ed /ɪd/. Spelling keeps x and lt as etymological flags, distinguishing it from native high, lofty.

Attestations anchor the climb: the Vulgate and King James (“every valley shall be exalted,” Isaiah 40:4); Shakespeare’s “exalted mistress” (Venus and Adonis); Milton’s “exalted power”; Wordsworth’s “exalted and sublime”; and modern headlines (“exalted to the peerage”). Each preserves the vertical metaphor: worth is height. To speak of a “less exalted level” is therefore to step down a cosmic ladder without leaving it.
