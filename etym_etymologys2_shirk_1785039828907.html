Earliest stage to present SHIRK (origin debated; no secure PIE root): - Early Modern German Schurke "scoundrel, rogue, knave, villain" (16th c.) — the proposed source; its own origin is uncertain, possibly connected to Middle High German schürgen "to poke, stir" (modern German schüren "to stir up, poke"), but that link is debated. The older theory linking the word to PIE *sker- "to cut" is now rejected. - English shirk (verb) "to go evasively, to slink or sneak away" (c. 1580s) — the earliest English sense, probably shaped by association with English shark "to live by one's wits" (c. 1600) or borrowed from Schurke. - English shirk (noun) "a needy parasite, disreputable sponger" and (verb) "to practice fraud, to live by one's wits" (c. 1633). - English shirk (verb) "to evade one's work or duty" (1785, colloquial/slang). - English shirker (noun) "one who shirks" (1799). - Modern English SHIRK "to avoid or neglect a duty, responsibility, or labor out of laziness or fear." Note on the debate: no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be fixed, and even the immediate source is uncertain. The German Schurke derivation is the most widely accepted, but the word may instead be an independent English development influenced by shark; the two words long overlapped in the sense of swindling.
