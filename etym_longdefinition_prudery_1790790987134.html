Prudery is a noun denoting an excessive, affected, or hypocritical modesty, squeamishness, or moralistic distaste regarding sexual matters, physical nudity, bodily functions, or sensual pleasures. Originating in seventeenth-century French court culture as a satire of outward piety masking private corruption, the term has evolved into a key concept in sociology, psychoanalysis, and literary criticism to describe the defensive suppression of eroticism, bodily enjoyment, and artistic frankness.

The term encompasses several primary, psychological, and cultural dimensions:

  • Moral, Behavioral, and Social Senses: In its everyday social sense, prudery refers to behavior characterized by exaggerated propriety, primness, and an acute, often performative horror of anything perceived as sexually suggestive, immodest, or vulgar. A person exhibiting prudery is easily shocked or offended by frank discussions of human anatomy, romantic relationships, or natural bodily processes. Unlike genuine modesty—which is unpretentious and quiet—prudery is censorious, defensive, and ostentatious, frequently demanding that others conform to its hyper-sanitized standards.
  • Historical and Victorian Institutional Censorship: Historically, prudery became a defining cultural characteristic of nineteenth-century bourgeois Victorian society. During this era, institutional prudery led to widespread "bowdlerization" (the aggressive editing and expurgation of classical literature, Shakespeare, and biblical texts to remove passages deemed offensive to young women) and extreme linguistic euphemisms (such as referring to chicken legs as "dark meat" or piano legs as "limbs" to avoid uttering the word "leg").
  • Psychological and Psychoanalytic Dimensions: In psychoanalytic theory (established by Sigmund Freud and his successors), prudery is understood as a psychological defense mechanism—specifically a form of reaction formation. The individual's conscious mind develops an exaggerated, vocal abhorrence of sexuality precisely to repress, deny, and mask intense subconscious erotic desires and anxieties. In this view, prudery is not the absence of sexual interest, but its neurotic, hyper-focused inversion.
  • Literary, Aesthetic, and Critical Discourse: In literary theory and aesthetic criticism (such as the writings of Roland Barthes and Susan Sontag), prudery is used metaphorically to describe the intellectual squeamishness of critics who fear or dismiss the visceral, erotic, and ecstatic pleasures of literature in favor of sterile, dogmatic, or purely ideological analysis. "Ideological prudery" views artistic beauty and sensory enjoyment as frivolous or politically suspect, demanding that all creative writing submit to solemn didactic surveillance.

The semantic bridge connecting the seventeenth-century courtly pretender to the modern literary critic is the concept of anxious defensive denial. In every context, prudery acts as an artificial shield constructed to defend the ego against the disruptive, ecstatic, and uncontrollable power of sensual and aesthetic pleasure.
