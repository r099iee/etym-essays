HAWTHORN is a genuine native compound of two Old English elements, haga "hedge, enclosure" and þorn "thorn", each with its own separate Indo-European prehistory. Both chains are given in full below. CHAIN 1 — HAW (the hedge element): PIE *kagh- "to catch, seize, enclose" (c. 4th millennium BCE; status as a PIE root is debated — see note) → Proto-Germanic *hagaz "enclosure, hedge" (also *hagōn; c. 1st c. CE; cognates include Old Norse hagi "pasture", German Hag "hedged wood", Dutch haag "hedge") → Old English haga "hedge, enclosed plot of land, enclosure" (8th c. CE) → Middle English hawe "hedge; by extension the hedge-fruit" (12th–15th c. CE; the fruit-sense arises by metonymy, "the berry of the hedge") → English haw "the small red fruit of the hawthorn" (12th c. CE onward) (NB: this element continues inside the compound; the final node of the compound is bolded at the bottom.) CHAIN 2 — THORN: PIE *ster-n- "thorn, prickle" (Pokorny; c. 4th millennium BCE; the reconstruction is not universally accepted — see note) → Proto-Germanic *þurnuz "thorn, prickly plant" (c. 1st c. CE; cognates: Gothic þaúrnus "thorn", German Dorn, Old Norse þorn) → Old English þorn "thorn, thorn-bush, prickly plant" (8th c. CE; also the name of the rune ᚦ) → Middle English thorn "thorn" (12th–15th c. CE) → English thorn "thorn" (16th c. CE) COMPOUND (joined chain): Old English hagaþorn / hagaporn "hedge-thorn" (haga + þorn; 9th c. CE) → Middle English hawethorn "hawthorn" (12th–15th c. CE; hiatus between the two elements smoothed) → English hawthorn (15th c. CE onward) → HAWTHORN NOTE ON DEBATE: The PIE status of *kagh- is uncertain. Some scholars derive the Germanic *hagaz group (and Latin caulae "enclosures", Lithuanian žagaras "brushwood") from PIE *kagh- "to catch, enclose", but others treat it as a West/Central European root without a secure Indo-European pedigree; the question is unresolved and is stated here rather than guessed. The thorn word is likewise a reconstruction: Pokorny posits *ster-n-, but some modern scholars prefer a separate dental-initial root; the Germanic forms themselves are securely attested. The compound itself is uncontroversial: hagaporn is recorded in the 9th century, and the modern form hawthorn results from ordinary Middle English smoothing of the -a-/-e- hiatus, with the first element re-analysed against the fruit-word haw.
