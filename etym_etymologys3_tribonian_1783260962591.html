THE NAME OF THE CODIFIER: FROM THREE TRIBES TO ONE LAW BOOK The story of Tribonian is not the story of a common word at all but of a personal name that became a byword — an onomastic history, in which a Roman family name travelled through Latin, Greek, and modern historiography to become the emblem of legal codification. It is also a history with two entangled threads: the genuine philological derivation of the name, and the folk-etymological tradition that reads Trib- as "three." FROM PIE *treyes TO LATIN TRIBUS At the root of the name lies the Proto-Indo-European numeral *treyes "three," one of the most stable elements of the reconstructed vocabulary, preserved in Latin trēs, Greek tría, Sanskrit tráyaḥ, and English three. Latin formed from it the word tribus, traditionally analysed (de Vaan) as *tri-bh-u- "a third part," with the second element the PIE root *bhu- "to be, become" — hence "that which the people is divided into three." Ancient Roman tradition held that the earliest Roman people were divided into three tribes, the Ramnes, Tities, and Luceres, supposedly corresponding to the three ethnic elements of the founding population, and tribus originally designated one of these thirds. Whether the "three tribes" tradition is history or myth, the word tribus demonstrably meant "tribe, civil division of the Roman people," and it is the documented source of a whole family of Roman words: tribūnus "tribune" (an officer of a tribe), tribuō/tribuere "to assign, allot" (whence tribute and attribute), and the gentilic names formed on the stem. FROM TRIBUS TO TRIBONIUS TO TRIBONIANUS Roman naming used family names (gentilicia) built on common stems, often with the suffix -onius: from tribus came the attested gentilic Tribonius "man of the tribe, member of the Tribonian gens." Personal names proper were then formed from such gentilics by the adjectival patronymic suffix -ianus "belonging to, descended from" — the same formation that gives Julianus from Julius, Augustianus from Augustus. The jurist's Latin name Tribonianus therefore means, etymologically, "of the family of Tribonius," and the hellenized Tribōnianós (Τριβωνιανός) is simply that Latin name wearing Byzantine Greek morphology. The name itself is thus completely Latin in structure; the man who bore it was a Greek-speaking Pamphylian who rose to the summit of the imperial legal service. TRIBONIAN THE MAN: 500–542 CE Born at Side in Pamphylia around 500 CE, Tribonian rose under Justinian I (reigned 527–565) to become quaestor sacri palatii, "quaestor of the sacred palace," the empire's chief legal officer. In 528 he was already on the commission for the first Code; by 530 he headed the commission of sixteen lawyers who compiled the Digest, reading and excerpting some 1,500 books of classical jurisprudence into fifty ordered volumes in barely three years; in 533 the Institutes, a textbook for law students, appeared under his direction; and in 534 the revised Code was issued. His death in 542, possibly in the great plague, came as the project of the Novels continued around the shape he had given it. Posterity's name for the whole achievement — Corpus Juris Civilis — is modern, but the man who made it possible has been inseparable from it from the start. A DOUBLE REPUTATION AND THE FOLK ETYMOLOGY OF "THREE" Tribonian's reputation was double from the beginning. The Digest preserved classical law; Procopius, in the Secret History, accused its editor of avarice, of selling legal decisions, and of irreligion — charges that follow the name into Gibbon's Decline and Fall, where the historian numbers Tribonian among the marvels of Constantinople in the same breath as Belisarius and Simplicius, gravely related in "the rich and polite Constantinople." The card's mnemonic adds a third thread: because Trib- resembles tri- "three," and because the Corpus has three principal parts (Code, Digest, Institutes), the name is memorized as "the man of three law books." This is a folk etymology — historically the tripartite shape of the Corpus is a coincidence, and the "three" in Tribonian comes, if it comes at all, through tribus "a third part of the people," not through the Digest. But like the best folk etymologies it is pedagogically potent, and it has genuinely reinforced the identification of the man with the codification. THE NAME AS BYWORD From proper noun, Tribonian has drifted toward common noun and adjective. Legal historiography speaks of Tribonianism for the systematic reconciliation of contradictory sources — the editor's art of selecting and harmonizing the jurists — and the phrase "a Tribonian" can name any master compiler of law. The shift is a textbook case of eponymy, the same process by which sandwich, boycott, and silhouette became common words. Yet unlike those, Tribonian has never left the legal-historical register: it remains a name first and a type only second, its power coming from the concrete man who, in one crowded decade, saved a thousand years of Roman law for the West.
