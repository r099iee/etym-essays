Origin unknown; the word is an expressive (onomatopoeic / phonesthetic) formation of the English and Middle English vocabulary, so no Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed — the chain honestly begins at the first attested English forms. Middle English (c. 1400): shoggen "to shake, jolt, move with jerks" — an expressive verb of the sh- + short vowel + -g- pattern typical of verbs of abrupt motion; possibly related to Old English sceacan "to shake" (ancestor of modern shake). English dialect (16th c.): shog "to shake"; in the phrase "to shog along", "to move slowly on one's way". English (c. 1540): jog "to shake up and down, to nudge" — either an alteration of shoggen or a parallel expressive formation; first recorded in the mid-sixteenth century. English (16th c.): joggle — frequentative of jog, "to shake with small repeated motions". English (17th c.): jog along / jog off / jog on "to depart or proceed at an easy trot" — the verbs step sideways from shaking to locomotion; also the noun jogtrot "an easy, steady, monotonous trot" (Shakespeare, The Winter's Tale, c. 1610: "Jog on, jog on, the foot-path way"). English (17th c.): jogging — verbal noun and participle, "moving at an easy, bouncing trot", in the equestrian and pedestrian senses. English (19th c.): jogging — the sense of easy travelling continues; jogtrot is extended metaphorically to "any humdrum routine of life". English (1948): jogging — running slowly and steadily for exercise, a New Zealand development promoted by coach Arthur Lydiard and spread internationally by Bill Bowerman and W. E. Harris, Jogging (1966). Modern English (20th c. to present): jogging "moving at a steady, gentle, bouncing pace". NOTE ON DEBATE / UNKNOWN: The word has no known Indo-European etymon. It belongs to the expressive stratum of English, and the relationship between shoggen, shog, jog, jolt (16th c.), jounce (18th c.), and jostle is one of family resemblance rather than documented descent; the exact intermediate form between shoggen and jog is unattested, and the derivation of jog is inferred, not proven.
