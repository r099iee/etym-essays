- [Latin 'impedire': to put the feet in shackles or a snare; to entangle; a farmer's and soldier's word for tangling up walkers and baggage trains] - [Perception: wholly physical; the image is of feet caught in a fetter or net]
- [Late 16th-century English: 'impede' is borrowed directly from Latin 'impedire' for 'to entangle, catch by the feet,' almost immediately generalized to 'to hinder'] - [Perception: enters bookish and legal prose; the physical image still audible to educated readers]
- [17th–18th centuries: parliaments impede bills, wars impede trade, 'impediments' block marriages] - [Perception: settles into dignified formal registers; loses its rural and military flavor]
- [19th century: the word family spawns science — Heaviside coins 'impedance' (1886) for a circuit's opposition to current] - [Perception: the ancient foot-image circulates anew as an electrical metaphor; the family gains a high-tech branch]
- [20th century: 'impede the investigation,' 'impede progress,' 'impede diplomatic efforts' become press and administrative staples] - [Perception: the default verb for institutional obstruction — neutral, bureaucratic, slightly bloodless]
- [Today: still formal; ordinary speech prefers 'slow down,' 'hold up,' or 'get in the way'] - [Perception: 'impede' has an official, legal aroma; never rude, never warm, never slang]
