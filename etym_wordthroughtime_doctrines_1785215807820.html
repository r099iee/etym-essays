- Late medieval (14th c.) - Doctrine enters English from French/Latin as the general, neutral word for teaching and instruction, what a master imparts.
- Reformation era - the word hardens into institutional creed: the articles a church or school requires one to hold, still a neutral description of an established teaching rather than an opinion.
- 17th-19th centuries - doctrine becomes a term of art in law, politics, and scholarship; in Mill's century it denotes the asserted principles of an intellectual system, usable for careful, quasi-legal delimitation of who owns an idea.
- Modern use - expanded to the policy pronouncements of states and parties (the Truman Doctrine, the Bush Doctrine); in religion it keeps the sense of fixed official teaching, more neutral than dogma and more formal than mere theory.
