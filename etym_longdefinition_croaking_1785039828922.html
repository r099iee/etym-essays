**CROAKING — the low, harsh sound of the throat** At its plainest core, **croaking** (the present participle of the verb *croak*) means making a deep, harsh, guttural, grating sound in the throat — the low, hoarse call of a frog, raven, or crow, or any rough throat-noise made by a creature in distress or extremity. The word is imitative at its root: the initial hard /kr-/ and the open, gravelly vowel reproduce the rasp of a rough surface rubbing in the larynx, and the -ing ending keeps the action continuous and ongoing. Croaking is not a single cry but a sustained, repeated, grinding utterance. **Primary sense: the animal call** In its oldest and most concrete sense, croaking is the vocalisation of certain animals: frogs and toads croak at night, ravens and crows croak from tree and roof, and the corncrake and the rook have their own rasping croaks. The word is fixed in the amphibian register from the fifteenth century — a frog croaks — while retaining its older bird associations. It is a low, hoarse, rattling noise, markedly different from the clear note of most birdsong or the melodic call of a songbird. The croak is produced deep in the throat, as if the sound itself were being scraped out of the body. **The literary sense in *The Hobbit*** In the catalogue of pandemonium that erupts in the goblin cavern after the lights go out — "yells and yammering, **croaking**, jibbering and jabbering; howls, growls and curses; shrieking and skriking" — Tolkien builds a sound-portrait of panic in which each verb picks a distinct vocal register. Croaking sits among the lowest, most animalistic tones: the thick, rattling, frog-like noise of terror breaking in the throat, dehumanising the goblins into mere amphibian and birdlike noise-makers, their panic having passed beyond articulate speech into pure animal utterance. The deliberate register-mixing is the point: the yells and yammering are still human speech of a kind, but the croaking drops the mob below the threshold of language altogether. Tolkien's croaking goblins sound less like enemies than like a swamp of frightened, ill-omened beasts. **Polysemy and secondary modern meanings**
  • Of a human voice (hoarse/rasping): a person whose voice is low, hoarse, and grating — from a cold, from strain, from age, or from emotion — is said to croak. "He croaked out a reply." The semantic bridge is direct: the same rough throat-friction that produces the frog's call produces the human rasp.
  • Slang "to die" (croaked): from 1812 English slang, "to croak" means to die — from the sound of the death-rattle, the last rough breath leaving the throat. "He croaked last night." This is the darkest of the word's extensions, and it preserves the imitative core: dying is imagined as the body's final croak.
  • To complain / forebode evil (a croaker): because the raven was a bird of ill omen, "to croak" from the mid-15th century could mean "to complain, grumble, or prophesy evil"; a "croaker" (1630s) is a prophet of doom who croaks forebodings and complaints.
  • Of birds (ill-omen): the raven's croak has been read for centuries as a portent of evil, disaster, or death — the folkloric undertone that drives the figurative senses above.
  • Of mechanical or environmental noise: by extension, croaking can describe any low, harsh, grating, repeated sound — a rusty hinge, a strained machine, dry wood — anything that scrapes like a throat.
  • The noun "a croak": from 1560, back-formed, meaning "a low, hoarse, guttural sound" — "the croak of a frog," "a croak of protest."
**Historical bridge and semantic reasoning** Every sense of croaking flows from a single acoustic image: the low, harsh, grating sound of a rough throat. The word enters English as an imitative verb of the crow, raven, and crane (early 14th century), extends to frogs by c. 1400, and from that double animal anchor spreads outward in two directions — upward, into the figurative language of doom and complaint (via the raven's ill omen, mid-15th century), and downward, into the slang of death (via the death-rattle, 1812). The participle croaking in *The Hobbit* thus compresses five centuries of accumulated darkness — the bird of ill omen, the frog's harsh pulse, the foreboding croaker, and the death-rattle — into the goblins' panicked, inhuman throat-noises, the final descent of their mob into animal utterance. **Register and usage** Croaking is vivid, sensory, and faintly ominous. It is used of voices, of animals, and of strained mechanical things, and it carries an undertone of age, illness, or menace. It must be distinguished from *growling* (a continuous, menacing rumble from deep in the chest, more angry than pained), from *howling* (long, sustained, and mournful), from *shrieking* (high and piercing, the opposite register), from *yammering* (loud, monotonous, complaining but articulate talk), and from *gurgling* (a liquid, bubbling throat-sound — explicitly excluded by the shortDefinition, since gurgling runs with fluid while croaking is dry and grating).
