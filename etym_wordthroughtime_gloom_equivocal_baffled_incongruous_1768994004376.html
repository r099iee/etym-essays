gloom:
- 14th-15th c. - first recorded as a word for darkness and duskiness, of obscure origin; the earliest sense is plain and physical, a dim place or the gloaming of evening
- 16th-17th c. - the figurative transfer: gloom becomes melancholy, sullenness, and dejection; darkness of the world shades into darkness of the spirit, and the two senses begin to feed each other
- 18th-19th c. - the great literary age of the word: gothic fiction makes gloom an atmosphere - the deepening gloom of caves, forests, and ruined abbeys that presses on the spirits and seems to scowl
- Contemporary - gloom runs two settled lives: the physical gloom of shadow-filled places and the psychological gloom of depression and foreboding, now reinforced by the modern idiom 'gloom and doom'

equivocal:
- 17th c. - the word enters English meaning literally 'of equal voice': a word or act that calls equally in two directions and admits of two readings
- 18th-19th c. - it acquires a sharper moral charge in rhetoric, law, and religion: equivocation is the vice of the double-tongued, and an 'equivocal answer' is suspect, evasive, designed to keep two possibilities alive
- 20th c. - the word retreats into formal registers - diplomacy, law, science - where an 'equivocal result' is one that cannot be classified; its modern counterpart is the clarifying negation 'unequivocal'
- Contemporary - still the precise word for a deliberate or structural doubleness of meaning, and in this scene exactly right for a sound that is both metal and mouth at once

baffled:
- 16th c. - 'to baffle' is a public ritual of disgrace: to subject someone to ridicule, mockery, or infamy, a shaming of the offender before others
- 17th-18th c. - the sense turns from shame to frustration: to baffle a plan, a pursuit, or an enemy is to defeat and foil it, to check its progress
- 19th c. - the mind enters the picture: to baffle the understanding is to bewilder and confound it, and 'baffled' becomes the state of having one's comprehension defeated
- Contemporary - the verb of the unclassifiable: a baffling mystery actively dodges comprehension, and the ear that is baffled has met something that slaps away every attempt to name it

incongruous:
- 17th c. - borrowed into English meaning 'not fitting together': things incongruous are inconsistent, out of agreement with each other
- 18th c. - the word becomes an engine of comedy in aesthetic theory: the incongruous - the sudden jarring of mismatched things - is analysed as a principal source of laughter and the ridiculous
- 19th c. - it takes on an uncanny, sinister edge in gothic and horror writing: an incongruous detail unsettles precisely because it should not belong
- Contemporary - the standard formal word for the aesthetic mismatch, 'out of place' made precise: dry metal and wet mouths are incongruous, and their refusal to fall together is the source of dread
