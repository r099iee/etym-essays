The English word heathen (noun and adjective) is an ancient native Germanic formation that emerged during the conversion of Germanic tribes to Christianity in Late Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages. Its ultimate etymological root is the Proto-Indo-European noun *kaito- (or *ḱay-to-), meaning "forest, clearing, uncultivated open land, or heath" (which also yielded Celtic cognates such as Old Irish cill, Welsh coed, "wood, forest," and place names like Chetwode in England).

Proto-Germanic Formation and the Heath-Dweller Connection:

  • In Proto-Germanic, PIE *kaito- underwent regular sound shifts according to Grimm's Law (where the voiceless velar stop /k/ shifted to the voiceless velar fricative /x/ or /h/), yielding the noun *haiþiz ("heath, open uncultivated wasteland overgrown with shrubs").
  • From this topographic noun, Germanic speakers formed the adjectival derivation *haiþinaz by appending the adjectival/relational suffix *-inaz (cognate with Latin -inus and English -en). The literal original meaning of *haiþinaz was "belonging to the heath, dwelling on the open uncultivated moorland, wild, rustic."
  • This morphological formation is directly parallel to the Romance term pagan (from Latin paganus, "dweller in a pagus or rural village"). In both linguistic traditions, the Christian faith spread first and most rapidly through fortified Roman administrative cities and urban commercial hubs. The rural peasantry living out on the wild, isolated heaths and forests were the last to be reached by Christian missionaries, clinging steadfastly to their traditional polytheistic ancestral rites. Consequently, urban converts began applying the term for "rustic moorland dweller" as an ecclesiastical label for all non-Christians.

Gothic Attestation by Bishop Ulfilas (c. 370 CE):

  • The earliest written attestation of this Germanic word appears in the Gothic Bible translation created by Bishop Ulfilas in the fourth century CE. In Mark 7:26, translating the Greek phrase γυνὴ Ἑλληνίς (gynē Hellēnis, "a Greek/Gentile woman"), Ulfilas rendered the noun into Gothic as 𐌷𐌰𐌹𐌸𐌽𐍉 (haiþnō, "a heathen woman").
  • Scholars continue to discuss whether Ulfilas coined haiþnō as an intentional loan-translation (calque) of Latin paganus or Greek ethnikos, or whether he was adapting an already widespread Germanic colloquialism for wild, unbaptized outsiders living beyond the Roman frontier. In either case, the word quickly spread across all North and West Germanic dialects: Old High German heidan, Old Saxon hēthin, Old Frisian hēthin, Old Norse heiðinn, and Old English hǣðen.

Old English hǣðen and the Viking Age:

  • In Anglo-Saxon England, hǣðen was recorded extensively from the eighth century onward. In the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the chroniclers consistently referred to the invading Danish and Norwegian Vikings not primarily by ethnic labels, but as "þā hǣðenan" ("the heathens" or "the heathen men").
  • The clash between Anglo-Saxon Christian kingdoms and Viking invaders was framed as an existential holy war between the baptized church and the ferocious heathen hordes devoted to Odin and Thor. When King Alfred the Great defeated the Danish warlord Guthrum at the Battle of Edington (878 CE), the terms of peace famously required Guthrum to undergo Christian baptism, symbolically transforming him from a hǣðen into a Christian brother.

Middle English, Colonial Expansion, & Modern Reclamation:

  • In Middle English (c. 1150–1500), the word developed into hethen or hepen. During the Crusades and medieval chivalric literature, hethen was applied indiscriminately to Saracens, Turks, and any non-Christian foes encountered by European knights.
  • During the British imperial expansion of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Victorian missionary societies raised vast sums to convert "the heathen in distant lands," embedding the word in colonial paternalism.
  • In twentieth-century colloquial speech, the term softened into an informal, domestic descriptor for ill-mannered or messy individuals. In the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries, practitioners of reconstructed Germanic polytheism (Heathenry / Ásatrú) reclaimed Heathen as an authentic, positive self-identifier honoring their ancestral spiritual heritage.
