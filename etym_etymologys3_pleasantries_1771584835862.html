The biography of pleasantries is a story of smoothing — and the word itself is a smoothed thing. It begins with a root whose literal meaning is "flat," travels through "calm" and "pleasing," passes through the French theatre and the English salon, and ends as a small, polished social coin, exchanged at the doors of drawing-rooms before the real business of rank and obligation begins.

1. The root: the flat that pleases. The Indo-European root *plāk- means "to be flat, to be level, to spread out," and its cognates keep that literally: Greek πλάξ (pláx) is "a flat surface, a tablet," and Latin placenta, "a flat cake" — the word behind English placenta, the afterbirth so named for its flat, cake-like shape. Somewhere in the Italic branch the root took a remarkable turn. From "flat, level, spread out" Latin developed the idea of "smooth, calm," and from "calm" it reached "pleasing" — the logic being that what is smooth and level offers no resistance, and what offers no resistance calms, and what calms pleases. The verb plācēre, "to be pleasing, to be acceptable," is thus etymologically "to be smooth." The whole family shows the same path: placidus "calm, peaceful" gives English placid; plācāre "to soothe, to appease" gives English placate and implacable; complacēre "to be very pleasing" gives English complacent. The ancient image — a flat surface smoothed until it calms and pleases — is the deepest root of all these words, and of the pleasantry itself.

2. From Latin to French: the pleasing becomes the jest. Latin plācēre passed into the Romance languages as the verb "to please": Old French plaisir, source of English please (13th century), and its participle plaisant, "pleasing, agreeable," source of English pleasant (14th century); the noun pleasure (14th century) is the same verb nominalised. The decisive step for our word came when French made a verb from plaisant: plaisanter, "to joke, to jest," and from that verb the noun plaisanterie, "a jest, a witty or pleasant remark." The semantic bridge is worth noting: a jest is a remark designed to please — the pleasing has become, in the theatre and the salon, the witty. In sixteenth-century French, a plaisanterie is a piece of playfulness, a sally of wit; the word still belonged to the world of humour, not yet of mere politeness.

3. English borrowing and the two senses. English borrowed plaisanterie in the mid-seventeenth century as pleasantry, and for its first two centuries in English the word could mean a jest or witty remark — "a pleasantry" as "a piece of wit." This is the sense recorded by the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century lexicographers, and it survives in the language of the theatre and the essay. But alongside it, and increasingly dominant, grew the softer sense: a polite, agreeable, and inconsequential remark. The shift is part of the great eighteenth-century movement of the English vocabulary toward politeness — the same movement that refined pleased into a word of mild satisfaction and turned conversation itself into a polite art. By the nineteenth century the word had settled into its modern form: the plural pleasantries, fixed in the formula "to exchange pleasantries," names the scripted courtesies of social encounter.

4. The word and the novel of manners. The nineteenth-century novel of manners made the word one of its small instruments of social observation. "To exchange pleasantries" is exactly what characters do at the thresholds of the great houses — before business, before dinner, before obedience. The card's own sentence is a perfect specimen: Lady Wang exchanges a few pleasantries, and then, "obediently," rises and withdraws. The narrator notes the pleasantries as a genre of speech — scripted, unmemorable, socially indispensable — and the word's double life (a jest that pleases, a courtesy that smooths) allows the irony: the pleasantries are the oil that lets the gears of rank and obligation turn. In the very word, "pleasing" remembers its ancient meaning of "smoothing over": the polite remark is the flat, calm surface laid across the rough edges of hierarchy.

5. The modern word. In contemporary English, pleasantries is mildly formal and lightly ironic, used almost always in the plural. It names the phatic layer of speech — the remarks whose function is not information but the maintenance of social relation — and it is the standard word for the courtesies exchanged before and after business, diplomacy, and ceremony. The singular pleasantry survives for a single polite remark; the older "jest" sense is largely absorbed, though it glimmers in literary and historical usage. The word is a small museum of the whole history: the flat stone of the Indo-European root, the calm water of the Latin "to please," the witty sally of the French theatre, and the smooth courtesy of the modern drawing-room all lie packed inside it, and every exchange of pleasantries performs, in miniature, the ancient act of smoothing the world until it pleases.
