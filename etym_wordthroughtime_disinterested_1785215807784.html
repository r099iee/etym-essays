- 17th century - Enters English from French (désintéressé) meaning “not influenced by personal interest; impartial, unbiased”; a dignified moral term for judges, arbiters, and selfless motives.
- 18th–19th century - Reaches its classical height in moral philosophy and biography: disinterestedness is the virtue of acting without regard to self-advantage; Mill's “disinterested labours” at Avignon carry the full weight of noble selflessness.
- 20th century (controversial re-sense) - A new meaning spreads: “disinterested” increasingly used for “uninterested, bored, lacking curiosity”, provoking fierce usage-guide condemnation as a corruption.
- Contemporary - A living battlefield of semantic drift: careful writers reserve “disinterested” for impartial/selfless, while the “bored” sense is ubiquitous in informal speech.
