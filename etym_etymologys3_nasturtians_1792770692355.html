I. Indo-European Etymology and the Roman "Nose-Twister"

The etymological roots of nasturtian trace back through classical antiquity to the Proto-Indo-European lexical heritage. The compound term is formed from two distinct Indo-European bases: the root *nas- (denoting the nose or nasal organ, ancestor of Sanskrit nāsā, Old English nasu, and Latin nāsus) and the verbal root *torkʷ- or *terkʷ- (signifying "to turn, twist, bend, or wind," which yielded Sanskrit tarku, Greek atraktos, Old Church Slavonic traku, and Latin torquēre). In Proto-Italic, these elements combined into a descriptive nominal compound denoting a pungent physical irritant that causes the facial features to contort.

In Classical Latin, this compound crystallized as the neuter noun nāsturtium. The ancient Romans applied this name strictly to pungent Eurasian cresses, primarily the wild watercress (known today as Nasturtium officinale in the Brassicaceae family) and garden cress (Lepidium sativum). The prominent Roman encyclopedist Pliny the Elder (Naturalis Historia, Book XIX, Chapter 44) explicitly confirmed this etymological origin, remarking: "Nasturtium nomen accepit a narium tormento" ("Nasturtium received its name from the torment of the nostrils"). Similarly, the Roman polymath Marcus Terentius Varro (De Lingua Latina, V.107) recorded: "Nasturtium, quod nasum torqueat dicunt" ("They say it is called nasturtium because it twists the nose"). When chewed, the plant's glucosinolates release allyl isothiocyanate, an acrid volatile mustard oil that stimulates the trigeminal nerve and causes an instinctive, reflex wrinkling or twisting of the nose.

II. Spanish Exploration, Transatlantic Botanical Transfer, and Semantic Shift

For more than a millennium, nasturtium referred exclusively to wild European watercress. However, the Age of Discovery in the sixteenth century initiated a dramatic transatlantic botanical and semantic revolution. Following the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire in Peru, European naturalists encountered a flamboyant creeping vine cultivated by indigenous Andean peoples for its medicinal properties and peppery, edible foliage. Spanish physician and botanist Nicolás Monardes published the first detailed European description of the plant in his 1569 medical treatise Historia Medicinal, christening it Mastuerzo de Indias ("Indian Nasturtium").

When seeds were introduced to England via continental botanical exchanges in the 1580s and 1590s, master herbalist John Gerard cultivated them in his Holborn garden. In his famed Herball, or Generall Historie of Plantes (1597), Gerard described the plant under the title Nasturtium Indicum or "Indian Cresses," noting that while its flowers were splendid and shaped like little helmets with spurs, its leaves possessed the exact same bite and medicinal virtues as native European watercress. Thus occurred an extraordinary botanical metonymy: the ancient Latin name for a Eurasian aquatic crucifer was permanently transferred in common speech to a completely unrelated terrestrial trailing flower from South America belonging to the family Tropaeolaceae.

III. Linnaean Taxonomy and the Metaphor of the Martial Trophy

In the eighteenth century, Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus sought to rationalize scientific nomenclature in his monumental Species Plantarum (1753). Recognizing that the popular garden flower was entirely distinct from true watercress, Linnaeus established the separate botanical genus Tropaeolum, deriving the name from the classical Greek tropaion (τρόπαιον, "trophy of arms"). In ancient warfare, a trophy was erected upon a battlefield tree trunk displaying the captured shields and blood-stained helmets of defeated enemies.

  • Shield-Like Foliage: Linnaeus saw in the peltate, circular leaves of the nasturtium an unmistakable resemblance to round classical Greek bucklers (aspides).
  • Helmet-Like Blossoms: The fiery red and orange spurred petals reminded him of bronze helmets stained with the blood of combatants.

While formal botany adopted Tropaeolum majus for the plant and reserved Nasturtium for true watercress, vernacular English firmly refused to abandon the popular name, retaining nasturtium as the universal common identifier for the garden flower.

IV. Phonetic Naturalization: From Latinate -ium to Dialectal -ian

During the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, spoken British English underwent an organic phonetic assimilation in rural working-class dialects. Across the southern and midland shires of England, ordinary cottage gardeners and agricultural laborers unfamiliar with classical Latin grammar frequently substituted the familiar English adjectival and substantival suffix -ian (or -ion) for the foreign neuter termination -ium. This shift was facilitated by natural phonetic analogy with well-known garden and medicinal plants:

  • Gentian (from Latin gentiana) and valerian (from Latin valeriana) provided established models where the unstressed palatalized ending /-ʃən/ felt entirely native to English tongues.
  • Pronouncing the word as nasturtian avoided the stilted, academic, and hyper-formal sound of the Latin singular -ium and plural -iums, seamlessly blending into the melodic rhythm of rural English speech.
  • English dialect glossaries from Berkshire, Hampshire, Somerset, and Yorkshire in the 19th century consistently record nasturtian, sturtian, or stursion as standard cottage parlance.

V. Literary Attestation: Tolkien's Hobbiton and the Shire Aesthetic

The most famous and enduring literary appearance of the spelling nasturtians occurs in Chapter 1 of J.R.R. Tolkien's 1954 epic The Fellowship of the Ring. Describing the hillside garden of Bag End as preparations begin for Bilbo Baggins's monumental eleventy-first birthday feast, Tolkien writes: "The flowers glowed red and golden: snapdragons and sunflowers, and nasturtians trailing all over the turf walls and peeping in at the round windows."

As the Rawlinson and Bosworth Professor of Anglo-Saxon at the University of Oxford, Tolkien possessed an unparalleled understanding of English dialectology and philological history. His choice of nasturtians was neither an error nor a whimsical eccentricity; it was a deliberate stylistic triumph. By using the unpretentious country-garden variant nasturtians, Tolkien instantly signaled the cultural identity of the Hobbits: an agrarian, contented, provincial folk whose language preserves the soft, melodic contours of pre-industrial English rural life, uncorrupted by modern pedantry or urban standardization.
