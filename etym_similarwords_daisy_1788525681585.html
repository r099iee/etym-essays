• marguerite: Similar because both refer to white-rayed composite flowers of the Asteraceae family; differs because marguerite is borrowed directly from French, typically denotes larger shrubby species (such as Argyranthemum frutescens or Leucanthemum vulgare), and carries a more formal horticultural register.
  • buttercup: Similar because both are humble, widespread wild meadow flowers of European folklore and pastoral poetry; differs because buttercup belongs to the entirely distinct family Ranunculaceae (genus Ranunculus), possesses cup-shaped glossy yellow petals, and contains blistering toxic ranunculin.
  • primrose: Similar because both are heralds of early spring associated with innocence and rustic meadows; differs because primrose belongs to the family Primulaceae (Primula vulgaris), has a pale yellow five-lobed corolla, and grows primarily in woodlands and hedgerows.
  • aster: Similar because both belong to the composite family Asteraceae with prominent central discs and spreading rays; differs because aster features starry multi-coloured petals (purple, blue, pink, lilac), blooms primarily in late summer and autumn, and takes its name directly from the Greek word for star.
  • dandelion: Similar because both are ubiquitous lawn composites with bright yellow floral pigmentation; differs because dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) consists entirely of yellow ligulate florets without white ray petals, has a hollow milky stem, and features sharply jagged leaves.
  • blossom: Similar because both denote flowering botanical reproductive structures; differs because blossom is an expansive generic noun referring especially to the flowers of fruit trees and woody shrubs rather than a specific herbaceous species.
  • humdinger: Similar because both were used in nineteenth- and twentieth-century colloquial slang to denote a first-rate, admirable, or remarkable specimen; differs because humdinger has no botanical association and carries a much more forceful, informal American slang register.
  • corker: Similar because both function as colloquial slang praise for something exceptionally good or striking; differs because corker derives from the sealing of bottles (implying a final, decisive point) and possesses an abrasive, emphatic colloquial tone.
