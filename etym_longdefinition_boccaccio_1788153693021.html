Boccaccio (Italian Boccaccio, /bəʊˈkætʃiəʊ/ in British English) is above all the surname of Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375), the Italian writer, humanist and pioneer of the Renaissance. Born in Certaldo (or Florence), the illegitimate son of a Florentine merchant, Boccaccio spent his youth in Naples, trained first for commerce and then for canon law — both against his bent — and found his way instead to letters, the classics and the vernacular. His fame rests on the Decameron (c. 1348–1353), a frame-collection of one hundred prose tales told by ten young Florentines sheltering from the Black Death, a book that gave Europe the modern short story and the modern comic realism.

Primary sense — the author. Used alone, ‘Boccaccio’ means the man and his oeuvre: the early Neapolitan romances (Filostrato, Teseida, Filocolo), the pastoral Ameto and the psychological Fiammetta, the great Decameron, the humanist mythography of the Genealogia deorum gentilium (a fifteen-book encyclopedia of pagan mythology that became the Renaissance’s standard reference on the ancients), the Latin eclogues and epistles, the Vita di Dante and Trattatello in laude di Dante, and the public lectures on Dante he was invited to deliver in Florence in 1373–4. His friendship with Petrarch from 1350 onward anchored his turn from romance to humanism.

Secondary senses and extensions.

  • The surname as family name: Boccaccio (variants Boccacci, Boccaccini) remains a living Tuscan/Italian surname, and ‘Boccaccio’ in documents usually means the poet unless specified.
  • Boccaccian literature: the adjective ‘Boccaccian’ and the Italian boccaccesco designate earthy, witty, sexually frank frame-tale storytelling: a ‘Boccaccian plot’ is a comedy of adultery, trickery and fortune.
  • Cultural metonymy: ‘a Boccaccio’ in criticism means the Decameron world — plague, wit, and narrative as survival. The name appears in titles of films (Pasolini’s The Decameron), restaurants and literary prizes (the Premio Boccaccio at Certaldo).
  • Genre history: in comparative literature, ‘Boccaccio’ marks the bridge from the Old French fabliau to the Italian novella — the very borrowing Pater’s text cites (‘Boccaccio borrowed the outlines of his stories from the old French fabliaux’).

Semantic bridge. All the derived senses run from one biographical fact: the notary’s-and-merchant’s son who wrote the Decameron. The book made the name synonymous with the comic tale and with vernacular eloquence; the humanists made it one of the ‘three crowns’ (Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio); the Counter-Reformation made it a byword for licentiousness that had to be expurgated; Romantic and modern critics made it a badge of Renaissance vitality. Surname → author → genre → epithet: the man’s name drained into the literary commonwealth he founded.

Register and tone. In scholarship the word is neutral and honorific; in ordinary speech it suggests amusing, slightly scandalous narrative; in Italian idiom boccaccesco is decidedly colloquial and knowing. The name never became a common noun in English (unlike its Italian derivatives), but it is a pinning adjective — ‘Boccaccian’ — to every witty tale-collection from Chaucer onward.
