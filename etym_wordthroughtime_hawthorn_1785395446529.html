- [Old English: hagaþorn] - The compound 'hedge-thorn' names the plant by its function as living fencing; already the archetypal boundary shrub of the English countryside.
- [Middle English: hawethorn / haw] - 'Haw' (the fruit, from Old English haga, 'hedge/enclosure') attaches to the thorn, the small red berry eaten by children and used as famine food; name and boundary lore merge.
- [Tudor to Victorian: May blossom & folk-belief] - Its white blossom — 'May' — turns the tree into the emblem of spring, May Day, and village festivity; folklore surrounds it with fairy and love associations, while its dense thorns still do the practical work of hedging fields and stock.
- [20th century: hedgerow nostalgia] - The hawthorn is canonised as the symbol of the English lane in May — whitethorn blossom, boundary hedges, villages — the stock plant of 'olde England' nostalgia.
- [Contemporary sense] - Genus Crataegus of the rose family: rustic, homely, and precisely local, the plant of farms, boundaries, and folk-belief rather than gardens; in Tolkien's passage its absent berries measure the party's starvation.
