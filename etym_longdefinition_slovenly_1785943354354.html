SLOVENLY: untidy, dirty, and careless in appearance, dress, habits, or work — the adjective that describes a person or thing neglected, sloppy, and lacking in neatness, order, or care. It is the adjectival form of the noun sloven, "a careless, untidy person" (also, historically, "a slattern, a base fellow, a rascal"), and it carries strong moral and aesthetic disapproval: a slovenly person is not merely momentarily messy but habitually negligent, letting things slide. In the source context it describes a manner of movement — to walk "in an awkward, unsteady, slovenly manner", a gait that is itself unkempt, slack, and not properly held together.

Core senses and current polysemy:
  • Appearance and dress (the core sense): a slovenly appearance means unkempt, dirty, or badly neglected clothing — unwashed, wrinkled, stained garments worn without any care for how one looks; neglected grooming; hair uncombed; shoes down at heel. The word describes the visible neglect of the body and its covering.
  • Habits and conduct: slovenly habits are careless routines — leaving things wherever they fall, doing chores lazily and incompletely, failing to keep oneself or one's surroundings in order. The word shades here into habitual negligence, a settled way of doing things badly on purpose or through indifference.
  • Work and intellectual performance: slovenly work is sloppy, slipshod, careless execution — work done without attention to detail, accuracy, or finish. Slovenly thinking, slovenly scholarship, a slovenly argument describe reasoning that is loose, disorganised, unrigorous, and lazy. This extension from physical dirt to mental carelessness is the word's most interesting modern drift, and it is the sense most alive in academic and professional usage today.
  • Speech and writing: slovenly English, slovenly diction, a slovenly style — careless, imprecise, grammatically slipshod language, the verbal counterpart of slovenly dress.
  • Manner of movement (as in the source context): to walk in a slovenly manner is to walk with a slack, drooping, unsteady gait — the body itself untidy, not held upright, the step dragging or lurching, as if the whole person were neglecting to hold themselves together.
  • Historical senses: in earlier English the adjective could also describe a person of low or mean character (a "slovenly fellow"), and the adverb "slovenly" meant "in the manner of a sloven"; the noun sloven was also used of an idle, worthless, or lewd person, senses now obsolete or archaic.

The historical bridge: The noun sloven is first recorded in Middle English in the 15th century, meaning a base, careless, or filthy person, and is a borrowing from the continental West Germanic region (Middle Low German slōf and Middle Dutch slof, "careless, negligent"). The adjective slovenly, formed with the adjective suffix -ly, follows in the 16th century. From the very beginning the word describes character as much as appearance: a sloven is someone who neglects — clothes, house, duties, or thoughts — so the later extension to slovenly work and slovenly scholarship is natural, a movement from the person to the performance. The moral charge — disapproval of laziness and neglect — has been present throughout the word's recorded history, and it explains why "slovenly" is never neutral: it always names a failure of care.

Register: The word is ordinary but censorious. It is used of people, habits, houses, and work, always with disapproval, and it has no affectionate or neutral use. In modern English the literal sense is slightly dated (calling a woman "a sloven" sounds archaic), but the word remains common in the collocations "slovenly appearance", "slovenly work", "slovenly habits", and "slovenly thinking". In force it sits between "untidy" (mild, neutral) and "filthy" (extreme, physical): slovenly names the combination of disorder and neglect that reveals a careless character.
