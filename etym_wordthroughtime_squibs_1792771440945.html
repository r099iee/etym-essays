- [Early 16th c. Tudor England c. 1520s–1530s] — Coined of imitative origin mimicking the hissing, sputtering noise of igniting powder, designating a small paper cylinder or roll filled with gunpowder that hissed and exploded — [perception: exciting, noisy folk novelty used in street revels and Midsummer pageants].
- [Late 16th–17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean Literature] — Extended metaphorically into literary and political warfare (Thomas Nashe, Ben Jonson) to mean a sharp, witty, short-lived satire or caustic paragraph directed against a rival — [perception: sharp, stinging rhetorical weapon tossed into public debate].
- [18th c. Augustan Era & Common Law Landmark] — Central to 18th-century coffeehouse political journalism, and immortalized in British legal history in the seminal torts case Scott v. Shepherd (1773), the "Squib Case," which established legal causation and transferred intent when a tossed squib was batted between bystanders before exploding — [perception: prominent in legal jurisprudence and political pamphleteering].
- [19th c. Victorian Industrial Era] — Gave rise to the enduring idiom "damp squib" (first recorded in early Victorian journalism to describe any heavily promoted event, speech, or invention that ended in an anticlimactic fizzle); also adopted into mining engineering for small blasting fuses — [perception: supreme linguistic metaphor for disappointing failure and shattered hype].
- [20th–21st c. Modern Cinema, Fantasy & Contemporary Usage] — Adopted into Hollywood filmmaking terminology for miniature explosive charges used to simulate bullet hits on actors; celebrated in fantasy literature by Tolkien and J.K. Rowling (who used "Squib" for a non-magical person born to magical parents) — [perception: multifaceted technical term, popular fantasy trope, and enduring cultural idiom].
