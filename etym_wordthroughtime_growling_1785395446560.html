- 15th c. (earliest English, groulen) - first recorded of thunder and of the belly; a plain, neutral rumble with no menace attached.
- 16th c. - first applied to the threatening throat-sound of dogs and wolves; acquires its core register of threat, dominance, and territorial displeasure.
- 17th-19th c. - sense narrows toward the menacing canine growl; figurative use appears - 'to growl an answer', a surly, hostile way of speaking.
- Modern - extended to thunder, engines, and low music; still a deep, continuous, barely contained force, informal and earthy in register, the -ing form (growling) stressing sustained menace.
