The history of pitter is the history of a half-word that slowly earned a life of its own. Unlike most English nouns, it does not descend through the ordinary channels of Germanic inheritance or Romance borrowing; it was born inside the sound-symbolic machinery of echo-words and only gradually detached itself from its twin. Its story therefore belongs to a special corner of the language: the productive system by which English imitates the world in syllables.

The echoic base. English has long used the syllable pit to imitate a small, quick, light impact. The consonant p is a voiceless bilabial stop — a puff of air closed and released at the lips — which is inherently short, dry, and percussive; a short front vowel and a final t (another stop) cut the syllable off sharply, so pit sounds exactly like a tiny tap. By contrast pat uses the more open vowel a, which gives a fuller, slightly heavier and rounder tap. This i/a alternation is a classic template in English ablaut-reduplication, the pattern that pairs a high-front vowel with a low vowel to suggest repetition, alternation, or gradation of size — tick-tock, ding-dong, flip-flop, ping-pong, hip-hop. In every such pair the i-form is perceived as the smaller, quicker, higher member, and the a- or o-form as the larger, slower, deeper one. Pitter-patter obeys the same law: pitter is the small, fast half, patter the fuller half.

First attestation — inside pit-a-pat. The earliest recorded form of the family is not pitter but the adverbial pitapat (also pit-a-pat, pitapat), first attested in the 16th century, meaning “with a quick, trembling beat,” and used almost exclusively of the heart. In early modern usage a frightened or eager heart “went pit-a-pat,” a phrase that compresses the heart’s double beat into three light syllables. The -a- in pit-a-pat is a linking vowel or syllable of no meaning, whose function is purely rhythmic: it fills the gap between the two imitative elements and turns the sound into a metre. Writers of the 16th and 17th centuries exploited this rhythm extensively in lyric and drama, where the phrase became a stock figure for anxiety, love, and fear.

The crystallising of pitter-patter. In the late 17th century the pair began to take its modern shape. Pitter-patter — with the frequentative suffix -er on each half — first appears as the full reduplicative for rapid light tapping, applied to rain, small feet, and hearts alike. The suffix -er (the same frequentative seen in chatter, flicker, dawdle) marks repeated, continued action, so pitter-patter does not name one tap but a whole rapid succession, and its rhythm — pit-ter-pat-ter — is exactly the sound of quick feet or a quick pulse. From this point the two halves had separate potential lives. Patter, the fuller half, was already established as an independent word (itself with a separate homograph from paternoster, discussed on the PATTER card); pitter remained for a long time a semi-independent element, appearing mainly inside the pair and in such variants as pitty-pat, pit-a-pitter, and pitter as a verb meaning “to make light quick taps.”

Detachment and modern senses. From the 18th and 19th centuries onward, pitter developed a limited free-standing life as a noun — the lighter, quicker element of the double-beat, used of a racing heart, a light rain, or tiny feet. Its uses are always coloured by the pair: a heart that goes “all of a patter and a pitter” (as in Tolkien’s The Hobbit, 1937) is being described through the old nursery rhythm, and pitter brings with it the whole echoic world of smallness, tenderness, and quickness. In the 20th century the idiom “the pitter-patter of tiny feet” fixed the pair as the conventional sound of small children approaching — and, in comic use, of an expected baby — turning the two imitative words into a cultural shorthand for new life. Modern dictionaries treat pitter as a real word, but its meaning still leans on its twin; it rarely appears alone, and when it does it imports the entire pitter-patter rhythm.

Socio-cultural note. The word’s survival is a triumph of sound symbolism over economy. English could have done without pitter — tap and tick exist — but speakers value the precise acoustic gradation it provides: patter for rain, pitter for a bird’s heart, pit-a-pat for a lover’s pulse. This expressive niche is why echo-words resist change: they are grounded in the physical experience of sound and carry an emotional temperature (tenderness, anxiety, delight) that ordinary vocabulary cannot match. In Tolkien the choice is deliberate — the small, fluttering pitter makes fear childlike and touching, and the very oldness of the word lends the moment a fairy-tale warmth.

In summary:

  • 16th c.: pitapat / pit-a-pat “with a quick trembling beat,” of the heart — the family’s first recorded form.
  • Late 17th c.: pitter-patter established as the reduplicative for rain, feet, and pulses, with frequentative -er on each half.
  • 18th–19th c.: pitter begins limited free-standing use as the pair’s lighter, quicker member.
  • 20th c.: the idiom “the pitter-patter of tiny feet”; Tolkien’s “all of a patter and a pitter” in The Hobbit (1937).
  • Present: pitter lives in the pair’s shadow, always small, fast, and tender.
