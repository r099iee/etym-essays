"to speak, command, order"

Classical Arabic (c. 7th c. CE)

Arabic phrase (c. 9th–10th c. CE)
ʾamīr al-baḥr
"commander of the sea"
↓
Medieval Latin (Sicily) (11th–12th c.)
amiratus / amiralis
"naval commander, emir"
↓
Old French (12th c.)
amiral / admiral
"naval commander, Saracen chief"
↓
Middle English (early 13th c.)
amiral / admiral

↓
Modern English (16th c.–present)
admiral
"highest naval flag officer"

Note: The intrusive letter d entered the Romance and English forms through medieval scribal folk etymology, mistakenly associating the Arabic military title with the Latin verb admirari ("to wonder at, admire") and admirabilis ("admirable"). The terminal suffix -al is a remnant of the Arabic genitive proclitic article al- ("the") severed from compound titles such as ʾamīr al-baḥr ("commander of the sea") or ʾamīr al-umarāʾ ("emir of emirs").
