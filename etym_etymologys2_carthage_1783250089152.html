Carthage is a Semitic (Phoenician/Punic) name with no Proto-Indo-European ancestry. The chain runs from the city's own Punic name through Greek and Latin adaptation into English: 1. Punic/Phoenician *qart ‘city, town’ (attested as QRT in Punic and Phoenician inscriptions) + *ḥadašt ‘new’ (attested as ḤDST in Punic) → the compound Qart-ḥadašt ‘New City’ — the name the Carthaginians wrote for themselves (Punic inscriptions; the same ‘new town’ compound recurs for several Phoenician colonies across the Mediterranean). 2. Greek Καρχηδών (Karkhēdōn) ‘Carthage’, attested from the 5th century BC (Herodotus) — the Greeks adapted the Punic compound as a single place-name, losing the transparent ‘new city’ meaning in the process. 3. Latin Carthāgō (genitive Carthāginis) ‘Carthage’, attested from the 3rd century BC (Plautus; the historians of the Punic Wars) — the Romans Latinized the Greek form; the stem Carthāgin- underlies English Carthaginian. 4. English Carthage, attested from the 15th century, taken from Latin Carthāgō (with possible Old French mediation of the spelling) — used thereafter for the ancient city, its state, and (after the 19th-century excavations) the ruins near Tunis. 5. Carthage — the modern English proper noun. Debated points: the precise route of the spelling into English (directly from Latin vs. via Old French) is not documented with certainty, though both paths converge on Latin Carthāgō; the meaning ‘New City’ is secure, but the Greek rendering Καρχηδών is a foreign adaptation of a foreign compound and preserves no Punic morphology. The name has no PIE root: the earliest attested stratum is Punic/Phoenician (Semitic), and all Greek and Latin forms are loans from it.
