WEATHERCOCK is a compound noun — weather + cock — naming, in its literal sense, a weather vane in the shape of a cock (rooster), mounted on a vertical pivot so that it turns freely and points into the wind, showing the direction from which the wind blows. Traditionally it crowns church steeples, barns, guildhalls and old civic buildings; a fixed pointer or arrow is attached to its tail end, and the whole assembly swings around a stout spindle, often above compass letters. The cock form is so traditional that in ordinary speech "weathercock" and "weather vane" are treated as synonymous, though strictly the cock is only the most famous shape of vane.

  • Steeple and church sense. The gilded church-cock is attested from the early Middle Ages — the famous bronze cock of Brescia survives from the 9th century — and medieval tradition linked the rooster to St Peter's denial and repentance (Luke 22:34, "before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice"), so that a cock on a tower reminded Christians to wake to faith; legend attributes the fashion to a decree of Pope Nicholas I in the 9th century, though the custom certainly predates any single decree. On English parish churches the weathercock became a standard fixture from the later Middle Ages onward.
  • Figurative sense — the fickle person. Because a weathercock swings with every shift of wind, the word has meant "an inconstant, changeable person; one who turns with prevailing opinion" since the 16th century: a "political weathercock", a man "as changeable as a weathercock". This is the sense Wilde's Town Councillor exploits when he declares the statue "as beautiful as a weathercock … only not quite so useful" — a sly joke about ornamental vanity: the gilded cock is beautiful but turns aimlessly, exactly like the councillors themselves.
  • Nautical jargon — weathercocking. In seamanship, "to weathercock" describes a moored boat or buoy swinging head to wind, and the tendency of an anchored vessel, seaplane or floating object to align itself with the wind and current; a sailing boat that "weathercocks into the wind" offers more resistance and is harder to hold on an anchor.
  • Aerospace jargon — weathercock stability. In aerodynamics, "weathercock (directional) stability" is the tendency of an aircraft to rotate about its vertical axis so that its nose aligns with the relative airflow, exactly as a farm vane aligns with the wind; the fin and rudder are sized to give the machine this stability, and the metaphor is borrowed consciously from the barn-vane.
  • Verb sense. "To weathercock" is also used of opinions, prices or policies that swing with events ("the market weathercocked on the news"), and, of a compass needle or pointer, to oscillate before settling.
  • Semantic bridge. All secondary senses descend from one observation: the object has no fixed orientation and obeys the wind. Humans and machines that mimic it — the turncoat, the trimmer, the directionally stable aircraft — share that same pivoting obedience; by the Elizabethan period the word had carried the censure "unfixed, unprincipled, time-serving" into political and moral speech, where it has remained ever since.

Register and current use. In modern English the compound feels slightly archaic and literary in its literal sense — "weathervane" is the plain word of the hardware shop — but the figurative sense is alive in journalism and political commentary, where "weathercock" still names the politician who bows to every prevailing wind. The word also survives in proverbial form ("as changeable as a weathercock") and in the technical vocabularies of sailing and flight.

In context. In Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888) the Councillor's remark — the statue is beautiful "as a weathercock … only not quite so useful" — contrasts the statue's ornamental brilliance with the humble work of a real weather instrument; the joke turns on the word's double life, since the statue, like a vane, is beautiful, gilded, and given to turning, while the councillors who praise it are themselves human weathercocks of opinion. The compound's transparent ingredients — weather and cock — make the jest immediately legible to every reader.
