Epistle is a noun denoting a formal, elegant, didactic, or literary letter addressed to an individual or a group. Far more elevated and stylized than an ordinary casual note, an epistle is typically composed with public or posterity readership in mind, intended to communicate moral instruction, philosophical argument, theological doctrine, or poetic reflection. In Christian church liturgy, the Epistle specifically designates the scripture reading from the apostolic letters of the New Testament that immediately precedes the Gospel reading during the celebration of Mass or the Divine Liturgy.

The term encompasses several primary, liturgical, and literary dimensions:

  • Biblical and Ecclesiastical Senses: In Christian theology and scriptural canon, the Epistles are the twenty-one apostolic letters included in the New Testament. These comprise the Pauline Epistles (fourteen letters attributed to the Apostle Paul, such as Romans, Corinthians, and Galatians) and the Catholic/General Epistles (written by James, Peter, John, and Jude). Written during the first century CE, these letters addressed specific pastoral crises, doctrinal controversies, and community organization across early Mediterranean churches, forming the foundational theological architecture of Christianity.
  • Liturgical Senses: In the liturgical calendar of Western (Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran) and Eastern (Orthodox) Christian churches, the Epistle refers to the designated scriptural lesson chanted or read from the Epistle side of the altar (the south side) during the Liturgy of the Word. In historical liturgy, a musical "prose" or "sequence" was frequently sung immediately following the Epistle (or Gradual) before the proclamation of the Gospel.
  • Classical and Enlightenment Literary Genres: In classical and European literature, the epistle developed into a polished poetic and prose genre. Renowned classical examples include the verse epistles of Horace (Epistulae, including the Ars Poetica), Ovid's Heroides, and Seneca's philosophical Epistulae Morales ad Lucilium. During the neoclassical Enlightenment, English poets such as Alexander Pope perfected the verse epistle (e.g., An Epistle to Dr. Arbuthnot, Epistles to Several Persons), using the letter format for trenchant philosophical disquisitions and sparkling satirical portraits.
  • The Epistolary Novel: In literary history, the eighteenth century witnessed the rise of the "epistolary novel"—a narrative constructed entirely through a sequence of letters exchanged among characters (such as Samuel Richardson's Pamela and Clarissa, and Pierre Choderlos de Laclos's Les Liaisons Dangereuses), allowing intimate psychological realism and multi-perspective drama.
  • Humorous and Arch Modern Usage: In modern conversational and journalistic English, epistle is frequently employed humorously, ironically, or archly to describe an unusually long, serious, or elaborate personal letter, email, or written communication (e.g., "He sent me a ten-page epistle outlining his complaints").

The semantic bridge uniting these senses is the Greek concept of epistolē—a dispatched message sent across distance to bridge a separation, bearing the author's voice, authority, and crafted thought to an intended audience.
