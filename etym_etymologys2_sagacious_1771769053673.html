Linear derivation chain for the single root word SAGACIOUS: Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European: *sāg- (also reconstructed *seh₂g-) 'to track down, to seek out, to detect by scent'. Reconstructed, not directly attested; the standard reconstruction (Pokorny, LIV) posits a hunting/sensory root. Modern handbooks caution that the exact shape and laryngeal profile are uncertain, and some scholars write it as *sag-. Stage 2 — Latin (archaic/early): sagīre 'to perceive keenly, to have sharp senses, to scent out'. The Latin verb is the direct continuation of the root's sensory core; it is attested chiefly in compounds and grammatical notices, with its simple form surviving into classical texts (e.g., in Cicero's philosophical vocabulary and in grammarians who gloss sagīre as 'to perceive sharply'). Stage 3 — Latin (1st c. BCE onward): sagāx (genitive sagācis) 'keen-scented, acute, shrewd, perceptive'. Formed on the stem of sagīre with the adjectival suffix -āx (denoting a disposition or tendency). Roman writers apply it first to hunting hounds and then by metaphor to judges, observers, and generals; Cicero and Varro use the transferred intellectual sense. Stage 4 — Latin (classical): sagācitās 'keenness of perception, shrewdness, sagacity'. Abstract noun built on the stem sagāc(i)- with the suffix -tās; the abstract sense 'sharp-sighted judgment' is fully established in classical Latin prose (e.g., in Cicero's rhetorical works). Stage 5 — French: sagacité 'sagacity' (16th c.), a learned borrowing from Latin sagācitās; this French form furnished one of the channels by which the family entered English. Stage 6 — English (16th c.): sagacity 'keenness of perception, shrewdness' — borrowed partly from French sagacité and partly directly from Latin sagācitās; first attested in English in the sixteenth century. Stage 7 — English (17th c.): sagacious 'having or showing keen discernment and good judgment' — formed in English by adding the suffix -ous (expressing 'full of, characterized by') to the Latin stem sagāc- (from sagāx, genitive sagācis), following the standard Renaissance pattern seen also in audacious, tenacious, and vivacious. First attested in English in the seventeenth century. Note on debated stages: (1) The PIE reconstruction *sāg-/*seh₂g- is secure in its broad shape but debated in detail — whether it carried a laryngeal, and whether it is identical with the root behind Latin sagīre as against alternative 'strike' roots, is discussed in the handbooks (Pokorny, LIV, de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin). (2) The comparison of Latin sagīre with Greek hēgéomai 'to lead, guide' is now widely rejected or treated as highly uncertain and is NOT part of this chain. (3) The identification of Germanic *sōkijaną 'to seek' (English seek, beseech; German suchen) with the same PIE root is widely accepted but not absolutely certain; it belongs to the root's wider family, not to the direct ancestor line of sagacious.
