• Crimson: Similar because both are rich, intensely saturated red colors; differs because crimson has distinct bluish, purplish, or violet undertones resembling venous blood, whereas scarlet has fiery orange undertones that give it a brighter, hotter, and more blazing appearance.
  • Vermilion: Similar because both describe vivid orange-red hues; differs because vermilion is historically and chemically a specific opaque mineral pigment made of mercuric sulfide (cinnabar), whereas scarlet is a broader descriptive color and textile term encompassing natural insect dyes and modern synthetic pigments.
  • Carmine: Similar because both originate from historical scale-insect dyes; differs because carmine is a deep, purplish-red pigment extracted specifically from cochineal (*Dactylopius coccus*), yielding a darker, more ruby-like tone compared to the radiant, orange-flared heat of scarlet.
  • Ruby: Similar because both convey luxurious, precious red hues; differs because ruby denotes the deep, gem-like, vitreous translucence of the corundum gemstone with subtle blue shadows, whereas scarlet is an opaque, overt, and blazingly bright exterior hue.
  • Maroon: Similar because both belong to the red color spectrum; differs because maroon is a dark, muted, brownish-red hue derived from the French word for chestnut (*marron*), lacking the luminous saturation and fiery brilliance that defines scarlet.
  • Cardinal: Similar because both describe vivid red shades associated with Roman Catholic ecclesiastical vestments; differs because cardinal red is slightly darker and more deeply crimson, whereas traditional cardinal cloth is often formally described in liturgical manuals as scarlet.
  • Cerise: Similar because both are bright, eye-catching shades of red; differs because cerise is a lighter, distinctly pinkish-red hue derived from the French word for cherry, whereas scarlet is far more fiery, assertive, and orange-tinted.
