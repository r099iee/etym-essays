Parole in its dominant modern sense is the conditional release of a prisoner from custody before the full term of the sentence has been served, granted on the prisoner's pledged word of honour to live lawfully, report regularly, and submit to supervision until the sentence officially expires.

In the source sentence the word is used with a dry figurative humour: after some telling remark, the man seemed to regard himself as on parole, and dried up — that is, he behaved like a prisoner let out on licence, suddenly guarded, watchful, and silent, as though one more loose word would send him straight back inside.

Core meaning in detail: Legal parole is not pardon and not freedom: the sentence continues to run while the parolee lives outside the walls under conditions — keeping a residence, holding work, avoiding certain company, meeting a parole officer, sometimes wearing a tag or observing a curfew. Breach of any condition revokes the licence and returns the person to prison. Parole boards weigh remorse, behaviour inside, risk to the public, and release plans; the decision balances mercy and rehabilitation against safety, and the word therefore carries both hope and a leash.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Conditional early release from prison (primary modern sense, from 19th c.): release on licence under supervision, revocable on breach. This is the sense every modern reader reaches for first.
  • A prisoner's pledged word of honour (historical, from 17th c. military usage): the original parole was literally a promise — a captured officer gave his parole, his spoken word, not to escape or to return to fighting, and was trusted on it. The bridge is direct: the modern licence still rests on a pledged word.
  • Release of prisoners of war on promise (military, 17th–19th c.): as in Napoleonic and American Civil War practice, where thousands lived at large on parole. The bridge extends the honour-promise from the individual officer to whole systems of captivity.
  • Speech as opposed to system in linguistics (technical, from Saussure 1916): parole is the individual act of speaking, set against langue, the shared system of language. The bridge runs through the French sense of parole as spoken word: what a prisoner pledges with the mouth becomes, in theory, what every speaker performs with the mouth.
  • Figurative guarded conduct (literary and conversational): to be or feel on parole is to act as though watched and one slip from punishment — the source sentence's wry use. The bridge is metaphorical: social caution feels like supervised liberty.
  • Any promise or assurance given (archaic/literary): earlier English used parole and its twin parol for a mere spoken pledge, especially in law where oral testimony counted. The bridge is the French root itself, which simply meant word.

Register and connotation: parole is formal and institutional in legal use — parole board, parole hearing, parole violation — yet faintly romantic in its older military sense, where a gentleman's word was his bond. The figurative sense is gently comic, suggesting a person tiptoeing through conversation as carefully as a licensee past a police station.
