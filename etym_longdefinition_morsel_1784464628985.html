MORSEL (n.) — a small piece, bite, or mouthful of food; a little fragment of anything. At its centre morsel names the portion small enough for a single bite, often with a hint of choiceness — the carefully selected piece one savours rather than the leavings one eats hurriedly. Primary Sense — A small bite or piece of food
  • Definition: a small piece or quantity of food, especially a delicacy or a portion chosen to be enjoyed; literally 'a little bite'.
  • Core examples: 'a morsel of bread', 'the last morsel of chocolate', 'an after-supper morsel'.
  • Context: morsel emphasises both size (small, one bite) and quality (choice, appetizing, often pleasing); it is not primarily a word for scraps.
Secondary and extended senses
  • Figurative: a small piece of anything — 'a morsel of news', 'a morsel of truth', 'a morsel of comfort' — where the word transfers the image of a bite-sized portion to knowledge, gossip, or comfort taken in small, satisfying doses.
  • 'A choice morsel' / 'a tasty morsel': a person or item presented as especially attractive or appetizing, often in social or romantic contexts; also a juicy piece of gossip.
  • 'Not a morsel': an idiom meaning 'not the least bit of food', i.e. nothing at all ('I have not eaten a morsel all day').
  • Culinary compounds and set uses: morsel appears in recipes and menus for small serves; the word also occurs in religious and moral texts as 'a morsel of bread' shared in charity.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge
  • MORSEL is a Latin-derived diminutive of the idea of biting: from Latin mordēre 'to bite' came morsus 'a bite' (both the action and what the teeth take), and from that a diminutive *morsellum, literally 'a little bite'.
  • The word's whole meaning is contained in that diminutive: a morsel is by etymology and by sense the portion a single bite can take — small, manageable, and inherently satisfying.
  • The figurative extension from food to information (a 'morsel of news') rests on a simple metaphor: knowledge, like food, is consumed in small appetizing portions; the news that makes one bite-sized titbit is a 'tasty morsel'.
  • Distinct from scrap: scrap implies leftover waste or discarded remnants; morsel implies something chosen and appetizing. Distinct from crumb: crumb is a tiny broken fragment, especially of bread; morsel is a whole small bite. Distinct from snack: snack is a modern, informal word for any light meal; morsel is older, slightly bookish, and emphasises smallness and choiceness.
Register and usage notes
  • MORSEL has a gentle, literary, almost old-fashioned flavour; it is common in narrative prose, food writing, and idioms, less common in everyday speech.
  • Countability: fully countable ('a morsel', 'two morsels'); the idiom 'not a morsel' is fixed and uncountable in feel.
  • Related words in the same Latin family: mordant (biting, caustic), mordacious, remorse (literally a 'biting back' of the conscience), and the technical morsus in scientific Latin.
  • In Tolkien's The Hobbit, Bilbo's 'after-supper morsel' is exactly this inherited sense: a small, carefully baked seed-cake, the hobbit's quiet planned bite of pleasure — a word whose diminutive history matches the hobbit scale of appetite.
