Tattered is a participial adjective — and the past tense and past participle of the verb tatter "to tear into tatters" — meaning "torn into ragged pieces, hanging in shreds, worn to ruin": a tattered coat, a tattered flag, tattered pages. It descends from the noun tatter "a torn scrap of cloth" (Middle English tater, from Old Norse tǫturr "rag, torn piece"), and its image is always of cloth or paper that has been pulled apart into loose, hanging fragments rather than merely soiled, thinned or smoothed. In the source passage — "wrapped in an old bundle of tattered oddments that he used as a pillow" — the word is doing exactly this descriptive work: Bilbo Baggins sleeps with the Arkenstone wrapped in a bundle of torn, assorted rags, and the poverty of the pillow throws the fabulous preciousness of the stone into sharp comic relief.

Primary senses.

  • Of cloth and clothing: rent into tatters, ragged: a tattered shirt, tattered sails, tattered banners. The emphasis is on visible tearing and hanging shreds — the result of years of wear, hard service or battle.
  • Of paper and books: tattered pages, a tattered manuscript — leaves torn at the edges or into strips; the word is standard in bibliography for damaged pages.
  • Of persons: "a tattered beggar", "tattered soldiers" — badly, raggedly clothed. The historical frequency of "tattered soldiers" (in accounts of the Thirty Years' War, the American Revolutionary army and the American Civil War) made the word an emblem of exhausted armies marching on.

Secondary and figurative senses.

  • Figurative ruin: "tattered hopes", "tattered reputation", "tattered fortunes", "tattered nerves" — anything reduced to a broken, shredded condition. The bridge is the ancient image: as cloth loses its fabric and hangs in shreds, so hopes, reputations and plans lose their coherence and their dignity. The figurative use is recorded from the eighteenth century and is now extremely common in journalism.
  • The idiom "in tatters": "the plan lay in tatters" — total ruin, with the same image of being reduced to scraps.
  • Related nouns and derivatives: tatter (a single scrap), tatters (ragged clothing), and tatterdemalion ("a ragged, beggarly person", recorded from the early seventeenth century and still used playfully).

Boundaries and register. Tattered is stronger than threadbare (worn smooth but intact), frayed (worn at the edges only) or worn (mildly used); it is more specific than shabby (dowdy from neglect) and more advanced than torn (a single rent). Its connotation is ruin with pathos — things that have been through too much — and its register is vivid, literary and slightly affectionate: tattered things are damaged but often still loved, exactly as Bilbo's bundle of oddments is still his pillow.

Syntax, derivation and minor senses. Tattered works both ways grammatically: as a passive participle of the verb tatter (the coat was tattered by years of service) and as a pure adjective (a tattered flag) that can be graded and intensified (more tattered, most tattered). It also extends from textiles to anything sheet-like or assembled from fragile parts: tattered pages, tattered sails, tattered maps, tattered curtains and, in descriptions of decayed landscapes, tattered huts and a tattered corner of the city. The noun tatter remains current in the plural collectives “in tatters” and “tatters of cloud”, and rare derivatives such as the adverb tatteredly and the seventeenth-century tatterdemalion testify to the word's long association with beggarly ruin. In all these uses the semantic constant is the same: material that was once whole has been pulled into scraps, and the scraps still hang together — damaged, but not yet gone.
