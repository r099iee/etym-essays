reproachful
- 14th century — English takes the verb reprochen and noun reproche from Anglo-Norman: to upbraid, to blame; the sense is of pressing a fault home against someone.
- 16th century — the adjective "reproachful" is formed with the native suffix -ful ("full of reproach") and enters the vocabulary of blame.
- 17th–18th centuries — it settles on the look and the voice rather than the shout: the reproachful glance, the reproachful tone, wounded censure that names a fault and begs the offender to recognise it.
- 19th century — Victorian and Edwardian domestic fiction make it the stock word of disappointed love and sibling judgment; its register is literary and formal.
- Contemporary — slightly formal, replaced in everyday speech by "disappointed," "guilt-tripping," or "huffy," yet still precise for the glance that says "you have let me down" without a word of anger.
