The form mingled belongs to one of the oldest everyday verbs of Germanic speech, and its history is a textbook case of the "frequentative" reshaping of the lexicon. The ancestor is Old English mengan "to mix, blend" (Mercian mengian), a common verb attested across the Old English corpus — in psalm glosses, homilies and narrative prose of the ninth to eleventh centuries — most often in the prefixed forms gemengan and gemenged "mixed". The participle gemenged already carried the adjectival force that modern mingled retains: late Old English prose routinely describes "gemenged" earth, bread, wine and peoples, i.e. things stirred together into a composite state.

The Germanic background. Old English mengan continues Proto-Germanic *mangjaną "to mix, knead together", with a well-attested cognate set: Old Saxon mengian, Old High German mengen (surviving in Modern German mengen "to mix, mingle"), Old Frisian menga, Middle Low German mengen, and Old Norse menga. Modern German is instructive for an English audience because it keeps two rival "mix" verbs: mengen, the old inherited word, and mischen, from a different Indo-European root (*meik-). English went the same way in reverse: mingle (from mengan) was gradually outflanked by the French-derived mix (via mixte, from Latin miscēre), so that mingle survived as the poetic and dignified member of the pair.

The Indo-European question. Beyond Germanic the trail is real but debated. The traditional handbooks (Pokorny, Watkins) join Germanic *mang- to a PIE root *mag- "to knead, fashion", citing Greek mássein "to knead", magís "a kneaded mass, cake", Lithuanian minkyti "to knead" and Old Church Slavonic męsiti "to knead, mix". The fit is attractive because kneading is the archetypal mixing, and *mang- can be analysed as a nasal-suffixed variant of *mag-. But the Oxford English Dictionary judges the further origin of Germanic *mang- uncertain, and the equation should therefore be presented as probable rather than proven. A responsible chain runs: PIE *mag- "to knead" (probable) → Proto-Germanic *mangjaną → Old English mengan → Middle English mingen → minglen (c. 1400, with frequentative -le) → modern mingled.

Middle English and the frequentative -le. The English of the thirteenth century had mingen/mengen, which in the fourteenth century was reinforced as mynglen by the Germanic frequentative suffix -le — the same formative seen in gobble, wriggle and sparkle, which gives continual or repeated action. It was with this new form that mingle began its modern semantic career. Early Modern English then broadened the word from substances to society: Elizabethan drama is full of mingling armies, mingling households and mingled voices, and "to mingle with" a crowd or a court becomes a stock phrase by 1600.

The Authorized Version and the literary settlement, 1611–1900. The King James Bible gave mingle an unshakeable sacred register: Leviticus 19:19 forbids a field sown "with mingled seed" and a garment "mingled of linen and woollen"; Ezra 9:2 records that the holy seed "have mingled themselves with the people of those lands"; Psalm 106:35 sums up apostasy as "were mingled among the heathen". From that point the word carried, alongside its plain sense, a moral and poetic resonance: to be mingled is to be fused with what is other. The Romantic poets then canonised the synaesthetic mingling of light, sound and water as a figure of harmony, and Victorian prose made "with mingled feelings" a fixed idiom of polite self-analysis. By 1900 mingle had settled into the position it holds today: everyday speech prefers mix; mingle is the elevated, faintly archaic alternative, kept alive by literary narration.

Modern profile. J. R. R. Tolkien, whose The Hobbit supplies this card's context ("in your hoard is mingled much of the wealth of his halls and towns"), cultivates the participle deliberately as part of his antique, chronicle-like diction. In contemporary English mingled is: (1) a standard idiom with emotion nouns ("mingled feelings"); (2) a vivid literary adjective for physical mixtures; and (3) a semi-archaic verb of social circulation ("mingle with the guests") still fully alive in event planning. The sixteenth-century reduplication mingle-mangle "a confused mixture" survives as a curiosity. The word's career illustrates a general rule of English vocabulary: when a native word and a Romance loan compete (mingle vs. mix), the native word often retreats into the poetic register — and, sometimes, wins a long second life there.
