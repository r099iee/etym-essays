allays is the third-person singular present form of the verb allay — he/she/it allays — meaning to make something troublesome less intense: to calm fears, relieve pain, reduce suspicion, temper grief, or quench thirst in older use. To allay is not to eliminate or solve but to soothe and diminish pressure, as a balm allays irritation or reassurance allays anxiety. In the source sentence the practical-purpose view of behaviour allays our ontological insecurities: it flatters, pacifies, and quiets deep worries about who we are, rendering us complacently simpler than we really are.

The verb governs abstract nouns of disturbance — fears, anxieties, doubts, suspicions, concerns, worries, pain, grief, anger — and implies a gentle, often palliative action. Unlike cure or resolve, which promise removal of cause, allay promises mitigation of feeling; unlike suppress or repress, which imply force, allay implies easing. This makes it favoured in diplomatic, medical, and consolatory registers: governments allay public fears, doctors allay pain, explanations allay doubts.

  • Core emotive sense: to calm fear or anxiety — to allay apprehensions, to allay panic. The oldest continuously living sense, dominant since Middle English, and the one active in the source.
  • Alleviatory physical sense: to relieve pain, fever, inflammation, or hunger — to allay pain, allay thirst. Historically bridged from the emotive sense via the medieval humoral idea that both passions and bodily distempers need laying or cooling.
  • Epistemic sense: to reduce doubt or suspicion — to allay scepticism. A metaphorical extension where uncertainty is treated as an irritation to be soothed by evidence or assurance.
  • Archaic intensive senses (now rare): to lay low, subdue, or put down (enemies, tumults); to alloy or dilute (metals, liquids); to lay aside. These survive only in historical texts and explain early spelling overlap with alloy and allege, but modern use has narrowed to soothing-mitigation.
  • Grammatical behaviour: transitive verb requiring a direct object (allay something); regularly inflected allay/allays/allayed/allaying; frequently passivised (fears were allayed); collocates with adverbs partly, largely, temporarily, effectively, somewhat, signalling incomplete relief.
  • Stylistic nuance: formal-literary rather than colloquial; everyday speech prefers calm down, ease, soothe, reassure. Its presence elevates tone, suggesting measured, almost therapeutic authority — which is why polemicists use it ironically, as in the source, where flattery allays insecurity in a suspect, self-deceiving way.

Polysemy is thus narrow but instructive: from Old English alecgan, to lay down, the verb generalised from laying physical things down to laying passions to rest. The modern third-person allays inherits this entire soothing history in a single -s: the subject — a theory, a voice, a gesture — performs the laying-to-rest upon our fears. To say a view allays is to grant it power while questioning its honesty: relief may be real yet purchased by simplification.
