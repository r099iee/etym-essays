ells is the plural of ell, an old English unit of length used chiefly for cloth, standardised in England at 45 inches (1.143 metres, one and a quarter yards). In the source sentence a thousand ells denotes an enormously long fishing net, emphasising communal labour and scale. More generally, an ell is any of several related pre-metric forearm-based measures (English, Scottish, Flemish, French) that varied by country and century.

The core modern senses are:

  • Historical unit of length (principal sense): In England, 45 inches from the reign of Edward I onward; in Scotland, about 37.06–37.2 inches; elsewhere, local equivalents such as the Flemish ell and French aune. Used in sentences like three ells of linen. The plural ells is now almost entirely historical or literary.
  • Cloth-trade measure: Specifically the amount of woven fabric one ell long by the loom width. Laws required woollen cloth to be sold by the ell with sealed brass ell-wands for checking. Hence phrases such as ell-wand, ell-measure, and statutes against false ells.
  • Body-based measure and figurative extension: Originally the length of the forearm from elbow to fingertip (a cubit). This produced the proverb Give him an inch and he will take an ell, meaning a person granted a small concession will demand much more — the foundation of the modern give an inch, take a mile. Here ell functions as the much larger counterpart to inch.
  • Architectural survival — L-shaped extension (homograph): In American and British building usage, an ell (usually spelled ell or L) can also mean a wing or extension at right angles to the main building, named from the shape of the letter L. This is etymologically distinct (letter-name) but appears in dictionaries alongside the measure and must be distinguished to avoid confusion.

Domain-specific and literary nuances matter. In economic history, ells signal pre-industrial standardisation struggles: towns kept iron ells chained in market squares; weavers, drapers, and customs officers quarrelled over which ell applied. In Scottish history the Scottish ell (about 94 cm) remained legally distinct until the 19th century, so Burns and Scott use ell with national colour. In biblical and classical translation, cubit is preferred today, but 16th- and 17th-century writers often glossed cubit as ell to make it intelligible to cloth-buying readers. In the Polynesian passage, ells is chosen for exotic grandeur: a thousand English ells is over a kilometre, an intentionally staggering length that justifies a hundred men and a proto-state.

Polysemy bridges are transparent. The anatomical forearm gave the cubit; the cubit gave the commercial cloth unit; the commercial unit, because it was much longer than an inch or hand, gave the figurative sense of a large, greedy amount in proverbs; the letter-L sense arose independently by visual analogy and converged in spelling. Secondary meanings today are therefore:

  • Plural historical reference: almost always with numerals (a thousand ells, ten ells) to evoke scale.
  • Idiomatic component: fossilised in take an ell, where few speakers know the literal inches.
  • Architectural homonym: they built an ell onto the farmhouse, unrelated to measurement but listed under the same headword.

Grammatically ells is a regular count plural noun, taking numerals and adjectives (long ells, Scottish ells). It is archaic and formal; everyday modern English uses metres, yards, or feet. Register is literary-historical, often with a slightly romantic or antiquarian flavour. Misreading it as a typo for eels or hells is a common learner error; context (long, nets, cloth, measure) disambiguates.
