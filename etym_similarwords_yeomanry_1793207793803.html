• Militia: Similar because both are citizen-soldiers serving part-time at home; differs because militia is the general compulsory levy of all able men, while the yeomanry were select mounted volunteers of the farming class.
  • Volunteers: Similar because the yeomanry were the quintessential volunteers of the 1790s; differs because volunteers covers every willing amateur corps (rifles, artillery), while yeomanry means specifically the county horsemen.
  • Cavalry: Similar because the yeomanry cavalry fought — or drilled — on horseback like regulars; differs because cavalry is the professional mounted arm, while yeomanry were amateurs who farmed on weekdays.
  • Freeholders: Similar because yeomen were classically defined as freeholders of small estates; differs because freeholders is a dry legal term for tenure, while yeomanry glows with patriotic legend.
  • Peasantry: Similar because both name the farming multitude collectively (the -ry suffix does the same work); differs because peasantry suggests tenants and labourers at the bottom, while yeomanry claims independence and property.
  • Gentry: Similar because both are celebrated middling orders of old England; differs because the gentry are the armigerous families above (squires and gentlemen), while the yeomanry are the substantial farmers just below them.
  • Territorials: Similar because the yeomanry became part-time Territorial regiments in 1908; differs because territorials is the twentieth-century citizen-army at large, while yeomanry is its county-cavalry inheritance with older titles.
  • Reserves: Similar because both serve as backup military manpower; differs because reserves are ex-regulars recalled to the colours, while the yeomanry were civilians who drilled between harvests.
  • Home Guard: Similar because both were home-defence volunteers mustered in invasion scares; differs because the Home Guard were the Dad’s-Army infantry of 1940, while the yeomanry were the mounted squires’ corps of the 1790s.
