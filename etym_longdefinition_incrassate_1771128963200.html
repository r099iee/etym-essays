Incrassate is a learned verb meaning to make or become thick, dense, or viscous, and, as an adjective, thickened, swollen, or fleshy in form. It is a deliberately technical word, drawn straight from Latin and used almost exclusively in historical and specialised registers: the medicine of the humours, natural history, botany, and — when modern writers use it at all — a knowingly archaic or mock-scientific tone. In the reading context supplied with this card — 'When the balance is lost, the humours begin to ______ and lose their natural fluidity' — the word names the pathological thickening of a bodily fluid, the moment at which healthy, mobile, balanced humour turns sluggish and viscous, and the humoral economy of the body begins to fail. That context is precisely the word's natural habitat: incrassation was a technical event in the physiology of the age before modern science. The word deserves its reputation as the 'thickening' verb for connoisseurs. Unlike the everyday 'thicken,' it carries a Latinate, almost anatomical gravity; unlike 'coagulate' or 'congeal,' it does not insist on a sudden chemical or temperature-driven state change, but on a slow accumulation of density and substance. It suggests swelling fatness, a fluid growing heavy and slow, a leaf growing leathery — the body of the thing increasing at the expense of its ease of movement. The full polysemy of the word can be set out exhaustively:
  • Sense 1 — transitive verb, 'to make thick or dense': the active sense, 'to cause a liquid or fluid to become viscous or inspissated'; used in historical medicine, cookery, and technical prose.
  • Sense 2 — intransitive verb, 'to become thick, dense, or viscous': the sense required by the card's sentence, describing the spontaneous thickening of humours, juices, or fluids when balance is lost.
  • Sense 3 — adjective, 'thickened, swollen, fleshy' (botany and natural history): describing plant parts and organs that are thickened or succulent — the 'incrassate leaves' of a desert plant, for example, fleshy with stored water. The adjective sense is actually the most persistent in scientific use.
  • Sense 4 — humoral-medical event: within the theory of the four humours, incrassation was the pathological thickening of blood, phlegm, or bile that clogged the body and produced disease; 'incrassants' were remedies that thickened, 'incrassative' things that caused thickening.
  • Sense 5 — figurative and mock-scientific: occasionally pressed into rhetorical service, as when an argument or mood is said to 'incrassate,' i.e. grow dense, heavy, and clogged; in modern use the word is a flag of deliberate pedantry.
The semantic bridge is entirely physical. Latin crassus meant 'thick, dense, solid, fat,' and the intensive verb incrassāre meant 'to make thick' — the prefix in- here strengthening the action, not negating it (an important trap for English readers, who are used to in- as 'not'). English inherited the word as a technical borrowing in the 17th century, when scientific and medical writers ransacked Latin for precise vocabulary. Today it is archaic in the general sense — no one speaks of soup incrassating — but it remains alive in the adjective, in botanical description, and as a favourite word of writers who want a thickening with gravitas, a sense that something has grown not just thicker but heavier, duller, and more difficult to move through.
