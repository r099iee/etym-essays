From the orchard to the family tree. The biography of SCIONS is the story of a word that left the orchard and never looked back — or, rather, that left the orchard, kept the orchard alive inside its meaning, and used the orchard's image to dignify the heirs of great houses. At the beginning stands a Germanic word for a plant's newest growth. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic *kīþą / *kīþō, "sprout, shoot, germ," is attested across the Germanic dialects in its daughters: Old English cīþ, "sprout, germ, shoot," a word of the fields and the garden; Old High German kīdi, "sprout"; Middle Low German kīt; Middle Dutch kiede. Every one of these words is purely botanical. A cīþ is what pushes out of the seed and out of the branch — the fresh, living outgrowth of a plant, the part of the tree that carries the next season's growth. The deeper connection of this family to a proposed Indo-European root *ǵey-, "to sprout," has been suggested by some scholars, but the correspondence is not established with certainty, and the attested record of the family begins with the Germanic dialects themselves; the honest history starts there. Through Frankish into French. The route into English ran through the Franks. The Frankish Germanic dialect spoken by the rulers of Gaul carried a form *kīþō, "sprout, shoot," into the Gallo-Romance speech of the region, and Old French developed from it the noun cion (also sion), attested from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, meaning "a shoot, twig, or scion of a tree" — the cutting taken from one tree to be grafted onto another. In Old French the word was exclusively horticultural: it belonged to the vocabulary of the vineyard and the orchard, where the grower cuts a shoot from one vine or tree and grafts it onto a rootstock, so that the new plant carries the character of the parent it was cut from. This is the literal, practical sense the English borrowed, and for its first century or so in English the word stayed on the land. The graft becomes a metaphor. Middle English took the word in the fourteenth century as sioun (or cioun), "a shoot, twig, scion," and within the same century English writers discovered the metaphor that would remake the word's future. A scion grafted onto a rootstock carries the life of its parent tree into new ground; in the same way, a child carries the life of a family into the next generation. The figurative sense appears early in English and grows steadily through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries: a scion of a family, a scion of a race, a scion of a royal house — the young descendant grafted, as it were, onto the stock of the lineage. The metaphor was helped by the deep cultural standing of the family-as-tree image, in which a dynasty is a living trunk and its children are branches, shoots, and fruit. Applied first to princely and noble offspring — the natural outgrowth of a royal stock — the word acquired the dynastic warmth and botanical colouring it still carries today. The spelling grows an s. The English word was first written cion or sion, matching its French source. In the seventeenth century the now-universal form scion appears, with an intrusive s- before the c, and within a century or two this spelling had driven out its rivals. The reason for the added s- is not securely explained: it may be scribal or analogical, and it is not derived from Latin — the modern word is not a Latin borrowing but the old French/Germanic word wearing a new spelling. The word's pronunciation, however, never changed, and the horticultural and genealogical senses remained one word with one image. Two lives in the modern language. Modern English carries scion, and its plural scions, in two working senses. In horticulture and viticulture the word is plain technical vocabulary: a scion is the shoot or bud selected for grafting, "scion wood" is the material collected from a chosen parent plant, and the scion/rootstock pairing is the daily business of orchards and vineyards. In the genealogical sense the word is formal and literary: the scions of a banking empire, of an aristocratic house, of an old dynasty gather, negotiate, and succeed, and the word quietly performs its ancient trick of presenting the heir as a living shoot of the family tree. The card's sentence uses the plural in exactly this register: "the noble scions of the old dynasty" are not lawyers and claimants but living branches of the trunk, outgrowths of the ancient house, and the word dignifies them precisely by making them botanical — products of blood and growth rather than of statute. From the Germanic field-word for a sprout, through Frankish and Old French into the orchard speech of medieval England, and from the orchard into the council chamber, scion has never once lost the image of the living shoot; a scion is always, in both senses, the newest growth of an older stock.
  • Proto-Germanic (reconstructed): *kīþą / *kīþō "sprout, shoot, germ" — attested in Old English cīþ, Old High German kīdi, Middle Low German kīt.
  • Frankish (reconstructed): *kīþō "sprout," borrowed into Gallo-Romance.
  • Old French (12th–13th c.): cion / sion "shoot, twig, scion," purely horticultural.
  • Middle English (14th c.): sioun / cioun "a shoot for grafting"; the genealogical sense follows in the same century.
  • 17th century: the spelling scion, with intrusive s-, established.
  • Modern: horticultural and genealogical senses side by side; scions of a dynasty is the formal, lofty use seen in the card's sentence.
