The origin of the animal word is uncertain: no securely reconstructed Proto-Indo-European ancestor is attested for Proto-Germanic *rammaz, and older attempts to connect it with a PIE root meaning "to support, to be strong" (e.g. *rem-) are speculative and not accepted by modern handbooks. The chain below therefore records only the attested Germanic record, with the ultimate (pre-Germanic) stage stated explicitly as unknown. Chain for the animal word (the sole root word of this card): - Proto-Germanic *rammaz (a male sheep; masculine a-stem, with the geminate consonant *-mm-), reconstructed from its attested descendants and dated conventionally to the first half of the 1st millennium CE. No certain PIE etymon is attested; a frequently repeated link to PGmc *ramaz "strong" (cf. Old Norse ramr "strong, mighty", recorded 13th c. CE) is debated, and the ultimate source may be a pre-Germanic or substrate word. State: origin unknown. - Old English ramm / ram (male sheep), attested from before 725 CE (the Epinal Glossary), continuing PGmc *rammaz with the geminate later simplified. - Middle English ram (male sheep), attested from c. 1175 (Lambeth Homilies), with the inflected forms preserving the old stem. - Modern English RAM (the male sheep; the transferred siege-device sense attested from the 14th century; the verb "to ram" attested c. 1425; the naval-ram and hydraulic-ram senses later, from the 18th–19th centuries). Note on the battering sense: the siege "ram" is a native English transfer from the animal — the same image as Latin aries "ram, battering-ram" — not a separate borrowing. Note on the homophone: the computing acronym RAM (Random Access Memory, from the 1950s) is a 20th-century initialism, not a descendant of this chain; its identity with the sheep-word is a coincidence of sound.
