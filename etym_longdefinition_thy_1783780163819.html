thy (possessive adjective) — the archaic and poetic second-person singular possessive form corresponding to thou and thee, equivalent in function to modern "your" when addressed to a single person. It always modifies a following noun ("thy hand," "thy kingdom," "thy wayes"), in contrast with thine, which appears before vowel-initial words ("thine eyes") and as a standalone possessive pronoun ("it is thine"). Core grammatical senses. Thy is the weak or pre-consonantal possessive adjective of the old second-person singular paradigm: nominative thou, oblique thee, possessive thy/thine. The distribution of thy versus thine is a purely phonetic rule inherited from Middle English: before a consonant the final -n dropped (exactly as min became my), giving thy ("thy father," "thy will be done"); before a vowel or silent h the -n was kept ("thine eyes," "thine honour"); and in absolute, predicative position only thine is used ("it is thine"). Modern my/mine preserves the identical pattern, and the two pairs developed in lockstep. Register and social charge. Like thou and thee, thy was once the ordinary singular possessive of English, used for friend and stranger alike. With the rise of the "T/V" politeness system in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the you-forms (possessive your) took over the polite register, and thy became the possessive of intimacy, devotion, and solemnity — the natural voice of prayer, of address to God, and of personal address to kin, lovers, or inferiors. Today it survives chiefly in liturgical and biblical language, in poetry, and in deliberately archaizing prose. Polysemy and secondary senses.
  • Liturgical and biblical "thy": the King James Bible (1611) and the Lord's Prayer ("thy name," "thy kingdom," "thy will be done") keep thy alive in modern worship; it is the possessive of address to God, carrying reverence and intimacy at once.
  • Poetic "thy": from Shakespeare through Milton, Wordsworth, and the Romantics, thy is a dignified and metrically convenient possessive. Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) opens "I thence / Invoke thy aid to my adventrous Song," where thy belongs to the direct apostrophe of the heavenly Muse.
  • Fixed phrases and archaisms: "thy will be done," "thy father," "thy neighbour as thyself," and the reflex thyself preserve the form in quotation and allusion.
  • Dialectal survival: alongside thee, parts of northern England retain thy (often spelled thi) in ordinary regional speech, where it functions as the everyday possessive.
  • Dramatic apostrophe and parody: historical drama and fiction use thy to signal period flavour; comedic misuse of the archaic set (e.g., thy for thou or you) is a standard Shakespeare-parody device.
  • Quaker usage: Friends' "plain language" extended to the possessive: Quakers wrote thy in everyday correspondence as part of the egalitarian pronoun set.
Historical semantic bridge. The meaning of thy — "of thee," the possessive of the singular second person — never changed. What shifted was its social register: the pronoun set was driven from the default to the marked pole by the sixteenth-century generalization of you/your. The form itself also has a neat internal history: thy is the pre-consonantal phonetic variant of thine, and it settled into this split alongside my/mine during the Middle English period, before the whole set retreated into the sacred and poetic registers where it lives today.
