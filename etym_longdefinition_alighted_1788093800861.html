Alighted is the past tense and past participle of the verb alight, meaning "to come down from the air and settle, to land after a period of flight, or to get down from a horse, carriage or vehicle." In the reading it is the great raven Roäc who "alighted stiffly on the ground" before Thorin and his dwarves — a perfect instance of the core avian sense: a controlled descent from flight followed by settling. Alighted belongs to a deliberately literary and slightly formal register: writers choose it when they want an air of gravity, ceremony, or physical precision that the blunt modern word landed cannot supply.

Unlike many English verbs, alight has only a handful of genuine senses, but each has a distinct contour and history:

  • Primary sense — to descend and settle after flying: used of birds, insects, aircraft and (poetically) of supernatural beings. "A butterfly alighted on the rose"; "the seaplane alighted on the lake." The motion is deliberate, graceful and controlled — the opposite of crashing, tumbling or dropping. This is the sense at work in the card context, where the raven alights "stiffly": the adverb quietly combines decorum with the ungainliness of the great bird.
  • Secondary sense — to get down from a conveyance: "to alight from a horse, carriage, train or bus." Dominant from the 16th to the 19th century, this use survives mainly in formal notices (British railway announcements still tell passengers to "alight" at a station) and in period fiction. The semantic bridge is obvious: as with a bird settling, the person comes down from a higher position to the ground.
  • Tertiary and figurative senses — to alight on/upon: of the eyes, gaze, attention or thought, "to come to rest on" an object ("Her gaze alighted on the letter"); and, by further extension, "to happen upon or arrive at" something after searching ("he alighted on a solution"). The metaphor treats attention as a flying creature that flies about and finally settles — the same image as "light upon."
  • Register boundaries: in ordinary modern speech the verb is rare; speakers say land, get off, hop off, step down, touch down. Alight(ed) is felt as bookish, ceremonial or archaic-romantic — which is exactly why novelists use it to dignify a moment.
  • Variant form: the alternative past form alit (patterned on light – lit) has existed since the early modern period and is perfectly correct, though alighted is the standard written form today.
  • Essential warning — a homograph trap: English also has the adjective alight meaning "on fire, glowing" ("the windows were alight", "eyes alight with joy"). It is a totally different word in origin (from the old phrase on light, "in the light"), so "the barn was alight" has nothing to do with landing — while the past participle alighted is always the verb. Learners must not confuse alight the verb with alight the adjective.

Grammar and forms: alight is a weak verb conjugated alight – alighted/alit – alighted; alighted forms the perfect with have/has ("the bird has alighted") and appears in passive and participial constructions ("an alighted moth"). Note that alighted is pronounced with four syllables /əˈlaɪtɪd/, the full -ed syllable being pronounced because the stem ends in /t/.

Collocations to know: alight on (a branch, a roof, a surface); alight from (a horse, a carriage, a train); alight at (a station, a stop); alight upon (a discovery, a solution, a passage in a book). In aviation discourse "alight on the water" remains the standard phrase for emergency landings.

Why the sense set is the way it is: historically the verb's oldest meaning was "to dismount from a horse", developed from the idea of relieving the horse of its burden (see the etymology fields). The "settling after flight" sense grew out of the dismounting sense — a bird "dismounts" from the air onto a perch — and the figurative senses grew out of that in turn. Every meaning of alighted is thus a variation on one physical image: a body coming down from a higher place and coming to rest.
