In this sentence - an outline of a book on poetics - intertwined appears twice, and each time its meaning is precise. The author writes that an exposition of his theme demands "an initial examination of the partly distinct, partly intertwined theories" that have informed critical approaches to poetics; and then, as the bulk of the volume, "a detailed analysis in turn of rhythm and metre, themselves also partly distinct and partly intertwined". The word names a relation in which two things retain their individual identity yet are braided so closely together that they cannot be cleanly separated. The tone is analytical and measured: the author is describing a conceptual entanglement that is neither confusion nor fusion. Unlike "identical" or "merged", intertwining keeps each strand visible while insisting that together they form one fabric - the perfect image for a pair of concepts (theories; rhythm and metre) that are two things and one thing at once. The word is a compound of two old roots: the Latin prefix inter-, "between, among", and the Germanic noun and verb twine, "a thread twisted of two strands; to twist together". To intertwine is literally to twist one strand between and about another, and everything the word means today preserves that image of strands that remain distinct even as they become inseparable. PRIMARY SENSES OF INTERTWINED TODAY:
  • Physical twisting or weaving together (literal): cords, threads, ropes, yarns, vines, branches, roots and hair are intertwined when wound about one another so that each remains visible but cannot be separated without untying - "intertwined strands of rope", "intertwined branches".
  • Botanical growth: vines, creepers and root systems that grow round one another - the organic, wholesome image of living things "growing together".
  • Abstract interconnection of lives, fates, and destinies: "intertwined fates", "intertwined histories", "their lives became intertwined" - the most common modern sense, describing two or more people, families, or institutions bound together by events and relationships.
  • Intellectual and conceptual entanglement: the sense of the present passage - theories, arguments, concepts, disciplines or questions that are closely interlinked and partly interdependent, so that each must be understood with the other ("intertwined issues", "intertwined problems").
  • Narrative and literary structure: intertwined plots, storylines, themes, or motifs - multiple threads of a narrative braided together so that they run in parallel and cross at intervals.
  • Technical - genetics and molecular biology: intertwined strands of DNA (the double helix is the archetypal intertwined pair of strands), intertwined molecular chains, intertwining of filaments.
  • Technical - systems, software, and engineering: "intertwined codebases", "intertwined dependencies", "intertwined supply chains" - complex systems whose components are mutually dependent, so that a change in one propagates to the others.
  • In music and prosody (as in the passage): rhythm and metre are "partly distinct and partly intertwined" - two technical dimensions of verse that can be analysed separately yet are experienced together; the word is the natural term for such a relation of close, mutual dependence.
  • Psychological and emotional: intertwined feelings, intertwined relationships - emotions or attachments so interwoven that they cannot be cleanly separated in experience.
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE: The historical path runs from the weaver's craft to the highest abstractions. Old English twin literally meant "a double thread" - a thread twisted from two strands - and the verb twine meant "to twist". When inter-, "between", was prefixed in the mid-seventeenth century (the first recorded use of the verb intertwine is from 1640), the compound meant exactly "to twist between each other". From ropes and threads the image passed naturally to vines and roots, and then, by a steady metaphorical ascent, to anything that stands in the relation of strands: lives, fates, arguments, theories, DNA chains, codebases, plotlines. In every case the word insists on a delicate balance: the strands are distinct, and yet they cannot be pulled apart without damage to both. That is why "intertwined" is the gentlest and most organic of the words of combination: two vines growing about each other, still each itself, yet one cannot be moved without the other. Distinctions against near-synonyms: "interwoven" stresses the orderly, textile crossing of warp and weft; "interlaced" suggests a systematic, often decorative lacing of bands; "entangled" carries a negative tone of messy, restrictive tangling; "inextricably linked" emphasises the impossibility of separation; and "blended" or "merged" imply the loss of distinct identity. Intertwined alone keeps the image of visible, distinct strands that yet form one fabric.
