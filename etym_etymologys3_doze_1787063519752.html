Obscure beginnings. The history of doze is a study in obscurity: every authoritative etymological source labels its origin as uncertain or unknown, and the word is a textbook example of a common word whose beginning cannot be traced with confidence. The verb first appears in English around the middle of the 16th century (the 1540s–1560s) with the meaning "to be dull, sluggish, stupefied, or drowsy" — a mental state closer to "daze" than to sleep. Only in the early 17th century (the 1640s) does it acquire its modern sense of light sleep, and from that point its popularity grows steadily.

The Scandinavian theory. The most commonly proposed source is a Scandinavian family of drowsy verbs: Old Norse dúsa "to doze, slumber lightly", Norwegian dusa and døse "to doze, to make drowsy", Swedish dåsa "to doze", and Danish døse "to drowse, to make dull". On this theory English borrowed the word during its long contact with Norse speech, which by the 16th century was no longer new but still echoed in northern dialects; the word could have entered standard English from a regional form. The semantics are easy: a "dusy" head is exactly a dull, half-asleep head. The difficulty is that no Middle English instance of a direct ancestor is securely recorded, and the earliest English uses already show the "dull/stupefied" meaning that Scandinavian forms also carry, leaving the exact borrowing route unclear.

The native "daze" theory. An alternative tradition connects "doze" to the native Germanic family of "daze" and "dizzy": Old English dysig "foolish, stupid", Middle English dosen "to be dull, become stupid". The Middle English verb dosen is indeed attested before the modern "doze" (from the 15th century), meaning "to become dull, to grow drowsy", and it is a plausible missing link: if English doze descends from this native dosen, it would belong to the same old family as "daze" (from Old Norse dasa "to weary") and "dizzy" — all words for a stunned, dull, reeling head. Many scholars see a cross-current rather than a straight line: the native "dullness" word and the Scandinavian "sleep" word reinforced each other, so that modern "doze" may be a blend of both.

The "dose" confusion. A third factor complicates the history: the word "dose" (a quantity of medicine, from Greek dosis via Latin and French) was, in the 16th and 17th centuries, also a verb meaning "to give medicine to, to drug". A person dosed with medicine could be "dosed" into drowsiness, and the two near-homophones "dose" and "doze" (both /doʊz/) became thoroughly entangled: for two centuries writers used "dose" and "doze" almost interchangeably for stupefaction, and even the modern "doze off" was once written "dose off". The spelling eventually parted them — "dose" for medicine, "doze" for sleep — but the historical overlap is real and is one reason the word's origin is so hard to pin down.

Rise of the modern sense. From the 17th century onward, "doze" settled into its modern meaning of light sleep, and the 18th century gave it full noun citizenship: "a doze" became a short, light sleep, and the verb "to doze" the act of taking one. The Enlightenment and Victorian periods, with their new cult of productivity and punctuality, gave the word its familiar comic and censorious uses: servants and clerks caught "dozing" at work became figures of mild ridicule, while invalids and the elderly were allowed their "little doze" by the fire. In the 20th century the phrasal verb "doze off" — the sudden, involuntary slip into sleep — became the word's most frequent home, especially in the two great modern settings of accidental sleep: the classroom and the car.

The modern word. Today "doze" is a small, warm, well-loved word that perfectly describes its own meaning: it hovers, as its users do, between sleep and waking, between "dose", "daze", "dizzy", and "drowse", between the Viking and the Anglo-Saxon and the apothecary. No single origin can be proven, but the word's meaning has never been in doubt for long — to doze is to slip into that shallow, gentle sleep where the world goes soft and blurred, exactly the state Bilbo passes through on his way from nightmare to full waking in the source passage.
