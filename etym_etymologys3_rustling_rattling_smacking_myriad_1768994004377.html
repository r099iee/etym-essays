1. RUSTLING — the voice of dry friction. Rustling is a word born directly of the ear — an imitation of its own sound. Its ancestor, Old English hristlian (also hrustlian), meant "to make a soft, muffled, sibilant noise", and it belongs to a stratum of imitative verbs that English inherited from Germanic, where each language created its own rustle-word on the same acoustic template: German rauschen "to murmur, rustle, swish", Swedish rassla, Dutch ritselen. There is no reconstructible Indo-European root behind these words — they are sound-symbolism, the language of dry friction, and they differ subtly in every speech community, like regional accents of the wind in the leaves. English has used rustle longest for leaves, silk, paper, and reeds — the soft dry agitation of many small surfaces — and the verb also grew a second, famous life on the American frontier: to "rustle" cattle, to steal them, and a "rustler" for the thief, a sense probably born not of the sound but of the older dialectal "to hustle, to move briskly". In the cave, "a sharp rustling as of metal dragged over rock", the word does its classic work: it names a dry, abrasive, secretive sound, and its sibilants practically make the noise on the tongue; it can describe a gown or a page, and it slips easily into menace when the surfaces are metal and rock rather than leaves.

2. RATTLING — the percussive rat-tat-tat. Rattling, like rustle, is born of the ear — an imitative word built from the repetition of a percussive syllable, rat-tat-tat made word. Its oldest English ancestor, hrætel, was the name of a plant (rattlewort) whose seeds rattled in their pods; the verb ratelen followed, and by Middle English the whole family — noun, verb, adjective — was in place. The Germanic cousins agree on the shape: Middle Dutch ratelen, German rasseln "to rattle, clatter", all founded on the same acoustic gesture. From the plain "rapid series of short sharp sounds" the family proliferated: the baby's rattle (the toy, from the sixteenth century), the rattlesnake named for the warning percussion of its tail, the "death rattle" of the terminal breath, and the colloquial intensifier "a rattling good yarn". The word also migrated from things to persons and speech: to "rattle on" is to talk rapidly and endlessly, and to "rattle" someone is to discompose their nerves — the physical clatter becomes the figure for agitation, a metaphor that works in almost every European language. In the cave, the rattling adds a mechanical, lawless layer: chains, plates, or loose metal colliding, things not fastened down being dragged — the sound of disorder on the move. Its percussive phonetics make the word itself a small drumbeat, and the horror proceeds by the accumulation of such small beats.

3. SMACKING — the taste that became a sound. Smacking is the richest case, for it hides two separate etymologies that have merged in one form. The older, native strand is a taste-word: Old English smæc, "taste, flavour", from Proto-Germanic *smakkaz — the same root that gives German Geschmack "taste" — and in its oldest use a "smack" is what the mouth perceives: the flavour of food, and by extension a small trace or suggestion ("a smack of salt", "a smack of scandal"). Then, in the sixteenth century, a second, imitative smack arrived from the Low Countries — Middle Dutch smacken, "to strike the lips sharply" — a fresh sound-imitation naming the noise itself: the sharp, quick strike of lips in tasting or kissing. "To smack one's lips" became the audible sign of appetite and relish, and the two strands converged: a smack is both a flavour and a sound, and the physical act of tasting connects them. The family widened into the slap (a smack as a sharp blow) and the kiss (a loud smack of affection), always keeping its wet, percussive, strikingly physical quality. In the cave, "the smacking of myriad wet, enormous mouths" is the most uncanny word of the whole sentence because it is the most familiar: it takes the ordinary, comfortable sound of eating — of lips working with relish — and detaches it from any visible eater, turning appetite itself into the voice of the cave. The word that names our pleasure in food becomes the word of the monster feeding.

4. MYRIAD — from ten thousand to the numberless. Myriad is the odd one out: not a sound-word but a number-word, and its history is a history of counting. In ancient Greek, myríoi was the precise numeral "ten thousand", and myriás (μυριάς) named that number as a concrete thing — the full strength of a Greek regiment, a unit of account used in the mathematics of large sums. The origin of the numeral is unknown and possibly Pre-Greek; no secure Indo-European etymology is established, and honest scholarship leaves the deep source open. The word entered English in the sixteenth century through the classical tradition (Latin myrias, Greek myriás), and in early mathematical and astronomical texts it still meant literally "ten thousand". But even in Greek the numeral had begun to fray: myríoi also meant "numberless, countless", for a host of ten thousand was already beyond the power of the ordinary mind to imagine as individuals. English inherited both senses, and the hyperbole won: by the nineteenth and twentieth centuries myriad was the standard formal word for any number too great to count, working with equal ease as a noun ("a myriad of possibilities") and an adjective ("myriad ways", "myriad stars"). It is the numeral that outgrew arithmetic to become a figure of the sublime — the word for a scale that defeats enumeration. In the cave, "myriad wet, enormous mouths" deploys precisely that history: not one mouth, not ten thousand, but a count that cannot be made — a horror that can only be felt because it cannot be numbered.

  • rustling: Old English hristlian (8th–11th c.) → Middle English rustelen (14th c.) → rustle → rustling; imitative, no PIE root; 19th-c. "cattle rustling" is a separate homonymic sense.
  • rattling: Old English hrætel "rattlewort" (10th–11th c.) → Middle English ratelen (14th c.) → rattle → rattling; imitative, no PIE root; the family spreads to toy, snake, death-rattle, and nervousness.
  • smacking: (a) Old English smæc "taste" < PGmc *smakkaz (taste strand); (b) Middle Dutch smacken "to strike the lips" (16th c. sound strand); smacking merges both — flavour and lip-sound, appetite made audible.
  • myriad: Greek myríoi "ten thousand, numberless" (Homeric) → myriás "ten thousand" (5th c. BCE) → Latin myrias → English myriad (16th c.); numeral precision dissolves into hyperbole.
