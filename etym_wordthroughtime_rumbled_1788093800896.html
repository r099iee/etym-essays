- (late 1300s) - First means “made a low, heavy, continuous sound” — chiefly of thunder; an imitative word borrowed from Dutch and Low German that echoes the noise itself.
- (1400s–1600s) - Extends to vehicles and bodies: carts rumble over bridges; empty bellies rumble; bass voices rumble.
- (1500s–1600s cant) - In thieves’ cant, to rumble = to perceive, detect, understand; the sense fades, but leaves the seed of modern slang.
- (1700s–1800s) - The industrial age: trains, artillery, and factories rumble; also “moves with a rumbling noise” (the wagon rumbled past).
- (1900s–1950s US) - A rumble = a street gang fight; rumble senses proliferate in teenage slang, immortalised by West Side Story’s rumble.
- (1950s–) - British “you’ve been rumbled!” revives the old cant sense of being found out, in a cheerful mock-conspiratorial tone.
- (today) - Literal thunder/stomach/traffic sense undimmed; slang rumbled (exposed) is standard informal British; the word carries both a deep noise and a sly secret.
