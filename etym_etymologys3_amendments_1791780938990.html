The Latin verb emendare “to free from fault, correct” is built on ex- “out of” and menda, a noun for a blemish or fault attested in early comedy. The image is concrete: a fault is a stain or a flaw, and to emend is to take it out. Roman scholarship adopted the verb for the correction of texts, and emendatio became the standard term for what a good editor does to a corrupt copy.

  • menda and its doublet mendum belong to the vocabulary of bodily and material defect; the word is not common in high classical prose.
  • emendare is common in Cicero and in the grammatical tradition, where it covers the correction of speech, writing and character alike.
  • The Romance languages inherit the verb rather than the noun: Italian emendare, Spanish enmendar, French amender.

The Gallo-Romance stage

Old French amender shows the characteristic shift of prefix and vowel: Latin emendare appears with initial a-, a development usually explained by substitution of the Romance prefix descended from ad-, though a phonetic route through an earlier *esmendare has also been proposed. The detail is debated, but the outcome is not: by the twelfth century amender meant “to correct, to make better, to repair”, and its derivative amendement covered both reparation and moral improvement.

Medieval English: mending the soul

Middle English borrowed amendement around 1300 with a strongly religious flavour. To make amendment was to repent and reform one’s life; amendment of life is a fixed phrase in Tudor and Stuart devotion, and the penitential sense survives in the liturgy long after the legal sense has taken over. The same period saw the aphetic shortening mend, which quietly split off as the everyday verb for repairing clothes, roads and health.

From correction to legislation

  • Textual scholarship: humanist editors of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries spoke of emendations of manuscripts, and the English word followed suit for corrections to printed books.
  • Parliamentary procedure: as bills came to be drafted and debated clause by clause, a proposed correction to a clause became an amendment. The vocabulary of moving, carrying and rejecting amendments is fixed by the eighteenth century.
  • Constitutional entrenchment: the American founders gave the word its most solemn modern use. The Bill of Rights, ratified in 1791, consists of amendments to a constitution that was only a few years old, and “the First Amendment” has become a name in its own right, invoked in speech far beyond legal argument.

Modern extension

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word spread from statutes to every kind of instrument: contracts, patents, insurance policies, budgets, planning permissions, even soil. A soil amendment is material added to improve the ground, and the phrase shows how completely the original notion of removing a fault has generalized into “make better by adding or adjusting”.

Form and pronunciation

The English noun keeps the French stress on the second syllable: /əˈmend.mənt/, plural /əˈmend.mənts/, with a schwa in the first syllable and a compressed final cluster. The spelling amend- reflects the Old French a-, not the Latin e-; the etymological e survives only in the learned doublet emendation, which is why the two words look unrelated to many speakers although they are the same stock.

Connotation today

Amendments is a neutral, procedural word with a persistent undertone of improvement. Politicians exploit that undertone: a “technical amendment” sounds harmless and may be anything but, while a “friendly amendment” claims the authority of consensus. In ordinary prose the word remains the precise choice whenever a document is corrected item by item rather than rewritten.
