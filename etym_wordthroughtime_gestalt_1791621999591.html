- 8th to 11th c. — Old High German gistalt (past participle of gistellen "to place, set up"): referred concretely to the physical posture, station, or bodily standing of an individual.
- 12th to 16th c. — Middle High German gestalt: broadened into general German to mean outward shape, figure, appearance, physical stature, or aesthetic form in poetic and biblical contexts.
- Late 18th to 19th c. — Elevated in German Romanticism and morphology (notably by Goethe and Kant) to denote a living, dynamic morphological form whose parts exist in purposeful organic reciprocity.
- 1890s — Formally introduced into philosophical psychology by Christian von Ehrenfels (*Über Gestaltqualitäten*), exploring why melodies survive key transpositions.
- 1910s to 1930s — Adopted untranslated into English by academic psychologists to describe the Berlin school's experimental laws of perceptual grouping and holistic consciousness.
- 1950s to 1970s — Entered popular counterculture and clinical vocabulary via Fritz Perls's "Gestalt therapy", creating widespread public conflation with humanistic personal growth.
- Late 20th to 21st c. — Fully naturalized across English cognitive science, graphic arts, UI/UX design, and systems engineering as the indispensable term for an irreducible, holistic system.
