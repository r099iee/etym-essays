- Classical Antiquity (5th–4th c. BCE) - Greek *aphrodisiakos* meant "pertaining to Aphrodite, sexual love, or erotic pleasure"; used in natural philosophy and medicine (Hippocrates, Aristotle) for sexual reproduction and physical passion.
- Hellenistic & Roman Medicine (1st–2nd c. CE) - Integrated into Greco-Roman medical treatises (Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica*, Galen) to categorize specific pharmacological herbs, foods, and unguents believed to heat the blood and stimulate generative seed.
- 17th–18th Century - Entered Modern English medical and botanical lexicons (first recorded 1719) as a formal noun and adjective classifying therapeutic substances that excite venereal desire.
- 19th Century (Romantic & Decadent Eras) - Broadened beyond pharmacology into poetry, art criticism, and aestheticism; used metaphorically for exotic perfumes, intoxicating harmonies, decadent literature, and opulent visual art that arouses sensual indulgence.
- 20th Century - Co-opted by post-Freudian psychology, literary theory (Roland Barthes on textual pleasure, Susan Sontag), and popular culture to describe intellectual fascination, charismatic political power, and gastronomic luxury.
- 21st Century - Pervasive across gastronomy, perfumery, neurobiology, and marketing to denote both literal dopamine-releasing agents and any irresistible catalyst of intense aesthetic, intellectual, or romantic passion.
