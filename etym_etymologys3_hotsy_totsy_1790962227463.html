The Linguistic Explosion of the Jazz Age:
The decade following the First World War in the United States—the 1920s, commonly known as the Jazz Age or Roaring Twenties—witnessed an unprecedented explosion of playful, irreverent, and highly creative slang. Rapid urbanization, the rise of commercial radio, the nationwide popularity of syndicated newspaper comic strips, and the youth rebellion against Victorian sobriety fostered a linguistic environment that celebrated novelty, rhythmic assonance, and absurd rhyming compounds. During this era, expressions such as the bee's knees, the cat's pajamas, heebie-jeebies, and hotsy-totsy became cultural touchstones.

Billy DeBeck and the Comic Strip Incubator:
The specific coinage and nationwide viral dissemination of hotsy-totsy is widely credited to the American cartoonist William Morgan "Billy" DeBeck (1890–1942). In his enormously influential syndicated comic strip Barney Google and Snuffy Smith, DeBeck proved to be one of the most prolific slang inventors in American history (having also popularized heebie-jeebies, google eyes, and sweet mama). Around 1924, DeBeck introduced hotsy-totsy into Barney Google's dialogue as an exclamation of triumphant satisfaction whenever a scheme succeeded or when life was going swimmingly.

Morphological Mechanics: Rhyming Reduplication:
From a morphological perspective, hotsy-totsy was formed through rhyming reduplication based upon the colloquial adjective hot. Since the late 19th century, hot had expanded beyond thermal temperature to describe anything exciting, remarkably skillful, highly fashionable, or intensely appealing (as in "hot jazz" or "a hot idea"). By appending the hypocoristic/diminutive suffix -y to produce hotsy, and pairing it with the nonsense rhyming echo totsy (likely influenced by tootsy / tootsie, a contemporary term of endearment for a sweet young woman), speakers created a euphonious, self-contained jingle that was instantly memorable and satisfying to pronounce.

Transatlantic Migration and Literary Canonization:
The cultural reach of American mass media during the interwar years ensured that jazz slang quickly crossed the Atlantic to the British Isles. British authors, particularly P.G. Wodehouse, seized upon hotsy-totsy with great comedic relish. In novels such as Right Ho, Jeeves (1934), Bertie Wooster adopts the phrase to describe the delicate restoration of romantic harmony between quarreling lovers ("rendering everything hotsy-totsy once more"). Wodehouse exploited the humorous contrast between the venerable, tradition-bound setting of an English country estate and the frantic, modern American slang bubbling through Bertie's consciousness.

Semantic Bifurcation and Modern Cultural Resonance:
During the mid-20th century, the word experienced a subtle semantic bifurcation in American English. While it retained its primary meaning of "all right" or "in fine order," urban speakers and crime-fiction writers began employing it with sarcastic irony to mimic haughty, high-society affectation (blending conceptually with hoity-toity). If someone was acting "too hotsy-totsy," they were putting on airs. In the 21st century, hotsy-totsy endures as a beloved emblem of vintage Americana, celebrated in retro cocktail bars, period literature, and theatrical performance.
