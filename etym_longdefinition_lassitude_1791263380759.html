lassitude is a noun denoting a state of profound physical or mental weariness marked by listlessness, diminished energy, and disinclination to exertion. Unlike ordinary tiredness, which implies a healthy need for rest after effort, lassitude suggests heaviness, languor, and a slackening of drive that rest alone may not cure. It can describe the body, the mind, or a whole mood or culture.

Its core sense is medical and physiological: weakness, heaviness of limbs, faintness, and lack of vitality, often as a symptom of illness, heat, hunger, overexertion, or convalescence. Extended senses cover psychological apathy, boredom, ennui, and moral or political lethargy where a person or group lacks motivation, enthusiasm, or will to act.

  • Physical weariness and heaviness: The primary sense. A subjective feeling that the limbs are heavy, movements require effort, and energy is depleted. Example: the lassitude of influenza, tropical heat, or the afternoon after a heavy meal. Semantic bridge: directly from Latin lassitudo, faintness and weariness of soldiers and travellers.
  • Mental listlessness and inability to concentrate: A secondary sense where fatigue is cognitive: attention drifts, thinking feels slow, even simple decisions seem burdensome. Often collocates with mental lassitude. Bridge: metaphorical extension from body to mind, common in 18th-century nervous physiology which treated nerves as fibres that could tire like muscles.
  • Apathy, indifference, and lack of motivation: A further extension where lassitude means not merely being tired but not caring: voter lassitude, moral lassitude, spiritual lassitude. The person could act physically but lacks impulse. Bridge: from depletion to disinclination; prolonged weariness teaches the will to remain passive even when strength returns.
  • Languor as pleasant relaxation or satiety: In literary use lassitude can be positively tinged: the sweet lassitude after love, after a swim, after success, where exhaustion is enjoyable because desire is satisfied. The source sentence uses exactly this nuance: exhausted lassitude of satiety or peace, the slack calm after hunting or war. Bridge: when weariness coincides with fulfilment, its unpleasantness is reinterpreted as repose.
  • Clinical symptom versus ordinary fatigue: In medicine lassitude is distinguished from fatigue, lethargy, and somnolence. Fatigue is objective loss of force on repeated effort; lassitude is subjective lack of energy and initiative; lethargy implies drowsiness; malaise implies vague unwellness. Lassitude often heralds systemic illness such as anaemia, depression, hypothyroidism, chronic infection, or heat illness, and is recorded as lassitude, loss of appetite, loss of weight.
  • Social and political lethargy: Modern journalism uses lassitude for collective moods: economic lassitude, market lassitude, cultural lassitude, meaning stagnation, low dynamism, and complacency. Bridge: the body politic imagined as a body; when citizens are sated or disillusioned, public life droops like tired limbs.
  • Grammatical and collocational notes: Countable and uncountable. Usually singular and unmodified or with adjectives: general lassitude, overwhelming lassitude, creeping lassitude, post-viral lassitude. Verbs: feel lassitude, complain of lassitude, overcome lassitude, sink into lassitude, shake off lassitude. Register is formal, literary, and clinical; in conversation tiredness, exhaustion, or can't-be-bothered would be more natural. It does not mean laziness as a character trait, though moralists may use it to imply blameworthy slackness.

In short, lassitude names weariness as a condition rather than a moment: slackness of fibre and of will, where body and motivation droop together. To grasp its full range is to see how English uses a single Latin abstraction to link muscle, mind, mood, and even society under one image of loosened tension.
