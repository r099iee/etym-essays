PART ONE — A river named by light: the Indo-European root.

The name of the Elbe begins with a color. Most scholars trace it to the Proto-Indo-European root *albh-, "white, bright", the same root that produced Latin albus "white" (whence English alb, album, albino, and the Alps, "the white mountains") and Greek alphós, "white leprosy". The river's name would thus originally have meant "the white one" or "the bright river" — a reference, it is usually supposed, to the pale gleam of its water in a landscape of dark forests, or possibly to the white chalk cliffs and sandy reaches of its course. The Germanic tribes who gave the name to their river presumably used the word *albi- for a stream that shone white or bright against the surrounding gloom, just as other Indo-European peoples named rivers for their color: the Ebro (from a similar *albh- base) in Spain, the Aube and the Aubonne in France, and a host of European "white waters".

A rival minority theory cuts the color out entirely. It proposes a Proto-Germanic water-word *albiz, meaning simply "river", independent of any color root — a possibility entertained because Germanic river-naming uses many opaque water-words, and on this view the Elbe was just "the river" par excellence of its region. Neither theory can be proven from the attested record alone, and honest scholarship states the dilemma plainly; the chain of recorded forms, however, is secure whichever semantic value we assign to the root.

PART TWO — The Roman frontier: Albis and the failure of empire.

The first name of the river that survives in literature is the Latin Albis, recorded by Pliny the Elder (Natural History 4.100) and by Tacitus (Germania 41), who notes that the Albis rises among the Hermunduri and flows into the sea by many mouths. The Romans gave the river enormous strategic meaning. Augustus's stepson Drusus Germanicus campaigned along it in 12–9 BCE, and his premature death by accident (a fall from his horse, the sources say) cut short the plan to cross and subjugate the Germani beyond it. Tiberius later renewed the project and briefly stood with the army on the far bank of the Albis; the geographer Strabo and the historian Velleius Paterculus record the moment when the legions came within sight of the river. But the revolt of Arminius and the catastrophe of the Teutoburg Forest (9 CE) ended all further ambition, and Augustus's successors settled for the Rhine as the frontier. The Elbe thus became, in one of the great might-have-beens of ancient history, the line Rome reached, glimpsed, and never held.

In this classical register the river's Latin name Albis carries a specific historical charge: it is the easternmost limit of Roman civilization, the boundary beyond which Roman arms, Roman religion, and Roman administration never prevailed. Gibbon's usage on this card depends entirely on that Roman legacy. "The superstitions of the Elbe" are the pagan beliefs of the free Germanic world that Rome could not conquer — and the phrase sets the baptized, Romanized "German princes who reigned at Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna" against the still-pagan rulers of Wessex and Mercia, the Anglo-Saxon kings whom the followers of Ida and Cerdic brought across the sea.

PART THREE — From Latin Albis to German Elbe: sound shifts.

The change from the Roman Albis to the modern German Elbe is a model case of Germanic sound development. Within Germanic, the voiced aspirate *bh- of the PIE root *albh- lost its aspiration and became plain *b-; the form *albī (or *albi-) underlies the recorded Germanic names. When Germanic speakers and their Latin-writing neighbors rendered the name, the result was Albis in Latin (the -is a Latin nominative ending) and Elb-/Elf- in the Germanic vernaculars, where the initial vowel was leveled and raised (a > e under certain conditions) and the medial -b- later became -f- in some Old High German spellings (the name appears as both Elba and Elfa). Old High German Elba, Middle High German Elbe, and Modern German Elbe follow in an unbroken, regular line from the 9th-century attestations onward. English simply borrowed the modern German name, displacing the classical Albis that English geographers had used through the Renaissance.

The Slavic branch shows the same name going through a different sound system. The Czech name Labe is the Germanic *albī adapted to Slavic phonology: initial *a- became *o- and the name was fitted to a feminine noun, giving Old Czech Labe and modern Czech Labe. The two modern names, German Elbe and Czech Labe, are thus transparently the same word in two disguises — a living reminder that the river is a border water shared by two language families.

PART FOUR — The river in history and memory.

  • Antiquity: Albis, the frontier the Romans never crossed; Drusus and Tiberius reach it, Arminius bars it.
  • Middle Ages: the axis of German eastward expansion; Magdeburg and the Ottonian empire on its bank; the divide against the Elbe Slavs.
  • Modern commercial age: Hamburg on the lower Elbe grows into the great North Sea port; the river becomes a highway of Hanseatic and world trade.
  • World War II: Elbe Day, 25 April 1945 — Soviet and American soldiers meet at Torgau, the "Spirit of the Elbe".
  • Cold War: the Elbe at several points marks the Iron Curtain, renewing its ancient role as a frontier between East and West.
  • Today: a working navigable waterway of Germany and the Czech Republic, an emblem of European geography, and — in Gibbon's literary usage — the byword for the unconquered, still-pagan Germanic world beyond the Roman frontier.

The biography of the name Elbe is therefore the biography of a frontier: named for brightness in the deep Germanic past, engraved on Roman history as a line that failed, revived by Gibbon as a moral geography of paganism and civilization, and reborn in the twentieth century as a meeting place of East and West.
