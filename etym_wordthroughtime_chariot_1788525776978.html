- Bronze & Iron Age Antiquity (c. 2000–500 BCE) - The physical two-wheeled vehicle (Sumerian, Egyptian, Hittite, Mycenaean, Roman) revolutionized ancient warfare, hunting, and circus racing - Military supremacy and imperial glory.
- 14th Century (Middle English) - Borrowed via Old Northern French chariot from Late Latin carrus; used in biblical translations (Ezekiel's chariot, Elijah's chariot of fire) and Arthurian romances - Sacred visionary transport and knightly majesty.
- 16th–17th Century (Renaissance & Elizabethan) - Cemented in cosmological and mythological literature (Shakespeare, Milton, Spenser) as the celestial car of Apollo (the Sun) and Selene/Diana (the Moon) - High mythological grandeur and cosmic poetry.
- 18th–19th Century (Romantic & Victorian) - Celebrated in classical poetry and aesthetic prose (e.g. William Blake's 'Chariot of fire', Oscar Wilde's 'Sun in his chariot of gold') as an emblem of transcendent power, divine splendor, and visionary imagination - Sublime aesthetic and romantic metaphor.
- 20th–21st Century - Preserved in classical archaeology, museum collections, cultural allegories (e.g. 'Chariots of Fire'), and religious spirituals as an enduring icon of ancient combat, triumphant speed, and celestial transcendence - Iconic historical, athletic, and spiritual archetype.
