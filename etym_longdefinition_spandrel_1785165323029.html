Spandrel is a word of the builder's trade that has been transplanted, together with its geometry, into evolutionary biology. In its original architectural sense it denotes the roughly triangular space bounded by the outer curve of an arch and the horizontal member above it — the rectangular frame, cornice, or lintel beneath which the arch is fitted — or, between two adjacent arches, the tapering panel that descends from the crowns of the arches to the point where they spring from their piers. Because a round-headed arch must always be fitted beneath a rectangular bay, and must always spring from a vertical support, these triangular voids are not part of any deliberate design: they are the necessary geometrical leftovers of the craft. Given an arch and a rectangle, the spandrel must exist; the builder does not choose it, he inherits it, and then fills, carves, or decorates it. The term therefore covers a whole family of related spaces: the spandrel of a doorway, the spandrel of a window, the spandrel panel between the crowns of the vaults in a Gothic ceiling, the spandrel wall of a masonry bridge (the solid mass between the arch and the roadway above), the spandrel step of a staircase (the triangular rise beneath the tread), and, in modern reinforced-concrete construction, the spandrel beam that stiffens the outer wall across the tops of a row of openings. Everywhere the logic is identical: a void produced by the meeting of a curved opening and a rectangular frame, present by necessity, not by preference.

In the present context the word has been lifted out of masonry and set down in evolutionary theory. The speaker calls some human trait a spandrel, meaning that it is "largely considered an evolutionary side effect" — a feature that arose not because natural selection shaped it, but as an unavoidable byproduct of other, selected changes — "but one so useful that humans quickly turned it into a survival tool." This is precisely the sense introduced by the evolutionary biologists Stephen Jay Gould and Richard C. Lewontin in their famous 1979 paper "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm," in which they used the triangular spaces of the cathedral of San Marco in Venice as an image for any biological feature that exists because of structural constraints imposed by features that were themselves selected. A spandrel is a non-adaptive trait: it was never selected for its own sake, yet it is not an accident in the sense of being rare or random — it is a structural necessity of other adaptations. Crucially, as the passage underlines, a spandrel can later be co-opted: what begins as an incidental leftover can be seized upon and turned into something genuinely functional, a "survival tool." The word thus names an origin (byproduct of constraint), not a fate (such byproducts may go on to be adapted).

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:

  • Architecture (core sense, 15th century to today): the triangular space beside, above, or between arches; also the spandrel wall, spandrel step, and spandrel beam. This sense is strictly technical and professional, used by builders, surveyors, and architectural historians.
  • Evolutionary biology (metaphoric sense, 1979 onward): a phenotypic trait that arises as a byproduct of the structural or developmental constraints of other selected traits — the sense used in the passage.
  • Philosophy of science and cognitive science (extended sense, 1980s–1990s): following Gould's later writings (e.g., on the human chin, language, and "Darwinian fundamentalism"), the word is used for any incidental feature of a system that was not selected for, including cognitive or cultural byproducts.
  • Software and design (loose jargon, late 20th century): in a few technical registers, "spandrel" is used for an accidental capability or a constraint-generated space in a designed system, echoing the biological metaphor.
  • Bridge engineering (specialized sense): the spandrel is the area of a bridge between the arch and the deck, sometimes filled, sometimes left open.

The semantic bridge between the senses is the geometry of inevitability: in architecture the spandrel is what remains because arches and rectangles do not fit; in biology the spandrel is what remains because one adaptation imposes side-consequences on another. The word's value in both fields is that it separates "made for a purpose" from "merely there," while allowing that the merely-there can later be made useful. In the sentence given, the emphasis falls on the second clause — "one so useful that humans quickly turned it into a survival tool" — so the full meaning of the term in context is: an evolutionary byproduct of constraint, later co-opted for survival. The spelling is spandrel (with the older variants spandril, spaundrel); the register is technical but increasingly familiar through popular science.
