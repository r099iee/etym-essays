Proto-Germanic *hnaijaną "to neigh, utter the horse-cry" (an imitative / onomatopoeic formation built to mimic the aspirated, nasal, high onset of a horse's call; no secure pre-Germanic PIE lexical root is reconstructible in the same meaning) → Old English hnǣgan "to neigh" (attested from the Anglo-Saxon period; preserves the initial hn- complex — a voiceless nasal with h-colouring that later simplified) → Middle English neyen, neighen, nyghen, neien "to neigh" (12th–15th centuries; the h- is lost in spelling and speech) → Early Modern English neigh (the -eigh- spelling stabilises; 16th–17th centuries) → Modern English neigh /neɪ/ with present participle and gerund NEIGHING "the act or sound of a horse neighing." Note on the debated origin: Because the word is imitative, scholars treat it as sound-symbolic rather than as the reflex of an ordinary PIE lexical root. Sister Germanic languages solved the same communicative need with independent formations — Old Norse gneggja, German wiehern, Dutch hinniken — which underscores that no single inherited root need be assumed. Proto-Germanic *hnaijaną is itself reconstructed chiefly on the basis of the Old English and related West Germanic evidence.
