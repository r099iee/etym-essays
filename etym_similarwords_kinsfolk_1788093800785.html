• Kin: Similar because it names the same blood-tie; differs because "kin" is the bare relational noun (and legal term), mass-like and neutral, while "kinsfolk" is a warm, old-fashioned collective for the whole body of relatives.
  • Kinfolk: Similar because it is the same compound, same meaning; differs because it is the American respelling without the genitive -s- (recorded 1817, southern US dialect and literature) while "kinsfolk" is the standard British and literary form.
  • Kindred: Similar because it denotes the family group and also means "similar in nature"; differs because "kindred" has a broader abstract reach ("kindred spirits") and can be an adjective, whereas "kinsfolk" stays a people-word.
  • Relatives: Similar because it names the same people; differs because it is the plain, modern, administrative word — factual and flat, with none of the archaic warmth or solidarity of "kinsfolk".
  • Relations: Similar because it means family members ("all his relations"); differs because it also means connections between things, and carries a more formal, legalistic flavour than "kinsfolk".
  • Family: Similar because it is the closest kin circle; differs because it is the default modern word, centred on parents, children and household, and never implies clan, tribe or heroic solidarity.
  • Folks: Similar because it is a colloquial collective for family or relatives; differs because it is informal, often means parents specifically ("my folks"), and has no archaic dignity.
  • Kinsmen: Similar because it names male relatives of the same body; differs because it is gendered and partly archaic, while "kinsfolk" is the gender-neutral collective that includes women and children.
  • Clan: Similar because it denotes a wider kin group with shared descent; differs because it implies a formal structure with name, chief and territory (especially Scottish Highland usage), which "kinsfolk" does not require.
  • Tribe: Similar because it is a kinship-based social unit; differs because it denotes a whole distinct community, often with political and territorial sense, not simply one's own relatives.
  • Kith: Similar because it pairs with "kin" in the idiom "kith and kin"; differs because "kith" alone means acquaintances, friends and neighbours — the known circle — not blood relatives, and it is now archaic outside the doublet.
