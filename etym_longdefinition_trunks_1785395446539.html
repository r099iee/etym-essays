TRUNKS is the plural of trunk: the main woody stems of trees, as distinct from the branches and roots. A trunk is the massive central axis of a tree between roots and crown, the great column that bears the branches. In this scene from The Hobbit the trunks are handholds and shields: they are the great bare stems of the mountain pines, thick enough to hide a hobbit behind and strong enough to anchor a man's swing and deflect bounding boulders. The word here is stripped of all metaphor and returned to its oldest, plainest sense, and the plural form distributes the danger among many trees, so that the whole company is scrambling among them at once. PRIMARY SENSES
  • The main stem of a tree — the great central woody axis between roots and crown, distinct from branches and roots; the primary and oldest English sense.
  • The human torso — the main body of a person or animal, excluding head and limbs (15th century): a transfer from the tree, on the analogy of the main central body from which limbs project.
  • The elephant's proboscis — the long, flexible trunk of an elephant (17th century): the projecting main body of the face, named by the same analogy.
  • A large storage box or travelling chest (16th century) — originally a chest made from a hollowed tree-trunk, hence the name; in American English, the luggage compartment of a car.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • The main body of a pillar, column, or carriage (17th century) — the central shaft as distinct from base and capital, or the main body of a vehicle.
  • Trunk road, trunk line, trunk route (19th century) — the main road or railway line from which branches diverge, on the analogy of the tree's central stem; hence trunk call, a long-distance telephone call carried on a trunk line.
  • Trunks (plural) as swimwear or sports shorts (20th century) — short trousers worn for swimming, from trunk-hose, the short loose breeches of the 16th century that covered the trunk of the body.
  • Anatomical and zoological use — the trunk of a blood vessel, nerve, or artery: the main channel from which branches divide, used throughout medical terminology.
  • Computer and network jargon — a trunk in telephony and networking is a main line or link carrying many channels between exchanges, again the main-stem image.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES All the senses hang together on the single image of a main central body from which smaller things project. The tree-trunk is the first and root sense; the torso is the trunk of the body; the elephant's proboscis is a projecting trunk; the chest is a hollowed trunk; the trunk road is the main line from which branches run; and trunk-hose covered the trunk of the body, giving trunks its modern swimwear use. Each transfer is a metonymy or analogy from the tree, and the word has proved one of the most productive of such transfers in English. The plural trunks, in particular, has split in two: many tree-stems on the one hand, and a single pair of swimming trunks on the other — a plural noun, like trousers, for a garment that is two. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, the word is entirely concrete and physical — the trunks are objects to grab, to swing from, and to shelter behind. Some caught hold of them and swung into lower branches; some, like the little hobbit, got behind a tree to shelter from the onslaught of the rocks. The plural form distributes the danger among many trees, so that the whole company is scrambling among them at once, and the reader sees a grove of great pine stems being used as a refuge as the mountain sheds its rocks. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Bole — the literary, poetic word for the thick lower part of a tree's trunk, the great column from ground to first branches; warmer and more antique in tone than trunk.
  • Stem — in botany, the whole above-ground axis of a plant, but in ordinary speech suggesting the slender support of herbs and shrubs rather than a massive tree.
  • Stalk — the slighter support of leaves, flowers, or fruit; never a tree.
  • Stump — what remains after felling or lopping; a truncated trunk, not a standing one.
  • Stock — the lower part of a trunk, or the rooted portion from which new growth springs.
  • Body / torso — the human trunk, but torso is the technical anatomical word, and body is entirely general.
  • Chest / box / case — storage containers; a trunk is the largest and sturdiest, built for travel and storage, often with a hinged lid.
GRAMMATICAL AND FORMAL NOTES Trunk is a countable noun; the plural trunks is used both of many tree-stems and, as a plural-only noun, of a pair of swimming trunks. The word is also a modifier (trunk road, trunk line, trunk call, trunk route) and appears in the compounds trunk-hose and trunks (swimwear). Its origin is Latin: truncus "maimed, lopped; the lopped tree-stem", of uncertain deeper pedigree, reaching English through Old French tronc in the 14th century. Register: solid, plain, and structural in the tree sense; the word is at home in ordinary speech, and its range of transfers makes it one of the most versatile concrete nouns in English.
