- 16th–17th c. - divest (from Latin divestire, 'to undress', de- + vestire, 'to clothe') enters English meaning literally to strip of clothing; the earliest uses are physical, of bodies being unclothed.
- 17th–18th c. - the legal and ceremonial sense swells: to divest is to strip a person of property, possessions, office, or rights - a systematic, item-by-item removal carried out with the gravity of law rather than the violence of theft.
- 18th–19th c. - the word colonizes political and economic prose - a monarch 'divested of power', an heir 'divested of the estate' - while the reflexive form, to 'divest oneself', gains a flavour of deliberate, almost ritual renunciation.
- 20th–21st c. - corporate finance appropriates it (to divest a holding; 'divestiture'), and in literary prose the word keeps its eerie neutrality: to be divested is to be relieved, item by item, of everything that clothed one - name, origin, family, history, identity - until only the bare, unclaimed self remains.
