DIDST is a fossil of the second-person singular past of do, and to tell its story is to tell two stories at once: the ancient career of the root that built do, and the slow death of the English thou-conjugation that once carried forms like didst. The ultimate source is the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeh₁-, "to put, place, set," one of the most productive roots in the entire family. In the verbal system of PIE this root characteristically appeared in a reduplicated present — the stem built by repeating the first syllable, *dʰi-dʰeh₁- — and its descendants in the daughter languages are striking for their symmetry. Vedic Sanskrit preserves dadhāti, "he places, puts," directly from the reduplicated form; Ancient Greek has títhēmi, "I put, place," with the same structure, and from its nominal forms come Greek thḗkē, "receptacle, box" (hence English theca), and the root the- that underlies thesis, theme, hypothesis, and synthesis. In the Italic branch the root was extended with a velar element, giving Latin faciō, facere, "to make, do," whose enormous Romance progeny includes French faire and Italian fare, and whose Latin participles supplied English fact, factor, factory, feat, faction, and fashion. The Baltic and Slavic branches continue the root in Lithuanian dėti, "to put," and Russian det', "to place, put," and even Hittite, the earliest attested Indo-European language, has dāi-, "to place." The root's original sense was physical: laying one thing upon another, setting, making. The Germanic branch took a different, characteristically Germanic course. From the same *dʰeh₁- came Proto-Germanic *dōną, "to do, put, act," which became Old English dōn. The past tense was built not on a suffix but on a reduplicated stem — Proto-Germanic *dēd-, the direct descendant of the PIE perfect *dʰe-dʰeh₁-, "he placed" — which appears in Old English as dyde, in Gothic as the ending -dedum, and in Old Saxon as -dun. Old English inflected this past tense with the person endings of the strong verbs: dyde "I/he did," dydon "they did," and for the second person singular, used with the pronoun þū "thou," dydest — "thou didst." This -est ending is the ancestor of the whole modern English -st/-est family of archaic thou-forms (hast, wert, satst, mad'st), and it descends from the ancient Indo-European second-person singular desinence reinforced in Germanic by the clitic pronoun "thou." In Middle English the vowels and syllables were levelled. The past stem dyde simplified and shifted toward did, and the full second-person form dydest was contracted to didest and finally didst, with the two-syllable diddest retained for rhythm when the pronoun was omitted. This contraction is the same sound change that produced satst from satest and know'st from knowest: the unstressed middle vowel of the -est ending drops when it follows a consonant cluster, leaving the cluster -dst. By the fourteenth century both didest and didst were in circulation, and the contracted form steadily won in verse. Early Modern English inherited the completed system. The King James Bible (1611), Shakespeare, and Milton use didst as the standard thou-form of the past tense of do, in every one of the verb's roles: as the lexical verb ("Thou didst write"), as the auxiliary in periphrastic pasts and emphatics ("Thou didst inspire That Shepherd" — the very Miltonic line on the Oreb/Sinai card), and as the pro-verb. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were the great age of the thou-paradigm in elevated English: didst, like wast and satst, was the intimate and reverent form of address to God, to a beloved, to a single soul, and it carried a weight of direct address that modern "you did" has entirely lost. The collapse of the thou-conjugation sealed didst's fate. As you displaced thou in ordinary speech during the eighteenth century, didst vanished from the living verb and was preserved only in scripture, liturgy, prayer, and archaizing poetry. The translators of the Bible had locked it into the most printed and most memorized sentences in the language, and the Book of Common Prayer kept it in the mouths of congregations, so didst never quite died — it became a deliberate archaism, the marker of hieratic and poetic diction. Milton's "Thou didst inspire" is today the most famous line of the word's later career, a perfect demonstration of the form's metrical utility: didst supplies a strong monosyllable that plain "did" could not place at the head of the clause in the same way. Sound and grammatical notes:
  • The cluster -dst (d-i-d-s-t) is one of English's most compact final consonant groups; it survives audibly only in this archaic form and in the parallel satst/hadst forms.
  • The -st ending is the second-person singular marker shared by the whole archaic conjugation (dost, didst, wast, satst, hadst, know'st) and descends from the Indo-European secondary ending *-s, reinforced in Germanic.
  • Register: in modern English didst is 100% archaic and stylistic; it adds no grammatical meaning beyond what did conveys, only the solemn, reverent, intimate tone of thou-address.
  • Pronounced /dˈɪdst/, a single syllable; the two-syllable diddest was reserved for rhythm in the same period.
In sum, didst is the past tense of one of the most polysemous verbs in the language, locked into the thou-paradigm at the moment of its greatest literary glory, and preserved ever since as a fossil of early modern sacred diction.
