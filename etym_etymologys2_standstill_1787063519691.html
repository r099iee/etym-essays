PART 1 — STAND:
Proto-Indo-European (c. 4500–3000 BCE)
*steh₂-
“to stand, to be standing”
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*standaną
“to stand”
↓
Old English (8th–11th c.)
standan

↓

standen
“to stand”
↓

stand
“to be in an upright position;

PART 2 — STILL:
Proto-Indo-European (c. 4500–3000 BCE)
*stel-
“to place, put, set”
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*stilli-
“motionless, quiet”
↓
Old English (8th–11th c.)
stille
“motionless, quiet, calm”
↓
Middle English (12th–15th c.)
stille
“still, quiet”
↓
Modern English (present)
still
“without movement; quiet;
even now”

COMPOUND:
English (1685)
standstill
“a complete stop, a state in
which movement ceases”
↓
Modern English (present)
STANDSTILL
“a complete cessation of
movement, work, or progress”

Note on debate: The PIE root behind Germanic *stilli- is debated; it is commonly linked to *stel- “to place, set” (the idea being “placed, at rest”), but a zero-grade derivation from *steh₂- “to stand” has also been proposed. The two roots are themselves probably related. The compound standstill was formed in English from the verb phrase to stand still and is first recorded around 1685.
