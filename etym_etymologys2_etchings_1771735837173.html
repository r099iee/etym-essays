PIE *h₁ed- "to eat" (one of the oldest and widest-attested roots of the family; zero-grade *h₁d-) ├── Germanic: Proto-Germanic *etaną "to eat" │ ├── Old English etan → English eat, ate, eaten (sister branch) │ ├── Old High German ezzan → German essen (sister branch) │ └── Proto-Germanic *atjaną (causative of *etaną, "to cause to eat, to feed") │ ├── Old High German ezzen "to cause to eat, to graze, to feed" │ │ └── Middle High German etzen "to cause to eat away; to corrode" │ │ └── German ätzen "to cause acid to eat into metal; to corrode; to etch" (the printmaker's term) │ │ ├── German Ätzung "etching, corrosion"; Ätzwasser "acid" (literally "eating-water") │ │ └── Middle Dutch etsen → Dutch etsen "to etch" │ │ └── English etch "to bite a design into metal with acid" (1630s, borrowed from the Flemish-Dutch engraving world) │ │ ├── English etching (n.) "a print made by etching; the process" (17th c.) │ │ ├── English etcher, etchant │ │ └── English etchings (plural n.) "such prints as a body of work; the suite of plates illustrating Piranesi" — OUR CARD NOTE (debate): the chain from *h₁ed- "to eat" through the Germanic causative to German ätzen and Dutch etsen to English etch is the standard, well-attested account; the semantic bridge is the image of acid "eating" into metal. The English word is a seventeenth-century borrowing from the Dutch/Flemish printmaking world, not an Old English inheritance.
