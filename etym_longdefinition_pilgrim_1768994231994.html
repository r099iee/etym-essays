In this passage — a boy sleeping on the deck of a ship, adrift among strangers while the dim shore rises and falls — pilgrim does far more than name a traveler. The narrator takes a child who is homeless, friendless, and in flight and translates his solitary westward motion into the idiom of sacred quest. A pilgrim is not merely someone who travels; he is someone whose wandering is itself a form of seeking, a slow movement toward an unknown sanctuary. The word imports the quiet solemnity of medieval devotion into a scene of raw, secular motion, so that the crowded deck becomes a kind of caravan of the faithful and the boy, sleeping among his fellow passengers, is dignified rather than merely pitied. The tone is elegiac and faintly reverent: the writer withholds ordinary labels like refugee or drifter and reaches instead for a word heavy with centuries of holy journeying, granting the child's exile the gravity of a vocation. Nothing in the physical detail — the gray seabirds, the low shore, the deck at night — is sacred in itself; what is sacred is the frame the narrator chooses to place around the boy's passage, and the metaphor quietly prepares the reader to understand his subsequent wanderings as a pilgrimage through a landscape of loss. The word's senses radiate outward from a single core of sacred journeying:
  • One who journeys to a sacred place (primary, religious sense): a person who travels, often on foot and across great distances, to visit a holy site as an act of devotion, penance, thanksgiving, or vow — the Muslim to Mecca and Medina (the hajj), the Christian to Jerusalem, Rome, or Santiago de Compostela, the Hindu to Varanasi, Prayag, or the Char Dham, the Buddhist to Bodh Gaya. Pilgrimage is one of the oldest and most widespread religious practices in the world, and the word names its practitioner.
  • A traveler or wayfarer in general (extended, now chiefly literary): a person who journeys, especially one whose traveling has the quality of seeking — a wanderer moving through the world with purpose rather than aimlessness. This is the sense the passage uses, and it sits halfway between the religious sense and the merely geographical one.
  • The Pilgrim Fathers (historical proper-noun use): the English Puritans who sailed on the Mayflower and founded Plymouth Colony in 1620, called the Pilgrim Fathers from the 18th century onward; the word here carries the whole Puritan idiom of the New World as a Promised Land and the Atlantic crossing as an exodus.
  • One who travels in a spirit of earnest seeking (secular metaphor): since the 17th–18th centuries the word has generalized to any earnest seeker — a pilgrim of knowledge, a pilgrim of art, a pilgrim of freedom — someone whose wandering is consecrated by an inner purpose rather than a literal shrine.
  • The whole of life as a journey (pervasive metaphor): in the Christian tradition above all, life itself is a pilgrimage and every believer a pilgrim passing through the world toward a heavenly home — the sense made famous by Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress (1678) and by the patristic image of the City of God, where Augustine calls the faithful peregrini in the earthly city.
  • Tourist and heritage use (modern softening): in the age of mass travel, "pilgrim" and "pilgrimage" are used, sometimes ironically, of any devoted visit to a site of significance to a person's identity — a pilgrimage to Graceland, to a literary birthplace, to a stadium — the sacred frame applied to secular devotion.
Set against its near-synonyms, pilgrim is defined by the fusion of three ideas: distance, foreignness, and devotion. A traveler is anyone who undertakes a journey, with no implication that the goal is holy or that the going is a discipline; a wayfarer stresses the physical act of walking the road and carries an archaic, courteous flavor, but it lacks the religious charge entirely; a wanderer suggests aimlessness or exile and inverts the pilgrim's purposefulness — a wanderer goes where chance carries him, while a pilgrim proceeds by conviction; a sojourner names a temporary resident rather than a mover; a voyager emphasizes the sea and the crossing; a seeker names the inner purpose without the journey; and a hajji is the specific title of one who has completed the pilgrimage to Mecca. What keeps "pilgrim" distinct is the deliberate fusion of travel and transcendence: the pilgrim's road is also a spiritual state, and his destination, however distant, is the point at which the seeking ends.
