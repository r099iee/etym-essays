- [Early 18th c. borrowing (c. 1703)] - Taken from French pittoresque, itself from Italian pittoresco (coined 1648 by Marco Boschini to praise art 'after the manner of painters'); in English it first meant something like the painter's own animated, sketchy handling, with the accent on the painter rather than the picture.
- [Folk-etymology respelling (early 18th c.)] - English quickly respelled the foreign pittoresque as picturesque, fusing it with the familiar word picture; the meaning slipped from 'like a painter' to 'like a picture', so the word now described scenes that looked as if they had been composed for a painting - scenic, striking, visual.
- [Aesthetic category of the Picturesque (1740s-1750s)] - William Gilpin and Uvedale Price lifted the word into landscape theory as a distinct category set between the smooth Beautiful and the terrifying Sublime: scenery that pleased precisely through its roughness, irregularity, and painterly variety. Public perception: a term of art-criticism and gardening.
- [Descent into tourism (19th c.)] - The word tumbled out of the treatises into the guidebook; any scene 'worth a picture' - a ruined abbey, a crooked lane, a village of half-timbered houses - was 'picturesque', and it became the standard mild compliment of the travelling public, praising charm rather than grandeur.
- [Modern register] - Today 'picturesque' still means 'strikingly visual, as if composed for a painting' - of scenery, of villages, and, applied to language, 'vivid, graphic'. Its register has settled into a gently approving, faintly touristic praise: attractive and charming but never overwhelming, the pretty postcard rather than the sublime vista.
