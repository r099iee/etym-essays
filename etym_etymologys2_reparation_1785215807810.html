Linear derivation chain for REPARATION (single root): 1. PIE *per- ("to produce, procure, bring forth; to carry over") — a broad, polyvalent root attested across the family; the source of the Latin verb parāre. (Some scholars separate the "produce/bring forth" sense from the "carry over" sense; the connection between the two groups is debated.) 2. Latin parāre ("to make ready, prepare, provide, procure") — the productive verbal stem; classical usage from early Latin onwards. It formed the base of a vast family of prefixed verbs (compare, prepare, apparatus, empire). 3. Latin reparāre ("to restore, mend, recover, make ready again") — formed with the prefix re- ("again, back") + parāre; attested from Classical Latin. At first wholly concrete: mending a wall, a harvest, a depleted store. 4. Latin reparātiō, reparātiōnis ("restoration, renewal, repair") — abstract noun in -tiō derived from reparāre; Late Latin also used it for compensation and recompense. 5. Old French reparation ("repair, restoration") — the Romance vernacular outcome of reparātiō, passed into medieval French. 6. Middle English reparacioun ("repair, restoration") — borrowed c. 14th century from Old French; the earliest English sense is concrete, the mending of damaged property. 7. Early Modern English reparation ("compensation, the making of amends for a wrong") — the moral and legal sense developed in the 16th–17th centuries alongside the older concrete sense. 8. Modern English REPARATION — and the plural reparations, which from the early 20th century names war indemnities (e.g., after 1918 and 1945) and, later, demands for historical justice (reparations for slavery and colonialism). Note on debate: the link between Latin parāre and PIE *per- is generally accepted but the root is notoriously broad; Roman grammarians also associated parāre with parere ("to give birth, bring forth"), and the two are often treated together as twin developments of the same root. The English word is a vernacular borrowing through Old French, not a learned reborrowing, which is why its earliest English sense is the concrete one of repairing.
