- c. 1300: "rank" borrowed from Old French = a row, line, series (of things or people); concrete, spatial.
- 14th–15th centuries: military specialization: a rank = a line of soldiers abreast; "the ranks" = the common soldiery; perception: ordered, disciplined, hierarchical.
- 16th century: extension to society: "men of rank" = people of high standing; the row becomes a grade; perception: social elevation, dignity.
- 17th–18th centuries: official grades of office (military rank: captain, general); idioms "to break ranks", "to close ranks", "to rise from the ranks"; perception: institutional, formal.
- 19th century: "to rank" (verb) = to place in graded order; "the ranks of the unemployed" — collective membership sense; perception: statistical, collective.
- 20th–21st centuries: rankings everywhere — sports rankings, university rankings, search-engine rankings; "to rank" = to be graded algorithmically; the physical line of soldiers survives chiefly in historical and fantasy prose (Tolkien's "pierce their ranks").
