MUSE has two separate etymological lines that merge in English spelling: the noun (the primary rootWord sense) and the verb. CHAIN 1 — the noun Muse "goddess of inspiration": PIE *men- ("to think, remember, have in mind"; attested through reflexes in all major branches — Sanskrit mánas "mind," Latin mens "mind," Greek mnēmē "memory," Germanic *gamundiz "remembrance"; c. 4000–3000 BCE) → Greek *mont-yā (feminine formation from the *men- root, "the remembering one"; the -s- cluster in the outcome is not entirely regular and a Pre-Greek substratum origin has also been proposed — Beekes notes the uncertainty, but the traditional *men- derivation remains standard) → Greek Moûsa (Μοῦσα, "Muse"; goddess of the arts, daughter of Zeus and Mnemosyne, Memory; attested from the archaic period) → Latin Mūsa ("Muse"; classical, 1st c. BCE) → Old French Muse (9th–14th c.) → Middle English Muse (late 14th c., already in Chaucer) → Modern English Muse (noun) ◄ TARGET CHAIN 2 — the English verb muse "to ponder" is a DIFFERENT root: Gallo-Romance *mūsa ("muzzle, snout"; of obscure origin — possibly Germanic, compare Middle Dutch muys "snout," or onomatopoeic; the ultimate source is unknown) → Old French muser ("to loiter, idle, gape with the muzzle in the air; to muse, ponder"; 12th c.) → Middle English musen ("to ponder, reflect, be absorbed in thought"; late 14th c., borrowed from Anglo-French/Old French) → Modern English muse (verb) ◄ TARGET MERGER NOTE: the noun and the verb came to share a spelling in English and have been conflated since the late fourteenth century, though they are etymologically unrelated. From the same French verb-line also derive amuse (Old French amuser, "to occupy the mind, divert") and bemused.
