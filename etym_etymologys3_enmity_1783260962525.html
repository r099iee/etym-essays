A WORD BUILT FROM ITS OWN OPPOSITE The story of enmity begins, paradoxically, with a word for love. Latin amāre, "to love," is itself of disputed ancestry: some scholars connect it with PIE *h₂em-, "to grasp, take into the hand" (cf. Sanskrit ámiti, "seizes, oppresses"), the idea being that to love is to "take hold of"; others treat it as an inherited nursery or baby-talk word; de Vaan marks the etymology uncertain. Whatever its origin, amāre produced amīcus, "friend" — etymologically "one who loves / the beloved" — and from amīcus Latin built the abstract amīcitia, "friendship," the ancestor of English amity. Negation then produced the enemy: in- ("not") + amīcus gives inimīcus, "not-a-friend, hostile, enemy," and from inimīcus an abstract noun in -itia, inimīcitia, "the state of being an enemy; hostility." The state of being enemies is thus, etymologically, a negative image of friendship itself: enmity is "not-friendship," and its antonym amity is its exact mirror built from the same root. It is a striking fact that the deepest structure of the word encodes a denial — to be an enemy is, in the root of the word, to be a not-friend. ROMAN POLITICS AND THE ROAD TO ENGLISH In Republican Rome inimīcitia was a formal political fact. An inimīcus was a declared personal opponent — distinct from hostis, the public enemy of the state (from an unrelated root, PIE *ǵʰostis, "stranger") — and inimīcitiae (the plural) could denote a standing feud between senators or factions, an institutionalized personal opposition conducted through the courts and the assemblies. Through the accusative inimīcum, Vulgar Latin gave Old French enemi, whence English enemy; through the abstract inimīcitia, Old French built enemisté (twelfth century), which Middle English borrowed as enmite and enemytie in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. The spelling and sound settled over the early modern period, the word losing the French-derived pronunciation of its source and hardening into the modern enmity. FROM PERSON TO NATION TO COSMOS The word's semantic career is a steady expansion outward. In Middle English it meant personal, mutual hostility — the state of being enemies between individuals. The Reformation and the religious wars carried it into confessional and national registers: the enmity between faiths and peoples, a word of sermons and chronicles. In the Authorized Version and theological prose it acquired cosmic weight — the biblical enmity between the seed of the woman and the serpent, between God and sin — a moral state of irreconcilable opposition. And in 18th- and 19th-century historical prose it became the great abstraction of national history: Macaulay's "after ages of enmity" compresses the entire chronicle of Anglo-Scottish hostility — border wars, dynastic struggles, religious division, recurrent invasion — into a single dignified noun, and then dissolves it in the next clause into "indissoluble ties of interest and affection." That turn is the word's perfect use: enmity as the long darkness, friendship as the light, both built from the same root of love.
