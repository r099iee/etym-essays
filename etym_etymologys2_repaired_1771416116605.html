Derivation chain for REPAIRED (the "to go, betake oneself" sense; single root word, though the verb is a compound of the prefix re- and the root pater, both traced here):

1. Proto-Indo-European *ph₂tér- / *pəter- (reconstructed) — "father," one of the oldest kinship words in the family, built with the kinship suffix *-ter. Reflexes span the Indo-European world: Sanskrit pitṛ "father" (Rigveda, c. 1500 BCE), Avestan pitar-, Greek patēr, Latin pater, and Germanic *fadēr (ancestor of English father).

2. Latin pater — "father" (classical, attested); from it the derived adjectives patrius "paternal" and paternus "fatherly," and the noun patria "fatherland" — the land of one's fathers.

3. Late Latin repatriare — "to return to one's native land," formed in Late Latin (attested 4th–6th centuries) from the prefix re- "back, again" + patria "fatherland"; the direct ancestor of English repatriate and of the "going" sense of repair.

4. Old French repairer / repatrier — "to return home, betake oneself," 12th–13th centuries; in Old French the verb broadened from "return to one's country" to simply "go, betake oneself to a place."

5. Middle English repairen — "to go, betake oneself," 14th century; borrowed with the French meaning already generalised.

6. Modern English repair — "to go, betake oneself (to a place)," formal and archaic in modern use; the card's inflected form is the past tense REPAIRED.

Note on the prefix: Latin re- "back, again" has an uncertain Proto-Indo-European pedigree — it is sometimes derived from *wret-, the zero-grade of the root *wert- "to turn," but this equation is debated, and the Latin prefix is best treated as a Latin-internal element. Note on the homograph: the verb repair "to mend" is a separate borrowing of Latin reparare (re- "again" + parare "to make ready"), via Old French reparer; it shares only the prefix and the spelling.
