The biography of finery begins with a word whose own ancestry is a quiet admission of scholarly ignorance. Latin finis, "end, boundary, limit," has no securely established Indo-European pedigree: the standard handbooks (de Vaan among them) class it as without a certain etymology, and the once-popular attempt to attach it to a PIE root meaning "to set, put" — and to the "stake" family of PIE *dʰeygʷ- — is judged uncertain or folk-etymological. So the honest tree starts not at some reconstructed proto-form but at the attested Latin noun itself, and builds its great fortune from there.

The word's power lay in the single idea of the "limit" or "end": whatever is brought to its end is finished, completed, done. From finis Latin derived finīre, "to end, to set bounds to," and from the participle fīnītus, "brought to an end, completed," came a chain of adjectives and nouns meaning bounded, defined, and — crucially — perfected. The link between "ended" and "excellent" is the deep semantic engine of the whole family: a thing carried to its finishing limit is a thing fully made, and a thing fully made is a thing of quality. Latin words from the same family streamed into English: final and finite (pertaining to the end or boundary), define (to mark off a boundary), confine (to keep within bounds), infinite (without boundary), finish (the act of completing), and finance, which began as Old French finance — the settlement of a debt, the money that "ends" an obligation. Even the legal fine, the penalty, is the same word: the sum that settles a case.

The transfer that matters most for our word happened in Old French. There the adjective fin meant both "end" and "fine, pure, refined, of high quality": something carried to its finished limit was, by natural extension, excellent. Middle English borrowed the adjective as fine in the thirteenth century — "of high quality, delicate, pure, excellent" — and the whole modern English family grew from it. The crucial enrichment came when English added the suffix -ery (from Old French -erie, marking a collection or a place of an activity) to the adjective fine: by the seventeenth century the word finery appears, meaning "showy, expensive clothes and ornaments" — the mass noun for the brilliant, finished exterior of a person dressed for display. The sense is attested from around the 1670s, and it carried the double temperature that still marks the word: genuine admiration for the splendour, and a knowing smile at the effort and vanity that produce it. "Sunday finery" and "in all one's finery" are the surviving idioms of that register.

Almost simultaneously, the same word-family entered the vocabulary of iron-making. In the seventeenth century a finery was the hearth or forge in which pig iron was refined into wrought iron — the "finery fire," the "finery process," the finery where crude metal was brought to its fine, usable state. English refine is itself re- + fine, "to make fine again, to purify," and the finery is literally the furnace where the metal is fined. The two senses of the word are thus one family, not an accident of homophony: the fine dress of the ballroom and the fine iron of the forge are both things carried to their finished, perfected condition. The metallurgical sense has now receded with the technology, but it survives in the histories of industry, while the dress sense remains the living word.

The nineteenth century was finery's novelistic golden age. In the age of Austen and the marriage market, finery was the standard word of the ballroom: the ladies "in their finest finery," the display of dress that was itself a form of courtship and competition. The card's context — "The ladies were all dressed in their finest finery for the ball" — is precisely this world. The word mingles admiring spectacle with a knowing smile at the effort behind it, exactly the tone it keeps today: alive but informal-literary, a little too warm for cold formal prose, still carrying affection or mild satire, and surviving chiefly in idioms like "dressed in all one's finery." From the silent Latin word for "end" to the glittering dress of the ballroom, finery has spent two thousand years proving that what is finished is fine, and what is fine is worn for display.
