- Old English - stingan means simply 'to stab, thrust, prick': a wound made by a point; stinging is the act of piercing, with no insect connection yet.
- Middle English - the sense focuses on insects and nettles: bees and nettles 'sting', and 'stinging nettle' becomes the plant's ordinary name; at the same time 'the sting of conscience' enters devotional prose - the moral prick begins.
- 16th-17th centuries - the great figurative fix: the King James Bible's 'O death, where is thy sting?' (1 Cor. 15:55, 1611) makes the sacred sting famous, and satirists and preachers speak of 'stinging' words and lampoons; the word now wounds as well as bites.
- 18th-19th centuries - science adopts it: naturalists classify stinging insects and stinging hairs; 'stinging' becomes both a precise classifier and still an everyday adjective for cold, smoke, and pain.
- 20th-21st centuries - journalism and folklore: 'stinging defeat', 'stinging criticism', 'stinging attack' are formulaic in news prose; the noun's offshoot 'sting operation' (police trap) shows the sting as sudden capture; perception: sharp, small, and surprisingly painful.
