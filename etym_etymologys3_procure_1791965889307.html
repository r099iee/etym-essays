Everything in procure turns on the Latin noun cūra, “care, concern, anxiety, attention”. The word is common in Republican Latin — Plautus uses it of worry and of attentiveness, Cicero of political concern and of literary care — and it has no agreed Indo-European etymology. The handbooks are candid: cūra stands isolated, and any deeper root is conjectural. That is why the chain above begins with the attested Latin noun rather than inventing a prehistoric ancestor.

Care as management

From cūra Latin formed cūrāre, “to care for, look after, attend to”, and with the prefix prō-, “for, on behalf of”, the compound prōcūrāre, “to take care of something for someone, to manage, administer”. The Roman procurator was a manager or agent — of an estate, of a business, of another person’s affairs — and under the Empire the title spread to the equestrian officials who administered imperial finances and provinces. Roman religion also knew procuratio, the expiation or averting of prodigies, literally the “taking care of” the gods’ anger. The notion of care is thus present at every step: to procure is to see to something.

Into French and English

Old French inherited procurer in the thirteenth century with the senses “to obtain, to bring about, to act as agent”, and English borrowed it around 1300 as procuren. The earliest English sense is causative: to procure a result, a meeting, a death. The sense “to obtain” follows in the fourteenth century and gradually becomes the everyday one, while the causative survives in legal and literary phrases such as “procure a miscarriage” and “procure someone’s death”. The same French stock gave English procurator, proctor and, through the abbreviated procuracy, proxy.

  • The sexual sense. From the sixteenth century the verb is used of obtaining a person — usually a woman — for another’s sexual use. The euphemism works by transferring the language of supply from goods to people, and its legal descendants are the offences of procuring and the figures of the procurer and procuress.
  • Administrative sense. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, armies and navies “procured” provisions, stores and transport, and by the twentieth century procurement had become a named function of governments and corporations, complete with officers, contracts and regulations.
  • Semantic core. Across all these uses the original structure persists: someone causes something to be available for someone else, with care and method. The difference between “procure a licence” and “procure a woman” is the difference between supplying an object and treating a person as one.

Register and modern use

Procure is now a marked word: formal, slightly bureaucratic and often a little ominous. In business it is neutral and technical; in general prose it can sound like a euphemism or an accusation. The idiom “procure for himself” in the source is a good illustration of its older flexibility — it means “obtain for his own benefit”, not “supply himself with goods”. Modern writers usually prefer obtain or get unless they want the suggestion of careful arrangement, and they avoid the verb entirely when a person is the object, unless they are writing law or history.

Note on the root

Latin cūra has no secure Indo-European derivation, so no PIE stage is shown. The prefix prō- is the ordinary Italic preposition “before, for”, from PIE *pro-; it contributes the sense of acting on someone’s behalf, which is what separates procure from the simpler cure.
