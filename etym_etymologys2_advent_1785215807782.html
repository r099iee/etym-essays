ADVENT descends from a single linear chain: - PIE *gʷem- (full grade *gʷem-, zero grade *gʷm̥-) "to go, come" (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; one of the most widely attested PIE roots: Sanskrit gacchati "he goes" and gámanam "going", Greek baínō "to go, step" (whence basis, English base and basis), Germanic *kweman (Old English cuman, English come), Latin venīre "to come") → Latin venīre "to come" (the regular Italic outcome of the root's zero grade *gʷm̥-; attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin advenīre "to come to, arrive" [ad- "to, toward" + venīre] (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Latin adventus "an arrival, approach, coming" (past participle of advenīre used as a noun; attested 1st c. BCE–CE; in imperial Latin also the formal word for an emperor's ceremonial arrival) → Church Latin Adventus (Domini) "the coming of Christ; the four-week season of preparation before Christmas" (Christian Latin from the 4th c.; the Greek counterpart is parousía "presence, arrival") → Old French advent / Middle French avènement "advent, accession" → Middle English advent "the coming of Christ; the season of Advent" (borrowed 12th–14th c., first in the liturgical sense) → English advent, secularized: "the arrival or coming of any notable person, era, event, or invention" (generalized sense attested from the 15th–16th c. onward) → Modern English ADVENT. Note on debate: the root grade underlying Latin venīre (the zero grade *gʷm̥-) is standard, and the Italic development *gʷm̥- > *gwem- > ven- is regular; the precise laryngeal and accentual reconstruction of the PIE root is debated but does not affect the attested chain. No stage between PIE and Latin venīre is directly attested. The generalized English sense developed within English from the liturgical word, a semantic broadening well documented from the fifteenth century.
