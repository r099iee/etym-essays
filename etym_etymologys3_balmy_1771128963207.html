The history of balmy is the history of a perfume: the word descends from the ancient trade in fragrant resinous gums, and the modern weather-adjective — "pleasantly warm" — is the distant, metaphorized descendant of a healing ointment. The chain begins outside Indo-European, in the Semitic languages of the ancient Near East, where a word such as Hebrew bāśām designated balsam — the aromatic resin of certain trees, prized for perfume and medicine. The precise source is debated: some scholars point to Hebrew bāśām, others to Akkadian burāšu "juniper", but all agree that the Greek word was a Semitic loanword. Ancient Greek bálsamon (attested by the 5th–4th century BCE) named the balsam plant and its precious fragrant gum, and it is from Greek that Latin took balsamum in the 1st century BCE.

In the classical world balsam was among the most expensive substances on earth — a fragrant resin used in incense, unguents, and medicine, associated with royal courts and with the famous balsam groves of the East. The Latin word balsamum travelled into the Romance languages, and in Old French it became basme, later baume — "balm, fragrant ointment". It is from this Old French/Anglo-French form that Middle English borrowed balme (13th century), which survives as modern English balm: a soothing, fragrant healing ointment. Already in Middle English the figurative use was active — "balm" for anything that heals or comforts the spirit — and the biblical "balm in Gilead" (Jeremiah 8:22) made the word a permanent metaphor for healing consolation in English religious language.

Phonologically, the word's journey is one of systematic abbreviation and simplification: Greek bálsamon → Latin balsamum → Old French basme → Middle English balme → modern balm, a sequence in which the unstressed final syllables and the internal cluster were progressively reduced, and the sequence -als- simplified to -al-. The adjective balmy was formed in the 16th century (1500s) by adding the common suffix -y to the noun — "like balm, bearing balm, soothing, healing". Its earliest senses were literal and medicinal: a balmy plant yields balm; balmy air heals. From this the meaning extended naturally from substance to atmosphere: because balm soothes, warm air that soothes and refreshes — especially fragrant spring and summer air — was called balmy. By the 18th century the word was fixed as a term for pleasantly warm, mild, fragrant weather, and the Romantic poets and travel writers made it a favourite epithet for gentle Mediterranean and pastoral climates.

The 19th century completed the dominance of the weather sense while preserving the older ones: a "balmy evening" is a warm, soft, fragrant evening; "balmy sleep" is a restorative sleep; a "balmy climate" is one of gentle warmth. At the same time, a curious near-twin emerged in British English: the word barmy, from barm (the froth that forms on fermenting ale), meaning "frothy, hence light-headed, foolish, eccentric". Because barmy and balmy are near-homophones and were often spelled interchangeably in dialect and slang, "balmy" came to be used — and misused — for "slightly mad", giving the comic potential of a "balmy" (foolish) person on a "balmy" (pleasant) day. Careful modern usage distinguishes the words, but the confusion is long-standing and documented.

Literary attestations track the word's career from the 16th-century herbals and religious prose through Milton's use of "balmy" for healing, restorative air and sleep, into the Romantic poets' warm southern landscapes and Victorian descriptions of mild seaside and tropical climates. Today "balmy" is used chiefly of weather — warm, gentle, scented air — and carries a distinctly pleasant, often nostalgic register. Its long journey from an exotic Semitic perfume, through Greek pharmacy, Latin trade, French romance, and English poetry, to a word for a perfect spring afternoon is one of the great examples of a physical substance becoming a meteorological metaphor: the air itself has become an ointment for the senses.
