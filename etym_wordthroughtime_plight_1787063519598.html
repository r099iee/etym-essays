- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Existed as two distinct Germanic words: the noun pliht ("danger, risk, peril, damage, forfeiture") and the verb plihtan ("to expose to danger, compromise, pledge"), rooted in the idea that giving a pledge placed one's honor and life in peril.
- Early Middle English (c. 12th–13th c.) - Following the Norman Conquest, Anglo-Norman pleit / ploit (from Latin plicāre, "to fold/pleat", meaning "fold, condition, entanglement, state") entered English as plit / playt.
- Late Middle English (c. 14th–15th c.) - A major phonetic and graphic merger occurred: Anglo-Norman plit ("condition, state") merged completely with Germanic pliht ("danger, pledge"). The spelling standardized on plight, blending "state/condition" with "inherent danger/adversity."
- 16th to 17th Century - The verb to plight was codified in the Book of Common Prayer (1549) in the solemn matrimonial formula "and thereto I plight thee my troth" (pledge one's loyalty), while the noun plight increasingly narrowed toward unfortunate, distressed, and perilous states.
- Modern Usage - The noun plight is universally understood as a distressing, pitiable, or dangerous predicament (especially in humanitarian and political contexts), while its pledge/troth sense is preserved solely as an archaic ceremonial formula.
