PIE *ud- "up, out, away" (adverbial particle; reconstructed) → PIE *ud-tero- "outer, further, later" (with the contrastive-comparative suffix *-tero-; reconstructed) → Greek hysteros (ὕστερος) "later, after, second, further" (comparative adjective; attested in Greek) → Greek hystera (ὑστέρα) "womb" (traditionally derived from hysteros as "the later one, the last or innermost organ"; attested from the 6th–5th c. BCE, the Hippocratic corpus) → Greek hysterikos (ὑστερικός) "of the womb, suffering in the womb" (adjective; attested in the Hippocratic corpus) → Latin hystericus "hysterical" (borrowed from Greek; attested in medical Latin of the imperial period) → English hysteric / hysterical "of or affected with hysteria; wildly uncontrolled in emotion" (17th c., borrowed from Latin hystericus) → English HYSTERICALLY "in a manner affected by wild uncontrolled emotion" (adverb formed with the native suffix -ly, 18th c.) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The derivation of hystera "womb" from hysteros "later" is ancient and traditional, but its exact motivation is uncertain — whether the womb was "the later organ" in birth order or "the outer/inner organ" — and Beekes treats the word as possibly Pre-Greek; the chain from *ud-tero- to hystera is therefore probable, not certain. (2) The Sanskrit cognate uttara- "upper, further, later" (Hindi uttar "answer, reply; north") securely matches *ud-tero-, but Sanskrit udara- "belly" (traditional sister of hystera) is disputed. (3) English hysteria (the noun) was coined in modern Latin in 1801, and hysterics "a fit of hysterics" is 18th c.; these are collateral formations of the same Greek stem, not separate roots.
