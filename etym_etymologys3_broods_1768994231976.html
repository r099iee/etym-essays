broods — the biography of a warm thought. The story begins not with thought at all but with water on the boil. The Proto-Indo-European root *bʰreuh₁- named the roiling, bubbling, swelling motion of a liquid heated past its patience — the noise and rise of boiling — and it is from this physical roiling that several separate careers in the daughter languages begin. In the Hellenic and Italic families the root stayed close to the fire: Greek phrúgō roasted, and Latin frīgere — via Old French frire — became English fry, so that the modern frying pan is a direct descendant of the boiling root. Latin fervēre “to boil” gave fervor and ferment: the leaven, the yeast that bubbles, was the “boiling agent,” and from that physical bubbling English borrowed a whole vocabulary of spiritual heat — fervent, fervid, fervor — in which the seething of the pot became the heat of the soul. It is the pattern the whole family obeys: the root keeps moving from the physical to the emotional without ever quite leaving the fire behind.

The Germanic kitchen. In the Germanic family the root specialized in two directions at once. One strand applied the bubbling to the brewing and baking of food: Proto-Germanic *brewwaną gave English brew, *brudą gave broth, the “boiled thing,” and *braudą gave bread, the loaf that rises because a leaven bubbles inside it — the same ferment the Latin branch had named. The other strand is ours. Proto-Germanic *brōdō named the young of a bird — not a word of the nest itself but of the warmth that covers it — and the handbooks trace it to the warm-grade twin *bʰreh₁- / *bʰrē- of the same boiling root, the “swell and warm” face of the family. From that noun the verb formed itself: Old English brēdan meant “to keep warm, to cherish, to nurture,” and Middle English broden carried the sense “to sit on eggs” — the hen applying her own heat to the clutch, the boiling of the original root become the patient warmth of incubation. The earliest English sense of the word is therefore bodily and tender, an act of heat and patience, with no thought in it yet.

The mind takes the image. The transfer from nest to mind happened in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and it happened exactly as medieval writers described meditation: to brood on a thought is to sit on it, to keep it warm in the dark of the mind until it hatches. Shakespeare's age used the word of both hens and meditative men, the bird still visible in the metaphor — a brooding melancholy was literally a melancholy being sat upon and incubated. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the figurative sense hardened and turned sullen. To brood became to dwell gloomily, resentfully, on a grief or a wrong; the word collected the morose, silent register that defines it today, and the nest receded into the background. Poets and novelists kept the double image alive — a “brooding” landscape or sky is one that hangs over and threatens like a great sitting bird — and by the twentieth century the split was complete: zoology keeps the literal sense (a bird broods its eggs) while everyday English uses the verb for obsessive, unhappy rumination.

The literary reactivation. What the passage under study does is reactivate the older incubating heat. When McCarthy writes “in him broods already a taste for mindless violence,” he reaches past the sullen thinker to the sitting hen, so that the violence is not chosen or learned but hatched — warmed in the boy's body as an egg is warmed — and the third-person singular form broods fuses the two senses at once. It is a demonstration of how etymological memory works in literature: the word's whole career, from boiling to hatching to brooding on grief, is present in the single use.

  • c. 4500–2500 BCE: PIE *bʰreuh₁- “to boil, bubble, ferment, swell”; warm-grade twin *bʰreh₁- “to warm, swell.”
  • c. 500 BCE–200 CE: Proto-Germanic *brōdō “the warmed young, the brood”; sisters *brewwaną “brew,” *brudą “broth,” *braudą “bread.”
  • c. 800–1000 CE: Old English brōd “brood, offspring”; brēdan “to keep warm, cherish, nurture.”
  • c. 1200–1500: Middle English broden “to sit on eggs, to incubate.”
  • 16th–17th c.: the mind takes the image; to brood on a thought is to sit on and incubate it.
  • 18th–19th c.: the sense hardens into sullen, resentful rumination.
  • 20th–21st c.: literal sense confined to zoology; figurative sense dominant; literary writers reactivate the hatching heat.
