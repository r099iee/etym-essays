In the doggerel that lists 'the big families and most powerful magnates of the area,' magnates are the local great ones—landed, monied, and networked clans whose influence outruns formal office. For a magistrate like Yu-cun, they are not abstract VIPs but practical powers who can intimidate, bribe, or ruin. The tone of the word is weighty and slightly old-fashioned: it stresses bigness of status and resources rather than elected legitimacy. The verses function as a map of who truly runs the region; magnates names that unofficial aristocracy of wealth and kinship. At its core, a magnate is a person of great power, wealth, and importance, especially one who dominates a major business, industry, or landed estate. The word descends from the Latin idea of 'greatness' (magnus 'great'), and it preserves that root's double emphasis: greatness of status and greatness of scale. A magnate is not merely rich—many rich people are not magnates—but a figure whose resources let him dominate an industry, a district, or a political faction. In modern business English the word is most at home in compounds like steel magnate, oil magnate, railroad magnate, and media magnate, where it denotes the commanding head of a vast commercial empire rather than a mere successful entrepreneur.
  • Core senses: (1) historical: a member of the high nobility or the ruling great men of a realm, especially the great feudal lords of Poland, Hungary, and Spain; (2) modern: a wealthy, powerful person in business, industry, or media; (3) extended: anyone whose wealth and influence tower over a local community—the 'great ones' of a district, exactly the regional families catalogued in the novel.
  • Historical sense and its semantic bridge: Latin magnas (plural magnates) literally meant 'great man'; medieval Europe used magnates for the great nobles and prelates whose landed power made and unmade kings. When the word reached English in the fifteenth century it kept that aristocratic sense. The modern 'captain of industry' sense grew out of it in the nineteenth century by analogy: industrialists and financiers were the new 'great men' of their age, dominating nations through capital as feudal lords had through land. The bridge is power of scale, not the exact source of the power.
  • Jargon and technical usages: in media studies, 'media magnate' names the owner of a large press or broadcasting empire; in political history, 'magnate' designates the powerful aristocracy of medieval Hungary, Poland, and Bohemia; in financial journalism 'oil magnate' and 'property magnate' are stock formulas. In all these the word keeps its Latinate dignity and a faint whiff of old privilege.
  • Metaphoric and colloquial extensions: 'local magnate' or 'small-town magnate' can be used, sometimes with irony, of a dominant businessman or political boss in a small place. 'Captains of industry,' 'barons' (robber barons, oil barons), and 'bigwigs' are parallel metaphors, but magnate remains the most formal and least glamorous-casual of the set.
Synonym boundaries: tycoon and mogul emphasize modern business empire-building (often industrial or media) and can sound journalistic or glamorous. Oligarch stresses rule by a small wealthy clique and often carries political critique. Aristocrat and noble imply hereditary rank and titles, which magnates may lack while still dominating through money and clan. Bigwig and VIP are colloquial and lighter. Local strongman suggests coercive personal power more than dynastic wealth. In this translation of a Chinese novel, magnates usefully covers great gentry-merchant families without forcing European titles. Collocations: industrial/steel/oil/media magnates, landed magnates, local magnates, political magnates. Register is formal, historical, or journalistic. In academic social history it pairs with gentry, elites, and patronage networks.
