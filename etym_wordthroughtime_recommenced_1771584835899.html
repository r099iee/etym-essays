- [16th century: enters English as 'to begin again'] - Carries the full weight of its Latinate architecture: formal, ceremonious, at home in law, scholarship, and grave narrative prose, where an action is not merely repeated but solemnly undertaken afresh.
- [18th-19th century: the language of measured narration] - The word of the formal novel and the official report: a restart performed with dignity, never haste; its formality signals that the resumed act is a deliberate undertaking rather than a reflex, and its tone in fiction is quiet and stately.
- [Contemporary sense] - Rarely used in everyday speech, where 'restart', 'resume', and 'take up again' have displaced it; 'recommence' now reads as stiff, deliberate, even faintly ironic - a ceremonious way of saying 'begin again', with an old-fashioned gravitas that can tip into pomposity.
