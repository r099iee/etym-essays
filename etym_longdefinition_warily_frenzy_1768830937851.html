warily — adverb: in a cautious, watchful, guarded manner; with alert awareness of possible danger. The word describes not timidity but heightened perception. In the card's context, Turjan steps aside warily because he has read the threat before a word passes — the woman's mouth is tight and white with anger, her eyes glow with a peculiar frenzy — and his body answers the perceived danger with controlled, vigilant movement. The step is not a retreat but a deliberate repositioning: the adverb frames it as an act of intelligence, the fighter's senses fully engaged. 'Warily' belongs to the register of watchful caution that pervades adventure and fantasy prose, in which experienced fighters read enemies by the tension of a mouth and the light in an eye.
  • cautiously — prudent and risk-averse in general; differs in not implying active scanning for a specific threat.
  • watchfully — keeping careful observation; differs in emphasizing the act of watching rather than the readiness to act on what is seen.
  • vigilantly — keeping strict, sustained watch; differs in suggesting long-enduring alertness rather than a single guarded move.
  • guardedly — speaking or acting with reserve; differs in stressing self-concealment and caution about what one reveals.
  • suspiciously — with active doubt of another's motives; differs in implying distrust of persons rather than awareness of danger in general.
  • gingerly — with delicate, careful handling; differs in describing the tender handling of objects or steps, not wariness of attack.
  • apprehensively — with fear or dread of what may come; differs in adding anxiety about the future, whereas warily is cool and composed.
  • nervously — with agitation and instability; differs in implying fear and unsteadiness, the opposite of controlled wariness.
  • charily — cautiously, frugally sparing; differs in stressing reluctance and economy of action or expenditure.
  • cannily — with shrewd, calculated prudence; differs in stressing cunning and self-interest rather than defense against danger.
  • cagily — with wary evasiveness; differs in implying slyness and reluctance to commit, more a politician's caution.
  • discreetly — with careful judgment and tact; differs in stressing prudence of speech and behavior rather than physical vigilance.
frenzy — noun: a state of wild excitement, agitation, rage, or temporary derangement in which the reason is overpowered. On the card, the woman's eyes 'glowed with a peculiar frenzy' — the word names a light of ungovernable passion burning behind the eye, a mind-fever too hot for reason to contain. The word's history is medical: in its earliest English use, frenzy was an actual disease, an inflammation of the brain producing delirium, mania, and hallucination, and even in modern use the clinical ghost remains — a frenzy is not mere anger but anger that has begun to look like temporary madness.
  • Sense 1 — clinical (archaic): delirium, mania, inflammation of the brain; 'a frenzy' = a fever of the mind.
  • Sense 2 — wild excitement: any fit of violent, uncontrolled activity or emotion: a feeding frenzy, a frenzy of buying, a frenzy of activity.
  • Sense 3 — rage / fury: anger so intense it overmasters the reason; 'in a frenzy of fury.'
  • Sense 4 — metaphorical combustion: passion as fire, a mind on fire; the poetical and rhetorical sense dominant in the 18th-19th centuries.
The semantic bridge from disease to mood is transparent: frenzy names the state in which the mind is overcome, whether by fever or by passion, and the medical origin supplies the word's characteristic intensity — it is reserved for emotion that has burned past the reach of reason, a blaze that no discipline can cool. This is precisely why the word is chosen for the woman's eyes: her fury has crossed into something resembling madness.
