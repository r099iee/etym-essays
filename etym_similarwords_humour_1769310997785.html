• Wit: Similar because both name the comic quality of speech; differs because wit is intellectual, sharp, and verbal — the rapid, pointed play of ideas — while humour is broader, warmer, and often more sympathetic.
  • Comedy: Similar because both name the amusing; differs because comedy is the genre or form of the comic (plays, films), while humour is the quality itself.
  • Joke: Similar because both name something amusing; differs because a joke is a specific comic utterance with a punchline, while humour is the general capacity and quality.
  • Jest: Similar because both name comic remarks; differs because jest is archaic and literary for a playful joke or sport, while humour is the modern general term.
  • Banter: Similar because both describe playful, amusing exchange; differs because banter is specifically light, teasing, good-humoured conversation between friends, while humour is any comic quality.
  • Facetiousness: Similar because both describe amusing lightness; differs because facetiousness is inappropriate or frivolous joking where seriousness is expected, while humour is a neutral or positive quality.
  • Comicality: Similar because both name the quality of being funny; differs because comicality stresses the absurd or laughable effect of a situation, while humour can be quiet and wry without being laughable.
  • Mood: Similar because both can name a temporary state of mind; differs because mood is the neutral, everyday word for a passing frame of feeling, while humour in this sense is formal and carries the historical medical flavour.
  • Temper: Similar because both can name a prevailing emotional state; differs because temper stresses the balance or control of feeling (often anger), while humour names the whole cast of mind and mood.
  • Temperament: Similar because both can name the constitutional cast of a person's nature; differs because temperament is the durable psychological constitution, while humour (in the mood sense) is more the passing or habitual frame of mind.
  • Disposition: Similar because both can name a person's natural frame of mind; differs because disposition is the settled character, while humour in Austen's usage is the sharper, more playful cast of temper.
  • Spirits: Similar because both name a prevailing emotional state ("in high spirits"); differs because spirits is plural and suggests energy and cheer, while humour is the general term for temper.
  • Whim: Similar because both can name a passing fancy; differs because whim is a sudden, arbitrary impulse, while humour (the "a humour" sense, now archaic) was a fixed temper or mood.
  • Caprice: Similar because both can name a changeable frame of mind; differs because caprice is unpredictable, impulsive changeableness, while humour is a state of temper, not a disposition to change.
  • Indulge (verb sense): Similar because both describe complying with someone's wishes; differs because indulge allows oneself or another anything pleasant without the goal of pacifying, while humour has the specific sense of going along to keep someone calm or happy.
  • Appease (verb sense): Similar because both describe pacifying someone; differs because appease suggests buying off a threat or yielding to pressure, while humour is a gentler, more indulgent compliance.
  • Pacify (verb sense): Similar because both describe calming someone; differs because pacify aims to end conflict or agitation, while humour aims to keep a mood agreeable.
  • Placate (verb sense): Similar because both describe soothing someone; differs because placate is conciliatory in the face of anger or complaint, while humour is indulgent of whim or mood.
  • Gratify (verb sense): Similar because both describe pleasing someone; differs because gratify satisfies a desire for its own sake, while humour satisfies to manage the person's mood.
  • Fluid (etymological sense): Similar because both name a liquid, and humour literally means "fluid" in Latin; differs because fluid is the general modern word for any liquid, while humour in the anatomical sense survives only in the aqueous and vitreous humours of the eye.
