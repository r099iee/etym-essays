- c. 1300: "percen" borrowed from Old French percier = to make a hole in, to thrust through; concrete, physical.
- 14th–15th centuries: penetration extended to sound, light, cold, weapons ("pierce the ranks"); figurative "to pierce the heart" = to affect deeply; perception: sharp, forceful, decisive.
- 16th–17th centuries: intellectual sense: "piercing wit", "to pierce a mystery" — the mind penetrates obscurity; perception: keen, acute, admirable.
- 18th–19th centuries: "piercing" (adjective) = of cold, cries, eyes: intense, penetrating; technical piercing of metal and leather; perception: sensory, industrial.
- 20th century: body piercing as countercultural practice (1960s–70s subcultures) becomes mainstream by the 1990s; "a piercing" is a common noun; perception: fashionable, rebellious-then-normalized.
- Today: "pierce" remains both everyday (needle, ear) and epic (Tolkien's "he could not pierce their ranks"); figurative uses (piercing silence, piercing look) are standard literary currency.
