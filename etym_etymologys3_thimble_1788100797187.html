The word "thimble" is a small fossil of English domestic history. Its story runs from an Indo-European root for swelling, through the Anglo-Saxon thumb, to the seamstress's finger-cap of the modern sewing box, and it records a slow drift of both sound and meaning: the original instrument covered the thumb alone; the modern word covers any finger, yet still says "thumb" in its bones.

1. The Indo-European root: *teuə-, "to swell". Behind "thimble" stands the PIE root *teuə- (also written *teuh₂-), "to swell, grow strong, be fat". From it come Sanskrit táviṣī ("strength"), Greek τύλος ("callus, swelling"), Latin tumēre ("to swell") and tumor, and across Germanic a family of words for the stout, swollen, or prominent: German Daumen ("thumb"), Dutch duim, and English thumb, thigh (the "swollen" joint), tumour, and thousand (a "great" many, a swollen hundred). The thumb, in this ancient way of seeing, is simply "the stout one" of the hand — the thick, strong, prominent digit — and the Germanic peoples named it exactly that. The same swelling-root later fed the whole medico-vernacular family of "tumour", "tumescence", and "turnip"-like words of plumpness, so that the seamstress's tiny tool is ultimately kin to the tumour: both are things that have swollen.

2. Proto-Germanic *þūmô and Old English þūma. The Germanic word for the thumb, *þūmô (a weak noun of the "swollen" family), yields Old English þūma (modern thumb), attested from the earliest English records; the same base appears in the derived verb þȳmian, "to press with the thumb". The Anglo-Saxons already knew the sewing tool: the earliest English thimbles of bone and bronze, and the word for them, appear together in the 10th century, and the word sits in the manuscript record beside nædl ("needle") and spinl ("spindle") — the working vocabulary of the women's textile economy that formed the heart of Anglo-Saxon household production.

3. Old English þȳmel: the born word, c. 1000. The Old English name for the tool was þȳmel (also spelt þȳmel, þȳmyl), formed from þūma plus the Old English instrumental suffix -el — the same forming-element that gives handle ("thing held"), sickle ("thing that cuts"), shovel ("thing that shoves"). þȳmel therefore means literally "thing for the thumb" or "thumb-cover". Note the umlaut: the ū of þūma becomes ȳ in þȳmel because of the following -il- i-mutation, a sound change that marks the derivation's antiquity — the word was formed in the living Old English of the earliest period, not borrowed from any foreign source. The tool and its name are thus thoroughly native: unlike "fever" (a Latin loan) or "steeple" (built on a native adjective), the thimble is an original English formation, a word the Anglo-Saxons coined for themselves out of "thumb".

4. Middle English thymel → thimble: sound and spelling. In Middle English the word appears as thymel, thimbel, thymbil from the 13th–14th centuries, now firmly the seamstress's finger-stall of brass or latten. Pronunciation shifted as the Old English ȳ (a high front rounded vowel) unrounded to i, giving the modern vowel. The spelling with -b-, first appearing in the 15th–16th centuries, is a scribal and etymologising intrusion: writers who knew the "thumb" connection restored a b by analogy with thumb, whose own b was already silent in pronunciation. Where "thumb" kept the silent b, however, "thimble" took a different path: its restored b came to be spoken, so that modern thimble /ˈθɪmbəl/ has a real /b/ while thumb /θʌm/ does not — a telling example of spelling changing pronunciation itself, a century's worth of ABC-book English folding back into the spoken word.

5. Semantic history: from thumb-stall to every finger. The meaning shifted early and quietly. Although the name says "thumb", by the Middle English period the thimble was worn on various fingers, and the word simply generalised to "finger-protector for sewing". More interesting is the word's social career. Workshops of the late medieval towns made thimbles in quantity — the brass-working city of Nuremberg became the great thimble foundry of Europe, shipping dimpled caps across the continent — while in the 16th–17th centuries the goldsmith's trade produced thimbles of silver and gold as courtly and sentimental gifts. The domestic emblem of thrift acquired, in the 18th–19th centuries, a sentimental and finally erotic charge: the decorated thimble as a love-token appears in fiction and folk custom, and the "thimbleful" (early 17th century) made the tiny cap a standard minimiser for strong drink. In 19th-century street slang the thimble became the dishonest pea-hider of "thimble-rigging", and in nautical usage it named a rope-eye protector — both senses preserving the ancient logic of a hard shell defending something soft.

6. The modern word. Today "thimble" is one of those rare English words whose form has barely moved in a millennium: a thousand years separate þȳmel from "thimble", yet the change is chiefly the loss of the umlaut vowel and the intrusion of a single letter b. The tool itself — dimpled metal cap, thumb-sized, worn while sewing — is recognisably the same object the Anglo-Saxon seamstress used, and the word still carries, hidden in its etymology, the thumb it once exclusively covered: a living clue to how a people named the things they held, and how a tiny cap of brass kept its ancient name through the sewing boxes of a thousand years.
