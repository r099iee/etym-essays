Wilde's lions have "eyes like green beryls": beryl is the mineral whose finest green variety is the emerald. Scientifically, beryl is beryllium aluminium cyclosilicate (Be₃Al₂Si₆O₁₈), crystallising in hexagonal prisms, hard at 7.5–8 on Mohs' scale, and tough enough for daily-wear jewellery. It is one of the world's great gem families, coloured by traces of other metals: pure beryl is colourless ("goshenite"); chromium or vanadium turns it green — the emerald; iron makes it sea-blue — the aquamarine; manganese and caesium yield pink (morganite); iron in other proportions gives golden-yellow heliodor; and a rare red beryl is mined in Utah. The "beryl" of antiquity and of Pliny was a sea-green or blue-green stone from India — precisely the colour of Wilde's lion eyes.

Ancient and biblical career. The Sanskrit word vaiḍūrya named a prized stone associated with the cat's-eye; the word travelled west through Middle Indian, Greek (bēryllos) and Latin (beryllus). Pliny (Natural History 37.20) locates beryls in India, describes their sea-green tint, and notes that the fine long crystals were cut into cylinders and drilled for signet rings, necklaces and earrings — a reminder that "beryl" began as a gem name, not a mineral species. Greek Bible versions identified beryl with the stone tarshish — the stone of Ezekiel's vision of the heavenly wheels — and the Revelation of John gives beryl as the eighth foundation-stone of the New Jerusalem, so that the gem glitters in Scripture from the Old Covenant to the Apocalypse.

Modern science reorganises the word. In 1798 Nicolas-Louis Vauquelin, analysing beryl and emerald together, found an unknown "earth" in both and named it glucina — "sweet" — from the sweet taste of its salts; in 1828 Friedrich Wöhler (and independently A. A. B. Bussy) isolated the metal and named it beryllium. Beryl ore has since become the world's principal source of beryllium, a light, stiff, non-corrosive, non-sparking metal on which aerospace alloys, X-ray windows, nuclear moderators, space telescopes and precision instruments all depend; occupational exposure to beryllium dust can cause the chronic lung disease berylliosis. The mineralogists' beryl is thus simultaneously the jeweller's emerald and the engineer's ore of a strategic metal — one of the rare gem names that is also an industrial commodity.

Today's senses. (1) As a mineral species name — the whole beryllium-silicate family, from emerald to aquamarine to morganite; (2) as the antique gem sense — the greenish, "sea-coloured" stone of ancient jewellery, engraved by Roman craftsmen; (3) as a loose literary word for any green or blue-green precious stone — "beryl eyes" is a standing poetic topos for glittering green eyes; (4) in older English, also any clear crystal or a diviner's "beryl" (a scrying ball), stone and glass having blurred together in medieval optics. The plural beryls is the ordinary plural of the noun and works both countably (cut stones — "two beryls") and collectively (the mineral — "eyes like green beryls"). In the gem trade today "beryl" alone usually means the yellowish-green or colourless grades; the star members of the family travel under their own names — emerald and aquamarine.

  • Family: emerald (green), aquamarine (blue), morganite (pink), heliodor (yellow), goshenite (colourless), red beryl.
  • Uses: gemstones (aquamarine is the March birthstone, emerald the May birthstone); ore of beryllium; industrial metal.
  • Literary: "eyes like green beryls" — the glittering sea-green of the ancient Indian stone.
