Dreary is a word with blood on it. It descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰrews-, "to fall, to drop, to drip, to break apart, to crumble", which in the Germanic world produced a whole family of falling verbs: Gothic driusan, Old Saxon driosan, Old High German triosan and Old English drēosan, all meaning "to fall" — of rain, of leaves, and above all of warriors in battle, who fall in death. From drēosan Old English made the noun drēor, "gore, spilt blood", literally that which has fallen, the blood dropped to the ground, and from drēor came the adjective drēorig. In Anglo-Saxon, therefore, drēorig meant, with awful concreteness, "gory, blood-stained": a drēorig field was a field of blood. But already in Old English the word wore a second face, "sad, sorrowful, dejected", for the grief of a lord who has watched his retainers fall is itself a kind of bleeding, and in the elegies of the Anglo-Saxons the two senses, physical gore and emotional grief, were never far apart. The Wanderer and the Seafarer and the great battle-poems move constantly between the two registers of drēorig, and the word's double life is one of the most moving details of the Old English lexicon: the same sound names the blood on the field and the grief in the breast, because to the Anglo-Saxon poet the one was felt through the other. Middle English inherited the word in the thirteenth century as drery with the bloody sense already fading. The sadness remained, and by the fourteenth century the word meant "sorrowful, dismal, gloomy" with no trace of gore. This is the crucial first transfer: drēorig had named a person's condition (the gory, grief-stricken warrior), and drery still named a person's condition (the sorrowful soul), with no suggestion of landscape or weather. The great modern shift came in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when dreary moved from describing a person's grief to describing a place's atmosphere. A landscape, a road, a sky could now be dreary, cheerless, dull and monotonous, the moods of the mind transferred wholesale to the scene. Poets prized the clipped form drear, a back-formation of the seventeenth century (1620s) which versifiers kept alive for centuries; the noun dreariness consolidated the word's hold on the description of weather, that most dreary of English subjects, and it is even possible that English drizzle belongs to the same ancient family of falling. Key stages and shifts:
  • PIE *dʰrews-: "to fall, to drip, to crumble" — a root of downward motion and disintegration.
  • Old English drēosan (8th c.): "to fall", of rain, leaves and warriors — the root's Germanic shape.
  • Old English drēorig: "gory, blood-stained" and "sad, sorrowful" — the double sense of the battlefield and the grieving heart.
  • Middle English drery (c. 1300): the gory sense fades; the word means "sorrowful, dismal, gloomy", a mood of the person.
  • 16th–17th century: the word transfers from persons to places; a landscape, road or sky becomes dreary.
  • Modern: dreary is the grey, wearying, colourless word for draining weather, scenes and lives; nothing of the battle-plain's gore survives.
Socio-cultural factors: the shift from blood to greyness tracks a change in how English speakers experienced melancholy. The Old English world felt grief as physical wounding; the early modern world, increasingly domestic and urban, found its characteristic melancholy not in the battlefield but in the weather and the landscape, the long wet afternoon, the empty road, the closed-in horizon. Dreary is that shift in a single word. In the context sentence from The Hobbit, Tolkien uses the landscape sense at the very moment the comfortable Shire gives way to the wild: "Not far ahead were dreary hills, rising higher and higher, dark with trees." The word charges the land with depression and menace — hills should be places of heath and wind, but these are dark with trees, and their relentless climbing tells the reader the road is about to lead upward into danger. The word's whole history, from battlefield gore to the grey of the hills, is compressed into that one adjective.
