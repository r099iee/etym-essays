A word of the weaving-house Tassels belongs to a small but important class of English words that come not from the Indo-European family tree but from the Mediterranean world of trade and textiles. Its proposed ultimate source is a Semitic etymon, *tistala, a word for a knotted hanging fringe or tassel, belonging to the rich Semitic vocabulary of knotting, plaiting, and tying that produced the ornamental fringes of carpets, curtains, and ceremonial dress. Because the word is a loan from the Semitic world, it has no Proto-Indo-European ancestry at all, and the careful etymologist must resist every temptation to construct a PIE root for it: the correct procedure is to trace its actual historical path, from the Semitic base through the Romance languages into French, and from French into English. The ornament itself — a gathered bunch of threads, knotted at one end and left to hang free — is one of the oldest pieces of textile decoration in the Near East, and its name travelled with the cloth. The attested historical trail begins in Old French, where tassel is recorded from the twelfth century with two related senses: “a tassel, a fringe, a hem” and “a fastening, a clasp”. The two meanings are one idea seen from two sides: the knotted bundle of threads is simultaneously the decoration that terminates a cord and the little tie that fastens a mantle — the tassel as clasp and the tassel as fringe are the same object doing two jobs. From Old French the word passed into English about 1300, at first chiefly in the fastening sense, “a clasp, a mantle-fastener”, which is why the earliest English records include the occupational surname Tasseler, “maker of tassels”. By the late fourteenth century the sense had settled on the ornament: “a pendent ornament, in the usual form of a hanging bunch of small cords”. Medieval Latin had already taken the word in as tassellus, a textile and ecclesiastical term, so the word circulated through the clergy, the courts, and the workshops of the passementiers at the same time. The doubtful Latin theory The older dictionaries offered a neat Latin derivation that the OED has since doubted. The theory ran that tassel came from Latin taxillus, the diminutive of talus, “ankle, knucklebone” — taxillus meaning “a small die or cube” — on the idea that a tassel resembles a little die swinging on a cord. The OED's judgment, in the great 1989 edition, is that this is doubtful: the semantic link between a small die and a bunch of threads is weak, and the forms do not line up convincingly. The OED notes instead the English variant “tossel”, and an association with the verb toss — a folk-association prompted by the tassel's swaying motion, which anyone who has watched a tassel swing can understand. The honest position is that the deeper origin of the word is unknown; the Semitic base *tistala remains a plausible but unconfirmed hypothesis, and the word must be described as of obscure origin rather than forced into a false ancestry. The career of the ornament The ornament itself has a far longer history than its English name. Tassels and fringed cords appear in the textile record of ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, on the shawls and mantles of the Assyrian court; in the Hebrew Bible, the tzitzit — the fringes and blue cords on the corners of the garment — are a religious command that preserves the ancient Near-Eastern tassel as a sign of remembrance; and the tassel moved through the Byzantine and Islamic worlds as the standard finish of imperial silks and saddle-cloths. In China, the tassel finished the cords and girdles of court dress — precisely the role it plays in the novel's description of Bao-yu's court girdle, whose elaborate knotwork terminates in long tassels, the costume's moving last word. In Europe, the tassel became the ornament of the church and the court: the tasselled baldachin, the fringed and tasselled upholstery of the baroque palace, the tasselled academic hood and cap. The modern tassel In the modern world the tassel has two famous careers. The first is academic: the graduation tassel on the mortarboard, worn on the right and moved to the left at the conferral of a degree, is a nineteenth-century American institutionalisation of the old ceremonial ornament, and it remains the word's most public modern use. The second is botanical: in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word was transferred to the tassel-like inflorescence of the maize plant — the pendant male flower cluster at the top of the cornstalk, which sheds its pollen onto the silks below. The transfer is a beautiful instance of the eye finding the same shape in two worlds: the maize tassel hangs and sways exactly as the weaver's tassel does, and the language simply gave the plant the ornament's name. Between the academic cap and the cornfield, the tassel also still sways on curtains, cushions, lampshades, key chains, and ceremonial dress — a small, ancient, and remarkably persistent ornament, whose English name we cannot fully explain but whose history reaches back to the first weavers who knotted threads at the end of a cord. Social context Finally, the social history of the tassel is a history of rank made visible. Because tassels cost labour and silk, they have always marked status: the fringe and the tassel distinguished the priest, the noble, and the graduate from the plain-dressed ordinary man. The novel's court girdle — coloured silks, elaborate knotwork, long tassels — is exactly such a signal: in the Jia household, even the display of rank is finished with the weaver's most ancient ornament, and the tassels that terminate the girdle are the costume's way of saying that this is a house where even the ends of things are beautifully made. The word that we cannot trace to a root has nonetheless traced, in its long career, the whole geography of luxury: from the Near-Eastern fringe, through the French and English workshops, to the academic cap, the curtain, and the cornstalk.
