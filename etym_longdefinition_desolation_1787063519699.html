Desolation is a noun naming (1) the condition or state of a place that has been destroyed, emptied, and abandoned — devastation, ruin, a barren and deserted waste — and (2) the profound sense of abandonment, emptiness, and loneliness that such a place, or any great loss, produces in a person. It holds together a landscape and a feeling: the desolation of a blasted plain is the visible form of the desolation of the heart. The word is high-register and literary, at home in scripture, epic, and serious prose, and it carries an emotional weight that plainer words like destruction or ruin do not.

In Tolkien's The Hobbit, the Company arrives at the Desolation of the Dragon — the blasted, burned, empty land that Smaug's depredations have left around the Lonely Mountain. The capitalised phrase functions almost as a proper name, a place that is defined not by what it contains but by what it has lost: it is desolate because the dragon has emptied it. This usage crystallises the word's double structure: a physical waste and an abandonment, with the dragon's ruinous presence felt in the emptiness.

Primary senses

  • Devastation, ruin, laying waste (state of place): the condition of a region or building that has been destroyed, emptied of inhabitants, and left waste. E.g. the desolation of the war-torn city, a land of desolation, the Desolation of the Dragon.
  • A desolated region (countable): a place that is waste, barren, and deserted; a wilderness of ruins. It was a desolation = the place itself was a waste. The countable use names the blasted landscape directly.
  • Grief-stricken abandonment, lonely misery (state of person): a feeling of profound loneliness and desertion, as of one abandoned in an empty world. E.g. a sense of desolation after a loss; the desolation of bereavement. This is the emotional sense, long used in religious writing for the state of the soul.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Religious and biblical use: in the Bible, desolation renders Hebrew terms for ruined cities and wastes and the divine judgement that leaves them empty; the abomination of desolation (Daniel, Matthew) names a profanity that pollutes the holy place and leaves it spiritually waste. The word thus carries an ancient theological freight of judgement and exile.
  • Literary and poetic use: Romantic and Victorian writers made desolation a keynote of the sublime — the ruined abbey, the empty moor, the waste land. It names the awe and grief of standing where life once was and is no more.
  • Ecological and landscape sense: in modern writing, desolation can describe barren, uninhabitable terrain — desert, polar waste, lava fields, land laid waste by fire or industry — with the sense of emptiness that makes a place feel not merely empty but emptied.
  • Emotional metaphor: the interior desolation of depression, grief, or abandonment is a standard metaphor in psychology and literature; the word transfers the blasted landscape to the inner life.

Grammar and register

  • Desolation is a mass noun in its state sense (desolation spread across the land) and a count noun in its place sense (a howling desolation, the northern desolations).
  • Register is formal and literary. It is rare in casual speech, where devastation, ruin, emptiness, or loneliness usually appear instead; desolation is chosen when the writer wants the full weight of abandonment.
  • It is morphologically a deverbal abstract noun formed with the Latin suffix -ation (state or result of an action), from the verb to desolate (to lay waste, abandon). The adjective desolate and the verb desolate are the older and simpler members of the family.

Collocations

  • scene/land of desolation — a ruined, empty place.
  • utter/complete desolation — intensified emptiness.
  • the Desolation of ... — a proper-name phrase for a ruined region (as Tolkien's Desolation of the Dragon).
  • a sense/feeling of desolation — the emotional sense.
  • in desolation — in a state of ruin or grief.

Semantic relations

  • Near-synonyms: devastation, ruin, wasteland, waste, wilderness, barrenness, solitude, isolation, loneliness; each differs in emphasis (see similarWords).
  • Antonyms: prosperity, abundance, habitation, life, cheer, comfort, company. Desolation is the negative of a settled, flourishing, inhabited life.
  • Related words: desolate (adjective and verb), desolately, desolator (one who lays waste).
