- [15th century: from the shellfish] - 'Scallop' names the fan-shelled mollusc, sacred as the pilgrim's badge of St James; the adjective 'scalloped' first means armed or decorated with scallop shells themselves.
- [16th-18th century: the shell edge] - The word shifts to describe any edge or border cut in a series of rounded curves like the shell's fluted rim; connotation is ornamental and refined - a decorative finish on cloth, wood, and pastry.
- [18th-19th century: the cookery sense] - 'Scalloped' extends to food baked in a scallop shell or a shell-shaped dish (scalloped potatoes, scalloped oysters); connotation is cosy and domestic, a second life far from the sea.
- [Contemporary sense] - Primarily a decorative adjective for semicircular edges - hems, borders, frills - and a cookery term for baked shell-shaped dishes; connotation is ornamental and gently feminine, the soft, wavy geometry of a scallop shell repeated in cloth and food.
