Alters is the inflected present-tense form of the verb alter, and its biography is the biography of a Latin adjective of 'otherness' that slowly grew into English's standard formal verb of change. The story runs from an Indo-European pronoun-root of distance and otherness, through Republican Rome, medieval France, and fourteenth-century English translation, and finally into modern legal, technical, and psychological vocabulary. It is a textbook case of how a word about being 'other' becomes a word about making things different. 1. The Indo-European root: otherness as a primal concept The chain opens with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *h₂el-, with the core meaning 'beyond, other.' This root supplied a family of 'other' and 'elsewhere' words across the whole family: Greek allos 'other,' Latin alius 'other' and alienus 'foreign' (source of English alien), Gothic aljis 'other,' and English else (from *alj- 'other'). What makes the root secure is its spread: nearly every early Indo-European branch preserves a member meaning 'other,' and that consistency is the strongest available evidence that 'otherness' was a basic relational concept in the ancestral language. From *h₂el- Latin builds the contrastive pronominal adjective alter — literally the combination of the 'other' base with the suffix *-tero-, which marks one of a pair or set. Alter therefore means 'the other of two,' the second of two contrasted items, and it is precisely this 'making into the other' logic that the verb will eventually exploit. 2. Republican Rome: alter and the birth of alterāre Latin alter is classical and well attested from early Latin comedy onward; Plautus and Terence use it constantly in the everyday sense 'the other, the second.' The step from adjective to verb is a post-classical and medieval one: Latin alterāre, 'to make other, to change,' is built directly on the adjective with the standard causative verb-suffix -āre. The verb is found in late and medieval Latin, where alterare has the straightforward sense of turning something into its other state — the same transparent formation that produces English make-other compounds. Its logic deserves emphasis: when you alter a thing you do not destroy it; you shift it to its other version, which is why alteration has always implied modification rather than replacement. This is the deepest semantic bridge of the whole word family, and it is also the reason the verb remains suitable for gentle as well as drastic changes. 3. French mediation and the corrupting twist Through the twelfth century Old French forms the verb alterer, borrowing the medieval Latin verb directly. Old and Middle French largely preserve the core sense 'to change,' but the word develops an interesting secondary nuance: alterer can mean 'to impair, to debase, to falsify' — to change a substance dishonestly, as when wine or coin is 'altered' or adulterated. This 'corrupting' branch matters because it shows how a neutral verb of change can tilt negative when the change is unauthorized or for the worse. English, as we shall see, takes the word without fully importing this negative tilt, but the nuance lingers in the family and resurfaces in contexts of tampering. 4. Middle English: a translation word of the 1300s Middle English borrows alteren in the later fourteenth century, in the great wave of Latinate and French lexis that accompanied biblical translation and courtly literature. The verb appears in religious and moral prose with the sense 'to change, transform, make different,' typically of dispositions, laws, or states of affairs. Its arrival coincides with English's deliberate enrichment from Latin models, and the word enters as the formal, bookish partner of the native change. By the sixteenth century alter is fully naturalized: Shakespeare uses it of fortune, nature, and opinion, and alteration becomes the standard noun for any act or result of change, from the alteration of a garment to the alteration of a kingdom's course. 5. From Renaissance formality to modern technical reach Modern English retains alter as a neutral-to-formal verb of modification and greatly widens its fields. In tailoring and dressmaking, to alter is to reshape a garment for a better fit — the professional, craftsman sense memorably exploited by the card's mnemonic of a tailor adjusting a jacket. In law and administration, alteration names changes to documents, plans, and structures. In computing, ALTER is a standard SQL command for modifying a database object's structure, a precise extension of the 'change without replacement' core. In psychology and psychiatry, the mid-twentieth century produces the noun alter for a distinct personality state in dissociative identity disorder — literally 'an other self' — which is the same Latin 'otherness' radicalized into the notion of a separate identity. 6. The third-person present form in context The form alters is the ordinary third-person singular present: he alters, it alters, news alters. In Tolkien's passage the form reports a standing effect — 'this news alters them much for the better' — and the sentence's value judgement ('for the better') is supplied by context, not by the verb, which remains strictly neutral about improvement. The choice of alter over change here is a register choice: the speech is a council in the elevated, deliberative mode of heroic narrative, and a Latinate verb of revision suits the tone of sober recalculation better than the plainer native change. 7. False friends and near neighbours Two traps deserve explicit mention. English altar, 'the table of sacrifice or worship,' is a homophone of alter but is etymologically unrelated (Latin altāre, 'high place,' from altus 'high'), so the two words are a classic spelling hazard. And alteration, alternative, alternate, and altruism all descend from the same Latin alter/alius 'other' family, whereas altercation 'a quarrel' comes from a different Latin verb altercārī 'to dispute' — similar-looking, but not part of the 'make other' lineage. Keeping these apart preserves the clean single line of descent from otherness to change.
