Roscius is a name with two lives, and the two have been entangled since antiquity. One belongs to a country landowner caught in the machinery of Sulla's proscriptions; the other to the most celebrated actor of the Roman stage. Both are genuine, and the confusion between them is itself part of the word's history.

A gentilicium of the Roman Republic

Roman citizens of the upper orders ordinarily carried three names: a praenomen, a nomen and a cognomen. The nomen was the clan name, inherited by all members of a gens and, in the classical period, ending typically in -ius. Roscius fits that pattern exactly, and the family it names — the gens Roscia — belonged to the municipal aristocracy of Umbria rather than to the old Roman senate. The Roscii of Ameria were equestrians, men of property and local influence, the sort of family that appears in history only when a crisis reaches it. That is precisely what happened in 81 BCE.

  • Ameria, modern Amelia, lies in southern Umbria and was an old Umbrian town with a Roman citizenship grant before the Social War; its leading families were locally powerful and socially ambitious.
  • The name is attested in the feminine Roscia and in the derived adjective Roscianus, the ordinary machinery of Latin onomastics.
  • Because the trial speech was published, the name of a otherwise obscure equestrian became one of the best documented in Republican prosopography.

The trial and its afterlife

The facts reconstructed from Cicero's speech are these: the elder Roscius was killed in Rome, his name was retrospectively added to the proscription lists, his estate was sold cheaply to Chrysogonus, and his son was then accused of the murder by men who had bought parts of the property. The prosecution's case was less a legal argument than a manoeuvre to silence the only person who could reclaim the estate. Cicero, twenty-six years old and without a criminal reputation, took the case and won it. The speech he published is our principal source for the episode and one of the earliest surviving examples of Roman forensic oratory in full.

Cicero later judged his own performance harshly. In the Brutus he says that the speeches of his youth, including the Rosciana, were exuberant and over-coloured, too full of the Asian manner he had learned from his teachers. That self-criticism is the first stage in the name's literary afterlife: Rosciana, “the speech for Roscius”, became a school text and a byword for youthful forensic daring. Renaissance editors, Jesuit schoolmasters and nineteenth-century commentators all used the case to teach the ethics of advocacy — the defending of an innocent man against a powerful faction.

  • The speech is a key document for the mechanics of proscription: how names were added after death, how confiscated property was sold, and how purchasers acquired legal cover.
  • It is also a document of Cicero's caution: he names Chrysogonus repeatedly but never directly indicts Sulla, and the jury's own vulnerability is part of his argument.
  • Legal historians cite it for the procedure of the quaestio de sicariis and for the horror of the poena cullei.

The actor and the English byword

Quintus Roscius Gallus, born about 126 BCE, was a comic actor of such skill that Cicero and Sulla both cultivated him; Sulla granted him the gold ring of equestrian rank, and Cicero defended him in a partnership dispute. Roman writers treat him as the standard of theatrical excellence, and his name passed into modern languages as a compliment. In English the usage is recorded from the seventeenth century and flourishes in the eighteenth: Garrick is “the English Roscius”, and the phrase “a Roscius” can be applied to any outstanding performer. This is a genuine semantic development of the name — from proper noun to allusive common noun — although it is literary and now archaic.

What the name does not tell us

The etymology of Roscius is unknown, and the uncertainty should be stated plainly rather than papered over. The two Latin words that sound closest are rūscus, the butcher's broom, and russus, “red”; neither provides a derivational path to Roscius, and the -sc- cluster points instead to a stem of local, possibly Umbrian or Etruscan, shape. Roman gentilicia were frequently taken from place names, and a municipal family of Ameria may simply have borne a name that was old in that region. The popular gloss “reddish” is a modern guess based on resemblance, not a lexicographic fact.

Modern reference

Today the name is confined to three contexts: the trial of 80 BCE, the actor, and the history of the English stage. “The Roscius case” is shorthand among legal historians for the prosecution of an innocent man for profit under a revolutionary regime; “the English Roscius” survives in theatre history; and the bare name in a classical text almost always means Sextus, unless the context is comedy. The word has no productive modern use, but it remains a small hinge on which the early career of Cicero turns.
