enraptured: the past participle of the verb enrapture, meaning "to fill with intense delight, to transport with joy, to carry away into a state of rapture." As a participial adjective it describes a person utterly charmed, swept up, or overwhelmed by pleasure, beauty, or joy — "an enraptured audience," "enraptured at the prospect." The word sits at the top of the scale of delight: it implies a pleasure so strong that it momentarily carries the mind away, overriding calm judgment. Its root image is of being "carried off" — snatched up and transported by emotion.

PRIMARY SENSE — filled with intense delight (participial adjective)

In modern use enraptured describes a person (or a face, gaze, or attention) filled with, or possessed by, keen delight:

  • Enraptured by / with + source — "the young audience was enraptured by the magician's stunning performance," "he was enraptured with the landscape."
  • Enraptured at + prospect — "enraptured at the prospect of doing a good turn for a mandarin" (context card): the delight is caused by the anticipation of something desired.
  • Attributive use — "an enraptured expression," "enraptured eyes," "an enraptured crowd": the person's whole bearing shows the transport.
  • Grammatical form — as a past participle it can follow "was/were" to make the passive ("she was enraptured by the music") or act purely as an adjective; the verb enrapture means "to throw into rapture, to delight intensely."

SECONDARY AND REGISTER USES

  • Literary and devotional — the word retains a slightly elevated, literary register; in older religious writing it named a state of spiritual transport, the soul carried toward the divine.
  • Aesthetic transport — music, art, nature, and performance are the classic causes: "the audience sat enraptured through the symphony."
  • Romantic ecstasy — "he was enraptured by her beauty": the word is a strong, romantic, somewhat bookish way to say "delighted."
  • Ironized use — because the word implies being carried away beyond rational control, it can be used with irony, as in the context card, where Feng Su's "enraptured" state is not spiritual but sycophantic — a greedy, blinded delight at the prospect of favour.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES

  • vs delighted / pleased / charmed — moderate, fully conscious states that leave reason intact; enraptured is far more intense and momentarily overwhelms rational awareness.
  • vs entranced / spellbound — these stress a passive, hypnotic, trance-like absorption that freezes attention; enraptured stresses an active, joyful sweep of emotion.
  • vs ecstatic — similar intensity, but ecstatic often implies an outward, visible explosion of frantic joy; enraptured implies a deeper, more inward being carried away.
  • vs captivated — attention held and won; enraptured floods the whole emotional state, not just attention.
  • vs happy / glad — ordinary, everyday contentment; enraptured is a peak state of exceptional transport.

HISTORICAL BRIDGES AND ETYMOLOGICAL RESONANCE

The word is built on rapture, from Latin raptus, "snatched away, carried off" — the past participle of rapere, "to seize, carry off by force." The deepest sense is of being physically snatched and carried away. Christian theology took this vocabulary of violent abduction for the experience of divine transport: the prophet or saint seized by a vision was raptus, "carried off" into heaven. English coined enrapture in the early seventeenth century as "to put into rapture," and enraptured followed immediately. Every modern use of the word — the enraptured audience, the enraptured gaze — carries the buried image of abduction: delight so powerful it seizes the person and carries them out of their ordinary self.
