Note: The historical evolution of ache and its participle aching features one of the most famous orthographic and phonological anomalies in English lexicography. In Old and Middle English, the verb and noun followed a regular Germanic grammatical alternation: the verb was a strong verb acan (pronounced with a hard velar stop /k/, modern /eɪk/), while the noun was derived with front mutation as æċe (pronounced with an affricate /tʃ/, rhyming with the letter 'H'). In Shakespeare's era, this phonological distinction was strictly preserved: the noun was pronounced 'aitch' (generating famous Elizabethan puns, as in Much Ado About Nothing and The Tempest), while the verb was spelled ake. In the 18th century, lexicographer Dr. Samuel Johnson mistakenly believed that the word was of Ancient Greek origin, derived from ἄχος (achos, "grief, pain"), and erroneously established the Greek-style pseudo-etymological spelling ache for both noun and verb, while the /k/ pronunciation of the verb permanently displaced the /tʃ/ of the noun.
