Single root word: PORK. The chain runs from an Indo-European word for the young pig, through Latin and the French of the Norman kitchen, into Middle English as a culinary noun. Proto-Indo-European *porḱos "young pig, piglet; pig" (reconstructed base; ~4500–3000 BCE; widely attested in the western Indo-European branches — Italic, Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic; its deeper etymology is debated, some connecting it with a root meaning "digging," because pigs root in the earth, but that link is speculative) → Latin porcus "a pig, a hog, a swine" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE; the ordinary classical word for the pig, in contrast with sus, the older generic term) → Old French / Anglo-Norman porc "a pig; also the flesh of the pig" (attested from the 9th–11th c.; the French word continues both the animal and its meat) → Middle English pork / porc "the flesh of the pig used as food" (recorded from c. 1300; the culinary restriction develops as the Norman table-word is taken into English) → Modern English PORK (mass noun; the culinary flesh of the pig, and head of dish-compounds such as pork pie, pork chop). Note on debate: the Germanic reflexes of *porḱos — Old English fearh "young pig," whence English farrow "a litter of pigs," and German Ferkel "piglet" — are sister outcomes of the same Indo-European root, not ancestors of the English culinary noun; the direct line to English pork runs Latin → French → Middle English. The deeper origin of *porḱos is nuanced: many scholars associate the pig-words of the western branches with a base meaning "to dig or root," since pigs grub in the soil, but the equation is not securely established, and the root may equally be of substrate origin within the farming vocabulary of western Europe. The restriction of the English word to the culinary sense is a sociolinguistic narrowing (Norman table-language over native animal-words), not a change in the word's form.
