Hawthorn is the common English name for the thorny shrubs and small trees of the genus Crataegus, family Rosaceae (the rose family), which bear deeply lobed leaves, dense clusters of white or occasionally pink five-petalled flowers opening in late spring — the celebrated "May blossom" — and small, round, red fruit called haws. In Britain the common hawthorn, Crataegus monogyna, is the classic hedgerow plant, grown for centuries as the living fence of fields, and it also grows wild as a sturdy, craggy little tree on chalk downs, in scrub, and at woodland edges. Its hard wood, long sharp thorns, and dense branching habit made it the stock material of English hedges, and that very function is fossilised in its name, which literally means "hedge-thorn". In the Tolkien passage, Bilbo scans the bare hillside for something to eat and finds that the blackberries are still only in flower and there are no nuts, "not even hawthorn-berries". The word therefore sounds a precise note of autumnal promise denied: the haws, though edible and long used as famine food, have not yet formed, and even that humble, sour red berry is unavailable to the starving party. Hawthorn thus carries in this context the double weight of the country calendar — the season's slow, reliable fruitfulness — and of its failure for a traveller in desperate need, so the noun quietly measures how near starvation the company has come. Literary and rural associations run deep. In English tradition the hawthorn in May is the emblem of springtime and village festivity, the tree of May Day garlands and country dances; the white blossom whitens whole hedgerows in a single week. Its dense, thorny growth also made it the practical boundary plant of the English landscape, and its folklore is rich: it is the fairy tree, associated with wells and with supernatural danger, and bringing its blossom indoors was widely held to be unlucky, even deathly. The word moves easily between the botanist's Crataegus, the countryman's thorn, and the poet's May. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Crataegus species (botany): The genus as a whole — over a hundred species worldwide, including C. monogyna, C. laevigata (midland hawthorn), and in North America C. crus-galli (cockspur thorn) and C. phaenopyrum (Washington thorn). The word is the standard common term for all of them, from hedgerow shrubs to small orchard-like trees.
  • May, May tree, whitethorn, quickthorn: Regional and folk synonyms naming the same plant by its blossom (May), its pale flowers and bark (whitethorn), or its fast, living growth as a hedge (quickthorn, from "quick" meaning living). The same plant seen through different eyes.
  • The fruit, haw: The small red pome used as famine food, in jellies, wines, and ketchups, and eaten by birds and children; the fruit named the whole plant by part-whole transfer, so that "haw" alone could mean the berry or, in older English, the hedge itself.
  • Hedge and boundary (functional sense): The living fence — a hawthorn hedge is a wall of interwoven thorns; the Old English element haga meant "enclosure", and the whole word preserves that agricultural function.
  • Medicinal and herbal (modern jargon): In modern herbalism and pharmacology the hawthorn is a cardiotonic — extracts of flowers, leaves, and haws (standardised as Crataegus oxyacantha or C. monogyna) are recognised remedies for mild heart failure, arrhythmia, and hypertension; the clinical literature calls it the "heart herb".
  • Ornamental and urban (horticulture): Cultivated varieties with pink, crimson, or double flowers (Paul's Scarlet and similar), used as garden shrubs, street trees, avenue trees, and even bonsai subjects; North American hawthorns are popular ornamentals in their own right.
  • Place-names and surnames: The element hawthorn survives in countless English place-names (Hawthorn, Hawthornden) and in the surname Hawthorn(e), fossilising the plant's ancient role in marking boundaries, settlements, and parish limits.
  • Metaphoric and idiomatic: The sprig of hawthorn functions figuratively for May, spring, and a certain rustic Englishness; "hawthorn-berries" in the passage exemplifies the transparent compound used to name a specific wild food of the countryside.
Historical bridges: the semantic career of the word is a steady widening from function to object to symbol. The compound began as a functional name — "the thorn that makes the hedge" (Old English haga "hedge, enclosure" + þorn). By the late Old English and early Middle English period the first element had transferred by metonymy to the hedge's fruit, the haw; by Tudor times the blossom had fixed the word's association with May; and in modern botany, pharmacy, and gardening the word stretches to cover the whole genus and its dozens of species. Every one of these senses is connected by an unbroken line: a hedge plant that gave the countryside its fences, its spring garlands, its famine berries, and its heart medicine. The nuance throughout is rustic, homely, and precisely local — the plant of farms, boundaries, and folk-belief, not of gardens alone.
