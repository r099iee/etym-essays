• Swamp: Similar because both are waterlogged, spongy wetlands that hinder travel; differs because a swamp is typically low-lying, often wooded with trees, and fed by rivers or groundwater, whereas a bog is acid peatland built of moss, treeless, and quaking rather than merely wet.
  • Marsh: Similar because both are soft, wet, natural ground; differs because a marsh is open, grassy, often tidal or brackish and firmer underfoot, while a bog is moss-built, acid and deep-mired with no open water at its surface.
  • Fen: Similar because both are peat-forming wetlands with saturated ground; differs because a fen is alkaline or neutral, drained by springs and ditches, sedge-rich and cultivable, while a bog is acidic, rain-fed and poor in nutrients.
  • Moor: Similar because both are wild, open, uncultivated landscapes of the north; differs because a moor is a high, exposed tract of heather and acid grass on mineral soil, often dry, whereas a bog is the waterlogged peatland itself.
  • Mire / Quagmire: Similar because both name ground that yields underfoot and entraps; differs because mire and quagmire name unstable, muddy ground generally (and their figurative 'difficulty' sense), while a bog is the specific moss-and-peat wetland.
  • Quag: Similar because it is a shaking, boggy place that yields underfoot; differs because quag is a rarer, dialectal or poetic word for soft shaking ground, without the scientific peatland content of bog.
  • Slough: Similar because it is a deep muddy or marshy place that traps; differs because slough is a hollow of mud, a boggy pool or (in America) a stagnant backwater, less about peat accumulation than about soft mud.
  • Peatland: Similar because it is the scientist's umbrella term for bog, fen and mire; differs because peatland is neutral, technical and covers all peat-forming ground, whereas bog specifies the acid, rain-fed, moss-built kind.
  • Muskeg: Similar because it is a waterlogged, mossy bog of the far north; differs because muskeg is the Algonquian-derived term for Canadian and Alaskan sphagnum bog and means, in that landscape, what bog means generally.
  • Wallow: Similar because it is a wet, muddy, soft place; differs because a wallow is a shallow pool where animals roll, not a deep peatland, and carries none of bog's treachery or ecology.
