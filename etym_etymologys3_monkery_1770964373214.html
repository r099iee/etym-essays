The biography of monkery is really two stories joined at the hip: the long descent of the word monk from ancient Greek solitude, and the short, sharp career of a suffixal coinage that absorbed the religious polemics of the sixteenth century. The first component begins with Greek mónos, "alone, solitary", attested already in Homer. Its own deeper etymology is uncertain — modern handbooks (Beekes and others) give it no secure Indo-European parent, and older suggestions connecting it to a root for "thinking" have not won acceptance. The honest starting point for the chain is therefore attested Greek, not a reconstructed proto-form.

From mónos, Greek built monachós, "solitary, alone", using the adjectival suffix -achos. The word had existed as a simple adjective, but it took on a world-historical meaning in the fourth century CE, when Christian asceticism swept the eastern Mediterranean. The desert fathers of Egypt — Antony the Great above all — retreated into the wilderness to live alone in prayer and fasting, and the language of the Church seized on monachós for exactly that figure: the person who lives alone for God. From Coptic Egypt the term spread through Greek Christendom, and monasticism itself developed from solitary eremitism into communal cenobitism, first through Pachomius's rule in Upper Egypt and later through the great Latin rules, above all the Rule of St Benedict (c. 530), which became the backbone of Western monastic life.

Latin borrowed the word as monachus, attested in Christian Latin from the third–fourth centuries CE (the age of Jerome and Augustine), and a colloquial syncopated variant monicus or monacus travelled with the missionaries who carried Christianity to northern Europe. From this variant Old English took munuc, "monk", attested from the eighth century; the Old English form shows the vowels of the spoken Latin word rather than the written classicising monachus. Middle English re-shaped it as monek and then monk (from the twelfth century), the form English has kept ever since. The word entered French and English high culture through the church, the school, and the chronicle, always denoting a member of a vowed religious community living under a rule of poverty, chastity, and obedience.

The second story is the suffix. English had been building nouns in -ery since the Middle Ages, borrowing the pattern from Old French -erie (itself Latin -āria plus -ia), which produced collectives and place-nouns such as nunnery, cookery, and drapery. On this template English formed monkery — "the place, state, or system of monks". The word is first recorded in the sixteenth century, and its timing was catastrophic for its fortunes. The English Reformation, and in particular the Dissolution of the Monasteries under Henry VIII (1536–1541), dismantled every monastic house in England and Wales and confiscated their lands. The propagandists of the new order needed a language of contempt, and they found it in the -ery collectives: just as "nunnery" could be turned to mockery, "monkery" became the sneering shorthand for a religion reduced to candle-lit business, idle chanting, and relic-selling superstition.

Through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word carried this polemical freight. Protestant divines denounced "popish monkery"; travel writers used it of the convents they toured with amused distaste; Enlightenment sceptics borrowed it as a synonym for obscurantism. In the nineteenth century it survives in history books and fiction about the Middle Ages, usually with a nostalgic or Gothic colouring, and Romantic writers could even use it with a touch of picturesque romance — a "monkery" perched on a crag. By the twentieth century it was archaic, kept alive chiefly in historical narrative, in anti-clerical asides, and in jocular references to any reclusive or celibate way of living. In the source text, Shi-yin's question "It must be lonely here in your monkery" is thus quietly double-edged: it names a place of monks, and it lets a faint wry doubt about the value of such loneliness hang in the air.

Summary of the evolution:
  • Formal chain: Greek mónos → monachós → Latin monachus → Vulgar Latin monicus → Old English munuc → Middle English monk → English monk + -ery.
  • Sound history: syncope in Latin (monachus → monicus) shows in Old English munuc; Middle English levels the vowels to monk.
  • Semantic drift: "solitary" → "religious solitary, monk" → "a monk's way of life" → "the monastic system" → "monasticism as a suspect, money-driven institution" (pejorative) → "any secluded life" (archaic/ironic).
  • Socio-cultural engines: fourth-century Egyptian eremitism; Benedictine cenobitism; the conversion of northern Europe; the English Reformation and Dissolution of the Monasteries; Enlightenment anti-clericalism; nineteenth-century Gothic historicism.
  • Literary attestations: Reformation polemic and sermons; seventeenth- and eighteenth-century travel and controversy writing; Victorian historical fiction; twentieth-century historical novels and ironic reference.
