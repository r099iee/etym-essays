The story of tapestries begins not in a workshop but on a trade route. The Greek word τάπης (tápēs), "carpet, rug, hanging," is a cultural loanword taken from an Iranian source at a time when Persia was the great producer and exporter of fine woven floor coverings. The Iranian base is commonly reconstructed as *tapiš-, set beside the verbal root *tap- "to twist, to spin, to weave," the same base that later gives Persian tāftan and its participle tāftah, "woven," from which English taffeta descends. From the fifth century BC Greek authors used τάπης for the luxurious oriental rugs and hangings that filled Persian palaces and, increasingly, Greek and then Roman houses. The word therefore arrived in Europe carrying its social prestige with it: it was never a plain folk word but the name of an imported luxury, and that prestige stuck to the word for the next two thousand years. 1. The Iranian-Greek-Latin relay
  • Iranian source *tapiš- / *tap- (1st millennium BCE): a weaving-trade word meaning "woven cover," from the verbal idea of twisting and spinning thread (Persian tāftan). Beekes treats τάπης as an oriental "Wanderwort"—a travelling culture-word—rather than an Indo-European inherited lexeme; no secure PIE root for the borrowing is claimed.
  • Ancient Greek τάπης, genitive τάπητος (attested from the 5th century BCE) — "carpet, rug, tapestry, hangings." The word entered Greek during the period of active Persian cultural exchange, and by the Hellenistic period was the standard word for fine eastern floor- and wall-coverings.
  • Latin tapēte / tapētum (from the 1st century BCE/CE) — "carpet, coverlet, tapestry." Latin authors took the word directly from Greek; it appears in Pliny and in descriptions of oriental hangings, and from it the Romance and northern languages later drew their forms.
2. The European fanning-out
  • Romance stream: Vulgar Latin *tapitum / *tappetum produced Old French tapis, "carpet"; from tapis came the verb tapisser, "to cover with carpet or hang with tapestry," and the agent-noun tapissier, "upholsterer, maker of wall-hangings." From tapissier was formed tapisserie, the craft itself.
  • Germanic stream via Latin: Latin tapetum reached Old High German as teppih, giving modern German Teppich, "carpet" — a Germanic loan from the same Latin word, but through a different route than the French chain that gave English its term.
  • Romance cognates: Spanish tapiz, Italian tappeto, Portuguese tapeçaria, and Modern French tapisserie all descend from the same relay, so nearly every European language owes its word for the woven covering to the Persian-Greek-Latin chain.
3. The English line
  • Old French tapisserie passed into Anglo-Norman, and from there into Middle English as tapisserye (15th century). The word was recorded in English well before the final medieval form was fixed.
  • Modern English tapestry /ˈtæpɪstri/, plural tapestries /ˈtæpɪstriz/ — the exact form of this card. The English spelling preserves the French -ss- and -erie, and the word has kept its ceremonial, imported-prestige register for five centuries.
4. Semantic migration from floor to wall to metaphor
  • The decisive semantic shift came in the later Middle Ages: the same craft that had clothed floors produced the great woven panels of the medieval and Renaissance hall, and the word followed the craft, so that "tapestry" narrowed from "carpet or cover" to "large pictorial wall-hanging." The Bayeux Tapestry (technically embroidery, c. 1070s) fixed the word in public memory as the name of a woven record of events.
  • In the 19th century the word migrated a second time, from cloth to metaphor: the tapestry of history, the rich tapestry of life. The figurative sense—any interwoven whole of many threads made one picture—is now fully current alongside the concrete art-historical sense, so that "tapestries" can name museum treasures, woven memories, or the woven texture of experience itself.
