Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ped- "foot, step" (reconstructed, 5th–4th millennium BCE; the well-attested foot-word of Latin pes, Greek poús, Sanskrit pád-, English foot). [DEBATED: many handbooks link the fetch verb to the idea of "going on foot (to get)", but the connection is not universally accepted and is treated below as probable rather than certain.] → Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *fetōną / *fetjaną "to go, come; to fetch, bring" (reconstructed, late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; a proposed base *fet-, possibly related to the foot-word family *fōts). → Stage 3 — Old English fetian / fetigan "to fetch, bring, take" (8th–10th centuries CE), alongside the variant feccan, a West Saxon development showing assimilation of the earlier consonant cluster. → Stage 4 — Middle English fecchen / fechen / fetchen "to go and bring" (12th–15th centuries CE). → Stage 5 — Early Modern English fetch "to go and bring back" (16th century CE onward, spelling stabilized as fetch). → Stage 6 — Modern English FETCH — the card's target form: still the plain household verb of going-and-bringing, and the exact imperative Gandalf uses. NOTE on the debate: Old English shows both fetian and feccan; the precise relationship of the two is disputed (feccan may be an assimilated variant of fetian or a separate formation). The deeper link to PIE *ped- "foot" is plausible — fetching is, literally, going on foot to get — but not securely established, and some handbooks treat the whole family as Germanic-internal with no certain pre-Germanic etymon. The attested chain from Old English onward is secure; the deeper stages are flagged as reconstructed and debated.
