BATTERY in the phrase battery hen denotes the system of intensive egg production in which laying hens are confined in small wire cages arranged in tiers or batteries, with automated feeding, watering and egg collection. A classic battery cage allots each bird less floor area than an A4 sheet, preventing nesting, dust-bathing, wing-stretching and perching. In the falcon sentence — as helpless as a spastic ______ hen, a bird that broke its own eggs — the pesticide-thinned peregrine, whose DDT-weakened shells crack under the incubating parent, is likened to that factory bird: calcium-starved, trembling, laying shell-less or cracked eggs on wire. Nobility collapses into industrial pathos; the king's hunter becomes the cheapest layer.

Battery is otherwise one of English's great polysemes, and learners must hold its many lives together to feel the insult's weight. It can mean beating, artillery, electrical storage, legal assault, a set of tests, a baseball duo and a kitchen outfit, all radiating from the idea of things that batter or are massed to batter. The hen sense belongs to the massed-unit branch: a battery of cages like a battery of guns, rows deployed together for concentrated output. Register is agricultural-technical turned polemical; since Ruth Harrison's Animal Machines (1964) and subsequent campaigns, battery hen connotes cruelty, salmonella risk and cheapness, driving EU bans on barren cages from 2012 and British free-range labelling.

  • Primary contextual sense: battery cage system and its hen. Barren battery, enriched or furnished cage, colony system. Hens suffer osteoporosis, feather loss, foot lesions and stress behaviours. Eggs are stamped with farming-method codes, battery being code 3. Semantic bridge: from French batterie beating to group of artillery beating walls, to any grouped apparatus, to grouped cages. The bridge preserves multiplicity plus pounding routine: hens hammered by confinement as walls by cannon, laying on an industrial barrage rhythm.
  • Secondary senses: artillery unit, electrical device, legal wrong. A battery of guns, anti-aircraft battery; a torch battery, car battery, lithium-ion battery pack; assault and battery as unlawful touching. Each preserves a different facet: artillery keeps massed discharge, electricity keeps Franklin's 1748 analogy of Leyden jars discharging like cannon, law keeps the oldest sense of beating from the 1530s. Bridge: functional analogy of striking together or storing blows to release together. Understanding these helps learners see why battery hen sounds violent even before they know poultry science.
  • Tertiary senses: sets, teams and kitchen. A battery of tests, a battery of questions press barrage; baseball battery of pitcher and catcher working as unit from 1867 slang; batterie de cuisine set of cooking pans from beaten metal. Bridge: from grouped guns to any complementary set assembled for effect, whether examinations, players or saucepans. The culinary sense even loops back to beaten metal ware, Middle English bateri meaning forged vessels. All share ordered multiplicity for concerted function.

To call a peregrine a battery hen is therefore a triple degradation: wild to domestic, free to caged, hunter to layer. It gains force precisely because readers know other batteries as powerful — guns that destroy, cells that energise — while this battery only weakens. The word's own violence turns against the victim: things that batter have been battered.
