- Roman roots — to leap upon: Latin assilire, to spring at, gives the word its pouncing core, used of soldiers and speakers alike.
- c. 1200 — English adopts it: Old French assaillir enters Middle English for storming towns and striking foes, the knight's verb.
- Medieval — courts and confessionals: theologians write of the devil assailing souls with temptation, extending the battlefield into the conscience.
- 16th–17th c. — the age of controversy: pamphleteers assail doctrines, reputations, and thrones in print, and the word becomes the standard verb of polemic.
- 18th–19th c. — nerves and noses: novelists let doubts, fears, and stenches assail their heroines, completing the journey from fortress walls to the senses.
- 20th c. — headlines and hills: journalists report assaults while climbers and examinees assail peaks and papers, the sporting sense keeping the leap literal.
- Today — literary force: attack does the daily work, but assail survives wherever suddenness and violence need dignity, from fantasy pursuits to assailed consciences.
