- 14th c. — ponderen borrowed from Old French as a learned word meaning "to weigh in the mind, consider" — the register is clerical, legal, and philosophical; pondering is the duty of the wise judge.
- 15th–16th c. — pondering spreads into moral and devotional prose; the -ing form names the act of devout reflection, often on scripture or on one's own sins.
- 17th c. — pondering becomes the mark of the rational mind in the age of science and philosophy; to ponder is to weigh evidence, in the spirit of Bacon and Locke.
- 18th–19th c. — pondering appears in narrative prose for the thoughtful hero contemplating a course of action; it gains a romantic, meditative colour (pondering the future, pondering fate).
- 20th–21st c. — pondering is fully general but keeps its weighty, literary tone: pondering a decision, pondering the meaning of life — still the word for slow, serious thought rather than quick calculation.
- Today — pondering sits above "thinking" in gravity and below "brooding" in gloom; it is the favoured word of narrators, philosophers, and anyone turning a weighty question over in the mind.
