The PIE root of pouring. The family of gush goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰeu- "to pour", a root of extraordinary range in the daughter languages. In Greek it gives khéō "I pour" (the source, through khumós "juice", of the physiology word chyme); in Sanskrit the same root appears in the sacral verb juhóti "pours (an offering into the fire)", the language of Vedic sacrifice; and the Germanic languages build on it their fundamental word "to pour": Gothic giutan, Old Norse gjóta, Old High German giozan (modern German gießen), Old Saxon giotan, and Old English gēotan "to pour, shed, cast (metal)". In Old English the verb was everyday, but after the Norman Conquest the ordinary verb of pouring was replaced by the Romance pour; gēotan survives in modern English only in relics — the dialect noun gote "a drain, watercourse, gutter" and place-names — a reminder of the vigorous native pouring-word that gush alone revived.

The continental question: where exactly does "gush" come from? The English verb gush appears suddenly in the late 14th century as guschen ("to rush forth violently, of water, blood, fire"), and its form is the problem: regular development from Old English gēotan would have produced something like "yeet" or "geet", not "gush". The vowel -u- and the fricative -sh- point to a continental source. The standard account, given by both the OED and Etymonline, links the word to Middle Low German guschen/gussen (and related continental forms) — verbs meaning "to pour, to rush out", belonging to the same Germanic family as gēotan and gießen. The path of entry was almost certainly commercial: the Hanseatic and Flemish trade of eastern England put Low German shipwrights, brewers, weavers and merchants on English soil in numbers, and their vigorous pour-word crossed the water with them. Some authorities also suspect reinforcement by independent onomatopoeia, since "gush" imitates the sound of a sudden pour almost perfectly. The chain PIE *ǵʰeu- → Germanic *geutaną "to pour" → Low German guschen → Middle English guschen is therefore the accepted route, with an honest note: the exact Low German donor form and the exact date of the borrowing are inferred, not documented — the OED marks the relation of gush to its continental cousins as "perhaps" — but no serious alternative etymology exists, and the word's onomatopoeic nub makes it feel native to any ear.

Middle English to the 16th century: the physical word. In the century and a half after its first appearance, gush is purely physical: blood gushes from wounds in the romances; water gushes from springs and burst conduits; flames and smoke gush from burning buildings; Chaucer's age and its successors use it of springs, floods and the newly important vocabulary of surgery and siegecraft. The noun "gush" ("a sudden outpour") follows in the 17th century. The register is demotic and vivid: a word with an onomatopoeic nub that any speaker feels without being told, equally at home in the botched surgery of a field hospital and in the spouting plumbing of a town.

The figurative leap: 1660s. The great event of the word's life is the transplanting of the torrent into speech and feeling, dated by Etymonline to the 1660s: words, tears, praises and grievances begin to "gush" out of people. The age of sensibility embraced the image — the overflowing heart, the gush of sympathy, the gush of eloquence — and by the 18th century the "gushing" person was a recognised social type: one whose feelings, and their verbal expression, pour out with suspicious abundance. The participle "gushing" thus acquired its double life: physically neutral (a gushing wound is a first-aid fact), emotionally charged (gushing praise is excessive, unmeasured, possibly insincere). The gender politics of the age attached the label mainly to women's tears and enthusiasm, but by the 19th century sentimental male poets and reviewers could gush with the best of them.

The 19th century: sentiment and oil. The Victorian period completed the figurative apparatus: "gushy" (adjective, sentimental in an unguarded way) appears in the 19th century; "gushing" becomes a stock critique of popular emotion, of sentimental novels, of effusive journalism. Side by side, American petroleum engineering coined "gusher" (recorded 1886, in the boom years of the Pennsylvania and Texas oilfields): an oil well that flows copiously without pumping — the literal sense, in a word of spectacular industrial drama. The two senses ran in parallel for a century: the gusher of the oil patch and the gusher of the tearful heart, both pouring without effort, both hard to stop.

The 20th–21st centuries: celebrity and catastrophe. Modern English uses both registers at full strength. Literal: gushing pipes, gushing wounds, gushing springs, and the literary "there was a hiss, a gushing whirl, and then silence" (Tolkien, The Hobbit, 1937), where the participle captures the violent outrush of water as the dragon crashes into the lake — sound and flood fused in one word. Figurative: celebrity culture made "gushing" ubiquitous — gushing reviews, gushing fans, gushing over a star or a baby, gushing with gratitude — the word usually signalling affection laced with excess, or mock-affection laced with irony. In psychology and sociology prose, "gushing" is a label for unfiltered emotional expression; in fan culture it is reclaimed with affection; in journalism it describes the unpaid praise of press junkets. The word's sound still does half its work: gush is a splash in the mouth.

Summary of the drift. Gushing descends from the PIE pour-root *ǵʰeu- through Germanic *geutaną and a Low German cousin into late-14th-century English as a word of violent physical outflow; in the 1660s the torrent migrates from the body and the spring to speech and feeling; the 19th century adds both the sentimental critique ("gushy", "gushing") and the industrial drama of the oil gusher (1886); and the modern word spans burst pipes and overflowing fans with undiminished onomatopoeic force — always the sound and image of the sudden, unstoppable outpour.
