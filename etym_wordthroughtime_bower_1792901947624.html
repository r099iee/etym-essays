- Old English — a dwelling place: būr names a room, hut, or chamber, any roofed habitation, from the verb for dwelling itself.
- c. 1200 — the lady's chamber: in romance and ballad the bour becomes her private apartment, the bower-maiden's domain, intimate and feminine.
- 13th–14th c. — leaves join in: the sheltered-chamber idea extends to arbours of branches, and Chaucer can dream in a bower of green.
- Renaissance — the poet's favourite: Spenser, Shakespeare, and Milton embower lovers, fairies, and holy damsels alike; the word grows greener and more amorous with every masque.
- 18th–19th c. — gardens and ballads: landscape gardeners build bowers of trellis while antiquaries revive the bower-maiden; Burns keeps the cottage sense warm in Scots.
- 20th c. — Tolkien's Fangorn: the Ents lay Pippin in a living bower, fusing the woodland nook and the safe chamber in one image of tree-tended rest.
- Today — romance preserved: garden arbours, bridal bowers, and fantasy hideouts keep the word green, while the nautical and card senses survive as homely homonyms.
