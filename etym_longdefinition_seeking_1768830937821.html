seeking (present participle, gerund, and participial adjective; pronounced /ˈsiːkɪŋ/) denotes the active, intentional, goal-directed pursuit of a person, object, truth, state of being, or location. It is the participle of the verb to seek, whose foundational meaning is to go in search of something not yet possessed, hidden, or unattained, moving toward it with deliberate purpose. The word always implies a direction and a desire: seeking is not wandering but aiming, not idle curiosity but directed striving.

1. Primary Modern Senses

  • Active physical pursuit and search: the oldest sense — to look for, to search for, to go in quest of a person, animal, object, or place. A seeking hand or a seeking glance reaches across space toward its target; to seek shelter, refuge, or help is to move physically toward what one needs. This remains the concrete core of the word.
  • Intellectual and investigative seeking: to seek the truth, an answer, a solution, or knowledge — the pursuit of information and understanding. In scholarship and science, seeking describes methodical inquiry (research as a seeking out), and "information seeking" is the technical term of library and information science for the human activity of searching for and retrieving information.
  • Spiritual, religious, and philosophical seeking: to seek God, enlightenment, wisdom, salvation, or meaning. A spiritual seeker is one who pursues transcendence or ultimate reality; the language of the seeker and the seeking soul runs through scripture, the mystics, and the philosophical tradition ("seek, and ye shall find").
  • Legal and formal seeking: in law and official prose, to seek redress, damages, an injunction, or a ruling — to apply for or petition for something through formal channels; "the applicant seeks an order that..." is standard legal syntax.
  • Economic and professional seeking: to seek employment, a job, a contract, or a market; "job-seeking" and "the job seeker" are standard terms of the labour market, and "seekers" are candidates or applicants.
  • Technical seeking (computing and data storage): in computing, a seek is the positioning of a read/write head over the correct track of a disk; "seek time" is the time taken to move the head, and "to seek" data is to locate it on a storage medium — a literal transfer of the search image to machinery.

2. Secondary and Figurative Senses

  • Seeking in biological and botanical description (the sense on this card): in scientific and literary prose, seeking tendrils, seeking roots, or seeking fibrils reach toward light, water, or support; the participle gives a hungry purposiveness to plants and tissue, as in "the beautiful female body whose intestines trailed out into the nutrient solution like seeking fibrils" — the filaments seem to search for nourishment of their own accord.
  • Self-seeking and attention-seeking: compounds in which seeking names the goal of the seeking — a self-seeking person pursues only their own advantage; an attention-seeking child pursues notice; these are pejorative because the object of the seeking is selfish or trivial.
  • Seeking as a noun (the act): the gerund names the activity itself — "the seeking of truth," "endless seeking"; the noun form nominalizes the whole project of the search.
  • Seeking advice, counsel, or approval: to seek counsel, advice, permission, approval, or refuge — the verb of going to a source for what one needs; "seeking asylum" is the standard legal-phraseological use for refugees applying for protection.

3. Semantic Bridges and Historical Development

The word's history is a chain of transfers from hunting to the whole of human purpose. Old English sēcan descended from a Proto-Germanic verb meaning to seek out or search for, built on a root whose oldest sense was to track or smell out — a hunter's word, the verb of following a scent or a trail toward game. From the physical trail the verb spread to every goal: seeking truth, seeking God, seeking shelter, seeking employment, seeking an order in court. The participle seeking then loosened into an adjective of direction and desire that could be applied even to the inanimate — seeking tendrils, seeking fibrils, seeking glances — so that the word now names any reaching out of a thing toward what it lacks. The whole polysemy is unified by one invariant: seeking always has an object, a direction, and a want.

4. Distinctive Nuances

  • seek vs. search: search emphasizes the physical or systematic act of looking (sifting through space or data); seek emphasizes the inner intentionality and the goal — one can search a room but seek the truth.
  • seek vs. look for: look for is informal and concrete; seek is more formal, earnest, and able to take abstract objects.
  • seek vs. hunt: hunt implies aggression and a living target; seek is neutral and can be quiet or spiritual.
  • seek vs. pursue: pursue implies chasing something moving ahead; seek can aim at something hidden or immobile.
  • seeking as a participle: unlike the verb, the participle adds continuous, present-tense desire — a seeking thing is still reaching, still wanting, still on its way.
