The second element: rush. The English word rush descends from Old English rysc (also risc), a West Germanic word reconstructed as *rusk- and attested in cognates across the continental dialects (Old High German rusk, Middle Dutch rusch). It is a word of the northern marshes, and its history beyond Germanic is not secure: no convincing Indo-European root has been agreed for it, and the dictionaries mark the etymology as uncertain. Whatever its deep ancestry, rysc was the common Old English name of the humblest useful plant of the wetland. Rushes carpeted the floors of medieval halls and cottages — strewn fresh and changed with the seasons — and Erasmus, visiting England in the 1510s, marvelled at the habit. Rushes made rushlights (weak candles of peeled rush dipped in fat), wove into baskets and chair seats, and the northern English kept the old rhythm of "rush-bearing", carrying fresh rushes to church for the summer festivals — a custom still alive in the Lake District. By Middle English the standard forms rusch, risch, rysch are all in place.


The first element: bul-. The compound bulrish, bulrys(c)he is attested in the 15th century. Its first element is almost certainly the animal word bull used as an intensifying epithet of size and coarseness — "the great, strong rush" — matching an old English habit of tagging outsized wild things with animal names (horse-mushroom, bull-nettle); the bulrush is the bull among rushes, bigger, stouter and rougher than its kin. The Oxford English Dictionary records the derivation as uncertain — other "bul-" words of swelling have been floated as sources — but the size-epithet reading remains the standard explanation; no Germanic root *bul- meaning "plant" is attested.


The Bible makes it a word. English scripture needed a name for the Nile's reeds. Tyndale's 1530 Pentateuch renders the reed-basket of Moses as the "ark of bulrushes"; the King James Bible of 1611 keeps "an ark of bulrushes" (Exodus 2:3) and "bow down his head as a bulrush" (Isaiah 58:5), and the word enters the bloodstream of English religion and art: "Moses in the bulrushes" became one of the most painted scenes in Western art from the 16th to the 19th centuries. Meanwhile the plant itself, papyrus, had been the engine of Egyptian civilisation — its rafts, ropes, sandals, mats and paper made the Nile delta a paper-mill of the ancient world, and the papyrus stands of the riverbanks and the Sudd were the kingdom of the hippopotamus and the crocodile: precisely the world Wilde evokes with his riverhorse couching among the bulrushes.


Modern drift. In the 18th–19th centuries botanists tried to discipline the folk name: British use attached "bulrush" to the great club-rush (Scirpus) and increasingly to the reedmace (Typha) — which Americans, finding the name unsatisfying, relabelled the "cattail" for its dark velvet spike. The result is a word with two passports: British bulrush = club-rush or reedmace; American bulrush = cattail; and a third, historical passport in which bulrushes are papyrus. The plural form — BULRUSHES — is steadily literary: it names a stand, a habitat, a scene, and nowadays an engineering solution, since wetland-restoration projects replant "bulrushes" as filters and habitat. Every retelling of Wilde's swallow or Moses' ark keeps the old marsh-word green in English letters.


  • Eras: West Germanic *rusk- → Old English rysc → Middle English rusch; the bull + rush compound c. 1440 → Tyndale (1530) → King James Bible (1611) → modern American "cattail" competition.
  • Semantics: a coarse great rush → the biblical reeds of the Nile → papyrus → the modern wetland emblem; register shifts from everyday marsh-word to scriptural and literary plural.
  • Culture: medieval rush-strewn floors, rushlight and rush-bearing, Moses in the bulrushes, the hippo's habitat, wetlands restoration.
