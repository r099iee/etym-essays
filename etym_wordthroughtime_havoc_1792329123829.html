- [14th–15th c. Late Middle English] — Military command borrowed from Anglo-Norman crier havok, serving as an official operational signal granting soldiers unrestrained license to pillage, loot, rape, and massacre defeated populations without military discipline — [perception: terrifying martial battle cry signaling impending atrocity and legal suspension of mercy]
- [Late 16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — Shifted from an operative spoken signal into a noun denoting the catastrophic aftermath of violence, famously immortalized in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar ("Cry 'Havoc!' and let slip the dogs of war"); criminalized under martial codes where uttering the cry without royal authority was punishable by beheading — [perception: viewed with solemn dread as the zenith of military barbarism and lawless carnage]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Eras] — Metaphorically extended beyond battlefield slaughters to natural disasters, raging epidemic fevers, tempestuous oceanic weather, and the physical ravages of time on empires and architectural monuments ("the havoc of time") — [perception: perceived as sublime, tragic elemental devastation rather than purely human cruelty]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Generalized into widespread figurative and colloquial spheres, overwhelmingly crystalized in standard phrasal collocations such as wreak havoc and play havoc, applying freely to software glitches, logistical disruptions, metabolic dysfunction, supply chain delays, and everyday domestic pandemonium — [perception: ubiquitous idiom for severe functional chaos, disorder, or disruption, stripped of its original bloodthirsty martial terror]
