Bower carries two great intertwined senses that English has cherished for a thousand years: a shady recess formed by trees or climbing plants bending together overhead, and an inner room or dwelling chamber, especially in poetry a lady's private apartment. Both senses share the deep idea of a sheltered, enclosed, intimate place set apart from the open world.

In the source sentence Pippin is carried to a bower under the trees and laid upon a soft bed: here Tolkien uses the woodland sense, a natural arbour of branches within Fangorn Forest, yet the soft bed and the tender handling let the chamber sense ghost through, so that the forest nook feels for a night like a bedroom, safety woven out of leaves.

Core meaning in detail: As a garden feature a bower is an arbour or leafy alcove, branches trained or naturally grown into a roof, offering shade, coolness, and secrecy; lovers meet in bowers, poets dream in them, and children build them instinctively out of blankets and branches. As a domestic word, now literary, a bower is a boudoir before boudoirs existed: the innermost room where the lady of the house withdrew with her attendants for needlework, music, and private talk.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A shady leafy shelter or arbour (primary modern sense): trees or vines arching into a green roof, whether wild as in Fangorn or trained over a garden trellis; the sense every modern reader pictures first.
  • A lady's private chamber (literary, medieval onward): the bower-maiden attends her mistress there in ballad and romance; the bridge is enclosure itself, since both the leafy nook and the inner room are walled, intimate retreats, and Old English used one word for both kinds of dwelling.
  • A dwelling or abode in general (archaic, poetic): Spenserian shepherds keep their bowers as cottages; the bridge runs through the oldest root sense of bower as simply a room or habitation before it specialized.
  • A peasant cottage or hut (dialectal, Scots and Northern): Burns and his kin use bower for a humble rural home; the bridge is social descent, the lord's chamber word sliding down the scale to any snug dwelling.
  • An anchor carried at the bow (nautical, distinct etymology converged in spelling): the best bower and small bower are the two principal anchors stowed at a ship's bows; the bridge is purely positional, the bow-anchor, though sailors long heard the leafy word echoing in it.
  • A card-game term for the highest trump (American euchre): the right bower (jack of trumps) and left bower (the other jack of the same colour); the bridge runs, by most accounts, from German Bauer, a farmer or jack, anglicized by ear into the familiar English word.

Register and connotation: Bower is tender, romantic, and faintly antique: bowers are where proposals happen, where fairies lurk, where heroines hide. The word blushes with greenness and secrecy, and even its hard nautical and gaming senses cannot quite strip the perfume from it. To say bower instead of arbour or bedroom is to choose music over precision, and every poet from Chaucer to Keats has paid that price gladly.
