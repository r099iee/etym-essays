Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*sent-
"to head for, go; perceive, feel"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*sentī-
"to feel, think, perceive"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
ad- + sentire → assentīrī / assentire
"to think with, agree to, approve"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE)
assensus
"agreement, approval, sanction"
↓
Old French / Anglo-Norman (c. 12th c.)
assentir (v.) / assent (n.)
"to consent, agree; approval"
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
assenten (v.) / assent (n.)
"to agree formally, give sanction"
↓
Modern English (c. 1500–present)
assent
"to express formal agreement;"
"concurrence, sanction"

Note: Assent entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman legal administration from Latin assentire ('to think with'), combining ad- ('to') and sentire ('to feel, think'). In common law and constitutional history, it specifically designates legislative enactment (Royal Assent) and contractual consensus (mutual assent).
