1. PIE root *streyg- / *streig- "to be tight, to bind, to press" (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE; some handbooks cite variants such as *streyg- "stroke, rub, press" or group the family under *ster- "stiff, rigid" extensions). Stated explicitly: the exact root shape is reconstructed and debated. 2. Latin stringō, stringere, strīxī, strictum "to draw tight, to bind, to compress; to touch lightly, to graze" (attested from the 3rd century BCE, e.g., Plautus), from the PIE root above. 3. Latin strictus "drawn tight, narrow, compressed, strict" — perfect passive participle of stringere, used adjectivally in the sense "narrow, tight" (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g., in Cicero). 4. Old French estreit (also estroit; Anglo-Norman estreit) "narrow, tight, strict" (attested 9th–12th c., from Latin strictus; modern French étroit "narrow"). 5. Middle English streit / strait (adjective) "narrow, tight, strict, close" (attested 13th c., from Old French). 6. Middle English strait (noun) "a narrow passage of water" (attested late 13th–14th c., e.g., in navigational and historical usages such as "the straits of Gibraltar/Messina"); already also figuratively "a narrow, awkward position, difficulty." 7. English straits (plural) — the plural of strait, standard for narrow waterways (a single channel often called "the Straits of X") and, by metonymy, for difficult circumstances ("in straits," "dire straits"). STRAITS ◄ TARGET Debated/nuanced note: the PIE root is reconstructed and its exact form/vocalism debated; the Latin → Old French → Middle English route, by contrast, is fully attested and certain. The geographic and figurative senses both flow from the single image "drawn tight, narrow.
