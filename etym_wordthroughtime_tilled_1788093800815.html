- tilian (Old English): "to strive, aim at, labour for" — a verb of purposeful effort, applied to working the land as the archetypal labour. Connotation: honest toil, service.
- Late Old English / Middle English tillen: narrows toward agriculture — "to cultivate, plough" — while ME edges like "to gain, obtain, provide for" still flourished. Genesis gives it sacred weight (Wycliffe, c. 1382, "to till the ground"). Connotation: humble agrarian work, now holy.
- 16th–17th c.: "tilled" is the plain word for cultivated land; the King James Bible (1611) fixes "till the ground" in Genesis 2:5 and 4:12 — at once a curse (Cain) and a calling (Adam). Connotation: biblical gravity, moral labour.
- 18th–19th c.: "tilled" stays the standard agrarian participle while farming industrialises; Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Clare) use it for pastoral nostalgia. Connotation: rustic, traditional, faintly old-fashioned.
- Modern: "tilled" lives on in agriculture, farming journalism and literary idiom ("till the soil"); everyday speech prefers "ploughed" or "cultivated". Tolkien: the Dale valley "had become tilled again and rich". Connotation: dignified, slightly archaic, biblical echo.
