wisp — a small, thin, loosely gathered bunch, streak, or filament of something light and insubstantial: hay, straw, hair, smoke, mist. In the sentence “A wisp of white appeared and waxed to a pillar of boiling black smoke,” the word marks the absolute first moment of the summoning — a single slender thread of whiteness emerging out of nothing. Its literary tone is one of extreme fragility and uncanny delicacy: a wisp is not a cloud, not a plume, not a mass; it is barely a presence at all, something ghostly and fibrous, so thin that it seems to be dissolving even as it appears, the least possible quantity of substance. The old association with the will-o’-the-wisp — the marsh-light that lures travellers to their doom, named from the wisp of burning straw swung as a torch — deepens the menace: in Vance’s dying world the wisp of white is the first flicker of a force that will boil into blackness, and the word carries the folk-treachery of the deceptive light.

Core meaning and etymology as semantic bridge. The word’s oldest attested sense is material and humble: a handful or bundle of hay, grass, or twigs, used for kindling, for cleaning, or to sit upon (late 13th c.). The shift from bundle to streak is the semantic bridge: both are small, thin, loosely gathered collections of dry matter, and when a wisp of straw is lit or a wisp of hair hangs loose, the material object becomes the slender filament we now see in smoke and mist.

Primary senses of wisp.
  • A small, thin, loose bunch or lock of hair, straw, hay, or similar material (the oldest sense).
  • A thin streak or filament of smoke, cloud, mist, or other vapour (the dominant modern sense).
  • By metaphor, anything ghostly, fleeting, and barely present — a faint trace of a person, a memory, a sound.
  • The will-o’-the-wisp: the marsh-light (also called ignis fatuus), and by extension any deceptive lure that leads one astray.
  • As a verb, to wisp: to rub down with a wisp, or to move in a wisp-like, drifting manner.

waxed — grew, increased in size, quantity, or intensity; the simple past of wax. In “a wisp of white appeared and waxed to a pillar of boiling black smoke,” the verb governs a transformation from the thinnest thread to a towering column, and it does so with a distinctive cadence: waxing is steady, smooth, organic, inevitable growth — the way light fills a dark room or the moon fills toward fullness. It is the antonym of wane, and the pair has been locked together for centuries in the great lunar idiom of increase and decrease.

Core meaning and a crucial homonym trap. Modern English has two unrelated words spelled wax. The verb wax, “to grow, to increase,” descends from PIE *h₂ewg- (“to increase, grow, strengthen”) through Proto-Germanic *wahsijaną and Old English weaxan; it is a native Germanic strong verb (wax–waxed–waxed, formerly wax–wōx–waxen). The noun wax, “the substance (beeswax, candle-wax),” is an entirely separate word, from PIE *wokso-, and the verb to wax meaning “to polish or coat with wax” belongs to it. In Vance’s sentence the meaning is unmistakably the growth-verb — but the homonymy is worth naming, because it is a classic source of confusion for learners.

Primary senses of waxed.
  • Increased in size, amount, or degree; grew (the moon waxed; the smoke waxed to a pillar).
  • Passed into a specified state, especially of speech: waxed eloquent, waxed lyrical, waxed philosophical — “grew or became (in a certain manner).”
  • (Of the moon) to grow in apparent size as it moves toward fullness — the fixed astronomical sense, always paired with wane.
  • Homonymic (not this word): coated or polished with wax; also recorded past of the noun-verb (waxed the floor, waxed the skis).

How wisp and waxed work together in context. The two words are the two poles of the sentence’s motion: the wisp is the minimum, the waxed is the growth from that minimum to a maximum. The sequence is an apocalypse in miniature — a ghost-thin filament swelling to a boiling pillar of black smoke — and the pairing of a diminutive noun with a growth verb of organic inevitability gives the sentence its uncanny, relentless tone.
