- Ancient Greece (Pre-5th Century BCE) - Known but exotic, primarily medicinal/cosmetic, associated with 'barbarian' cultures. Connotation: foreign, therapeutic, less prestigious than olive oil.
- Classical Greece (5th-4th Century BCE) - Still not a culinary staple, mentioned in medical texts (Hippocrates) for its healing properties. Connotation: functional, non-culinary, a curiosity.
- Hellenistic Period & Roman Empire (3rd Century BCE - 5th Century CE) - Word borrowed into Latin as 'butyrum', indicating increasing awareness and limited use, though olive oil remained dominant in the Mediterranean. Connotation: known, but still secondary to olive oil.
- Early Medieval Europe (5th-10th Century CE) - As 'butyrum' spread into Germanic languages, butter became a dietary staple in Northern Europe. The *word* 'boutyron' itself faded from common Greek usage but its Latin descendant flourished. Connotation: The *concept* of butter became essential in many cultures, though the original Greek term became archaic.
- Modern Usage - 'Boutyron' is now primarily an academic or historical term, used in etymological studies to trace the origin of 'butter'. Connotation: historical, linguistic, scholarly.
