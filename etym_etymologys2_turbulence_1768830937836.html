1. Greek týrbē (τύρβη) “turmoil, disorder, confusion” (attested from the 8th–5th c. BCE, in Homer and classical prose). No secure PIE ancestry: modern scholars (Beekes) regard it as probably Pre-Greek — a relic of the languages spoken before the Hellenic migrations.
2. Latin turba “turmoil, commotion; a crowd, a mob, the rabble” (attested from the 2nd c. BCE; loanword from Greek týrbē, per de Vaan).
3. Latin turbō, turbāre “to disturb, agitate, throw into disorder” (attested classical); from turba. (From the related noun turbō, turbinis “a whirlwind; a spinning top” come English turbine, 19th c., and turbid.)
4. Latin turbulentus “full of commotion, storm-tossed, restless” (attested classical; applied to seas, winds, and rebellious peoples).
5. Latin turbulentia “commotion, disorder” (attested in Late Latin / scholarly use).
6. Middle French turbulence “agitation, disorder” (14th c.), from Latin.
7. English turbulence “disorderly commotion” (15th c.); the scientific fluid-dynamics sense follows in the 19th c. (Lord Kelvin, Osborne Reynolds).

NOTE ON DEBATE: The traditional link of Greek týrbē to PIE *(s)twer-, “to stir, turn, whirl,” is now rejected by modern scholarship (de Vaan, Beekes). That root does exist — Sanskrit tvarate, “hastens,” and Germanic *sturmaz, “storm,” descend from it — but the phonetic and semantic bridge to Greek týrbē is too weak, and turba is now treated as a loanword from Greek. Treat the *(s)twer- connection as obsolete.
