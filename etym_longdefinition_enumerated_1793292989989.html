Enumerated is the past participle of enumerate: counted, listed, or named one item at a time in an ordered series, so that every member of a set is explicitly identified and none is skipped. To enumerate is to say first, second, third — whether numbering reasons in an essay, articles in a constitution, or species in a survey — and enumerated describes what has undergone that ordering.

In the source sentence the word closes a reflection on orality and literacy: aspects of the human condition are far too numerous ever to be fully enumerated. Here it carries its classical exhaustive-listing sense with a deliberate paradox — the innumerable cannot be enumerated — and the passive participle stresses completion attempted and found impossible: even the fullest catalogue would leave remainders.

Core meaning in detail: Enumeration has two fused faces, counting and listing, and enumerated covers both: enumerated heads of cattle have been tallied to a total, while enumerated rights have been written down article by article. The common thread is item-by-item explicitness — each unit distinguished, ordered, and made retrievable — as opposed to mass or vague reference. Computing sharpens this: an enumerated type is a data type defined by naming every permitted value in advance.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Counted one by one to a total (primary sense, from 17th c.): as in enumerated ballots or an enumerated population in a census. The bridge is the Latin compound itself: ex-, out or fully, plus numerus, number — to number out completely.
  • Listed item by item in order (co-primary modern sense): enumerated reasons, enumerated powers in constitutional law. The bridge runs from counting to naming: Roman rhetoric already moved between numbering arguments and listing them, and English keeps both motions in one verb.
  • Specified exhaustively in law and logic (technical sense): enumerated rights, enumerated exemptions, enumerated premises. The bridge is legal precision: what is enumerated is protected or excluded exactly as far as the list reaches, and everything outside the list is another matter — hence the Ninth Amendment debate over unenumerated rights.
  • Defined by exhaustive naming in computing (specialist sense, 20th c.): an enumerated type such as days of the week in code. The bridge is direct borrowing: programmers took the rhetorical ideal of the complete list and made it a machine-enforced contract.
  • Reckoned among, counted as part of (figurative extension): to be enumerated among the greats. The bridge passes through the census image: just as citizens are counted into a population, persons or things are counted into a class by public acknowledgement.
  • Expounded point by point in rhetoric (literary sense): a preacher enumerates the virtues of patience. The bridge is pedagogical: breaking a whole into numbered heads makes it memorable, so enumeration becomes a method of teaching and persuasion.

Register and connotation: enumerated is formal, orderly, and slightly bureaucratic — censuses, statutes, syllabi, and scholarly prose rather than conversation. It promises thoroughness and accountability: what is enumerated can be checked off, audited, and appealed to. The source sentence plays this promise against infinity, using the driest word for listing to gesture at what no list can hold.
