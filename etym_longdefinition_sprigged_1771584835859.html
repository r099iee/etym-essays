Sprigged is the past participle of the verb to sprig, "to decorate with small shoots, twigs, or sprig-shaped floral motifs," used adjectivally to describe a fabric, garment, or other surface ornamented with small, scattered floral or branch-like patterns. In the literary context of this card the word describes a garment: "He had changed his robe and jacket for a rather more worn-looking rose-coloured gown, __________ with flowers." The word does far more here than identify a textile pattern; it orchestrates a quiet collision of elegance and decay. On one side, sprigged belongs to the fashionable dressmaking vocabulary of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — sprigged muslins, cottons, and dimities were prized, deliberately purchased goods, and a gown "sprigged with flowers" still carries the residual aura of the dressmaker's art. On the other side, the gown is "worn-looking" and rose-coloured rather than crimson or new, so the delicate sprigs are set against fabric that has faded through use. The participle thus marks the gown as an object of faded elegance: still visibly ornamented, still legible as the property of a cultivated household, but demoted from ceremonial glory to casual, intimate wear. The tone is one of gentle, observant pathos — the sprigs remember their bloom even as the cloth confesses its age.

The senses of sprigged are layered, though they all hang from the noun sprig (a small shoot, twig, or spray):

  • Of fabrics and garments (primary modern sense) — ornamented with small sprig-shaped floral motifs, whether the flowers are woven into the cloth, printed on it, or embroidered across it. "Sprigged muslin," "sprigged cotton," "a sprigged gown."
  • Of surfaces generally — decorated with small, scattered sprig or twig motifs, applied by any technique: "a wallpaper sprigged with tiny blue forget-me-nots."
  • Literal botanical sense — of a plant or branch: bearing or covered with small shoots and sprigs, full of young twigs. This is the oldest strand of meaning, kept alive in botanical and horticultural description.
  • As the past participle of the verb "to sprig" — meaning "decorated with sprigs," "planted or set with sprigs," or, in ceramics, "ornamented with applied clay motifs" (sprigged pottery, where small moulded reliefs are attached to the body).
  • Historical dressmaking jargon — in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, "sprigged" was a precise trade term for cottons, muslins, and linens scattered with small, usually light, floral sprays — the fresh, delicate alternative to the heavier figured silks and the richly embroidered fabrics of formal dress.

The semantic bridge from botany to textiles is a simple, pleasing transfer: a sprig is a small shoot or spray, and a fabric decorated with small floral sprays was felt to be covered with little sprigs — as though the cloth itself had sprouted. The word's own etymology carries this out in miniature: sprig is probably connected to the verb spring, so that a sprig is etymologically "that which springs" from a branch. The modern sense, in which the decorative motif matters more than the botanical reality, is a historical overlay on this older image.

In contemporary English the word is period-flavoured and textile-specific. It is used of old-fashioned floral dress fabrics, of wallpaper and chintz with small scattered flowers, and, in the crafts, of pottery and ceramics decorated with applied sprig motifs. It is rarely used of large, bold floral patterns — the sprig is by definition small, delicate, and scattered — and it carries a faint but persistent nostalgic register: sprigged things belong to gardens, summers, muslin dresses, and the quiet domestic elegance of an earlier century.
