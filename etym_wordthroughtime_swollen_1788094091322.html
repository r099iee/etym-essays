- (700s–1000s) - geswollen, participle of swellan "to swell": of wounds, tumours, and rising waters — a plain physical word for distension, with the ge- prefix of past participles.
- (1100s–1400s) - swollen: bodily swelling (limbs, belly, tongue) and rivers in spate; medical and natural registers dominate; the prefix drops away with Middle English inflection.
- (1500s–1600s) - The moral leap: "swollen with pride", "swollen with anger" — the Bible and its translators make internal excess the key image; a swollen heart is a proud one, and the physical expansion becomes a moral diagnosis.
- (1700s–1800s) - Medicine systematizes the word ("swollen glands", "swollen joints"); "swollen head" and "swelled head" name conceit; Romantic poetry loves the "swollen stream" of spring floods — the word bestrides clinic and sublime.
- (1900s–2000s) - Quantitative inflation: "swollen profits", "swollen bureaucracy", "swollen ranks"; modern slang "swole" (muscular) splits off as a positive badge of gym culture; rivers swollen by rain remain the classic use, the old aroma of dangerous excess intact.
