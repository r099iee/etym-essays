Pawing (the present participle of the verb paw) means 'to feel, scrape, or move about with the hands as if they were an animal's paws,' used here intransitively in 'He went pawing about': the figure, in darkness, gropes on hands and knees, his hands reduced to forepaws, brushing and scrabbling over floor and wall. The verb is drawn from the animal world — a dog or a horse paws the ground, scraping and stamping without finesse — and its application to a person quietly strips away upright human bearing. He does not walk or feel his way; he goes pawing about, an animal moving blindly through the dark, touching everything with the flat, pad-like surfaces of his hands. The tone is deliberately dehumanizing and comic-pathetic — a small being's helpless scrabbling, all touch and no sight — and the archaic 'went ... about' construction gives the sentence a folk-tale, almost ballad-like cadence, as though the creature's fumbling were being narrated by a patient storyteller. The -ing form makes the action ongoing, unresolved, and open-ended: a continuing, directionless search that the sentence deliberately refuses to conclude.

Distinguishing it from similar verbs sharpens the picture: groped implies careful, deliberate tactile search by a human hand, methodical and purposeful; fumbled suggests clumsy handling of objects, usually with a specific thing in mind; scrabbled is frantic, clawing, and hurried, often with the nails scoring a surface; scraped stresses friction against a hard surface rather than the motion of the hand itself; clawed implies sharp instruments — nails, talons — and aggression; and touched and felt are delicate, neutral, and deliberate. Pawing uniquely names the whole flat hand used as an animal uses its forefoot — blunt, broad, unspecialized, with no precision at the fingertips — and so it conveys at once the loss of human dignity and the utter blindness of the motion, the seeker touching not to examine but simply to find, in the dark, with whatever is broadest and most available.


Polysemy and the full modern range of pawing:
  • Animal sense (literal): of horses, dogs, bears, and other quadrupeds, to strike, scrape, or beat at the ground or a surface with the forefoot — a horse paws the stable floor in impatience, a dog paws at the door, a bear paws at a log for grubs. The motion is purely kinetic, impatient, or questing.
  • Human clumsy-handling sense (from the seventeenth century): to handle roughly, clumsily, or carelessly with the hands — 'stop pawing the merchandise' — the hand used as a blunt instrument rather than a tool.
  • Pejorative 'fondling' sense (from the eighteenth century): to paw at someone is to touch or fondle them in a clumsy, insistent, and unwanted way; 'pawing' in this sense is a staple of fiction and journalism for unwelcome physical advances, with a clear undertone of animality and reproach. Bridge: the animal verb transfers directly to the human hand that has lost its manners.
  • Figurative / transferred senses: to 'paw the air' is to struggle ineffectually, reaching and beating at nothing (as a struck animal does); 'pawing through' things is rifling through them roughly; 'paw over' is to handle cursorily. In each, the sense of blunt, unspecialized contact remains.
  • The idiom 'cat's paw': a cat's paw is a dupe, a person used by another to pull chestnuts out of the fire (from the fable of the monkey and the cat) — the paw as the blind instrument of another's profit, the body doing the work the mind will not claim.
  • Register and technical uses: in animal-behaviour study (ethology), pawing is the technical term for the ground-scraping display of horses and cattle; in boxing and martial-arts slang, a 'pawing' jab is a probing, uncommitted touch with the lead hand, testing range rather than landing force.

Thus the full modern range of pawing runs from the literal ground-scraping of an impatient horse, through the clumsy human hand, to the unwelcome fondler and the ineffectual struggler; every sense retains the animal origin — the whole flat forefoot — and the historical movement of the word is a steady transfer of animality onto the human hand. In the passage the literal animal sense is restored, and the dehumanizing force of the sentence rests on that fact.
