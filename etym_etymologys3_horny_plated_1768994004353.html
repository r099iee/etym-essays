The biography of horny: from the head's crown to the aroused body.

The story of horny begins with a gesture of pointing at the body's highest part. The Proto-Indo-European root *ḱer- signified the "top of the head" and whatever grows out of it, and from that single point of meaning it sent horns in every direction across the Indo-European world. In the Indic branch it hardened into śṛṅga, the animal's horn — alive in modern Hindi as sīṅg and in Odia as śiṅga — while the sibling śiras, "head," gave Hindi sīr, so that in India the horned animal and the head that carries it still stand side by side. In Greek, kéras named the horn and entered the scientific vocabulary of modern Europe through keratin, the protein of horn, hair and nails, and through the "horned face" of the triceratops; Latin cornū produced cornea, cornet, cornucopia, the foot's "corn" (a callus hard as horn), and unicorn, the "one-horn." In Celtic, carw, "deer," means literally "the horned one," and in Germanic the root became *hurną, Old English horn — the straight outgrowth on the head of cattle, and by extension the horn as musical instrument and as drinking vessel.

From the noun, Old English hornig — our horny — was formed for anything "made of or resembling horn": horny-handed labor, horny plates of armor, horny skins. The adjective kept its material meaning for more than a millennium, a workaday word of the workshop and the farm, and it still does today. Then came the great semantic leap, recorded in nineteenth-century American slang: horny came to mean "sexually aroused," the aroused body's stiffness compared to the rigid horn. The leap is a metaphor rather than a sound shift, but it is no less real a change: a word that for a thousand years pointed to the highest, hardest part of the animal body now points to the animal in its most vital state, and the slang sense has become, in modern speech, the commonest one. The shift shows the power of embodied metaphor: the same word that names the material of cattle's crowns now names a state of bodily arousal, and the older material sense survives beside it in technical and literary use.

When Burroughs writes of "horny-plated bodies," the adjective carries that entire history at once — the material hardness of keratin, the ancientness of the horn itself, and a body sealed in the very substance of cattle and rhinoceros. The compound rests on the oldest, material sense of horny, deliberately reclaiming it from the slang, and the literary effect depends on that archaism: a word most readers now meet in the sexual sense is here restored to its primaeval meaning, and the grotesque armor of the vortlup glows with the strangeness of a reclaimed etymology.
The biography of plated: from spreading to sheathing.

The life of plated begins not with armor but with a gesture of spreading. The PIE root *plat- meant "to spread out, to be flat and broad," and from it the Indic branch produced pṛthu, "broad, wide," and, from pṛthu, the feminine pṛthvī — "the Broad One," the Earth herself — which survives in Hindi prithvī and Odia pruthibī as the name of the planet. In Greek the root gave platýs, "flat, broad," which built a remarkable technical vocabulary: platýpous, the "flat-foot" platypus; plátanos, the spreading plane tree; plateía, the "broad street," which passed into Latin as platea and then into Italian piazza and French place, so that the French for "town square" and the English word place are, at bottom, the same spreading gesture. In Germanic the root produced *flata-, "flat," the ancestor of English flat and German flach.

The path to plate runs through Greek and Latin and the Romance languages. From Greek platýs came the Vulgar Latin adjective *plattus, "flat," and from *plattus came Old French plat, "flat, level," and then, on the feminine, plate, a "flat piece" — of wood, of stone, but above all of metal. English borrowed plate in the thirteenth century for a flat sheet of metal, for the platter and dish, for the sheets of armor that protected a knight (hence plate armor, and later the armor-plated warship and tank), and eventually for the rigid slabs of the earth's crust (tectonic plates) and the sheet of glass or metal that carries an image (a photographic plate). The participle plated, "covered with a layer or with sheets," spread the same image through every sense: gold-plated, nickel-plated, armor-plated, and, in zoology, the plated bodies of armored reptiles and fish.

The compound horny-plated fuses these two long biographies in a single hyphen: the material history of horn and the sheathing history of plate, welded into one continuous integument for the vortlups of Burroughs's Mars — a living suit of armor that grows rather than is forged, assembled by the deep time of two Indo-European roots, one pointing at the top of the head, one at the spreading of the earth.
