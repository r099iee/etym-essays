- [Late Latin 'interludium': a play or performance inserted between others at festivals] - [Perception: purely theatrical and occasional; the word is a calendar of between-times]
- [Medieval England (14th–16th c.): 'enterlude/interlude' names a vigorous minor genre — short comic or moral plays acted between courses at feasts, between mystery plays, or between acts of longer dramas] - [Perception: the most famous early English drama is called by this name; licensed and loved as a light, to-the-point entertainment]
- [17th–18th centuries: the word stretches from the stage to time itself — 'an interlude of peace,' 'a calm interlude' in prose and verse] - [Perception: no longer only a play, but any breathing-space between larger events; the theatrical color fades but never fully]
- [19th century: music takes it up — operatic and orchestral interludes (and the sibling 'intermezzo') standardize the musical sense] - [Perception: program-notes prose; the word gains gravity in concert halls and church liturgies]
- [20th century: 'romantic interlude,' 'comic interlude,' O'Neill's 'Strange Interlude' (1928) — the word becomes a favorite for naming a brief, vivid episode of life] - [Perception: half-tender, half-wry; an interlude is a parenthesis in the main story]
- [Today: pop albums publish short 'interludes' between songs; climate writing calls the Holocene 'an interlude between glaciations'] - [Perception: the word still means what it meant in Rome — a little play between the great ones]
