- 15th century: "nick" = a small cut, notch, or slit in a surface (e.g., a nick in a blade) — plain physical sense, neutral register.
- Late 16th century: "in the nick" (1580s) = at the exact moment; the notch as a tally-mark measuring time — "the nick of time" is born; perception: precise, urgent, fortunate timing.
- Mid-17th century: "Old Nick" = the Devil, a euphemistic folk-name built on the personal name Nicholas; perception: playful-but-sinister personification of evil.
- 17th–18th centuries: verb "to nick" = to cut into, score, snip; also "to cheat, to trick" — the cutting sense slides toward sharp practice.
- 19th century: British thieves' cant: "to nick" = to steal; "the nick" = a prison or police station; perception: criminal, slangy, underground.
- 20th century: cricket jargon "a nick" = a fine edge off the bat; "in good nick" = in good condition (British/Australian); perception: specialist but warm and informal.
- Today: "in the nick of time" is the dominant literary idiom; British slang "nicked" (stolen/arrested) is everyday; "nickname" (from "an eke-name") keeps the word in every schoolyard.
