The evolution of shears begins with the busiest cutting root in the Indo-European family, PIE *sker-, to cut. Its descendants crowd English dictionaries: shear and share (what is cut apart is shared out), shore (where the sea cuts the land), score (a cut tally), sharp (what cuts well), short (cut short), shirt and skirt (cut cloth). Among this prolific kin the tool took the plainest name: Germanic *skero, the cutter, Old English scear, the cutting implement, plural scearra because blades travel in pairs.

Old English already knew the tool in every trade that divides: the tailor's long blades, the sheep-reeve's spring shears, the gardener's hedge blades. The plural hardened early into grammar, a plurale tantum like tongs and bellows, because no single blade shears alone. Charters and guild rolls list shears among the smith's finest work, and shearing time — the great spring gathering when flocks were stripped and fleeces weighed — was already the pastoral festival that ballads would sing for a thousand years.

Middle English multiplied the tribes without changing the blood. Chaucer's age ground tailor's shears for broadcloth, gardener's shears for knot-gardens, and tinner's shears for plate, each craft lengthening handles or bowing loops to its grip. The verb to shear worked beside the noun across the same fields: to shear sheep, to shear cloth (raising then cropping the nap), to shear through water and air in the ship's and bird's shearing passage. Tool and act glossed each other, the noun naming the blades and the verb naming their stroke.

The early modern centuries gave the word its mythology and its music. Atropos, the third Fate, snips the thread of life with her shears in every emblem book, moralising the garden tool into destiny's instrument — what shears wool shears years. Meanwhile the blades sang: the clack of sheep shears fills pastoral verse from ballads to Hardy, the spring soundscape of the shearing shed as recognisable as bells. Tolkien inherits both charges when Sam's shears clack beneath the window: honest labour's music set against the silence of power.

Phonetically the journey shows the great English palatalisation: Germanic sk- softened before front vowels to Old English sc- pronounced sh, so that *skeran became scearan, sounded much like modern shear. The long vowel levelled through Middle English schere to modern shear and shears, the spelling keeping its sh- digraph as the fossil of that softening. No other drama touched it; cutters keep their edge, and their names keep their shape.

Stages and bridges at a glance:

  • Cutting to sharing: the root's first great metaphor — what is cut apart is shared out — seeds half the modern vocabulary of division.
  • Act to instrument: Germanic derives the tool-noun from the verb, the cutter named for the cutting, blades for the stroke.
  • One blade to a pair: the plural hardens into grammar because shearing needs two edges meeting, the singular surviving only in compounds like shear legs.
  • Trade to trade: tailor, gardener, shepherd, and tinsmith each grind the same blood into their own edge without renaming it.
  • Wool to years: the Fates borrow the shepherd's tool for mortality, the final shearing shadowing every spring one.
  • Silence to song in Tolkien: Sam's shears clacking nearer the window turn the ancient tool into the sound of the Shire itself, labour persisting inside gathering dark.

So shears today carry four thousand years of cutting in two syllables: every hedge trimmed, every sheep stripped, every thread of life snipped, all meeting in Sam's small garden music beneath the window of power.
