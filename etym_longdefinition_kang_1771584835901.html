Kang (炕, pinyin kàng; earlier romanized k'ang or khang) is the traditional heated brick platform-bed of northern China — a raised structure of brick, clay, or adobe, hollow inside, with a network of flues running beneath its top surface. A firebox at one end — often connected to the kitchen stove — feeds heat and smoke through the flues, which warm the whole platform and exhaust through a chimney, so that the top stays warm through the winter night. The kang is simultaneously bed, sofa, dining surface, workbench, and social centre: by night the family sleeps across it in a row, each under his own quilt; by day it serves for sitting, eating, sewing, gambling, receiving visitors, and gossip. In the bitter winters of the northern plain and Manchuria it is the domestic heart of the home. Primary senses and cultural scope
  • Kang (the platform) — the heated brick bed-platform of the northern Chinese household; the standard noun sense in English.
  • Kang bed-stove — the nineteenth-century English gloss used by travellers and missionaries, a doubled translation that testifies to the fact that English had no single word for a bed that is also a stove.
  • Verb kàng (炕) — in Chinese, "to dry or roast by fire, to bake, to toast": grain, cloth, and food may be kàng'd over the fire. This verb sense is the older, primary sense; the platform is named for being warmed by fire, not for being slept on.
  • The kang (idiom of place) — in ethnographic and translated prose, the word stands for the intimate, heated core of the northern home, normally used with the definite article: "the kang", "on the kang".
Polysemy and the historical bridge The word is essentially a single, culturally saturated concept rather than a many-branched polysemy: a hollow, heated platform. Its double life lives inside Chinese grammar, where kàng is first a verb ("to dry by heat") and secondarily a noun ("the heated platform") — a development driven by northern China's harsh winters, in which a platform that could be warmed by flues became "the dried-and-heated bed". English borrowed only the noun, in the nineteenth century, through travel and missionary accounts, which met the object with a mixture of fascination and perplexity; early spellings include khang and k'ang, and the modern pinyin kang is now standard. The semantic bridge is functional: the object is defined not by posture but by heat — a kang is "something that is dried by fire" — which is why no European furniture word ever matched it, and why English simply borrowed the word instead of translating it. Register and use in the novel In English, kang is a cultural loanword — precise, unapologetically Chinese, normally used with the definite article or a demonstrative, and often italicized or glossed on first use. It is virtually the only Chinese furniture word to have entered English. In The Story of the Stone, the "large kang underneath the window, covered with a scarlet Kashmir rug" places the reader inside the warm, intimate heart of the Jia household: the rug itself is a luxury import from Kashmir, meeting the most ancient northern technology in one image — the oldest kind of heat, built into brick, dressed in the finest wool of the Himalayas. For Dai-yu, the detail confirms that she has entered not the ceremonial public halls but the lived-in core of the house, where comfort is engineered and warmth is laid on by architecture. English near-equivalents — bed, daybed, divan, platform — all miss the essential heating; the true technical relatives are the Korean ondol (a flue-heated floor) and the Japanese kotatsu (a heated table), neither of which is a platform one sleeps on.
