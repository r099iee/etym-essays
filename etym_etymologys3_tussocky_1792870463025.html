The history of tussocky is the history of a mystery with a perfectly regular ending: the suffix -y is textbook, but the root it grows on, tussock, is one of those blunt rural words that walks into print in the 1500s with no pedigree papers, already meaning what it means today.

The received handbook story derives tussock from tuft plus the diminutive ending -ock seen in hillock and bullock: tuft itself comes through Old French tofe, a crest or plume, ultimately Germanic, and on this account countrymen nativised an unfamiliar clipping into tussock by the same sound-play that turns tuft into something rounder and grassier on the tongue, with the double -ss- mimicking the hiss of seed-heads: the Oxford English Dictionary records tussock from the 1540s in exactly the modern sense, a tuft or clump of grass or sedge, yet labels the etymology uncertain, and honest lexicography must repeat that caution rather than smooth it away.

  • Tudor fields: the word’s first century is agricultural and local, naming the coarse uneaten clumps that mock the mower and shelter the coney, the rough that proves the pasture is truly pasture.
  • The regular adjective: by the 1700s writers need a word for land characterised by such clumps and reach for the productive suffix -y, already proven in boggy, mossy, and rocky, so tussocky enters farm surveys and county histories without fanfare, formed exactly as expected.
  • Settlement English: the great expansion comes with emigration, since New Zealand’s South Island, Australia’s high country, and the Falklands all grow true tussock grasslands dominated by Chionochloa, Poa, and Festuca species, and colonists make tussock and tussocky into landscape words on the scale of prairie and pampas, seeding them with sheep-runs, tussock fires, and conservation battles that continue today.
  • Sport and science: back in Britain the word settles into golf, where a tussocky lie ruins a stance, and into ecology, where tussocky sward becomes measured habitat, since voles tunnel under the thatch, bumblebees overwinter in it, and ground-nesting birds depend on exactly the roughness farmers once cursed.
  • Literary texture: poets and novelists from the Romantics onward keep tussocky for its mouth-feel, the triple sibilant-and-stop that trips the tongue the way the ground trips the foot, and Tolkien’s choice in the journey through the thickets belongs to that lineage: one dialect-textured adjective doing the work of a paragraph of explanation.

Phonetically the word is pure expressive English: the initial t- taps, the -uss- hisses like wind in seed-heads, the -ock thumps like a boot against a root-stool, and the final -y opens into the adjective with the ease of long habit, so that even without knowing its origin every hearer guesses it means lumpy grass, which is perhaps why an uncertain etymology never hindered a certain and useful word.
