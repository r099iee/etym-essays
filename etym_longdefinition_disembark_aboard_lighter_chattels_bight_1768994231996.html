disembark — The settlers "disembarked aboard a lighter": they left their oceangoing vessel and came down into a small transfer boat. The word marks the precise threshold between the fluid world of the ship and the solid world of the land, and here that threshold is doubly negotiated, because they step not straight onto a quay but onto an intermediate craft. The tone is formal, logbook-archival: disembark is the verb of manifests and shipping records, and its very dryness makes the settlers' arrival feel procedural, a matter of record, even as the shoreline they study is strange and faint.

  • Primary sense: to leave a ship or aircraft and go ashore — the passenger's act of stepping off a vessel onto land.
  • Secondary technical use: in aviation, "passengers will disembark through the rear door" — the word has crossed from sea to air, becoming the standard formal verb for leaving any passenger conveyance.
  • Derived forms: disembarkation (the noun, remodelled on French débarquement), disembarkation point, disembarkation card (the immigration form).
  • Antonym: embark, "to go aboard," keeping the pair in the vocabulary of departures and arrivals.

Synonym boundaries: Land is the general verb applying to ship or people; disembark refers only to the people leaving the vessel. Go ashore is the plain spoken idiom where disembark is bureaucratic. Debark is a learned seventeenth-century synonym now mostly technical. Alight belongs to getting down from a vehicle and stresses the descending motion. Unload applies to cargo, not people. Disembark occupies the formal, agent-focused end of the spectrum.

aboard — The settlers "disembarked aboard a lighter": they came down off the big ship and onto the smaller boat, so "aboard" here means "on or into" the lighter. The word marks the state of being on a vessel — or the act of entering it — and its usefulness lies in covering both the static condition (being aboard) and the dynamic entry (to come aboard). The tone is plain, nautical, and immediate; "aboard" is one of the oldest function words of the sea, the small brick on which whole sentences of maritime life are built.

  • Primary senses: (1) preposition/adverb meaning "on or into a ship, aircraft, train, or other vehicle"; (2) the cry "all aboard!" — the boarding call; (3) the idiom "welcome aboard," extended to any enterprise or team.
  • Extension beyond the sea: "aboard the train," "aboard the plane," "aboard the bus" — from the 19th century the word rides any conveyance, and in modern office idiom "welcome aboard" means joining a company.
  • Etymology within the word: "a-" (on) + "board" (the planked deck/side of a ship). To be aboard is to be "on board," the two forms diverging in register, "aboard" the more compact and nautical.

Synonym boundaries: On board is the analytic equivalent, slightly more formal in some uses; aboard is the fused, traditional form. On alone is the neutral preposition but lacks the specific sense of being on a vessel or vehicle. Embarked names the act of going aboard, whereas aboard is the resulting state or the location.

lighter — The settlers crossed "aboard a lighter": a flat-bottomed boat used to ferry goods and passengers between a deep-draft ship and the shore. The word is a worker's noun, named for what the boat does — it lightens, or relieves, the big ship of part of its load. The tone is practical and harbor-specific, the everyday machinery of a port rendered in one blunt word.

  • Primary sense: a flat-bottomed barge, unpowered or lightly powered, used to load and unload ships that cannot come close to the quay; the trade and its fee are lighterage, and the worker is a lighterman.
  • Secondary sense (modern common): a pocket device for producing a flame — a cigarette lighter. This is the same word's descendant by metaphor of function: what makes light.
  • Ambiguity trap: lighter is also simply the comparative adjective "more light (in weight)"; "this box is lighter than that one" is the adjective, not the boat. The context (a vessel) disambiguates.

Synonym boundaries: Barge is the general term for a large flat-bottomed cargo boat; a lighter is the specific type used for transferring cargo between ship and shore. Ferry carries people across a fixed route; a lighter is for freight and runs between ship and shore. Dinghy is a small tender for passengers, not cargo. Punt is a shallow flat-bottomed boat poled along rivers, not used for ship-to-shore cargo.

chattels — The settlers came "with their chattels": their movable personal property, the possessions they could carry onto the boat and into the new land. The word is legal in register and historical in weight — the small things of a life, pots, tools, bedding, that move with their owner. It stands in deliberate contrast to the fixed world of land and house left behind.

  • Primary sense (law): movable personal property, as distinct from real property (land and buildings). The old legal formula "goods and chattels" means all personal belongings.
  • Historical sense: from Old French chatel "property, wealth," the word originally included livestock — indeed it is the doublet of cattle: the same Latin root split into two English forms, one meaning "property" (chattel) and one meaning "livestock" (cattle).
  • Dark modern extension: chattel slavery — slavery in which human beings are legally reduced to movable personal property, the harshest legal category the word has ever named.

Synonym boundaries: Belongings and possessions are the plain, neutral everyday words; chattels is the technical legal term. Effects (as in "personal effects") is legal-administrative and slightly cooler. Goods is commercial. Assets implies value for payment of debts, a financial rather than a domestic view. Only chattels carries the double register of property law and of the historical livestock-from-property derivation.

bight — The settlers study "the thin bight of sand and scrub pine swimming in the haze": a shallow curve of the coastline, a wide gentle indentation holding the sand and trees between two points of land. The word is geographic and nautical at once, and its tone is precise and understated — a bight is not a dramatic bay but a modest, sweeping curve, exactly the low shoreline in haze that the sentence describes.

  • Primary sense (geography): a wide, shallow curve or indentation in a coastline — larger and more open than a cove, shallower and gentler than a gulf. Fixed names include the Great Australian Bight and the Bight of Benin.
  • Secondary sense (seamanship): the loop or slack middle section of a rope between its two ends — "the bight of the line." The bend of the rope echoes the bend of the shore.
  • Etymological note: the word is native English, from Old English byht "a bend," kin to bow "to bend," bough "an arm/branch (a bent limb)," and elbow. The underlying idea in both geography and seamanship is curvature.

Synonym boundaries: Bay is larger and more enclosed, a body of water partly landlocked; a bight is a shallower, more open curve of the coastline itself. Cove is small, sheltered, and circular; a gulf is large and deep. Inlet is any opening of the sea into the land, emphasizing entry rather than curvature. Loop is the general word for a rope's circle, of which the nautical bight is the specialist term.
