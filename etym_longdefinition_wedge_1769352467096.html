wedge is a noun and verb of English that names one of the simplest and oldest mechanical devices: a piece of wood, metal, or stone with a thick end and two sloping sides that taper to a thin edge, driven between objects to split them apart, to secure them, to lift them, or to hold them open. In geometry and everyday description the word names anything of that tapering triangular shape — and from the shape it has spread, in modern English, into golf, food, fashion, sports, and social metaphor. In the context card, "wedge" describes the split V-shaped print left by a deer's cloven hoof, each half of the hoof being a tapering, wedge-like structure.

PRIMARY SENSE — the tapering tool

  • The mechanical wedge — a thick-to-thin piece of wood, metal, or stone driven into a crack or between two objects; it splits logs along the grain, splits rock along cleavage lines, secures a door, raises or levels a heavy object, or holds two parts together. The wedge is one of the six classical simple machines, a double inclined plane, and one of the most ancient tools of humanity.
  • Split / secure / lift — to drive a wedge is to force the sloping faces between objects; the pressure converts the driving force into a powerful lateral splitting or separating force.
  • Historical and technical uses — the feller's wedge (steel or wood, driven into a saw cut to stop the saw binding), the axe-wedge that holds the axe head on the handle, the masonry wedge for splitting stone, the door-wedge that holds a door open or shut.

SECONDARY SENSE — the V-shaped object (geometric transfer)

Because every wedge is a tapering triangle, the word came to name anything of that shape, spreading through countless everyday domains:

  • Food — a wedge of cheese, a wedge of pie or cake, a wedge of lemon: a triangular slice or chunk cut from a larger round or block.
  • Geometry and science — a wedge is any solid tapering to a thin edge; the "wedge" of light, a "wedge-shaped" formation; in physics, a wedge is a simple machine; in mechanics the "wedge" is studied as a device that converts force.
  • Golf — the "wedge" is a family of high-lofted clubs (pitching wedge, sand wedge, gap wedge, lob wedge) designed to hit short, high, soft shots; the club head is wedge-shaped. The term is modern golf jargon from the twentieth century.
  • Footwear — the "wedge heel" (or simply "wedges"), a shoe whose heel and sole form a single tapering platform; the shape transfers from the tool to the shoe.
  • Animal tracks and natural shapes — the context card: the split tracks of a deer's cloven hoof are described as "wedge-shaped"; each half-hoof tapers like a wedge. Bird flight formations ("a wedge of geese") and tectonic or geographic wedges use the same shape.

TERTIARY SENSE — the verb

  • To wedge something — to fix, jam, or force something into a narrow space with a wedge-like motion: "he wedged the door open," "she wedged herself into the seat." The verb applies the tool's action to any tight, forceful insertion.
  • To be wedged — to be stuck fast, unable to move, as if held by a wedge: "the rock was wedged in the crevice."
  • To wedge between people — to force separation between persons or groups, as a wedge splits wood: "to wedge them apart."

QUATERNARY SENSE — the metaphor of division and beginning

  • To drive a wedge between people — to create division or hostility between persons, groups, or nations; the metaphor is as old as the tool: the wedge splits what was united.
  • The thin end of the wedge — a small, seemingly harmless step or concession that is the beginning of a larger, more dangerous development; the phrase (attested from the nineteenth century) warns that a slight opening will be driven wider.
  • Political and social use — "wedge issue" or "wedge politics": a deliberately polarising policy or topic used to split an opponent's coalition, exactly as a wedge splits timber.
  • The flying wedge — a triangular tactical formation in military and sports history, a body of men or players arranged like a wedge to break a line.

REGISTER AND SUMMARY

  • Literal core — the tapering tool for splitting, securing, lifting, or holding; the word's oldest, most mechanical sense.
  • Geometric transfer — any V-shaped or triangular-tapering object, from cheese slices to hoof prints to golf clubs to shoes.
  • Verbal force — to force, jam, or insert tightly; to be stuck fast.
  • Figurative power — division, splitting, polarisation, and the "thin end of the wedge"; the metaphor runs from personal quarrels to national politics.
