Danes (plural proper noun, singular Dane) denotes the Germanic people of Denmark — historically the Viking-Age Scandinavian raiders, traders, and settlers who attacked and colonised England, Ireland, France, and beyond (8th–11th centuries), and in modern use simply nationals or natives of Denmark. Durant's phrase "eleventh-century Danes" means the late-Viking Danes of Sweyn Forkbeard and Cnut, who conquered England in 1013–1016 — presented by Durant as still semi-primitive peoples among whom cannibalism was allegedly found.

The Danes emerged from Iron-Age Jutland, Zealand, and Scania, unified under kings like Gorm and Harald Bluetooth (who Christianised Denmark c. 965), launched the Viking expansion (Lindisfarne 793 onward), established the Danelaw in England, Normandy in France (Rollo, 911), and briefly a North Sea empire under Cnut. By the eleventh century they were Christian, literate in runes and Latin, burying their dead in churchyards — the opposite of funeral-less savages.

  • Viking-historical sense (core in Durant): Norse invaders and settlers: Great Heathen Army, Danelaw Danes, Cnut's housecarls. Connotation in English sources: fierce, pagan, tribute-exacting (Danegeld). This is Durant's frame — Danes as late barbarians proving savagery survived into the Middle Ages.
  • Medieval-settler sense: Danes as colonists turned neighbours: farmers of Yorkshire, founders of York (Jorvik), Dublin, Rouen. Bridge: raiders settle, marry, farm, convert. Archaeology shows mixed Anglo-Scandinavian villages, not cannibal camps.
  • Modern national sense: Citizens of Denmark: the Danes voted, the Danes design furniture. Neutral, contemporary demonym. Bridge: nation-state replaces tribe; Viking past becomes heritage branding (museums, football chants).
  • Adjectival-ethnic extensions: Danish language, Danish pastry (actually Viennese origin), Great Dane (German dog breed), Danish bacon. Bridge: Denmark as quality-mark; meaning bleached of ethnicity.
  • Durant-era anthropological claim: Eleventh-century Danes as cannibals with no funerals. Bridge: early-20th-century uncritical use of hostile chronicles (Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Norman propaganda) and saga exaggeration. Modern assessment: demonstrably false — 11th-c. Denmark is one of archaeology's best-attested funeral cultures (Lund, Roskilde churchyards; rune-stones commemorating the dead; elaborate ship and chamber graves just before).
  • Grammar: Always capitalised. The Danes with article = the people collectively; Danes without article in general statements. Singular Dane rare in Durant-like prose; adjective Danish for language/culture.

In sum, Danes spans thirteen centuries: from tribal Dani known to Jordanes, through Viking Dene feared and taxed, to modern Danes designing chairs and wind turbines. Durant arrests the word at its most frightening hour to serve a universal-savagery thesis; the fuller history shows Christianisation, settlement, and law — the Danelaw's wapentakes and thing-mootes — already carrying Danes deep into medieval civilisation.
