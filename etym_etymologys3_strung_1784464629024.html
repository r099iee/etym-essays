Strung is not an ancient root in its own right but the strong past tense and past participle of the verb string, so its true biography is the biography of string — a native Germanic word whose whole history turns on one stubborn image: a thin cord, drawn taut, loaded with things or stretched to sound. To tell the story honestly we must begin with the Indo-European idea of “tautness,” watch it become a Germanic cord, follow the cord into Old English, and then trace how the verb — and above all its strong past strung — spun out the whole modern web of necklaces, bows, rackets, nerves, and vibrating strings of physics. 1. The Indo-European root and the Germanic cord The secure continental starting point is the reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *strangiz, “cord, rope, string,” built on a root whose basic sense was “tight, stiff, drawn taut.” The daughter forms are abundant and mutually confirming: Old English streng, Old Saxon streng, Old Norse strengr, Old High German strang — all “cord, rope, string.” Because the word appears in every Germanic branch with the same concrete meaning, it must already have been part of the shared Germanic vocabulary spoken in the late second and first millennia BCE, long before the Anglo-Saxons crossed the North Sea. Beyond Germanic, the same “taut, stiff” root is usually connected with Latin stringere, “to draw tight, bind, press together,” the ancestor of English strict, constrict, stringent, and strain. The semantic core that joins them all is tension: a cord is made of fibres twisted and pulled tight, and every later sense of string — musical, martial, neural, physical — is an echo of that original tautness. The exact shape of the Indo-European root is debated — some handbooks write *strenk-, some *streng-, some *stere- “thick, stiff” — but the Germanic-to-English line is historically secure. 2. Old English streng: a cord in a culture of cordage In Old English the noun streng meant a cord, a rope, a string, or a sinew — the everyday fastening technology of an early medieval world that had no buttons, zips, or elastic. Strings lashed, bound, hung, and tightened. From the same noun the Anglo-Saxons made verbs: to bind or furnish with a cord, to stretch a line taut. The culture of streng was practical and total: ropes on ships, strings on lyres and harps, sinews drawn on bows, cords lacing garments and hanging tools. The noun did a double duty that the modern word still does — it named both the physical cord and, by a natural extension, the sequence of things gathered on it, for a string of onions or of beads was literally one cord run through many items. 3. Middle English: noun, verb, and the birth of the threading verb Middle English inherited the word as streng and string, and from the noun the language formed the denominative verb strengen / stringen: to furnish with a string, to bind with a cord, to stretch tight. The verb's two great transitive jobs are both attested early and both survive: stringing a bow (fitting it with a taut cord) and stringing objects on a cord (threading beads, pearls, and other small valuables). The threading sense is the one the dwarves' song exploits at its most ancient and luminous — and it is worth noting how old the pearl-and-necklace image is: the whole European tradition of threading precious beads on a line is reflected in the verb from its earliest recorded English. Alongside the verb, the noun string also named the row itself — “a string of horses,” “a string of beads” — the sequence sense hardening early. 4. Early modern English and the mystery of strung The inflectional heart of this card appears in the early modern period. Originally the verb string was weak (regular): its past and participle were stringed. But from the sixteenth century onward, English speakers increasingly formed the strong past strung by analogy with the great ablaut class of sing–sang–sung, ring–rang–rung, and swing–swung. The analogy was irresistible: same shape, same rhyme, same feel of a physical action. By the seventeenth century strung had won in the transitive verb; stringed retreated into the musical adjective, where a stringed instrument is “fitted with strings” but a bow is strung and pearls are strung. The split is a live example of English morphology changing under the pressure of analogy — no committee decided it; thousands of speakers simply preferred the rhyming strong form. By the modern period the distinction was fixed: stringed for the equipment (stringed instruments), strung for the action and its result. 5. Semantic drift outward: from cord to nerves, slang, and physics With the form settled, the senses travelled far beyond cordage.
  • Highly strung (18th–19th centuries): a temperament “drawn too tight,” literally like an over-tuned string; the transfer from instrument tension to human nervousness is one of English's most perfect nerve metaphors.
  • Strung out (20th century, slang): originally of drug addicts whose nerves were frayed as if by an over-stretched cord; then broadened to anyone exhausted, frazzled, or desperate. The word keeps its ancient physics: strain under tension.
  • Strung together: loosely assembled, improvised — “a few chords strung together” — the sequence sense without the craft.
  • String theory (physics, late 20th century): the name borrows the image of a vibrating, tensioned line, though the “string” is mathematical and sub-atomic, not cordage.
  • Strings of characters (computing) and stringing someone along (deceiving, delaying): two modern extensions of the sequence-and-line image.
6. In the dwarves' song In Tolkien's stanza — “On silver necklaces they strung / The flowering stars” — the strong past participle carries the full weight of the word's history. The action is threading, the oldest recorded use of the verb; the past form marks it completed in the legendary past; and the cord image, born as ordinary Germanic cordage and strained through music, bows, and nerves, is here put to its most luminous service — light itself strung on silver like pearls on a thread. The card's STRUNG is deliberately the strong form, not the base string and not the regular stringed: it is the shape English speakers chose, by analogy and by ear, for the completed act of threading and tensioning, and the song makes that ancient act a myth.
