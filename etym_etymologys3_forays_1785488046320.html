The story of FORAYS runs from the herding of livestock in Indo-European antiquity to the academic excursions of modern prose. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *peh₂-, whose concrete sense was "to protect, to tend, to graze, to feed" — the root of the shepherd guarding his flock and leading it to pasture. The root shows its age across the whole Indo-European family. In Italic it produced Latin pāscere "to feed, to pasture", with its derivative pāstor "shepherd", the ancestor of English pastor and pasture; pāscere also generated pānis "bread", the food that sustains, and through Italian pane the culinary loan panino. In Indo-Iranian the same root gave Sanskrit pāti "protects, tends" and pālana "nurture, protection", preserved in modern Hindi pālan (पालन). In the Germanic branch the root took the shape *fōdrą, "food for animals, fodder" — literally what is fed to beasts — and produced a whole family: Old English fōdor (modern fodder), fōda (modern food), and the verb *fōdijaną (modern feed).

It was the Germanic noun that carried the word into French and then into English. The Franks, a Germanic people settled in Gaul, brought their *fōdar "fodder" into the Gallo-Romance speech around them, and it passed into Old French as forre or fuerre, "straw, hay, fodder". From forre the French formed the verb forrer, "to forage, to pillage for provisions", and its relative forrayer, "to ride out on a raid". In medieval warfare the connection was brutally direct: armies and garrisons needed forage for horses and livestock, and they obtained it by riding out into enemy territory to seize it. The raid for fodder — the foray — was one of the most common operations of medieval campaigning, a swift, limited cavalry expedition designed to gather provisions, destroy the enemy's crops, and test his defences. The chronicles of the Hundred Years War and the border wars of Britain are full of such forays.

English took the word in the fourteenth century. The verb forrayen, "to plunder, to raid", is recorded in Middle English, and the noun foray, "an incursion or raid", followed. For the first three centuries of its English life the word was strictly military: chronicles record forays against the Scots, the Welsh, and the French, and the word carried all the menace of raiding and devastation. Its companion forage came in at the same time and from the same French source, so that the two words travelled together, one naming the plunder and the other the act of seeking it.

The great semantic change came in the seventeenth century, when the military sense began to loosen:

  • 14th–16th c.: "a raid for plunder and fodder" — hostile, violent, military.
  • 16th–18th c.: the sense widens to "any sudden attack or incursion beyond one's own line" — still aggressive, no longer tied to fodder.
  • 19th c.: the slide toward the benign begins; a foray becomes "a brief venture or excursion into unfamiliar territory" — short, purposeful, slightly risky, but no longer martial.
  • 20th–21st c.: foray is almost always figurative — a venture into a neighbouring field of knowledge, a new market, an unfamiliar style.

The author's "forays into the domains of linguistics, psychology, and music" thus preserve, in ghost form, the medieval picture of mounted raiders riding briefly into foreign territory to gather useful provisions and then returning home. The plunder is now knowledge, the enemy fields are neighbouring disciplines, and the only violence left is the faint thrill of risk that makes the word more vivid than excursion. The word's modern success is exactly this: it keeps the memory of a raid — short, bold, self-contained, foreign — while being wholly respectable.
