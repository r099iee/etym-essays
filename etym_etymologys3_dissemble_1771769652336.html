The biography of dissemble begins not with masking but with likeness. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *sem-, which encoded unity and sameness — "one," "together," "the same" — the base of the entire same/similar family across the Indo-European languages. In Latin, *sem- produced similis "like, similar," and from similis Latin built a remarkable cluster of likeness-verbs: simulāre "to make like, to imitate," assimilāre "to make alike," and the passive-colored sembler-line that passes into Romance. The positive craft of likeness — imitation, art, resemblance — sits at the center of the family, and it is from this craft of making things look alike that the whole vocabulary of seeming descends: English simulate, similar, same, assemble, resemble, and semblance are all cousins.

Roman rhetoric sharpened the semantic tools. Latin simulāre could mean both neutral "to imitate, to copy" and morally loaded "to feign, to pretend." When Roman orators and moralists wanted a stronger term for the art of concealing the truth, they prefixed dis- ("apart, un-") to reverse the vector: dissimulāre is not "to fail to imitate" but "to make [what is true] look unlike itself" — to disguise, cloak, or conceal. Where simulation projects a false positive likeness (feigning a thing that is not there), dissimulation hides a real state under a different surface. Cicero and later imperial rhetoricians treated dissimulatio as a recognized rhetorical figure — irony or feigned ignorance — and moralists began to use the word family for the very social vice that polite society cultivated.

The medieval transmission is where the modern doublet was born. Old French took Latin dissimulāre through two channels: a learned form dissimuler and a vernacular form dissembler, the latter reshaped by association with sembler "to seem." English borrowed both: dissemble (the more idiomatic, theatrical literary verb, arriving in the late 14th century, e.g. in Chaucer) and, in the 15th century, the directly Latinized dissimulate. English thus holds a genuine doublet pair — two words, one ultimate root, each slightly different in register and nuance.

The ethical history of the word in English is rich:
  • 14th-15th century — Middle English dissemblen means "to pretend, to conceal," used in religious and moral contexts where hypocrisy is the sin under attack; the noun dissimulacioun and the adjective dissembling appear alongside it.
  • 16th century — The verb becomes a standard term of courtly and political analysis: the Tudor court, and the literature it produced, made "dissembling" a keyword for the gap between outward favor and inward intent. Shakespeare uses forms of the word across comedies, histories, and tragedies as shorthand for false seeming; moral and conduct literature contrasts "plain dealing" with "dissembling."
  • 1611 — The King James Bible gives a memorable attestation of the agent noun: the Psalmist refuses to sit with "vain persons" nor go in with "dissemblers" (Psalm 26:4), cementing the word's association with hypocritical company.
  • 17th century — Francis Bacon's essay "Of Simulation and Dissimulation" (1625) makes the word family a systematic topic of political ethics, distinguishing degrees of concealment and weighing when dissimulation is prudent and when it is corrupting.
  • 18th century — The formula "I will not dissemble…" becomes a polished gesture of candor in memoir, essay, and oratory; Gibbon's close is its most famous instance, where the renunciation of the mask is itself a performance of sincerity.
  • 19th century to present — The verb retreats from speech into elevated prose; everyday English prefers "hide," "pretend," or "conceal," while "dissemble" keeps its theatrical, deliberate force in historiography, political writing, and literary fiction.
The etymological opposition to resemble remains instructive: to resemble is to look like; to dissemble is to refuse to look like what one truly is. In Gibbon's closing line, the refusal to dissemble restores likeness between face and feeling — the scholar lets the outer expression match the inner joy of recovered freedom and, perhaps, established fame. The word that began as a craft of likeness ends as the conscious choice of candor.
