bolas are a throwing weapon consisting of two or more heavy balls, traditionally of stone, metal, or wood covered in leather, connected by strong cords or thongs, whirled and thrown so that the cords wrap around and entangle the legs or body of an animal or person. In the source context they appear in a museum catalogue of primitive weapons alongside knives, lassos, and boomerangs, as instruments by which early humans secured land and safety from beasts.

The core modern sense is always plural in form even when denoting a single weapon, because the weapon comprises multiple balls. A typical South American boleadora has two balls for war or large game called bola grande, or three balls for small game. The hunter holds one ball, spins the others overhead to build momentum, and releases, the weapon spreading in flight and then cinching tight on impact. Range is short, roughly twenty to fifty metres, and skill lies in timing the release so the cords arrive extended.

  • Primary weapon sense: weighted balls on cords for hunting rhea, guanaco, cattle, and horses in Argentina, Uruguay, Patagonia, and historically among Inuit and other peoples with similar designs. The balls injure by impact and, more importantly, hobble by wrapping.
  • Museum and ethnographic sense: bolas as type-specimen of hunter technology, displayed as relics of human ingenuity. This is the sense in the source sentence, where bolas stand for a stage of civilisation.
  • Sport and recreation sense: in modern gaucho festivals and historical re-enactment, throwing bolas at posts as accuracy contests, with rubber or padded versions for safety.
  • Figurative sense: anything that entangles or trips progress, as in bureaucratic bolas or legal bolas, drawing on the wrapping image. Rare but attested in journalism.
  • Design and toy sense: ladder golf or monkey-fist toys marketed as bolas, and climbing or bondage gear using the same principle of weighted ends.

Grammatically bolas functions as a plurale tantum noun in English: we say a pair of bolas or a set of bolas, rarely a bolas. The singular bola exists but is technical. Verb use is marginal: to bola means to throw bolas. Adjectival compounds such as bolas-throwing appear in ethnography.

Domain usages include anthropology, where bolas illustrate convergent invention across continents; military history, where Roman-era plumbata and medieval chain-shot are compared; and ranching, where elderly gauchos still prefer bolas for capturing animals without gunshot wounds that spoil hides. Literary nuance is romantic and archaic: bolas evoke open pampas, horsemanship, and a pre-industrial skill now preserved in museums, exactly the elegiac tone of the source passage about ungrateful posterity inheriting security.

Polysemy is limited because the weapon sense dominates, but the bridge from ball to weapon to entanglement metaphor explains each extension. Roundness motivated the Spanish name, wrapping action motivated the English figurative use, and museum display motivated the relic sense. No other common noun bolas exists in English, so ambiguity is low, unlike lure or sling which have many senses.
