- Early 19th c. - Emerged in written English literature from dialectal echoic speech, imitating the soft, hollow sound of an object falling into water.
- 1830s–1860s - Adopted into Victorian fiction and poetry as an expressive adverb (falling *plop*) and intransitive verb of movement.
- Late 19th–Early 20th c. - Canonized in children's literature and fantasy (Potter, Tolkien) to depict humorous, ungraceful, cushioned physical falls.
- Mid-20th c. - Commercialized in iconic advertising jingles (*Plop, plop, fizz, fizz*) and everyday domestic colloquialisms.
- Contemporary - Widely used across colloquial English for collapsing in exhaustion (*plopping on the sofa*) and vivid sensory nature writing.
