banjo primarily denotes a plucked stringed musical instrument with a distinctive round, drum-like body covered with stretched skin or plastic, a long fretted neck, and four or five strings played by plucking, strumming, or clawhammer picking. Its bright, twanging, percussive timbre, louder and sharper than a guitar, makes it emblematic of American folk, bluegrass, old-time, minstrel, Dixieland jazz, and Caribbean musics. A typical five-string bluegrass banjo has a resonator back, metal tone ring, bridge on a vellum head, and a short fifth-string drone peg halfway up the neck; a four-string tenor or plectrum banjo lacks the drone and serves jazz rhythm. To play the banjo is to conjure front porches, riverboats, and string bands even before a note is heard.

In the Wodehouse source sentence the instrument appears not as musicology but as racialised comic image: Every time you do it, I expect to see him turn black and start playing the banjo. The line invokes early-20th-century minstrel stereotypes that indecently tied Blackness to banjo-playing entertainers in blackface troupes. Modern readers must recognise this history: the banjo is indeed of Black Atlantic origin, created by enslaved Africans from West African lutes, but its reduction to a comic blackface prop in British humour of the 1920s-30s is a racist appropriation that the modern definition must name and contextualise rather than repeat uncritically. Understanding the word today therefore requires both organology and cultural history.

  • Organological core sense: round-bodied lute with membrane soundboard, long neck, 4-6 strings; e.g. five-string banjo, tenor banjo, banjo-ukulele, electric banjo. The bridge is acoustic: skin head plus short scale gives piercing attack that carries outdoors and over dancing feet. Makers distinguish open-back (folk, clawhammer) from resonator (bluegrass, loud).
  • Musical-genre emblem sense: shorthand for bluegrass, folk, minstrelsy, trad jazz; e.g. banjo roll, banjo picking, banjo band. Here the instrument stands for a whole sound world: hearing banjo predicts repertoire, technique, and social setting. The bridge is metonymy from tool to tradition.
  • Historical African-American origin sense: the banjo as Black creation, from Senegambian akonting, xalam, ngoni lutes remade with gourds on plantations. E.g. 18th-century Virginia banjer played by enslaved musicians. This recuperative sense, stressed since the 1970s, corrects earlier white-inventor myths and explains why minstrel appropriation stung: whites in blackface mocked the very creators.
  • Minstrel-stereotype and comic-prop sense (dated, offensive if unmarked): banjo as minstrel-show property alongside burnt cork, white gloves, and coon songs; e.g. Wodehouse line, early Hollywood cartoons. The bridge is racist metonymy: one Black art reduced to white comic mask. Modern usage cites this only historically and critically, never as innocent joke.
  • Extended slang and metaphor: in British and Australian slang banjo can jocularly mean buttocks or a shovel, and in sport to banjo something means to hit it hard (from the twang). E.g. cricket he banjoed it to the boundary. These are low-frequency puns on shape and sound, not core senses, but dictionaries record them.

Practically, collocations are musical: to play, pick, pluck, strum, tune the banjo; banjo player, banjo roll, banjo head, banjo neck, banjo strings. Verbs of sound are twang, plunk, ring, rattle. Adjectives are five-string, tenor, open-back, resonator, clawhammer. The definite article in playing the banjo follows instrument rule as with the piano. Pronunciation is straightforward, spelling never varies, plural banjos with -s.

For learners the takeaway is double: know the thing, a round drum-lute with brilliant voice, and know its story, from West Africa to Carolina plantations to blackface stages to bluegrass festivals to world-music revival, so that a line like Wodehouse is heard accurately as period humour carrying a history that modern performance seeks to honour and repair.
