A moose is a very large deer (Alces alces), the largest living member of the deer family, with long legs, a heavy overhanging muzzle, a dewlap or bell, and in males broad palmate antlers, native to northern North America, Scandinavia, and Russia, where it is called elk in British English.

In Wodehouses simile — the quiet stolidity of a stuffed moose — the animal matters less as zoology than as décor and character: a mounted trophy head or whole beast in a hall, glassy-eyed, massive, and solemnly unresponsive, the perfect visual for a man in discomfort who refuses to react. The comedy lies in scale: comparing a dinner-jacketed Englishman to half a ton of Canadian ungulate makes his dignity both impressive and absurd.

Biologically, moose are solitary browsers, not grazers: they strip twigs, bark, and aquatic plants, swim powerfully, and rut noisily in autumn. The Algonquian name itself means twig-eater, an exact ecological description fossilised as a proper name.

Polysemy and extended senses today include:
  • The animal itself (primary zoological sense): bull moose, cow moose, calf; moose meat, moose hunting, moose crossing signs. The bridge is direct borrowing from Algonquian for the New World animal.
  • Mounted trophy or toy image: a stuffed moose head, a moose on the wall, Bullwinkle the cartoon moose. The bridge is metonymy from living beast to its preserved likeness as décor or mascot.
  • Emblem of northern solemnity or goofiness: to look like a moose means to look large, solemn, and slightly bewildered. The bridge is visual and behavioural analogy exploited by Wodehouse and later comedy.
  • Canadian / Alaskan identity marker: the moose as national symbol alongside the beaver and loon — on coins, jerseys, and tourist shops. The bridge is cultural emblematism, turning fauna into nationhood.
  • British-American lexical trap (elk vs. moose): what Britons call elk, Americans call moose, and vice versa for wapiti. The bridge is colonial misnaming of large deer, now fixed by usage.
Unlike deer, which is a generic family term from Old English, moose is a precise New World loan with no verb or adjective family except moosey (humorous for moose-like) and compounds like moose-call, moose-hide, moose-knuckle (slang by separate analogy). Its zero plural — one moose, many moose — marks it as a hunted animal in English grammar.

Behaviourally, the animal behind the name reinforces the simile Wodehouse exploits: moose are famously unhurried, standing motionless for long periods while browsing, staring levelly at intruders before deciding to move, and tolerating deep cold and insect swarms with apparent indifference. Hunters and naturalists from Lewis and Clark to Farley Mowat remark on this grave, almost judicial demeanour, which makes the beast seem stolid by nature rather than by effort. To call a man moose-like is therefore to credit him with size, stillness, and imperviousness at once, while quietly suggesting that his dignity, like the animals drooping muzzle and ungainly gait, may be grand and comical in the same instant.
