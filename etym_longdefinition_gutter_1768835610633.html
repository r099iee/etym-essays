gutter (verb, intransitive): of a candle, to burn unsteadily so that the melted wax runs down the side in channels, causing the flame to splutter, waver, and waste itself. In the sentence from Titus Groan the verb is an instrument of characterisation by negatives: the candles were “never allowed to fail or even to gutter.” Peake places “fail” and “gutter” on a scale of vigilance — failure is total extinction; guttering is the subtler betrayal, the first tremor of a flame already losing its discipline. To gutter is for the candle to dissolve into the very substance that feeds it: the wax melts asymmetrically, forms a channel — literally a gutter — down the side, and the flame, now swimming in its own fuel, flickers, darkens, and burns low. Rottcodd, the perfect sacristan of the Hall of the Bright Carvings, prevents even this microscopic untidiness; his nightly “replenishment” before nine o'clock is a rite against entropy. The word's tone is precise, faintly technical, and quietly ominous: it describes not catastrophe but the beginning of catastrophe, the first wobble of order. It belongs to the vocabulary of maintenance and decay that governs Gormenghast — a castle of perpetual minor erosion, where the guttering candle is the small allegory of everything wearing down.

Like the object it describes, the word is polysemous, and its senses radiate from the noun's core image of a channel or trough:
  • Noun — roof channel: the shallow trough fixed along the edge of a roof to collect and carry away rainwater (a rain gutter, eaves gutter).
  • Noun — street channel: the gutter at the side of a street where water and refuse run; hence the open drain or kennel of a road.
  • Noun — bowling: in ten-pin and lawn bowling, the channel or “gutter” beside the lane that catches a ball and runs it out of play (a gutter ball).
  • Noun — typography: the blank space or channel between printed columns, or the inner margin of a bound book where the pages are sewn (the gutter of a book).
  • Noun — figurative, social: “the gutter”, the lowest or most degraded level of society — the gutter press, gutter politics, to live in the gutter — the word for the lowest channel society can pour through.
  • Verb — candle (the sense in this card): to burn unevenly so the wax runs down in channels and the flame splutters and wastes; also, of a flame or light, to waver and dwindle toward extinction.
  • Verb — flow sense: to flow or run in a stream, or to channel a liquid; also to groove or cut channels in a surface (to gutter a road).

The historical bridge from noun to verb is direct and physical. The noun entered English in the thirteenth century from Old French goutiere, “a water-channel”, itself from the “drop” word goutte; for centuries it named the grooves that collect falling water. When chandlers and housekeepers in the eighteenth century needed a word for a candle burning badly, they reused the drainage sense: the melted wax literally runs down the candle's side in a little channel — a gutter — and the flame pours itself into its own drain. The verb is thus a perfect re-application of the noun's core image, and its figurative extension (a life “guttering out” like a candle wasting in its own wax) is a standard figure of wavering life approaching extinction in poetry and fiction from Dickens to the modernists.
