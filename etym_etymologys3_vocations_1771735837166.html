The word vocation is the story of a Latin root for speaking being drawn steadily inward until it names the most private thing a worker can feel — that a particular work is meant for them. Everything begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *wekw-, "to speak, to say, to utter," a root securely attested across the family: Greek preserves it in the aorist eipón, "I said"; Sanskrit in vákti, "he speaks"; Avestan in vačah-, "word." In Latin the root became vox, vōcis, "voice," and from it Latin built the denominative verb vocare, "to call, to summon, to name." Already in this verb the sense is wider than "speak": to call someone is to summon them, to address them, to give them a name. From vocare, with the noun suffix -tiō, came vocātiō, "a calling." In classical Roman use, vocatio was a practical, legal word: a summoning, a summons to court, an invitation or call to appear. There was nothing mystical about it. The transformation came with Christianity. Saint Paul, writing in Greek, spoke of the klesis — the calling — of God, the summons by which a person is brought into the Christian life; Jerome's Vulgate translated klesis as vocatio. Romans 11:29 speaks of the vocatio Dei that is irrevocable; 1 Corinthians 7:20 urges every person to remain in the vocatio in which they were called. Christian Latin thus endowed vocatio with a spiritual charge: the call of God to the individual soul. It was in this exclusively religious sense that the word entered English in the 1430s: a vocation was a divine summons, most famously a call to the priesthood or the religious life. A person "had a vocation" or was "called" by God to the cloister. The Reformation of the 16th century changed the word's social scope profoundly. Martin Luther and John Calvin rejected the medieval hierarchy that made the cloistered religious life the only true vocation; they taught that God calls every believer to serve him in the ordinary sphere of daily work. One's trade, craft, or household labor was itself a divine calling — the Latin vocatio applied to the farmer, the shoemaker, the mother as much as to the priest. This doctrine, sometimes called the Protestant work ethic, secularized the word while keeping its spiritual force: vocation broadened from "religious summons" to "the occupation to which God calls one and for which one is suited." By the 18th century the word could be applied to any honest occupation pursued with commitment, and the purely religious sense receded to the background (though it survived, as it still does, in Catholic usage for the call to the priesthood). The 19th century, the very century the passage describes, added the institutional and professional dimension:
  • Professionalization. As history, medicine, law, and the sciences formed professional identities with distinct methods, training, journals, and standards, each discipline differentiated into recognizable career roles. The passage registers this exactly: historians drew on archaeology, philology, anthropology, sociology, and economics, creating "new vocations of searchers for facts and movements and forces."
  • The plural form. The plural vocations became natural in this context: not one undifferentiated calling but many specialized ones, each with its own identity and training.
  • Vocational education. From the 19th century, "vocational" named training aimed at preparing people for specific occupations, and later "vocational guidance" meant helping people discover the career they were suited to — institutionalizing the very idea of aptitude-and-summons that the word had carried since the Reformation.
  • Modern secular sense. In 20th- and 21st-century English, vocation most often means a strong feeling of suitability for a particular career — work that fits one's abilities and sense of purpose, often pursued regardless of pay. The word still faintly echoes its origins: to call a job a vocation is to claim it summons you.
The sound-shift history is brief and clear because the word was borrowed, not inherited: Latin vocātiō became Old French vocacion, with the ordinary Romance loss of the medial short vowel and palatalization of ti- before -o (as in nation from nātiō). Middle English took vocacioun from French in the 1430s, and modern English vocalized the spelling to vocation. The semantic drift is the reverse of the phonetic path: the word began as an external legal summons, moved inward to a divine call, broadened to any God-given occupation in Reformation theology, and finally settled on a secular sense of aptitude, purpose, and professional identity — the sense in which Gibbon's discipline could create new vocations.
