- Old English strēaw — the dry stalk residue of threshed grain, used for bedding, thatch and fodder: a humble, everyday agricultural word with no special connotation, and the plural is a plain count of stalks.
- Middle English (13th–14th centuries) — the plural settles into proverbial dismissal: to be “not worth a straw” (or a stree) is to be worth nothing at all; the material comes to stand for absolute cheapness and worthlessness.
- 16th century — proverbs fix the fragile imagery at plural-form scale: a drowning man “catches at a straw” (by the 1530s), “straws in the wind” show which way events are blowing (late 16th c.); the plural becomes the natural form of these meteorological metaphors.
- 17th–18th centuries — “straw man” enters law and politics (a person of no substance stood as surety; a sham opponent); straw hats and straw-work become fashionable crafts; “a straw in the wind” joins the idiom stock.
- 19th century — the fateful tube: hollow rye-grass stems are sold as drinking “straws” (1850s); Marvin Stone patents spiral-wound paper straws (1888); “draw straws” for lots and “split straws” for quibbling are in use; “the last straw (that breaks the camel's back)” is fixed by mid-century (Dickens, 1848).
- 20th century — plastic displaces paper (1940s–1960s), and “straws” become the ubiquitous, cheap, disposable restaurant staple; the plural now defaults to the drinking-tube sense, while “legs like straws” remains the stock phrase for dizzy, buckling weakness.
- 21st century — the environmental turn flips perception: plastic straws, once the icon of convenience, become the icon of waste; bans and paper/bamboo/metal alternatives make “straws” a contested political word — while the old farm sense quietly endures in every idiom.
