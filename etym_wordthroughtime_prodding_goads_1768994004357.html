prodding:
- 16th c. - prod appears as a noun: 'a pointed instrument, a goad, a pricking stick'; a blunt instrument of unknown descent, plausibly echoic of the quick jab it names.
- 16th–17th c. - the verb follows: to prod, to prick or urge with a point; prodding names the repetitive, insistent jab of the drover, sitting between the casual poke and the violent jab.
- 19th–20th c. - the figurative life: to prod someone into action, to prod a memory, a conscience - small, persistent stimulus that gets a reluctant thing moving.
- 20th c. - the cattle prod makes the ancient tool modern and impersonal; prodding is now mechanical, businesslike, faintly comic - the routine jab of exasperated handlers.

goads:
- Old English - gād, the drover's and shepherd's pointed stick, recorded in Ælfric's glosses and the pastoral law; an ancient word of driving.
- Middle English goode to modern goad - the iron-tipped stick for driving cattle, the harsh mechanical link between human urgency and animal inertia.
- Figurative life - to goad someone into action, the goad of ambition, a taunt that goads; in the 17th c. gadfly joins the family, a fly that stings cattle as a goad would.
- Contemporary - the concrete instrument persists (often electric), and the figurative goad - anything that stings or urges a person into action - remains the standard image of compulsion.
