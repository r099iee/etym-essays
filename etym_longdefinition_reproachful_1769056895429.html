reproachful (adjective) — full of reproach: expressing blame, disapproval, or disappointed censure, especially through the eyes, the face, or the voice. In the passage from The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, the word carries the whole moral temperature of a sibling quarrel: "'Susan! How can you?' said Lucy with a ________ glance." Lucy has just announced that the wardrobe leads to Narnia; Susan, the eldest, has dismissed it as fantasy; and Lucy's glance is the rebuke of the true believer to the sceptic — hurt, wounded, indignant, but not angry. The nuance is precisely the fusion of accusation and affection that "reproachful" supplies: the glance does not shout or sneer; it tells Susan, "you, of all people, ought to know better; you have failed me, and yourself." Where a furious look would end the conversation, a reproachful glance is an appeal — it names the fault and simultaneously begs the faulting person to recognise it. It is the moral look, the look that makes the other person feel the distance between what they are and what they should be. The word is therefore the perfect instrument for Lucy, the honest child of the book, whose certainty is so complete that even her disapproval is a form of faith. There is irony in the word too: the reproach is justified, since Narnia is real, and Susan — standing in for the sceptical adult — is the one whose doubt the novel will quietly judge. The synonym field is a ladder of blame, and "reproachful" occupies the personal, wounded rung. Disapproving is cooler and more distant: a raised eyebrow, a pursed mouth, a verdict passed from outside — it can concern behaviour, taste, or fashion and need involve no personal hurt. Reproving is didactic and parental: it corrects, instructs, and expects amendment, the tone of a teacher or elder rather than a peer. Rebuking is sharper and more vocal — a rebuke is spoken out loud, often sternly, and carries public censure. Condemnatory escalates to formal, absolute judgement, the language of courts and moralists, with no residue of affection. Scornful and contemptuous add contempt and superiority — the sneer of someone who looks down, where the reproachful person typically stands on the same level and is hurt rather than haughty. Accusatory bluntly charges the other with a specific wrong and invites defence. Admonishing and chiding verge toward open lecturing or scolding. What makes reproachful unique is the mixture of blame with pain and expectation: the reproachful glance says "you have let me down," not "you are worthless" — and it presupposes a relationship worth appealing to. It is the word for disappointed love, and its etymology ("to bring a fault back near") is the etymology of a fault made intimate, pressed up against the offender. Collocationally, reproachful rides on the organs of expression: "a reproachful look/glance/stare/gaze," "reproachful eyes," "a reproachful tone/voice," "to be reproachful (toward/with someone)," "in a reproachful manner," and the adverb "reproachfully" ("he looked reproachfully at her"). It appears with verbs of speaking and looking — "said/gave/glanced with a reproachful expression" — and typically in dialogue-heavy narrative prose, especially of the Victorian and Edwardian kind that Lewis's children's fiction quietly imitates. Its register is literary and slightly formal; in everyday speech we would more likely say "guilt-tripping," "huffy," or "disappointed." The word carries no rage — only the quiet, damning arithmetic of a love that has been disappointed — and that is exactly the note struck when Lucy, clutching her truth, turns her reproachful glance on the sister who does not believe her. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:
  • Expressing blame or disapproval (primary) — the adjective "full of reproach": expressing censure, disappointed blame, or criticism, most often through the look, the eyes, the tone, or the voice rather than open accusation.
  • Disappointed, hurt-tinged censure — the shade that distinguishes the word from colder synonyms: a reproachful manner presupposes affection and expectation, so it conveys wounded disappointment ("you have let me down") rather than detached judgement.
  • Manner and adverb — "reproachfully," "a reproachful way," describing how an action or remark is delivered; the adverb is common in narrative ("she looked at him reproachfully").
  • Related nouns and verbs of the family — "reproach" itself (the noun: "a reproach," "beyond reproach," "a reproach to X"; the verb: "to reproach someone with/for something"), "self-reproach," "reproachfulness," and "unreproachful" — the whole family turns on the same bringing-a-fault-near image.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge — the word is the noun/verb "reproach" plus the native suffix -ful ("full of"), built in English by the sixteenth century. "Reproach" entered Middle English from Anglo-Norman in the fourteenth century, from Old French reprochier "to upbraid, blame," from a Vulgar Latin verb *repropāre "to bring (a fault) back near," formed from re- "back, again" and prope "near." The entire meaning is packed into that etymology: to reproach is to fetch the fault that someone pushed away and set it close to them again, within arm's reach of their conscience — and a reproachful glance does precisely that, silently, without a word of anger.
