BLEAT is the imitative verb for the thin, trembling, plaintive cry of sheep, goats, and young calves — a vocalization so distinctive that the word reproduces its own sound. In the goblins' jeering song, Yammer and bleat!, the verb is hurled as a taunt at the captives: the goblins reduce their prisoners to livestock, creatures whose only protest is a weak, wavering, helpless noise. The nuance is deliberately dehumanizing. A bleat is pitiable and without dignity; it is the sound of an animal that cannot fight, only complain, and the goblins relish the coming slaughter by first making their victims sound like sheep queuing for the block.

Etymologically, BLEAT belongs to a family of imitative verbs — Old English blǣtan, German blöken, Latin flēre "to weep" — all rooted in the natural, expressive cry of the animal. From its first attestation it named the sheep's call; only later did it extend, by a transparent metaphor, to human speech marked by weakness, trembling, or whining complaint. Today the word lives a double life: literally of livestock, and contemptuously of people.

Core senses in detail:

  • Primary — the animal cry (verb): To utter the characteristic weak, wavering, plaintive call of a sheep, goat, or young calf; also of calves and young deer. "The lambs began to bleat in the mist."
  • Primary — the cry itself (noun): The sound so made; a single short, quavering call or the sustained chorus of a flock. Recorded as a noun since c. 1500.
  • Extended — human complaint (verb): To complain or speak in a feeble, plaintive, self-pitying way; to whine about one's troubles. "Stop bleating about your bad luck." This is the most common modern use, always faintly contemptuous.
  • Extended — human complaint (noun): A feeble protest or complaint; a whine. "Their bleats of protest were ignored."
  • Extended — any thin, wavering sound: Of wind, bagpipes, or reed instruments, any sound resembling the animal cry; a pipe or horn that produces such a sound. This sense appears in older poetry and in technical descriptions of reedy instruments.
  • Extended — timidity (now rare/dialectal): In older Scots and dialect use, to be timid or to draw back, from the shyness of the sheep; related to Scots blate "bashful, sheepish."

Semantic bridge and rationale:

  • From animal to human: The sheep's cry is thin, trembling, and powerless; transferred to a person, the word accuses them of the same helpless, querulous self-pity. The metaphor is ancient — Latin flēre "to weep" descends from the same imitative root.
  • Dehumanizing function: Because the word belongs above all to livestock, applying it to humans strips them of dignity, reducing protest to the mere noise of a victim. This is exactly its function in the goblins' song.
  • Register: The word is mildly contemptuous in human contexts and neutral in literal ones; it is not vulgar, but it is vivid, sound-symbolic, and always carries the trembling note of weakness.

Related and derived forms:

  • Bleater: one who bleats; a habitual, feebly complaining person (also used of a sheep).
  • Bleating: the act or sound; also the participial adjective describing the cry.
  • Blate (Scots): a parallel form meaning bashful or sheepish, from the shyness of sheep.
  • In fixed phrases: "bleating excuses," "a bleating crowd," "the bleat of the lamb," "to bleat for mercy."

Modern technical and jargonal use:

  • Animal husbandry and zoology: bleat is standard terminology for the sheep's and goat's vocalization, used in farming manuals, veterinary writing, and behavioral studies.
  • Hunting: a "bleat-call" or "bleat can" imitates the distressed cry of a fawn or lamb to attract predators or game.
  • Music: a "bleating tone" describes a thin, quavering, reedy instrumental or vocal quality, especially of high reeds, bagpipes, or tremulous falsetto.
  • Complaint culture: in informal modern speech, "bleating" is a dismissive term for persistent, weak complaining, often aimed at media, social media, or workplace griping.
  • Literary/critical jargon: prose describing weak characters or victims frequently borrows bleat for its dehumanizing force — the character is reduced to a helpless noise.

In short, BLEAT compresses weakness, trembling, and a silly, helpless note into a single verb — the perfect sound for prisoners the goblins regard as livestock to be eaten, and for any modern speaker who wants to deny dignity to a complaint.
