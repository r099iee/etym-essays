Boadicea is the Latinised, anglicised and now largely archaic form of the name of Boudica (also Boudicca), queen of the Iceni, the British tribe whose territory covered much of modern Norfolk and parts of Suffolk, Cambridgeshire and Lincolnshire. She led a major revolt against Roman rule in AD 60 or 61, destroyed Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans), and was defeated by the governor Gaius Suetonius Paulinus. In English the name has two lives: a historical one, as the name of that queen, and an allusive one, as a byword for a formidable, warlike or domineering woman. The Wodehouse context belongs to the second life: Aunt Agatha, cast as Boadicea in a pageant, is being compared to a warrior queen.

Historical figure. Boudica was the consort of Prasutagus, king of the Iceni, who ruled as a nominally independent ally of Rome. When Prasutagus died, he left his kingdom jointly to his two daughters and to the emperor Nero, hoping to secure his family’s position. The Romans ignored the will, annexed the kingdom and seized the royal property. According to Tacitus, Boudica was flogged and her daughters were raped; Cassius Dio adds that Roman financiers, including Seneca, called in forced loans. The revolt that followed was ferocious. The rebels destroyed Camulodunum, the colonia of discharged Roman soldiers, then Londinium and Verulamium. An estimated seventy to eighty thousand people were killed. Suetonius regrouped his forces, chose a narrow battlefield and defeated the much larger British army; Boudica died shortly afterwards, by poison according to Tacitus, by illness according to Dio. The crisis was serious enough to make Nero consider withdrawing from Britain.

The name and its meaning. The name is Celtic, not Latin. It derives from the Brythonic element *boudi- “victory, gain, profit”, with a feminine adjectival suffix *-kā, giving a sense such as “victorious woman” or “she who brings victory”. The same element appears in Welsh budd “advantage, profit” and Irish buaidh “victory”, and in Gaulish names and dedications such as Boudiga, a goddess honoured at Bordeaux. The Welsh form of the queen’s name is Buddug. Some scholars have suggested that *Boudīkā was an honorific title rather than a personal name, in which case her original name is unknown. The name may therefore mean something closer to “the Victorious One” than to a conventional individual name.

From Boudica to Boadicea: the textual history. The classical sources spell the name differently. Tacitus, the earliest and most reliable, writes Boudicca in the Annals and Boudica in the Agricola; Cassius Dio, writing later, has Buduica. Medieval scribes copied and corrupted these forms: the second c became e, and u became a, producing Boadicea. The spelling appears as a misprint in 1624 and was used deliberately by Thomas Browne in 1658. It was popularised by William Cowper’s poem Boadicea: An Ode (1782) and later by Tennyson’s Boädicéa (1864), and it became the standard Victorian form. Modern scholarship has restored Boudica, which is now preferred in academic and serious popular writing.

The Victorian icon and the pageant. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries Boudica became a symbol of British liberty, courage and imperial destiny. Cowper’s ode imagined her as a patriotic prophetess; Tennyson made her a violent, bloodthirsty warrior whose revolt foreshadowed the rise of the British Empire. Thomas Thornycroft’s bronze group Boadicea and Her Daughters, modelled between 1856 and 1871 and erected on the Victoria Embankment in 1902, fixed the image of the queen in a scythed chariot, a detail with no secure classical warrant and probably anachronistic for Boudica herself. Victorian pageants, school histories and patriotic tableaux loved her because she combined female daring with national resistance. It is this stage tradition that Wodehouse is sending up when he has Aunt Agatha appear “as Boadicea” in a pageant: the formidable aunt is being dressed up as the ultimate formidable British woman.

Allusive and figurative use. Because the historical queen was a warrior and a leader, her name can stand for any woman who is perceived as fierce, commanding or unmanageable. The comparison is not usually neutral: it can imply admiration, but it can also imply that a woman is domineering, aggressive or out of control. In Wodehouse the joke is at Aunt Agatha’s expense: she is already a domestic tyrant, and casting her as Boadicea merely makes the tyranny visible. The allusive use is therefore a good example of how a proper name can become a common noun in all but grammar: one can say “she thinks she’s Boadicea” without referring to the historical queen at all.

Modern spelling and pronunciation. Boadicea is pronounced /ˌbəʊədɪˈsiːə/ in Received Pronunciation, with the main stress on the penultimate syllable. Boudica is pronounced /ˈbuːdɪkə/ or /boʊˈdɪkə/, and Boudicca similarly. The difference in spelling is not merely cosmetic: Boadicea reflects a long history of scribal error and Victorian taste, while Boudica reflects the modern attempt to recover the classical form. In contemporary usage Boadicea survives in older literature, in the name of the London statue, in the names of ships and in period pastiche; Boudica is the default in museums, schools and scholarship.

Historical caveats. Almost everything known about Boudica comes from two Roman writers who wrote decades after the events and who had their own moral and political purposes. Tacitus was interested in the contrast between Roman corruption and barbarian courage; Dio was interested in dramatic set pieces and invented speeches. The figures of seventy to eighty thousand dead and an army of 120,000 are probably rhetorical. The site of the final battle is unknown. Boudica’s name, her appearance and even her death are uncertain in detail. The name Boadicea therefore carries a double uncertainty: it is a corrupted form of a name that may itself have been a title.

  • Historical referent: the Iceni queen who revolted against Rome in AD 60/61.
  • Onomastic sense: a Celtic name meaning “victorious woman”, perhaps an honorific.
  • Allusive sense: a formidable, warlike or domineering woman.
  • Cultural sense: a Victorian and later emblem of British liberty and female courage.
