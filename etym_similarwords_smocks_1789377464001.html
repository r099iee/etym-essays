• Tunic: Similar because both are loose, slipover garments extending from the shoulders to the hips or knees; differs because a tunic is typically an ancient or classical garment worn as a primary piece of clothing (such as Roman tunics or medieval tunics), whereas a smock is predominantly an overgarment worn specifically to protect other clothes or featuring decorative gathered pleats.
  • Apron: Similar because both are protective garments worn over daily clothes to prevent staining during manual tasks; differs because an apron is typically tied around the waist or neck and covers only the front of the body, whereas a smock provides complete torso coverage with full sleeves and back enclosure.
  • Overall: Similar because both are heavy-duty utilitarian protective garments worn by workers; differs because overalls (or boiler suits) are one-piece protective trousers or trousered suits often with bib straps and made of rugged denim or canvas, whereas a smock is a skirted tunic or coat-like garment without divided legs.
  • Pinafore: Similar because both are protective overgarments traditionally worn by young children and domestic workers; differs because a pinafore is sleeveless and worn pinned or tied over a dress (from *pin-afore*), whereas a smock historically has full sleeves and often incorporates smocked gathering across the chest.
  • Chemise: Similar because both historically referred to a simple linen slip or undergarment worn next to the skin; differs because chemise has retained its delicate, intimate, feminine undergarment association (evolving into modern lingerie and nightwear), whereas smock migrated outward into rugged agricultural, artistic, and occupational outerwear.
  • Frock: Similar because both describe loose, outer robes or coats associated with rural labor (e.g., smock-frock); differs because frock is a broader term that historically denoted a monk's habit, a military frock coat, or a girl's dress, whereas smock specifically designates protective covering or gathered pleating.
  • Tabard: Similar because both are sleeveless or short-sleeved loose outer garments slipped over the head; differs because a tabard is open at the sides and historically associated with heralds, knights, or modern grocery clerks, whereas a smock is a fully enclosed garment with gathered fabric.
