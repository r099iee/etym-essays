- 14th–15th Century - Blear (Middle English blere, blerien) arose to denote watery, inflamed, or rheumy eyes afflicted by disease or weeping; the idiom "to blear someone's eye" meant to hoodwink or deceive them (found in Chaucer and Langland) - Carried clinical, sorrowful, and deceptive connotations.
- 16th–17th Century - Compound formations like blear-eyed became common in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama (including Shakespeare) to characterize drunks, fools, or individuals blinded to moral truth - Used both as a physical descriptor of drunkenness and as a moral metaphor for foolish blindness.
- 18th–19th Century - The suffix -y became firmly attached to produce bleary-eyed, shifting predominantly from literal eye disease toward the familiar physical aftermath of burning the midnight oil, heavy drinking, or early morning rising - Established in Victorian realism and domestic fiction.
- 20th–21st Century - Cemented in modern idioms, journalism, and comedic novels (such as P. G. Wodehouse's Bertie Wooster stories) to portray morning grogginess, hangover recovery, and modern screen-induced fatigue - Regarded as a vivid, relatable, and universally recognized colloquial descriptor of exhaustion.
