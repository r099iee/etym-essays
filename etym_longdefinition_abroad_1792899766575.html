Abroad is an adverb (and by extension a predicative adjective) whose founding sense is spatial and outward-bound: in or to another country, beyond one’s own borders, across the sea. To live abroad, to study abroad, to flee abroad — in each case the word draws a line between home and the foreign, and places its subject on the farther side of it. It never takes a prepositional complement of its own; one goes abroad, not abroad to somewhere, because the vagueness is the point: abroad is everywhere that is not here.

In the source sentence — ‘You are ______ late — the word wears its older, humbler sense: out of doors, outside the house, away from one’s own place. Said to someone arriving after dark, it means simply that the night is already out there waiting, that the lateness belongs to the world outside the door rather than to any timetable. This domestic sense is the seed from which the grander foreign-travel sense grew: first merely outside the house, then outside the district, then outside the country.

Core meaning in detail: Modern use centres on two poles. The first is geographical: abroad as foreign soil — news from abroad, holidaying abroad, manufactured abroad. The second is circulatory: abroad as out in the open, at large in the world — there is a rumour abroad, a spirit of unrest abroad in the land. Both share the image of something that has left the enclosure — the house, the country, the secret — and is now moving freely in wider space.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • In or to a foreign country (primary modern sense, from mid-15th c.): she lives abroad, orders from abroad. The bridge from the older outside the house sense is simple widening: as English horizons stretched with trade, exploration, and empire, outside came to mean outside the nation, and the sea-crossing became part of the word’s furniture.
  • Out of doors, outside the house (original sense, c. 1300, now literary): to walk abroad, abroad in the fields. The bridge is compositional: a- (on, in) + broad (open expanse) — literally out in the broad open, under no roof. The source sentence uses exactly this sense.
  • In general circulation, at large (figurative, from 16th c.): a story abroad, mischief abroad. The bridge is metaphorical escape: a rumour behaves like a traveller, leaving its house of origin and wandering the world until everyone has met it.
  • Away from home, astray, wide of the mark (extended): older writers speak of wits gone abroad or plans gone abroad — the mind travelling when it should have stayed. The bridge carries the traveller image inward: attention itself goes visiting.
  • Away fixture, on opponents’ ground (sporting, 20th c.): to play abroad, goals scored abroad. The bridge is the footballing map: the home ground is home, everywhere else — even domestically — is figured as foreign soil.
  • From abroad (phrasal extension): aid from abroad, a voice from abroad. Here the word becomes almost a noun of origin, naming the whole foreign world as a single source, the way from above names the sky.

Register and connotation: Abroad is warm with distance and mild glamour — gap years, postcards, and foreign correspondents — yet it can chill in political prose, where threats from abroad or money from abroad darken the foreign into the suspect. The circulatory sense (rumour abroad) is faintly archaic and literary, lending any sentence a King-James gravity. Grammatically it stands alone and unmodified, an adverb that needs no passport of its own because it is itself the stamp of elsewhere.
