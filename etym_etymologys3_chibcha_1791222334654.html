Chibcha is not Indo-European at all but an Americanist exonym whose evolution must be traced inside the Chibchan language family of Colombia, Panama and Costa Rica. The self-name of the highland people around Bogotá and Tunja was Muisca (people, person) and of their speech Muysccubun (speech of the people). Spanish conquistadors under Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada (1537–1539) heard a word sounding like chibcha and adopted it as a convenient label for the people, their language, and eventually a whole stock of related languages from Honduras to Ecuador.

Colonial sources preserve the ambiguity. Bernardo de Lugo's Gramática en la lengua general del Nuevo Reino llamada Mosca (1619) calls the language Mosca/Muisca, while civil administrators write Chibcha for tribute lists. Indigenous analysis recorded later parses chib-cha as chib (staff, sceptre) + cha (man, male) = staff-bearer, chief, headman — hence by extension the chiefly people. Lexvo/UWN and Italian summaries repeat this: Chib from staff, Cha from man, meaning Chief of the Community, exemplified by the god Chibchacum (he who holds the earth on a staff). Other Colombian philologists gloss it as our people/we, i.e. an endonym of belonging. Both parses may be folk etymologies after the fact; what is certain is that Spanish fixed chibcha as the book-name.

  • 1537–1770: Colonial administrative term: Chibcha denotes the Muisca confederation of Zipa (Bogotá) and Zaque (Tunja), some 500,000 people, intensive maize-potato farmers, salt miners at Zipaquirá and Nemocón, emerald and gold workers famed for the El Dorado raft rite at Lake Guatavita. Missionary grammars document Muysccubun while the Crown uses Chibcha.
  • 1770–1830: Ban and extinction: Charles III's decree of 10 May 1770 bans Indigenous languages as part of Bourbon de-indigenization; Muysccubun ceases as a daily vernacular by the late 18th century, surviving only in place-names (Bogotá from Bacatá, Tunja from Hunza), surnames, and loanwords for plants and foods.
  • 1814–1900: English adoption as stock name: Merriam-Webster dates English Chibcha to 1814 for the people, extended to the extinct language. Alexander von Humboldt, Uricoechea's Gramática chibcha (1871), and Handbook of South American Indians establish Chibchan as a family including U'wa, Bari, Kogi, Talamanca and Isthmian languages. Pronunciation stabilizes as /ˈtʃɪbtʃə/.
  • 1900–present: Revival and identity: Colombian constitution of 1991 recognizes Indigenous languages; a school at Cota (Jizcamox) teaches Muysccubun to ~150 children. Colloquially chibcha in Colombia can affectionately mean Colombian thing/culture (chibcholombiano joke), while scholarship prefers Muisca for the people and Chibchan for the family.

Phonetically no European sound shift occurs beyond orthographic adaptation: Muisca /tʃibtʃa/ → Spanish /ˈtʃibtʃa/ → English /ˈtʃɪbtʃə/ with English vowel reduction of final -a to schwa. Capital C is retained as an ethnonym. There is no PIE chain; the Chibchan stock is an independent American family, older than Aramaic in local boast but linguistically of unknown external affiliation. The debate noted in S2 — staff-man vs our people vs derivation from the god Chibchacum — is explicitly unresolved in the literature and must not be forced.

In the source sentence, Chibcha Indians exporting rock salt refers to the famous Zipaquirá halite mines, traded for lowland maize and cotton at weekly markets, the economic base that funded Muisca goldwork and the legend of El Dorado that lured the Spanish.
