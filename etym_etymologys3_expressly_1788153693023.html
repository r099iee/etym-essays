1. Pressing out: the physical image. The whole family of express, expression and expressly begins with a picture: pressing. Latin premere ‘to press’ combined with the prefix ex- ‘out’ produced exprimere, which first meant ‘to press out’ quite literally — oil pressed from olives, juice from grapes, wax from a mould. The mould-image is the key: what is pressed into a mould takes on an exact shape, and what is pressed out of the mould is a perfect, distinct copy. From this concrete act the Romans built a metaphor of speech: Cicero uses exprimere of representing a thought or an absent person in words or art. To express is to press the invisible mind out into visible, distinct form.

2. The Latin adverb: expresse. Latin grammarians and rhetoricians formed the adverb expresse ‘plainly, distinctly, in so many words’ from the participle expressus ‘pressed out, clear, explicit’. Already in Classical usage expresse marks the exactness of what is said or meant — the ancestor of the modern phrase ‘in express terms’. The word carried this precision into legal and theological Latin, where explicit statement is everything, and into the Romance vernaculars.

3. Old French and Middle English. Old French built expressement (cf. modern French expressément) on the same participle, and Middle English either borrowed it or re-formed it from express + -ly in the 14th century. The earliest English attestations are in precisely the registers where precision matters: Wyclifite biblical translation (‘expressly commandeth’), statutes, wills and liturgical prose. In these texts expressly is already doing its double duty of ‘plainly stated’ and ‘bindingly intended’ — the King James Bible later uses it in the same way (‘the Spirit speaketh expressly’, 1 Timothy 4:1).

  • Early modern widening: from 1500 onward expressly leaves the pulpit and the courtroom for the letter, the novel and the conversation of good society; ‘he came expressly to see her’ makes the deliberate-purpose sense commonplace.
  • The legal pair express/implied: in the 18th–20th centuries the science of contract made the opposition explicit: express terms are written or spoken, implied terms are read into the deal. Expressly therefore became technical again, the adverb of drafting — ‘expressly excluded’, ‘expressly provided’.
  • Usage register today: dictionaries present expressly as formal; speakers use it for emphasis and denial; its opposite impliedly belongs to law. The word thus spans conversation, pulpit and statute book without splitting.

4. The semantic bridge in one sentence. From ‘pressed out’ (a mould) the word moved to ‘pressed out of the soul’ (a thought uttered), from ‘utterly so’ to ‘in so many words’, and from ‘in so many words’ to ‘on purpose and for that very end’ — because a purpose stated in express words is a purpose the speaker stands behind. Every modern sense of expressly is still the oil from the press: distinct, deliberate, and impossible to mistake.
