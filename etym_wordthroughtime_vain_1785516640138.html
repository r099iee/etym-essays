- [13th century English borrowing] - Borrowed from Old French, vain meant 'empty, worthless, idle', carrying Latin vānus's core sense of hollowness with it.
- [Biblical and moral phase] - Through the phrase 'in vain' and the Vulgate's 'vanity of vanities' (Ecclesiastes), vain became the great moral word for the hollowness and futility of earthly striving, echoed by Milton and Shelley.
- [Renaissance self-regard phase] - By the 16th-17th centuries the sense 'empty pride in one's appearance or worth' hardened ('vain glory', the Dauphin's vain boasts), giving the word its dual load of futility and conceit.
- [Contemporary divided phase] - Modern vain runs two parallel lives: the idiomatic 'in vain' for effort that comes to nothing, and the everyday sense of a person excessively proud of their looks, both grounded in the idea of emptiness.
