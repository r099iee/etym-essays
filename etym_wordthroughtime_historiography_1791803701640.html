- 1560s — enters English meaning “the writing of history,” an activity and a literary craft; a historiographer is simply a historian, often one employed by a prince.
- 17th–18th c. — antiquarian, courtly and ecclesiastical history flourish; the word stays bookish and is largely interchangeable with “history-writing”.
- 19th c. — the professionalisation of history (seminars, archives, doctoral training, national editions of sources) turns historiography towards the critical study of how history is written and of national schools of scholarship.
- 20th c. — becomes a distinct academic field: Marxist, Annales, Whig and revisionist historiographies are named and debated; Hayden White's Metahistory (1973) shifts attention to narrative form.
- 21st c. — expanded by global, decolonial and memory studies; in everyday academic prose it also serves loosely for “the literature on a subject”.
