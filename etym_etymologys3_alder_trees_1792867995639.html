The compound alder-trees joins two of the oldest tree-words in the language, each with an unbroken lineage from prehistory, and its story must therefore be told as two histories meeting in one hyphen.

Alder: the tree that loves water. Reconstructed PIE *h₂élisos is one of the secure tree-names, reflected in Latin alnus, Old Irish fern, and Germanic *alizō alike — proof that the alder was already a named neighbour wherever Indo-European speakers found wet ground. Proto-Germanic *alizō gives Old High German elira, Old Norse ǫlr, and Old English alor, aler: the riverside tree of shields, bowls, and piles. Middle English regularised the form to alder, borrowing the -n- that had grown up in compounds and oblique cases, so that aller in some dialects and alder in the standard both descend from the same wet root.

Tree: from oak-wood to every tree. PIE *dóru meant strictly the oak — the hard timber tree — and its children divide accordingly: Greek dóru (spear-shaft, beam), Latin dūrus (hard, as oak is hard), Russian derevo (tree, wood), and Germanic *trewą, which broadened from oak-wood to wood in general and then to the living tree. Old English trēow still meant both the standing tree and the timber — hence treow as material and treowe as faith (that which holds like timber) — and Middle English tre shed the old inflections to become the clean monosyllable tree.

Sound and spelling shifts:

  • Laryngeal colouring in alder: PIE *h₂élisos colours the following vowel to a, giving Germanic al- rather than el-; the s voices to z by Verner's law in Germanic, surfacing as r in Old English alor.
  • The intrusive n: phrases like alor-man (leader, elder of men) fused the genitive plural -na into the stem, yielding alderman and, by analogy, the simplex alder.
  • Grimm's law in tree: PIE *d devoices to Germanic *t, so *dóru becomes *trewą; Old English diphthong ēo in trēow monophthongises to Middle English tre.
  • Compounding habit: Early Modern English loves transparent X-tree compounds for metre and clarity — Shakespeare's alder stands alone, but ballads and novels reach for alder-tree when the line needs the extra stress.

Socio-cultural context: the alder was the poor man's timber and the engineer's secret: too soft for beams, too quick to rot in air, yet immortal under water. Venice's palazzi, Amsterdam's warehouses, and the mills of the Pennines ride on alder piles; Highlanders made shields of it, cloggers shod Lancashire with it, and gunpowder mills burned it to the finest charcoal. Folklore repaid the utility with awe: the cut stump bleeds orange, the green wood burns green and hot, and the fairies — the old stories insist — forbid the felling of the fairy alder. Tolkien, philologist that he was, knew every strand of this: his leaning alder-trees are philology made landscape.
