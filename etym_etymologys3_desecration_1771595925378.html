From the sacred boundary to the ethics of art: the evolution of desecration The biography of desecration begins at the boundary between the holy and the common, the oldest line that human societies draw. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *seh₂k-, a root of the sacred and the solemnly binding — the same root that in Latin produced sacer, the word for both the holy and the accursed. This double face is not an accident: in ancient religion the taboo was two-sided, and a thing set apart could be set apart for blessing or for doom — the two poles of the same frightening power. Latin sacer, "holy, dedicated to the gods," was also the word for a criminal given over to the gods for destruction, the homo sacer whose killing no one was bound to avenge. Around this root Latin built a small priesthood of words: sacrāre, "to make sacred"; sacrificium, "a making-sacred," the offering, which English borrowed as sacrifice; sanctus, "consecrated," the source of saint, sanctify, sanction, sanctuary, and sanctimony; sacerdos, the priest, literally "one who sets the sacred in place," the source of sacerdotal; and sacrilegium, "the theft of sacred things," from sacer and legere, "to gather," the source of sacrilege. Even the Sabellic dialects confirm the root — Oscan and Umbrian have their own sakro- words for sacred things — so that the root was already the property of the whole Italic world; and the Anatolian Hittite šāklāi-, "custom, rite," is widely, if debatedly, claimed as an older sister in the second millennium BCE. The decisive act for our word is the prefix dē-. Latin dēsecrāre means "to make unholy," to undo a consecration, and the Romans used it for the precise opposite of sacrifice: where consecration sets a thing apart for the gods, desecration tears it back into the common world. The noun dēsecrātiō was formed for the act itself — a word of late Latin and of the Church, carried into English in the seventeenth century as desecration, with the verb desecrate following shortly after. The prefix is everything: dē- is a reversive, an undo-button, so that desecration is, etymologically, the "undoing of the sacred," and the word was born into a tight antonym pair with consecration. In theological use the pair governed a whole ritual technology: things were consecrated by prayer and rite, and if they were desecrated they were set apart again by rites of reconsecration. From the first, then, the word presupposed a cosmos divided into the holy and the profane, and named the crime of forcing something across that boundary in the wrong direction.
  • Sound changes: the Latin -cr- cluster of dēsecrātiō passed through ecclesiastical Latin and learned French into English unchanged in spelling; the modern pronunciation shows the ordinary English reduction of the medial vowel (desecration, stress on the third syllable, /dˌɛsɪkɹˈeɪʃən/).
  • Semantic drift: from "the reversal of a consecration" (Latin, theological) to "the violent profanation of any sacred thing" (seventeenth-century English) to "the contemptuous violation of anything held in high reverence" (nineteenth century and after) — a migration from the altar to the monument, the memory, the ideal, and the natural world.
  • Socio-cultural context: the seventeenth century used the word in religious controversy over churches and holy places; the nineteenth century, secularising the sacred, let Ruskin and others apply it to art, to the mind, and to nature, so that a word born of the altar became the vocabulary of aesthetic and moral indignation.
  • Literary attestations: Ruskin's "desecration of the highest powers of the human mind" is the canonical secular use; the word also runs through Victorian writing on the Alps and the landscape (Ruskin again), twentieth-century writing on war and monuments, and contemporary journalism on the flag, graves, and heritage.
In modern English desecration has become the standard word for a broad class of violations that a community feels as sacrilege even without a God: the desecration of a flag, a grave, a temple, a forest, a memory, an ideal. The word carries the full weight of its religious origin into these secular settings — that is the secret of its force, and of Ruskin's use of it. To call the misuse of the mind's highest powers a desecration is to claim that the mind itself is holy, that perception, imagination, and truth are set apart, and that to debase them is not an error of taste but an unhallowing. The word has travelled from the boundary of the Roman temple to the boundary of human dignity, and at every stage it has done the same work: drawing a line around what must not be touched, and naming the crime of crossing it.
