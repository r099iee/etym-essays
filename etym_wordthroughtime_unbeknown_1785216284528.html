- [Middle English: beknown] - The verb beknowen (from Old English becnāwan, "to recognize, acknowledge") yields the past participle beknown, "known, acknowledged," which survives into Early Modern English chiefly in dialects and legal-style phrases.
- [1630s: unbeknown is coined] - Speakers negate the participle with the privative prefix un-, producing unbeknown, "not known," and then press it into a distinctive adverbial-predicative slot: unbeknown to him, "without his knowing"; the semantics tip from "not known (by anyone)" to "not known by a particular person," an epistemic narrowing that gives the word its signature to-phrase grammar.
- [19th century: unbeknownst] - An excrescent -st, on the model of amongst and against, produces unbeknownst (c. 1848), which spreads widely in American usage though it never displaces the older form in Britain.
- [Contemporary sense] - The word reads as archaic, dialectal, and storytelling-flavoured - an omniscient narrator's whisper of dramatic irony - marking a secret hidden from a specific person while the reader alone is let in on it.
