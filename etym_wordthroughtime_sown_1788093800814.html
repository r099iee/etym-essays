- sāwen (Old English, 8th–11th c.): purely agricultural — "scattered or cast (of seed)" — describing the hand-sowing of corn. Connotation: everyday farmwork, neutral and concrete.
- sowen (Middle English): the same literal sense, now also "to spread abroad" of words, rumours and teachings; the seed-metaphor begins to grow. Connotation: still concrete, but ready for abstraction.
- 16th–17th c.: figurative "sown" flowers in biblical English — "sow the wind and reap the whirlwind" (Hosea 8:7, KJV 1611) and "whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap" (Galatians 6:7). Connotation: moral and providential — every act has a harvest.
- 18th–19th c.: "sown" settles into literary register while "sowed" takes over speech; poets use it for pastoral landscapes ("fields sown with..."). Connotation: dignified, archaic-tinged, nostalgic.
- Modern: "sown" is the formal/literary participle; in journalism and politics "seeds of doubt/division were sown" is the standard formula; in fantasy and high register it keeps its ancient gravity (Tolkien: "Over snow by winter sown"). Connotation: deliberate, hidden causation — what is sown today surfaces later.
