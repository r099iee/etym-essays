- 13th c. — the adjective is born: Middle English flegge means feathered and fit to fly, a falconer’s and fowler’s word for a chick ready to leave.
- 16th c. — the noun fledges: fledgling appears for the young bird itself, downy but winged, hopping at the nest’s edge.
- 17th–18th c. — naturalists adopt it: bird books use fledgling precisely for the post-nest, pre-independence stage, beside nestling and adult.
- 19th c. — metaphor takes wing: writers call young poets, lovers, and schemes fledgling, and the plural gathers novices of every kind.
- 20th c. — the rescue confusion: suburban gardeners meet grounded fledglings each spring and learn — via newspapers and wildlife trusts — to leave them to their parents.
- Today — science and symbol: ornithology’s precise stage-term and journalism’s favourite image for anything young and untested, from democracies to start-ups.
