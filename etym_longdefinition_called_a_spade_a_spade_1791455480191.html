Called a spade a spade is an idiom meaning to speak plainly and bluntly, to name a thing directly instead of dressing it up in euphemism. Its force is deliberately unglamorous: a spade is an ordinary digging tool, and to call it a spade is to refuse the polite fiction that it is something grander. The phrase praises candour, but it also warns that candour may be blunt, tactless, or socially awkward.

1. Core meaning

To call a spade a spade is to use the plain, direct word for something, especially when the subject is uncomfortable, indelicate, or politically inconvenient. It implies that others are evading the obvious. In the source passage the narrator says the medieval dialogue was “racy of the days when they called a spade a spade” — that is, when writers and speakers named bodily and sexual matters frankly rather than in polite circumlocutions.

  • Plain speaking: the central sense; the speaker refuses euphemism.
  • Bluntness: the phrase can shade into rudeness or tactlessness.
  • Moral courage: it can imply the speaker is willing to say what others will not.

2. The literal image and its rhetorical point

The image is agricultural and domestic. A spade is a humble tool for digging; calling it a spade is the least glamorous act of naming imaginable. The idiom therefore draws its rhetorical power from the contrast between the ordinariness of the object and the social difficulty of the truth being named. It says: the thing is what it is, however much we might prefer a prettier word.

3. Variants and intensifiers

  • Call a spade a bloody shovel: an Australian and British intensification that adds aggression and coarse humour.
  • Call a fig a fig: a learned variant that reaches back to the Greek original.
  • Call things by their proper names: a more formal paraphrase.
  • Tell it like it is: an American colloquial equivalent, more about attitude than naming.
  • Mince no words: a near-synonym that stresses the absence of softening.

4. Register and pragmatics

The idiom is informal but widely acceptable in journalism, politics, and after-dinner speech. It often functions as a prefatory boast — “let’s call a spade a spade” — before a speaker delivers an unwelcome judgement. Because it claims honesty, it can be used manipulatively to license rudeness. In British English it carries a faintly old-fashioned, no-nonsense flavour, associated with plain-dealing characters and exasperated commentators.

5. Related idioms and contrasts

  • Beat about the bush: the opposite; to avoid the point.
  • Mince words: to soften or obscure what one says.
  • Name the elephant in the room: to raise an obvious but avoided issue; narrower than the spade idiom.
  • Pull no punches: to criticise without restraint; more aggressive and less about precise naming.
  • Speak truth to power: a loftier, politically charged phrase; the spade idiom is homelier and more general.

6. A modern caution about the word “spade”

The idiom is sometimes wrongly assumed to carry racial overtones because “spade” was used as a derogatory slang term for a Black person from the 1920s, apparently by analogy with the black suit in playing cards. That slang is a separate, later development. The digging-tool idiom is recorded in English by 1542 and has no connection with it. Careful writers may still avoid the phrase in contexts where the slur could be misheard, but the idiom’s own history is agricultural and literary, not racial.

7. Semantic bridge

The bridge from tool to truth is metonymic: naming a humble object by its plain name becomes the model for naming any awkward reality by its plain name. The phrase’s endurance comes from the way the concrete image keeps the abstraction honest — it is hard to be pompous while talking about a shovel.
