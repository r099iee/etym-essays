Hashish is in its primary modern sense the concentrated resin of the cannabis plant, pressed from the flowering tops of female plants into dark cakes, balls, or pliable slabs, then smoked or eaten for its powerful narcotic effect: a dulling of pain, a loosening of time, and a heightening of colour, sound, and appetite that can tip from euphoria into confusion and stupor as the dose rises.

In the source sentence the word carries its most legendary association: the drug said to have been given to the medieval Nizari Ismailis, the sect Europe called the Assassins, to work themselves into a frenzy for murder, so that assassin itself was long explained as hashish-eater. Whether or not the history is true, and modern scholars doubt it, the sentence preserves the founding myth that carried hashish into European languages: an Eastern narcotic, a vow of killing, and a drug-induced frenzy.

Core meaning in detail: True hashish is not the leaf but the resin, rich in the plant's glandular trichomes where the psychoactive compound THC is concentrated. Workers sieve or rub the dried flower heads, collect the golden-brown powder, and press it with gentle heat into blocks that range from blonde Lebanese to black Afghan. Smoked in a pipe or mixed with tobacco, or eaten in sweets and confections, it acts more slowly and often more heavily than herbal cannabis, with effects that last for hours. Strength varies enormously with origin and craft, from mild traditional grades to modern high-potency extracts.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Cannabis resin as a drug (primary modern sense, 19th c. in English): the pressed resin described above, bought, smoked, or eaten for intoxication. This is the sense of drug laws, travel writing, and counterculture memoirs alike.
  • Dried hemp herb generally (older and Arabic-rooted sense): in Arabic ḥašīš first meant dry herb or hay of any kind before narrowing to hemp, and early European writers sometimes used hashish loosely for any cannabis preparation, leaf included. The bridge is part-to-whole: the general word for cut herbage specialised to the one herb that intoxicated.
  • The Assassin legend drug (historical-mythic sense): the potion of Marco Polo's famous tale, in which the Old Man of the Mountain drugged youths into a garden paradise and sent them out as killers. The bridge is etymological folklore: hashishiyya, a term of abuse for the Nizaris, was read literally as hashish-takers, and the story hardened into a dictionary fact for a century.
  • The bohemian literary sacrament (19th-century French sense): le hachisch of Gautier, Baudelaire, and the Club des Hachichins, eaten as green dawamesk paste in Parisian salons to court dreams and visions. The bridge runs through Napoleon's soldiers returning from Egypt: a soldier's vice became a poet's experiment in artificial paradises.
  • A controlled substance in law and medicine (20th–21st c. technical sense): hashish as a scheduled narcotic in statute books, and its active principles as objects of pharmacology, from pain relief and anti-nausea research to debates over dependence and psychosis. The bridge is institutional: the same resin moves from bazaar to laboratory to courtroom without changing its chemistry.
  • Figurative stupor or dreaminess (rare, journalistic): as in talk of a hashish of routine or hashish of nostalgia, something that dulls the will like a drug. The bridge is metaphorical: any influence that clouds judgment borrows the drug's power to intoxicate.

Register and connotation: hashish is the exotic, old-world word for cannabis, smelling of bazaars, caravans, and 19th-century travelogues, where marijuana sounds American-clinical and weed sounds casual. It carries menace in prohibition rhetoric and romance in counterculture memory, sometimes both at once. Writers reach for it when they want mystery rather than botany: the very syllables seem to promise the Orient its first European users imagined.
