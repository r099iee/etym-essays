bellhop is the North American term for a hotel porter — typically a young uniformed attendant who meets guests at the entrance or lobby, carries suitcases to rooms, and runs small errands. In the source sentence Vance worked as a bellhop for what he called a miserable year: hauling baggage, answering bells, and enduring the low-paid, high-deference service world of Depression-era hotels.

The word is a transparent compound of bell (the front-desk or room bell rung to summon service) plus hop (to jump quickly to a task). The -hop element is mildly jocular and slightly demeaning: the boy is imagined as one who hops whenever the bell rings. American hotels of the late 19th century coined a cluster of such terms — bellboy, bellhop, bellman — and bellhop emerged as the brisk, slangy favourite.

  • Core occupational sense: uniformed luggage-carrier and errand-runner in hotels, inns, and clubs. Duties include greeting arrivals, loading and delivering baggage, escorting guests to rooms, explaining facilities, fetching ice and messages, and hailing transport. The feminine or neutral equivalents in modern usage are bell attendant or bellperson; bellhop itself is historically male and youthful.
  • Hierarchy and neighbours: the bellhop stands below the concierge (who arranges tickets, tables, and secrets), beside the doorman (who guards the entrance) and the porter (the broader British term for carrier). In grand hotels a bell captain directs the team. Tipping the bellhop per bag remains customary in North America.
  • Uniform and icon: pillbox cap, short jacket with brass buttons, white gloves — the bellhop silhouette became visual shorthand for hospitality itself in advertising, film, andTintin-style comics. Cinema turned the figure into comedy and pathos by turns: the scurrying kid with too many bags, the witness who sees everything the guests hide.
  • Social history: in the United States bell work, like Pullman porter work, was for decades disproportionately assigned to young Black men and immigrant youths, poorly paid and dependent on tips. Memoirs of the 1920s–30s, Vance's era, remember it as gruelling, hierarchical, and humiliating — hence a miserable year. Labour history thus clings to the word in a way it does not to blander synonyms.
  • Extended and playful uses: as a verb (to bellhop, rare) meaning to work as or serve like one; attributively (bellhop uniform, bellhop cap); metaphorically for anyone who jumps to serve (the intern was their bellhop). The tone is affectionate when nostalgic, dismissive when marking servility.

Usage notes: bellhop is chiefly American; British English prefers bellboy or porter, though global chains now standardise bell attendant for inclusivity. It is countable and takes the regular plural bellhops. As a compound it is written solid (bellhop), earlier hyphenated (bell-hop), never two words in the occupational sense. To work as a bellhop frames a life stage — youthful, transient, physically demanding — which is why biographies reach for it to signal hard beginnings.

  • Register: standard but faintly nostalgic and informal; travel guides and HR manuals increasingly replace it, while fiction and memoir preserve it for period colour.
  • Boundary checks: a bellhop carries and fetches; for authoritative guest services ask the concierge; for entrance ceremony ask the doorman; for station or airport carrying ask a porter or skycap. Do not use bellhop for the desk clerk who checks you in.

In short, bellhop packs a uniform, a bell, a jump, and a social world into seven letters: the hotel boy who hops when the bell rings, remembered here as a year of misery on the road to elsewhere.
