hath (verb, archaic 3rd person singular present indicative of have) — The historical, liturgical, and literary third-person singular present tense of the verb have (corresponding to modern standard has). It is used with singular subjects (such as he, she, it, or singular nouns) and indefinite or interrogative pronouns (such as who, everyone, no one) to express possession, ownership, relationship, experiential condition, obligation, or to serve as an auxiliary verb constructing the present perfect tense. While supplanted by has in standard modern spoken English, hath remains iconic in religious liturgy, classical drama, legal formulas, proverbial idioms, and historical literature.

1. Auxiliary Grammatical Function — Constructing the Present Perfect. In early modern and classical English grammar, hath served as the universal third-person singular auxiliary verb paired with a past participle to form the present perfect aspect. It denotes an action initiated or completed in the past that holds immediate relevance, moral weight, or consequence in the present moment. In the reading context from Oscar Wilde's The Selfish Giant, the Giant gazes upon the bleeding wounds on the child's hands and feet and cries out in furious indignation: “Who hath dared to wound thee?” Here, hath dared expresses an outrage committed in the past whose searing evidence stands directly before the speaker's eyes, invested with biblical gravity.

2. Primary Lexical Sense — Possession, Holding, and Enduring Quality. When functioning as a main transitive verb, hath indicates holding, owning, containing, or being endowed with an attribute, virtue, or physical possession:

  • Divine and Moral Endowments: Used in biblical and philosophical prose to describe eternal qualities or physical faculties ("He that hath ears to hear, let him hear"; "The tree hath good fruit").
  • Inherent Attributes: Describing intrinsic properties or capabilities ("Music hath charms to soothe a savage breast").

3. Proverbial, Liturgical, and Poetic Idioms in Modern English. Although largely obsolete in conversational English, hath survives indelibly embedded in numerous famous literary quotations, proverbs, and liturgical prayers that form part of the cultural memory of the English-speaking world:

  • "Hell hath no fury like a woman scorned" — William Congreve's famous line from The Mourning Bride (1697), commonly quoted as a proverb.
  • "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away" — The Book of Job (Job 1:21) in the King James Bible, universally recited in funeral liturgies.
  • "Music hath charms to soothe a savage breast" — William Congreve's opening line from The Mourning Bride.
  • "What hath God wrought!" — The first official Morse telegraph message sent by Samuel Morse in 1844, quoting Numbers 23:23.

4. Dialectal and Historical Grammar — The Northern vs. Southern Inflectional Divide. The coexistence and eventual competition between hath and has represents one of the most famous dialectal developments in the history of the English language. In Middle English, the southern and Midland dialects of England formed the third-person singular present indicative with the inflectional suffix -eth (producing hæfþ / haveth / hath), whereas the northern dialects and Scottish English formed it with -es / -s (yielding has). During the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, hath was the dominant written and formal standard (dominating the Book of Common Prayer and the 1611 King James Bible), while has gained ground rapidly in colloquial London speech and the theater, eventually prevailing during the late 17th and 18th centuries.

5. Stylistic and Aesthetic Tone. In 19th- and 20th-century literature, authors like Oscar Wilde, Walter Pater, and Alfred, Lord Tennyson deliberately employed hath in specific aesthetic contexts to evoke biblical majesty, ancient authority, sacred solemnity, or fairy-tale timelessness. In The Selfish Giant, Wilde's shift into archaic diction ("Who hath dared to wound thee?" and "the wounds of Love") elevates the narrative from a charming nursery story into a profound Christian allegory of divine grace.
