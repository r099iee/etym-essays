Little Trianon is a proper noun: an English rendering of the French Petit Trianon, the name of the small Neoclassical château built in the park of Versailles between 1762 and 1768 to the designs of Ange-Jacques Gabriel. It is not a common noun and has no plural in ordinary use. The name denotes a building, the estate immediately around it, and — by extension — the whole private world of the French royal family in the last decades of the ancien régime.

What the name denotes

  • The building: a compact three-storey block of pale stone, five bays wide on each face, with Corinthian pilasters on the garden side and a flat roof behind a balustrade. It is a landmark of the transition from Rococo to Neoclassicism, praised for the purity of its cubic outline.
  • The estate: the Petit Trianon stands within the larger Trianon domain, which also contains the Grand Trianon, a much larger palace of pink marble built by Jules Hardouin-Mansart in 1687 on the site of an earlier Trianon de porcelaine of 1670.
  • The name in English: “Little Trianon” is a literal calque of Petit Trianon. Nineteenth-century English writers, Carlyle among them, translated the French adjective; modern English normally keeps the French form Petit Trianon.

History of the place

The Trianon estate began as a hamlet in the fields outside Versailles. Louis XIV acquired it and built first the Trianon de porcelaine, a pavilion decorated with blue-and-white tiles, and then the Grand Trianon, where he could escape the oppressive etiquette of the main palace. Louis XV commissioned the Petit Trianon from Gabriel for Madame de Pompadour, who died before it was finished; it was completed in 1768 and became associated with Madame du Barry, the king’s favourite, and the king used it for botany, gardening and small suppers. Its famous mechanical dining table rose from the basement already laid, so that the king could eat without a crowd of servants — privacy engineered into the architecture.

In 1774 Louis XVI gave the Petit Trianon to Marie-Antoinette, who made it her own. She had the gardens redesigned in the Anglo-Chinese taste, built the Théâtre de la Reine and, in 1783, the Hameau de la Reine, a model village of thatched cottages and a working farm around an artificial lake. The queen’s insistence on a private domain, closed to courtiers, was resented; in revolutionary polemic the Trianon became shorthand for royal extravagance and secrecy, and the buildings were emptied after 1789. They survived, were restored in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and are now among the most visited parts of the Versailles estate.

In this reading

Carlyle’s sentence — “The shepherd of the people has been carried home from Little Trianon, heavy of heart, and been put to bed in his own Chateau of Versailles” — refers to the last illness of Louis XV in May 1774. The king had been at the Petit Trianon when he fell ill and was brought back to the main palace to die. “Little Trianon” is Carlyle’s deliberate Englishing: the diminutive adjective makes the retreat sound small and domestic, and sharpens the contrast with the immense official palace to which the king is carried. The pastoral irony of “the shepherd of the people” and “the flock knows it, and heeds it not” depends on that contrast between the private little house and the public seat of power.

Current uses and extensions

  • Heritage and tourism: the building and its gardens are part of the Musée national des châteaux de Versailles et de Trianon; “Trianon” alone in a Versailles context may mean either palace or the estate.
  • Marie-Antoinette mythology: the Petit Trianon is central to the modern image of the queen — privacy, dressmaking, the farm, the supposed shepherd life — and appears in biographies, films and exhibitions.
  • Geopolitics: the Treaty of Trianon, signed in the Grand Trianon on 4 June 1920, fixed the borders of Hungary after the First World War; “Trianon” in Hungarian public memory means that treaty, not the château.
  • Naming: hotels, porcelain patterns, restaurants and residential blocks borrow “Trianon” for its aura of French elegance; the English calque “Little Trianon” is now archaic outside historical writing.
