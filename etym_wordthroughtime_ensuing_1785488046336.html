- [late Middle English: literal following] - Ensue, from Old French ensuivre (Latin insequi, "to follow after"), first means simply to follow after in place or time, in legal and narrative writing.
- [16th-17th c.: logical consequence] - The sense of "to follow as a result" crystallises: what ensues does not merely come later but grows out of what preceded it, in a formal, measured register.
- [18th-20th c.: the narrative formula] - The participle adjective "ensuing" settles into stock narrative use ("ensuing chaos," "the ensuing weeks") with a slightly literary, dramatic colouring.
- [Modern: attributive adjective] - Today ensuing survives mainly before nouns, meaning "immediately after and as the direct consequence of," carrying a hint of inevitability; the bare verb ensue is now comparatively rare.
