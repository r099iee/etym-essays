The story of dominions begins not with an empire but with a house. The word is the final, imperial chapter of a chain that runs from Proto-Indo-European *dom "house", through the Latin household and its master, through Roman property law, into the vocabulary of medieval lordship, and finally into the plural political noun that Gibbon uses for the composite territories of the Roman state. The Indo-European house. The PIE root *dem- "to build", with its nominal form *dom / *domos "house", is securely attested across the family: Sanskrit dama, Greek domos, Slavic dom, Armenian tun — all meaning "house, home". The Italic branch produced Latin domus, which, like its cognates, meant both the physical building and the household as a social unit: family, slaves, property, and ancestors. In a culture where the paterfamilias ruled his house nearly as absolutely as a magistrate ruled the state, the step from "house" to "master" was short and natural. From house to lord. Latin dominus is derived from domus and means "master of the house" — the man whose word ran inside the household. The word then generalized along the social scale: a dominus is a lord, an owner, a ruler; the owner of slaves is their dominus, the head of a school is the school's dominus, and Christians addressed God as Dominus. The feminine domina gave Romance and English titles of address — dame, madam — and the verb dominari "to be lord, rule" gave English dominate and dominant. Even the Christian Sunday got its name from the Lord: Spanish domingo, French dimanche, Italian domenica all descend from dominicus "of the Lord". From mastery to property. Roman law developed a technical noun, dominium, for the fullest right a person could hold over a thing: absolute ownership. The jurists distinguished dominium (ownership) from mere possessio (possession, which could be temporary or even wrongful). This legal precision is the hinge of the whole etymology: dominium turned a social relationship (master and household) into a proprietary one (owner and thing). It is the master's right abstracted into law. From property to territory. Medieval writers began applying proprietary talk to states, so that a ruler's dominium became his lordship over lands. Old French and Law French carried the word into English: dominion appeared around 1450 for rule and sovereignty, and by the sixteenth century it meant the territories over which a sovereign ruled. The plural dominions became the standard English word for a composite set of realms — exactly Gibbon's usage. English also imported siblings from the same Latin family: domain (a sphere of control), the feudal demesne (land kept for the lord's own use), and the theological Dominions (the angelic rank that exercises lordship, from Latin dominationes). Gibbon and the eighteenth century. Gibbon's plural "those dominions which had been acquired" is deliberately proprietary. The word lets him treat the Roman provinces as a portfolio: accumulated by republican conquest — "the policy of the senate, the active emulation of the consuls, and the martial enthusiasm of the people" — and then merely conserved by the emperors. The etymology underwrites the argument: a household-master's word has become the word for the world-house of an empire. The Dominion of the British Empire. The last institutional career of the word was constitutional. In 1867 the British North America Act created the "Dominion of Canada"; Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and Newfoundland followed as "Dominions" — self-governing members of an empire, equal in status to Britain, acknowledging only a shared crown. The term was a careful compromise: it avoided "kingdom" (which would have affronted the crown's unity) while granting real autonomy. The Statute of Westminster (1931) gave legal form to this equality. Canada renamed Dominion Day as Canada Day in 1982, quietly retiring the word; but "dominion" survives in legal and historical prose and in the memory of the Commonwealth. The angelic and the biblical. Two religious uses kept the word alive in non-political registers. In angelology, the Dominions are the fourth choir of angels, from Latin dominationes — beings whose very nature is lordship, holding God's rule. In the King James Bible, "dominion" names the authority granted to humanity: "have dominion over the fish of the sea" (Genesis 1:26). Both senses descend from the same master-of-the-house root and show how far the word travelled from its humble architectural beginning. Conclusion. From *dom "house" to domus, dominus, dominium, dominion, dominions: a single chain, each step abstracting one degree further from the physical building — household, master, ownership, lordship, territory, constitutional status, and finally the poetic imperial plural of Gibbon. The word has never quite lost the smell of the paterfamilias.
