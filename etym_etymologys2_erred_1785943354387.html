ERRED is the regular past-tense form of English err, and the whole word descends in a single attested line from an Indo-European root meaning "to be in motion, to wander." The chain is traced below from the root to the modern inflected form. 1. Proto-Indo-European *ers- (also set *h₁ers-) "to be in motion, to wander, to go astray" (reconstructed; projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; cognates include Old English eorre "anger, wrath" through the sense of the mind being set in motion, Old Norse jǫrask "to be angry," and the extended family in Germanic) — the root of motion and straying from which the Latin verb derives. 2. Latin errāre "to wander, to rove, to stray from a path; to go astray, to be mistaken" (attested from the earliest Latin literature, e.g. Plautus and Terence, 3rd–2nd c. BCE) — the direct Italic continuation of *ers-, already carrying both the physical sense "wander" and the transferred sense "be wrong"; the source of Latin error "a wandering, a mistake" and errāticus "wandering, erratic." 3. Old French errer "to wander, to travel, to roam; to go astray, to be mistaken" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries) — the regular Romance continuation of Latin errāre, with the infinitive ending -āre > -er; the Old French sense "to travel, to journey" explains the medieval "knight errant," the knight who wanders seeking adventure (chevalier errant). 4. Middle English erren "to err, to go astray, to be mistaken; to wander" (attested from the 13th–14th centuries; borrowed from Old French errer) — the verb enters English with both the physical and the moral-intellectual senses; spellings include erre, erren. 5. English err "to be mistaken, to be wrong; (archaic) to wander, to stray" (modern form; the senses "wander" and "stray" decay into obsolescence and survive only in errant, erratic, and the phrase "knight errant") — the base verb. 6. English ERRED (past tense and past participle, formed with the regular weak inflectional suffix -ed on the stem err-; attested from the Middle English period onward as erred(e), and standard in modern English) ◄ TARGET — the exact form of this card. NOTE ON DEBATE AND NUANCE: (1) The reconstruction of the root as *ers- "to be in motion, to wander" is standard and supported by the Germanic and Italic cognates, but the precise laryngeal grade (some scholars write *h₁ers-) and the exact semantic contour of the root are inferred. (2) The famous proverb "To err is human, to forgive divine" (Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism, 1711) is not an etymology but a literary attestation that fixed the modern moral register of the verb; the underlying religious sense of erring as sinning predates Pope. (3) English "err" descends through Old French, not directly from Latin; the Old French intermediary is attested and should not be skipped, even though the Latin form is the direct parent of the French one. (4) The modern idiom "err on the side of caution" is a 20th-century development; in it "err" is used ironically and has shed almost all negative force.
