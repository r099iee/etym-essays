Squeaked is the simple past tense (and, identically, the past participle) of the verb squeak: to utter or produce a short, high-pitched, thin sound — the cry of a mouse, the noise of a hinge or a shoe, the sharp note of something small under pressure. Applied to speech, it marks the voice as small, strained, undignified, or involuntarily sharp rather than resonant or heroic. When Bilbo "squeaked" that the dwarf-door seemed "a great big hole" to him, the narrator undercuts his protest with pure sound: he has no dragon-scale for comparison, only hobbit-holes, and his objection comes out mouse-high. Tone: comic, physical, mildly humiliating; the past tense freezes one small vocal failure in the map debate. The word is one of English's imitative (onomatopoeic) treasures: its sound is its meaning, and the s-/sk- onset with the high front vowel encodes a thin, high, friction-heavy noise. It belongs to a whole family of expressive words — squeal, screech, shriek, creak, squawk — that English has coined and reinforced for centuries because the sound itself does the work. The past form squeaked is entirely regular, formed with -ed, so the morphology offers no surprises; the interest lies in the sound and in what it does to the dignity of whoever makes it. Primary senses
  • Of small animals (mice, bats, birds): to utter a short, thin, high-pitched cry — the defining noise of a mouse.
  • Of objects: to make such a sound by friction or pressure — a hinge squeaks, a shoe squeaks, brakes squeak, rubber squeaks on a floor.
  • Of a person's voice: to speak or cry in a thin, high, strained tone, usually when nervous, frightened, excited, or physically small — "he squeaked in protest".
  • Of an infant or pet: to emit small high sounds of complaint or pleasure.
Polysemy and figurative senses
  • To squeak through / past / by: to succeed or pass by a very narrow margin — "the bill squeaked through parliament", "the team squeaked into the playoffs" — the image of passing through an opening so small it nearly catches you.
  • A (narrow) squeak: a close shave or narrow escape — "that was a narrow squeak!" — the same image of a tight margin.
  • Squeaky: (of a voice or object) making squeaks; and in the idiom "the squeaky wheel gets the grease", the loudest complainer gets attention.
  • Squeaky-clean: scrupulously clean, and by metaphor morally or reputationally beyond reproach — the sense of an object so clean it squeaks.
  • Not a squeak: not a single sound, word, or piece of information — "we haven't heard a squeak from them".
  • As a noun: the sound itself, and in informal use a chance or whisper of success — "not a squeak of hope".
The semantic history of squeaked runs from the body outward: the literal mouse-and-hinge sound first, then the transferred voice-sense, then the metaphors of narrow margin and of smallness that give "squeak" its comic, undignified colour. Every use retains the core image of a thin, high, brief noise — which is why, applied to a hobbit's protest in the middle of a dragon-argument, the word deflates and endears at once.
