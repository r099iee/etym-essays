• Ford: Similar because both involve crossing a body of water on foot or on horseback; differs because ford specifically denotes traversing a stream or river at a recognized shallow crossing point (a ford), whereas wade refers to the physical motion of walking through water of any depth or location regardless of whether a crossing is completed.
  • Paddle: Similar because both describe walking in shallow water; differs because paddle denotes casual, leisurely, or playful splashing in ankle-deep water (often barefoot at the beach), whereas wade implies overcoming resistance, deeper immersion, or deliberate, laborious forward progress.
  • Trudge: Similar because both describe slow, laborious, heavy-footed walking against resistance; differs because trudge emphasizes weariness, fatigue, and heavy footsteps on dry ground or uphill roads, whereas wade specifically requires a fluid, viscous, or dense enveloping medium like water, mud, or snow.
  • Plod: Similar because both describe slow, rhythmic, unhurried, effortful steps; differs because plod suggests a dull, monotonous, mechanical walking pace across any terrain without the specific immersion resistance inherent in wading.
  • Slog: Similar because both describe difficult, exhausting advancement through harsh conditions or dense tasks; differs because slog conveys punishing toil, blunt force, or physical struggle through mud and rain, often carrying an emotional register of grim endurance.
  • Swim: Similar because both involve aquatic locomotion across a body of water; differs because swim requires buoyancy and propulsion through water without feet touching the ground, whereas wade strictly requires walking with feet planted on the submerged bed or bottom.
  • Navigate: Similar because both can describe moving through complex terrain or dense material; differs because navigate is a cerebral, strategic, or technological process of plotting a course or steering a vessel, whereas wade is visceral, physical stepping against drag.
  • Trek: Similar because both involve arduous travel; differs because trek describes an extensive, multi-day overland journey across wild country, rather than the immediate physical action of walking through an impeding medium.
