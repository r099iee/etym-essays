- [Late 14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed into English legal and theological prose from Anglo-French defrauder, signifying the illicit deprivation of property, lands, or lawful wages through deceitful stratagems — [perception: a mortal sin of covetousness, violating Christian fellowship and civic trust]
- [15th–16th c. Early Modern English] — Cemented in statutory law (Tudor statutes on bankruptcy and false tokens) and Protestant translations of the Bible (Tyndale, Geneva, KJV) targeting oppressive landlords who withhold harvest wages — [perception: an unrighteous economic crime against God and the commonwealth]
- [17th–18th c. Enlightenment & Commercial Expansion] — Expanded with the rise of modern joint-stock mercantilism, maritime insurance, and negotiable paper credit; the term came to define commercial swindles, forged bills of exchange, and fraudulent conveyances — [perception: a severe violation of the financial confidence necessary for civil society]
- [Early 19th c. Romantic Era] — Abstracted by poets such as William Wordsworth in The Prelude to denote the psychological, spiritual, or aesthetic deprivation of promised joy or poetic inspiration — [perception: a poignant, melancholic withholding of natural or spiritual communion]
- [20th–21st c. Modern Era] — Standardized in modern white-collar criminal jurisprudence, securities regulation (SEC), consumer protection statutes, and digital cybersecurity (wire fraud, phishing scams) — [perception: a ubiquitous, sophisticated corporate or cyber menace requiring stringent regulatory and forensic prosecution]
