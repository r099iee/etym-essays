Colonists looks like a simple plural, but its history reaches back to the Latin verb colere, "to till, cultivate, inhabit, worship". A colonus was a farmer or tenant, and a colonia was a settlement of such cultivators, often a town planted by Rome in conquered or allied territory. The agricultural meaning is primary: to colonise was first to put land under the plough, and only later to put it under a flag. English "colony" enters in the late fourteenth century from Old French colonie, and "colonist" is a later English formation, first recorded around 1701, from colony plus the agent suffix -ist (probably reinforced by French coloniste).

Roman and Greek precedents. The Roman colonia was a legal and military institution as much as an agricultural one: veteran soldiers and citizens were settled in newly conquered land, receiving plots and forming a loyal urban nucleus. Greek cities planted apoikiai around the Mediterranean for trade, land and political exile. English historians apply "colonists" to both, although the Roman colonus could be a native tenant as well as an incoming settler, and the Greek apoikos left a mother city rather than a distant empire. These ancient cases supplied the vocabulary and the precedent for early modern European expansion.

Early modern expansion. From the sixteenth century the word's modern sense took shape in the Atlantic world. English "plantations" in Ireland, Virginia and the Caribbean, Spanish colonias in the Americas, and French settlements in Canada and the Antilles created populations that were both resident and politically dependent. The actors called themselves "planters", "adventurers", "servants" or "subjects" more often than "colonists"; "colonist" became the retrospective and analytical term. The word's neutrality is misleading, because the settlements rested on land taken from indigenous peoples and, in many cases, on enslaved labour.

Revolution and the political category. In the 1760s and 1770s the American crisis turned "colonists" into a political noun. The settlers claimed the rights of Englishmen while denying parliamentary sovereignty; British writers used "colonists" to distance them, and the Declaration of Independence spoke of "the inhabitants of these colonies" rather than of colonists. After independence, "colonists" became a historical label for the pre-revolutionary period, and the new citizens were "Americans". The episode shows how quickly a word of settlement can become a word of allegiance.

The settler empires. The nineteenth century was the great age of the colonist in the Anglophone world. British emigrants to Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa, French colons in Algeria, and Dutch and British settlers in southern Africa built societies that claimed the land as their own. Imperial writing celebrated colonists as pioneers, builders and bearers of civilisation; indigenous and later postcolonial writing described the same people as invaders and beneficiaries of dispossession. The word's emotional temperature depends almost entirely on who is speaking.

Twentieth-century critique. After the Second World War, decolonisation and the rise of postcolonial studies changed the vocabulary. Scholars distinguished colonisation as a process, colonialism as a system, colonizers as agents, and colonists as the settler population. The concept of settler colonialism, associated with historians such as Patrick Wolfe, describes societies in which settlers come to stay and must therefore eliminate or displace indigenous claims to land; in this framework "colonist" is not a neutral description of residence but a structural position. The term has since been applied to Israel/Palestine, Australia, Canada, the United States and Latin America, often in sharply contested ways.

Scientific and technical extensions. Outside politics, the word keeps its original sense of establishing a population in a new place. In ecology, colonists are the first organisms to occupy a disturbed or newly formed habitat, from lichens on lava to grasses on abandoned fields; in island biogeography they are the arrivals whose immigration and extinction rates determine species richness. In medicine, a colonist microbe establishes a stable population on a host without necessarily causing disease, a usage that preserves the old contrast between settlement and invasion.

Contemporary life. The word now appears in at least four registers: historical ("Roman colonists"), political-critical ("settler colonists"), scientific ("colonist species") and futuristic ("Mars colonists"). It survives in institutional names such as the Victoria Times Colonist, and in gaming and science fiction. Its plural form, colonists, is especially common because settlement is a collective act: one does not usually found a colony alone.

  • Semantic core: cultivation > settlement > political occupation.
  • Evaluative swing: neutral administrative term > imperial compliment > postcolonial accusation.
  • Related doublets: "cultivate", "culture", "cult" and "colony" all descend from Latin colere, showing how farming, dwelling and worship shared one root.
  • False friend: "colon" (punctuation, anatomy) comes from Greek kōlon, not from colonia.

That ordinary, agricultural ancestry is the key to the word's long and troubled afterlife.
