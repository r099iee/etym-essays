Boatswain, pronounced bosun, is a compound of two old words that had long and separate histories before the sea joined them. The first, boat, descends from Old English bāt and Proto-Germanic *bait-, a word of uncertain but fascinating origin: many etymologists, following Watkins, trace it to the PIE root *bheid-, "to split, cleave," on the theory that the first boats were dugouts — tree-trunks split and hollowed out, vessels carved from a single piece of wood. The same root that would have named the dugout scattered its family far and wide: it is the root of Latin findere, "to split," giving English fission and fissure, the splitting of the atom; of Sanskrit bhid-, "to split," with its Hindi descendant bhed, "division"; and of the Germanic biting-words, since to bite is to cleave with the teeth — English bite and bait are members of the family too. The alternative view holds that the Germanic boat-word may simply be an extension of the name of some part of a ship, and some scholars suspect an obscure or substrate origin; the dugout story is vivid but not certain. Either way, boat came to English through the Germanic sea-people and spread even back into the Romance tongues, which borrowed it — Old French batel gave English bateau, and Italian battello and Spanish batel are Germanic loans as well. A boat, in its deep etymology, is a thing that has been split out of the world.

The second element, swain, descends from Old Norse sveinn, "boy, servant, attendant," from Proto-Germanic *swainaz, which etymologists derive from the great reflexive pronoun *s(w)e-, "self, one's own" — a swain is literally "one's own man," the personal retainer bound to his lord, his household, or his boat. The word entered English around the mid-twelfth century as swein, "a young man attendant upon a knight," and from that courtly sense it wandered downward and upward at once: by 1570 a swain was a country labourer, and a decade later, in the artificial world of pastoral poetry, a swain became a lover and wooer, the shepherd-swain sighing in the eclogues. English never lost the servant-sense either — the swain remained the attendant — and it is that servant-sense that the sea preserved: the boatswain is, literally and exactly, the "boat's-servant," the man who tends the boat and its gear.

The two words came together early in English sea-speech: the compound is attested in late Old English as batswegen and in Middle English as bot-swein from the mid-15th century, at the moment when the age of sail was organising its hierarchies. The office itself was already ancient — classical galleys had their navarch and the Vikings' ships their stýrimaðr — but English fixed the boatswain as the warrant officer of the deck, in charge of rigging, anchors, sails, and the hands who worked them, armed with the boatswain's call to pipe orders and the rope's-end to enforce them. The pronunciation compressed almost immediately: shipboard speech collapsed boat-swain into bosun, one syllable, and the spelling has ever since lagged behind the mouth — bosun, bos'n, bo's'n all appear, while the etymological boatswain remains the dictionary form.

The office itself became a literary institution. In the age-of-sail fiction of Smollett, Marryat, Melville, and Stevenson the boatswain is a stock figure — profane, weather-beaten, competent, the disciplinarian of ropes and men, the man who knows the gear the way the captain knows the charts. With the coming of steam the role shrank and shifted (in the modern U.S. Navy the rating is the boatswain's mate), and the word grew historical, echoing the age of sail rather than the machine-age navy. In this passage the story uses the word's whole cultural payload at once: "a Maltese boatswain shoots him in the back with a small pistol" — the trade does the work of a dossier, the rank makes the murder professional and administrative, and the "small pistol" is the moral size of the deed. A boat's-servant grown to a hardened warrant officer, a word split and joined across a thousand years, an office piped into English speech as one clipped syllable — the boatswain is the age of sail made flesh, and the age of sail made murder.

  • 8th–9th c.: Old English bāt "boat"; c. 1150: Middle English swein from Old Norse sveinn "boy, servant."
  • Late OE / mid-15th c.: batswegen, bot-swein — the compound "boat-servant" is attested.
  • 16th–17th c.: the boatswain is a fixed warrant officer of the age of sail, piped aboard by his call.
  • 18th–19th c.: stock character of sea fiction — profane, weather-beaten, competent.
  • 19th–20th c.: steam-era continuity as boatswain's mate; the word grows historical.
  • Contemporary: nautical-historical and literary register, always pronounced "bosun" — cold professionalism of the deck.
