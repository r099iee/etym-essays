AMBIGUITY is a noun meaning the quality of a word, phrase, or text that admits of more than one interpretation, so that meaning is not fixed by the surface form alone. It is a compound noun built inside Latin on two roots — the prefix ambi- "around, on both sides" and the verb agere "to drive, do, act" — and its history is the history of the idea that a single utterance can point in two directions at once. In this card the word crowns the title of William Empson's famous critical study, Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930), whose title page also advertises the author's earlier books Some Versions of Pastoral and The Gathering Storm; even the title page, a mere paratext, functions as a frontispiece for a theory in which double meaning is not an accident of language but the very engine of poetic power. The core sense is the simultaneous availability of two or more genuinely defensible interpretations of the same utterance, a condition that may be unintended (a flaw to be disambiguated, to be removed by clarifying) or, in literature, deliberately cultivated as a source of density and tension. Polysemy and allied notions must be carefully separated. Vagueness is a deficit of information: the boundaries of meaning are blurred because details are missing, and no competing precise readings exist; a vague sentence is under-specified, whereas an ambiguous one is over-specified in two rival directions at once. Polysemy is the dictionary fact that one word carries several settled, distinct senses (bank as river bank and financial bank); it is a lexical inventory, neutral and usually resolvable by context, whereas ambiguity is a live uncertainty in a particular utterance. Equivocation is the deliberate, often deceptive use of double meaning to mislead, especially in debate, and implies bad faith; ambiguity itself is morally neutral. A double entendre is a narrower, usually humorous or suggestive ambiguity hinging on a single phrase. Modern technical and register senses:
  • Linguistics: lexical ambiguity (one word with two senses), syntactic or structural ambiguity (a sentence parseable two ways, e.g. "I saw the man with the telescope"), scope ambiguity (in logic and semantics, the different readings of quantifiers), and semantic ambiguity are standard technical terms.
  • Law: ambiguity is a technical defect; the doctrine contra proferentem resolves an ambiguous contract clause against the party who drafted it, and "latent" versus "patent" ambiguity distinguishes hidden from obvious doubt.
  • Literature and criticism: since Empson, deliberate ambiguity is celebrated as the source of poetic richness; "Empsonian ambiguity" names the systematic study of multiple simultaneous meanings in a poem.
  • Rhetoric: ambiguity in classical and Renaissance rhetoric was a vice (equivocatio), the sophist's weapon, to be exposed and avoided; the revaluation into a virtue is Empson's twentieth-century achievement.
  • Everyday and journalistic use: "there is no ambiguity about X" means X is perfectly clear; "to remove all ambiguity" means to settle the meaning; ambiguity is felt as vagueness or evasiveness in ordinary speech.
  • Psychology and decision theory: "ambiguity aversion" (Ellsberg) names the preference for known risks over unknown ones, where ambiguity is the sheer unreliability of information rather than its double meaning.
Register ranges from everyday to highly technical; the word is equally at home in a contract clause and a poem. The historical bridge from the Latin compound is transparent and worth keeping in mind: ambigere meant "to drive both ways," to drive something around and around, and so to hesitate, wander, or be in doubt; the noun ambiguitas named the state of being driven two ways at once — the double direction of meaning that this card's word names. Empson's title thus inherits, in a single Latin coinage, the oldest image of indecision in the European languages: a mind, or a word, driven simultaneously toward two different destinations.
