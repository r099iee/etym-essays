Wily (adjective) describes a person, creature or scheme that is full of wiles — that is, skilled in the use of clever, often deceptive strategies to gain an advantage over others. The word denotes a shrewd, subtle kind of cleverness that operates in the shadows of intention: the wily figure rarely attacks head-on, preferring to out-think, out-manoeuvre and out-wait opponents. In the sentence from Tolkien's The Hobbit, the dragons' wily ways are precisely their reputation for trickery and sly cunning — the very thing that makes them dangerous.

Core meaning. The essential sense is “possessing or exhibiting craftiness, slyness and subtle deceitfulness.” Unlike plain “intelligent” or “clever”, wily implies a tactical, self-interested cunning that is frequently directed against others. It often carries an edge of moral disapproval, yet is just as frequently tinged with grudging respect for the sheer effectiveness of the trickery involved. The word always points to the mind behind the action: a wily act is a planned, manipulative act, not a lucky or spontaneous one, and the wily agent is expected to be several moves ahead of everyone else.

Polysemy and shades of meaning. While the word is not heavily polysemous, it shows distinct nuances depending on context:

  • Skillful cunning (strategic): the wily politician, general or negotiator who uses foresight, misdirection and long-term planning to win. Here the word is almost admiring, suggesting mastery of the game of wits.
  • Sly deceit (tactical): the wily trickster, fox or swindler who uses small lies and manipulative ruses. Here the word is pejorative, implying dishonesty and unreliability.
  • Animal and fable usage: from Aesop's fox to folk-tale tricksters, “wily” is the stock epithet for animals (foxes, serpents, coyotes, weasels) famed for cunning in story and proverb.
  • Literary-archaic flavour: the word carries a slightly literary, even archaic register in modern English; it is more common in narrative prose, fantasy and fable than in everyday speech.
  • Guarded praise: in sports, business and politics, “a wily veteran” means an experienced competitor who wins through craft and experience rather than brute force — a compliment wrapped in a warning.

Grammar and morphology. Wily is a regular gradable adjective (comparative wilier, superlative wiliest); the -y suffix means “full of, characterized by”. It derives from the noun wile (“a trick, stratagem, piece of craftiness”), which is now chiefly used in the plural (wiles, as in “feminine wiles” or “the wiles of the devil”). The related adverb is wilily, and the abstract noun is wiliness. The adjective is almost always used of people, animals or personified qualities, and can stand attributively or predictively without restriction.

Related words and phrases.

  • Wiles (plural noun): deceitful or manipulative stratagems; enticements and beguilements.
  • Wiliness: the quality of being wily; sly craftiness.
  • Wilier / wiliest: comparative and superlative forms.
  • Collocations: “a wily old fox”, “a wily scheme”, “a wily opponent”, “wily ways”, “wily charm”.

Usage notes. In the Tolkien context, Smaug is imagined as knowing the wily ways of dragons — their cunning, deceptive, trickster habits — and the narrator observes that knowledge of such ways would have made Bilbo more frightened and less hopeful. The word aligns with Tolkien's mythopoeic register: Old English and Norse tradition frequently celebrated and warned against wiles in heroic and comic literature alike. Today “wily” is used about people, animals and abstractions (“a wily plan”), and almost always implies that the cleverness is used to gain advantage, usually at someone else's expense. It is not a high-frequency colloquial word, but it is instantly understood and vividly evocative of a cunning that is at once admired and feared.
