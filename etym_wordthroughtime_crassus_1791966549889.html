- 2nd–1st c. BCE — a hereditary cognomen of the Licinii, literally "stout, thick-set"; a neutral descriptive surname with no pejorative force.
- Late Republic — after his death in 91 BCE, the orator's name becomes a byword for supreme eloquence, fixed by Cicero's Brutus and De Oratore as a standard of Latinity.
- 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE — the triumvir's career and death at Carrhae pull the name toward wealth and disastrous ambition, and the two Crassi begin to be confused in popular memory.
- 16th–18th c. — English readers meet the name chiefly in translations of Cicero and Plutarch; "as rich as Crassus" enters the phrase-book, permanently attaching the name to the triumvir.
- 19th–21st c. — classical scholarship uses "Crassus" contextually and often adds "the Orator"; general culture knows only the triumvir, while the insult survives separately in the adjective crass.
