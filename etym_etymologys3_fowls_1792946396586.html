The fowls of the farmyard descend from one of the great mysteries of Germanic vocabulary. Proto-Germanic *fuglaz, meaning simply bird, stands alone: unlike goose, duck, or sparrow, it has no certain relatives anywhere else in Indo-European. The leading hypothesis links it to PIE *plew-, the root of flying, flowing, and floating — seen in Greek plein, to sail, and Latin pluma, feather — imagining the bird as the flying one, named for what it does rather than what it is. It is a beautiful guess, and most handbooks print it, but the honest dictionaries add the warning: the phonology is irregular, the cognates are missing, and *fuglaz may be a purely Germanic coinage whose deeper story is lost with the bird-catchers who first spoke it.

Whatever its ultimate source, the word thrived in every Germanic tongue: Old Saxon fugal, Old Norse fugl, Gothic fugls, German Vogel, Dutch vogel — a parliament of cognates testifying to how central birds were to early northern life, as food, as augury, as weather prophets. Old English fugol ranged across the whole feathered creation: poets wrote of the fugol in the air as naturally as of the fugol on the dish, and riddles teased listeners with birds described but never named. The compound fugeltimber even meant a young bird, timber in the old sense of material or stuff.

Centuries of narrowing:

  • Old and Middle English breadth (9th–14th centuries): Fugol, then foul or fowel, remains the ordinary word for any bird. Chaucer's Parlement of Foules gathers eagles and turtles, not chickens, to choose their mates on Saint Valentine's day — fowls at their most aristocratic and allegorical.
  • The King James crown (1611): Behold the fowls of the air fixes the broad sense in immortal English. Millions who never kept a chicken knew fowl as the Bible's word for bird, and hymns, sermons, and funeral readings kept that grandeur circulating for three more centuries.
  • The rise of bird (16th–18th centuries): The young upstart bird — originally the nestling, brid — grows up and evicts fowl from the wild kingdom. By 1700 a naturalist speaks of birds in the bush and fowls in the yard; the semantic partition is complete, though poets keep trespassing across it.
  • Sport and menu (19th century): Divided, the word prospers in both halves: wildfowlers pursue wildfowl on the estuaries under their own ancient laws, while housewives order a fine fowl from the poulterer. The compounds wildfowl, waterfowl, and fowling-piece fossilise the old hunting world inside the new.
  • Modern farmyard (20th century to today): Industrial poultry farming nearly killed the word, replacing fowls with broilers, layers, and stock; then the backyard-hen revival brought it fondly back. To keep fowls now is a statement — of tradition, of self-sufficiency, of resistance to the battery cage — and the chattering yard of the source sentence sounds all the sweeter for it.

Sound shifts and kin: The Germanic f for PIE p is Grimm's Law in action, the textbook consonant shift that separates Germanic from its sisters. Old English fugol lost its middle vowel, Middle English varied the spelling wildly — foul, fowel, fowle — and the modern pronunciation kept the owl-like ow diphthong that distinguishes fowl from foul, foul meaning rotten, only in spelling and context. German Vogel and Dutch vogel remain its closest living kin, still meaning bird in the grand old sense that English has mostly surrendered.
