- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Inception) - Coined in Early Modern English during the Elizabethan era by appending the native Germanic adverbial suffix *-ly* to the Latinate adjective *erroneous* (from Latin *errōneus* "wandering, straying"); primarily utilized in theological, ecclesiastical, and scholastic treatises to denote straying from orthodox Christian doctrine, scriptural truth, or canonical rules.
- 17th Century (Scientific Revolution & Empiricism) - Embraced by founding figures of natural philosophy and the Royal Society (Francis Bacon, Sir Thomas Browne in *Pseudodoxia Epidemica*, John Locke); shifted away from religious heresy toward empirical epistemological critique, describing how common human senses and ancient superstitions "erroneously" misinterpret physical nature.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Juridical Formalization) - Established as a foundational keyword of Enlightenment historiography, jurisprudence, and lexicography (William Blackstone, Samuel Johnson, David Hume); adopted by common-law judges to characterize judicial rulings or verdicts delivered upon flawed evidence without impugning the moral integrity of the magistrate.
- 19th Century (Comparative Philology & Natural Sciences) - Deeply entrenched in academic scholarship, especially the emergent discipline of comparative philology and historical linguistics; deployed systematically by philologists (Max Müller, W. W. Skeat, James Murray) to dissect "folk etymology," explaining how speakers erroneously associate phonetically similar words across unrelated historical stems.
- 20th Century (Administrative, Scientific, and Medical Standardization) - Became ubiquitous across academic peer-review journals, statistical errata, appellate court opinions, and clinical pathology reports to denote false-positive diagnostic outcomes, calculation discrepancies, and administrative misattributions.
- Contemporary Usage - Thrives in computing, artificial intelligence, and algorithmic data auditing (e.g., users erroneously blocked, transactions erroneously flagged), retaining its time-honored reputation as the quintessential objective adverb for honest factual and systemic error.
