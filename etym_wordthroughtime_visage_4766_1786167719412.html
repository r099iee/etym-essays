- 14th century – 'visage' enters Middle English from Old French (from Latin visus, 'sight, appearance', the past participle of vidēre, 'to see'), meaning the face — but the face as an object of sight and regard, not the neutral body part.
- 16th–17th centuries – the word thrives in the high registers of epic, romance, drama, and devotional verse: a dignified, slightly formal face 'as beheld,' the surface from which observers read character and emotion.
- 18th–19th centuries – 'visage' remains the literary and portrait-painter's word — Wordsworth's 'The Child is father of the Man' treats the face as a readable document of history — while everyday speech already prefers the plain 'face.'
- 20th–21st centuries – 'visage' is now consciously archaic and formal, reserved for solemn, dramatic, or mock-literary effect; the word has narrowed from the ordinary word for the face into a deliberate stylistic elevation of it.
