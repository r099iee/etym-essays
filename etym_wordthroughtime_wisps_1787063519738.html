- Earliest recorded sense (14th century): a small bundle or handful of straw, hay, or twigs, used for sweeping, wiping, or lighting — purely concrete and agricultural.
- By the 16th century extended to anything thin and loosely gathered — strands of hair, feathers, tufts of grass.
- In the 17th century used of a faint, evanescent streak of smoke, mist, or cloud — the word turns ethereal and delicate.
- In the 18th–19th centuries fully figurative: a wisp of a smile, a wisp of a song, a wisp of memory — faintness becomes emotional delicacy.
- Modern use: "will-o'-the-wisp" for any elusive illusion; a ghostly wisp in fantasy and gaming; a wisp of smoke as a stock descriptive phrase — the word travels from coarse bundle to ghostly trace.
