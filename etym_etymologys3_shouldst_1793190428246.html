The biography of shouldst is the biography of obligation itself, beginning with guilt. The standard reconstruction traces it to PIE *skel-, a root concerning fault, debt, or being under a charge — the condition of owing something to someone, whether money or amends. In Proto-Germanic this hardens into the preterite-present verb *skulan, to owe or be obliged: a strange verb whose present tense wears past-tense clothes, because obligation was felt as something already incurred, a debt standing over from before.

Old English debt-language: The Anglo-Saxons say ic sceal, I owe or must, and thū scealt, thou owest or must — duty phrased as debt. Its past is sceolde, owed or was obliged to, ancestor of modern should. Already the verb reaches beyond money: wyrd, fate, lays sceal on heroes — thou must die, thou art doomed — obligation shading into necessity and destiny. Law codes, charters, and the Lord’s Prayer bind the community in sceal-language.

Middle English softening: After the Conquest the forms level to shal, shalt, sholde, sholdest. Crucially the past form begins its famous drift: should no longer means merely owed in the past but comes to mean ought in the present, more tentative and courteous than shall. Chaucer’s pilgrims sholde do a dozen gentle duties, and Wyclif’s Bible lays shalt and sholdest on prophets and sinners.

The thou system: Early Modern English keeps the full second-person singular with -st or -est: thou hadst, thou wouldst, thou shouldst. The ending descends from Old English -est, worn down by centuries of speech. Shouldst thus packages three archaisms in one word — the lost pronoun, the lost ending, and the inverted question order of the source line — which is why a single Why shouldst thou not? dates a text to 1550–1700 at a glance.

Sound shifts: Grimm’s Law turns PIE *sk into Germanic sk (preserved, unusually, before front vowels in this word while shall later palatalises to sh-); the long vowel of sceolde rounds and shortens through Middle English to the modern should, and the -ld- cluster simplifies in speech to something near shood, though spelling keeps the l as a memorial.

Death and afterlife: The 1700s kill thou in polite southern speech, and shouldst falls with it, lingering among Quakers, in northern dialect, and wherever the 1611 Bible is read aloud. The 20th century nearly finishes shall in America too, but should thrives — advice columns, manuals, and reproaches — while shouldst stands frozen, duty in period costume.

Literary witnesses:

  • The King James Bible (1611): thou shouldst across law, prophets, and epistles — the form millions still hear in worship.
  • Shakespeare: reproachful and counselling shouldsts in plays and sonnets, from fathers to fools.
  • Quaker plain speech: thee and thou kept alive into modern times, shouldst among them, as a testimony of equality.

From a Germanic debt-verb to a prophet’s reproach, shouldst preserves what modern English discarded: an ought addressed to one soul alone.
