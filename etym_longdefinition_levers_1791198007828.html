Levers is the plural of lever: rigid bars that pivot around a fixed point (fulcrum) to transmit and multiply force, used to lift, pry, move, or control something; by extension, any handle or control operated by such pivoting motion, and any means of exerting influence.

Physically, a classic lever is a straight bar of wood, stone, or steel resting on or pinned at a fulcrum: effort applied at one end moves load at the other. First-order levers (seesaw, crowbar, stone pry-pole as in the source passage) place fulcrum between effort and load; second-order (wheelbarrow, nutcracker) place load between; third-order (tweezers, fishing rod) place effort between. The defining quality is mechanical advantage: a long effort arm moves a heavy load a short distance.

  • Core prising-tool sense (source sense): Crowbars, jemmies, pry bars, capstan bars, handspikes. In the passage - "wedges, levers, axes" - levers are stout wooden poles or stone-amplified branches used to prize boulders, topple trees, or shift megaliths.
  • Machine-control sense: A projecting handle that regulates a mechanism: gear lever, brake lever, clutch lever, throttle lever, signal-box lever, slot-machine lever. Here the effort is informational rather than muscular; the lever selects states.
  • Internal-mechanism sense: Small pivoted pieces inside devices: lock levers in a Chubb lock, piano action levers, watch levers (lever escapement). Invisible but essential.
  • Figurative influence sense: A source of power to move people or systems: levers of power, levers of change, policy levers, diplomatic levers. Finance extends this to leverage - using borrowed capital to amplify returns (and risks), as a long bar amplifies force. To lever or leverage someone means to use advantage to compel them.

Domain distinctions are important. Engineers distinguish lever from wedge and inclined plane (all simple machines) by pivoting versus sliding. Drivers distinguish lever (hand control) from pedal (foot control) and button. Financiers distinguish leverage (ratio) from levers (instruments). Biophysicists describe bones as levers pulled by muscles around joint fulcrums - the forearm as a third-order lever.

Connotation is ingenuity and disproportionate power: the small moving the large through clever placement. Unlike hammer (brute force) or wedge (insinuation), lever feels intelligent and Archimedean - reason over mass. In plural levers evokes a signal box bristling with handles, a Stonehenge crew with poles, or a strategist's dashboard of pressures - multiplicity of controlled force.

Finally, the ergonomics of levers explain their ubiquity: place the fulcrum close to the load for maximum force, or close to the effort for maximum speed and distance. Rescue crews drill this by feel - a short bite under the slab lifts centimetres with ease, while a long throw flips lighter debris clear. Physiotherapists apply the same arithmetic to crutches and limb braces. Once the three-class system is internalised, learners start seeing levers everywhere - in door handles, oars, wheelbarrows, and even челюсти - and the plural stops meaning many bars and starts meaning a whole grammar of advantageous pivoting.
