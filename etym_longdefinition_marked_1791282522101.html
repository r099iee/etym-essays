Marked as an adjective means clearly noticeable, conspicuous, or pronounced — something that stands out sharply enough to be remarked upon. To put on a waistcoat "in a marked manner" is to do so in a pointedly deliberate, conspicuously dignified or offended way, so that the manner itself draws attention and conveys feeling without words.

The core idea is difference that registers: a marked improvement is not slight but unmistakable; a marked accent is immediately audible; a marked contrast leaps to the eye; a marked man is singled out. The word always implies a baseline of normality against which the marked thing deviates. It is observational rather than judgmental: it says "easily seen," leaving whether the difference is good or bad to context.

Grammatically marked is the past participle of mark fully adjectivised, gradable (very marked, more marked, most marked) and common in formal and journalistic prose. It collocates with manner, degree, and change: marked manner, marked success, marked decline, marked reluctance, marked resemblance, marked effect.

  • Noticeable / pronounced / conspicuous (primary adjectival sense): readily perceived, striking. Bridge: that which has been "marked" — signed, stamped, pointed at — is by definition distinguished from the unmarked background.
  • Deliberate / pointed in behaviour: done so as to be noticed, often expressing disapproval, coldness, or emphasis — "a marked silence," "marked courtesy." Bridge: pragmatic extension from passively noticeable to actively attention-seeking; the actor intends the noticing.
  • Designated / singled out (often ominous): a marked man is targeted by fate, police, or assassins; marked cards are dishonestly identified for cheating. Bridge: literal sense of a physical mark placed on a person or object to select it, preserved in idiom.
  • Linguistic/technical — marked vs. unmarked: in linguistics the marked term carries extra features (e.g. lioness is marked relative to lion); in genetics and semiotics similar oppositions apply. Bridge: structuralist generalisation of the everyday idea that one member of a pair stands out as special while the other is default.

Tone is neutral-formal and efficient. Writers choose marked when big is too vague, extreme too strong, and noticeable too bland; it conveys measured, credible emphasis — the difference was real enough that any careful observer would remark it.

In narrative it often carries social intelligence: a "marked manner" tells the reader that a character is performing feeling — coldness, dignity, reluctance — through visible behaviour rather than speech. Wodehouse relies on exactly this: Bertie wordlessly dressing with exaggerated deliberation so that Jeeves cannot miss the rebuke, the waistcoat itself becoming a signal flag.

Do not confuse adjectival marked with verbal passive ("he was marked by smallpox"): the former evaluates salience, the latter reports a literal imprint, though both spring from the same boundary-stone root.
