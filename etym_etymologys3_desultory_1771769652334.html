At the deepest recoverable layer of desultory stands a Proto-Indo-European leap-root, commonly cited as *sel- (or *sl̥-), reconstructed with the sense "to jump, to leap, to spring." Its Latin heir is salīre "to leap, to jump, to spring," a verb attested from the earliest centuries of Latin literature. From that single verb Rome built a dense family of compounds and frequentatives, and it is from one of those compounds — the circus term dēsultor — that English derives the adjective whose modern meaning is "lacking a plan, purpose, or continuous course."

The Roman leap-family
Latin salīre generated a remarkable cluster of words, most of them compounds with preverbs:
  • saltus "a leap, a jump" (also a mountain or woodland "leap" of land);
  • saltāre "to dance, to leap about," the frequentative of salīre — from it come Italian saltare and French sauter, and, through Old French, English somersault (a leap that turns the body over);
  • saliēns "leaping, springing," the source of English salient "prominent, standing out" (literally leaping forward);
  • assilīre "to leap toward or upon," the source of English assault and assail;
  • īnsilīre "to leap upon," the source of English insult — originally "to jump on" someone, later "to attack verbally," a dramatic shift from physical to verbal injury;
  • resilīre "to leap back," the source of English resilient and resilience, retaining the physics of springing back and then metaphorizing psychological recovery;
  • and exsilīre "to leap out," related to English exult (to leap for joy) through the derived frequentative.
The semantic drift within the family is dramatic: assault keeps the sense of hostile onset; insult shifts from physical "jumping on" someone to verbal abuse; resilient keeps the image of springing back and generalizes it. English inherits all of these as learned or Romance loans rather than as native Germanic inheritance from the same leap-root — the English native word for the motion is the unrelated leap itself.

The desultor and the adjective
The specific ancestor of desultory is the circus technical term dēsultor: the performer who leapt down from one galloping horse and onto another (dē- "down/off" + the salīre family). Roman audiences knew the act as a display of daring agility, and Roman writers also used the word disparagingly of a person who flits from one pursuit to another. The adjective dēsultōrius (formed with the suffix -ōrius, "pertaining to") first meant "pertaining to a desultor," then, by an easy metaphor, "inconstant, superficial, skipping about." Early modern English borrowed desultory in the transferred sense: discourse or study that vaults from point to point without staying the course. The concrete rider fades; the pattern of motion remains. English did not keep the rider himself — the noun desultor is attested only as a learned archaeological term — but the adjective thrived.

Eighteenth-century intellectual evaluation
By the eighteenth century the word is fully at home in moral and intellectual evaluation. Gibbon can oppose "desultory training" to the disciplined formation of the "well-flogged critic" without any need to explain horses: his readers already hear unmethodical leaping. The word had become the standard adjective for study that jumps from author to author, grammar to lexicon, without settling into systematic drill — fragmented enthusiasm against institutional discipline. Military English keeps a more physical residue in "desultory fire," shots that spatter rather than concentrate; "desultory conversation" softens into social small talk that never settles; "desultory reading" is the scattered browsing of the amateur. Throughout, the semantic core is not mere laziness but discontinuous attention: energy spent in jumps rather than in a single line of march.

Register and modern life
Today desultory belongs to educated and slightly formal English, thriving in literary criticism, memoir, historiography, and journalism rather than in casual speech. It commonly carries a mildly self-deprecating or gently critical tone, and in academic self-description it signals modest understatement ("my desultory acquaintance with…"). Writers are advised to reserve it for genuine irregularity of attention or topic rather than use it as a fancy synonym for "random." Its etymology remains transparently alive in its meaning: even a reader who has never seen a Roman desultor feels the word's buried image of vaulting from one thing to the next — the circus leap that Gibbon's "desultory training" in Greek re-enacts on every page of his self-portrait.
