At the root of gentry stands one of the most securely attested words in all of Indo-European, the verb *Ƿenh₁-, “to beget, give birth to, produce.” Its spread across the family is a textbook demonstration of how the Indo-European languages descend from one source: Sanskrit jánati “begets” and jana “people” (Hindi still says जन jan “people” and जन्म janma “birth”), Greek gígnomai “to become, be born” with génos “race, family” (our gene, genetic, genesis), Latin gignere “to beget” with genus and gens, the Germanic kin and king — the king being etymologically “son of the (royal) kin” — and the Balto-Slavic word for woman, the child-bearer, in Russian žená and Lithuanian žmoną. Everywhere the root clusters around the same ideas: birth, descent, kind, family, and people. It is the single most generative root for social vocabulary in the family, and Latin used it to build nothing less than the Roman concept of the clan.

The Roman gens. From gignere, “to beget,” Latin formed the noun gens, gentis, the extended family of those who shared one name: the gens Iūlia, the gens Cornēlia — clans bound by common descent, common cult, and common name. The gens was a social and political unit of enormous weight in the Roman republic; membership conferred a name, a place in public life, and a web of inherited alliances and obligations. From gens Latin made the adjective gentilis, “belonging to the same family,” and this single adjective then split the English vocabulary in two. In the social and legal sense, gentilis came to mean “of good family, high-born, noble”; in the ecclesiastical sense, through the Vulgate's gentes “the nations” (the word for the non-Jewish peoples), it gave English Gentile. The two strands never met again: one became a class label, the other a religious one.

Into French and English. The noble sense of gentilis passed into Old French as gentil, “noble, well-born, courteous” — a word of chivalry, the ancestor of English gentle, gentleman, and genteel. From gentil the Old French formed the abstract noun genterie (also genterise), “gentle birth, nobility, the rank of a gentleman,” by adding the abstract suffix -erie, and Anglo-French passed the word into Middle English as gentrie in the fourteenth century. At first the word meant simply “gentle birth, good family” — an abstract quality; but a class sense quickly grew, and by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries “the gentry” was a settled English social label for the people of gentle birth as a body: the untitled landowners, knights, esquires, and gentlemen who stood below the peerage. The spelling and pronunciation stabilized (gentry, with the /dʒ/ of gentle and the abstract -ery).

Semantic drift within English. The drift is the story of a birth-word becoming a class-word. In Middle English the emphasis was on lineage and courtesy — “gentle manners” as the mark of gentle blood, a courtesy that “gentle” still conveys. In the early modern period the emphasis shifted to estate and office: the gentry were the governing class of the shires, holding land and exercising the commissions of the peace; the word became political as well as social. By the eighteenth century “the gentry” and “the clergy” (or “the priesthood”) were the two conservative pillars of the old regime that Macaulay names in his opening sentence — the class whose loyalty determined the fate of the Stuarts. The modern sociological and critical uses continue the drift: gentrification (20th century) turns the respectable improving class of the nineteenth century into the middle-class colonizers of working-class neighbourhoods, the old ideal of gentility now viewed with suspicion.

Macaulay's gentry. In the History of England the word is used with full corporate force. The gentry are not individuals but an estate of the realm; their loyalty is treated as a national resource, their alienation as a catastrophe for the Crown. The sentence's pairing — gentry and priesthood — echoes the ancient model of the two swords, the temporal and the spiritual powers of the old regime, both of which James II managed to drive into opposition. The word carried, and still carries, the full weight of that history: birth, land, local power, Parliament, and Protestant loyalty.

  • PIE: *Ƿenh₁- “to beget, give birth to” (Sanskrit jánati, Greek gígnomai, Latin gignere) — one of the most widespread roots of the family.
  • Latin: gens “clan, family, people” → gentilis “of the family, noble; (church) of the nations” — the point where class and religion split.
  • Old French: gentil “noble, courteous” → genterie “gentle birth, nobility” (12th–13th c.).
  • Middle English: gentrie, genterie “gentle birth, the class of gentle-born people” (14th c.).
  • Modern English: gentry “the untitled landed class below the nobility” (16th c. onward) → gentrify, gentrification (20th c.).
  • Sister offshoot: Latin gentilis “of the nations” → English Gentile “the non-Jew” (via the Vulgate).
