Cinders are the partially burned remnants of coal, wood, or bone: hard, brittle, blackened fragments, heavier and coarser than fine ash, which may still glow dully while hot but are far past flame — the dead residue of a fire that has done its worst. In the goblins' roasting-song — “till hair smells and skins crack, fat melts, and bones black in cinders lie beneath the sky!” — the word is the final, grimmest stop in the song's inventory of destruction. The dwarves' bones do not merely burn to dust; they lie among the charred waste of the great fire, blackened and half-consumed, still recognizably what they once were. Because cinders suggests a fire so thorough that its own refuse becomes the only resting-place left, the line becomes an image of total annihilation dressed as kitchen-song — the incantatory rhythm and the hard, clinking consonants (c…n…d…r…s) making the fragments feel present in the mouth as well as on the ground. The plural is idiomatic and emphatic: English speaks of cinders as a mass, rarely of a single cinder, and that collectivity suits a song sung over an entire fallen people.

1. The core sense and its boundary.

The core meaning is precise and occupies the exact middle of the fire's debris-field. Ashes are the fine, powdery, fully mineralized residue of complete combustion — the grey-white dust left when nothing more can burn. Embers are pieces of coal or wood still alight, glowing and able to rekindle a fire; they belong to the fire's life, not its death. Cinders sit between the two: no longer flaming, no longer able to rekindle, but not yet reduced to dust — hard, brittle, still recognizable fragments, often still glowing dully, that can fly into the eye, scar the skin, and linger overnight in the hearth. The word thus carries both senses at once: the last warmth and the final waste.

2. Secondary and technical senses.

  • Metallurgy and the forge (the oldest sense): originally sinder named the fused, stony dross or scoria thrown off in iron-smelting and hammering — industrial waste of the smithy, the crust of metal-working, value-neutral in its early use.
  • The hearth and household fire: from the workshop the word settled onto the partially burned, still glowing fragments left in fireplaces and stoves — the modern everyday sense, coarser than ash, harder than ember.
  • The railway and steam age: 'cinders' flew from locomotive fireboxes — flying sparks and burnt fragments stinging the eye — and the word passed into the plural idioms of a whole era: a cinder in one's eye, a cinder track (the ash-cinder running surface of early athletics grounds), and the cinder block, the building material made by mixing ash and clinker with cement.
  • Volcanology and geology: a cinder cone is a steep conical hill of loose volcanic fragments (lapilli and scoria) built around a vent — the word applied, by analogy, to the coarse burnt-looking debris of eruptions.
  • Botany (technical, rare): in some old gardening and forestry texts, cinder names the burnt or scorched state of plants or the sooty deposit left by fires.
  • Figurative and literary use: 'to be reduced to cinders' is the idiom of complete, emphatic destruction — total annihilation leaving only brittle waste — stronger and more final than 'to be burnt to ashes', because ashes may be clean while cinders remain ugly, hard, and half-alive.

3. Grammatical character.

The plural form cinders is itself meaningful. Though the singular cinder exists (one glowing fragment), English overwhelmingly uses the plural as a mass-like collective, in the same pattern as ashes, embers, embers, remains, and dregs. The plural gathers the scattered waste into a single grim picture, and it is this collectivity that Tolkien exploits: the whole of the dwarves' burning lies 'in cinders', one shared bed of charred fragments beneath the sky. The word also feeds the folk-etymology that connects it to the fairytale name Cinderella — the little ash-girl of the hearth, so named because she was covered in cinders — a link that is real, though indirect (see the etymology notes on the French contamination of the native word).

4. Why the word fits the passage.

Cinders is the last word of the song's long catalogue of ruin, and it is chosen with exact craft. Where blaze and glaze belong to the fire's climax and the eye's reflection, and crack and smell to the body's surrender, cinders names what is left when the singing ends — the hard, brittle, half-consumed waste of the burned, lying black beneath the open sky. It is at once the most material word in the line (you could pick up a cinder) and the most final (nothing is left to pick up but refuse). The word does not describe the fire; it describes the fire's dead signature.
