- Classical Latin — 'Abundāre' denotes a river overflowing its banks (from 'ab-' + 'unda' wave), shifting metaphorically into oratorical eloquence and material wealth.
- Late Latin / Old French — Spelled with false 'h-' ('habunder') by confusion with 'habere' (to have); used in chivalric literature to denote overflowing virtue and bounty.
- c. 1300 (Middle English) — Borrowed as 'abounden'; provides a high-register Romance verb for wealth and plenty, distinct from everyday Germanic 'fill' or 'teem'.
- 16th c. (Reformation) — Etymological spelling 'abound' restored; canonized in Protestant English Bibles (Tyndale, KJV) as the premier theological verb for overflowing divine grace.
- 17th to 19th c. — Pervasive in natural history, travel literature, and poetry to describe ecosystems richly stocked with wildlife or mineral wealth.
- 20th to 21st c. — Ubiquitous in scientific, academic, and philosophical prose to describe the widespread presence of patterns, examples, or phenomena across a domain.
