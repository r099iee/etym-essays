Introduction. Fastened is the past tense and past participle of fasten, a verb that has been in English since the eighth century and that descends unbroken from the Germanic adjective fast "firm, solid, secure". Where pierce is a Romance newcomer, fasten is native English stock: its chain runs from the PIE root *past- "firm, solid", through Proto-Germanic *fastuz "firm, fixed", through the denominative verb *fastinōną "to make firm", into Old English fæstnian, and so into every later stage of the language. The word's entire history is a meditation on firmness: what is fastened is held, bound, secured, immovable — and from that physical core the word has reached into navigation, fashion, safety, accusation, and even the psychology of attention.

Indo-European and Germanic foundations. The PIE root *past- "firm, solid" is a small and poorly attested root, reconstructed chiefly on the strength of the Germanic family (Sanskrit pastyàm "dwelling, abode" is sometimes adduced, though the connection is debated). What is certain is Proto-Germanic *fastuz "firm, solid, fixed", which produced Old English fæst "firmly fixed, steadfast, secure" (modern fast), Old Norse fastr, Old High German festi (modern German fest "firm, solid"), and Gothic fastan "to keep, to observe". From the adjective, Germanic formed a denominative verb *fastinōną "to make firm, to secure", which became Old English fæstnian, attested from around 725 in glosses and from the ninth and tenth centuries in prose: to make firm, to fix, to bind, to secure, to confirm. The verb and adjective grew up together: "to hold fast" is literally "to hold firmly", and "to fasten" is "to make fast".

Old and Middle English. In Old English, fæstnian already carried the full concrete range: to bind (a prisoner), to fix (a post in the ground), to secure (a door), to ratify (an agreement — "to fasten a compact"), and even to build and establish. The famous Anglo-Saxon poem Beowulf uses the related adjective of the firm-founded hall and the fastened war-gear. Middle English inherited the verb as fastnen and festnen (the e spelling reflecting dialect variation), attested from about 1200, with the same senses: to attach, to bind, to fix, to make secure. Crucially, the phrasal sense "to fasten on/upon" — "to seize upon, to attach oneself to" — is already alive in Middle English: a hound fastens on its quarry, a man fastens on an argument, blame is fastened on the guilty. This is the very sense that surfaces in Tolkien's image of bats that "fastened vampire-like on the stricken": a predatory, clinging attachment, eight centuries old in English.

Early Modern English: from clothes to ships to minds. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw fasten applied across the whole material and mental world. Clothes were fastened with buttons, laces, and hooks; doors and windows were fastened against intruders; ships were "made fast" to the dock — the nautical "make fast" (secure a rope) entering the language of the sea, where it remains today. The figurative senses multiplied: "to fasten one's eyes on", "to fasten one's affections on", "to fasten a quarrel or a debt on someone" — to fix attention, love, or blame upon a person as firmly as a rope is fixed to a post. Shakespeare uses the word in both registers, and the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) gives it scriptural depth: "fasten" appears for pitching, binding, and fixing, as when a soldier's spear is fastened in the ground.

Modern and contemporary English. The industrial age multiplied fastenings: screws, rivets, clasps, and, from the late nineteenth century, the zip fastener — the word fastener becoming a technical noun for any device that joins. The twentieth century gave the verb its most global formula: "fasten your seatbelt", the safety instruction of every aircraft and automobile, heard in every language of the world, and now so fixed in the phrase that fasten is probably most often spoken by flight attendants and printed on safety signs. In contemporary English, fastened is a neutral, everyday word — the gate is fastened, the dress fastens at the back, the rope is fastened to the ring — while its figurative uses keep their grip: fastened attention, fastened blame, fastened hope. Its thirteen-century unbroken descent from fæstnian makes it a living monument of English's Germanic core: a word for making firm that has itself remained, across the centuries, remarkably firm.
