rustling:
- 14th c. - 'to rustle' appears in Middle English as an imitative verb for a soft, dry, whispering sound: the agitation of many small surfaces moving together - leaves, silk, papers
- 16th-19th c. - the sense holds steady and widens: rustling becomes the standard word for the stealthy, dry friction of cloth and foliage, quietly suggesting approach and concealment
- 19th c. - a separate American sense branches off: 'to rustle' (from a dialectal word meaning 'to hustle, to drive') comes to mean stealing cattle, and 'rustling' gains its outlaw echo
- Contemporary - the word keeps both lives: the soft sibilant sound of many small surfaces, still charged with quiet and stealth, and the cattle-stealing verb; for the sound, no synonym is drier or more secretive

rattling:
- 14th c. - 'to rattle' enters Middle English as an imitative verb for a rapid series of short, sharp, irregular sounds: things shaken, knocked, or dragged colliding
- 16th-18th c. - the word branches into speech and nerves: to 'rattle on' is to talk rapidly and endlessly, and to 'rattle' someone is to discompose them; the physical clatter becomes the figure for agitation
- 19th c. - the colloquial intensifier appears - 'a rattling good yarn' - while 'the death rattle' fixes the word at the grim edge of the language
- Contemporary - the neutral, expressive verb of disorder: rattling chains, the rattle of gunfire, the rattle of a snake - always a sound of things not fastened down

smacking:
- Old English - the word 'smack' first means taste and flavour, the very old word for what the mouth perceives
- 16th c. - a second, imitative 'smack' appears for the sound itself: the sharp, quick strike of lips in tasting or kissing; 'to smack one's lips' becomes the audible sign of appetite and relish
- 18th-19th c. - the senses converge: a 'smack' is a sharp blow or a loud kiss, and 'smacking' keeps its wet, percussive, strikingly physical quality
- Contemporary - smacking is the sharp, quick lip-sound of appetite made audible - which is why, in this passage, it is the most uncanny word of all, turning the ordinary sound of eating into the voice of a cave

myriad:
- 16th c. - borrowed from Greek, where it means precisely 'ten thousand': a myriad is a definite, countable number, the full strength of a Greek regiment
- 17th-19th c. - the number dissolves into hyperbole: myriad becomes a poetic figure for any number too great to count, and works as both noun and adjective
- 20th c. - the word finds a home in journalism and literary prose - 'a myriad of possibilities', 'myriad ways' - always formal, always suggesting scale beyond enumeration
- Contemporary - the word for the overwhelming count: not one mouth but a countless, swarming multitude, a horror that can only be felt because it cannot be numbered
