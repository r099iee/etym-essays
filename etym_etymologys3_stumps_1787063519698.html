The history of stumps reaches back through the Germanic languages to the shared vocabulary of tree-cutting and truncation that the Germanic peoples carried with them. The word first appears in English in the early fourteenth century as stumpe, the truncated portion of a tree — the base left standing after felling. It is a borrowing from the continental Low German world: Middle Low German stump stump, blunt end and Middle Dutch stomp stump, blunt are the most likely immediate sources, and some accounts also compare Old Norse stumpr, the Scandinavian word for a stump. All these forms descend from the Proto-Germanic adjective-noun *stumpaz stump, truncated, blunt, which English, Low German, Dutch, and Norse each inherited in their own branch. The deeper ancestor is a Proto-Indo-European root *steb(h)-, a root meaning post, trunk, to support firmly, which also gave Latin stipes a stake, post, trunk, stipula stalk, straw, and English's own stub and step. The fundamental idea is of a thick, short, upright piece of wood — a post or truncated trunk — and the word family from the beginning was rooted in the forest and the timber trade.

The borrowing into Middle English shows the economic world of the age. In the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, English woodland was managed for timber, and the language of felling was rich in Scandinavian and Low German loans; stump joined trunk, log, and block in the vocabulary of the woodcutter. From the start, however, the word's meaning was broader than the tree. A stump was any short, blunt, projecting end — of a limb, a tooth, a candle, a horn, a tail — and this general sense, a truncated remnant of anything long, has remained constant for six hundred years. The plural stumps, as a collective of such remains, is as old as the singular and is the natural form for describing a cleared or devastated landscape, which is why Tolkien's image of broken and blackened stumps works so powerfully: the plural itself names a ruined forest by its severed bases.

In the early modern period the word began to generate verbs. To stump originally meant to reduce to a stump or to stub out; a related dialectal sense, to walk heavily and clumsily, preserves the stumpy shape in motion. In the seventeenth century the word acquired its most famous fixed domain: cricket. The wicket — the target defended by the batsman — is made of three upright stumps, and by the mid-seventeenth century the word was a technical term of the game. To stump a batsman (to dismiss him by breaking the wicket with the ball in hand) and the adjectival stumped (caught out, brought to a standstill) both grew from the cricket pitch, and from cricket the sense of being suddenly checked spread into general slang. By 1811 I'm stumped meant I am baffled, at a loss, an idiom now universal, though few who use it think of a wicketkeeper lifting the bails.

The early nineteenth century produced the word's most colourful American development. In the 1820s, frontier politics discovered the stump: orators climbed onto the flat top of a felled tree to address open-air crowds, and the stump became the improvised platform of American democracy. A stump speech was a campaign address, to stump the state was to campaign across a region, and to be on the stump was to be actively electioneering. The usage spread with American politics and remains standard in political journalism on both sides of the Atlantic — a remarkable afterlife for a word whose oldest meaning is a severed tree base.

Modern English keeps the full inheritance. In forestry and everyday speech, stumps are the truncated bases of felled or destroyed trees — the blackened stumps of a burned forest, the stumps of a cleared field. In cricket, the stumps are the wicket posts, and stumps alone can mean the close of play. In informal speech, stumped is the universal word for bafflement, and up a stump survives as a dialectal idiom for being in a fix. The word has never left its original ground: every one of its senses, from cricket wicket to political platform to intellectual perplexity, still carries the image of the short blunt remnant that stops progress dead — a severed tree that still stands, saying what once was there.
