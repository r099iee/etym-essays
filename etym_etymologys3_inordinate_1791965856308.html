The history of inordinate begins with a Latin noun for a row. Ordō (genitive ordinis) means a line, a row, a rank, a class, and eventually “order” in the abstract. Its oldest concrete sense is thought to be the row of threads stretched on a loom, which is why the related verb ordior means both “to begin” and “to lay the warp”. From the loom the word extends to any arranged series: rows of seats, ranks of soldiers, the senatorial and equestrian orders, the order of a speech. The etymology beyond Latin is contested. The traditional account connects ordō with PIE *h₂er- “to fit together”, the root behind Latin ars, artus and arma; other handbooks treat the derivation as unproven. What is certain is the internal Latin development, not the prehistoric root.

From arrangement to transgression

Ordināre means to set in rows, to arrange, to appoint; ordinatus is the thing so arranged, and inordinatus is the negative — unarranged, disorderly, without due order. In classical and later Latin the word is used of troops without formation, of speech without arrangement, and, increasingly, of desires without control. Christian Latin gave this last use its full moral weight. Augustine’s idea of an ordo amoris, a right ordering of loves, and the scholastic vocabulary of inordinatio — the disordered appetite that loves what should not be loved, or loves it too much — supplied exactly the conceptual slot that English inordinate would fill.

English reception

English borrowed the word directly from Latin in the late fourteenth century, without a French intermediary, as inordinat. Its earliest sense is literal: irregular, disorderly, not governed by rule. The quantitative sense, “immoderate, excessive”, appears in the sixteenth century, carried by religious prose in which an inordinate love is one that exceeds due measure. The Authorised Version of 1611 fixed the phrase “inordinate affection” in Colossians 3:5, and generations of sermons and devotional books repeated it, so that the word acquired a specifically moral and slightly archaic flavour.

  • Semantic shift. The path is not from “many” to “bad” but from “out of order” to “out of measure”: once order is understood as a hierarchy of due proportions, any breach of order becomes an excess.
  • Loss of the literal sense. By the eighteenth century, “inordinate” as “disorderly” is archaic; the word survives almost entirely in the sense of excess, though legal phrases such as “inordinate delay” retain a trace of the older idea of proceedings out of their proper course.
  • Register. The word never became colloquial. It belongs to the written vocabulary of moral criticism, politics, law and belles-lettres, and its formality is part of its meaning: it signals a considered judgement rather than an outburst.

Modern life and afterlife

In contemporary English the adjective is comparatively rare, but the adverb is not: inordinately is a workhorse of journalism and conversation. The idiom “an inordinate fondness for beetles”, attributed to the biologist J. B. S. Haldane, has given the word a humorous afterlife, and “inordinate delay” remains a fixed phrase in legal writing. Meanwhile the morphological family keeps the original metaphor visible: order, ordain, ordinance, ordinary, ordinate and subordinate all descend from the same Latin row. To call something inordinate is still, beneath the modern sense of “too much”, to say that it has fallen out of line.

Note on the root

Because the derivation of Latin ordō from PIE *h₂er- is traditional rather than demonstrated, the chain above marks the earliest stage as reconstructed and debated. The Latin stages, by contrast, are securely attested, and the semantic development from “disorderly” to “excessive” is documented in English usage from the sixteenth century onward.
