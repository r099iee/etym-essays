Turnpike-roads, the plural of turnpike-road, are in the primary historical sense roads maintained by tolls collected at barred gates: a traveller paid at the turnpike and the spiked barrier turned aside to let horse, cart, or carriage through, the money going to mend the ruts, gravel the surface, and line the ditches. From the 1660s to the railway age they were the arteries of inland England — some twenty thousand miles of them by 1830 — and the turnpike trust, the body of local gentlemen empowered to levy the toll, was the Georgian answer to the ministry of transport.

In the source sentence Cobbett waves them aside with the inns: his object was not to see inns and turnpike-roads but to see the country — the farmers at home and the labourers in the fields — and for that one must go on foot or on horseback. The turnpike-road stands here for the smooth, official, toll-paying England of coaches and timetables, the England that hurries past the cottage door without looking in; Cobbett quits the metalled highway for the lane and the down.

Core meaning in detail: A turnpike proper was the gate itself: a frame of sharp pikes that revolved — turned — on a post, cousin to the military cheval-de-frise. By extension the whole tolled stretch took the name, distinguished from the free parish lanes by its trustees, its mileposts, its weighing engines, and its tariff board of tolls for horses, cattle, and carriages. To turnpike a road, as a verb, was to place it under such a trust.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Tolled highways of the trust era (primary historical sense, 1663–1895): the Bath road, the Great North Road, and a thousand lesser stretches. The bridge is the gate that names the system: the turning pike becomes the road that pays for itself, mile by mile, penny by penny.
  • The gate or barrier itself (original sense, 15th c.): a revolving frame of spikes set across a road or passage. The bridge is literal composition: turn plus pike — the spiked bar that turns — before the gate lent its name to the entire highway.
  • American toll motorways (living modern sense): the Pennsylvania Turnpike, the New Jersey Turnpike — high-speed limited-access highways financed by tolls. The bridge is institutional memory carried across the Atlantic: the colonies that tore down royal toll-gates rebuilt the name for the motor age.
  • Any toll road, formal or figurative (generalised sense): as in a modern turnpike tab on a satnav or a contractual turnpike clause. The bridge is function over form: wherever passage is sold by the journey, the old gate-word returns.
  • Barrier and delay in metaphor (figurative): parliamentary turnpikes, bureaucratic turnpikes on the road to reform. The bridge runs from the physical gate that stops the traveller to any checkpoint that stops a cause — the toll being time, money, or patience.

Register and connotation: turnpike-roads with its hyphen is Cobbett's own Georgian spelling, redolent of coaches, ostlers, and tariff boards; the modern American turnpike is brisk and infrastructural — green signs, E-ZPass lanes, service plazas. In Britain the word carries a double memory: improvement and extortion together, for the trusts mended the roads and milked the traveller in the same motion, and the Rebecca rioters of Wales smashed the gates in 1843. Cobbett's scorn fixes the tone: the turnpike-road is the England you pay to pass through, set against the England you stop to know.
