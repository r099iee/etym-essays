The history of vanity presents a quintessential study in semantic drift: how an abstract Indo-European root denoting physical emptiness evolved through medieval Christian theology into a moral vice, a celebrated literary motif, and ultimately a piece of household bathroom furniture.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Latin Foundations:

  • The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *h₁weh₂- (often cited as *wā- or *eue-), signifying "to leave, abandon, give up, or become empty." This root is also the source of English wane, want, waste, and Latin vacuus / vastus.
  • In Proto-Italic, the suffixed adjective *wānos gave rise to Classical Latin vānus, meaning "empty, void, hollow, devoid of truth or substance, fruitless."
  • By appending the abstract noun suffix -tās (forming qualities or states), Latin created vānitās ("emptiness, nothingness, worthlessness, delusion"). In classical usage, Cicero and Sallust used vanitas to characterize groundless boasting, empty pretensions, or unreliable claims.

2. The Vulgate and Medieval Christian Theology:

  • The defining watershed for the semantic evolution of vanitas was Saint Jerome’s 4th-century Latin translation of the Hebrew Bible (the Vulgate). In rendering the Hebrew hebel (literally "vapor, breath, transient puff of wind") in the Book of Ecclesiastes, Jerome chose vanitas: "Vanitas vanitatum dixit Ecclesiastes vanitas vanitatum omnia vanitas" ("Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity").
  • Throughout the Middle Ages, vanity was understood primarily as the tragic delusion of trusting in mortal, fleeting worldly goods (beauty, power, wealth) rather than the eternal reality of God. In Old French (vanite) and Middle English (vanitee, arriving via Anglo-Norman in the 13th century), the word stood for the profound ephemerality and illusory nature of human existence.

3. From Cosmic Futility to Psychological Egotism:

  • During the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance, a psychological shift occurred. If earthly beauty and power are "empty" (vanities), then the individual who becomes excessively obsessed with their own beauty or status is succumbing to the vice of vanity. Morality plays like Everyman and allegorical works personified Vanity as a foolish character gazing adoringly into a looking-glass.
  • In 17th-century art, the vanitas still life emerged in the Netherlands as an artistic meditation on death and transience. Simultaneously, John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) introduced the celebrated allegory of "Vanity Fair"—a bustling town market representing the endless, corrupt temptations of worldly consumerism and social climbing (later brilliantly satirized in William Makepeace Thackeray’s 1848 masterpiece novel Vanity Fair).

4. Material Secularization and Everyday Objects:

  • By the mid-18th and 19th centuries, the connection between vanity and mirrors led to material transfers. A "vanity case" or "vanity table" originally described a lady's personal dressing table fitted with mirrors and cosmetic containers.
  • In 20th-century North American terminology, this shifted further into architecture and plumbing, where any bathroom cabinet housing a sink and countertop is casually termed a vanity. Concurrently, idioms like vanity press and vanity project preserve the critical core meaning: spending effort or money purely to gratify one's own ego.
