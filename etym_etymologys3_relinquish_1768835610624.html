relinquish — the biography of the leave. Relinquish is the biography of the leave. Its Indo-European ancestor is *leikʷ-, 'to leave behind, to remain', one of the most stable and fertile roots in the entire Indo-European family. In Sanskrit the root gave riṇákti, 'he leaves', with the participle riktá, 'left, empty', which survives into modern Hindi as rikt, 'empty'. In Greek it gave leípō, 'to leave', from which the language built its most elegant compound: ekleípō, 'to leave out, to fail', whence ékleipsis — the moment the sun 'leaves' the sky — which English borrowed as eclipse; and elleípō, 'to fall short', the source of ellipsis and ellipse. The root's career in Greek is itself a lesson in how 'leaving' migrates toward absence, emptiness and failure: what is left behind is empty, what fails is that which has left its duty. In Latin the same root gave linquō, 'to leave', and from it a set of compounds that dominate the Romance side of the family. The most important for the modern word is relinquō, 'to leave behind', formed by adding the prefix re-, 'back' — to relinquish is, etymologically, to leave something behind oneself. The Latin participle relictus, 'left behind', is the source of English relic, 'a thing left behind' by the past, and reliquary, the vessel that preserves it — a beautiful proof that the root's central image is always the thing abandoned, surviving its abandonment. A second compound, dēlinquō, 'to leave one's duty, to fail', produced dēlinquēns and English delinquent: the delinquent is, etymologically, the one who has left his obligation behind. Germanic worked the same root in a gentler direction. Proto-Germanic *laihwniz, 'a leaving', became Old English lǣn, 'gift, loan', and lǣnan, 'to lend' — to lend is, etymologically, to leave something with another person. Our words loan and lend are therefore distant cousins of relinquish, and the whole family is bound by the single image of something left, given over, or put aside. The direct line to the modern verb runs through the Romance vernaculars. Old French relenquir, with its inchoative -iss- stem relenquiss-, was borrowed into Middle English in the fifteenth century as relinquisshen. The -ish- of the English verb is the fossil of that inchoative ending, so that relinquish means literally 'to begin to leave behind' — a beautiful accident of transmission that gives the English word a sense of process, of the onset of separation. The verb matured in legal and military registers: one relinquishes a claim, a right, a title, a command, a post. Its semantic career is the career of deliberate, willed separation. Unlike 'drop', relinquish never happens by accident; unlike 'abandon', it implies no desertion or heedlessness; unlike 'surrender', it requires no enemy; unlike 'renounce', it involves no public declaration. It is the quiet, formal act of parting with what one holds, performed with awareness and usually with regret. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word had refined the reluctant edge that gives it its modern emotional register. In Romantic and Victorian prose, relinquish carries an ache: one relinquishes a hope, an ambition, a beloved place, a dream. It became the verb of sorrowful surrender, of the will reluctantly unbuckling its grip. Rottcodd, in Gormenghast, is the perfect negation of the word: 'he had never been known to relinquish the feather duster from his grasp.' His grip on the duster, kept through 'all his dozing', is the body's refusal to perform the act the word names — a terminal commitment in which the leaving is forever postponed, the post kept, the hold maintained through sleep. The word's whole history — from the Indo-European act of leaving, through the Roman and French formal registers, to the Victorian ache of reluctant parting — is concentrated in that single negative: never relinquished. Relinquish is the biography of the leave, and Rottcodd is the man who never wrote its final chapter.
Summary of the evolutionary path
  • Root: PIE *leikʷ- 'to leave behind, to remain' — with Sanskrit riktá 'empty' (Hindi rikt), Greek leípō (eclipse, ellipse, ellipsis), Latin linquō.
  • Latin compound: relinquō, 'to leave behind' — re- 'back' + linquō 'to leave'; relictus gives relic, reliquary.
  • Siblings: dēlinquō → delinquent (one who leaves his duty); Germanic *laihwniz → loan, lend (to leave with another).
  • Romance crossing: Old French relenquir / relenquiss- → Middle English relinquisshen (15th c.), the -ish- being the inchoative fossil.
  • Modern career: legal and military registers (claims, rights, commands) in the 16th–17th c.; reluctant, sorrowful letting go of hopes and dreams in 18th–19th c. prose.
  • Literary use: Peake's Rottcodd, who 'never relinquishes' the duster, inverts the word's meaning — the will refusing to perform the act of leaving that gives the word its life.
