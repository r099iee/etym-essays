• Oak: Similar because both are large, strong, culturally significant European timber trees often paired in folklore; differs because oak wood is much heavier and harder-grained, used for building and ship timber, whereas ash is lighter, more flexible, and favored for handles and bats.
  • Elm: Similar because both are tall deciduous hardwood trees of forest and parkland; differs because elm has a distinctly different (serrated, asymmetric) leaf and was historically water-tolerant, while ash has compound leaves and greater elasticity.
  • Beech: Similar because both are smooth-barked deciduous timber trees of the temperate north; differs because beech wood is hard and even-grained but less elastic, and beech has simple oval leaves unlike the ash's compound ones.
  • Rowan / Mountain-ash: Similar because both are named 'ash' and sometimes grow in similar habitats; differs because the rowan (Sorbus) is actually a rose-family tree with red berries, only loosely related to the true ash of the genus Fraxinus.
  • Yew: Similar because both yield tough, flexible wood used for bows; differs because yew is an evergreen conifer with toxic foliage and was the premier bow-wood, whereas ash is deciduous and used broadly for tools.
  • Maple: Similar because both are hard, smooth-barked trees valued for strong, workable timber; differs because maple is in a different family (Sapindaceae) and its wood, while hard, lacks the ash's characteristic elasticity and pale, ringed grain.
