- [Old English: an everyday word] - glēd is the ordinary Anglo-Saxon word for a burning coal or ember; every hearth, forge and hell-fire in Old English writing is full of gledes; a word of daily fire.
- [Middle English: the fireplace word] - glede/gleed names the live coal of the medieval home — the glow under the pot, the spark carried to light a new fire; common in household, devotional and literary texts.
- [15th–17th centuries: retreat before "ember"] - Steady encroachment by ember (Old English ǣmerge, reshaped with the Norse cognate eimyrja) and the spreading use of coal squeeze glede out of standard southern speech; the word survives as a northern dialect word (gleed) and in fixed literary and religious uses.
- [18th–19th centuries: the poetic revival] - Romantic and Victorian historical poets rediscover glede as an archaism of fire — old-fashioned, burnished, dramatic; it always means a live coal, never a dead ash.
- [20th–21st centuries: fantasy diction] - Fantasy and historical fiction (Tolkien's The Hobbit, 1937: "sparks and gledes") keep the word alive as a deliberate archaism; modern readers meet gledes mostly in epic prose, where they glow with the glamour of an older, more violent English.
