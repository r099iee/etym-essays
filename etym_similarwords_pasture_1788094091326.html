• Meadow: Similar because both are grass-lands for livestock — the source line pairs them deliberately. Differs because meadow is primarily mown for hay (a hay-meadow) and is the softer, more picturesque word; pasture is grazed directly and is the working agricultural term.
  • Grassland: Similar because both name grass-covered land. Differs because grassland is the neutral ecological-geographical umbrella term, covering natural prairies and savannas too; pasture is managed and animal-centred.
  • Lea / Ley: Similar because both are grass-lands. Differs because lea is an archaic-poetic word and ley is crop-science jargon for a reseeded pasture; pasture is the general working word.
  • Field: Similar because both are enclosed land. Differs because a field may grow corn, roots, or anything; pasture is specifically grazing land, though “cow field” blurs the line.
  • Paddock: Similar because both are enclosed grazing. Differs because paddock is small, fenced, often adjacent to the farmyard for close management; pasture may be vast and unfenced.
  • Range / Rangeland: Similar because both are grazing land. Differs because range is the vast, unfenced, often semi-arid grazing land of the Americas and Australia; pasture implies managed, or at least manageable, grassland.
  • Sward: Similar because both denote grass cover. Differs because sward is the turf itself, viewed as a surface (a close-cropped sward), with no grazing management implied.
  • Turf: Similar because both are grass. Differs because turf is the surface layer of roots and soil, cut for lawns or sports grounds; pasture is the standing crop of a farm.
  • Green / Common: Similar because both are shared village grass-land (the village green, common pasture). Differs because green is village recreational land and common is legal-historical shared land; pasture is neutral.
  • Pasturage: Similar because it is the same family — the right of grazing or the pastured land itself. Differs because pasturage is legal-technical and now rare; pasture is the ordinary word.
  • Herbage: Similar because both name the feed-grass itself. Differs because herbage is botanical-legal jargon for plant cover; pasture names the land-and-feed whole.
  • Grazing / Grazing land: Similar because both denote land where animals graze. Differs because grazing is the activity (in Australian and New Zealand usage, the pastured area itself), while pasture is the land in its own right.
  • Savanna / Steppe / Prairie / Veldt: Similar because all are grassland biomes where animals graze. Differs because they are climatic-geographical types — tropical, Eurasian, North American, South African — with no fencing or management implied; pasture is the managed, productive counterpart.
  • Moor / Heath: Similar because both can carry grazing sheep. Differs because they are acid, semi-wild upland or heathy vegetation, grazed thinly rather than pastured in the strict sense.
