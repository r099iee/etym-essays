- Old English neutral blowing (blawan, to breathe, blow wind) - perceived as ordinary bodily and natural action; wind blows, men blow horns.
- Middle English regular participle variation (blowen / blowed / blown) - perceived as unmarked grammar; dialects keep -ed, London favours -en.
- Seventeenth- to eighteenth-century nautical oath (blow me down, blow me tight) - perceived as hearty forecastle swearing; wind knocking a man flat becomes astonishment.
- Nineteenth-century respectable euphemism (I’m blowed) - perceived as working-class parlour-safe damn; Dickens, music-hall and servants make it ubiquitous and comic.
- Wodehouse era class marker - perceived as quaintly low or old-fashioned against Bertie’s dashed; valets, aunts and constables say blowed, gentlemen say dashed.
- Contemporary archaism - perceived as period Bertie-voice or Dickens quotation; charmingly dated, instantly signalling 1900s–1930s England.
