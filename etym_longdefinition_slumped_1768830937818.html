SLUMPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to slump, and it names a sudden, heavy, graceless giving way — of a body, a price, a career, or a structure. In the Vance passage, "the great head slumped and bumped to the floor of the cage" describes the exact instant of death, when muscle tone vanishes and the head drops like a released weight. Below is the full exegesis of every sense of the word. SECTION 1 — THE PHYSICAL AND SOMATIC SENSES To slump is to fall or sink suddenly, heavily, and limply, usually with a loss of muscular tension and posture: a person slumps into a chair, over a desk, onto the floor. The word always implies weight, limpness, and a touch of gracelessness — there is no elegant way to slump.
  • Exhaustion and defeat: "he slumped in the armchair" — the body of the exhausted worker, the defeated athlete, the man who has simply given up.
  • Grief and despair: "her shoulders slumped" — the visible sag of disappointment, the posture of surrender, the body announcing what words do not.
  • Death and unconsciousness: "the head slumped and bumped" — the limp, unsteered fall of the dead or unconscious body; this is the sense at the heart of the Vance scene, and the word's sudden heaviness is exactly the physical fact of death: all tension released at once, the head dropping to the floor.
  • Posture, not a fall: "slumped" also describes a settled sagging position — sitting slumped over a desk — the body that has given way to gravity while still upright enough to hold a seat.
SECTION 2 — THE MATERIAL AND STRUCTURAL SENSES From bodies, the word extends to things that give way suddenly and heavily.
  • Structures: a building or roof slumps — sinks, subsides, or collapses heavily into ruin; a heap of snow slumps under its own weight.
  • Ground and mass: earth or a snowbank that slumps slides down or sinks suddenly — the "slumping" of a hillside after rain.
  • Matter: soft materials slump when they lose their set — a cake that slumps, a pile of mud that slumps outward. The shared image is mass surrendering to gravity.
SECTION 3 — THE FIGURATIVE AND ECONOMIC SENSES The most famous modern use of slump is economic: a sudden heavy fall.
  • Prices and markets: "prices slumped," "the market slumped" — a sudden, sharp drop in value; an economic slump is a depression or recession, a period when the whole economy gives way. The word entered this sense in the 19th century and was cemented by the Great Depression of the 1930s.
  • Performance: a batting slump, a scoring slump — a period when an athlete's performance falls far below normal; the image is a sudden, unexplained loss of form, as if the player's ability had collapsed.
  • Mood and spirit: morale slumps, confidence slumps — the heaviness of the body transferred to the inner life, a sag of the spirit.
SECTION 4 — ETYMOLOGY AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE The verb "slump" is first recorded in English in the 17th century as a dialectal word meaning "to fall or sink heavily, to drop into mud or slush." Its origin is uncertain but clearly sound-symbolic: the word's shape — the sl- cluster shared with slip, slop, slush, slosh, slug — mimics a soft, heavy mass meeting something wet, and the final -ump mimics a dull, heavy impact (compare bump, thump, lump, hump). The bridge from "sink into mud" to "a body collapses" and from that to "the market collapses" is the constant idea of a sudden, heavy, graceless fall: whether a body, a price, or a building, what slumps loses its support and drops.
