1. The Indo-European inheritance. The verb gird descends from a reconstructed Indo-European root customarily given as *gherdh-, "to encircle, enclose, gird", an extended grade of the broader root *gher-, "to enclose, fence in", from which English also derives yard (an enclosed space), garden, and, by different extensions, forms in many branches. The Germanic reflexes are solid and early: Gothic gairdan "to gird", Old Norse gyrða, Old Saxon gurdian, Old High German gurtan, and Old English gyrdan. The consistency of the Germanic set strongly supports an inherited Proto-Germanic verb *gurdan even though, as with many roots of this shape, the precise pre-Germanic form (the -dh- extension in particular) is debated among specialists; where the root's vocalism and extension are concerned, scholarship speaks of reconstruction, not of attested forms.

2. Old English. Old English gyrdan (attested from the eighth century) is a strong-weak verb meaning "to gird, encircle, surround, invest", and from it derives the noun gyrdel, "girdle, belt", ancestor of modern girdle. In Anglo-Saxon culture the belt was a fundamental item of male costume — it carried the knife and purse, tightened the tunic for work — so gyrdan was a homely, everyday verb, in no way elevated. The same root-family produced gyrd "rod, yard, wand" (a measuring rod), which later merged confusingly with the homonym yard from *gher-.

3. Middle English sound changes. By early Middle English the verb appears as girden/gurden/gerden, with the Old English fronted vowel of gyrdan settling as /i/ before -r- plus consonant (a regular Middle English development seen also in bird from Old English brid). The semantic field widened: one girded armour, horses (technical "to girth" or harness), swords, and, increasingly, one's own mind. The irregular preterite girt (rather than girded) stabilised in this period as the standard form in verse, from Chaucer through the Elizabethans, before the regularised girded returned to dominance in prose.

4. The biblical turning point. The decisive event in the word's English history is the scriptural idiom "gird up thy loins". Tyndale's New Testament (1526) and the Authorized Version (1611) render the Hebrew and Greek idiom literally, and the phrase entered English with immense authority: "gird up now thy loins like a man" (Job 38:3), "Let your loins be girded about" (Luke 12:35), "having your loins girt about with truth" (Ephesians 6:14), "with your loins girded" (Exodus 12:11). The image — tucking the long robe into the belt to free the legs — became the standard figure for readiness, discipline, and the assumption of a task before God or man. From the Reformation pulpits the idiom spread into secular rhetoric, so that by the seventeenth century "girding one's loins" could be used without any scriptural allusion and yet carry a Scriptural gravity.

5. Military and ceremonial senses. Parallel to the biblical line runs the martial one: "to gird on the sword" (to belt a weapon to oneself) and, in the rituals of knighthood, the "girding" of the new knight with sword and belt — a solemn act of investiture in which the belt is the mark of the order. This ceremonial sense kept the verb alive in high style long after the everyday garment-belt had vanished: epic and Victorian verse — Tennyson's "gird on the sword" diction, pre-Raphaelite romances — leaned on it, and it survives today in formulaic phrases ("to gird oneself for battle").

6. Technical offshoots. The verb also generated concrete nouns that kept the root productive in plain speech. Girdle (Old English gyrdel) stayed in common use for a belt, then for a woman's foundation garment (mid-twentieth century), and in anatomy for the pelvic or shoulder girdle. Girth, from Old Norse gjǫrð, is the belly-band of a saddle and, by extension, a measure of circumference. And girder (originally "one who girds", then a binding beam of wood or iron, sixteenth century onward) carried the encircle/brace idea into bridge and building engineering, where an iron girder literally braces — girds — a structure against flexure.

7. The -ing form and the modern register. The specific form girding is attested throughout this history as participle and gerund. Its modern value is archaising and rhetorical: newspapers speak of a ministry "girding for the election", writers of "the austere girding of the loins in youth". A word that was once the humblest of household actions has come to signify, in its surviving uses, precisely the opposite of the domestic — the heroic, the penitential, the disciplined. The trajectory of girding is thus a textbook case of semantic elevation: from belt-fastenings to spiritual and moral self-armament, carried the whole distance by the imagery of the Bible and the language of knighthood.
