Abound is an intransitive verb meaning to be present in large numbers or great quantity, to teem, or to be richly supplied with something. Abounded is its past tense and past participle: "fish abounded in the lake", "the region abounded in wildlife", "rumours abounded". The word is formal and slightly literary, but it is not archaic; it survives vigorously in the impersonal constructions that dominate modern usage — rumours abound, evidence abounds, opportunities abound — and in the prepositional pattern abound in or abound with.

Primary sense: to be plentiful. The core meaning is quantitative abundance. When something abounds, there is a great deal of it, and the quantity is sufficient to characterise the place or moment: "wild garlic abounds in the wood", "errors abounded in the first edition", "contradictions abounded". The subject is usually a mass or a category rather than a single countable thing, and the verb is typically used in the simple present or past without a progressive: "the evidence abounds", not usually "the evidence is abounding". This is one of the word's most distinctive grammatical fingerprints.

Secondary sense: to be rich in, to teem with. The prepositional construction shifts the subject from the plentiful thing to the container: a place, text, period or person abounds in or abounds with something. "The library abounds in rare manuscripts"; "the coast abounds with seabirds"; "his prose abounds in Latin tags". Here the meaning is not merely "there is a lot of X" but "X is a defining, characteristic presence". The two prepositions are close but not identical: abound in is the older and more formal pattern, often used of qualities and resources; abound with is commoner with concrete things and animals and is felt to be slightly more vivid. Both are standard.

  • Place: "the forest abounds with deer", "the region abounds in mineral wealth".
  • Text or speech: "the letter abounds in complaints", "his account abounds with detail".
  • Period or situation: "the period abounded in speculation", "the market abounds with cheap imitations".
  • Impersonal formula: "rumours abound", "doubts abound", "examples abound".

Tertiary and archaic sense: to overflow. The etymological sense, still visible in the Latin ancestor, is "to overflow" — of a river, a vessel or a liquid. English retained this literal sense in older prose ("the river abounded over the fields") and in the related noun abundance. Modern English has largely lost the physical overflow and keeps only the quantitative result: plenty rather than spilling. The ghost of the water image nevertheless survives in the word's favourite metaphors — a region "awash with" something, a market "flooded" with goods, a text that "pours out" examples.

Biblical and religious usage. Abound has a strong scriptural lineage. The Vulgate uses abundare for grace, consolation, suffering and sin, and the English Bible translators chose abound for many of these passages. The King James Version's "where sin abounded, grace did much more abound" (Romans 5:20) is one of the most quoted sentences in English religious prose, and it fixed the verb's association with moral and spiritual superabundance. The participle abounding is common in devotional writing ("abounding grace", "abounding in hope"), and the word can still carry a faintly biblical, elevated tone.

Modern journalistic and academic usage. In contemporary English the verb is a workhorse of impersonal reporting. "Rumours abounded", "speculation abounded", "evidence abounds", "examples abound", "opportunities abound" are fixed formulas of news and institutional prose. The effect is to report a general condition without naming a source: if rumours abound, nobody in particular is spreading them. This impersonal quality makes the word useful for cautious or diplomatic writing, and it also makes it slightly evasive. The past tense abounded, as on this card, typically frames a historical or narrative description: "the rock salt that abounded in their territory".

Register and connotation. Abound is formal-neutral, bookish and mildly archaic in flavour; it belongs to the same register as teem, prevail and be rife. It is usually positive or neutral when the thing is desirable (opportunities, wildlife, evidence) and negative when the thing is not (errors, rumours, contradictions). It is rarely used in casual speech; a speaker would more naturally say "there's plenty of..." or "there are loads of...". In writing it signals a slightly elevated, generalising perspective.

Grammar and forms. Abound is regular: abounds, abounded, abounding. It is intransitive and does not take a direct object; the older transitive use ("to abound something") is obsolete. The progressive is unusual, and the imperative does not occur. The noun abundance and the adjective abundant are far more frequent than the verb in everyday English, and they have largely taken over the plain "plenty" sense, leaving the verb to the impersonal and literary formulas.

Boundaries with near-synonyms. Teem implies swarming, often with animals or people, and takes with; swarm is more concrete and dynamic; proliferate stresses multiplication over time; prevail stresses dominance rather than quantity; be rife is negative and often used of undesirable things; flourish and thrive imply healthy growth rather than mere quantity; overflow and brim keep the liquid image; abound alone is the neutral, general, slightly formal word for "there is a lot of it here".
