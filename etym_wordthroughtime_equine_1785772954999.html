- Classical Latin equinus — Technical/relational "of horses" in agricultural and descriptive Latin; not an English word yet.
- Medieval–Renaissance Neo-Latin — Kept alive in scholarly veterinary and natural-history discourse across Europe.
- Late 18th-century English adoption — Enters English as a learned adjective during taxonomic and scientific professionalization; perceived as precise, classical, non-rustic.
- 19th century — Widens into racing journalism, veterinary institutional language, and literary physiognomy ("equine features"); solidifies as the default formal horse-adjective.
- 20th–21st century public perception — Strongly associated with veterinary clinics, horse welfare policy, equestrian sport science, and formal writing. Ordinary speakers still say horse problem not equine problem in casual speech; choosing equine signals expertise or elevated style. No taboo; mild risk of pretentiousness if overused in informal contexts. The exact form equine is stable in spelling and meaning.
