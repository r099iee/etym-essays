- Its Old Norse ancestor (kúra) was a calm, neutral word meaning "to lie quiet, doze, sit still" — the posture of a resting or waiting animal, with no fear in it at all.
- Borrowed into Middle English (curen/couren, c. 1300) it kept the hunter's sense: "to crouch in ambush, lie in wait" — a skilled, purposeful lowering of the body.
- By the 16th century the meaning tilted: English writers used it of crouching in dread, shrinking before a blow or a master, and the old "lying in wait" sense faded into the background.
- In the 18th-19th centuries it hardened into its modern shape: the standard word for an animal or person pressing low in terror, and a favourite of novelists for moments of panic (dogs cowering under tables, beaten servants cowering in corners).
- Today it still means crouching in fear, but it has also drifted into metaphor — nations "cower" before tyrants, players "cower" from a fast bowler — and it usually implies something weak or guilty rather than merely afraid.
