SUBDUING is the present participle and gerund of the verb SUBDUE, meaning the act of bringing something under control, into a state of obedience, or under one's power, especially by force or superior strength. As a participle it behaves adjectivally ("a subduing force"); as a gerund it names the activity or process ("the design of subduing the whole earth"). In the source passage, Gibbon's famous sentence from The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the word is used as the object of the preposition "of": Augustus is credited with relinquishing "the ambitious design of subduing the whole earth" and instead introducing "a spirit of moderation into the public councils". Here the -ing form stresses the ongoing, uncompleted process - the endless project of conquest - rather than a finished act, and that grammatical nuance is essential to the sentence's meaning. PRIMARY SENSE (conquest and control by force)
  • To bring under control by force: The central sense is military and political: to conquer a people, territory, or nation, and to keep it in subjection. "Subduing the whole earth" in Gibbon means the Roman project of bringing the inhabited world (the orbis terrarum) under Roman authority through conquest and annexation.
  • To quiet or overcome resistance: A closely related sense is to master a rebellious force, an uprising, or a hostile element: to subdue a revolt, a riot, or a dangerous animal by overpowering it.
  • To bring under the sway of reason or law: The word extends to non-military control - bringing passions, instincts, fears, or impulses under the command of reason or discipline: "to subdue one's anger", "to subdue one's passions".
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • To tame (animals): Applied to wild or dangerous animals, "subdue" means to tame, domesticate, or break them so that they obey. This is a concrete, physical extension of the same "bringing under" image.
  • To make less intense, bright, or loud: From the late seventeenth century the word gains an aesthetic-psychological sense: to soften, tone down, or muffle. "Subdued lighting", "a subdued voice", "subdued colours" all mean reduced in intensity, quieted, restrained. This sense is not about force but about gentle diminution, and it is the most common modern colloquial use.
  • To suppress or restrain (feelings, impulses): A figurative extension of the same idea: to keep an emotion, desire, or urge in check - "subduing one's curiosity". The overlap with "suppress" is close, though "subdue" retains the image of winning a victory over something.
  • In medicine and physiology (technical): The word appears in older technical prose for reducing an inflammation, an epidemic, or a bodily symptom - bringing a condition under control. Though rare today, this technical sense was common in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century medical writing.
  • In soil cultivation and older rhetoric (archaic-technical): The King James Bible uses "subdue" for the cultivation of the earth ("subdue it", Genesis 1:28), where the sense is to bring the land under human management and make it productive - an agricultural extension of the conquest image.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The word entered English from Old French "souduire", meaning "to seduce, deceive, lead astray" - a sense that today seems almost the opposite of "conquer". The bridge is supplied by the Latin ancestor "subducere" ("to draw from below, withdraw, take away"), whose "drawing away" could mean secretly removing or leading someone off course; in Old French this became "seduce, corrupt". Then, crucially, the English word was pulled in a different direction by association with the Latin verb "subigere" ("to bring under the yoke, subjugate") and "subdere" ("to put under, subject"), which were used for conquest and taming. By the fifteenth century "subdue" in English had come to mean "conquer, bring under control", and the "seduce" sense died out. The modern "soften, tone down" sense develops from the underlying image of pressing something down: what is pressed down is less intense, quieter, dimmer - hence "subdued" light and sound. GRAMMAR AND USE As a participial form, "subduing" appears in progressive constructions ("they were subduing the tribes"), as a gerund after prepositions ("the design of subduing the earth"), and as a noun naming the activity. It takes a direct object ("subduing X"). The agent can be a person, an army, a state, or abstractly reason, fear, or policy. In formal historical prose it carries elevated, even imperial weight; in everyday modern speech the participial form is rarer than the adjective "subdued", though "subduing" survives in journalism and official language for military and law-enforcement operations. CONTEXT IN THE PASSAGE Gibbon's sentence uses "subduing" to name the ambition he is about to critique: the Roman republic's "rapid succession of triumphs" was driven by the design of conquering the entire known world, and Augustus is praised precisely for relinquishing that design. The -ing form is important: the Romans did not merely conquer; they had an ongoing project of conquest that could be "relinquished", an ambition whose process is captured by the participle. The word also carries the entire semantic history outlined above - force, dominion, taming, and the quieting of resistance - which is why Gibbon can oppose "subduing the whole earth" to "a spirit of moderation".
