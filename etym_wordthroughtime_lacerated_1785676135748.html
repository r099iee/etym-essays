- [Late 16th century · violent tearing] - Borrowed from Latin lacerātus ('torn, mangled'); meant flesh torn by rough force in vivid, painful contexts — the opposite of a clean cut.
- [17th–18th century · medical and legal use] - Used in surgery and law to describe jagged, irregular wounds; clinical but still charged with the violence of the original tear.
- [18th–19th century · figurative extension] - Extends to the emotions: lacerated feelings, a heart lacerated by grief; keeps its raw, tearing force in the metaphor.
- [Modern · formal and literary] - A formal, literary register word; signals splintered, uneven tearing by a hostile surface rather than surgical incision, and still carries overtones of pain.
