Versts is the English plural of verst, an old Russian unit of itinerary distance equal to 500 sazhens, about 1.067 kilometres or two-thirds of a mile. In the source sentence the Eagle warns that it is many ______ to the Castle of Koshchei: many long Russian miles of flight lie between the hero and the deathless villain's fortress, and the plural stretches the road out before the reader like the steppe itself.

Core meaning in detail: The verst was Russia's road-mile from medieval times until the metric reform of 1924: verst-posts striped in imperial colours lined the great roads, and distances in every novel from Pushkin to Pasternak are reckoned in versts. A traveller told the journey was many versts heard what an Englishman hears in many weary miles: not a number but a sigh, the arithmetic of endurance. The English plural adds -s to the Russian stem, making versts a fully naturalised traveller's word.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Russian versts of distance (primary sense, from the 16th c. in English travel writing): ten versts to the village, many versts to Koshchei's castle. The bridge is straight borrowing: English travellers, soldiers, and translators needed the local mile and took it whole, striped posts and all.
  • The measure as menace (literary sense, as in the source): in fairy tales the counted versts are the counted dangers, each one a little further from home and help. The bridge is the journey's arithmetic of dread: distance becomes difficulty, and many versts promises ordeals as surely as many-headed promises monsters.
  • Colloquial exaggeration (figurative): versts of red tape, versts behind the times, in writers showing off a Russian colour. The bridge carries the unit off the road onto any long tedious expanse: versts of paperwork, versts of silence, versts of snow.
  • Boundary and frontier versts (historical-administrative sense): the mezhevaya versta (boundary verst, 1,000 sazhens) measured land, not roads. The bridge is the surveyor's double use: one word served the traveller's day and the landlord's acre, distinguished only by which sazhen-count the clerk intended.
  • Verst-posts as landmarks (metonymic sense): beyond the third verst can mean beyond the third verst-post from town. The bridge is the marker for the measure: striped pillars stood every verst, so the count of pillars and the count of units were one and the same to the walker.

Register and connotation: versts is exotic and bookish in English, a deliberate Russianness no travel agent would use today. It smells of snow, pine, and epic distance: translators keep it instead of converting to miles precisely to keep the sleigh-bells ringing. To write versts is to promise the reader Russia, vastness, and a long road with wonders and horrors spaced along it.
