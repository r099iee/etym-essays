Slew is the irregular past tense of the verb slay: “killed,” almost always violently — killed in battle, killed with a weapon, killed deliberately. In “whose father you slew in Moria,” the word is high-style epic diction: Gandalf reminds Dáin that he killed Azog, Bolg’s father, years earlier in the mines of Moria. The register is scriptural-heroic: slew is the past tense the King James Bible, the sagas in translation, and fantasy prose use for killing by blade or hand.

Grammar and form.

  • Slew belongs to the strong conjugation (slay – slew – slain), the Germanic vowel-change pattern now reserved for archaic, literary verbs; most other English verbs take the weak -ed ending. “Slayed,” now heard in slang, is a 20th-century weak-form innovation that standard prose still rejects for the killing sense.
  • It takes a direct object (he slew the dragon), and its participle slain is used in passive constructions (“many were slain”).
  • The homonymous noun slew (“a large number”) and verb slew/slue (“to turn or swing round”) are separate words (see below) — the past-tense killing sense is always clear from the object and the verb’s position.

Semantic range of slew (the killing verb).

  • Battle and heroic killing: the dominant sense — warrior kills warrior, champion kills monster; includes killing en masse: “he slew the whole company.”
  • Sacrificial and punitive killing: in biblical prose slew covers sacrifice, executions, and divine slaughter; the weight falls on the act’s finality.
  • Animals and hunting: beasts slew and were slain; 19th-century big-game prose retains slay for beasts of the chase.
  • Figurative extension: poets may “slay” time, hopes, or pain — killing as metaphor for destroying or ending utterly (“the frost slew the blossoms”).

The two homographs that collide with slew.

  • slew, noun — “a large number”: “a slew of problems” — a 19th-century Americanism from Irish sluagh “host, multitude,” now a common informal quantifier in US English. Same spelling and pronunciation as the past tense; entirely different source and grammar.
  • slew/slue, verb — “to turn or swing around”: an 18th-century nautical word for swinging a spar or vessel (“slew the boom”), of obscure origin, earlier spelled slue — the source has not been established.
  • All three are pronounced identically /sluː/; context alone separates “he slew the enemy” (past of slay), “a slew of offers” (multitude), and “she slewed the truck” (turned sharply).

Why slew sounds the way it sounds. The form continues the Old English strong past slōg: the strike-verb slēan had a past stem with a long vowel, which Middle English raised and rounded (slou, slow) until the Great Vowel Shift turned it into modern /uː/, spelled -ew by analogy with crew, drew, flew. Its survival while the present slay shrank to literary use is a matter of prestige: the strong form was carried by the Bible and by epic prose, and every generation that read those texts renewed the word’s solemn, archaic power. Slew is thus both one of the most archaic-looking and one of the most continuously attested past tenses in the language — an everyday Old English verb that became the ceremonial tense of heroic death.
