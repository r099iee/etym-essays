The biography of PARADING begins not with marching feet but with a hand at work. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *perə-, "to produce, to procure, to bring forward" (written *perh₁- in modern laryngeal notation) — a base that already carried the idea of bringing something into being or into readiness. The root is traditionally traced in Indo-Iranian to Sanskrit pṛṇāti, "he bestows, grants", and in Baltic to Lithuanian pir̃kti, "to buy, to procure" — a revealing shift in which "getting by one's own effort" hardened into commerce, though modern scholars treat both links with caution. Its most fertile development, however, was in Latin, where the root produced the twin verbs parere, "to bring forth, to give birth" (whence English parent, parturition, primipara), and parāre, "to make ready, to prepare, to arrange, to equip". Parāre proved one of the most productive verbs of the Latin language: apparāre gave apparatus and, through Old French, apparel, what one is "fitted out" in; praeparāre gave prepare; reparāre gave repair; and comparāre, "to procure", hardened again into Italian comprare, "to buy" — commerce blooming, as in Baltic, out of preparation.

The military turn. The decisive step for our word came in the soldiers' Latin of the late Empire. The past participle parātus, "made ready, prepared", developed a feminine form *parāta that named a military posture: a halting, a standing ready, troops drawn up and held in ordered array for inspection or review. (The form is reconstructed rather than directly attested, but the Romance descendants leave no doubt it existed.) From this martial "standing-place" the word marched out into the Romance languages in three directions, each keeping a different fragment of the sense. Spanish parada meant "a halt" and "the body of troops drawn up for review", and Spanish parar, "to stop, to stand", later furnished the English parade-ground, the physical place of assembly. Italian parata meant "a show, a display" and, in fencing, the "parry", the warding-off stroke — Italian parare, "to ward off", is the same verb applied to deflecting a blow. French parade, arriving in the sixteenth century, fused the two: it meant both a review of troops and any ostentatious display. It was the French form that English adopted.

English career. English parade is recorded from about 1656, meaning "a military review" and "a pompous public show", and shortly after, by extension, "the place where troops assemble" — the parade-ground. The word then widened in the eighteenth century to any public procession or festive spectacle staged for onlookers: a carnival parade, a victory parade, a parade of fashion. In the nineteenth century the verb completed the journey from the literal to the figurative: to parade something meant to walk it out or exhibit it ostentatiously before an audience, with proud self-regard — to parade one's wealth, one's learning, one's opinions. This is the sense the schoolroom handbooks incur in the passage from which this card is taken: they parade their lists of Greek terms and recherché metres, marching their erudition out in full ceremonial display as if on review, performing scholarship rather than quietly possessing it.

Modern life. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word settled into its modern range. A parade is still a military review or a public procession, but the dominant modern uses are figurative and often satirical: parading one's virtues, an endless parade of celebrities, "rain on someone's parade", the hit parade of popular music, the police identity parade. In fashion, parading is the modelling of clothes on a catwalk — literally walking out a product to be admired. Every one of these uses descends, through the nineteenth-century verb, the seventeenth-century English noun, and the sixteenth-century French noun, from the same Roman image: troops standing ready, drawn up in formation, prepared for display. To parade, even now, is to make oneself ready and to march it out before the world — the ancient hand of preparation and the modern vanity of the showman, joined in a single word.
