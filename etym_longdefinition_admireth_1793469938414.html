Admireth is the archaic third-person singular present tense inflection of the transitive and intransitive verb admire, characteristic of Middle and Early Modern English (equivalent to modern admires). Morphologically formed by appending the southern English verbal suffix -eth to the Latinate stem admire, the word occupies an extraordinary historical crossroads in English semantic evolution. In contemporary English, to admire means almost exclusively to regard someone or something with warm approbation, high moral esteem, or aesthetic pleasure. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, however, the word straddled two overlapping semantic worlds: the primary classical Latin meaning of admīrārī—signifying breathless wonder, marveling, astonishment, and being struck with awe—and the nascent modern sense of thoughtful, approving veneration.

In the source quotation from the Elizabethan theologian Richard Hooker (in The Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, cited by Samuel Johnson): “To the best and wisest, while they live, the world is continually a froward opposite; and a curious observer of their defects and imperfections, their virtues afterwards it as much admireth,” the verb operates with subtle double force. While living, great thinkers are harassed and dissected by their contemporaries; but once death removes their personal presence, the world stands back in stunned astonishment at the magnitude of the virtues it previously despised. The word captures both the world’s sudden, gaping awe (the Latinate sense) and its eventual, grudging moral homage (the modern sense).

Comprehensive exploration of polysemy and historical senses:

  • Regards with high esteem, respect, and moral approval (the emergent modern sense): To hold a person, character trait, achievement, or work in profound honor, feeling warm approbation for their excellence. The bridge between raw astonishment and moral esteem developed gradually as English speakers reasoned that what strikes the soul with genuine wonder is often inherently noble and worthy of emulation.
  • Marvels at, wonders at, or is struck with astonishment (the primary classical/Elizabethan sense): To be filled with deep amazement or perplexity, viewing something as an extraordinary marvel or anomaly. This sense preserves the direct meaning of Latin ad- (‘at, toward’) + mīrārī (‘to wonder’). In this sense, seventeenth-century writers could speak of admiring a horrifying monster or an earthquake without approving of it in the slightest (as Milton writes in Paradise Lost: ‘Let none admire / That riches grow in Hell’).
  • Gazes upon with aesthetic delight or visual fascination (the contemplative sense): To look upon beauty, craftsmanship, or architectural splendor with absorbed, pleasurable contemplation. The semantic bridge connects the intellectual pause of wonder with the visual delight of beholding an exquisite form.
  • Ironic or reluctant recognition (the rhetorical/pragmatic sense): In literary polemics and moral essays, the verb is frequently deployed to describe the involuntary tribute that vice or foolishness must eventually pay to genuine virtue. Hooker’s usage epitomizes this sense: the world does not want to praise the wise, yet it admireth their monumental legacy despite itself.

Morphology, dialectal register, and rhetorical weight: The suffix -eth (descended from Old English -eþ) was the standard third-person singular present inflection in southern and standard literary Middle and Early Modern English, gradually being displaced during the seventeenth century by the northern dialectal variant -(e)s. While everyday London speech adopted admires by the mid-1600s, admireth was consciously retained in elevated theological prose, philosophical treatises, and liturgical texts (such as the Book of Common Prayer and the King James Bible) to confer solemnity, rhythmic dignity, and timeless moral authority. Encountering admireth immediately establishes a stately, meditative cadence, framing human observation within the grand expanse of historical and philosophical reflection.
