HALLOOING is the present participle of the verb to halloo — the continuous, repeated act of shouting "halloo!", the loud, stylized cry used to attract attention, to hail someone from a distance, and, above all, to urge hounds forward in the chase. A single halloo is a one-off summons; hallooing is the on-going, iterated sounding of that cry, the vocal texture of a pursuit in full cry. In the passage from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit the goblins come down out of the trees "hooting and hallooing, and hunting among the trees" as they drive the hobbit before them. The word does triple duty here. It supplies pure sound — a loud, carrying cry that fills the woodland. It supplies a theatrical, almost carnivalesque tone, because hallooing is not mere shouting but the ceremonial call of the hunting field, the noise a huntsman makes when the quarry is afoot. And it supplies a sharp, deliberate irony: Tolkien has the most bestial creatures in his legendarium borrow the gentlemanly, almost courtly cry of the fox-hunt, so that the goblins' sport — a manhunt — is framed as the elegant pursuit of a gentleman's quarry. They announce themselves, relish the chase, and want the hobbit to know it is being hunted; the courtesy of the cry is itself a threat.

The core sense of the verb to halloo, attested continuously since the late 14th century and almost certainly of imitative (echoic) origin, is "to call or shout to, to hail; to urge on with shouts." The interjection halloo! is itself the raw cry: an onomatopoeic syllable of summons whose exact phonetic shape varied freely (halow, hallow, hallo, halloa, halloo, hollo, holla) as different speakers and regions stamped the same shout with different vowel colours. Out of this single expressive base grew a small family of English words — hello, the telephone-age greeting, holler, the American dialect verb of loud shouting, and the nautical holla — and hallooing is the participle of the member of that family that kept the hunt as its home ground.

  • Sense 1 — the hunting cry (the root sense): "to shout 'halloo' to urge hounds on in the chase; to raise the cry of the hunt." From its first Middle English records halow meant "to pursue with shouts, to shout in the chase," and this remains the word's definitive register: the cry that lets the hounds know the quarry is moving and tells the world that a hunt is on.
  • Sense 2 — to hail or summon: "to call out loudly to someone at a distance, to attract attention to oneself." The same raised voice that speaks to the hounds speaks to men — hallooing a person across a field, a street, or open water is an archaic but perfectly intelligible way of hailing them.
  • Sense 3 — to incite or encourage: "to egg on, to spur forward by shouts," applied by extension to people as well as hounds — to halloo a runner, a crew, or a mob. The sense preserves the primitive of the word: shouts as a tool of propulsion.
  • Sense 4 — the maritime cry: from the mid-15th century the same shout served as a shipman's call, raised to incite effort aboard ship and to hail between vessels in the absence of loud-hailers; the nautical holla and the greeting hello grow out of this branch of the family.
  • Sense 5 — the nominal "a halloo" / "halloos": the participle's base noun, a halloo, names the cry itself ("a distant halloo echoed in the valley"); the plural halloos is the sound of many voices calling at once — the aural image Tolkien uses in the plural participle.
  • Sense 6 — figurative and metaphoric: "to halloo" can name any loud, self-announcing clamour, and by metaphor it colours journalism and oratory — a headline "hallooing" a scandal is baying after it like hounds. The old proverbial "to halloo before one is out of the wood" (to celebrate before the danger is past) shows how early the cry became a figure for premature triumph.
  • Historical bridge for the secondary senses: all the later senses — hailing, inciting, nautical shouting, the telephone greeting — are the same primitive speech act, the loud summons of the raised voice, carried from the hunting field into other theatres where one voice must reach many ears. The shift is not a change of meaning so much as a widening of the cry's workplace: from covert to field, from field to ship, from ship to telephone.
  • Register and connotation: modern hallooing is deliberately archaic, ceremonial, theatrical, and faintly comical — the sound of pageantry and sport. It is not neutral loudness: it is loudness with a pedigree, and it is precisely that inherited dignity that Tolkien exploits when he puts the gentleman's cry in goblin mouths.

Why the participle, and not the verb, is the word of the sentence. The continuous form hallooing is not an arbitrary inflection. In the Tolkien line the goblins come down "hooting and hallooing, and hunting" — three present participles, three ongoing actions that overlap and interlock. The -ing form makes the noise durative, sustained, and contemporaneous with the movement: we do not hear one cry but a continuous baying that accompanies the whole descent. Hallooing therefore carries exactly the meaning the context demands — not "they shouted once" but "they kept raising the cry, cry after cry, as they hunted among the trees," and it is that unbroken, ritualized noise that makes the goblins' pursuit feel at once festive and menacing. The participle is the perfect grammatical form for a word whose whole nature is repetition.
