1. The Germanic family: a verb built on "fitting". The verb till is a Germanic verb whose deeper Indo-European pedigree is debated. Its foundation is the Proto-Germanic adjective *tilaz "fitting, suitable, good", attested across the family: Gothic ga-tils "fitting, suitable", Old Saxon til "good, fit", Old High German zil "goal, aim" (modern German Ziel "goal, target; limit"), and Old English til "good, useful, fitting". From this adjective Germanic built a class II weak verb *tilōjaną "to strive after, aim at, attain, make oneself fitted"; Gothic attests ga-tilōn "to attain, achieve", Old Saxon tilian "to obtain", Old High German zilōn "to aim, strive" — ancestors of German zielen "to aim". The semantic step is plain: "to make something fitting" became "to aim at something" and then "to work toward an end".

2. The uncertain origin of *tilaz. Whether *tilaz has an Indo-European etymology is not settled. The commonest hypothesis connects the family to the PIE root *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, set", the idea being a mark or goal that one sets for oneself (a "set target"); on this view English till, German Ziel and Gothic ga-tils all reflect a past-participial formation "set, placed, fitting". But the root derivation is contested, and many handbooks simply mark the Germanic adjective as of uncertain origin. Etymology demands honesty here: no single PIE etymon commands universal agreement, and the safest statement is that the family is Germanic with a probable but not certain Indo-European background.

3. Old English: tilian. Old English tilian (also tilgan) meant "to strive, endeavour, labour for, aim at; to gain, acquire; to provide for". It was a verb of effort in general — a man could tilian food, knowledge, or a living — but its most important application was the soil. Old English prose and poetry use tilian of cultivating land, and the participle tilod/tilad "cultivated" describes worked, productive ground. The agricultural sense triumphed because, of all labours, working the land was the most fundamental and the most universal: to tilian unqualified came to mean "to farm".

4. Middle English: tilen and the decay of the "obtain" sense. In Middle English the verb is tilen, tillen, with senses "to cultivate, plough" and "to gain, obtain, provide". Old and Middle English homilies and scripture translations keep both strands: the Wycliffe Bible (c. 1382) renders Genesis's "to till the ground". During the later Middle Ages the "obtain/provide" senses fell away — they survive only in the fossil idiom "earn one's living by the sweat of one's brow" conceptually and in dialect — leaving agriculture in sole possession of the verb.

5. The biblical register: a curse and a calling. The King James Bible (1611) fixed "till the ground" in Genesis: man is placed in Eden "to dress it and to keep it" (2:15), sent out "to till the ground from whence he was taken" (3:23), and Cain "was a tiller of the ground" (4:2). After the Fall, tilling is at once punishment — ground cursed, thorns and thistles, labour in the sweat of the face (3:17-19) — and dignity, the divinely ordered human vocation. No other word carried this double load, and "tilled" entered modern English saturated with Scripture.

6. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The word's register shifted as agriculture industrialised. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries "tilled" remained the plain, standard participle for cultivated land in farming, poetry (Wordsworth's rustic landscapes, John Clare's agrarian verse) and georgic literature; by the twentieth century everyday English increasingly used "ploughed" or "cultivated", and "till the soil" hardened into a literary-biblical idiom. The noun tillage and agent tiller continued in technical and formal use.

7. The modern profile. Today "tilled" is alive in three registers: agronomic and horticultural prose (tilled acreage, tilled soil), literary and high-register writing where it carries pastoral and scriptural resonance, and the opening sentence of Tolkien's restored-Dale passage, where "the valley had become tilled again and rich" makes cultivation the very emblem of peace reclaimed from the dragon's desolation. Phonetically /tɪld/ is a single syllable, the participle of a verb still used whenever land is broken, harrowed and made ready to bear.
