PIE *péd- "foot" (also "base, bottom, that which is lowest"; o-grade *pod-) ├── Italic: Latin pēs, pedis "foot" (attested from early Latin) │ ├── Latin pessum "to the ground, downward" (from *ped-som, "feet-first") — the adverb meaning "down, to the bottom" │ │ └── Latin pessimus "worst" (superlative of malus "bad"; from *ped-semos, literally "lowest"; the suppletive paradigm malus : peior : pessimus, "bad : worse : worst") │ │ └── Neo-Latin pessimismus "pessimism" (coined in the 18th c. on the model of optimismus, from Latin optimus "best"; pessimus = the "worst") │ │ ├── French pessimisme "pessimism" (1759, coined as the philosophical antonym of optimisme) │ │ └── English pessimism (1794, borrowed from French; the doctrine that the world is the worst possible, later the temperament of expecting the worst) NOTE (debate): the derivation of pessimus from *ped-semos "lowest" is the standard account (the "foot" root, cf. Latin pēs "foot"); the modern word pessimism itself is a Neo-Latin coinage built on Latin pessimus, first appearing in French in 1759 and in English in 1794. There is no continuous word "pessimism" in antiquity — the -ism noun is a product of eighteenth-century philosophical controversy.
