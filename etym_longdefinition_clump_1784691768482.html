A clump is a compact, dense cluster or mass — most commonly of trees, shrubs, or other plants growing closely together in a single group, and by extension any aggregated lump of material, as of earth, clay, or grass. The word's two lives, botanical and mineral, meet in the idea of cohesion: a clump is things stuck together in a tight, irregular mass, without the neatness of an arrangement or the size of a wood. As a verb it means to form such a mass, and, in a separate development, to walk with a heavy, thudding tread, the noise of which — a clump — mirrors the weight of a lump of earth. In landscape description the word is deliberately modest: a clump is smaller, denser, and more incidental than a grove or a wood, a chance gathering of growth rather than a deliberate planting.

Polysemy: the full semantic range.

  • Botanical sense (the primary sense): a small, dense group or cluster of trees, shrubs, bushes, or herbaceous plants growing closely together from a common base — "a clump of oaks", "a clump of daffodils"; a dense, accidental knot of growth rather than an ordered plantation.
  • Mineral / material sense: a compact, coherent lump or mass of earth, clay, soil, or any aggregated material — "a clump of dirt", "a clump of grass"; the word overlaps with clod and clot in this material sense.
  • Verb of aggregation: to form or gather into a clump, or to place things close together into a dense mass — "the seedlings clumped together"; the participial adjective "clumped" describes grouped or clustered distribution.
  • Verb and noun of heavy movement: to walk or move with a heavy, dull, thudding tread, and the sound so made — "the clump of heavy boots on stairs"; this sense is echoic, the noise of a mass of weight striking the ground.
  • Figurative and technical extensions: in astronomy, a clump of galaxies or of stars; in statistics and spatial science, a "clumped" distribution (aggregated rather than uniform or random); in geology and soil science, a coherent mass of soil; in horticulture, a "clumping" habit of growth (many stems rising from one base).

The sense in the context. In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the word names a small practical refuge. Soaked by a day of rain, the company "moved to a clump of trees, and though it was drier under them, the wind shook the rain off the leaves, and the drip, drip, was most annoying." The clump is exactly the right size of shelter — enough trees to break the downpour, standing together as a dense island of canopy on the open ground, but not enough to keep out the wind's mischief: each gust sends the collected water rattling down from leaf to leaf, and the company trades one misery for another. The word's denseness is felt in the scene: these are trees grown close together, their boughs overlapping, offering a partial, leaky roof to travellers who have no better one. It is a homely, exact, slightly comic word, and it anchors the scene in the concrete detail of a night spent under trees in a storm.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. A cluster emphasises arrangement — things grouped, though not necessarily touching; a thicket is dense, tangled undergrowth, too wild to shelter under comfortably; a copse or coppice is a managed, deliberately cut wood; a grove is a larger, more orderly group of trees; a tussock is specifically a clump of grass; a clod is a lump of earth. Clump keeps its dense, accidental, massed character among all of them.
