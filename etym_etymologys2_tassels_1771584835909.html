Chain for TASSELS (origin obscure; NO PIE root can be established — the word enters English through Old French, and its deeper origin is uncertain). Variable stages = 5. 1. Proposed Semitic base *tistala “a knotted hanging fringe, a tassel” (conjectural) — a Mediterranean textile term belonging to the Semitic vocabulary of knotting, plaiting, and tying; not directly attested, and its reality is uncertain. 2. → Old French tassel “tassel, fringe, hem; a fastening, a clasp” (12th c.) — attested in both the ornament sense and the fastening sense; its ultimate origin is obscure. 3. → Middle English tassel “a clasp, a mantle-fastener” (c. 1300) — the fastening sense arrives first in English. 4. → Middle English tassel “a pendant ornament, a hanging bunch of cords” (late 14th c.) — the ornament sense settles; Medieval Latin tassellus is used in the same period as an ecclesiastical and textile term. 5. → English tassel (noun) → English tassels (plural; the card's form) — Final node: English TASSELS (rootWord), the ornament sense, 14th century to present; the botany sense (the tassel of a maize plant) dates from the 18th–19th c. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The origin of Old French tassel is unknown; the proposed Semitic *tistala is conjectural and cannot be confirmed. (2) The rival account derives tassel from Latin taxillus, the diminutive of talus “ankle, knucklebone”, meaning “a small die or cube”, on the theory that a tassel resembles a little die; the OED (1989) judges this doubtful. (3) The OED instead notes the English variant “tossel” and an association with the verb toss, a nod to the tassel's swaying motion; this is a folk-association, not a derivation. (4) No PIE ancestry exists for this word; it belongs to the Mediterranean textile world, not to the Indo-European family.
