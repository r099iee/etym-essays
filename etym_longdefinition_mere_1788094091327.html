Mere (noun) is a word for still water: a lake, a pool, or a broad expanse of shallow water lying in the land. In the source lines — “Past the marsh’s waving weeds, / Through the mist that riseth white / Up from mere and pool at night!” — the mere is exactly this: a sheet of quiet water among the marshes, from which night mist rises. The noun must be distinguished from the familiar adjective mere (“only, just”), a French loan meaning “pure”; the two meet only on the page. The noun mere is the old English water-word, and it carries a surprising cargo of history.

1. Core sense — a lake or a pool. A mere is a body of standing water, usually broad and shallow, often fringed by marsh and reeds — the classic water of fen country. In modern British usage the word survives as a somewhat literary or regional term for a lake: a “broad mere,” a “lonely mere.” It is blunter and less romantic than Italianate lake, plainer than poetic tarn, and it carries the note of the open water-meadows and wetlands of northern and midland England.

2. Archaic and poetic sense — the sea. In Old English, mere meant “the sea” as well as “the lake”; the sea-sense survived in poetry into the modern period as archaism, and it survives still, hidden, in compounds: mermaid is literally a “sea-maid” (Old English mere “sea” + mægden “maiden”); the old mereswīn, “sea-pig,” was the porpoise; meregrot, “sea-stone,” was the pearl. The sea and the lake are one word because to the earliest speakers both were simply “water” — the standing water of the world.

3. The wetland sense — mere and marsh. In the landscapes of the English fens, meres are the broad shallow lakes threaded among the marshes — precisely the pairing of the source line. Historically the great meres of the fen country — Whittlesey Mere, Red Mere, Ugg Mere — were drained in the nineteenth-century fenland improvements, and their names preserve a vanished watery world. A mere is thus the quintessential marshland water: wide, shallow, mist-prone, weed-fringed.

4. Place names — the great relic. The noun’s largest modern population lives in toponyms, almost all in northern England and the fen edge: Windermere (“Vinandr’s lake,” Old Norse personal name + mere), Grasmere (“grass lake”), Buttermere, Ellesmere, Marton Mere near Blackpool, Aqualate Mere in Staffordshire (among the largest natural lakes of England’s Midlands). Every -mere that ends a lake name is this noun, thousands of years old.

5. The homographs — three words called mere. English has three unrelated words spelled mere:

  • The noun here (Old English mere “sea, lake”): the water-word, from PIE *mori- “body of water.”
  • The adjective mere (“only, just,” from Old French mer “pure, unmixed” < Latin merus “pure, undiluted”): the common modern word — utterly different in origin.
  • The obsolete boundary-word mere (Old English (ge)mǣre “boundary, limit”): the word of the old phrase “metes and bounds,” used in land law (“mere-stone,” a boundary stone); obsolete save in legal-historical contexts.

Readers of older texts must decide by context which mere they face; in the source line, the pairing with pool settles it: this is the water-mere.

6. Related vocabulary. The noun heads a small watery family: mermaid; the old mereswīn (porpoise); and marsh — Old English mersc, “marsh, fen,” formed on the same root *mori- with the suffix -isk-, so that mere and marsh are kin words: a marsh is “mere-ish” land, waterlogged land. Germanic cousins — German Meer “sea,” Dutch meer “lake,” Gothic marei, Old Norse marr — match Latin mare “sea” from the same PIE *mori-, and Celtic (Irish muir, Welsh môr, both “sea”) shows the root’s full Indo-European spread.

7. The semantic bridge. One PIE idea — “body of standing water, sea or lake” — ran through nearly every branch of Indo-European; in English it split into sea-words and lake-words. Mere once meant both, then narrowed to inland water, then retreated into poetry, dialect, and place names as lake (from Latin lacus via French) took the plain register. Today the noun mere is a relic that literature keeps alive: it is the old, honest English word for the still water among the reeds — the water the mist rises from.
