Commandments are authoritative orders or precepts that must be obeyed, especially the Ten Commandments of the Bible, and by extension any set of rules stated with absolute, quasi-religious authority — moral maxims, professional codes, or schoolroom prohibitions treated as binding and non-negotiable.

The word is almost always plural and almost always carries biblical resonance. Even when the content is trivial — grammatical commandments such as never split an infinitive or never begin with And — the form borrows Sinai gravity: a short numbered list, memorized in youth, recited with thou-shalt-not force,felt as sin rather than mistake. The source sentence exploits exactly this: ordinary adults carry away from high school not a theory of discourse but a few grammatical commandments, hazily recalled, half-understood prohibitions (do not use fragments, do not use I, do not end with a preposition) that moralize usage without explaining it.

  • Biblical and religious sense (primary): the Ten Commandments, the Decalogue revealed to Moses — prohibitions of idolatry, blasphemy, murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting, with commands to honour parents and keep the sabbath. Capitalized as the Commandments, they are inscribed on stone, taught by rote, and cited as the foundation of Western moral law. To break or keep a commandment here is a spiritual act.
  • Moral and institutional extension: any solemn code presented as binding — the commandments of the church, of the party, of the profession. Hospitals, armies, newsrooms, and religions publish them; members memorize them. The tone isIER formal and public: these are not tips but obligations.
  • Schoolroom and style-guide sense (source sense): grammatical or compositional prohibitions learned as absolutes: do not use contractions, do not write one-sentence paragraphs, avoid the passive. Called commandments ironically or critically, they are remembered for their force rather than their reason. Teachers invoke them for control; later writers must unlearn or relativize them. The adjective hazily in the source sentence completes the portrait: remembered well enough to inhibit, too vaguely to guide.
  • Figurative and parodic sense: any mock-decalogue — the ten commandments of dieting, of start-ups, of dating — where biblical form dignifies worldly advice or exposes it. Orwell Animal Farm is the classic parody: barn-wall commandments rewritten as power shifts, showing how monumental rules serve rulers.

Connotation depends on the speaker stance. The believer says commandments with reverence; the satirist with irony; the educator with impatience. To call a rule a commandment rather than a guideline, principle, or suggestion is already to take a position: it claims the rule demands obedience, brooks no exception, and comes from unquestionable authority. That is why critics of prescriptive grammar love the word — it lets them paint old prohibitions as dogma — and why defenders avoid it, preferring rules or standards. Number matters too: commandments come in memorable small sets (ten, seven, five), suitable for tablets, walls, and blackboards, unlike the sprawling contingencies of real usage.

Distinguish commandments from commands (individual orders, often military and immediate), commandments versus precepts (more formal, less biblical), rules (broader, often procedural), laws (enforceable by institutions), and injunctions (authoritative but usually singular and legal). A command sends soldiers forward now; a commandment governs a community always. In the source context the plural is essential: no single grammatical rule oppresses the citizen; it is the tablet-full of half-remembered thou-shalt-nots that must be superseded by genuine knowledge of written discourse.
