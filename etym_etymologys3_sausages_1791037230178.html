The plural sausages encodes 4,000 years of salt. Its singular sausage descends without break from the PIE word for salt, through Latin salted pork, through medieval French charcuterie, into the English breakfast plate. The final -s is the ordinary Germanic plural, but the stem is a Mediterranean traveller.

Begin with PIE *seh₂l-, salt, one of the few flavour words reconstructible across Indo-European: Latin sal, Greek hals, Old English sealt, Sanskrit lavana from different formation but same economy. Salt was preservative, currency and tax base. Latin made from sal, salis the adjective salsus, salted — salsus porcus, salted pork — and the verb sallere, to salt. Roman soldiers received salarium, salt-money, source of salary; Roman cooks salted minced meats with pepper, cumin, herbs to keep them.

  • Latin sal to salsus: Regular adjectival formation. Salsus means both salted and, by extension, witty or seasoned in speech — Roman metaphor already links salt and wit.
  • Salsus to Vulgar Latin *salsicia: Late Antique substantivization, probably elliptical for salsicia caro, salted seasoned meat, with suffix -icia denoting product. The form is reconstructed but confirmed by Romance reflexes: Italian salsiccia, Spanish salchicha, Romanian sâlsiciu. Meaning narrows from anything salted to minced seasoned meat stuffed in gut.
  • Vulgar Latin to Old North French saussiche / saucisse: Regular Gallo-Romance palatalization and vowel shifts: sal- to sau- before consonant, -icia to -isse / -iche. Northern saussiche with ch versus Central saucisse with c reflects Norman-Picard phonology. By the 12th-13th centuries it denotes spiced pork sausage, a town butcher's luxury, regulated by guilds.
  • Old French to Middle English sausige / sausage: Borrowed after the Norman Conquest with the rest of the French meat vocabulary — beef, pork, mutton, sausage — where French names the dish, English names the animal. Chaucerian London eats sausige: minced pork, spices, in casing. Spelling wobbles for 300 years (sawsyge, saycyge) before stabilizing as sausage in Early Modern print.
  • Early Modern to Modern sausage → sausages: Tudor and Stuart cookbooks multiply varieties: pork, beef, blood, Bologna. Industrial revolution brings the butcher's sausage, the breakfast banger, the Chipolata. Plural with regular -s from Old English -as gives sausages — the form Wodehouse uses for a dish of sausages and ham served at a country house breakfast.

Phonetically the journey softens: Latin /salsiːkia/ to French /sosis/ to English /ˈsɒsɪdʒ/ with affrication of -age as in cabbage, village. RP keeps short /ɒ/, General American /ˈsɔːsɪdʒ/. Plural adds /ɪz/.

Culturally the sausage moves from preservation necessity to comfort emblem. Romans carried it on campaigns; medieval towns made it festive; Victorian England made it the banger — so called because cheap wartime sausages burst in the pan — the soldier's and schoolboy's staple. Germans develop Wurst prestige, Italians salsiccia pride, Britons develop ironic affection: sausage dogs (dachshunds), sausage fingers (stubby), sausage factory (impersonal production), not a sausage meaning nothing. Wodehouse's "matter of the sausages and ham" is a running joke about Gussie Fink-Nottle's gluttony and nerves at a wedding breakfast — sausages as English normality threatened by farce.

Modern food history adds regulation (meat content laws), globalization (chorizo, bratwurst, merguez in British supermarkets), and vegetarian veggie sausages that keep the shape while abandoning the salted-meat etymology. Yet the PIE salt is still tasted: without salt there is no sausage, only mince. The plural sausages thus serves as a tiny archive of Indo-European diet, Roman logistics, French cuisine and English breakfast — salted, stuffed and browned.
