Squeaked is the ordinary weak past of an imitative verb — a word whose very sound is its meaning — and its history is a small masterclass in how English builds expressive vocabulary. There is no grand Indo-European root to trace, no chain of sound laws from Latin or Greek; instead there is the human mouth making a thin, high noise and a language enthusiastically naming that noise with itself. 1. The expressive stratum of English vocabulary English possesses a remarkable family of words built not by inheritance but by imitation and sound-symbolism: squeak, squeal, screech, shriek, creak, squawk, peep, cheep. These belong to the expressive layer of the vocabulary — words coined to sound like what they mean. The initial sk-/sq- cluster suggests a sharp, scraping onset; the high front vowel suggests thinness and height of pitch; the final -k a sudden cut-off. This is why the family keeps being reinvented in dialect after dialect: the sound itself carries the meaning, so no single ancestor is needed. Historical linguists therefore treat the English forms as imitative in origin, reinforced by the neighboring shrill-sound words, and honestly decline to reconstruct a single Proto-Indo-European root for the whole set. 2. The attested record: Middle English squeken The first attested English forms appear in the fourteenth century. Middle English squeken (with spelling variants such as squiken) means "to make a short, shrill sound," and it is used of both animals and objects from the beginning. The word is classed as imitative: its shape — the sibilant onset, the high vowel, the velar cut — was judged suitable by medieval speakers for the thin, sharp noises of mice and hinges, in exactly the same way modern speakers coin or accept new imitative words. Because it is expressive vocabulary, the boundaries are soft: it sits in a cluster with squeal and screech, and the forms reinforced one another across dialects and centuries. 3. Early Modern and Modern English: settlement and spread By the sixteenth century the form settles as squeak, used as both verb and noun, and the meaning extends from the literal sound of mice and doors to the transferred sense of a thin, high, strained human voice. The morphology is unremarkable: the past and past participle are formed regularly with -ed, giving squeaked, a weak verb with no archaic inflections. What makes the word interesting is not its grammar but its social colour: applied to a person, it is almost always slightly comic or diminishing. Adults "say" and "protest"; the nervous, the small, and the childlike "squeak." The verb itself performs a judgment of scale. 4. The figurative growth: narrow margins and clean surfaces From the image of a thin, tight, high note the word generated a set of metaphors. A "narrow squeak" is a close shave — the image of passing through a space so tight it almost catches you, as a mouse escapes its hole by a whisker. To "squeak through" or "squeak by" is to succeed by the smallest possible margin, preserving the sense of a thin squeeze. The adjective squeaky gives two famous idioms: "the squeaky wheel gets the grease" (the loudest complainer is heard) and "squeaky-clean" (so clean, or so blameless, that it squeaks). "Not a squeak" means not a sound or a word. Every one of these extends the same core image: smallness, thinness, tightness, brevity. 5. The literary witness: Bilbo's squeak In the passage from which this card is drawn, Tolkien writes: "'It seems a great big hole to me,' squeaked Bilbo (who had no experience of dragons and only of hobbit-holes)." The word does the comic work of the whole scene. Against dwarves who measure doors by the bulk of a dragon, the hobbit's scale is domestic — his sense of size comes from hobbit-holes — and his protest comes out mouse-high. The verb of speaking is chosen to match the scale of the speaker: not "said", not "objected", but squeaked, a word whose entire imitative history compresses mice, hinges, and thin nervous voices into one small sound. The past tense is the narrative's one-shot stage direction: it records not a habit of Bilbo's voice forever, but exactly how his objection came out in that map-room moment. The word's thousand-year life as a name for thin, high, tight noise is distilled into a single verb, and the reader hears the sound as clearly as the dwarves did.
