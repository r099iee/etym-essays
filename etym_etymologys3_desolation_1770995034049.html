ETYMOLOGY ESSAY: DESOLATION 1. THE PIE ROOT *s(w)e-: SELF AS SEPARATION The word's ancestry reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European reflexive root *s(w)e-, reconstructed with the sense "oneself; one's own; apart, separate." In the ancient Indo-European conceptual world the self was defined against the group: what is "one's own" is precisely what is cut off from the tribe. The root's reflexes are scattered across the family — Sanskrit svá "one's own, oneself," Greek heós/hós "his own," Old English swā (modern so, originally "in this manner") — but its most fateful daughter for our purposes is the Latin adjective sōlus, "alone, sole, solitary, single." 2. LATIN solus AND THE IDEA OF ALONENESS For the Romans, sōlus carried a precise, somewhat fearful charge: to be sōlus was to stand apart from the protective collective. The word generated a family of "aloneness" vocabulary — sōlitūdō "solitude," sōlitārius "solitary," and, with the intensive/downward prefix dē- ("thoroughly, completely, down from"), the verb dēsōlāre, "to leave utterly alone, to abandon, to lay waste." The semantic key is the intensifier: solus means merely "alone," but desolare means "to make completely, irrevocably alone," and when a Roman commander desolabat a province he left it not just empty but gutted — its people gone, its crops burned, its silence absolute. 3. LATE LATIN desolatio AND THE VULGATE From dēsōlāre Latin formed the noun dēsōlātiō, "a laying waste, a making desolate." The word acquired enormous religious weight through the Latin Vulgate Bible (late 4th-early 5th c. CE), where desolatio translates Greek words for abandonment and ruin in prophetic and apocalyptic contexts — most famously the "abomination of desolation" (abominatio desolationis, Matthew 24:15), the sign that the temple stands condemned and emptied. From the Bible, desolation entered Christian Europe pre-loaded with apocalyptic solemnity. 4. OLD FRENCH AND MIDDLE ENGLISH (13TH-14TH CENTURIES) The word passed into Old French as desolacioun / desolation, "ruin, destruction, misery," and entered Middle English in the late 14th century (Chaucer's generation) — first in the physical sense of lands laid waste. In medieval English it describes sacked cities, plundered countryside, and the ruins left by plague and war. The apocalyptic biblical colouring travelled with it, so that the word never lost its solemn overtone. 5. THE INWARD TURN: 16TH-18TH CENTURIES The great semantic development of English desolation was its transfer from the external landscape to the internal mind. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century religious and devotional writing begins to speak of the "desolation of the soul" — a state of spiritual abandonment familiar from the vocabulary of mysticism. By the eighteenth century the emotional sense is fully established, and Romantic and Victorian writers exploit both registers at once: a ruined abbey mirrored by a ruined heart. 6. ROMANTIC AND MODERN CAREER - 14th-15th centuries: physical ruin of places (wars, plagues, ruins). - 16th century: religious sense of spiritual emptiness; the inner sense begins. - 17th-18th centuries: emotional desolation fully established in devotional and literary prose. - 19th century: Romantic sublime — desolate moors, Alpine wastes, ruined castles as grand, awe-inspiring scenes; the emotional sense grows toward existential emptiness. - 20th-21st centuries: the emotional sense now dominates ("a sense of desolation"); the physical sense survives in journalism and history (war reportage, environmental description). 7. SOUND SHIFTS AND FORM Latin dēsōlātiō → Old French desolacioun → Middle English desolacioun → modern desolation. The suffix -ātiō became French -acion and English -ation; the Latin long vowels shortened as the word was naturalised. The spelling was later reformed under Latin influence from desolacioun to desolation. The pronunciation modernised from a medieval French-flavoured form to the modern /ˌdɛsəˈleɪʃən/, with the stress settling on the penultimate syllable after -ation. 8. LITERARY ATTESTATIONS The King James Bible (1611) cemented the apocalyptic register. Romantic poets (Wordsworth, Shelley) used the word for sublime wastes. Victorian novelists used it for the inner emptiness of bereavement and exile. In the source sentence — the parents left with "the sense of desolation left by the death of their only heir, which had turned their home into a quiet and empty place" — the word performs its full double duty: the house is a desolate place because the heart is a desolate place, both having been "left utterly alone."
