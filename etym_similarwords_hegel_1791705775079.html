• Kant: Similar because both Immanuel Kant and Hegel are the supreme twin titans of German Idealism who sought to establish the foundational conditions of human knowledge, morality, and freedom; differs because Kant maintained an unbridgeable division between our subjective perceptions (phenomena) and things-in-themselves (noumena), whereas Hegel boldly asserted that reason is immanent within reality itself and that the Absolute can be fully known through dialectical historical progression.
  • Fichte: Similar because Johann Gottlieb Fichte directly preceded Hegel in German Idealism and pioneered the dialectical triad of the Ego positing itself against the Non-Ego; differs because Fichte anchored his entire metaphysical system in subjective moral striving and the individual transcendental Ego, whereas Hegel viewed the subjective ego as merely an early stage in the objective historical self-realization of Spirit.
  • Schelling: Similar because Friedrich Schelling was Hegel's close university roommate and early philosophical collaborator in developing an objective idealism that unified nature and mind; differs because Schelling believed the Absolute could only be reached through immediate artistic intuition and mystical revelation, which Hegel famously mocked as the "night in which all cows are black," insisting on rigorous conceptual logic.
  • Marx: Similar because Karl Marx adopted Hegel's dialectical engine and teleological view of history as progressing through profound systemic conflicts and contradictions; differs because Marx explicitly rejected Hegel's metaphysical idealism, asserting that material economic conditions and class struggle, rather than disembodied ideas or Spirit, drive human history.
  • Schopenhauer: Similar because Arthur Schopenhauer was Hegel's fierce Berlin contemporary who sought to explain the fundamental nature of the cosmos; differs because Schopenhauer was a radical pessimist who viewed the cosmos as an irrational, blind, tormenting Will with no historical progress, and he bitterly detested Hegel, scheduling his university lectures at the exact same hour as Hegel's (to an empty classroom).
  • Spinoza: Similar because Baruch Spinoza's monistic vision of a single, all-encompassing Substance (Deus sive Natura) served as the vital metaphysical blueprint for Hegel's concept of the Absolute; differs because Spinoza's substance was static, geometric, and timeless, whereas Hegel insisted that Substance must become Subject—a dynamic, self-developing historical Spirit.
