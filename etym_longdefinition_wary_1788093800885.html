Core sense. Wary describes a person (or animal, or attitude) who is cautious and watchful, alert to the possibility of danger, deception, harm or loss: "to be wary of something" is to hold it at arm's length until one is sure of it. Unlike mere careful, wary carries a distinct note of suspicion and vigilance — the wary person does not simply tread carefully, but keeps watch, expecting that something may be wrong. The word governs the prepositions of and about (wary of strangers, wary about the deal, wary of giving offence), and it appears attributively before nouns of gesture and manner: a wary glance, a wary step, a wary silence.

Etymological resonance. Wary is the adjective formed from the old word ware ("aware, watchful") — the same root that survives in aware, beware, ward and warn. The word therefore carries its whole history inside it: to be wary is to be ware, on one's guard, with the ancient vigilance of a sentry at the edge of the settlement. In the source sentence — Thorin bidding Dáin come "with wary speed" — the adjective modifies speed itself: haste tempered by watchfulness, the army arriving fast but with every scout's eye open.

  • 1. Primary sense: cautious and watchful against danger or trickery. The everyday meaning — a wary investor, a wary negotiator, a wary hiker, a wary voter. The emphasis is on sustained alertness and withheld trust rather than on fear.
  • 2. Wary of + gerund: reluctance founded on risk. "Wary of committing", "wary of signing", "wary of praise": the construction expresses hesitation rooted in suspicion of consequences, a very common modern pattern.
  • 3. Behavioural attribution: the wary look. A "wary glance", "wary eyes", "wary footsteps", "a wary distance": the adjective qualifies posture and gesture, describing visible wariness — the demeanour of a hunted or experienced creature.
  • 4. Animal wariness. Naturalists and hunters use the word of wild animals: a "wary deer", "wary game", a bird "wary of the trap". Here the word is literal: the animal's survival depends on perpetual watchfulness.
  • 5. Political and strategic uses. "Wary diplomacy", "the wary neutrality of the small state", "a wary truce": in statecraft the word denotes prudence among powers, suspicion without hostility.
  • 6. Archaic and poetic survivals. The bare form ware survives in literary use ("be ware!", "well ware of"), and compounds of the family (unwary, wariness, beware) keep the root alive. Unwary — the careless, easily trapped — is the word's natural antonym and its shadow.

Semantic boundaries. Wary sits between cautious and distrustful. It is stronger than careful (which need involve no suspicion), more attitude-based than alert (which is neutral readiness), less formal than vigilant and circumspect, and less accusatory than suspicious or distrustful: the wary person holds back without yet accusing. It also differs from afraid: wariness is a cool, surveyed caution, not fear. The boundary cases are chary (sparing, reluctant — stronger reluctance, rarer word) and leery (informal, wryly cynical).

Register and connotation. Wary is standard, slightly formal, and faintly old-fashioned in flavour — it can sound prudent and experienced, even wise, but also ever so slightly cold: the wary person trusts slowly. It is equally at home in headlines about markets, in fables about foxes, and in epic prose about marching armies, which is exactly the register Tolkien needs for a dwarf-king moving "with wary speed" toward battle.
