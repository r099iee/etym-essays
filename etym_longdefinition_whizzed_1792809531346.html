Whizzed is the past tense and past participle of the verb whiz, also spelled whizz: to move, sound, or act with a speed so great that motion and noise fuse into one impression — a thin hissing, buzzing, fizzing rush through air, time, or work alike.

In the source sentence the word carries its most cinematic sense: Tolkien’s red-golden dragon does not merely fly, he whizzes — three screaming circuits over the heads of the crowd, fire jetting from his jaws, the roar of the crowd answering the roar of the beast. The verb gives the firework-dragon the physics of a rocket and the menace of a living thing at once: circular, deafening, too fast to follow, gone and back before the eye can settle. Every child who has watched a Catherine wheel or a screaming firework arc across the night sky knows exactly the motion Tolkien names.

Core meaning in detail: At its heart whizzed binds two ideas that English usually keeps apart — velocity and sibilance. A stone can hurtle silently and a kettle can hiss while standing still, but what whizzes does both together: arrows, bullets, fireworks, express trains, spinning tops, swung ropes, and switched canes all whizz because they displace air fast enough to sing. The spelling with double z is the older and more emphatic form, the buzz visibly vibrating on the page, while the single-z whiz dominates in American use; the past tense doubles the consonant in British style (whizzed) and often simplifies it in American style (whized), though both are accepted.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Rushed through the air with a hissing sound (primary sense, from the 1540s): the arrow whizzed past his ear, fireworks whizzed over the crowd. This is the founding sense — imitative of the sound itself — and still the first picture the word paints: small fast things tearing the air until the air complains.
  • Made or produced that rushing hiss (sound sense): the bullets whizzed overhead, the kettle whizzed on the hob. The bridge is a simple shift of attention from the mover to the noise: the verb that described the flight comes to describe the sound of the flight, so that even a stationary thing can whizz if it sings the right song.
  • Hurried or rushed somewhere (generalised speed sense, 19th c.): she whizzed off to catch her train, the nurse whizzed between wards. The bridge drops the sound and keeps the speed: once whiz meant any fast noisy motion, hurrying people borrowed it for any fast motion at all, noisy or not.
  • Completed or passed through something rapidly (transitive, 20th c.): he whizzed through his exams, she whizzed through the paperwork. The bridge runs from physical rushing to effortless mastery: the paper, like the air, offers no resistance, and the task is over before onlookers have settled in their seats.
  • Spun or rotated at high speed (mechanical sense): the top whizzed on the flagstones, the centrifuge whizzed up to full speed. The bridge is the shared acoustics of rotation: a spinning thing at sufficient revolutions makes precisely the rising whine of a passing rocket, so the same verb serves the gyroscope and the firework.
  • Left quickly or vanished in a hurry (colloquial): he whizzed off before questions could be asked. The bridge combines speed with escape: to whizz off is to convert oneself into a projectile, present one instant and a dwindling hiss the next.
  • British slang — stimulated by amphetamine, known on the street as whizz or speed (late 20th c.): whizzed out of his head all weekend. The bridge is the drug’s signature effect: the racing heart, the jabbering rush, the sensation of the whole nervous system tearing through the air like a firework — the user feels exactly what the dragon looked like.

Register and connotation: Whizzed is vivid, informal, and affectionate — the vocabulary of playgrounds, sports commentary, war memoirs, and railway platforms rather than of statutes or sermons. It flatters speed: things that whizz are exciting, skilful, or thrillingly dangerous, never sluggish or dull. The noun whiz spun off a famous American compliment, the whiz kid — a young prodigy who tears through problems as a rocket tears through cloud — proving how naturally English turns this little hiss of sound into applause for brilliance.
