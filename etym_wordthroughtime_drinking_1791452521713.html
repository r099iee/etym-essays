- Old English (8th to 11th century) - Drincan means to swallow liquid, and also to soak or absorb; in the mead hall it is a communal, heroic act, and the word carries no stigma at all.
- Middle English (12th to 15th century) - Drinken covers both ordinary thirst and festive ale; the verb is morally neutral, and drinking is simply part of hospitality and fellowship.
- 16th to 18th century - The absolute use, "he drinks", narrows the word toward alcohol; drinking becomes a recognised social habit, and the tavern and the punch bowl give it a convivial, slightly rakish colour.
- 18th to 19th century - The gin craze and the temperance movement polarise the word; "the demon drink" makes drinking a moral and political issue, and the abstentionist vocabulary of teetotal and the pledge arises in opposition.
- 20th century - Prohibition in the United States and licensing laws in Britain institutionalise the word; "drinking problem", "drinking age" and "drink-driving" turn it into a matter of law, medicine and public health.
- Contemporary - Drinking is split between the neutral health sense (drinking water), the euphemistic alcohol sense (he has been drinking) and the figurative sense of eager absorption (drinking it in).
