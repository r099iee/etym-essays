PIE *weg- "to be strong, be lively" (reconstructed; also the source of Latin vegere "to be vigorous" and, via Germanic, English wake and watch) → Latin vagus "wandering, roving, strolling; unsettled, unfixed" (attested 1st c. BCE, Cicero and Lucretius; formed within Latin on the verb vagari "to wander," the root's sense of "being on the move" yielding the idea of straying) → Old French vague "wandering, unfixed, straying" (12th c.) → Middle French vague "vague, indefinite, imprecise" (the sense shifts from "roaming" to "not able to be pinned down" in the 13th-14th c.) → English vague "unsettled, unfixed; indefinite, imprecise" (borrowed 1540s) → English vagueness "state or quality of being vague" (formed with the suffix -ness; first attested 1660s). NOTE: The connection of Latin vagus to PIE *weg- "to be strong, be lively" is the standard reconstruction but is not beyond dispute; the semantic link runs through the idea of restless movement. The French-to-English path, by contrast, is securely documented.
