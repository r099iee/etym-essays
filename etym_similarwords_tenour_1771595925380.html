• Tenor: Similar because it is the identical word, same meaning — the general course, drift, or purport of something; differs because tenor is the standard modern spelling while tenour is the older, more literary British -our variant that Ruskin and nineteenth-century prose retain.
  • Gist: Similar because it is also what a text means at bottom; differs because gist is the colloquial word for the essential core one extracts after reading — a summary — whereas tenour is the sustained character and direction of the whole discourse itself.
  • Drift: Similar because it names the general direction of meaning; differs because drift suggests a slow, possibly accidental or aimless movement, while tenour implies a consistent, intended course against which individual passages are measured.
  • Purport: Similar because it concerns the apparent meaning or import of words; differs because purport is the outward sense a reader takes from the words, while tenour is the settled inner character that governs the whole.
  • Tone: Similar because it is an underlying quality of a work or remark; differs because tone is the emotional or stylistic colouring — solemn, ironic, light — whereas tenour is the direction and substance of the matter.
  • Thrust: Similar because it names the main line of an argument; differs because thrust is the point pushed forward with energy and insistence, while tenour is the steady, even character of the whole, without the aggressive connotation.
  • Trend: Similar because it names a prevailing direction; differs because trend is statistical and temporal — the direction of change over time — while tenour is the settled character of a work, life, or course at a given moment.
  • Course: Similar because it is a continuous progression; differs because course is the path or track along which a thing moves, whereas tenour is the manner and direction of that movement — its consistent bearing.
