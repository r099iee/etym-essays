thence (adverb): From that place; from that source or cause; from that time onward. In the Preface to Modern Painters John Ruskin writes: "I have said that the old masters did not give the truth of nature; if the reader chooses, thence, to infer that they were not masters at all, it is his conclusion, not mine." Here the word is pressed into purely logical service: it does not point to a place on a map but to a statement — the preceding clause — treating the sentence just uttered as the ground from which the reader may depart. Thence is the pivot of the whole construction: everything before it is the ground, everything after it is the journey away from that ground. The tone is formal and legalistic; thence is an adverb of the courtroom and the lawbook, and its presence lends the sentence a flavour of judicial scrupulousness — the judge noting that a certain consequence does indeed follow from the evidence, but that the conclusion is the litigant's own. The nuance is one of careful delimitation: thence marks the point of origin of a logical motion, doing the work of a map-reference inside an argument. Polysemy and related senses:
  • Spatial sense (oldest): "from that place, from there" — "they travelled to the coast and thence by boat to the islands." This is the sense in which the word first appears in Old English, and it remains the most transparent.
  • Causal and source sense: "from that source or cause" — "thence arises the difficulty" — tracing an effect back to its origin, whether a person, a thing, or a state of affairs.
  • Temporal sense: "from that time onward" — preserved chiefly in the compounds thenceforth and thenceforward, "from that time forward," where the word marks a beginning point in time rather than space.
  • Logical sense: "from that statement, for that reason, consequently" — the sense in Ruskin, where the adverb links a premise to the conclusion that follows from it; an extension of the spatial image, for the mind departs from a proposition as a traveller departs from a place.
The historical bridge between these senses is the image of motion away from a fixed point. The spatial sense is original; the causal and logical senses are metaphorical journeys — an effect "comes from" a cause as a traveller comes from a place, and a conclusion "follows from" a premise as a road follows from a town. Its nearest kin is hence, its complementary twin built on the same demonstrative machinery: hence means "from this (place or statement)," pointing back at the speaker's own position, while thence means "from that," pointing outward at something just stated or understood. Whence, "from which," completes the trio. In Ruskin's sentence hence would work almost as well, but thence is the sharper instrument because it keeps the reader at a distance: the conclusion starts from Ruskin's claim, not from the reader's own words. Register and usage notes:
  • Thence is now chiefly literary, formal, and legalistic; in ordinary speech and prose it has been largely displaced by "from there," "therefore," and "from then on."
  • Its fossilised compounds thenceforth, thenceforward, and thenceforwardly are the standard elevated way of saying "from that time onward."
  • Because it carries a demonstrative pointing gesture, it functions like a pronoun in adverb form, keeping the reader oriented inside an argument.
  • The word is a small museum of ancient grammar: a demonstrative stem, an ablative ending of motion, and a dead genitive -s, all fused into one unanalysable particle.
