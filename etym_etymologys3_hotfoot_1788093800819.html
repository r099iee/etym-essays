The two roots: FOOT — a chain from the Indo-European dawn

The second half of HOTFOOT is the most securely traced word in the compound. English foot continues Old English fōt, which descends from Proto-Germanic *fōts, itself from the Indo-European root *pṓds "foot" — one of the best-attested reconstructions in Indo-European linguistics because it appears in virtually every branch: Sanskrit pád, Greek poús (genitive podós), Latin pēs (genitive pedis), Hittite pata-, Tocharian pe. In Germanic, the nominative *pṓds underwent the regular sound laws — initial *p became *f, the lengthened *ṓ was retained, and *d became *t by Grimm's Law — yielding *fōts. Old English shows the inherited form fōt, and Middle English fot/foot displays the later vowel history by which the long /oː/ was raised by the Great Vowel Shift and shortened before /t/, giving the modern pronunciation /fʊt/. The word has never left the centre of English vocabulary: its uses — anatomical, metrical (twelve inches), literary (a metrical foot), and idiomatic ("on foot", "put one's foot down") — all descend from that same ancient noun.

  • PIE *pṓds "foot" → Proto-Germanic *fōts → Old English fōt → Middle English fot → Modern English foot.
  • Phonetic notes: Grimm's Law (PIE *p → Germanic *f; PIE *d → Germanic *t); the long vowel /oː/ later became /ʊ/ before /t/ in standard English.

The two roots: HOT — a Germanic word of debated depth

The first half is younger and less transparent. Old English hāt "hot, burning, fierce" descends from Proto-Germanic *haitaz, with cognates across the family: Old Saxon hēt, Old Norse heitr, Middle Dutch heet, German heiß, Gothic heitō "fever-heat". Beyond Germanic the trail goes cold: no secure Indo-European cognates are agreed upon. Some scholars compare Lithuanian kaistù, kaĩsti "to grow hot" and reconstruct a possible root *kai- "to heat", but the correspondence is disputed and most handbooks mark the pre-Germanic origin as uncertain. The Germanic adjective already had the full semantic range — physical heat, fierce temper, burning desire — so the mapping "hot = angry/urgent" is not an English invention but an inheritance. Old English hāt (with the long vowel /ɑː/) became Middle English hoot/hot (with /oː/), and the modern /hɒt/ continues the short form of that pair after the Great Vowel Shift.

  • Proto-Germanic *haitaz → Old English hāt → Middle English hot → Modern English hot; a possible PIE *kai- "to heat" is debated.
  • The "heat = emotion" metaphor (hot temper, hot pursuit, hot news) is already visible in Old English usage.

The compound is born: "hot fot" (c. 1300)

Around 1300, Middle English develops the adverbial phrase hot fot — "with hot feet, at a run, in haste". The idiom is a small masterpiece of folk imagery: it does not say "quickly" but paints the runner's feet as burning, so that haste is felt as heat. The construction pattern — adjective + body part in an instrumental phrase — echoes other body-part idioms of the period ("with all his might", "on bare foot"), and the phrase is attested in medieval verse and chronicle prose as a vivid synonym for "post-haste". By the 16th century the phrase has fused into a single word, usually written "hot-foot" or "hotfoot", and it appears in drama and narrative wherever a messenger or pursuer must move with urgent speed.

Verbification in America (1896)

In the late nineteenth century American English performs a characteristic conversion: the adverb becomes a verb. "To hotfoot it" — "to hasten, to run fast" — is first recorded around 1896, and the construction follows the familiar pattern of "foot it" (to walk), where an empty "it" turns a motion idiom into a verb. The new verb is immediately popular in journalism and pulp fiction, giving reporters and detectives a kinetic way to move ("the gang hotfooted it out of town").

The prank (1934): a metaphor made literal

In 1934 the noun "hotfoot" is recorded for the classic practical joke: a match or strip of paper is slipped between the sole and upper of a victim's shoe and lit, so that the victim's feet genuinely become hot and the victim literally runs hotfoot. The prank inverts the medieval metaphor — what was once an image of urgency is now a physical fact — and it shows the word's comic resilience: the same compound that served medieval messengers now serves schoolboy mischief.

Modern fortunes

In the 20th and 21st centuries "hotfoot" survives in two registers: the adverb in lively narrative prose (as in Tolkien's The Hobbit, where the grim-voiced fellow "ran hotfoot to the Master" as Lake-town learns of the dragon's fall) and the colloquial verb "hotfoot it" in journalism and speech. It is mildly old-fashioned but far from dead, prized precisely because it bundles speed, urgency, and a whiff of comedy into a single crisp word — a rare survival of medieval folk-poetry in everyday modern English.
