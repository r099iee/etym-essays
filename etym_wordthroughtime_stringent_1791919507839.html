- c. 1600 — Stringent enters English as a medical word: a stringent medicine is astringent, contracting tissue and checking fluxes.
- 17th–18th c. — The sense extends to taste and smell, where stringent means harsh and puckering; the word stays technical and uncommon.
- 1840s — The figurative sense “rigorous, severe” is recorded of laws and arguments, and the word begins its migration from the pharmacy to the statute book.
- Late 19th–early 20th c. — Regulatory and financial uses multiply: stringent measures, stringent economy, stringent rules of evidence.
- 1960s — Microbiology adopts stringent response for the bacterial starvation programme, giving the word a precise technical sense remote from morality.
- 21st c. — The dominant sense is regulatory and budgetary; stringent is the standard adjective of compliance, and its connotation is usually burden.
