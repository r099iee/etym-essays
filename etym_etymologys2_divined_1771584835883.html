PIE *dyeu- “to shine, be bright; the bright sky” (Pokorny *dyēu-; Beekes *di-u-; reconstructed) → PIE derivative *deywós “celestial being, god, the Shining One” (reconstructed) → Latin dīvus “divine, deified” (from *deywós; also deus “god”) → Latin dīvīnus “of the gods, divine” (adjective in -īnus built on dīvus) → Latin dīvīnāre “to foresee, foretell, prophesy, act as a soothsayer” (first-conjugation verb built on dīvīnus; attested in classical Latin) → Old French deviner “to divine, guess” (Gallo-Romance descendant of dīvīnāre; medieval) → Middle English divinen “to foretell, reveal by supernatural means” (14th c., borrowed from Old French deviner) → English divine (verb) “to foresee by supernatural means; to discover by intuition” (modern); past tense / past participle DIVINED (as on this card) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) The PIE reconstruction *dyeu- is secure in broad outline, but the precise root shape (*dyeu- vs. *deyw- for the derivative) is debated among Pokorny, Beekes, and LIV; the details of the *dy > Latin d- development (dīvus, deus, Iūp(p)iter) are standard historical phonology and not in dispute. (2) The Romance erosion of the word is uneven: Late Latin indīvīnāre gave Italian indovinare, and addīvīnāre gave Spanish adivinar, both simply “to guess”; English keeps the older sacred sense alongside the secular one. (3) The Germanic reflex of *deywós is Proto-Germanic *Tīwaz (Old English Tīw, Old Norse Týr), preserved in English Tuesday—a cognate of dīvus, not an ancestor of divined, which comes down the Latin → Old French → English line.
