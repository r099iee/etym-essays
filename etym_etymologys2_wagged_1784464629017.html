PIE *weǧh- “to go, move, transport, convey” (reconstructed root, c. 4000–3000 BCE; the ancestor of English way, wagon, weigh) → Proto-Germanic *wag- / *wagōną “to move, sway, rock, shake” (reconstructed; the root specialized from travel-motion to swaying-motion) → Old English wagian “to move, shake, totter, fluctuate” (attested 8th–11th c.) → Middle English waggen “to swing, wobble, move to and fro” (attested c. 1300) → Modern English wag “to move briskly to and fro” (16th c.) → WAGGED “past and past participle; the regular weak -ed form” (present day). Debated / nuanced note: The derivation of English wag from PIE *weǧh- is the standard handbook account (the same root yields way, wagon, weigh, and Latin vehere “to carry”), with Proto-Germanic specializing a “go / carry” idea into the shaking-swaying motion of loose things. The reconstruction is widely accepted, though the semantic bridge from “travel / convey” to “wag” is long and not every scholar is equally confident in it; the Germanic stage itself (*wagōną, and the noun *wagnaz “wagon”) is solid. Scandinavian contact may have reinforced the English verb's rocking image — Old Norse vagga “to rock (a cradle), sway,” also the noun “cradle,” is a North Germanic parallel sometimes cited as reinforcement. The historically attested portion (Old English wagian through modern wag) is secure.
