The biography of bulging is a story of how a prehistoric word for swelling passed through a leather bag, a wallet, and a hump before becoming an adjective for the dreadful round eyes of imaginary lake-creatures.

The PIE root. At the deepest level stands the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰelǵʰ-, "to swell, bloat, puff up," a root built around the very image of distension. Its central sense is the swelling of a bag or a belly, and that image governs every later meaning of the English word. The root radiated widely: in the Germanic branch it produced Proto-Germanic *balgiz, "bag, bellows," the ancestor of Old English belg and belig, whence modern English belly (the body's own bag) and bellows (the twin bags of the forge). In the Indo-Aryan branch it gave Sanskrit √bṛh, "to grow great, to swell," source of bṛhant, "great, strong," and of the modern Hindi बृहत् (bṛhat) and Odia ବୃହତ୍ (bṛhat), "large, great, vast," where the idea of swelling became the idea of greatness itself. In the Celtic branch — the one that matters most for this word — the root produced Proto-Celtic *bolgos, "bag, belly," with descendants Old Irish bolg, "bag, belly" (still Irish bolg today), Welsh bol, "belly, paunch," and, decisively, Gaulish bulga, "leather bag, knapsack."

The Celtic loan into Latin. The Gaulish bulga entered Latin as bulga, "leather sack," a Celtic loanword carried into the Roman world by traders and soldiers along with the object it named. Latin then passed the word on to the Romance vernaculars, and Old French shaped it as boulge / bouge, "wallet, leather pouch," which with the Normans and the general traffic of the later Middle Ages crossed the Channel into English. Middle English bulge / bouge, attested from the 13th century, meant "wallet, purse; hump." Here the story takes its crucial turn: the word that in English would one day name an ugly protrusion was for centuries the everyday name of a leather pouch — a small, rounded container that swells when full. The semantic bridge from wallet to swelling is already latent in the object.

The birth of the verb. In the 16th century the noun's secondary sense "hump, rounded protuberance" generated the verb to bulge, at first "to bend, to curve outward," then "to swell out, to protrude." The logic is exact: a full wallet gives its shape to any rounded distension, and the verb began to be used of walls, sacks, pockets, and eventually bodies — anything whose surface swelled outward under pressure. By the 17th century the present participle bulging was fixed as an adjective for things that stand out roundly: bulging pockets, bulging muscles, and, with increasing vividness, bulging eyes. The sound of the word helped fix its meaning: heavy rounded vowels and hard g's give it a bloated, waterlogged quality that no synonym quite matches.

The modern career. From the 18th century the word spread into ever more domains: cartography and architecture use a bulge for any outward curve in a line or wall; demography speaks of a population bulge when one age group swells the pyramid; and in 1944–45 the Battle of the Bulge borrowed the image for the great outward salient of the German offensive in the Ardennes. In all these uses the same primitive image survives — a boundary pressed outward by what it contains. It was precisely this primitive, tactile force that J.R.R. Tolkien exploited in The Hobbit (1937): "nasty slimy things, with big bulging blind eyes, wriggling in the water." The participle makes the horror permanent — the eyes do not swell and subside; they simply are bulging — and the word's grotesque roundness matches the slime of the underground lake.

Parallel developments. Meanwhile the same PIE root lived on independently in other branches: English belly and bellows descend from the Germanic twin of the same root, and Hindi/Odia bṛhat preserve the Indo-Aryan sense of swelling grown into greatness. The history of bulging is therefore not one line but a fan — a prehistoric root that swelled out, like its own meaning, into bags, bellies, bellows, greatness, and finally into the dreadful eyes of creatures in the dark.

Key sound shifts and forms.
  • *bʰelǵʰ- → Celtic *bolgos: the laryngeal-bearing root simplified to the syllable bol- "swell," with -g- velar preserved.
  • Gaulish bulga → Latin bulga: a straight loan; Latin lacked the Celtic initial distinction and kept the form bulga, "leather sack."
  • bulga → Old French boulge: the liquid cluster and diphthong of Old French, later bouge by regular reduction.
  • Old French → Middle English bulge/bouge: 13th-century loan, at first "wallet, hump."
  • Noun → verb: 16th-century denominal formation to bulge, "to curve or swell outward."
  • Verb → participle adjective: 17th-century bulging, "swelling, protruding."
