- Medieval Russia — the turning-plough measure: vьrsta first names a furrow's length and an age of life, then settles as the road-mile of 500 sazhens.
- 16th–17th c. — the empire paces itself: striped verst-posts rise along the great roads, and every itinerary, tax-roll, and exile-march is counted in versts.
- 19th c. — the novelist's mile: Pushkin, Gogol, and Tolstoy reckon duels, pilgrimages, and retreats in versts, fixing the word as Russia's own measure of fate.
- 19th c. — English borrows it: travellers, war correspondents, and translators bring versts home, preferring its sleigh-bell sound to flat conversion into miles.
- 1924 — the metric axe: the Soviet Union abolishes the verst for the kilometre, and the posts come down, leaving the word to literature.
- Today — pure fairy tale: versts survives in translations like the source sentence, where the Eagle's many versts means not 1.067 km times N but simply very, very far.
