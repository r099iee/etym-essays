The Germanic strong verb. Sprang is a form of one of the most characteristically Germanic verbs in the language — spring, a class 3 strong verb with the ablaut series spring – sprang – sprung. Its ancestry runs back to a Proto-Germanic verb *springaną, "to spring up, burst forth, leap, rise," a verb shared by every major Germanic language: Old Norse springa "to burst, split, jump," Old Saxon and Old Dutch springan, Old High German springan "to jump" (modern German springen "to jump," Dutch springen), and Gothic spriggan (in us-spriggans "to sprout"). The ultimate Indo-European origin is debated: the root is often reconstructed as *sprengh-, "to move swiftly, to hurry," with Greek spérkhesthai "to hasten, be eager" as a possible cognate, but the connection is not certain, and some handbooks mark the deeper origin as obscure or as sound-symbolic. What is beyond dispute is that the "bursting forth" meaning is ancient and Germanic-wide: for Germanic speakers the verb named the sudden eruption of water from the earth, the sprouting of plants, the splitting of wood, and the leap of a man or beast — a single root idea of something pent-up suddenly releasing into motion.

Old English: sprang on the battlefield and in the mere. Old English inherited the verb as springan, and its past singular was already sprang. In the Old English corpus the verb is vigorous and physical. In Beowulf and the heroic poetry, warriors spring to arms and leap aboard ships; water springs from the ground, as in the famous passage where blood springs from the Grendel-haunted mere; flames spring up; and, importantly, the verb takes the sense of "to come into being, to arise," when a lineage or a custom is said to spring from an ancestor. Old English prose extends the verb to the natural world: rivers, seeds, and shoots all "spring." The past form sprang was, even then, the standard way to narrate a sudden past event.

Middle English: the sense grows with the language. In Middle English (12th–15th centuries) the verb springen broadened further. The past tense appears as sprang in the south and spronge/sprunk in northern dialects. The meanings multiply: to leap and dance, to spread (as news or a rumour "springs"), to grow (as corn and grass), to originate (as a word or a custom), and to dawn (as the day springs). Chaucer uses the verb freely, and by 1400 the word has essentially the full modern semantic range except for the mechanical senses.

Early Modern English: the great expansion. The 16th and 17th centuries, the age of the English Bible and the drama, pressed spring into a dozen new shapes. Shakespeare and his contemporaries use "sprang" for sudden emotion and sudden action — characters spring to their feet, spring to a revenge, spring a quarrel — and for deception: to "spring a trap" or "spring a mine" (from the early use of gunpowder mines in siegecraft, where a spring is a sudden explosion from below). The King James Bible (1611) gives the verb some of its most famous literary settings — springs of water, the fountain of life, things springing up — while the 17th-century prose of Bacon and Milton uses "sprang" for origins: Milton has his creatures "sprang" from the earth and from one another. By 1700 the verb was one of the most productive in English, and its past tense sprang was fixed in the literary standard.

Modern senses: mechanical, idiomatic, and professional. The industrial era added mechanical senses: a coiled spring recoils, a lock "springs," a leak "springs" in a hull, and a board "springs" when it warps. The 19th century fixed the idiom "to spring a surprise," and the 20th added Americanisms such as "to spring for" (to pay for) and "to spring" someone free from prison. In 20th- and 21st-century English, sprang lives chiefly in a set of vivid idioms — "sprang to mind," "sprang to life," "sprang into action," "sprang to his feet," "sprang a surprise" — and remains the approved literary past tense, while colloquial American speech increasingly substitutes sprung ("he sprung up"), a usage grammarians still mark as nonstandard. It is telling that the most enduring modern literary uses of the word are found in exactly the kind of adventure and fantasy prose where sudden, elemental motion matters — such as Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), where the waters of the Running River "sprang" from the mountain along with a steam and a dark smoke.

Why sprang endures. The form has survived virtually unchanged for over a millennium because strong verbs are the backbone of everyday English and because its sense — sudden, energetic, originating motion — never becomes obsolete. In a single monosyllable, sprang preserves the sound, the shape, and the vivid physical meaning of the earliest Germanic world: a spring of water, a burst of flame, a warrior's leap, all the pent-up energies of the old poetry, still jumping off the page.
