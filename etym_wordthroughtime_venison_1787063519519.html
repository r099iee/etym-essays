- [12th–13th c.] - [Anglo-Norman Feudal Law] Entered Middle English as venesoun from Anglo-Norman, designating all wild game beasts taken in the hunt under royal Forest Law.
- [14th–15th c.] - [Aristocratic Culinary Luxury] Associated exclusively with high aristocratic feasts, hunting lodges, and royal gift-giving (e.g., venison pasties), establishing a sharp social boundary between noble tables and peasant fare.
- [16th–17th c.] - [Biblical & Broad Literary Usage] Retained its broad scriptural sense of "any hunted wild game" in the King James Bible, while everyday usage increasingly centered on deer.
- [18th–21st c.] - [Specialized Cervid Delicacy] Fully narrowed to denote the culinary meat of deer, prized in fine dining, sustainable gastronomy, and game butchery as a healthy, lean alternative to commercial livestock.
