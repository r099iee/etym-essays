PIE *gʰel- "to shout, cry out, sing, utter an incantatory vocal sound" (reconstructed imitative root, c. 4000 BCE; also source of Greek khelidōn "the swallow", literally "the twittering bird", and Lithuanian gaidys "rooster", "the dawn-crower") → Proto-Germanic *galaną "to sing, chant, bewitch, utter incantatory sounds" (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–500 CE; cf. Old English galan "to sing, chant spells", Old High German galan, Old Norse gala) → Old Norse gala "to crow (of a bird), to sing, to chant spells" (attested, pre-11th c. CE; also the source of galinn "enchanted, mad, wild", whence Norwegian/Swedish galen "crazy") → Middle English gale "a wind, a breeze, a storm-wind" — the wind imagined as a howling, shrieking "singer" (first recorded c. 1540s) → English gale "a strong wind; a violent sustained blast; an outburst" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: The origin of gale "wind" is genuinely disputed. The traditional account — followed here — links it to Old Norse gala "to sing, crow": the storm-wind conceived as an invisible shrieking entity that "sings" or "crows" across the sea, the same root family that yields nightingale ("night-singer"). But the OED labels the wind-word's origin "uncertain", and alternative hypotheses derive it independently from English or dialectal formations, or associate it with the Germanic scream-words (cf. Old English giellan "to yell", Dutch gillen "to scream"). The stages from *gʰel- through *galaną to Old Norse gala are secure for the "sing/shriek" family; the specific step by which the wind-word attaches to that family is the debated link. The Middle English date (c. 1540s) and the later strengthening of the sense from "any wind" to "violent wind" are not in dispute.
