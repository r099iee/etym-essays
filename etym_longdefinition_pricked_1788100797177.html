PRICKED is the weak past tense and past participle of the English verb prick — “to pierce or puncture with a sharp point, to make a tiny hole in something, or to sting with a sensation like that of a sharp point”. In the source passage — again from Oscar Wilde’s The Happy Prince — the seamstress’s hands are “all pricked by the needle”: the participle names a cumulative, habitual injury, the countless tiny piercings of a working woman’s fingers. From that literal core the word has spread, over a thousand years, into physiological, emotional, agricultural and slang registers while keeping its essential image: a sharp point, a small entry, a quick sting.


1. Primary literal sense — pierced with a point. To prick is to make a small puncture with a needle, pin, thorn, spur or similar point: to prick a finger, to prick a balloon, to prick a blister. Unlike “stab” or “pierce”, “prick” implies a minimal, precise contact — the point enters and withdraws, leaving a pin-prick wound rather than a gash. The participle “pricked” therefore describes surfaces or bodies marked by many small punctures (“all pricked by the needle”) or a single sharp sting (“his finger was pricked”).



  • Sensation sense: “pricked” also names the feeling — a sudden, sharp, tingling pain (“a pricked sensation in the foot”); medical usage retains “prickling” and “pins and needles” for neuropathic tingling, and the allergy “prick test” (a skin puncture test) is a standard diagnostic procedure.

  • Conscience sense (figurative): “a pricked conscience”, “pricked by remorse” — the conscience stung as if jabbed by a point. The bridge: guilt arrives suddenly and painfully, like a needle’s entry, and lingers as a recurring sting. Fixed in English by the King James Bible, Acts 2:37 — “they were pricked in their heart” (1611).

  • Ears sense: “to prick up one’s ears” — an animal raises and points its ears to listen intently; by transference, “he pricked up his ears” (became suddenly attentive). The image: the ears as points that rise and aim towards the sound; the construction survives as “prick-eared” (of animals with erect ears).

  • Balloon sense (figurative): “to prick a balloon” — to deflate a grand illusion, pretension or self-importance; the image is the puncture that destroys an inflated object, and by extension any sudden deflation of pride.

  • Target sense (historical): the noun “prick” named the centre-mark of an archery target in the 16th–17th centuries (“to hit the prick”, to be exactly right), giving “the very prick” the sense of exactness or the precise point (also “prick of noon”, the exact moment of midday).

  • Agricultural sense: “to prick out” — to transplant seedlings by lifting them with a pointed dibber or similar tool; “pricking out” is entirely standard in modern horticulture, and the noun “pricker” survives as a gardening implement.

  • Archaic equestrian sense: to prick a horse was to spur it on (Middle English); hence “to prick” once meant “to ride fast” (“pricking over the plain”), and the same root gives the modern “spur” sense of urging on.

  • Marking sense: to prick was also to mark with dots — sailors pricked the ship’s position on a chart; bookbinders pricked gatherings of pages; and “prick-song” (16th century) was written-out music, i.e. notes “pricked” on paper, as opposed to extemporised singing.


2. Related meanings of the word family. The plural noun “pricks” today mainly means thorny points (“thistle pricks”); the rude sense of the bare noun (penis, recorded from c. 1590; later a general insult) is a separate headword that nonetheless descends from the same “point” image, by way of the pointed phallus. In contemporary medicine “prick test” and the pricked reaction site are technical uses; in colloquial speech the verb keeps its physical core while meaning also “to annoy persistently” (“it pricks me that…” — rarer, overlapping with “it galls me”).


3. Semantic bridge summary. Every sense — the sting of conscience, pricked ears, pricked balloons, pricked-out seedlings, pricked-song — flows from one concrete prototype: a sharp point making a small, sudden, precise contact. The emotional senses retain the suddenness and the recurring sting; the horticultural and nautical senses retain the pointed instrument; the vulgar sense retains the point as phallus. Nothing in the word’s history requires a new root: it is one image, steadily re-applied.


4. Grammar and register. “Pricked” is the regular weak past and participle (prick → pricked) and also stands alone as an adjective (“a pricked hand”, “pricked conscience”). Register today is neutral-to-archaic-literary: the word survives most vitally in fixed phrases (pricked conscience, pricked ears, pricked by curiosity, regret or ambition), while the literal needle sense persists in sewing, horticulture and medicine — exactly the world of the seamstress in the source sentence.
