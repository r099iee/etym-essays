The word taxonomy is strikingly young: it did not exist in antiquity at all, but was manufactured in 1813 by the Swiss botanist Augustin Pyramus de Candolle, who needed a name for the theory of plant classification in his Théorie élémentaire de la botanique. He formed French taxonomie from Ancient Greek taxis (arrangement, order) + -nomie (law, management, as in astronomie and économie), and English borrowed it within a few years, first recorded in 1816 and naturalized by the 1820s. Everything the word now carries — Linnaean ranks, Darwinian trees, library schemes, corporate charts — accumulated after that deliberate coinage.

To understand the coinage one must go back to its two Greek halves, which have entirely separate prehistories. The first half begins with the reconstructed PIE root *tag-, meaning to touch or handle, extended to putting things in order with the hands. In Greek this yields the verb tassein, to draw up in order — used of arranging soldiers in battle-array, ordering affairs, appointing to office. From it comes the noun taxis, arrangement or order, one of the great organizational words of classical Greek: military divisions were taxeis, civic order was eutaxia, rhetoric and grammar spoke of taxis of words. Latin borrowed it sparingly as taxis in medical writers for the manual reduction of hernias, preserving the hands-on sense, but it never became a general Latin word for classification.

The second half begins with a different PIE root, *nem-, to assign, allot, or distribute — the root of division and management. In Greek it yields nemein, to distribute (pasture, land, honours), hence nomos, that which is allotted: custom, law, management, and nomia, administration or distribution, seen in oikonomia (household management, whence economy) and astronomia (ordering of the stars). The combining form -nomy thus meant a principled system or science of ordering, not merely naming. When de Candolle glued taxo- to -nomie, he was therefore saying something precise: not just a list of names but the law-governed ordering of kinds.

  • Before the word: the practice without the name. Classification long predated the label. Aristotle divided animals into blooded and bloodless in the 4th century BCE; Theophrastus classified plants; medieval herbals, bestiaries, and Porphyrian trees ordered nature logically. Linnaeus in Systema Naturae (1735) and Species Plantarum (1753) gave biology binomial nomenclature and nested ranks without ever using the word taxonomy. De Candolle supplied the retrospective banner under which all this earlier work could march.
  • 1813–1859: the botanical and zoological sense. Throughout the early nineteenth century taxonomy meant almost exclusively the classification of plants and animals into species, genera, families, orders, and classes. It competed with systematics and classification; British writers often preferred taxology briefly, but taxonomy won. Textbooks treated it as the static, descriptive counterpart to dynamic physiology.
  • 1859–1940: Darwin reshapes the meaning. After On the Origin of Species, taxonomy could no longer be mere pigeon-holing: affinities were reinterpreted as degrees of common descent. The same ranks remained, but their justification shifted from resemblance to genealogy. Debates between lumpers and splitters, the rise of type specimens, and codes of nomenclature (botanical, zoological) made taxonomy an institutionally policed science.
  • 1940–1990: new systhesis, numerical and cladistic revolutions. Ernst Mayr, Simpson, and the Modern Synthesis tied taxonomy to population thinking; then numerical taxonomy (phenetics) tried to make it purely mathematical, and Willi Hennig cladistics insisted only monophyletic groups count. Each school fought over what a correct taxonomy is, swelling the literature with terms like alpha, beta, and gamma taxonomy.
  • 1990–present: the great metaphorical escape. With DNA barcoding and phylogenomics transforming the biological core, the word simultaneously escaped biology altogether. Business speaks of product taxonomies, computer science of data taxonomies and folksonomies, education of Bloom taxonomy of learning objectives, libraries of web taxonomies and ontologies. In this generalized sense — exactly the one in the source sentence, a chart of basic prose types, a taxonomy — it means any principled, hierarchical map of a domain, often drawn as a literal chart or tree.

Phonetically the word travelled with little change: French taxonomie with final stress and nasal vowels was anglicized to /tækˈsɒnəmi/, stress on the second syllable, the x pronounced /ks/, the ending levelled to the familiar -my of economy and astronomy. The adjective taxonomic and verb taxonomize followed in the nineteenth century. Spelling wobbles between taxonomy and the rarer taxinomy in early texts before settling.

Literary attestation tracks this career: de Candolle 1813 for the coinage; Lyell, Darwin, and Huxley for the Victorian consolidation; Bentham and the librarians for the extension to knowledge organization; Bloom (1956) for Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, the book that carried the word into every teachers college; and modern style manuals that casually promise a taxonomy of paragraphs, sentences, or plots. The source sentence belongs to this last stage, using the prestige of biological order to dignify a modest chart of prose types — a small, self-aware borrowing that only works because taxonomy now connotes completeness, hierarchy, and scientific seriousness.
