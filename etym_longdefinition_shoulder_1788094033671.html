Shoulder is both a noun and a verb. As a noun it names, first, the anatomical region where the upper arm meets the trunk — the complex of bones, muscles and joints (clavicle, scapula, humerus, and the glenohumeral joint) that gives the arm its extraordinary range of motion; and second, by an ancient and very productive metaphor, any projecting, rounded or sloping part of a larger thing: the shoulder of a mountain, of a hill, of a road, of a bottle or of a vase. In the sentence "the great shoulder of the south-pointing mountain-spur drew ever nearer," the word transfers the body's sloping shoulder-shape onto a spur of rock: the mountain-spur presents a broad, rounded flank like a giant's shoulder, and the travellers on the road watch it approach like a huge body part of the landscape itself.

Primary senses — the noun.

  • Anatomy: the region of the body at which the arm or forelimb attaches; by synecdoche, the whole upper part of the back and chest ("he stood with his shoulders square"); in animals, the joint of the foreleg with the trunk.
  • Topography and objects: the projecting part of a hill, mountain, or spur where a slope rounds off ("the shoulder of the pass"); the edge of a road outside the carriageway ("the hard shoulder" of a motorway); the sloping part of a bottle or vase where the neck meets the body; the "shoulder" of a book binding where the spine meets the boards.
  • Butchery and cookery: a cut of meat from the foreleg ("shoulder of lamb", "shoulder of pork"), prized for slow cooking and flavour.

Secondary senses — the verb.

  • To push with the shoulder: "he shouldered his way through the crowd" — physical shoving with the shoulder used as a tool; also "to shoulder (a weapon)": to place a rifle on the shoulder, in military drill ("shoulder arms!").
  • To take on a burden (figurative): "to shoulder responsibility", "to shoulder the blame", "to shoulder a debt" — the physical act of carrying on the shoulders becomes the metaphor for accepting a weight of duty or blame; this is now the most common use of the verb, and the bridge is transparent: burdens are literally carried on the shoulders.

Tertiary and idiomatic senses.

  • Cold shoulder: a deliberate snub or show of indifference; recorded from the early 19th century (first in Scott's The Antiquary, 1816), originally "to show the cold shoulder" — the image is of turning an unwelcome guest away with only a cold shoulder of mutton (or a cold welcome) rather than a warm embrace.
  • Shoulder to shoulder: standing close together in solidarity, originally military (troops in close order); now the standard idiom for united effort.
  • A chip on one's shoulder: a persistent grievance or belligerent attitude; an American 19th-century idiom (from the custom of a boy daring another to knock a chip of wood off his shoulder).
  • A good head on one's shoulders: good sense and judgement.
  • To look over one's shoulder: to be watchful, wary, or anxious; also, in professional life, the fear of rivals "looking over your shoulder".
  • On someone's shoulders: as that person's responsibility ("the fate of the quest rested on his shoulders").
  • Shoulder season: in tourism, the travel period between peak and off-peak seasons; the metaphor is of the sloping shoulder of a demand graph.
  • Shoulder pad / shoulder strap: fashion and clothing terms for the padded or strapped part of a garment over the shoulder; "shoulder bag" — a bag carried by a strap over the shoulder.
  • Shoulder surfing: modern jargon for surreptitiously watching someone's screen or keypad to steal information — the "over the shoulder" glimpse made into a security term.
  • Shoulder check: in hockey and driving, a quick glance over the shoulder to see what is behind.

Domain uses.

  • Engineering and roads: the "shoulder" of a road is its unpaved or hardened edge strip, a safety margin beside the traffic lanes; "soft shoulder" warns drivers that the verge is not firm.
  • Anatomy and medicine: "shoulder joint", "frozen shoulder" (adhesive capsulitis), "dislocated shoulder", "shoulder impingement" — the anatomical sense supports a large clinical vocabulary.
  • Geology and mountaineering: "shoulder" names the rounded upper flank of a mountain below the summit ridge; climbers and surveyors use it routinely.
  • Military and ballistics: "shoulder-fired" weapons are launched from the shoulder; "shoulder arms" is a drill command.

Why the word means all this. The semantic history is one continuous transfer from the human body outward. The Old English sculdor named the body part; because the shoulder is the body's most prominent rounded projection, the word was extended by analogy to any projecting part of a landscape, a road, a vessel or a garment. The verb grew out of the noun in the same way: people push with their shoulders, carry on their shoulders, and so "shoulder" became the verb for shoving and for bearing. The figurative sense — shouldering responsibility — is among the oldest and most natural of the metaphors, since carrying weight on the shoulders is the archetypal human image of burden-bearing. Every modern sense, from "hard shoulder" to "shoulder surfing", still runs through that single image: the rounded, load-bearing projection of the body.
