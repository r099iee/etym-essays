- Mid-16th Century (Tudor Era, c. 1540s) - Appears in English vernacular literature and drama as *trudge*, describing vagabonds, peddlers, and servants sent trudging on errands or packing off on foot.
- Late 16th to 17th Century (Elizabethan & Jacobean Eras) - Used by Shakespeare and contemporaries with colloquial energy (e.g., "trudge, pack, begone!"), meaning to pack up and march away or walk laboriously with packs.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Solidified in travelogues and rural poetry, describing peasants, pilgrims, and weary postal couriers walking long distances over unpaved country roads.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Reached peak expressive power in Victorian social novels and fairy tales (Charles Dickens, Thomas Hardy, Oscar Wilde), symbolizing the crushing physical exploitation of the laboring poor (as Little Hans trudged under the Miller's sack).
- 20th Century (Wartime & Modern Era) - Universalized in World War I and II trench literature to depict infantrymen surviving forced marches; adopted metaphorically into addiction recovery ("trudge the Road of Happy Destiny").
- 21st Century (Contemporary Usage) - Widely used in journalism, fiction, and everyday speech to express resilient physical endurance in blizzards or weary mental persistence through bureaucratic and digital toil.
