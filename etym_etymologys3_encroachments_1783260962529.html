The story of encroachment begins not in Latin or Greek but in the Germanic languages of north-western Europe, with the reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *krokaz — "a hook, a bent instrument". This is a word with no securely attested Proto-Indo-European ancestor. Although scholars have tentatively connected it to roots meaning "crooked, curved", no certain Indo-European cognates survive outside Germanic, and both the OED and Etymonline treat its ultimate origin as obscure; the family may be sound-symbolic, imitating the shape of a bent thing. What is certain is its remarkable distribution across the Germanic world: Old Norse krókr ("hook, bend, corner"), Old High German krahho ("hook"), and Middle Dutch croec all preserve the same shape and meaning. From the Norse form came the Middle English crok, ancestor of modern English crook and crooked — the latter shifting from physical bend to moral bend, so that by 1879 crook could mean a criminal who "bends" the law. English also has the cognate crutch and, through the Germanic idea of a hooked staff, the shepherd's crook.

The decisive development came on the Continent. From the Old Low Franconian (Frankish) form *krok, the word passed into Gallo-Romance as Old French croc, "hook, fang, tusk". Here the hook proliferated into a remarkable lexical family. Its diminutive crochet ("little hook") gave English both crochet, the looped needlework that hooks thread, and crotchet, the musical note whose stem is literally a little hook. The verb crochier meant "to hook, seize"; with the prefix en- ("in, into") it formed encrochier — "to catch with a hook, to seize, fasten on, grab hold of". The physical image is vivid: one imagines a hooked instrument catching a garment or a load and dragging it toward oneself, an act of possessive grabbing rather than open assault. The noun encrochement ("seizure, grasp") followed in the same period.

English took the verb in the thirteenth century as encrochen, "to seize, acquire unlawfully", and here the crucial semantic shift began: what was once a physical action of hooking became a legal and political one of unlawful acquisition. In medieval agrarian law an encroachment was precisely the extension of a fence, wall, or claim over a neighbour's land — the stealthy taking of a few feet of another's ground. By the sixteenth century the verb encroach had generalised to mean "to advance gradually, stealthily, and illegitimately beyond proper limits", the sense of creeping expansion displacing the sense of a single grab. The noun encroachment (with the suffix -ment, "action or result of") settled into the vocabulary of law and constitutional history, describing the slow accumulation of power across boundaries.

It was the seventeenth-century constitutional struggles of England that gave encroachment its greatest career. In the political prose of the Civil War, the Restoration, and the Glorious Revolution, the encroachments of royal power became the standard name for the gradual erosion of parliamentary liberties — the accusation that a king, without a single dramatic usurpation, could nibble away the constitution by degrees. The word perfectly matched the Whig anxiety that liberty dies by inches. Macaulay, writing in the nineteenth century, inherits this fully developed constitutional vocabulary: his "encroachments of kingly power" are the archetypal abuse that the Revolution settlement was designed to contain, and his irony — that the very system which blocked them "gave birth to a new class of abuses" — is the historian's commentary on the same word's long political life.

  • Sound change. The chain is a borrowing, not an inheritance through Germanic sound laws. The French croc → encrochier → encrochement → Middle English encrochen shows normal Romance palatalisation and the English simplification of the French sh-sound to ch (as in chaise → chase).
  • False-friend warning. Do not confuse encroach with encroach upon one's courtesy in the sense of presumption: both are the same word, the difference is only the object — land, power, rights, or patience.
  • Sibling words. Crook, crooked, crochet, crotchet, crutch, and the archaic incroach all belong to the same Germanic hook-family.
  • Literary attestations. Middle English legal records for the thirteenth-century sense; the constitutional pamphlets of the seventeenth century; Macaulay's History of England; and modern property law, environmental science, and the rulebook of American football for the contemporary senses.
  • Contemporary note. In modern use the land and property sense remains the legal core, the constitutional sense survives in political prose, and the sporting and ecological senses show the word still growing — all of them faithful to the ancient hook: something that seizes, holds, and pulls across a line.
