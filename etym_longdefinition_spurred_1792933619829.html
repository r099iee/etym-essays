Spurred is the past tense of spur: to jab a horse's flanks with the small spiked wheels or pricks worn on a rider's heels, driving the animal from trot to gallop in a single sharp lesson. It is the verb of sudden violent acceleration — steel against flesh, will against instinct — and everything English has built on it keeps that sting: whatever is spurred moves faster than it meant to, because something sharp has touched it.

In the source sentence a rider — one of the Black Riders hunting the hobbits — spurred his great horse right at the speaker, who leaps aside only just in time. Tolkien chooses the verb with horseman's precision: the Ringwraith does not merely ride or gallop but digs steel into his mount, converting menace into velocity. The terror of the scene lives in that single syllable — the horse was already great and already aimed, and the spur made it instantaneous. Readers hear the lunge in the consonants: spurred strikes like the rowel itself.

Core meaning in detail: Literally, to spur is to apply the spur — the U-shaped metal frame buckled to a boot heel, armed with a star-like rowel or a plain prick — to the horse's side just behind the girth. Done rightly it is a signal, not a wound: schooled horses answer the lightest touch. Done brutally, as here, it is compulsion. The past tense spurred covers the single act (he spurred his horse), the sustained urging (they spurred on through the night), and the whole figurative empire the image has conquered.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Urged a horse on with spurs (primary literal sense, from Old English): he spurred his great horse at me. The oldest and still the most vivid sense — steel, speed, and command fused in one gesture.
  • Incited or stimulated into action (dominant figurative sense): spurred by hunger into stealing, spurred to protest by the new tax. The bridge is the rider's heel carried into psychology: ambition, fear, or love plays the rowel, and the human horse leaps forward.
  • Accelerated growth or activity (of economies and trends): the discovery spurred investment, cheap steel spurred railway building. The bridge extends incitement from people to markets: demand pricks supply exactly as the heel pricks the flank, and journalists now report spurred rallies as routinely as riders once felt them.
  • Spurred on — kept going under pressure (phrasal extension): they spurred each other on to the summit. The bridge is the long ride: where one spur-stroke starts motion, repeated strokes sustain it, so on adds duration to the original instant.
  • Fitted with spurs (equipment sense): spurred boots, a spurred heel. The bridge turns the verb back on the rider: the boots that do the spurring are themselves described as spurred, adjective shading into inventory.
  • Pierced or ridged like a spur (of anatomy and botany): the spurred petals of the columbine, a spurredcock's leg. The bridge is visual analogy: flower nectar-spurs and roosters' fighting claws share the spur's hooked shape, and naturalists borrowed the rider's word for both.

Register and connotation: spurred is energetic and decisive — the vocabulary of charges, rallies, and turning points. Literally it can horrify (cruelty to horses) or thrill (the cavalry charge); figuratively it is almost always positive, praising whatever supplied the decisive stimulus. Economists, coaches, and historians reach for it when they need causation with drama: nothing merely causes when it could spur. Only the neglected horse answers with pain — every other spurred thing is grateful for the sharpness.
