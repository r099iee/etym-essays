hauled — past tense and past participle of the verb haul: to pull or drag with great effort, force, or difficulty; to move something heavy by sustained muscular exertion; to transport or convey a load over distance. In the card's context, 'hauled back on the reins' describes a desperate, powerful yank: the woman throws her full body weight backward through the straps, converting all her strength into a sudden braking command that snatches the horse up and around. It is the verb of sailors, carters, and drovers — the plain, muscular language of dragging heavy loads — and its coarse, workmanlike force contrasts tellingly with the martial elegance of 'wheeled' and 'charged': the haul is raw effort, the physical prelude to the horse's violent pivot. Nothing about hauling is graceful or effortless; the word insists on the cost of the action.
  • Sense 1 — pull / drag with effort: 'haul a rope,' 'haul a net aboard,' 'haul a cart out of the mud.'
  • Sense 2 — transport a load: 'haul freight,' 'haul coal,' 'haul gravel' — the workhorse verb of trucking and logistics.
  • Sense 3 — haul (noun), a catch / take: 'a good haul of fish,' 'the thieves' haul' — what is dragged in at one effort.
  • Sense 4 — nautical: 'haul on a line,' 'haul up the sail,' 'haul around' (of wind, to veer).
  • Sense 5 — figurative endurance: 'the long haul' (a long, demanding effort), 'hauled over the coals' (severely scolded), 'haul someone up' (to bring to account).
The modern senses grow from one physical root: the sailor's and carter's act of dragging something heavy with sustained, full-body effort. The noun 'haul' extends the verb in the seventeenth century to name what is dragged in at one pull — a catch of fish, a load of loot — and by the nineteenth century a 'big haul' is the day's takings, honest or stolen. The figurative senses keep the verb's core insistence that effort is real and costs something: 'the long haul' is the race that is run slowly because the load is heavy. In the mounted combat of this card, the word performs its oldest work — the raw, body-committed tug of one fighting momentum with nothing but leverage, sinew, and will. reins — plural noun (singular rein): the long, narrow leather straps fitted at one end to the bit in a horse's mouth and held at the other end in the rider's hands, by which the animal is guided, checked, stopped, and turned. The plural is idiomatic: the object is conceived as a matched pair, one strap to each side of the bit, and the pair is one instrument of command. The rein is the most direct mechanical link between human intention and equine motion — a single pull converts the will into pressure on the sensitive corners of the horse's mouth, and the tautness and angle of the two straps govern speed, direction, and obedience. In the card's context, the woman 'hauled back on the reins' — the verb's brute force applied to the word's controlling function — and the phrase shows the rein at work: the braking, the wheel, the charge, the strike all flow from the tension she puts through the straps. The word's figurative life is as old as its literal one: from the fifteenth century, ruling has been imagined as holding the reins.
  • to take the reins — to assume command.
  • the reins of government / power — political or managerial authority.
  • to hand over the reins — to yield authority to a successor.
  • a free rein — unrestricted liberty of action.
  • to rein in / rein back — to curb, restrain, or hold back.
These figurative senses are not modern slang but the word's oldest companions in English: from the fifteenth century, ruling has been imagined as holding the reins, and the metaphor hardened into the standard vocabulary of command. The semantic bridge is immediate and physical — steering a horse is the most vivid everyday image of control — and the metaphor branches into management ('taking the reins of a company'), self-restraint ('reining in one's temper'), and diplomacy ('a free rein to negotiate'). In the technical language of riding, the word also forms compounds: curb rein, bearing rein, check rein, double reins, and side reins. The word's literal home is the stable, but its daily life in speech is the master figure of command by direct, hand-held restraint.
