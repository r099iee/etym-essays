Gladsome carries sunshine inside it. Its first half, glad, does not originally mean happy at all in the modern interior sense; it means shining. PIE *ghel-, to shine or gleam, is one of the great light roots of Indo-European, yielding gold, yellow, glass, glimmer, glance, glow, and glad alike. Proto-Germanic *gladaz meant smooth and shining, then by natural metaphor bright in mood: a shining face is a happy face. Old English glæd still holds both: bright, as in glad sunshine, and joyful, as in glad heart. The bridge is facial and meteorological at once: brightness without and brightness within.

Old Norse glathr, Old High German glat, Gothic glaths preserved the same double sense of shining and willing. In Middle English glad settles toward cheerful, willing, pleased — I am glad to help — while keeping a poetic brightness. The suffix -some continues Old English -sum, cognate with German -sam and the adjective same, meaning characterized by or tending to: winsome, buxom, lonesome, wholesome. Added to glad in the 13th century, it forms gladsome, full of gladness, actively radiating joy rather than merely feeling it. The formation parallels gamesome, blithesome, winsome, all slightly literary words for exuberant qualities.

  • Medieval cheer: Early attestations around 1300 describe gladsome songs, gladsome days, gladsome tidings — outward, musical, communal joy. It belongs to carols, romances, and psalms, not private moods. To be gladsome is to show it.
  • Reformation and hymnody: Translators love gladsome because glad alone is too flat for worship. Gladsome hymns, gladsome light, gladsome Easter fill 16th- and 17th-century devotion. The King James tradition prefers joyful and glad, but hymn-writers keep gladsome for its singing vowels and archaic warmth.
  • Sound shape: No dramatic consonant shift; g-l-d survives intact from Proto-Germanic. The Great Vowel Shift shortens the a to /æ/ in glad, and -some reduces to /səm/. The whole /ˈɡlædsəm/ trips lightly, suiting its meaning. Spelling keeps d-s-m cluster without assimilation.
  • Victorian literary use: By Dickens's time gladsome is already slightly old-fashioned, which is why he chooses it. Nobody ever stopped him with gladsome looks means nobody ever beamed Christian goodwill at Scrooge. Modern cheerful or friendly would be flat; gladsome evokes carollers, church bells, and hearty handshakes. Its very archaism accuses Scrooge: he exiles himself even from old-fashioned kindness.
  • Modern retreat: In the 20th century gladsome recedes to hymns, poetry, and deliberate period style. Everyday speech prefers glad, happy, cheerful, joyful. Dictionaries now label it literary or archaic, but it survives precisely because no exact synonym keeps its outward-beaming, almost shining force.

To call looks gladsome is therefore to say more than friendly. Friendly can be polite; gladsome is luminous, unguarded, delighted to see you. Etymology explains the excess: a gladsome face still gleams with the old PIE light. From shine to joy to song, the word never darkened, only grew quaint, and Dickens uses its quaint glow to show what Scrooge never receives.
