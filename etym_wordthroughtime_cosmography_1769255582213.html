- 14th-century entry - Taken into Middle English (via Latin and Old French) as the medieval encyclopaedic 'writing of the world': one science holding astronomy and geography together.
- 15th-16th-century golden age - The word crowns Renaissance learning: Munster's 'Cosmographia' (1544) and Mercator's 'Atlas' (1595) make the cosmographer the master of the whole world-picture, wonder-charged and scholastic.
- 17th-18th-century decline - Astronomy and geography split into separate mathematical sciences, and cosmography's unified scope unravels; the word grows antiquarian and fades from practical use.
- 19th century to today - It survives mainly in the history of science and in reverent or poetic uses; modern English reaches for the narrower 'geography' and 'astronomy' and treats cosmography as a noble, obsolete discipline.
