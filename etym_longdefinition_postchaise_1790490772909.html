postchaise (also spelled post-chaise, French-influenced post chaise) was the fast, fashionable travelling carriage of 18th- and early-19th-century Britain and France: a closed, four-wheeled, horse-drawn vehicle hired with relays of horses at posting inns to carry one to three passengers swiftly over long distances. In the source passage the father could command a postchaise and pair for two months in the summer — that is, he hired by the season a chaise with two horses (a pair) from Long Acre, London's carriage-making quarter, had it fitted with a child's bracket seat, and toured England, Wales, and lowland Scotland calling on country customers. The postchaise here is both transport and viewing machine: four windows giving a panoramic opening on high-roads and cross-roads, the very school of Ruskin's eye for landscape, architecture, and geology.

Technically a postchaise differed from a stagecoach, mail coach, and private coach. A stage ran to timetable for many fares; a mail carried letters under guard; a gentleman's coach was owned and kept. A postchaise was hired chaise-plus-post-horses: you paid per mile and per horse, changed teams every 8–12 miles at a posting house, and posted — travelled by relays — at 7–10 miles an hour, sleeping in inns. Bodies were light, hung on thorough-braces, with a driver's box or with a postilion riding the near horse; later patterns added a rumble for servants. To travel post was to travel privately, quickly, and expensively, the Regency equivalent of hiring a car with driver. Austen's heroines elope or flee by postchaise; Dickens's Pickwick chases Jingle by postchaise; highwaymen stop postchaises because they carry money.

  • Hired relay carriage (head and source sense): e.g. to order a postchaise and pair, to travel post to York, chaise-and-pair. The emphasis is on hire and relays: fresh horses, posting inns, turnpike tickets, postboys' tips. The bridge from chaise (the shell) to postchaise (the system) is service: the vehicle plus the posting network.
  • Vehicle-type sense: e.g. a yellow postchaise, a postchaise with the blinds down. Here it names the shape: compact, glazed, door-windowed, often yellow-bodied with black lining. Coachbuilders' pattern-books list postchaises, landaus, gigs, curricles as distinct models.
  • Posting-system sense (post- element): e.g. post-horses, postboys, postmasters, posting-house, posting-road. Post here has nothing to do with letters directly but with posts — stations placed at intervals — where horses waited. To post was to ride that infrastructure.
  • Chaise as light carriage (chaise- element): e.g. a one-horse chaise, a Bath chair, a shay (dialectal). Borrowed from French chaise, chair, it first meant a sedan chair, then a light open two-wheeler, then the closed body used for posting. The bridge is seat-to-vehicle: a chair fitted with wheels and a horse.
  • Literary elopement and flight sense: e.g. a Gretna Green postchaise, a runaway postchaise. Because postchaises were private, fast, and available at night, they became the novelists' vehicle for abduction, elopement, pursuit, and escape. The semantic colour is romantic urgency.
  • Obsolete-technology sense today: e.g. a postchaise in a museum, a postchaise inn sign. Railways killed posting by the 1840s–1850s; the word survives in heritage, fiction, and pub names. Modern readers meet it as period flavour, like turnpike and ostler.

Ruskin's detail is exact social history. To hire regularly for the two months out of Long Acre meant dealing with the West End job-masters who stabled, coachbuilt, and let by the month; to have it bracketed and pocketed as we liked meant fitting a child's jump-seat and map pockets. The four windows made the chaise a moving panorama — the very word he uses — anticipating the railway carriage window and the cinema. What was transport for the father was education for the son: high-roads, cathedrals, inns, and the Abbot read at Kinross between stages.
