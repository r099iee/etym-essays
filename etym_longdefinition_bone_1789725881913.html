BONE denotes in its primary concrete sense any of the hard, calcified pieces forming the vertebrate skeleton: the femur is the longest bone, ribs guard the chest, the jawbone hinges speech. By extension it names the substance itself (bone china, bone meal), a remaining morsel of contention, and in plural the mortal remains (to lay one’s bones in native soil). The register is everyday and bodily, at once nursery-simple and surgical-precise, carrying connotations of hardness, permanence, and essential structure — to get down to the bone is to reach what cannot be stripped further, to feel something in one’s bones is to know it viscerally beyond argument.

In the immediate context, BONE is not anatomical at all but adverbial-intensifying inside a fixed idiom: Don Samuel goes as far as to describe him as talented, though ______ idle, but notes that he was an avid reader, and literally, to quote Samuel, ‘sated himself in books’. Here bone idle means utterly, incorrigibly idle, idle to the very marrow. Samuel concedes talent while diagnosing total indolence at schoolwork, immediately qualified by obsessive private reading. The idiom is affectionately damning schoolmaster slang: the boy would do nothing he was told, yet gorged himself on what he chose. Bone supplies the completeness — idle through and through, not merely a little lazy.

  • Primary sense: skeletal piece and substance. A broken bone, bone density, bone marrow, bone graft, to break a bone, to gnaw a bone. Semantic core is hard supporting structure. Bridge to idioms is that bone stands for the deepest, most durable part of a person or thing, so what reaches bone reaches the limit: chilled to the bone, cut to the bone, bare bones, bone of contention (a bone thrown between dogs to fight over).
  • Secondary sense: intensifier meaning completely, thoroughly. Bone idle, bone dry, bone tired, bone weary, bone stupid, bone china? (different formation). In bone idle and bone dry, bone functions like stone, stark, dead in stone cold, stark naked, dead certain — a hard noun bleached into an adverb of totality. Bridge: bones are the innermost residue when flesh is gone, so bone-X means X to the innermost degree, with faintly comic hyperbole. OED dates bone idle to the nineteenth century as school and naval slang, bone dry to prohibition America for utterly alcohol-free counties.
  • Tertiary senses: remains, dice, corsetry, and slang. Bones for mortal remains and for dice (to roll the bones), bone for a fishbone, wishbone, or trombone player’s bones, to bone up on to cram study, to have a bone to pick to raise a grievance, to feel in one’s bones, to make no bones about to speak plainly (from diced bones without scruple). Each preserves hardness or residue: dice were literally knucklebones, grievances are bones to gnaw, plain speech throws bones aside. Modern slang bonehead, boneheaded keeps bone as emblem of thickness and stupidity.
  • Collocation and tone in the idiom: bone idle almost always predicates persons, especially boys, pupils, and husbands, with amused exasperation rather than clinical diagnosis. It pairs naturally with concessive though and talented though bone idle is almost formulaic in British school memoirs. Antonyms split: for skeleton flesh, tissue; for intensifier slightly, a bit, partly. To call reading avid in the next clause redeems bone idleness as selective passion, not vacancy.

Grammatically BONE in this note is a pre-adjectival modifier, invariable and ungraded (*boner idle), while as a noun it counts freely (a bone, two bones) and compounds tirelessly (backbone, cheekbone, funny bone). Its power lies in bodily immediacy: everyone has felt bone through skin, so bone as limit needs no explanation.
