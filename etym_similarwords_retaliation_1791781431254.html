• revenge: Similar because it is harm returned for harm; differs by being personal and emotional, driven by wounded feeling, whereas retaliation can be institutional, measured and almost administrative.
  • vengeance: Similar in meaning and gravity; differs by its literary, archaic register and its implication of honour or righteous wrath, which retaliation does not carry.
  • retribution: Similar because both answer a wrong with punishment; differs in justification — retribution appeals to desert and justice, retaliation merely to the fact that one was struck first.
  • reprisal: Similar as a return of injury, especially between states; differs because reprisals are acts of armed force or coercion authorised by the law of nations, not personal or workplace payback.
  • requital: Similar because it means repayment of like for like; differs by being neutral or positive as often as negative, and by belonging to a formal, slightly archaic register.
  • payback: Similar as informal payback for a wrong; differs by being colloquial and often gloating, whereas retaliation can be discussed neutrally in law and strategy.
  • tit-for-tat: Similar because it describes reciprocal harm; differs by emphasising the alternating, petty symmetry of exchanges rather than a single deliberate response.
  • counterattack: Similar as a blow struck after being struck; differs by being a military or sporting manoeuvre aimed at seizing the initiative, not a punishment for a wrong.
  • countermeasure: Similar as a response to hostile action; differs by being technical and defensive — a measure designed to neutralise an attack rather than to inflict equivalent harm.
  • reciprocation: Similar in the logic of returning like for like; differs because it usually concerns favours, courtesy or trade concessions, and carries no suggestion of punishment.
  • comeuppance: Similar as a return of deserved harm; differs by being informal and moralising, implying that the target finally got what was always owed to them.
