DESPONDING — The Promise Given Away The lineage of desponding begins at an altar. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *spend- meant "to pour a libation" — to tip wine, oil, or blood from a cup as an offering to the gods — and from that physical act of pouring grew an entire vocabulary of solemn obligation. In Greek the same root produced spéndō, "I pour a libation," and the noun spondḗ, "a libation"; and because no treaty between Greek cities was ratified without a communal drink-offering, the plural spondaí came to mean "a truce, a treaty" — the covenant itself was the shared pouring. Even the metre that accompanied such ceremonies left a fossil in English prosody: the spondee, the foot of two long syllables, takes its name from the spondeîos, "of the libation." On the Italic side the root gave Latin spondeō, spondēre, "to promise solemnly, to pledge oneself, to answer for another" — a verb of oath and guarantee whose reach is astonishing. From it descend English sponsor, spouse and espouse (through the betrothed spōnsa), respond and responsibility, and the whole family of words in which a promise is given and must be kept. The crucial event for our word is a compound. Latin joined spondēre to the prefix dē-, "away, down," producing dēspondēre, "to promise away, to give up, to resign." Originally the verb had a concrete, almost contractual sense: a father could dēspondēre a daughter — betroth her, "promise her away" in marriage — and a man could dēspondēre a claim or a right, formally relinquishing it. This is the vocabulary of Roman law and of the stipulatio, the formal question-and-answer contract in which an obligation was created by solemn promise; to despond something was to hand it over by pledge. The decisive semantic shift happened in a fixed phrase: dēspondēre animum, "to give up one's spirit or heart." Used by Terence, Livy, and Seneca, the expression meant precisely "to lose heart, to despair" — the picture being of the animus, one's vital soul and courage, solemnly promised away and abandoned, much as a bride was handed over in betrothal or a claim was legally surrendered. The concrete senses of the root (pouring out, pledging, handing over) were absorbed into an inner drama: despair became an act of formal surrender, a resignation of hope as deliberate and complete as the renunciation of a right. It is a striking testimony to the Latin habit of thinking of inner states as obligations — you do not merely fall into despair; you give your heart away. English received the word as a learned borrowing in the seventeenth century. The verb despond, attested from the 1650s, meant "to lose heart, to give up hope," and from it English immediately formed desponding, its present participle, and despondency, the noun of the state. The participle was a favourite of the moral and essayistic prose of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, where it named not merely personal grief but public and political defeatism — "desponding" calculations about trade, "desponding" views of the age, the surrender of confidence in human progress. It is in exactly this register that Macaulay uses the word in the sentence that frames this card: the man informed of the past, he argues, will not take a "morose or desponding view of the present," because history itself refutes the pessimist. The modern form of the word has hardened. Today the settled adjective is despondent, and the noun is despondency; the participle desponding survives chiefly in literary and formal writing, valued precisely because it pictures hope in the very act of failing rather than as a finished state. The word has thus travelled from the pouring of wine at a sacrificial altar, through the treaty-ratifications of Greek city-states and the contractual pledges of Roman law, to the internal psychology of despair — and finally to the moral-historical vocabulary of the nineteenth century, where a whole age could be accused of desponding. At every stage the root's meaning is the same: a solemn giving-away. From the drink-offering to the surrendered heart, desponding has always meant the moment the promise is let go.
