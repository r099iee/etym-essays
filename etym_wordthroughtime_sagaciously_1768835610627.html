- Early 17th century – 'sagacious' enters English directly from Latin sagāx, literally 'keen-scented', and the adverb 'sagaciously' follows at once, trailing the earlier noun 'sagacity' (16th century, via Old French); in early modern usage the family is the vocabulary of the sage and the tracker — judgment conceived as keen scent, the mind working as a hound's nose works.
- 17th–18th centuries – 'sagaciously' thrives in elevated philosophical and political prose, describing prudent, far-seeing judgment in statecraft and letters; wholly positive and dignified, the adverb of the counsellor and the commander.
- 19th century – the word recedes from everyday speech and survives in formal and literary registers; its sensory root begins to feel archaic — discernment as a trail followed, a hunting metaphor nearly worn smooth.
- 20th–21st centuries – 'sagaciously' is now consciously literary and often ironic: used of shrewdness exercised upon the petty and the trivial, as when Peake's dusting-man peers 'sagaciously over his glasses down the length of the hall' — sagacity without an object, a hunter's instinct with nothing left to hunt.
