- 16th c. (1560s) — Borrowed into Early Modern English from Middle French clandestin and Latin clandestīnus, applied predominantly to unauthorized, secret marriages contracted without banns or parental consent ("clandestine marriages").
- 17th c. — Broadened during the English Civil War, Commonwealth, and Restoration to describe political cabals, subversive sedition, and illegal religious conventicles held beyond the reach of the Crown.
- 18th c. — Frequently employed in maritime and economic legal records to characterize contraband smuggling, tariff evasion, and illicit colonial commerce bypassing the Navigation Acts.
- 19th c. — Popularized in Victorian fiction, gothic romance, and revolutionary socialist pamphlets to describe forbidden amorous rendezvous, private diaries, and secret carbonari brotherhoods across Europe.
- 20th c. — Formalized in military and intelligence doctrine throughout World War II and the Cold War to designate underground resistance movements, clandestine radio stations, and black operations shielded from hostile observation.
- 21st c. — Expanded into cyber warfare, digital cryptography, and dark-web networks to describe hidden botnets, encrypted data exfiltration, and stealth cyber operations eluding digital forensic detection.
