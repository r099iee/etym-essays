The noun verbiage is a latecomer to English, yet it carries within itself an entire history of suspicion about language — the ancient and abiding fear that words, piled up, become a substance that hides meaning instead of revealing it. Its story runs from the Indo-European root for "speaking," through the classical Latin word for "a word," into a French coinage that means literally "a heap of words," and at last into the English of editors, judges, and plain-style reformers. The Indo-European core: a root for speaking.
  • At the very base lies the Indo-European root *werh₁-, meaning "to speak, say, utter." It is one of the oldest word-families for vocal language in the family, and it branched in every major daughter language. In its extended form *werdh- it produced the Germanic family: Proto-Germanic *wurda-, Old English word, and modern English word itself — so that the everyday word "word" and the learned word "verbiage" are ultimately cousins, both descended from the same ancient root for speaking.
  • In Greek the same *werh₁- base produced rhēma, "a word, a saying," and rhētōr, "an orator," the source of English rhetoric and rhetorician — the art of using words well. The same root thus stands behind both the practice of eloquence (rhetoric) and the criticism of its abuse (verbiage).
  • The branch that matters directly for verbiage is the Latin one. Latin verbum, "a word, an utterance, the verb," is the direct outcome of *werh₁- in Italic. In Classical Latin the word was entirely neutral: verbum meant simply "word," as in the proverb verbum sapienti sat est, "a word to the wise is enough," or in the phrase "not a word" (nihil verborum). There was no pejorative colouring at all.
Latin verbum and its English children.
  • Latin verbum has been extraordinarily productive in English, but almost entirely through learned borrowing. English took verb (the grammatical category, from Latin verbum), verbal, verbatim ("word for word," from the Latin adverb verbatim), proverb (from proverbium, "a set saying"), adverb, and the whole family of words in which verb- appears.
  • All of these kept the neutral, even dignified sense of the Latin original. The critical sense of "too many words" was NOT a Latin inheritance — it is a specifically French creation.
The French mediation: verbiage is born.
  • Old French carried Latin verbum into Romance as verbe, meaning both "a word" and "the verb." French then built a new word on this stem by attaching the suffix -iage, which descends from Latin -āticum and denotes a collection, quantity, or state of something. The same suffix gives French feuillage, "a mass of leaves," and the English borrowings of such formations.
  • The coinage verbiage thus literally meant "a mass or heap of words." Already in French, from its first attestations in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the word carried the pejorative colouring it would keep forever: not "words" but an excessive, diffuse, redundant abundance of them — speech and writing that multiply words without multiplying meaning.
  • This is the crucial fact of the word's history: the disapproval is native to the French coinage, not imported later. Where Latin verbum was a humble, honest word, French verbiage was born as a weapon of style-criticism, and it has stayed one ever since.
Into English: the eighteenth-century borrowing.
  • English borrowed verbiage in the early eighteenth century (the first attestations cluster in the 1720s), during the Age of Enlightenment, when English prose was being disciplined by critics, essayists, and editors who prized clarity, brevity, and plainness. The word arrived as a ready-made term of literary censure, and it was used almost immediately against pompous official and scholarly writing.
  • Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century usage cemented the word's critical register. Reviewers attacked the "verbiage" of dull poets, essayists complained of the "verbiage" of parliamentarians and judges, and lexicographers defined it simply as an excess of words.
  • The word found an ideal target in the expanding official world of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. "Legal verbiage," "bureaucratic verbiage," "official verbiage," and later "corporate verbiage" became fixed phrases, and verbiage became the standard term of complaint of the plain-language movement, which campaigns against the padded, opaque prose of institutions.
The double edge in this card's context.
  • The sentence that anchors this card uses the word with unusual subtlety. It says Mill's feeling "is not afraid of abundant verbiage" — conceding, with a kind of admiration, that the intensity of his emotion expressed itself in a copious flood of prose, while at the same time marking that flood as verbiage, surplus words that a more disciplined writer (or a more self-conscious one) would have cut.
  • The passage goes on to record the criticism of Mill's friend Grote, that only Mill's reputation could survive such displays — that is, that the verbiage was a luxury only a great man's standing could absorb. The word thus carries the full historical weight of its meaning: copious, sincere, yet excessive language, whose very abundance endangers the writer who indulges it.
Modern life.
  • In contemporary English verbiage remains everyday and pejorative. "Cut the verbiage" is a standard demand for brevity; editors strip "unnecessary verbiage"; stylistic guides condemn it. Its plural is rare, its tone is constant.
  • The word now also feeds a small family of coinages — "verbiage-free," "non-verbiage" — used in writing guides, and it sits alongside its cousins verbose and verbosity in the vocabulary of style-criticism.
Conclusion. Verbiage is the rare word whose history is a history of criticism: born in French as a label for a heap of words, borrowed into English in the age of plain prose, and used ever since to name the surplus language that obscures meaning. From the Indo-European root *werh₁-, through neutral Latin verbum, to the condemnatory French coinage, it has always been the word that reminds us that words are valuable only when they mean something.
