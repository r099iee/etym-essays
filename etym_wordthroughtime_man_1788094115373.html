- Old English mann/mon(n): the general word for any human being of either sex ('woman' = wīfmann, 'female person'); also 'servant, vassal' and 'mankind'
- 13th–14th centuries: the noun narrows — man comes to mean chiefly an adult male, while the generic sense survives in compounds (mankind, manhood) and proverbs; 'woman' separates from the paradigm
- c. 1450: the noun spawns the verb 'to man' — first of ships ('man the fleet', i.e. provide crews), then of fortifications and posts: the operational sense is born
- 16th–17th centuries: the generic use flourishes in philosophy and scripture ('what is man?'), while 'the Man' appears in Tudor slang for an authority figure, and 'man and wife' fixes the husband sense in the marriage service
- 18th–19th centuries: the verb expands — 'man the pumps', 'man the lifeboats', 'man the barricades' — emergency and military rhetoric make 'man' a verb of collective action; 'to a man' and 'man of the world' settle as idioms
- 20th century: the generic noun comes under fire — style guides from the 1970s argue 'man' is not truly neutral, so 'chairman'→'chairperson', 'man-hours'→'person-hours'; 'man up' (c. 1900s, revived 2000s) and 'the Man' (1960s counterculture) add slang layers
- Today: the verb is standard and unmarked ('man the desk'); the male noun is everyday; the generic use is formal-or-archaic and increasingly avoided — one of the clearest recorded cases of a word's social perception shifting in living memory
