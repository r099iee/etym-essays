Prodding: a blunt instrument of unknown descent.

The word prod is a blunt instrument of unknown descent. It appears in English in the sixteenth century as a noun meaning "a pointed instrument" — an implement for pricking and driving beasts — and within a century it had generated the verb to prod, to poke insistently with such an instrument. Its origin is genuinely obscure: no Indo-European ancestor can be securely reconstructed for it, and the etymology must therefore begin honestly with a question mark. Scholars have pointed to the Scandinavian dialect word prodda, "to stab, to poke," recorded in Norwegian dialects, and it is tempting to treat this as the source; but the connection has never been proven, and it is quite possible that the English word was independently echoic — a stabby little syllable whose very sound, prod, mimics the short, quick, irritating thrust it names. What is certain is the word's career. From the pointed drover's stick it spread to figurative use — to prod a person is to stimulate or provoke them by insistent small pressure — and in the twentieth century it acquired its most famous concrete form, the cattle prod, an electric instrument that makes the ancient drover's goad modern and impersonal. In the passage, the earthmen are "prodding their vortlups," and the participle captures the exact register of the word: mechanical, repetitive, businesslike, faintly comic — the routine jab of exasperated handlers rather than the violence of an attack. Prodding names the small, persistent pressure that gets a reluctant thing moving, and its whole etymology, uncertain as it is, points the same way: to urge by poking, patiently and insistently, until the sluggish body obeys.
Goads: an ancient word of driving.

The goad has a far older and better-certified pedigree than prod. Its story reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵhei-, "to drive, impel, set in motion," the same root that in Vedic Sanskrit produced hinoti, "he impels, urges, drives," and in Greek hīēmi, "to send, let go, release." From this root, with a dental extension (*ǵhei-d-), Germanic built Proto-Germanic *gaidō, "a goad, a pointed driving stick," whose Old English descendant is gād, the drover's and shepherd's pointed stick — recorded in Ælfric's glosses and in the pastoral law of Anglo-Saxon England, and used not only for cattle but as a spear-point in battle. The connection of *gaidō to *ǵhei- is traditional but debated: Etymonline follows it, while the OED marks the origin of goad "obscure," so the chain is a strong hypothesis rather than a certainty; if the link is rejected, the word's certain history begins only with Proto-Germanic. From Old English gād came Middle English goode and gōde, and modern goad, "an iron-tipped stick for driving cattle." The word kept its concrete, brutal usefulness for a thousand years and then sprouted a rich figurative life: to goad someone into action, the goad of ambition, a taunt that goads — in the seventeenth century the gadfly joined the family, a fly that stings cattle as a goad would. In the passage the goads are explicitly "iron-pointed," the tools of command that bridge human urgency and animal inertia. And the word still carries the full weight of its history: a goad is not a subtle instrument but a pointed, insistent prod — the harsh mechanical link between the driver's will and the driven beast, at once physical and moral, from the cattle of Anglo-Saxon England to the vortlups of Mars. The pairing of the two words in the passage is itself a history of English: prod, the sixteenth-century stranger of unknown birth, and goad, the ancient native word of the Anglo-Saxon fields, working side by side in the same sentence — one uncertain and modern, one deep-rooted and old — each insisting, with a point, that the sluggish monster move.
