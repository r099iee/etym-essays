mortified: (1) caused to feel acute embarrassment, shame, or humiliation; (2) literally deadened, or caused to die (of flesh or tissue); (3) in religious usage, having one's bodily desires subdued by self-discipline. Primary sense — acute shame and embarrassment. In modern everyday English mortified means deeply, painfully embarrassed: "I was mortified when I realized my mistake," "she was mortified to have forgotten his name." The feeling is stronger and more crushing than ordinary embarrassment — a blush of humiliation so intense that the sufferer feels figuratively "dead" with shame. This is the dominant sense today and the one in Jane Austen's line, where Elizabeth says Darcy's pride has "mortified" her own. Secondary literal sense — deadened flesh (medicine). The older, strictly literal meaning is "made dead": mortified flesh or tissue is dead, gangrenous, or necrotic. A surgeon may speak of a mortified limb; the OED's long record of this sense ties the word to putrefaction and the failure of living tissue. This is where the metaphor of shame begins: the emotional "death" of the self when its pride is destroyed. Tertiary sense — religious mortification of the flesh. In Christian ascetic tradition, "to mortify the flesh" means to put to death or subdue the body's sinful desires and appetites through fasting, vigils, penance, and self-denial, so that the spirit may live. This is a technical-theological sense, drawn from Scripture ("mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth," Colossians 3:5), and it was central to medieval monasticism. The word still appears in religious and formal writing: "the priest mortified his flesh through fasting." Extended sense — to crush or destroy a feeling. Between the physical and religious senses lies a broad figurative use: to mortify a pride, vanity, hope, or ambition is to strike it such a blow that it "dies." This is the historical bridge — Austen's Elizabeth says Darcy has mortified her pride, not her person; her self-esteem has been dealt a killing blow. Full polysemy map (all senses, historical and modern):
  • Acute embarrassment, humiliation, chagrin — modern dominant sense.
  • To deaden tissue; gangrenous, necrotic — medical/literal.
  • To subdue or kill bodily desires through self-discipline — religious ascetic.
  • To crush a feeling (pride, hope, vanity) — figurative, 18th–19th-century literary.
  • Historical: literally "to kill, put to death" — 14th–17th-century, now obsolete except in religious/medical remnants.
The semantic bridge. Latin mors "death" + facere "to make" gives mortificare "to make dead." The chain of meaning moves in stages: (1) to kill the body's sinful desires (asceticism), (2) to kill living tissue (gangrene), (3) to kill pride or vanity (metaphor), (4) to feel as if one were dead with shame (psychology). Every modern use of mortified is a fossilized piece of this escalating metaphor. Register and collocation.
  • "Mortified to …" / "mortified that …" — everyday, intense embarrassment.
  • "Mortified flesh/limb" — medical-historical, now mostly replaced by necrotic or gangrenous.
  • "Mortify the flesh" — religious-formal, ascetic.
  • Register: mortified is more intense than embarrassed, slightly less formal than humiliated, and distinct from ashamed (which implies moral guilt rather than social exposure).
  • Related family: mortal, mortality, immortal, mortuary, mortician, rigor mortis, mortgage (the "death pledge"), post-mortem — all descendants of the same death-root.
