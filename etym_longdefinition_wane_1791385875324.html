The verb wane denotes a gradual, steady decrease or diminution in magnitude, intensity, illumination, power, influence, or vitality. Unlike abrupt cessations or sudden collapses, waning represents an organic, progressive receding of strength or presence over time, frequently following a preceding period of culmination, fullness, or zenith. In the source sentence ("I found the debonair gaiety with which I had embarked on this affair beginning to __________ a trifle"), P. G. Wodehouse uses the verb to capture the slow, involuntary deflation of Bertie Wooster’s buoyant aristocratic high spirits as the grim reality of an impending awkward encounter sinks in.

Historically and scientifically, the prototypical anchor of the word lies in astronomy: the periodic decrease in the visible illuminated surface of the moon between the full moon and the new moon (the waning moon). This powerful celestial cycle provided human language with an indelible metaphor for transience, the wheel of fortune, and the inevitable fading of human glory, political supremacy, physical vigor, or emotional fervor.

  • Primary Sense — Astronomical Diminution (Lunar Phases): To decrease gradually in the extent of illuminated surface visible from Earth. In this domain, it forms an ancient, universal antonymic pair with wax (from Old English weaxan, "to grow, increase"). A moon is described as waning during the roughly two-week period as it progresses through waning gibbous, third quarter, and waning crescent phases toward the darkness of the new moon.
  • Secondary Sense — Gradual Decline of Power, Authority, or Prestige: Applied widely in historical, political, and sociological analyses to describe the ebbing strength of empires, dynasties, institutions, or leaders. For example: "British imperial dominance began to wane in the aftermath of the First World War." It implies not an immediate destruction, but a relentless internal exhaustion or diminishing global leverage.
  • Tertiary Sense — Fading of Emotions, Interest, and Energy: In psychological and social discourse, describes the slow cooling of enthusiasm, passion, courage, health, or attention. One speaks of waning enthusiasm among voters, waning appetite in illness, or waning interest in a cultural fad. This usage emphasizes an internal depletion of drive rather than external obstruction.
  • Temporal Sense — Drawing Toward an End: Applied to units of time, daylight, seasons, or life stages: "as the afternoon waned," "in the waning days of autumn," or "the waning years of the nineteenth century." It evokes a gentle, melancholy twilight quality, emphasizing the inevitable approach of a conclusion.
  • Noun Usage and Fixed Idiomatic Phrases: While predominantly verbal, wane frequently functions as a noun in fixed prepositional phrases:
    • On the wane: In a state of gradual decline or decay (e.g., "Her popularity was conspicuously on the wane."). This is one of the most frequent modern idioms containing the root.
    • Wax and wane: To undergo cyclical fluctuations between expansion and contraction, alternating between rise and fall (e.g., "Public sympathy tends to wax and wane with each media cycle.").
  • Semantic Distinctions and Boundaries:
    • Differs from ebb, which specifically evokes the tidal flow of water receding from a shoreline and often implies a future, rhythmic return.
    • Differs from dwindle, which strongly focuses on quantity or physical mass shrinking down to an eventual vanishing point or tiny remnant.
    • Differs from fade, which emphasizes the loss of brightness, vividness of color, or clarity of sound.
    • Differs from plummet or crash, which denote catastrophic, violent, and rapid drops rather than smooth, natural decline.
  • Grammatical and Morphological Characteristics: Primarily an intransitive verb (e.g., "His strength waned."). Rare transitive usages in Old and Early Modern English meaning "to cause to diminish" or "to curtail" are now completely obsolete. The present participle waning functions widely as an attributive adjective (the waning light, waning support).
