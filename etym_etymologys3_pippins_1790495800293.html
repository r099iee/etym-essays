pippins begins not in an orchard but in a seed. Old French pepin meant a pip — the hard seed of an apple, pear, grape or melon — and Middle English pipin meant the apple grown from such a seed, as opposed to one propagated by grafting a known variety. Every planted pip is a gamble: bees cross-pollinate, seedlings revert, and most yield sour crabs. Once in a generation a pip yields a sweet, keeping, aromatic novelty worth naming, cutting and grafting. That novelty is a pippin, and its name remembers the gamble.

The deeper ancestry of French pepin is disputed, and honest etymology must say so. The traditional account derives it from Latin pepo, peponis (melon, pumpkin), itself from Greek pepon (ripe, cooked by the sun, hence ripe melon), via a Vulgar Latin diminutive *pepinus (little seed, melon-seed). Phonologically Latin pepo /pepoː/ to French pepin /pəpɛ̃/ requires suffixation with -inus and nasalisation, which is plausible but unattested: no text writes *pepinus. The rival account treats pepin as expressive, built on a nursery syllable *pip- for a small round thing (cf. English pip, pimple, pigeon?, French piper, pépin), with no Latin parent at all. Etymonline and the OED mark the origin uncertain; the borrowing into English, by contrast, is dated and solid: Anglo-Norman charters and garden treatises write pipin, pypyn from the late 14th century for a choice apple.

  • Medieval seedling to Tudor cultivar (1300-1600): early pipins are any seedling apples, often sour. Husbandmen learn to top-graft the lucky sweet seedling onto crab stock, fixing it vegetatively. By the 16th century named pippins circulate: French Pippin, Golden Pippin, Holland Pippin. The Great Vowel Shift and doubling of -pp- stabilise spelling as pippin /ˈpɪpɪn/, plural pippins. French keeps pépin for the seed, English transfers it to the fruit — a metonymy gardeners understand instantly.
  • Golden age of the pippin (1650-1850): Ribston Pippin (raised from a Normandy pip at Ribston Hall, Yorkshire, c. 1707), Golden Pippin, and in America Newtown Pippin (found as a chance seedling in Newtown, Long Island, early 18th c., exported as Albemarle Pippin) make pippin synonymous with dessert quality. Pomologies list dozens; nurseries sell grafts; cider is left to bitter-sweets and crabs. Pronunciation is stable; compounds multiply: pippin-face, pippin-hearted, orange-pippin.
  • Slang leap to persons (c. 1800-1920): sporting men, then theatre and film, call a first-rate thing a pippin: a pretty girl, a fast horse, a telling shot, a rich joke. American Westerns and Wodehouse keep it archly alive. The semantic path is gustatory metaphor, the same that gives peach, plum, cherry, apple of one’s eye: sweetness eaten becomes sweetness admired. Ruskin’s moralised sweet pippins stands halfway between orchard and slang — horticulture as ethics.
  • Modern heritage survival: commercial orchards replace pippin names with Cox, Gala, Fuji, but all are pippins by descent (Cox’s Orange Pippin is literally a pippin raised by Richard Cox from a Ribston pip c. 1830). Apple days, National Trust orchards and American heirloom societies restore the word to labels. The plural pippins now sounds period and wholesome, which is why Ruskin’s sentence still tastes fresh.
