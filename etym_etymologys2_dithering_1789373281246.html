PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*dʰ- / *da-
"imitative / expressive base for trembling, fluttering, shaking"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE – 1st c. CE)
*did- / *dud-
"to shake, quiver, vibrate"
Note: Onomatopoeic frequentative base cognate with English dodder, totter, and daze.
↓
Middle English (c. 14th–15th c.)
didderen / dydder
"to tremble, quake, shiver with cold"
↓
Early Modern English (Northern Dialects) (c. 16th–17th c.)
didder
"to shake, chatter with cold, quiver"
↓
Modern English (Regional to Standard) (c. mid-to-late 18th c., c. 1770s)
dither
"to tremble, quiver; (intervocalic [d] > [ð] shift)"
Note: Phonetic softening of medial [d] to voiced dental fricative [ð], parallel to father, weather, and gather.
↓
Modern English (c. early 19th c., c. 1820s)
dither (psychological sense)
"to vacillate, hesitate, act indecisively"
↓
Modern English (Technical DSP & Engineering) (c. 1940s–1960s)
dither / dithering
"adding deliberate stochastic noise to eliminate quantization error"
↓
