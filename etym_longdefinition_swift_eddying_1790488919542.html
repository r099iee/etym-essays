swift-eddying is a hyphenated participial adjective meaning moving swiftly while forming eddies: of water that runs fast and at the same time turns back on itself in swirls, whirlpools, and回流. In the source sentence — which ran past it, clear-brown over the pebbles three or four feet deep; swift-eddying, — an infinite thing for a child to look down into — Ruskin characterises the Tay at Perth as seen through a garden door: shallow enough to see stones, brown with peat yet clear, deep enough to drown, fast in forward rush and locally circling behind stones and pilings. The semicolon and em-dash isolate the word as awed afterthought: swift-eddying — then silence — an infinite thing. The child looks down into moving depth that never repeats, and calls it infinite.

As a compound swift-eddying joins manner (swift: rapid) to motion-shape (eddying: circling). Swift alone covers any quick movement — swift runner, swift reply, swift justice, swift (bird) — while eddying alone covers any circling — eddying water, eddying smoke, eddying crowds, eddying leaves. Together they specify a river regime: spate water over a stony bed, where bulk flow is downstream and fast, but friction spawns upstream-turning vortices that trap leaves, foam, and light. Anglers read such water for salmon lies; children read it for endless pattern; poets read it for time.

  • Core hydrological sense (head sense, Ruskin's sense): fast-circling water: e.g. swift-eddying Tay, swift-eddying pool below the weir, swift-eddying current over pebbles. Literary, precise, visual; implies clarity (one sees the circling), depth (three or four feet), and danger (one could fall in). Near-synonyms fast-swirling, quick-whirling, rapid-churning lack the technical eddy with its back-turning etymology.
  • Swift as independent adjective (to be known for the compound): rapid, prompt, fleet; e.g. swift horse, swift decision, swift decline, swift retribution. From Old English swift perhaps originally turning quickly. Modern jargon adds SWIFT (banking network), swift boat, Taylor Swift (proper name) as homographs learners must separate. The bridge from turning to speed is a swept curve taken fast.
  • Swift as noun (bird): the sooty sickle-winged bird Apus apus, screaming round church towers in summer; e.g. swifts screaming, a screaming party of swifts. Named 1660s from swiftness of flight; earlier applied to fast lizards. Folk held it ill-omened for its shriek. Ruskin, a bird-watcher, would hear the echo: swift-eddying water below, swifts wheeling above.
  • Eddy as noun and verb (to be known for the compound): a circular current turning back against main flow; to turn in such circles; e.g. an eddy behind the pier, leaves eddied in the wind, smoke eddied, crowds eddied round the speaker. From Scottish ydy (15th c.), prefix ed- again, backwards + water. Physics distinguishes eddies (horizontal recirculation), vortices (vertical spiral), and whirlpools (strong vortex); everyday speech mixes them. Figurative eddies are backwaters of history, gossip, and thought.
  • Eddying as participle and adjective: presently circling; e.g. eddying mist, eddying debates, eddying snow. The -ing marks ongoing action, the hyphen in swift-eddying marks joint modification: not swift and eddying separately, but eddying swiftly. Victorian prose loves such double participles — clear-brown, swift-eddying, far-gleaming — to pack sense.
  • Literary and perceptual sense (infinite thing): water that fascinates because it never repeats yet never changes; e.g. Heraclitus' river, Tennyson's brook, Ruskin's Tay. The child sees pebbles fixed and water moving, brown colour and clear seeing, swiftness and return — contraries held in one glance. To call it infinite is not measurement but wonder: inexhaustible pattern.

To master swift-eddying is thus to hold speed and circle together: forward rush that cannot go straight because stones, banks, and depth force it to turn, and turning that cannot rest because fresh water drives it on. The Tay in three feet of depth shows in miniature what oceans show in miles.
