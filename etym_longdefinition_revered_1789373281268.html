Revered is an adjective and the past participle of the transitive verb revere, designating someone or something that is regarded with deep, solemn respect, profound veneration, awe, and deferential honor. Rooted in ancient Indo-European concepts of watchful wariness and developed through classical Roman religious terminology distinguishing holy awe from vulgar terror, the word characterizes figures, masterworks, institutions, or sacred principles that have earned an elevated, almost untouchable status across generations of human admiration.

In literary and cultural criticism—as illustrated in Terence Kilmartin's introductory reflection on C. K. Scott Moncrieff's legendary English translation of Marcel Proust's masterwork—revered represents the highest possible tier in a rising rhetorical triad: "enjoyed and admired, not to say revered." To enjoy a book is to experience subjective aesthetic pleasure; to admire it is to intellectually appreciate its craftsmanship and stylistic brilliance; but to revere it is to treat it as a sacred cultural monument. Yet as Kilmartin's context penetratingly implies, when a literary translation or artistic work becomes so intensely revered that it is viewed as sacrosanct, that very veneration can blind readers and scholars to its glaring textual defects, creating a cultural taboo against necessary revision and scholarly correction.

The term displays rich polysemy, psychological depth, and institutional breadth across diverse domains:

  • Cultural, Literary, and Artistic Canonization (Source Sense): In the arts, humanities, and literary criticism, revered characterizes masterpieces, founding literary figures, or monumental translations that have achieved canonical, near-sacred authority. Such works (e.g., the King James Bible, Shakespeare's First Folio, Scott Moncrieff's Proust) are treated not merely as reading material, but as cultural institutions. However, this status often provokes critical tension when historical scholarship reveals that the revered text contains corrupted readings or departures from the original source.
  • Religious Sanctity, Pious Veneration, and Sacred Objects: In theological and ecclesiastical traditions, revered describes saints, prophets, spiritual gurus, sacred scriptures, and holy relics held in supreme religious awe. Believers approach revered sites (such as shrines, holy mountains, or ancient sanctuaries) with formalized rituals of deference, lowered voices, and prostration, recognizing them as vessels of divine grace or spiritual transcendence.
  • Philosophical, Scientific, and Intellectual Eminence: In intellectual history and academia, the word designates foundational thinkers whose groundbreaking discoveries, ethical integrity, or monumental philosophical systems command universal intellectual deference (e.g., a revered elder statesman, the revered mentor of a generation of physicists). Their pronouncements are cited with authority, and their legacy serves as a beacon for subsequent scholarship.
  • Civic, Constitutional, and Democratic Institutions: In political science, sociology, and jurisprudence, revered characterizes the foundational legal documents, constitutional principles, or democratic customs of a nation (e.g., the revered constitutional guarantees of free speech, a revered supreme court justice). These institutions derive their legitimacy not merely from physical state force, but from the deep, shared civic reverence of the populace.
  • Ancestral and Familial Honor: In anthropology and traditional cultures, revered characterizes tribal elders, matriarchs, patriarchs, and departed ancestors. In societies governed by filial piety (such as Confucian ethics), elder members are revered for their accumulated wisdom and status as the living link between past lineage and future posterity.
  • Grammatical Nuance: Participial Verb vs. Fully Lexicalized Adjective: Grammatically, revered functions both as the past participle in passive verbal constructions (*she was revered throughout the continent for her philanthropy*) and as a fully converted, attributive adjective preceding a noun (*the revered master*, *a revered tradition*), where it signals established, permanent status rather than an isolated act of admiration.

The historical semantic bridge linking the fearful watchfulness of ancient ancestors, the religious dread of Roman augurs, the solemn deference paid to saints, and modern literary idolatry is the transformative refinement of fear into awe: Latin reverērī converted raw, survivalist wariness into an elevated, self-subordinating recognition of moral, spiritual, or aesthetic greatness.
