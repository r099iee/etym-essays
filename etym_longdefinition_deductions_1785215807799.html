DEDUCTIONS is the plural of the noun deduction, a word that belongs simultaneously to logic, to law, and to the counting-house. At its core a deduction is a conclusion drawn down out of general principles by rigorous reasoning: if the premises are true, the conclusion must follow, and the guarantee of the conclusion is carried entirely by the form of the argument rather than by observation or experiment. The plural form names a set or series of such inferences — several conclusions reached one after another, each resting on the ones before it.

Primary sense: deduction in logic and philosophy

In logic, a deduction is a movement of thought from the general to the particular. The classic model is the syllogism: "All men are mortal" (major premise), "Socrates is a man" (minor premise), "therefore Socrates is mortal" (conclusion). What marks the reasoning as deductive is its necessity — given the premises, the conclusion cannot be false. This is precisely the sense in which the word is used in the biographical passage behind this card, the record of John Stuart Mill's life: "the deductions are more difficult" refers to the laborious analytical inferences that the reader, the biographer, or the historian must draw from the evidence of a life — conclusions about the nature and influence of Harriet Taylor's mind upon Mill's work, each of them exacting and demanding, and harder than the easier empirical observations that surround them.

Secondary sense: arithmetic and financial subtraction

Entirely distinct in meaning though identical in form, a deduction is an amount subtracted from a total: a payroll deduction, a tax deduction, a deduction from an invoice or a bill. In this sense the word belongs to accounts, administration, and law rather than to the schoolroom. The two senses meet in the shared Latin verb dēdūcere, "to lead down" or "to draw off": the logician leads a conclusion down out of premises, and the accountant draws an amount off from a sum — the same physical image of leading downward serves both.

Tertiary senses, jargon, and modern extensions

  • Scientific deduction: in the natural and social sciences, a prediction derived from a hypothesis and tested against data — the deductive-nomological model of explanation, in which a law and initial conditions jointly imply the observed event.
  • Legal and official deductions: amounts lawfully withheld from income, such as income tax, national insurance, or pension contributions, or expenses set against taxable income (business deductions, charitable deductions, itemized deductions).
  • Detective-fiction deduction: the popular image of the detective's chain of reasoning from observed clues to the identity of the culprit — the famous "deductions" of Sherlock Holmes, in which the word is used loosely for any brilliant inference.
  • Games and puzzles: logic puzzles marketed as "deduction games," in which players eliminate possibilities and infer hidden information by combining clues.
  • Formal logic and artificial intelligence: "deduction" as a rule-governed inference procedure formalized in first-order logic, natural-deduction systems, and automated theorem provers.
  • Mathematics: the proving of theorems from axioms by chains of deductive steps — the Euclidean model that has defined mathematical certainty since antiquity.

Metaphoric shift and historical bridge

The word entered English in the fifteenth century by way of Middle French déduction and Latin dēductiō, "a leading down." Its earliest English senses were physical and concrete: the drawing off of water, the leading of a force down from a height, the subtraction of an amount from a total. Only with the medieval revival of Aristotelian logic did the word become an instrument of the mind — the downward movement from general principles to particular conclusions that Boethius and the scholastic logicians fixed as the counterpart of induction. The modern logical sense is thus a metaphor taken seriously: what is "led down" from the premises is the conclusion, and the spatial image survives visibly in the prefix de-, "down."

Register, grammar, and usage

The noun is countable in both its logical and its financial senses ("a deduction," "two deductions"), and it appears characteristically with the verbs make, draw, arrive at, and come to, and in the collocations logical deductions, a process of deduction, and deductive reasoning. The adjective deductive and the noun deductibility belong to the same family. In the Mill passage the plural is deliberate: a series of exacting inferences, each requiring labor, is set against the easier empirical observations of the surrounding text, and the tone is technical, intellectual, and slightly scientific — the vocabulary of a philosopher who did much to fix deduction as the rigorous counterpart of induction.
