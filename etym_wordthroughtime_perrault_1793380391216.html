- Late 17th c. (1687–1690s) — the radical modernist polemicist: Charles Perrault triggers the famous *Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes* with his 1687 poem, becoming infamous throughout European intellectual circles as the champion of the Moderns who dared to criticize Homer.
- 1697 to 18th c. — emergence of the fairy-tale master: Perrault publishes *Histoires ou contes du temps passé*, giving the world Mother Goose (*Ma Mère l'Oye*), Cinderella, and Puss in Boots, which rapidly achieve global popularity and gradually overshadow his polemical career.
- 18th–19th c. — neoclassical and Victorian cautionary symbol: literary figures from Swift (*Battle of the Books*) to John Ruskin invoke *Perrault* alongside Zoilus as a symbol of arrogant modern critics who belittle ancient genius to flatter contemporary trends.
- 20th c. — folkloric and psychoanalytic cornerstone: scholars (such as Bruno Bettelheim) and media pioneers (such as Walt Disney) canonize Perrault as the foundational architect of the Western fairy-tale tradition, exploring the psychological depths of his narratives.
- Today — dual cultural legacy: recognized globally as the immortal progenitor of classic fairy tales in popular consciousness, while remaining an essential figure in intellectual history as the pioneer of cultural progressivism and the battle against neoclassical dogma.
