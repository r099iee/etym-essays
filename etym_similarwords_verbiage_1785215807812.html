• Verbosity: Similar because both denote an excess of words; differs because verbosity describes the general quality or habit of using more words than necessary, often in speech as well as writing, while verbiage names the actual body of redundant words themselves as a thing.
  • Wordiness: Similar because both mean over-abundance of words; differs because wordiness is the plain, everyday, largely neutral term for using too many words, while verbiage is its slightly learned, dismissive cousin.
  • Prolixity: Similar because both denote wordy, overlong expression; differs because prolixity stresses drawn-out, tedious length and extension, especially in formal writing, rather than the pure redundancy of words that verbiage emphasizes.
  • Redundancy: Similar because both involve unnecessary words; differs because redundancy points specifically to the repetition of ideas or elements already expressed, while verbiage is a quantitative surplus of words generally, whether or not they repeat.
  • Circumlocution: Similar because both describe roundabout, wordy language; differs because circumlocution means speaking around a subject in evasive or indirect phrases, while verbiage is a quantitative excess — too many words carrying too little meaning — not necessarily evasive.
  • Pleonasm: Similar because both denote superfluous words; differs because pleonasm is a precise rhetorical term for the redundant expression of a single idea (e.g., "free gift"), while verbiage is a general, imprecise excess of language.
  • Padding: Similar because both name words added to inflate a text; differs because padding is the concrete, informal term for filler material inserted to lengthen a piece, while verbiage describes the wordy character of language itself.
  • Bombast: Similar because both denote inflated language; differs because bombast emphasizes pompous, high-sounding, pretentious diction, while verbiage emphasizes mere quantity and superfluity of words without the pomp.
  • Logorrhea: Similar because both denote excessive flow of words; differs because logorrhea is a clinical or jocular term for uncontrollable verbal flow (often pathological), while verbiage is an ordinary critical word for padded prose or speech.
  • Waffle: Similar because both denote language that says little; differs because waffle (chiefly British) is the informal word for vague, indecisive, long-winded talk, while verbiage is the more formal word for sheer verbal surplus.
  • Rhetoric (pejorative sense): Similar because both can name language criticized as empty or inflated; differs because rhetoric in its pejorative sense means language used for effect or persuasion rather than substance, while verbiage means a superfluity of words without regard to effect.
