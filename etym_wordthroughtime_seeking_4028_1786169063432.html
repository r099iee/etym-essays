- Old English ('sēcan') - 'to seek' meant both to strive after and to go to, approach, visit; the participle 'seeking' carried a physical movement of the whole body toward a person or place.
- Middle & Early Modern English - the sense of personal visitation fades, and 'seeking' concentrates on pursuit: to seek truth, advantage, counsel, or God — the verb of intellectual and spiritual questing.
- 18th-19th century - fully interiorized: seeking is a directed act of will, goal-driven striving, the opposite of idle curiosity; method, formality, and path are stripped away, leaving pure desire aimed at its object.
- Contemporary - freely loaned to the inanimate: seeking fibrils, seeking tendrils, seeking eyes; the participle has become literature's instrument for giving tissue, light, and machinery a hungry purposiveness — the bare vector of desire without a seeker attached.
