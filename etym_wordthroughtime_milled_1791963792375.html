- Old English — the verb means to grind corn at a mill, and the participle describes grain reduced to meal.
- 14th–15th c. — Milled cloth is fulled and thickened by agitation in water, and the word enters the language of textile finishing.
- 1660s — Milled coinage appears in England: machine-struck money with a marked edge, contrasted with hammered coin, and the adjective becomes a guarantee of integrity.
- 19th c. — Industrial machine tools make milled a general term for machined metal, while mill comes to mean a factory and milled implies factory processing.
- 1870s — Mill about and mill around are recorded for livestock and then for people, giving the form its crowd sense.
- 20th–21st c. — The crowd sense becomes the most familiar use of milled in journalism and fiction, coexisting with CNC-milled in engineering and milled edge in numismatics; slang adds the sense “beaten, defeated.”
