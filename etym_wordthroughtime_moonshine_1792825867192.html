- Old English — the compound is born: monan scinan and then moneshine name the moon's light plainly, poetry's favourite lamp.
- Middle English — lovers move in: romances and Chaucer make moonshine the light of trysts and madness alike — beautiful, cold, and slightly untrustworthy.
- 15th–16th c. — glow becomes emptiness: writers call alchemy, flattery, and fair promises moonshine — shining stuff with nothing in the hand.
- 19th c. America — nonsense fixed: whole moonshine and mere moonshine settle into Yankee speech as the laughing dismissal of tall talk.
- 19th–20th c. — the stills take the name: untaxed whiskey distilled by night becomes moonshine, sung in ballads and chased by revenuers through Prohibition.
- Today — all three moons shine on: garden moonshine for poets, political moonshine for columnists, and craft-distillery moonshine, legal at last, sold in mason jars with the outlaw romance intact.
