galloping: Moving at a gallop — the fastest and most powerful gait of a horse, a four-beat rhythm of leaping strides in which, between footfalls, all four feet are momentarily clear of the ground. In the present context the word is used acoustically first and visually second: "The sound of galloping hooves approached," and only then "he turned to find a black horse lunging break-neck along the bank of the pool." The sound of galloping is the sound of speed itself — a rhythmic, hammering, accelerating thunder — and it charges the whole scene with urgency and violence, reinforced by the black horse, the streaming black hair of the rider, and the "break-neck" phrase that follows. The participle galloping also carries a hint of something out of control: a gallop is the gait a horse cannot sustain and cannot easily stop, and its figurative uses — galloping consumption (the old name for tuberculosis), galloping inflation — all transfer that runaway momentum to whatever accelerates beyond control. Its gait-neighbors help to sharpen it: the walk is four-beat and slow; the trot is a two-beat diagonal rhythm, brisk and businesslike; the canter is a restrained three-beat gait, smooth and collected; the lope is a slow, relaxed gallop; the true gallop is the full four-beat extension, the horse stretched to its limit, all four feet striking and leaving the ground in turn — the sound of a creature running break-neck.
hooves: The plural of hoof — the hard, horny keratinous casing of the foot in ungulate (hoofed) mammals, the horse's foot being the classic instance. In this passage the hooves are the acoustic anchor of the entire scene: the sound of galloping hooves approaching is what alerts the speaker before the horse is seen, and the word grounds a supernatural, visionary moment in the most physical of realities — horn striking ground, rhythm, impact, the drumming that announced horses in the ancient world as surely as engines announce machines in ours. The plural form itself is worth noting: hooves, with its voiced v, is the modern regularization (alongside the older hoofs), formed by analogy with words like wolf/wolves; the word is almost always plural in narrative use because a horse is heard as four hooves, not one. Against its neighbors: feet are soft and flat, human or bare; paws are padded, clawed, and quiet; hooves alone are engineered for impact — the horse's foot is a single weight-bearing toenail, and its sound is the sound of the ancient world's primary technology of speed, war, and communication. Figuratively, the idiom "on the hoof" means "while still alive and moving" (of livestock sold and transported live) and, by extension, "on the move, without stopping."
lunging: The present participle of lunge — moving with a sudden, forceful, full-body extension forward, throwing the whole frame along a line in one explosive thrust. In the passage the horse is "lunging break-neck along the bank of the pool": the word suggests that each stride is not merely fast but violent, the animal's body stretched to its utmost length and hurled forward, as if it were attacking the distance. The word's history is fencing: a lunge was originally the attack in which a swordsman extends the sword arm and drives the whole body forward to its full length, and that ancestry gives lunging an edge of aggression and precision that its synonyms lack. Leaping and bounding emphasize height and spring; sprinting emphasizes speed over the ground; darting is quick but small; pouncing is downward and predatory; lunging is the sudden horizontal thrust of the entire body — the movement of a horse throwing itself forward "break-neck," which is exactly the movement the passage describes.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:
  • galloping: In economics and medicine ("galloping inflation," "galloping consumption"), dangerously rapid acceleration. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the fastest horse sprint transferred to unchecked economic or disease progression.
  • hooves: Idiom ("on the hoof"), live livestock transport or on the move. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: ungulate feet extended to live livestock transport and to human movement.
  • lunging: In equine training ("lunging a horse"), exercising a horse in circles on a long rope. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: fencing forward thrust transferred to circular horse exercise on a longe line; the two words lunge (thrust) and longe (training rope) are distinct but often confused spellings of related French forms.
