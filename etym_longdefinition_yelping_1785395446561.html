Yelping is the emission of short, sharp, high-pitched cries, typically involuntary expressions of sudden pain, alarm, or excited distress, and most often associated with dogs and other canines. The form in The Hobbit is the present participle of the verb yelp, and the -ing ending is essential to the effect: it stretches the brief cry into a repeated, insistent series, so that the sound becomes a pattern of shock rather than a single event. In the passage the yelping supplies the shrill upper register of the Wargs' soundscape, set against the deep growling of the pack: frantic, spasmodic, almost infantile, the noise of creatures driven by pain and panic as much as by rage. Too short and broken to be a howl or a bark, it is the sound of a creature hurt or startled, and it is this unpredictable mixture of low menace and high distress that unsettles a wizard who reads the psychology of sound. The same note of genuine suffering sounds earlier in the passage, where the Warg whose coat catches fire leaps to and fro 'yelping horribly', binding the word forever to pain. PRIMARY SENSE — THE INJURED OR STARTLED ANIMAL In ordinary and zoological use, a yelp is the brief, piercing, high-pitched cry a dog gives when hurt, startled, or suddenly excited: the yelp of a pup whose foot is trodden on, the yelp of a hound struck in the chase, the yelp of a fox or coyote as it reacts or calls. Unlike the howl, which is long, sustained, and communicative, the yelp is a single short burst of sound, almost a reflex; the animal does not choose to yelp so much as find itself yelping. This involuntary, reflex-like quality is the core of the word, and it explains why yelps cluster in moments of genuine pain or alarm — being burned, being struck, being startled from sleep. SECONDARY SENSE — THE HUMAN YELP Applied to people, to yelp is to give a short, sharp, involuntary cry of pain, fright, or surprise: she yelped as the pan scorched her hand; he yelped when the cold water hit him. The bridge from the dog is immediate — the human being is momentarily reduced to the animal register, the voice slipping out before the mind can form a word. A related figurative sense survives in modern English: 'to yelp in protest' or 'a yelp of complaint' means a small, sharp, rather undignified objection, the noise of someone who is not seriously aggrieved but cannot help crying out. TERTIARY SENSES — ZOOLOGICAL NAMES AND EXTENDED USES
  • In natural history, the name 'yelping jackal' is a local term for the golden jackal (Canis aureus), named for its sharp, barking-yelping call; foxes are said to yelp, and a fox's cry is often transcribed as a yelp.
  • In birding, 'yelp' describes the sharp, high-pitched call of certain birds, such as the yelp of the Northern flicker and of some woodpeckers, whose calls have been transcribed as single sharp notes.
  • In music and sound description, a 'yelp' is a sudden sharp high note or effect — a guitar 'yelp' in rock and roll, an excitable vocal yelp of joy; the animal reflex becomes a stylistic colour.
  • In commerce and technology, Yelp is the name of a major business-review and recommendation website; the brand is a modern coinage that deliberately reuses the word's sharp, attention-grabbing sound and is etymologically unrelated to the verb (it is a homograph, not a descendant).
  • In human behaviour and sport, a 'yelp' can be a cry of triumph or excitement, showing that the word has swung from pure pain toward high emotion whenever the pitch is right.
REGISTER, CONNOTATION AND SEMANTIC HISTORY The word is informal, animal, and slightly comic-pathetic in register. Even in The Hobbit, where the pain is real, the yelping of the burning Warg carries a note of the absurd — the great predator reduced to a creature that can only leap and shriek. This has always been the connotation: yelp belongs to small dogs, hurt pups, startled children, and clumsy moments, and it never rises to the dignity of howl or scream. Historically the word is a remarkable case of near-total semantic reversal: in Old English its ancestor gielpan meant 'to boast, to exult', a cry of pride condemned as vainglory in Anglo-Saxon homilies, and it took centuries of narrowing — through 'to cry aloud', to 'to give tongue' of hounds, to the sharp cry of a dog in pain — before it settled on the small, involuntary, high-pitched outcry it names today. The participle yelping, with its repeated -ing, is the word in full flood: the pain made serial, insistent, and impossible to ignore.
