The biography of deferred is the biography of a carried load. The Proto-Indo-European root *bher- meant “to carry,” and its ancient speakers carried it into nearly every branch of the family: Sanskrit bhárati, “he carries,” gave Hindi bhār, “burden,” and the husband-word bhartā; Greek phérō, “I carry,” still hides inside metaphor (a “carrying over” of meaning), euphoria (a “good bearing” of health), and phosphorus (the “light-bearer”); and the Germanic branch produced bear, birth, bairn, burden, and bier. Latin developed the root into the supremely versatile verb ferre, “to carry,” and then — like every busy people — began bolting prefixes onto it. Referre (carry back), transferre (carry across), conferre (carry together), circumferre (carry around), and sufferre (bear up under) all entered English as refer, transfer, confer, circumference, and suffer. Out of this prefix workshop came the two compounds that matter here.

Latin differre, “to carry apart,” combined dis- (“apart, in different directions”) with ferre. Its oldest sense was physical — to scatter, to spread this way and that — and from that scattering grew the abstract sense of divergence (different things are “carried apart” from each other) and, crucially, the temporal sense: to differ a matter was to carry it away from its appointed time, to scatter it into the future. Old French passed this on as differer, and Middle English borrowed it in the fourteenth century as differren and deferren, meaning both “to differ” and “to put off.” Meanwhile a second Latin verb, dēferre, “to carry down” (de- “down, away from”), had developed the sense of carrying a report down to an authority, and hence of submitting and yielding. Through Old French déférer this entered English in the same century as the other verb, spelled defer. Two different verbs — one meaning “postpone,” one meaning “yield” — collided in a single spelling, and English has cheerfully lived with the homograph pair ever since; their pronunciations are identical, and only context tells them apart.

The past participle deferred, the exact form on this card, came into its own as English formalised its vocabulary of time. In the eighteenth century the postpone-sense was fully dignified: Austen writes that an answer “deferred it all,” carrying Mrs. Bennet’s elaborate plans away from the present into a future that never quite arrives. The participle then hardened into a technical adjective. Victorian finance gave the world deferred payment and deferred income; the law gave deferred sentence and deferred admission; psychology and moral philosophy gave deferred gratification, the deliberate postponement of reward. Every one of these phrases still carries the ancient image of the load: the matter is borne away to a fitter hour, not dropped. Modern English mostly says postponed, but deferred keeps its weight — heavier, more formal, more dignified, and faintly euphemistic, which is precisely the note Austen’s narrator strikes when a letter silently carries a family’s hopes off the table.
