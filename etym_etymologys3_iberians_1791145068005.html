Iberians is an exonym whose deep origin remains debated, but whose classical chain is clear. Greeks from the 6th century BCE called the far-western peninsula Iberia and its inhabitants Iberes, apparently after the Iberus river — the Ebro, the great waterway of the northeast. Romans borrowed this as Iberia, Iberi (sometimes Hiberi, by confusion with Hibernia/Ireland), and Iberus. English in the Renaissance revived Iberian as a learned adjective for things Spanish-Portuguese, and Iberians as the collective noun for the pre-Roman inhabitants. The ultimate native word behind the river-name is uncertain: ancient authors already speculated, and modern scholars divide between an indigenous Iberian *iber "water, river" (supported by Basque ibar "meadow by river" and frequent Iber- hydronyms) and a purely Greco-Roman labelling of unknown substrate. The protocol therefore requires an explicit debate note: the Greek-Latin-English descent is secure; the pre-Greek source is not.

In antiquity Iberians denoted a mosaic, not a single nation: Turdetani, Bastetani, Contestani, Edetani, and others along the Mediterranean coast, distinct in language and material culture from Celts of the interior (hence Greco-Roman Celtiberi for mixed zones) and from Lusitanians and Aquitanians further west. They wrote in two related Paleohispanic scripts (northeastern and southeastern Iberian), left the Lady of Elche and warrior stelae, traded with Phoenicians and Greeks, and were conquered by Rome in the long Second Punic and Lusitanian wars (218–19 BCE).

  • Greek naming (c. 600–200 BCE): Hecataeus, Herodotus, and later Polybius use Iberes/Iberia. Meaning is geographic-ethnographic: the peoples past the Pillars of Heracles. Already a blanket term for diverse groups.
  • Roman systematisation (200 BCE – 400 CE): Iberi in Livy, Pliny, Strabo. Administrative provinces Hispania Citerior/Ulterior, later Tarraconensis, Baetica, Lusitania overlay the ethnic label. Iberi becomes a historical-archaeological term as Romanisation proceeds.
  • Medieval eclipse: Hispani, Gothi, Saraceni replace Iberi in live use. Iberian survives only in learned geography copying Ptolemy.
  • Renaissance revival (16th c.): Humanists revive Iberia/Iberian for the peninsula's antiquities. English travellers and historians adopt Iberian Peninsula to avoid saying Spain-and-Portugal.
  • 19th–20th c. archaeology: French and Spanish excavations define Iberian culture (6th–1st c. BCE): urban oppida, painted pottery, sculpture, coinage. Iberians now means that specific Iron-Age Mediterranean culture, not all ancient Spaniards.
  • Durant's usage (1930s): "The Irish, the Iberians, the Picts..." uses Iberians in the old universal-history sense — a primitive tribal people among others, alleged to have practised cannibalism. Modern scholarship treats such blanket cannibalism claims sceptically, as Greco-Roman slander of barbarians; Durant repeats early-20th-century anthropology uncritically.

Linguistically the plural -s is English; the stem Iberian- is Latin-Greek. Phonetically Greek /ibeːres/ > Latin /ibeːriː/ > English /aɪˈbɪəriənz/, with the Great Vowel Shift raising the initial diphthong and reduction of the middle vowels. Culturally the word migrated from river to people to peninsula to modern demonym (Iberian as neutral geographic adjective for Spanish + Portuguese). Durant's Iberians are the ancient, pre-civilised phase of that long identity — tribes before Rome, before Christianity, before nationhood.
