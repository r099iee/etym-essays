1. The Indo-European base: one word for “new” (pre-writing to classical antiquity). Every sense of renovated flows from the oldest reconstructed adjective in the Indo-European family for novelty: PIE *néwos, “new.” It appears in Sanskrit nava, Greek néos (whence neologism, neolithic), Latin novus, and Germanic *newjaz (English new). In Latin, the adjective novus generated a productive denominative verb, novāre “to make new,” and the classical historians, poets, and agricultural writers added the prefix re- (“again”) to produce renovāre. Already in classical Latin the verb was thoroughly versatile: Caesar and Livy use it of renewing war and courage, Cicero of renewing memory and friendship, and Virgil — in the famous theft of the golden bough — of “renewing” the years; agricultural writers spoke of renovare agros, refreshing exhausted fields by rotation. The classical verb thus already possessed the full polarity — physical restoration and abstract revival — that the English word still enjoys.

2. The learned highway into English (15th–16th c.). English did not inherit renovate through everyday speech but imported it from the classical languages at the moment of the Renaissance “open sesame” of Latin learning. First attestations cluster around 1400–1500: the verb renovate, the noun renovation (c. 1425), and the adjective renovated all entered the language of theology, law, and medicine, chiefly from the Latin past-participle stem renovat-. This birth as a learned word set the register permanently: renovated has never become slang and never quite become folk speech. In the sixteenth century the Reformation saturated the word with religious meaning. The reformers’ language of the renewal of the heart, the renovation of the covenant, and the regenerate or “renovated” man echoed Saint Paul’s injunction to be “renewed in the spirit of your mind” (Ephesians 4:23), and the English Bible’s use of “renew” kept the family at the centre of spiritual vocabulary.

3. Body, mind, and soul: the seventeenth-century expansion (17th c.). The seventeenth century widened the word in two directions. In physiology, the new science of circulation and metabolism described the living body as ceaselessly renovating itself: the blood is renovated by digestion, the spirits are renovated by sleep, and tissue is renovated by nourishment — usages that survive in modern histology’s talk of cellular renovation. In devotional and moral prose, meanwhile, renovation named the deliberate reformation of character. The result was a word equally at home in medical treatises and sermons, a range that would later let it slip with perfect ease from the soul to the sink.

4. Romanticism and the renovated spirit (18th–19th c.). The Romantic movement gave the word its most memorable literary incarnation. The source context of this card is Wordsworth’s two-book Prelude (first completed 1799), where the poet describes, after the climax of his crisis, a spirit “renovated” and “singled out” for holy service: poetic inspiration is figured as a priestly vestment laid over a renewed soul. The image joins the whole older tradition — Pauline renewal, Neoplatonic restoration of the soul’s original brightness — to the Romantic doctrine of the poet’s second birth. In the same generation, “renovate” began its downward migration: building and decorative uses, long present but subordinate, grew with the Victorian passion for improvement, so that by the end of the nineteenth century a house could be renovated as readily as a heart.

5. The industrial and digital renovation (20th–21st c.). The twentieth century completed the takeover of the concrete senses. Housing booms, urban renewal schemes, and the postwar restoration of war-damaged cities made renovation a mass activity and a mass noun; television makeover shows and property markets made “renovated” a household word of approval, almost always in praise of kitchens, bathrooms, and knocked-through walls. In historic preservation, professionals now distinguish renovation (updating while keeping character), restoration (returning to a documented original state), and rehabilitation (adaptive reuse) — a technical taxonomy the old Romans would have recognised, since their renovare already meant “to make new again,” whatever the object. The digital age added the final twist: software, data centres, and even codebases are now said to be renovated when modernised without full rewrite. The word’s career is complete: from an Indo-European adjective meaning “new,” through a Latin verb for renewing fields and souls, to a contemporary term for updating everything from a cathedral to a codebase — always meaning the same thing, made new again.
