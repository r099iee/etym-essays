The etymological origin of the name Rhode Island is one of the most fascinating toponymic puzzles in early American historiography. The name represents a confluence of sixteenth-century Mediterranean exploration, seventeenth-century Dutch maritime cartography, and early English colonial legislation.

1. The Two Converging Theories of Origin:

Historians and philologists have documented two distinct, compelling historical explanations for how the name originated, both of which likely reinforced one another in the minds of early English colonists:

  • Theory A: The Verrazzano-Rhodes Analogy (1524): In the spring of 1524, the Italian explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano, sailing under the commission of King Francis I of France aboard the ship La Dauphine, became the first documented European to explore Narragansett Bay. In his famous letter to the French king (preserved in the Cellere Codex), Verrazzano described an island near the entrance of the bay: "...we discovered an island in the form of a triangle, distant from the mainland three leagues, about the bigness of the Island of Rhodes...". While historical cartographers debate whether Verrazzano was referring to modern Block Island or Aquidneck Island, his description comparing the New World island to the famous Mediterranean island of Rhodes (Ancient Greek Ῥόδος, Rhódos, traditionally interpreted as "island of roses," from PIE *wrdho-, "sweet briar, rose") circulated widely throughout European navigation circles and appeared on early Italian and French maritime charts.
  • Theory B: Adriaen Block and 't Roode Eylandt (1614): Ninety years later, in 1614, the Dutch navigator and fur trader Adriaen Block explored Long Island Sound and Narragansett Bay aboard his vessel Onrust ("Restless"). As Block sailed into the bay, he observed the prominent reddish appearance of the shoreline bluffs along Aquidneck Island—caused by deposits of red sedimentary clay. In his Dutch navigational journal, Block dubbed the landmass 't Roode Eylandt (in Modern Dutch, het rode eiland), literally translating to "the Red Island." The Proto-Germanic root of Dutch rood is *raudaz, descending from Proto-Indo-European *h₁rewdʰ- ("red, ruddy"), the source of English red and Latin ruber. Dutch maps of the New Netherland colony published throughout the 1620s and 1630s clearly labeled the island and surrounding bay as Roodt Eylandt.

2. English Settlement and the 1644 Legislative Renaming:

When Roger Williams settled at Providence in 1636 and Anne Hutchinson and her companions settled at Portsmouth in 1638, the large island in Narragansett Bay was known by its native Algonquin Narragansett name, Aquidneck (alternatively spelled Aquethneck or Aquidnet, meaning "the island" or "at the island").

However, early English settlers were thoroughly familiar with both Dutch maritime charts (with whom they traded regularly) and English translations of Verrazzano’s voyages published in Richard Hakluyt’s celebrated collection Principal Navigations (1589). On March 13, 1644, the General Court of the colony, sitting at Portsmouth, passed a historic resolution officially renaming the island:

  • "It is ordered by this Court, that the Inslad commonly called Aquethneck, shall be from henceforth called the Isle of Rhodes, or Rhode-Island."
  • This official legislative act combined the Hellenic spelling (Rhode, reflecting Greek Rhodos) with the literal translation of Dutch Roode Eylandt, satisfying both classical literary erudition and geographic description.

3. The Royal Charter of 1663 and Compound Statehood:

In 1663, King Charles II granted the royal charter uniting the island settlements (Newport and Portsmouth on Rhode Island) with the mainland settlements (Providence and Warwick on the continental mainland) under the formal corporate name of The Governor and Company of the English Colony of Rhode-Island and Providence Plantations, in New-England, in America.

For over three and a half centuries, this dual nomenclature endured, honoring both the insular island of Aquidneck (Rhode Island) and the continental settlements (Providence Plantations). Over time, federal statutes, international diplomacy, and colloquial speech streamlined the state's popular designation to Rhode Island. In November 2020, voters formally amended the state constitution to excise "and Providence Plantations," permanently establishing the singular State of Rhode Island as the official name of the nation’s founding sanctuary for human liberty.
