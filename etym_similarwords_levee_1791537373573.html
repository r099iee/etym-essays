• Dike: Similar because both are engineered barriers built to hold back water and prevent the flooding of low-lying land; differs because a dike (or dyke) typically holds back the sea or ocean waters in coastal reclaimed lands (as in the Netherlands), whereas a levee specifically flanks rivers and alluvial floodplains to contain high seasonal discharge.
  • Embankment: Similar because both are raised, earthen or stone structures constructed above natural ground level; differs because embankment is a broader civil engineering term that includes raised structures supporting roadways, railways, or canal walls, while a levee is built explicitly for hydrological flood containment or riparian river-wharf landing.
  • Floodwall: Similar because both serve the identical primary function of shielding settled or agricultural land from floodwaters; differs because a floodwall is an artificial, vertical wall constructed of masonry, steel, or reinforced concrete used in tight urban spaces, whereas a levee is predominantly a broad, sloping earthen ridge.
  • Bund: Similar because both are constructed embankments built to retain water; differs because a bund is predominantly used in Asian contexts (such as India and China) to refer to small agricultural retaining walls in rice paddies or historical waterfront promenades (e.g., The Bund in Shanghai), carrying distinct regional engineering connotations.
  • Berm: Similar because both are raised ridges of compacted soil; differs because a berm is a narrow, horizontal ledge or low shelf constructed along a slope, ditch, or military fortification to break erosion, improve stability, or deflect runoff, lacking the immense scale and river-channeling purpose of a levee.
  • Quay: Similar because both can function as a paved waterfront landing place for mooring boats and handling commercial cargo; differs because a quay is a solid stone or masonry wharf built along a harbor or canal edge, whereas a river levee acts as a landing platform only incidentally on top of its primary flood-control earthwork.
  • Reception: Similar because both can refer to a formal gathering of guests convened to pay homage to a dignitary; differs because levee in its ceremonial sense specifically originated as a morning or early afternoon formal reception held by a monarch, head of state, or colonial governor, contrasting with evening parties or general social receptions.
