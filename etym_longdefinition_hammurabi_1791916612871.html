Hammurabi is a proper noun: the name of an Amorite king of Babylon who reigned in the eighteenth century BCE and who is remembered almost entirely for one object — a basalt stele inscribed with 282 laws, set up in Babylon and now in the Louvre. In the source passage the name appears in the fixed phrase the Code of Hammurabi, where it functions as a label for a body of law rather than for a man. The sentence lists three ancient authorities for retribution — Roman lex talionis, the Code of Hammurabi, and the “Mosaic” demand of an eye for an eye — and so treats Hammurabi as the Mesopotamian representative of a principle that the essay claims runs through all legal history.

The historical Hammurabi was the sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon, reigning according to the middle chronology from about 1792 to 1750 BCE. He inherited a small kingdom and, in his thirtieth year or so, broke the power of Rim-Sin of Larsa and then of Zimri-Lim of Mari, uniting southern and central Mesopotamia under Babylon. He built canals and fortifications, reorganised temple administration, and styled himself “king of the four quarters of the world”. His law collection was probably promulgated late in his reign and was copied and studied for centuries afterwards.

What the name denotes

  • The king: Ḫammurabi of Babylon, sixth ruler of the First (Amorite) Dynasty, a contemporary of Zimri-Lim of Mari and of Shamshi-Adad I of Assyria.
  • The code: a prologue, 282 numbered provisions and an epilogue, engraved on a stele about 2.25 metres high, probably erected towards the end of his reign. It covers procedure, property, irrigation, agriculture, marriage and inheritance, debts, wages, and the liability of surgeons, builders and shepherds.
  • The principle: by a well-worn shorthand, “Hammurabi” stands for retributive justice — an eye for an eye, a life for a life — although the code also contains graduated money penalties, class distinctions and numerous provisions that have nothing to do with retaliation.
  • The symbol: in modern legal and historical writing the name is an emblem of the moment when law was written down, made public and presented as divine command.

The stele and its contents

  • Material and form: a black basalt pillar, topped by a relief showing the king standing before the sun god Shamash, the patron of justice, who is seated and holds the rod and ring.
  • Discovery: found at Susa in 1901–02 by a French expedition under Jacques de Morgan, having been carried there as booty by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nakhunte in the twelfth century BCE. It was translated by Vincent Scheil and published in 1902.
  • Social order: the laws distinguish three classes — the free man (awilum), the commoner or dependent (mushkenum) and the slave (wardum) — and the penalty for the same injury varies with the status of the victim.
  • Talion: the famous clauses apply between equals: “If a man has put out the eye of another man, his eye shall be put out.” In other cases the code substitutes compensation, which is why historians resist reducing it to pure revenge.
  • Purpose: the epilogue declares that the king was appointed “to make justice prevail in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil, that the strong might not oppress the weak”.

Why it matters in this reading

The passage is an argument about the persistence of revenge in law. Citing Hammurabi alongside Roman law and the Mosaic commandment places the Babylonian code at the head of a lineage — the oldest of the three witnesses — and implies that the impulse behind modern punishment is continuous with the Bronze Age. The comparison is historically loose in one respect: the biblical “eye for an eye” is usually read by scholars as a limitation on vengeance (one eye, not seven), and the Mesopotamian provisions similarly mix talion with compensation. But as a rhetorical genealogy the trio is effective, and the name Hammurabi does most of the work, because it is the one name in the list that automatically evokes a written code.

Register and use

  • Register: academic and journalistic; almost never used outside the phrase “Code of Hammurabi” or a discussion of ancient law.
  • Attributive use: “Hammurabi’s laws”, “a Hammurabi-like severity”, “Hammurabic” (rare) — the name can be pressed into service as an adjective of harshness.
  • Spelling: English conventionally Hammurabi; older scholarship also wrote Hammourabi, Khammurabi and Ḫammurabi. The cuneiform spelling is syllabic: ḫa-am-mu-ra-pí.
