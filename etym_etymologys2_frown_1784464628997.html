The etymology of FROWN is debated beyond the attested Middle English and Old French stages; the chain is given with the uncertainty stated explicitly. Middle English frounen/frownen 'to frown' (late 14th c.) → Old French frognier/froignier 'to scowl, wrinkle the nose, snort in contempt' (12th–13th c.) → Old French froigne 'a grimace, surly look,' of uncertain deeper origin → possibly from a Germanic base meaning 'to wrinkle' (cf. Middle Dutch and Middle Low German fronsen 'to wrinkle, frown'; the assumed Germanic stem *frung- is speculative) → Modern English FROWN. Note on the debate: the common popular derivation connecting frown to PIE *bhru- 'eyebrow' (the source of native English brow) is rejected by modern scholarship: frown is a Romance (Old French) loan, not a native Germanic word, and no PIE stage can be traced through it with certainty. Some etymologists propose a Gaulish (Celtic) source in the semantic field of 'nose, snout, grimace,' which would fit the early French sense of wrinkling the nose in disdain; others support the Germanic 'wrinkle' connection. Both hypotheses remain open; the attested record is secure only from Old French onward.
