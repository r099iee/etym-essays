heaved is the past tense and past participle of the verb heave, a word whose core business is the application of great physical effort to move mass. At its heart, to heave is to lift, haul, or raise something heavy with a strong, sustained push or pull of the body, and by extension to throw it, to utter it with strain, or to rise and fall in a slow, laboured rhythm.

PRIMARY SENSE — EFFORTFUL LIFTING AND HAULING

  • The prototypical meaning: to raise or lift something heavy with effort — "the sailors heaved the anchor aboard", "he heaved the sack onto his shoulder".
  • The effort is always emphasised: heaving is not the delicate picking-up of a light object but the straining, grunting, whole-body work of moving dead weight.
  • In the source text (Tolkien's The Hobbit), the elves standing in the shallow water "heaved and shoved" — the word captures the pushing-and-lifting strain of shifting heavy barrels in a river, a scene of collective muscular effort.

SECONDARY SENSE — THROWING AND PROPULSION

  • To throw, fling, or launch something heavy, often clumsily: "he heaved a rock at the window", "she heaved her suitcase onto the top bunk".
  • The semantic bridge: lifting something heavy and then releasing it in an arc is a natural extension — the effort is identical, the outcome changes from position to trajectory.
  • Register: slightly rough or violent; you heave something when you do not handle it gently.

TERTIARY SENSE — EFFORTFUL UTTERANCE AND BREATHING

  • To utter with great effort or emotion: "heave a sigh", "heave a groan" — the breath itself is treated as something heavy that must be lifted out of the chest.
  • To rise and fall rhythmically and heavily: "her chest heaved with sobs", "the sleeping dog's sides heaved" — the body's involuntary respiratory motion is described with the vocabulary of lifting mass.
  • The bridge: the muscular effort of lifting is metaphorically transferred to the internal organs — breath and voice are "heaved" out of the body.

NAUTICAL JARGON — A SAILOR'S VERB

  • To haul, pull, or raise on a ship: heave the anchor, heave the capstan, heave on a rope.
  • Fixed nautical phrases: "heave ho!" (the sailors' rhythmical hauling cry), "heave to" (to bring a ship to a standstill), "heave in sight" (to come into view), "hove to" (the archaic strong past surviving in seafaring idiom).
  • The strong past hove (Old English hōf) survives only in these nautical fossils; the regular weak form heaved is the everyday past on land — a living reminder of the old split between sea dialect and standard English.

GEOLOGICAL AND PHYSICAL SENSES

  • Of the earth or rock: to rise, swell, or be displaced upward — "the ground heaved during the earthquake", "heaved strata".
  • Technical geology: a "heave fault" shows horizontal displacement along a fault line; mining uses heave for the upward pressure of rock.
  • Of a liquid surface: to rise and fall in long, slow undulation — "the sea heaved in long grey swells". The bridge: earth and sea are treated as giant bodies straining upward, exactly like a labouring chest.

VISCERAL AND SLANG SENSES

  • To retch or vomit: "the smell made him heave" — the stomach's convulsive effort to expel contents is the ultimate internalisation of the lifting motion.
  • "To give someone the heave-ho": to dismiss or fire them — built on the sailors' "heave ho!" cry, the phrase became 20th-century slang for ejection; the bridge is the idea of bodily throwing someone out.
  • Noun use: "a heave of the shoulders", "one good heave and it moved" — a single decisive effort.

METAPHORIC AND IDIOMATIC EXTENSIONS

  • Effort and labour: to "heave" at a task is to strain at it; the word colours any action with the sense of heavy, grinding effort.
  • Emotion and crowds: a "heaving crowd" or a "heaving" mass moves like a body of water, rising and falling with collective motion; "heaving with emotion" fuses bodily motion with feeling.
  • Sound: "heaving" can describe the slow, audible breathing of a large beast or a weary man — sound and motion fused in one word.

REGISTER AND STYLE

  • Heave is a strong, physical, Anglo-Saxon-rooted verb, at home in epic, adventure, and sea narrative (Tolkien, Melville, Conrad), in manual labour, and in vivid colloquial speech.
  • It is rarely technical in everyday use, but carries precise technical meanings in seamanship, geology, and respiratory medicine.
  • The past form heaved is fully standard; hove survives as a consciously literary or nautical variant, giving writers a register-switch that few English verbs still possess.
