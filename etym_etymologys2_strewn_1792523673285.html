PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500 BCE)
*sterh₃- / *ster-
"to spread out, stretch, extend, strew"
(source of Latin sternere, Greek stornymi)
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*strawjaną
"to scatter, strew, spread loosely"
(strong/weak verbal stem *strew-)
↓
Old English (c. 8th c.)
strēowian / strewian
"to scatter, spread, sprinkle"
(past participle ġestrēowod / ġestrowen)
↓
Middle English (c. 12th–14th c.; Chaucer, Wycliffe)
strewen / strowen
"to scatter loosely, disperse over ground"
(past participle strowen / strewen)
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.; Shakespeare, KJV)
strewn / strown
"scattered, dispersed, spread across"
↓
Modern English (present)
strewn
"scattered or spread loosely over a surface"

Note on ablaut variation and cognacy: The PIE root *sterh₃- gave rise to a vast family of English cognates centered on spreading and layering. These include straw (that which is strewn on floors or fields), stratum and stratosphere (from Latin sternere / stratum), street (from Late Latin via strata, a paved/spread road), and prostrate. In Old and Middle English, the verb exhibited both front-vowel (*strēowian -> strew / strewn) and back-vowel (*strawjan -> strow / strown) variants; Modern English has largely standardized on strew / strewn, though strown survives in poetic and archaic registers.
