malign (verb, and adjective): To speak about someone in a spitefully critical or injurious manner; to defame, slander, calumniate, or vilify; to damage a reputation by deliberate, malicious utterance or by spreading false or injurious reports. In the Preface to Modern Painters John Ruskin declares his mission "to bring honour to those great living Masters whom we now neglect or malign in the public press." The verb is chosen for the weight of its etymology: it means, literally, "born evil" or "of an evil disposition" — from Latin malignus, "ill-natured, spiteful, of an evil nature," itself malus "bad" plus the suffix -gnus "born." Ruskin's pairing "neglect or malign" names the two complementary failures of the Victorian press toward living genius: the passive sin of omission (ignoring the great) and the active sin of commission (slandering them). To malign is not to criticise but to injure; the word turns disagreement into a moral crime and turns the critic into a slanderer. Polysemy and related senses:
  • Primary sense (verb, transitive): to speak evil of someone, to defame, to attack a person's character with deliberate spite — the sense in Ruskin, always aimed at a person or a body of persons.
  • Adjectival sense: "evil, baleful, harmful in nature or tendency," chiefly literary or formal — "a malign influence," "a malign star," "malign neglect" — the older use of the word, in English since the fourteenth century and now largely displaced by malignant.
  • Extended figurative sense: applied to impersonal agencies that work harm — "malign fate," "malign forces," "a malign effect" — transferring the badness of a person's nature to forces, tendencies, and institutions.
  • Medical sense: the adjective malignant (and, in older usage, malign itself) names what tends to grow, spread, and kill — a cancer, a tumour, a fever — extending the idea of an evil, self-destroying nature from character to disease.
  • Astrological and old-science sense: a "malign aspect" of the planets was one deemed hurtful; the word was part of the technical vocabulary of Renaissance astronomy and humoral medicine, where a "malign fever" was a deadly one.
The historical bridge between these senses is the notion of a bad nature: the Latin word malignus describes not a bad act but a bad disposition, a badness present from birth and running in the blood. The adjective sense in English therefore always points to a quality inherent in the thing described. The verbal sense is a later development: in the seventeenth century, in the same era that gave English malignant (used first of deadly diseases and then, during the English Civil War, of the King's party), English speakers began to use malign as a verb meaning "to speak evil of." The shift from nature to deed is precise and revealing: one does not merely disagree with the victim of a maligning; one treats them as though they were evil at heart. The verb presupposes the adjective's fatalism, and that is what gives Ruskin's use its moral force — the press is not said to err but to act out of malice, treating living genius as though it were wicked. Register and usage notes:
  • Malign is the literary and moral verb for spiteful defamation; it is quieter and more poisonous than vilify, more deliberate than denigrate, less technical than slander and libel.
  • It is almost always transitive, taking a person or a group as object ("to malign a reputation," "to malign the great living masters").
  • As an adjective it is formal and now rare outside literary or rhetorical prose, having been largely replaced by malignant in medical and general use.
  • The word carries a strongly accusatory charge: to use it is to claim that the speech in question is not criticism but malice, an act committed against a name.
