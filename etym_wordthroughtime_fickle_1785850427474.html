- Old English ficol: treacherous, deceitful, crafty — perceived as active moral falsity and dangerous cunning
- Middle English fikel: false, unstable, unfaithful — broadening from pure deceit toward unreliability of allegiance
- 16th–17th c.: standard epithet for lovers, fortune, and worldly favor — inconstancy as theme of sonnets and moral essays
- 18th–19th c.: romantic and political prose — fickle public, fickle mistress Fortune; moralized changeability of taste and loyalty
- 20th c.: everyday adjective for unreliable preferences; weather and fandom clichés; less tied to "evil" than to frustrating instability
- Contemporary: still mildly reproachful; applies to people, markets, algorithms of fame, and climate of opinion; OE sense of deep treachery is historically obsolete in normal use
