The biography of ogres begins not in the nursery but in the underworld. The chain opens with Orcus, an Italic deity of the dead whom Roman writers used interchangeably with Dis Pater and Pluto; Orcus was at once a name for the realm of the dead and a grim god who punished oath-breakers. His own origin is lost. Some scholars propose an Etruscan source, pointing to an Etruscan underworld god; others connect him with Greek Ὅρκος (Horkos), the personified Oath that destroyed perjurers — a tempting link, since Orcus punished the perjured. Neither theory is secure, and there is certainly no established Proto-Indo-European root to reconstruct; the honest verdict is that the chain begins in the Italic and Etruscan world, not in PIE. Orcus is already attested in Plautus (3rd–2nd century BCE) and appears throughout Latin literature, including Vergil's Aeneid, where the shades of the dead dwell beneath the earth.

The decisive change is a long slide of rank and dignity in the Romance vernaculars. Where Latin authors kept Orcus a respectable, if terrible, lord of the dead, the spoken dialects of Italy degraded the name. Italian orco slipped from 'god of the dead' to a generic demon, and then, in popular folklore, to a cannibal giant of mountain and forest who caught and ate children — the very form in which the word crossed into literature. The Italian tale-collections give the figure a home: Giovanni Francesco Straparola's Le piacevoli notti (1550s) and Giambattista Basile's Il Pentamerone (1634–1636) both contain man-eating giants and orco-like ogres, establishing the child-devouring monster in print before it ever reached French literature.

  • The Italian folk descent: a word for the dread god of death becomes, over centuries of popular speech, a word for a child-eating bogey — the same name, drastically lowered in dignity, with the appetite of the underworld transferred from souls to children's flesh.
  • The French literary fix: Charles Perrault's Histoires ou contes du temps passé (1697), above all Le Petit Poucet, where the Ogre is the child-devouring lord of a bloody castle who sends his seven daughters to bed in a room of knives, establishes ogre as the archetypal fairy-tale cannibal giant in a single stroke. Whether Perrault coined the form from Italian orco or reworked a folk word is debated; from 1697 the French ogre names a specific literary monster — huge, humanoid, crude, and defined by a single appetite.

English took the word from French through the translations of the fairy tales, first as hogre in 1705 and then as ogre in 1713. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries it hardened into the stock man-eating giant of English nursery literature — the devourer of children who lurks in castles and dark places — while the derived forms ogreish and ogresse arrived quickly after. At the same time the nineteenth century began to use the word figuratively of persons: a cruel, devouring, or hideous human being, an ogre of a boss, keeping the appetite and the cruelty while dropping the supernatural size. The semantic arc is thus one of steady degradation — from a dread deity of the underworld, to a generic demon, to a child-eating giant of fairy tale, to a mere rhetorical figure, a 'horrible name' in tales.

  • The social transfer: any person, institution, or force that devours people, budgets, or hopes becomes metaphorically an ogre — by the twentieth century the figurative sense was firmly literary and journalistic ('the ogre of hunger,' 'the corporate ogre').
  • The genre codification: modern fantasy, from Tolkien's own Middle-earth through games such as Dungeons and Dragons and countless video games, turned the ogre into a stock species — huge, dim, humanoid, carnivorous — a fixed entry in the monster-menagerie distinct from giant, troll, and goblin; the fairy-tale cannibal became a catalogue entry.

In Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), the sentence of the passage exploits the word's accumulated history: 'all the horrible names of all the giants and ogres he had ever heard told of in tales.' The ogres are names only — storybook bogeymen who have done nothing, compared with the nameless horror of the dark tunnel. The whole arc, from a dread Italic deity of the underworld, through the degraded Italian orco, through Perrault's literary giant, to a rhetorical figure of nursery legend, is compressed into a single noun, and it is precisely the flatness of the final stage that gives the sentence its rueful irony: the famous monsters of story are powerless next to the real darkness that has come for the hobbit himself.
