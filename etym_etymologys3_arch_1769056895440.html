Arch is a biography of the 'bent bow.' Its deepest known ancestor is the Indo-European root *h₂erḱʷ-, a word for the hunting weapon that in historic times survived in only two branches: Italic and Germanic. In Latin it gave arcus, 'a bow,' and in Germanic it gave *arhwō, 'an arrow' — for the two parts of archery named the pair from opposite ends. English still keeps both words: arrow (from Old English earh and arwe, through the Norse and West-Germanic forms) and arch (from Latin arcus by way of French). Gothic preserved the northern form as arƕazna, and Old Norse ǫ, genitive örvar, shows the same word. The bow, in other words, was such a culturally central object that it bequeathed both the curve and its missile to modern English, each through a different doorway. The architectural turn. The decisive event for the modern noun came when Roman builders, who had learned to construct with wedge-shaped voussoirs, borrowed the image of the bent bow for the curved structure that carries weight across an opening. Late Latin arcus acquired the architectural sense 'arch,' and from there the word entered the Romance languages wholesale: Italian arco, Spanish arco, and the French arc that gave Middle English arche. In English the noun arch settled in the thirteenth century in exactly that sense.
  • The Roman arch was a genuine engineering revolution: the voussoir arch allowed spans far wider than any stone lintel, and Roman aqueducts, bridges, and monumental gateways spread the form across the empire.
  • The image is constant: the drawn bow is a curve under tension, and the stone arch is a curve under compression — both are 'bent' shapes storing and directing force.
  • The English noun arrived through the French-speaking court and clergy of the thirteenth century, part of the great inflow of French architectural vocabulary (tower, pillar, vault, ceiling).
The derived family. The derived vocabulary soon followed. Archer came from the Old French archier 'bowman,' who was simply the man of the bow; the sport and the surname Archer preserve it. Arc, the geometric curve, was borrowed in Chaucer's day (c. 1387) for the arc of a circle. Arcade, a row of arches, reached English from Italian arcata through French. The learned adjectives arcuate, arcuated, and arciform were stamped from Latin arcuātus 'curved' for technical and scientific use, and medical and anatomical vocabulary uses arcus (as in arcus senilis, the 'senile bow' of the eye). Even the electric 'arc' — the curved luminous discharge between electrodes — takes its name from the shape. Extension and caution. By the early modern period the noun also named any bowed form — the arch of the sky, of the foot, of the eyebrow — keeping the curve while shedding the load-bearing force. The ceremonial sense entered public language with the triumphal arch, the Arch of Titus, the Arc de Triomphe, making 'arch' the emblem of victory, passage, and commemoration. One caution must be registered: the prefix arch- of archbishop, archangel, and the adjective 'arch' ('playfully cunning') is an entirely different word from Greek arkhē, 'rule, chief,' and must never be merged with the builder's arch — a classic false friend. In the Narnia passage the ruined arch — still standing when the roof is gone — is literally the last 'drawn bow' left in the castle wall, and the word keeps all of that history in its curve.
