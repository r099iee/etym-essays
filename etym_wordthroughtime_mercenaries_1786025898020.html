- 14th c. - Entered Middle English (*mercenarie*) from Old French and Latin, meaning a hireling or one who performs service solely for wages, heavily used in religious critiques of corrupt clergy.
- 15th–16th c. - Shifted predominantly into military and political discourse (prominently in Machiavelli), denoting foreign soldiers hired for pay, viewed with suspicion and dread by nation-states.
- 17th–18th c. - Widely used to describe standing European mercenary regiments (Hessians, Swiss guards), while expanding into general English for anyone actuated by sordid monetary motives.
- 19th c. - Solidified in Victorian political rhetoric as a severe moral indictment against commercialized violence and unprincipled politicians.
- 20th–21st c. - Formalized as a distinct legal category under the Geneva Conventions, while finding widespread metaphorical application in corporate culture (*mercenaries vs. missionaries*).
