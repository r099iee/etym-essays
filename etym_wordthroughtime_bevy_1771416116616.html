- [15th-16th century origins] - A collective noun born in courtly and hunting language, naming a company of ladies or a covey of quails and roe-deer; bright, festive and slightly feathery.
- [17th-18th century expansion] - Extends to any animated gathering of women or small creatures; the phrase 'bevy of beauties' becomes the stock usage.
- [19th-century literary habit] - The word thrives in fiction for a lively cluster of young women or birds, always light, colourful and moving together.
- [Contemporary register] - Now mildly archaic and playful, reserved for graceful abundance; a writer uses it to conjure charm rather than numbers.
