Linear derivation chain for CLEAVE (the splitting verb; the "cling" homograph is treated in a note below): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *gleubh-, reconstructed, c. 4000+ BCE: "to tear apart, carve, cleave, hollow out." Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *kleubanan, c. 500 BCE–100 CE: "to split, cleave," a strong verb of class II. Stage 3 — Old English cleofan, attested c. 8th–11th centuries: "to split, separate by a sharp blow"; a class II strong verb with past cleaf and past participle clofen. Stage 4 — Middle English cleven, attested 12th–15th centuries: "to split, cleave"; past clef / clave, past participle cloven. Stage 5 — Modern English cleave, 15th century onward: transitive and intransitive verb, "to split, sever, divide cleanly by force of a blade." Note on the past forms (all real, none invented): cleaved (regular weak, from the 14th century), cleft (from the Old English past), clave (northern strong form, common until c. 1600, preserved in the King James Bible), cloven (the archaic past participle, surviving in "cloven hoof"). Note on the homograph: the English verb cleave "to adhere, cling" is a DIFFERENT word, from Old English clifian "to cling, stick" and ultimately PIE *gloi- "to stick" (the root of clay and glue). The two verbs are etymologically unconnected; their convergence in spelling and pronunciation began in Middle English and is a standing puzzle of English lexicography. Only the splitting verb is treated in the chain above. Cognate side-branch (for completeness): Greek gluphein (γλύφειν) "to carve, engrave," whence glyph, hieroglyph; Latin glubo "to peel, strip the bark" — both probable cognates of *gleubh-. Debated / nuanced note: The connection of Greek gluphein and Latin glubo to *gleubh- is broadly accepted but not without nuance (de Vaan treats the Latin form cautiously). No Indo-Iranian cognate for this root is generally accepted; none is asserted here.
