casket is a noun with two sharply different modern lives: in older and literary English it means “a small box or chest, especially one for jewels and valuables”, while in present-day American English it means “a coffin” — and the whole interesting history of the word is the story of how the second sense grew out of the first. In the passage from The Hobbit, an old man wrapped in cloak and hood bears “a strong casket of iron-bound wood” before Bard and the Elvenking — the Arkenstone, the great heart of the Mountain, is carried in a casket, and Tolkien deliberately uses the venerable, romantic sense of the word: a strong chest of iron-bound wood in which treasure of supreme value is carried. The word here glitters with jewel-box associations that the modern American “coffin” sense has obscured.

Primary sense — a small chest for valuables.

  • A small box or chest, often ornate, for holding jewels, money, letters, relics or other precious small objects: a jewel casket, a casket of pearls. In older and literary English this remains the core sense: royal treasure is kept in caskets; romantic heroines receive caskets of jewels.
  • A strong, secure box for money or documents — the casket is the ancestor of the strongbox; in medieval contexts caskets held charters, coin and relics. The diminutive form (small case) always implies portability and preciousness: a casket is carried, locked, given.
  • The famous literary instance is Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice (1596–98), where Portia’s suitors must choose among three caskets — gold, silver and lead — and the leaden casket holds her portrait; the word thus carries a permanent allegorical echo of appearance versus worth.

Secondary sense — a coffin (chiefly American English).

  • From the mid-19th century, almost certainly as a deliberate euphemism of the funeral trade, casket came in the United States to mean a coffin — specifically an ornate, expensive burial container, often with upholstery, while coffin tends to refer to the plainer, tapered box. The shift is documented from the 1840s–60s and was cemented during and after the American Civil War, a period of mass death in which the funeral industry consciously sought softened language.
  • In American usage today casket is the standard everyday word: “open-casket funeral”, “the casket was draped with the flag”, “remains were placed in a lead-lined casket”. The distinction has even been codified by the funeral industry: a casket is rectangular with a hinged lid; a coffin is six-sided and tapers at the shoulders. Modern cremation practice extends the word to urns: a “cremation casket” is an outer container for cremated remains.
  • In British English this sense remains marked as American; British usage keeps casket mainly for the jewel-box / treasure-chest sense, in poetry, fantasy and historical prose, and sometimes for the elaborate burial container of a head of state (a “state casket”).

Tertiary and technical senses.

  • Receptacle sense: any small enclosed case or receptacle for a precious thing — heraldic and ecclesiastical contexts speak of reliquary caskets.
  • “Casket” as a two-portion gift or keepsake box in modern retail and craft usage — the diminutive flat box used for jewellery, keepsakes and memorial items, reviving the old sense without the royal glamour.
  • Extended modern metaphors: the “casket” of a deceased person as the final containment of a life — funeral-language metaphors (the casket is the last property of the dead) appear in poetry and obituary prose.

Semantic bridge. The route from jewel box to coffin runs through preciousness and dignity. A casket is a chest for things too precious to be treated roughly. When the 19th-century American funeral trade wanted a word for the container of a loved body that would avoid the harsh, functional ring of “coffin”, it reached for the most precious chest it knew — the casket — and the body, like treasure, was enshrined in an iron-bound, silk-lined strongbox. The bridge is thus a euphemism built on the old association of caskets with what is dearly valued; the treasure becomes the dead, and the box becomes the shrine.

Register and morphology. Casket is a diminutive noun (the -et ending, from French, meaning “little”: a little case), and the doubling of the final consonant before the suffix is the normal English spelling pattern for short-vowel forms (case → casket shows the historical source; the modern form is not formed from case but borrowed ready-made). The word is phonetically compact, dignified and slightly old-fashioned in British ears, practical and standard in American ears — a single word that divides the Atlantic on its meaning and its mood.
