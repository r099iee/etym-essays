bides: The third-person singular present form of the verb bide, meaning to wait, remain, or stay in a place or state, especially to wait patiently and steadfastly for a destined event or outcome; by extension, to endure, tolerate, or continue to exist in a given condition without yielding. In the literary context of Dream of the Red Chamber ("The jewel in the casket bides till one shall come to buy"), the verb expresses a dignified, calm, and self-assured patience: the "jewel" (symbolizing Jia Yu-cun's unrecognized talent and virtue) remains secure in its casket, quietly awaiting the arrival of a buyer with the discernment to recognize its true value. The tone is serene, resolute, and stoic—a quiet faith that true merit will be rewarded at the appointed time. 1. Core senses
  • To wait patiently (the dominant modern sense): To remain in expectation of an event or opportunity, with a connotation of quiet confidence. The idiom bide one's time (to wait patiently for the right moment to act) is the most common modern survival, but the verb also appears without the possessive object: "the jewel bides," "she bides," "they bide."
  • To remain, stay, or dwell (archaic sense): In older English, bide simply meant to remain in a place or to dwell—"to bide here," "to bide at home"—a sense close to abide and abode. This is now literary or dialectal.
  • To endure, tolerate, or await (archaic transitive sense): Early English used bide transitively with an object meaning "to await" (bide the event, bide the outcome) or "to endure" (bide pain). "He bided his fate" or "I will bide the storm" are archaic but still understood in literary reading.
  • To continue to exist / to hold on (continuative sense): A thing "bides" when it persists through time in a given state—an old house bides, a hope bides, a virtue bides unrecognized. This is the sense that animates the Dream of the Red Chamber couplet: the jewel persists in patience.
  • Regional/dialectal survival: In some British dialects bide survives as an ordinary verb for "to stay" (e.g., "bide a wee," wait a short while, in Scots and northern English).
2. Register, collocation, and usage notes
  • Collocations: bide one's time, bide a wee (Scots), bide the outcome, bide the event, bide the storm, bide at home, bide till/as/till such time, a biding hope.
  • Register: The verb has receded from ordinary spoken English and now lives in literary, poetic, and idiomatic registers. Its bare form (without "one's time") is markedly archaic and dignified; bide one's time is the one fully idiomatic modern use. The verb carries connotations of patience, endurance, and unshakeable calm.
  • Inflection: Past tense bided (and archaic bode/bided); past participle bided (archaic biden); present third person bides. The archaic strong forms reflect its Old English heritage as a strong verb (bīdan).
  • Distinction from abide: Abide (from Old English ābīdan, "out" + bīdan) now chiefly means "to tolerate, put up with" ("I can't abide him") or, in literary use, "to dwell; to keep (a promise)"; bide stresses the act of waiting or remaining rather than tolerating.
  • Metaphorical reach: Because biding implies confident expectation rather than passive idleness, the word is favored in philosophical and devotional prose for describing faith, destiny, and patience in the face of delay.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge The word descends from Old English bīdan, "to stay, remain, wait, await," itself from the Proto-Germanic verb *bīdaną, "to wait, endure," and ultimately from the Indo-European root *bheidh-, "to trust, persuade, confide." The deep semantic bridge is striking: in the Germanic branch, the root sense of "trusting" was channeled into the temporal domain, producing the meaning "to wait with confidence, to remain because one trusts in time." Old English bīdan was a plain, common verb of dwelling and awaiting, and its derivatives ābīdan gave modern abide and the noun abode (from the Old English past stem bād, a "waiting-place"). Over the centuries the bare verb receded from everyday speech, surviving chiefly in the idiom bide one's time and in the dignified literary usage of which the Dream of the Red Chamber couplet is a perfect example: "the jewel in the casket bides"—it waits, and its waiting is a statement of confident worth, not of helplessness.
