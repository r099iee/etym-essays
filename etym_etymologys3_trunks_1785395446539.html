TRUNK is the story of a single Latin adjective about cutting, and of the astonishing family of English words — trees, bodies, elephants, luggage, roads, and swimming shorts — that grew from it. The word begins at Rome with truncus, an adjective meaning "maimed, cut short, lopped", and a noun meaning "the lopped tree-stem, a stump, a trunk". The core image is negative: a trunk is a tree that has been cut, a thing defined by what has been taken from it. From this Latin seed the word travelled through Old French into English, and there it branched, over seven centuries, into the richest network of transferred senses of any tree-word in the language. THE LATIN ORIGIN At Rome, truncus already had its double life. As an adjective it meant "maimed, cut short, lopped" — a truncated thing, cut off short. As a noun it meant the lopped tree-stem: the stump or the trunk of a tree, conceived as that which has been cut off from root and crown. The verb truncāre "to lop, cut short, mutilate" is the action behind the adjective, and it survives in English as truncate and truncated, still meaning to cut short. The deeper ancestry is obscure: the dictionaries, including Michiel de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin, mark the Proto-Indo-European pedigree as uncertain, and no secure PIE root can be reconstructed. The older attempts to link the word to Greek trýchō "to wear out" remain speculative. Truncus is thus a genuinely Italic formation whose pre-Roman roots are lost — an honest stop on the chain, where the inherited ancestor simply is not known. THE JOURNEY THROUGH FRENCH The Latin noun descended into the Romance languages: Italian tronco, Spanish tronco, Portuguese tronco, all meaning "tree-trunk, stem, log, torso". In French it became tronc, attested from the 12th century with the full range of senses — tree-trunk, torso, and alms-chest, the chest placed in church for offerings. The alms-chest sense is telling: a hollowed tree-trunk was the original strong-box of the medieval church, and the word for the tree became the word for the container made from it, exactly as the English travelling trunk would later be made from a hollowed trunk. Old French also formed tronçon, "a piece cut off", the augmentative that gives English truncheon, the cut-off staff or club; and from Old French trenchier "to cut" comes trench, the dug channel as a cut in the earth. The whole French family of truncus is a family of cutting and of things cut. THE ENGLISH SETTLEMENT English borrowed the word in the 14th century as trunke, meaning "tree-stem, body, box", and it soon settled into the modern form trunk. For four centuries the primary sense remained the tree — the great woody stem between roots and crown — and it is still the primary sense in British English. But the transfers began almost immediately. By the 15th century the human torso was the trunk of the body, on the simple analogy of the main central body from which limbs project, and this anatomical sense was standard for centuries, surviving in medical and everyday usage. By the 16th century a trunk was a large storage chest, built from the hollowed tree-trunk and then by any method, and the travelling trunk became the standard luggage of the age — the strong-box of the road, as the alms-chest had been the strong-box of the church. THE AGE OF TRANSFER The 17th century added two more senses: the elephant's proboscis was called its trunk, from the projecting main body of the face — and this usage, first recorded in 1562, spread into common currency; and the main body of a pillar or carriage was its trunk, the central shaft or frame. The 18th and 19th centuries carried the image onto the map and the railway: a trunk road or trunk line is the main line from which branches diverge, exactly as branches diverge from the tree-trunk, and the trunk call was the long-distance telephone call made through it. In anatomy and medicine the same image produced the trunk of a blood vessel, nerve, or artery — the main channel from which branches divide — and in modern telephony and networking a trunk is the main link carrying many channels between exchanges. THE GARMENT AND THE CAR The 16th century produced trunk-hose, the short loose breeches that covered the trunk of the body, and from these descended the 20th-century swimming trunks — a plural noun, like trousers, for a garment that is two. Meanwhile, in American English, the luggage compartment of a car came to be called the trunk, carrying the word from the hollowed tree-trunk through the travelling chest to the automobile. The modern English word juggles tree, body, box, and car in one compact noun: in British English the tree-stem sense stays primary and the luggage sense lives on in the humble loft-trunk, while in American English the car's trunk is on every street. LITERARY AND CULTURAL LIFE Trunk has a quiet but steady literary life. The phrase "the trunk of the body" is standard in both anatomy and everyday description; "trunk road" is the official word for a main artery of British transport; and the tree-trunk remains one of the most universal images in poetry and prose, from the sacred trunks of sacred groves to the pines of Middle-earth. In The Hobbit, the plural trunks is used with deliberate plainness — the great bare stems of the mountain pines are handholds and shields, objects to grab and shelter behind. The word's long history of transfer is invisible in the sentence; what the reader feels is the solid, vertical, structural weight that has been the trunk's meaning since the tree was first conceived as something cut short, and the word's journey from Rome to Middle-earth is complete.
