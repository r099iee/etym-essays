Clamour begins its recorded life in the proto-language that became both the Italic and the Hellenic families, in the root *kelh₁-, meaning simply "to call, shout, summon". In Greek the root produced kaéō (kaléō, I call), klētós (called), klḗsis (a calling, whence the liturgical word epiklesis), and the compound ekklēsía, the assembly "called out" of the citizen body, which became the Christian church — Latin ecclēsia and English ecclesiastical. In the Italic world the same base hardened into the legal and religious vocabulary of Rome: calāre meant "to call out, announce", and from it the Romans named the Kalends, the first day of the month on which priests "called out" the festivals; the calendārium, the account-book in which debts due on the Kalends were recorded, was the ancestor of our calendar. A second, -m- strengthened form, clāmāre, "to cry out", carried the root into every register of Roman speech: to clāmāre was to shout with grief, rage, protest, or battle-fury, and the noun clāmor named exactly that roar of a crowd or army which Livy and Tacitus describe on the battlefield and in the forum. The Roman world left no doubt about the sound: the clāmor of the legions and of the mob was a weapon in itself, a deliberate, coordinated raising of the voice to terrify and to demand.

It was this noun that crossed into Gallo-Roman speech and Old French as clamour (modern French clameur), which kept the sense of a loud collective outcry but gained a legal colouring: in Old French law a clameur was a public cry of distress or a formal complaint, and this is still visible in the idiom "a clamour for justice". The phonological changes of the passage are modest — the Latin vowel lengths simplify, the medial ā lowers and the final syllable weakens, and the French form loses the accusative ending — but the spelling change matters: the -our ending of Anglo-French and Old French becomes a permanent mark of the word, and it is this form that survives in British English and in Tolkien's text, while American English later trimmed it to clamor. Anglo-French carried the word into Middle English in the 14th century, where clamour described the shouting of crowds, the lamentation of mourners, and the howling of beasts alike; the earliest Middle English uses show both the noun, "a loud outcry", and the verb, "to cry aloud, to demand noisily", so the word arrived already equipped with its full double life.

Over the following centuries the semantic centre drifted from "a single loud cry" to "a sustained, confused uproar of many voices", and by the 18th and 19th centuries a distinct political sense had crystallized: the phrase "clamour for" — clamour for rights, for reform, for bread — hardened into a fixed idiom of urgent collective demand, and the word entered the vocabulary of newspapers, of parliament, and of protest. The older vocal sense never disappeared; it widened to take in bells, the sea, battle, and the animal world, so that any loud, urgent, collective sound could be a clamour. Two pressures shaped this drift. First, the etymology itself: because a clamour is etymologically a calling out, a demand for something, the word has always carried an undertone of wanting that mere noise lacks — a crowd clamours for something, it does not simply make a din. Second, the register of the word: slightly elevated and literary, clamour became the word of journalism and oratory for the voice of the people, louder and more purposeful than uproar, more dignified than racket.

It is exactly this freighted register that Tolkien draws on in The Hobbit (1937). When the ring of Wargs answers their grey chief "all together", their "dreadful clamour" almost makes the hobbit fall out of his pine-tree: the word supplies not just volume but coordination and purpose — the pack is not merely loud, it is collectively demanding, and the echo of the Latin battle-cry and of the crowd's shout for bread hangs over the howling wolves. The word's long journey — from an Indo-European call, through the Roman forum and the French courtroom, into the English mob and the fantasy novel — is a story of sound acquiring meaning: a noise that began as a summons ended as a demand, and every stage of the journey is still audible in the word.

  • Sound and etymology: PIE laryngeal and *kelh₁-; the Latin -m- enlargement clāmāre beside calāre; French sound-changes simplifying the Latin form; the -our spelling preserved in British English, American clamor.
  • Semantic drift: a call → a cry, outcry, battle-cry → a formal complaint (Old French law) → a sustained uproar of many voices → an urgent collective demand ("a clamour for justice"); extension to bells, sea, battle, and beasts.
  • Socio-cultural context: Roman assembly and battlefield rhetoric; medieval French law; English mobs and parliaments; the modern politics of protest; Tolkien's fantasy revival of the battle-cry sense.
  • Literary attestations: Plautus and the Roman historians for clāmor; Old French legal texts for clameur; Middle English 14th-c. texts; the political essays and journalism of the 18th–19th c.; Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937), the Wargs' dreadful clamour in the circle.
