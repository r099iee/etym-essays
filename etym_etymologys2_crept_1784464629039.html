Deep PIE stage: not securely reconstructed. Most handbooks keep the English family under Proto-Germanic *kreupaną and explicitly leave the pre-Germanic stage open; some speculative links to PIE roots meaning "turn, bend, curl" (of the *ger-/*grebh- area) have been floated but are not established. [DEBATED — see note below] → Proto-Germanic *kreupaną (to creep, crawl; class II strong verb), spoken in the late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; continued by Old Norse krjúpa, Dutch kruipen, German kriechen, Old Frisian kriāpa, and Old English crēopan → Old English crēopan (to creep, crawl; strong verb, singular past crēap, plural past crupon, past participle cropen), attested from the 8th century CE → Middle English crepen (to creep), 12th–15th centuries CE, with past and participle forms crop, crope, cropen → Modern English creep (to move slowly and stealthily close to the ground), with the strong preterite and participle crept standardized in the 16th–17th centuries → CREPT — the card's target form: the modern strong past tense (and past participle) of creep. NOTE on the debate: the deep PIE root is not established with certainty; the Germanic chain (*kreupaną → crēopan → crepen → creep/crept) is historically secure, but reconstructing an earlier Indo-European etymon is speculative and is therefore avoided here.
