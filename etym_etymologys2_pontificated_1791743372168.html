Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*póntoh₁s ("path, way, bridge") + *dʰeh₁- ("to put, make, do")
"path-preparer, bridge-maker"
↓
Proto-Italic compound (c. 1000 BCE)
*pontifaks
"maker or maintainer of bridges and ritual pathways"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 5th–1st c. BCE)
pontifex
"high priest, member of the supreme Roman priestly college"

Note: Compounded from pons ('bridge') and facere ('to make'). Scholars debate whether this referred literally to sacred guardianship of the wooden Pons Sublicius across the Tiber, or metaphorically to bridging the human and divine spheres.


↓
Late Latin / Ecclesiastical Latin (c. 4th–6th c. CE)
pontificare
"to officiate or hold office as a bishop or pope"
↓
Medieval Latin (c. 12th c. CE)
pontificatus
"papal or episcopal dignity, office, and administration"
↓
Early Modern English borrowing (c. 1590s–1610s)
pontificate (verb)
"to celebrate pontifical mass; to reign as pope or bishop"
↓
Modern English secular semantic shift (c. 1820s–1850s)
pontificate (verb)
"to speak dogmatically, pompously, and with assumed infallibility"
↓
English past tense / participle (attested modern form)
pontificated
"delivered pompous, dogmatic pronouncements; spoke with pretended infallibility"
