• El Dorado: Similar because both represent legendary geographic locations synonymous with boundless, glittering gold; differs because El Dorado is a sixteenth-century Spanish myth of a golden king and city hidden in the South American interior, symbolizing an elusive, feverish imperial quest, whereas Pactolus is an actual historical river in Anatolia whose physical alluvial sands supported genuine monetary economies.
  • Bonanza: Similar because both signify an overwhelming source of great wealth and prosperity; differs because bonanza (from Spanish bonanza, 'fair weather, calm sea') originally referred to the discovery of a rich pocket of mineral ore in a mine and modernly designates any sudden financial windfall, lacking the classical mythological and hydronymic heritage of Pactolus.
  • Golconda: Similar because both are actual historical geographical locations whose names became proverbial emblems of inexhaustible treasure; differs because Golconda was a medieval sultanate and fortress city in central India famous for producing the world’s largest diamonds (including the Koh-i-Noor), whereas Pactolus specifically denotes golden alluvial river sands and classical Greco-Lydian antiquity.
  • Cornucopia: Similar because both denote an endless, overflowing fountainhead of riches and abundance; differs because cornucopia (the mythological horn of Amalthea) represents fruitful agricultural, natural, and culinary abundance, whereas Pactolus specifically signifies metallic wealth, golden treasure, and financial coinage.
  • Goldmine: Similar because both refer to an inexhaustible repository of valuable assets; differs because goldmine is a literal or common commercial metaphor for any highly lucrative enterprise, business model, or raw dataset, lacking the lyrical literary resonance and mythological purification narrative of Pactolus.
  • Treasure-trove: Similar because both describe a concentrated accumulation of precious riches; differs because treasure-trove legally and historically refers to hidden caches of money, plate, or bullion discovered underground with unknown ownership, rather than an open, flowing natural riverbed.
