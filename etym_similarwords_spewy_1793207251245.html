• Boggy: Similar because both describe waterlogged ground that yields underfoot; differs because boggy evokes peat bogs and mossy hollows, while spewy stresses the active oozing-up of water in gravel or loam.
  • Marshy: Similar because both mean wetland ground saturated with water; differs because marshy is the neutral geographical term for marshland, while spewy is dialectal and pictures the ground discharging water like a mouth.
  • Waterlogged: Similar because both describe soil saturated past its capacity to drain; differs because waterlogged is plain technical prose for saturation, while spewy adds the vivid image of water spurting upward.
  • Sodden: Similar because both mean soaked through with wetness; differs because sodden stresses heaviness from absorbed water (sodden clothes, sodden fields), while spewy stresses water welling out under pressure.
  • Swampy: Similar because both picture low wet ground unfit for tillage; differs because swampy suggests stagnant pools and rank vegetation, while spewy suggests thin hungry gravel that merely weeps.
  • Oozy: Similar because both describe matter slowly seeping out; differs because oozy stresses thick slow mud or discharge, while spewy stresses a livelier spurting or welling action.
  • Spongy: Similar because both describe ground that gives elastically under the foot; differs because spongy is neutral about wetness (a spongy cake, spongy turf), while spewy is always wet and always pejorative.
  • Squelchy: Similar because both evoke wet ground that sucks and spurts at boots; differs because squelchy names the sound of treading mud, while spewy names the ground's habit of discharging water.
  • Miry: Similar because both condemn wet muddy ground in rustic diction; differs because miry stresses deep clinging mud (mired carts, miry lanes), while spewy stresses thin gravel oozing cleanish water.
