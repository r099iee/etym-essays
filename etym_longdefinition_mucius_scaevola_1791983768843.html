Mucius Scaevola is a Roman name, not a common noun. It consists of a nomen gentilicium, Mucius, which identifies the gens Mucia, and a cognomen, Scaevola, literally “the little left hand”, that is, “the left-handed”. In the source sentence — Cicero “was put under the guidance of the greatest lawyer of the day—in this case Mucius Scaevola” — the name denotes a particular member of that family: Quintus Mucius Scaevola, called “the Augur” (c. 159–88 BCE), consul in 117 BCE, member of the augural college, and the most respected jurist of his generation.

Which Scaevola is meant. The name was hereditary, and several distinguished lawyers bore it within two generations, so historians disambiguate them by additional titles.

  • Quintus Mucius Scaevola “the Augur” (c. 159–88 BCE): consul 117 BCE, augur, legal expert and political elder. Cicero was attached to him as a young man and listened to his legal opinions; the De Amicitia is framed as his recollection of a conversation held in 129 BCE. This is the man the source means.
  • Quintus Mucius Scaevola “the Pontifex” (consul 95 BCE, murdered 82 BCE): the Augur's younger cousin and son-in-law of Crassus the orator. He was the more original jurist, author of a systematic eighteen-book treatise on the civil law, and is often called the founder of scientific Roman jurisprudence. Cicero studied with him too.
  • Gaius Mucius Scaevola: the legendary hero of 508 BCE who thrust his right hand into a brazier before Lars Porsena. He is a figure of myth and exemplary history, not the lawyer of this sentence.

The legendary Scaevola and the origin of the cognomen. Livy tells how Gaius Mucius, a young Roman, entered the camp of the Etruscan king Porsena to assassinate him, killed a scribe by mistake, and was condemned to the fire. To show what Romans could endure, he held his right hand in the flames until it was consumed, whereupon Porsena, astonished, made peace and withdrew. The story explains the surname: Scaevola, “left-handed”, because the hero had destroyed his right hand and thereafter used the left. Whether the legend generated the cognomen or the cognomen generated the legend is impossible to say, but the second is at least as likely — an aetiological story attached to a family name that already existed.

How Roman names work here.

  • A patrician or notable plebeian man normally carried three names: a praenomen (Quintus), a nomen (Mucius) and a cognomen (Scaevola). The nomen is the clan name and passes to all members; the cognomen marks a branch or an individual and often becomes hereditary in its turn.
  • Cognomina were frequently descriptive: Naso “nose”, Balbus “stammerer”, Labeo “thick-lipped”, Crassus “fat”, Cato “shrewd”. Scaevola belongs to this class, and its diminutive form adds a touch of familiarity or euphemism.
  • Because the same combination recurs across generations, “Mucius Scaevola” in a text is a family designation before it is a personal one; the reader must use the context, or an extra title such as Augur or Pontifex, to know which man is meant.
  • In English the name is a proper noun with no plural and no generic sense. It is italicised or set in roman according to house style, and it is never used as a common noun: one cannot speak of “a Mucius Scaevola” except as a deliberate figure for a great jurist.

Why this name matters in the reading. Cicero's education had two halves, Greek and Roman, and the source names both: Molon the rhetorician for style, Mucius Scaevola for law. Roman law was not a university subject but a craft transmitted within aristocratic families and learned by attending on a distinguished jurist, listening to his responses, and memorising his formulae. To be “put under the guidance of” a Scaevola was therefore the legal equivalent of studying rhetoric with Molon: an apprenticeship in the practical knowledge that a senatorial career required. The name also carries a political charge. The Scaevolae stood for the old Senatorial order, its law, its dignity and its caution, at the very moment when Marius and Sulla were demonstrating that military force could override both.

Later echoes. In Latin prose Scaevola became a byword for juristic authority, and later Roman lawyers cite the libri Scaevolae as a standard of precision. In European literature the legendary hand-burner is a stock example of Roman courage — Dante places him among the heroes in the Paradiso — while the jurist's name survives in histories of law. The two figures are constantly confused, which is why a careful note must say which one is being named.
