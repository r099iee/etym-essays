Part I — The story of the CART

The vehicle-name at the heart of cartage is one of Germanic's oldest transport words, but its origins are strikingly uncertain. Old English cræt (c. 8th century) "cart, wagon" has exact cognates across the north of Europe — Old Norse kartr, and the continental Germanic family behind Middle High German kratte "basket" — which points to a shared Proto-Germanic noun *kart- (or *kratt-), "a wicker-bodied vehicle." The image is important: early carts were not boxy timber wagons but light vehicles whose body was woven wickerwork, a plaiting technology as old as basket-making itself.

Beyond Germanic the trail grows faint. Many scholars connect the group to a PIE root *ger- / *gert- "to turn, wind, plait," the root that elsewhere yields words for baskets and woven things; on this view the cart is literally "the plaited (wickerwork) thing on wheels." But the PIE stage is conjectural — it rests on a handful of vaguely similar forms rather than a solid correspondence set — and the OED treats the deeper origin of Germanic *kart- as "uncertain," possibly a northern European substratum or an early loan within Europe. Any account of "cart" must therefore begin honestly: we are sure of the Germanic word, unsure of the step behind it.

  • Old English: cræt, the everyday word for a cart or wagon, attested in glosses, charters, and prose from the earliest records.
  • Middle English: cart (13th c.), firmly established; the same word that names the cart of Chaucer's miller and of countless manorial accounts.
  • Semantic life: the cart was a workhorse — two-wheeled for field and farm, larger drays for town and port; "cart" kept a plain, labouring register, in contrast to the fine "chariot" or "carriage."
  • Modern shifts: the horse has gone, but the word was recycled for every small wheeled carrier — hand cart, shopping cart, golf cart — a classic case of a term outliving its technology and reattaching to its successors.

Part II — The story of the suffix -AGE

The second ingredient, the suffix -age, travelled a very different route: it came into English through the Romance languages and the Norman Conquest. Its Latin ancestor was -āticum, the neuter of the adjective-suffix -āticus ("pertaining to, belonging to"), used in Late Latin to form nouns denoting a state, charge, or payment — a suffix of belonging that easily became a suffix of fees and dues.

Old French turned -āticum into -age (with the loss of Latin endings that reshaped the whole Romance noun system), and with it French built a huge family of nouns for actions, results, states, and charges: voyage, heritage, language, courage, message, usage. After 1066 the Normans poured this vocabulary into English, and the suffix became one of the most productive in the language. Crucially for our word, -age specialised early in commercial English into naming charges for services: postage (the charge for the post), towage, pilotage, anchorage, wharfage, porterage, portage — and, by the same pattern, cartage. Each step of goods movement earned its own "-age" fee, and the English of the 17th–19th centuries is full of them.

Part III — Fusion: the birth of CARTAGE

English simply welded the two: cart + -age, "the action of carting" and "the charge for carting," in the manner of its sister words. The chronology is well documented:

  • 1610s: first recorded use in the concrete sense "carting, transportation by cart."
  • 1731: first recorded use in the money sense, "the charge or fee for carting," the meaning that dominates commercial documents.
  • 18th–19th centuries: a standard line-item of trade — "cartage and warehousing," "cartage and wharfage" appear across ledgers, customs schedules, insurance policies, and railway and dock company reports, where cartage was the vital road-leg connecting ship and railhead to warehouse and shop.
  • 20th century: with motorisation the act is more often called haulage or road haulage, but cartage survives in logistics contracts, moving-and-storage services, and legal definitions; the specialised term drayage (from dray, a low flat cart) continues the precise terminal-to-terminal meaning.

Semantically the word has been a model of stability: two senses (act, fee) from 1610 to today, with no drift, no slang, no figurative extension. Socioculturally, though, it changed colour — from the living jargon of the waggoner and the wharf to a slightly antique technical word, its horse-and-cart picture now the very thing that gives it charm and period flavour. In the passage from The Hobbit that frames this card, Smaug sneers at the dwarves' "delivery," "cartage," "armed guards and tolls" — a reminder of how completely these terms of medieval trade survived into the modern commercial imagination.
