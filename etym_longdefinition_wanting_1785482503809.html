In this passage on the sixteenth-century revaluation of classical literature, wanting appears in the fixed idiom "found wanting": once English poets, scholars and educators had placed the verse of Greece and Rome "on the highest of pedestals", it was inevitable that native English literary achievement should come under fresh scrutiny, and equally inevitable - "until the last quarter of the century" - that in the ensuing judgement the home-grown product should be found wanting. The tone is formal, quasi-judicial, almost forensic: the word carries the flavour of an assessment, a measuring, a weighing in the scales. To be "found wanting" is not to be wicked or false but to be insufficient - to come up short when inspected, to be weighed and found lighter than the standard demands. The participle here leans on the ancient sense of want as "lack" rather than on its modern sense of "desire", and it is precisely this double life of the verb that gives the idiom its special colour. At the most literal level, wanting is the present participle of the verb to want, and it functions in English as an adjective, a preposition and part of a verbal phrase. Its core modern adjective sense is "lacking, deficient, falling short of what is required". PRIMARY SENSES OF WANTING TODAY:
  • "Found wanting" (idiom, 16th c. onwards): judged deficient, measured against a standard and found short of it. The phrase has the ring of a trial or a weighing; it is used of people, performances, arguments, defences, characters and institutions that fail inspection ("the garrison was found wanting in discipline and courage"). This is the sense of the present passage.
  • Lacking, deficient, absent (adjective): "wanting in judgement", "a book wanting in originality"; also of a missing part - "the machine is wanting a wheel", "one volume is wanting from the set".
  • Without (preposition, formal/archaic register): "wanting his consent, no change is possible" - equivalent to "in the absence of".
  • Falling short of a standard or expectation (adjective): a performance, translation or argument "wanting" fails to meet the required level; often followed by "in" plus an abstract noun ("wanting in rigour", "wanting in taste").
  • Present participle of want in the "desire" sense (modern everyday use): "I am wanting to leave early" - the word as the active participle of the modern verb of desire, now common in colloquial and progressive constructions ("the child is wanting attention"). This is the sense most modern speakers meet first, and it sits at the opposite pole from the lack-sense.
  • Present participle of want in the "need" sense (formal): "plants wanting water", "a generation wanting a model to follow" - wanting as "needing, in need of".
  • In archaic and biblical English: wanting = "deficient" is frequent in older translations and in the Authorised Version ("being weighed in the balances and found wanting"); the word also carried the older sense of a "wanting" person - one lacking in sense or reason, feeble-minded - in older colloquial use.
  • In technical and statistical contexts: "data wanting" - values absent from a record; the idea of a missing value that must be accounted for is a direct descendant of the oldest lack-sense.
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE - FROM LACK TO DESIRE: The history of want is the story of a single great reversal. Old Norse vanta meant "to be lacking, to be destitute of", and Middle English borrowed the verb in the sense "to lack, to be without". From "to lack" came the noun want, "deficiency, need, poverty" - a person in want lacks necessities. The crucial pivot occurred when English speakers began to say "I want" to mean "I feel the want of": from perceiving a deficiency ("I lack food") the verb moved to expressing the psychological state that accompanies deficiency ("I desire food"). By the early eighteenth century (the change is generally dated to about 1706) the desire-sense had overtaken the lack-sense in the ordinary verb, so that modern "I want X" almost always means "I desire X" and only rarely "I lack X". The participial adjective wanting, however, never made that journey: it remained frozen in the older lack-sense, and through the idiom "found wanting" the ancient deficiency lives on in formal, quasi-judicial English. The idiom therefore feels like a deliberate archaism - a word of weighing and verdicts preserved from the language of the sixteenth century. That is exactly its power in the passage: the critics weigh native English verse, and the verdict is that it is wanting - empty of the qualities, the author implies, that make poetry worthy. Distinctions against near-synonyms: "lacking" is the plainest term for simple absence; "deficient" is formal and quantitative, naming a measurable shortfall; "inadequate" points to a failure to serve a purpose or reach a level; "inferior" implies a comparative judgement of rank; "devoid of" means completely empty of, total absence; "short of" is informal and physical. Wanting occupies a middle, idiomatic ground: it suggests a shortfall discovered by scrutiny - which is precisely why the word has lived for centuries in the set phrase "found wanting", and why it still feels most at home there.
