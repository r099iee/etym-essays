- Antiquity & Middle Ages (c. 12th–14th c.) — Borrowed into Middle English from Old French peutre / peltre; rapidly established in medieval England as the premier tableware of affluent merchants, guildhalls, and taverns, regulated strictly by the London Pewterers' Guild (chartered 1473).
- Early Modern & Colonial Eras (16th–18th c.) — Peak ubiquity across British and American colonial homes; serving as the universal standard for spoons, porringers, tankards, and platters before the widespread affordability of glazed porcelain.
- Industrial Revolution (Late 18th–19th c.) — Plagued by medical recognition of chronic lead poisoning from leaded pewter vessels; domestic usage collapses as Wedgwood ceramics, glass, and cheap electroplated silverware (EPNS) flood consumer markets.
- 20th c. — Reformulated as a non-toxic, lead-free tin-antimony alloy (Britannia metal) for decorative souvenirs, ceremonial quaichs, and miniature tabletop figurines; simultaneously adopted widely as an evocative color term for stormy skies and muted gray interiors.
- 21st c. — Cherished in heritage interior design, historic restoration, and artisan craft casting as a timeless decorative aesthetic representing antique warmth and understated dignity.
