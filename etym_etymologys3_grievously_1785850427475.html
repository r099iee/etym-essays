The history of grievously is the history of a word made of weight. Everything begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷreh₂-, meaning 'heavy'. This root, with its full-grade variant *gʷerh₂-, is one of the best-attested reconstructions in the Indo-European family: Greek barus 'heavy' descends from it (with the labiovelar weakening to b), Sanskrit gurú 'heavy, weighty, venerable' continues it in India (from the zero-grade *gʷr̥h₂-), and Latin gravis 'heavy, weighty, severe, grave' is its most famous Italic reflex. The same root, in its 'heavy stone' specialization, even produced the Germanic word for a hand-mill — Old English cweorn, the quern, literally the heavy stone — showing how deeply the idea of weight ran through the family. In Latin the root flourished: gravis gave gravitās 'heaviness, dignity, seriousness' (source of English gravity, gravitate, grave 'serious'), the verb gravāre 'to load, burden, oppress' (source of English aggravate and aggrieve), and gravid 'heavy with young'. The semantic world of the Latin family was thoroughly physical: weight, load, burden — and from weight came dignity, because in Roman thinking what was heavy was serious, solemn, and weighty in judgment. The transfer from Latin to French is where the modern shape appears. In Vulgar Latin and Old French, gravāre became grever, a verb meaning 'to burden, to oppress, to injure, to afflict' — the act of laying a weight on someone, literally or figuratively. From grever, Old French built the adjective grief, 'heavy, burdensome, sorrowful', and its variant grevos, greveus, built with the suffix -os (Latin -ōsus, 'full of, characterized by'). Old French also produced the noun grief, 'sorrow, grievance' — the burden felt. When the Normans crossed the Channel they brought this vocabulary with them, and Anglo-French handed it to English: the verb greven 'to grieve, to oppress, to injure' (Middle English greven, source of to grieve and aggrieve), the noun grief (source of grievance), and the adjective grevous 'heavy, burdensome, painful, severe, harmful', first recorded in English in the 13th century in religious and legal writing. The palatalization is audible in the spelling: where Latin had gravis with a hard g, Old French pronounced the g before the front vowel as /dʒ/, and English borrowed the word with that affricate, so that modern English grieve, grief, and grievous all begin with /ɡr/ written -griev- but historically shaped by French sound change. The adverb grievously — the exact word of this card — appears in the fourteenth century, formed in the ordinary English way by adding -ly (from Old English -līce, 'in the manner of') to the borrowed adjective. Its earliest uses are religious and moral: to sin grievously, to offend grievously, to trespass grievously — to transgress in a heavy, weighty manner, the adverb carrying the ancient image of a weight laid on the soul. In late medieval and early modern devotional prose the word became the standard intensifier for moral failure, the language of confession and penance, and it carried this solemn, pulpitary weight into the early modern period. It is the word of grave offence, not of casual annoyance. The same centuries saw the adverb in secular use for afflictions and harsh conditions: a realm grievously oppressed, a people grievously afflicted — the collective burdens of war, famine, and misrule. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the adverb moved outward from the pulpit into law, medicine, and tragic narrative. Legal and quasi-legal prose used grievously for serious offences and injuries — 'grievously hurt', 'grievously wounded', 'grievously wronged' — and the adjective laid the foundation for the fixed statutory phrase grievous bodily harm, codified in English law by the Offences against the Person Act 1861, where GBH denotes really serious injury and remains a central category of criminal law in England, Wales, and much of the Commonwealth. Military and medical reportage adopted the adverb for life-threatening wounds, so that a soldier could be reported grievously wounded with a precise, clinical gravity. At the same time the word's emotional sense deepened through the verb grieve: to be grievously affected was to be weighed down by sorrow and loss, and the adverb carried this fusion of gravity and grief into the nineteenth-century novel, where a character might be grievously disappointed, grievously deceived, or grievously bereaved. Nineteenth-century historians used it of whole kingdoms and institutions — a nation grievously oppressed, a treasury grievously burdened by high taxes, the collective counterpart of personal injury and exactly the usage of the context sentence about the kingdom burdened by taxes and border skirmishes. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries have preserved the word's formal dignity while narrowing its everyday use. In ordinary modern speech grievously is comparatively rare, chosen deliberately for solemnity, irony, or literary flavour; its plain synonyms severely and seriously do the unmarked work. But the word's two etymological strands remain audible to anyone who hears it: the old 'heavily, weightily' sense and the newer 'with grief and sorrow'. To say that a kingdom had been grievously burdened by high taxes is to reach for both at once — the physical weight of the burden and the sorrow it causes. Grievously is thus a fossil with a live core: three thousand years after a root for 'heavy' was first spoken, the word still means, at bottom, 'in a way that weighs you down'. Summary of the shift:
  • Proto-Indo-European *gʷreh₂- 'heavy': the root of a great family — Greek barus, Sanskrit gurú, Latin gravis, Germanic quern.
  • Latin gravis 'heavy, weighty, severe' → gravāre 'to burden' → gravitās 'gravity, dignity'.
  • Old French grever 'to burden, injure' and grevos / grief 'heavy, burdensome, sorrowful' (with /dʒ/ palatalization).
  • Middle English grevous 'grievous, harmful, severe' (13th–14th c.) → grevously 'grievously' (14th c., with native -ly).
  • 16th–17th c.: legal, medical, and tragic use; 19th c.: the fixed phrase grievous bodily harm (GBH).
  • Modern: formal, solemn, literary — 'with tragic weight', the modern heir of the ancient 'heavily'.
