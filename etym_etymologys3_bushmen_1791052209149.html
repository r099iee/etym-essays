Bushmen is a compound of bush (wild country) + men (plural of man). The two elements have entirely different ages: bush as wild land is a Dutch colonial word, man/men is ancient Indo-European. They join at the Cape in the 18th century to label the San hunter-gatherers who lived beyond Dutch farms, and enter English in the 1780s.

1. BUSH: English bush (shrub) and bush (wild country) are the same word diverged by colonisation. The root is Proto-Germanic *buskaz, *busk- thicket, via Old French bosc, Middle Dutch bosch, Middle English bush (thicket). In the Netherlands bosch meant managed woodland (as in 's-Hertogenbosch). At the Cape, Dutch boers applied bosch to the opposite: dry scrub, thornveld, unfarmed wilderness beyond settlement. Cape Dutch Boschjesman (little bush man) and Bosman meant one who lives in that wilderness, a forager, an outlaw. English settlers in South Africa and Australia borrowed this colonial sense: the bush is anywhere uncleared, uncivilised from a settler view — to go bush, bushcraft, bush telegraph. Thus Bushman originally means wild-land dweller, not shrub-man.

  • Phonetics: Dutch -sch /sx/ simplifies to English -sh /ʃ/: bosch > bush. Spelling Bushman with -sh reflects English adaptation by 1780s travellers (Sparrman, Barrow).
  • Australian parallel: Same Dutch-derived bush gives Australian bushman (white frontiersman), showing the word meant frontier-dweller generally before it fixed on San peoples.

2. MAN / MEN: Man continues PIE *man- (human, person), also *dʰǵʰem- earthling in some analyses, via Proto-Germanic *mann- person. Old English mann meant person of either sex (as in wifmann = woman), plus adult male. Plural men shows ancient i-mutation: mann > menn > men (vowel fronted before plural -iz). By Middle English man is both generic human and male. In compounds -man / -men denotes inhabitant or worker: Englishman, countryman, Bushman/Bushmen. The plural Bushmen therefore follows regular English morphology, though applied to peoples who do not call themselves by it.

Together, Boschjesman > Bushman enters English travel writing 1780s-1800s (John Barrow's Travels in Southern Africa, 1801) to distinguish foraging San (Bushmen) from herding Khoikhoi (Hottentots). Nineteenth-century anthropology hardened the split: Hottentots herd, Bushmen hunt. The source sentence — with the Bushmen of Africa it is always either a feast or a famine — repeats that stereotype: hunters gorge on a kill then starve, lacking storage. Early-20th-century economics used them as textbook improvident savages.

Twentieth-century research transformed understanding: San languages (!Kung, Ju/'hoansi, !Xóõ) with clicks, tracking, persistence hunting, egalitarian sharing, andLater, apartheid and independence made naming political. San communities increasingly prefer San, Ju, or specific group names (Ju/'hoansi, !Kung, Hai//om), seeing Bushman as outsider label implying wildness, though some in South Africa reclaimed Bushman (e.g., Khomani San, Bushman councils) and Afrikaans Boesman remains everyday. English style now advises San (or specific name), with Bushmen acceptable in historical quotation or when communities self-identify.

Debate note: Whether bushman for Australia and Bushman for Africa are independent extensions of the same Cape Dutch source or parallel developments from English bush (thicket) is debated, but Cape priority (Boschjesman 1680s) predates Australian bushman (1790s), supporting diffusion via Dutch seamen and English travellers. No PIE root for bush-as-wilderness beyond Germanic thicket; the semantic leap thicket > wilderness is colonial, not ancient.
