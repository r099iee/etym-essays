PIE *bʰāǵos “the beech tree (Fagus), or a similar nut-bearing tree” (reconstructed; the famous “beech root” of the Indo-European homeland debate; c. 4500–3500 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *bōkō “beech tree; beech wood used for writing tablets, hence book” (reconstructed; the same root underlies the “book” words across Germanic) → Old English bēce “beech” (with the characteristic palatalisation of the root consonant; beside bōc “book, writing tablet”; attested from the 8th c.) → Middle English beche “beech” (12th–15th c.) → Early Modern English beech (16th c.) → Modern English beech “the smooth-barked tree of the genus Fagus; its wood” — and beech-log, the collocation used in The Hobbit => beech (modern English noun) DEBATE / NUANCE: The root *bʰāǵos is securely reconstructed, but its original referent is debated: in Greek it shifted to the oak (phēgós, the sacred oak of Zeus at Dodona), in some Slavic forms to the elder (Russian buzina “elderberry”), and in Latin and Germanic it stayed the beech (Latin fāgus). The famous “beech argument” — that the wild beech does not grow east of a line through Poland and western Ukraine, so the PIE homeland must have lain west of that line if the word really named the beech — is a celebrated but contested inference in Indo-European studies. The Germanic “book” sense (*bōkō → bōc, Buch, bók) developed because runic writing tablets were made of beech wood.
