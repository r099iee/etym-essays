• Steak-and-ale pie: Similar because it is a British meat pie in a pastry crust; differs because it is hot, sauced, two-crust, eaten from a plate with gravy, whereas the pork pie is cold, jellied, and hand-held.
  • Sausage roll: Similar because it is a savoury meat-in-pastry snack; differs because it uses flaky puff pastry, has no jelly, and its filling is a distinct sausage forcemeat rather than the seasoned chopped pork of a pie.
  • Cornish pasty: Similar because it is a portable cold or warm hand-pie; differs because it is a crimped semicircular beef-and-potato miner’s meal, not the round, moulded, jellied market-town pork pie.
  • Raised pie: Similar because it names the whole family (game pie, veal-and-ham pie, pork pie) sharing the hot-water crust technique; differs because it is the generic class name, while PORK-PIE is the specific, most famous member.
  • Pork scratchings / crackling: Similar because both are pork products with a savoury, pub-culture register; differs because scratchings are fried pork skin, not a pastry dish, and share none of the pie’s form.
  • Pork: Similar because it supplies the meat and names the pie’s primary ingredient; differs because pork is the meat itself (from Old French porc), with no pastry, jelly, or pie-form — the element, not the compound.
  • Mince-pie: Similar because it is a traditional British closed pastry with a distinctive filling; differs because it is sweet, spiced, festive, and eaten warm at Christmas, the exact opposite register of the cold savoury pork pie.
  • Picnic pie: Similar because it is a cold, jellied, portable meat pastry made for eating outdoors; differs because picnic pie is a looser commercial term for cold pies generally, lacking the pork-pie’s precise hot-water-crust identity.
