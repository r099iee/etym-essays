Linear derivation chains for GROANED (two morphemes: root 'groan' + weak past suffix '-ed'). CHAIN 1 — root 'groan' (imitative; no PIE ancestry): 1. Proto-Germanic *granōną 'to weep, murmur, groan' (reconstructed weak verb; imitative or sound-symbolic in origin — the root has no reconstructible PIE ancestor). 2. Old English grānian 'to groan, lament' (attested from c. 900 in early prose). 3. Middle English gronen / groyne / grone 'to groan' (development of the Old English verb). 4. English groan (verb) 'to utter a deep low sound of pain or grief'; the noun 'a groan' follows by the 14th c. 5. groan + -ed → GROANED (weak preterite and past participle). Note on cognates: Middle Dutch groenen / groynen 'to groan, grumble' is a probable Germanic cognate, and German greinen 'to whimper, whine' is a parallel formation from the related expressive stem *grīnan-. Beyond Germanic the chain stops: groan is coined from the sound itself, and Greek γρύζειν (grūzein) 'to grunt' and Latin grunnīre 'to grunt, grumble' are parallel echoic formations, NOT cognates. English grunt (Old English grunnettan) is a sister expressive coinage in the same workshop. CHAIN 2 — suffix '-ed' (the one genuine Indo-European element): 1. PIE *dʰeh₁- 'to put, place, do' (source of the weak-past ending; sister reflexes Latin fēcī 'I made', Greek ἔθηκα éthēka 'I placed', Sanskrit दधाति dadhāti 'he puts', English do). 2. Proto-Germanic *-dē / *-daz — dental preterite and past-participle marker (a Germanic innovation that 'made' the past tense by adding a d). 3. Old English -ode / -de / -ed — weak past-tense endings. 4. English -ed — past-tense and past-participle suffix. 5. groan + -ed → GROANED.
