Porphyry is, in its primary geological sense, a very hard igneous rock with large crystals (phenocrysts) embedded in a fine-grained matrix, most famously the deep purple-red imperial porphyry of ancient Egypt quarried at Mons Porphyrites. In the source sentence the hero approaches a third Palace, of ______, roofed with golden tiles: a palace built of, or faced with, this princely purple stone, outshining the Hawk's and the Eagle's palaces as imperial purple outshines every lesser colour.

Core meaning in detail: Geologists use porphyry for texture as much as substance: any rock with two crystal sizes, big feldspar or quartz phenocrysts swimming in a fine groundmass, counts as porphyritic, and whole families (granite porphyry, diorite porphyry, andesite porphyry) take the name. But the lay and literary sense remains the Roman one: porphyry the purple stone of emperors, so hard it could be worked only with emery and slave labour, so costly that a sarcophagus of it declared the sleeper's rank louder than any inscription.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Imperial purple-red rock (primary historical sense, from antiquity): the red porphyry of the Eastern Desert, green porphyry of Greece, black porphyry of Egypt. The bridge from the Greek colour-word is the stone's blush: porphyreos meant purple, and the rock that wore imperial purple took the colour's name entire.
  • Porphyritic texture (geological technical sense, 18th c.): any igneous rock with large crystals in a fine matrix. The bridge generalises from the famous example to the structure: the imperial stone's speckled texture became the type-specimen, and all rocks sharing its two-stage crystalline architecture inherited its title.
  • Copper-porphyry ores (mining sense, modern): great low-grade copper and gold deposits hosted in porphyritic intrusions, the source of most of the world's copper. The bridge is the host rock: miners named the ore-body for the speckled intrusive that carries it, turning an emperor's luxury into an engineer's livelihood.
  • The colour purple (poetic sense): robed in porphyry, a porphyry sunset. The bridge runs backward from stone to shade: because the stone was purple, porphyry can stand for the purple itself, in verse where purple alone would sound too plain.
  • Emblem of empire and sanctity (symbolic sense): porphyry sarcophagi, porphyry columns in Hagia Sophia and St Peter's, the Porphyry Throne. The bridge is association turned to meaning: centuries of imperial use made the stone itself signify power, so that to build in porphyry, like the fairy-tale palace of the source, is to claim kingship in masonry.

Register and connotation: porphyry is learned and sumptuous, belonging to museums, geology lectures, and imperial history. It never sounds cheap: even the mining engineer's copper porphyry borrows grandeur from the emperors' stone. In fairy tales it does special work, telling the child reader that the third palace, larger and more elegant than its rivals, is the true seat of power, purple as Caesar's cloak and hard as fate.
