ASCENT is a noun meaning an act of rising, climbing, or moving upward — physically up a mountain, hill, staircase, ladder, or into the air — and, by figurative extension, a rise to higher status, power, achievement, or civilization. It is the noun counterpart of the verb ascend and the nominal twin in the familiar pair ascent / descent: just as descent is the act of going down, ascent is the act of going up. In the card's context — "The whole of King's Landing must witness his __________" — the word is used in its political, figurative sense: the rise of a claimant to the throne, an ascent to power that all must witness. It also appears in the example sentences in both senses: "Their __________ up the mountain was arduous" (physical) and "His rapid __________ to power shocked his rivals" (political).

Core sense 1 — physical climbing and rising: The act of climbing or moving upward: the ascent of a mountain, the ascent of a staircase, the balloon's ascent into the sky. As a concrete noun it can also mean the upward path or slope itself ("a steep ascent"), the ground that rises before the climber. Related physical uses include rock climbing (an "ascent" is a completed climb of a route), mountaineering records (the "first ascent" of a peak — the first climb to a summit), and ballooning and early aviation (the "ascent" of an aircraft or of the balloon).

Core sense 2 — figurative rise in status, power, achievement: Progress to higher rank, office, wealth, or eminence: "his rapid ascent to power", "her ascent through the ranks", "the ascent of man" (civilizational and evolutionary progress — famously the title of Jacob Bronowski's 1973 television series on the history of science and human development). This is a social and political metaphor: climbing the ladder of society.

Secondary sense 3 — technical uses (aviation, spaceflight, music, geometry, mathematics):
  • Aviation and rocketry: the "ascent" is the climb phase of flight; "rate of ascent" or "rate of climb" is the vertical velocity; a rocket's "ascent stage" is the part that lifts from a lower orbit or from the lunar surface (as in the Apollo Lunar Module's ascent stage).
  • Music: an "ascent" is a rise in pitch, an ascending melodic passage or scale.
  • Geometry and topography: "ascent" denotes an upward slope, incline, or grade; the "ascent" and "descent" of a route define its vertical profile.
  • Mathematics: alongside the famous method of "infinite descent" in number theory, "ascent" names the reverse, rising operation in some constructive proofs.
  • Astronomy and ballistics: the "ascending node" of an orbit and the rising of celestial bodies share the same root.

Secondary sense 4 — abstract and spiritual rising: In abstract writing, "ascent" names any upward course — moral, spiritual, or intellectual: "the ascent of the soul" (a Neoplatonic and mystical image of the soul's rise to God), "the ascent of reason". The word carries an upward vector in all these uses.

Derived and related words: ascend (verb), ascension (the act of rising; capitalized, the Christian feast of Christ's ascension into heaven), ascendant (rising; in astrology, the sign rising at birth; figuratively, "in the ascendant" = rising in power), and ascender (in typography, the part of a letter such as b, d, h, l that rises above the x-height). The opposite pair is descend / descent. Note the spelling and homophone trap: ascent (the noun, with -sc-) is pronounced exactly like assent (agreement — a different word, from Latin ad- + sentīre 'to feel'), but the two share nothing but sound; ascend keeps its -d while the noun drops it.

Semantic bridge summary: The chain runs from the Indo-European root *skand- 'to leap, spring, climb', through Latin scandere 'to climb' and its compound ascendere 'to climb up', to the Latin noun ascēnsus 'an ascending', and into English, where the noun was formed on the model of descent in the early seventeenth century. The physical climb is the source of every figurative sense: to rise in the world is to climb, and to climb is to ascend.
