Earliest stage. The root is Proto-Indo-European *kwrep- "body, form, appearance" (also reconstructed as *kʷrep-). This root is well attested: it underlies Sanskrit kṛp- "form, beautiful appearance", Old English hrif "womb, belly" (hence English midriff), and Latin corpus.

Derivation chain:

  • Proto-Indo-European *kwrep- "body, form, appearance, belly" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE).
  • Latin corpus, genitive corporis, "body, physical frame, corpse" (attested from c. 3rd century BCE; in Plautus, Lucretius, Cicero) — the regular Italic reflex of the PIE root; the semantic range "living body / dead body" is inherited.
  • Late Latin corporealis "of the body, bodily, material" (derived from corpus with the suffix -alis; attested from the 3rd–4th centuries CE in Christian Latin writers) — the direct ancestor of the English form, opposed to spiritualis "spiritual".
  • Old French corporel, "bodily, material" (12th–13th centuries).
  • Middle English corporel, later corporeal, borrowed from Old French and Medieval Latin (attested in English from c. 1400 onward).
  • CORPOREAL (present-day English).

Debate note: The spelling and the intermediate French form are straightforward; the only nuance is that English sometimes borrowed the word twice (via Old French corporel and directly from Medieval Latin corporealis), producing the variant corporal (now chiefly used of rank in the army, from a different caput "head" derivation) beside corporeal. The two forms have diverged in meaning. No further uncertainty attaches to the chain.
