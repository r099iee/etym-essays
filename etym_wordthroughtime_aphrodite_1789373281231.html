- Archaic & Classical Greece (c. 8th–4th c. BCE) - Worshipped across major pan-Hellenic sanctuaries (Corinth, Cyprus, Cythera) as the Olympian queen of sexual generation, maritime travel, and civic harmony; celebrated by Homer and Hesiod, while lyric poets like Sappho invoke her in intimate personal prayers of passionate longing.
- Hellenistic & Roman Era (c. 3rd c. BCE – 4th c. CE) - Fully syncretized with Roman Venus; celebrated in epic poetry (Lucretius's *De Rerum Natura*, Virgil's *Aeneid*) as the primordial generative force of nature (*Venus Genetrix*) and supreme patroness of the Roman imperial lineage.
- Middle Ages - With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, pagan worship of Aphrodite/Venus is systematically suppressed; she is demonized in Christian theological tracts as an emblem of sinful carnal lust, idolatry, and demonic temptation, though she survives allegorically in courtly romance and astrological treatises.
- The Renaissance (15th–16th c.) - Gloriously rehabilitated as humanists rediscover classical antiquity; Neoplatonic philosophers (Ficino, Pico della Mirandola) interpret her as a symbol of divine, intellectual harmony, while painters like Botticelli, Titian, and Raphael immortalize her birth and beauty.
- 19th Century (Romanticism to Decadence) - Re-envisioned through the dark prism of modernity; Romantic poets celebrate her as the pure ideal of classical pagan vitality, while Decadent and Symbolist writers (Charles Baudelaire, Algernon Charles Swinburne) expose the morbid, cruel, and tragic underbelly of her worship, linking erotic obsession to despair and death.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an enduring global icon in popular culture, feminist scholarship, psychology, and literature, symbolizing the complex spectrum of female empowerment, aesthetic sublime, and the untamable mysteries of erotic desire.
