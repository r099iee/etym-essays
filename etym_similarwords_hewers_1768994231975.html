• Lumberjack: Similar in being a person who cuts down trees; differs because lumberjack is the modern industrial worker of the logging camps, a trade with equipment and unions, while hewer carries the archaic, biblical gravity of a class label.
  • Logger: Similar in being someone who fells timber commercially; differs because logger is a neutral, modern trade-name for industrial timber extraction, with no scriptural resonance and no sense of shaping the wood by hand.
  • Woodcutter: Similar in being one who cuts wood; differs because woodcutter is the neutral craft term for anyone from peasant to professional, whereas hewer is weighted with Old-Testament servitude and the fixed phrase “hewers of wood.”
  • Feller: Similar in being someone who brings down timber; differs because feller is narrowly technical — it names only the man who cuts standing timber down, saying nothing of the splitting or shaping that hewing implies.
  • Chopper: Similar in denoting one who cuts with an axe; differs because chopper is informal and slightly contemptuous, evoking random blows rather than the skilled, measured work of hewing.
  • Axeman: Similar in being a man who works with an axe; differs because axeman is generic about the tool and often archaic or dialectal, while hewer specifies the action of cutting and shaping with that tool.
  • Woodsman: Similar in being a man of the woods who works with timber; differs because woodsman suggests a broader life in the forest — trapping, hunting, sheltering — while hewer is defined purely by the stroke of the axe.
  • Forester: Similar in working in woodlands; differs because forester is a modern professional who manages forests as a resource, not one who cuts and shapes timber by hand.
  • Carpenter: Similar in shaping wood with tools; differs because the carpenter works wood to build joinery and structures with saw, plane, and chisel, while the hewer works with the axe on timber in the rough — a craftsman of finished goods rather than a labourer at the woodpile.
  • Stonecutter: Similar in being a labourer who shapes material by cutting; differs because the stonecutter works stone, not wood, and the parallel sense of hewer (stone-hewer) is a trade name rather than a general word.
