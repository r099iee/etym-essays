Imitative/onomatopoeic base *hwin- "the sound of rapid breath, whizzing, or a thin whistling note" (sound-symbolic; no PIE ancestor — the word was coined from the imitation of sound, not inherited) → Proto-Germanic *hwinison "to whiz, whistle, whimper" (iterative formation on the sound base *hwin-; c. 500 BCE–500 CE) → Old English hwinan "to whiz, hiss, or whistle through the air, said of arrows and spears" (attested from the 8th century) → Old English hwinsian "to whimper" (frequentative doublet of hwinan, used of the whimpering of dogs; c. 9th century; the two verbs fused in Middle English) → Middle English whinen "to make a low, protracted, plaintive sound" (c. 13th century; ancestor of Modern English whine) → Early Modern English whinny "of a horse, to neigh softly" (c. 1500, form whinien; a diminutive-frequentative in -y/-ie, the same affectionate ending as in doggie) → Modern English whinny "a soft, tremulous equine call" (noun attested 1823) → Modern English whinnying "making high-pitched, tremulous equine cries" (present participle) Note on the debate: The chain above is a reconstruction, not an unbroken line of documents; the intermediate step between Middle English whinen and the first recorded whinny (c. 1500) is inferred from regular English word-formation (frequentative -en/-ien) and from the parallel survival of the sound root across Germanic. The independent imitative words for the horse's cry in other languages — Latin hinnīre "to neigh," Old English hnægan (Modern English neigh), German wiehern, Greek khremetízein — are sound-symbolic parallels arising separately from the same urge to echo the horse, NOT cognates of whinny, and must not be treated as the same root.
