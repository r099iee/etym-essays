A causative from the start. Felling belongs to one of the oldest and most productive patterns of Germanic grammar: the causative verb. Where the simple verb fall describes what happens by itself, the causative fell describes making it happen — the same relationship as sit/set (“to cause to sit”), rise/raise, lie/lay. In Proto-Germanic the pair was *fallan, “to fall”, and *falljaną, “to cause to fall”, formed with the -jan- causative suffix; the same pattern produced Old Norse falla/fella, Old Saxon fallan/fellian, and Old High German fallan/fellen — modern German still has fallen/fällen, where fällen is the everyday word for cutting down trees. The ultimate PIE base is commonly given as *pol-, “to fall”, with Baltic and Slavic relatives (Lithuanian puolti, “to fall upon, attack”; Old Church Slavonic pasti, “to fall”), but the wider Indo-European connections are debated, and some authorities decline to reconstruct beyond Germanic; an honest chain flags the uncertainty rather than hiding it.

Old English: umlaut and two dialects. Old English inherited the causative as fellan in the Anglian dialects and fællan in West Saxon. The vowel difference is the fossil of a sound change: in *falljaną the front vowel of the causative suffix -jan- umlauted (fronted) the root vowel *a to *e before the suffix's -j- was lost — i-umlaut, the same process that produced set from *satjaną and send from *sandjaną. The verb is attested in Old English glosses and prose from the 8th century onward, with the full range of meanings it still has: to strike down a man, to cut down a tree, to bring low a building or a city. In the Anglo-Saxon imagination the word sat naturally in both the forest and the battlefield.

Middle English: the two careers continue. Middle English fellen (12th–15th centuries) carried both senses forward. In the romances and chronicles, knights fell each other in combat; in manorial records and Bible translations, men fell trees for timber and fuel, and the Wycliffite Bible uses the verb freely for the cutting down of trees. The language already possessed the near-twin fallen (the intransitive), so the pair fell/fall was as crisp in Middle English as it is today: one verb caused, the other happened.

Forestry becomes a science (16th–18th centuries). The 17th century professionalised tree-felling. John Evelyn's Sylva (1664), the great English treatise on forestry and timber, discusses the felling of oaks in detail — seasons, ages, methods — and gives the word its technical footing in the language of estate management and naval timber supply. This is the moment the tree-sense begins to pull ahead of the combat-sense: as the Royal Navy consumed forests for shipbuilding, “felling” became a word of commerce and state policy. The combat sense, meanwhile, lived on in the language of duels, executions and pugilism: a “felling blow” was a blow that put a man on the ground.

The industrial era (19th century). The nineteenth century turned felling into industry. In Britain, the Americas and the colonies, professional “fellings” — clear felling, selective felling, thinning felling — became the standard terms of the timber trade, and the noun use (“the felling of the season”) entered commercial and legal prose. Railways, mines and cities consumed timber at unprecedented rates, and the word that had once named a woodsman's day's work now named a corporate operation.

The environmental reversal (20th–21st centuries). The twentieth century reversed the word's emotional colouring. In the era of conservation and climate politics, “felling” acquired a negative charge: it is the act that protesters gather to stop, the headline verb of deforestation (“the felling of the Amazon”), and the subject of logging bans. Meanwhile the figurative sense thrived in literary and journalistic prose — illness, grief, scandal and disaster “fell” the great and the ordinary alike (“felled by a stroke”, “felled by the news”) — and the sewing sense (to fell a seam, to flatten and stitch an edge) quietly preserved the verb's ancient meaning of “to lay flat”.

Summary. A PIE-era causative, an umlauted Old English verb, a Middle English pair with fall, a Stuart treatise, an industrial operation, an environmental protest — felling has spent fifteen hundred years bringing things down, from oaks to empires, and its image of the falling tree still organises every one of its senses.
