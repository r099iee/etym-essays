The story of deafening opens inside a stunned head. Its oldest recoverable ancestor is the Indo-European root *dheubh-, a word for confusion, stupor, and dulness — the mental fog after a blow, the haze that is also preserved in words for smoke and vapour. From this fog the Germanic tribes shaped *daubaz, an adjective covering the whole territory of dulled sense: deaf, stunned, dull-witted, insensible — one word for every way a head can fail to register the world. Deafness, in this ancient psychology, was not a defect of the ear alone but a cousin of dizziness and bewilderment.

The sealed ear in Old English: In Anglo-Saxon England dēaf meant unable to hear, but the old breadth survived in compounds and metaphors: a deaf nut was an empty hazelnut with no kernel inside, deaf earth was barren ground, and the deaf adder of the psalms would not be charmed. To be deaf was to be closed, unreceptive, hollow — the ear’s failure standing for every failure of response. The adjective was common, blunt, and faintly moral: the deaf man and the heedless man shared a syllable because the culture barely distinguished them.

  • Hearing to emptiness: the deaf ear becomes the empty shell — deaf nuts, hollow and kernel-less, rattle through medieval texts.
  • Emptiness to obstinacy: what hears nothing answers nothing, so deaf lends itself to every sermon on the sinner who will not listen — none so deaf as those that will not hear.
  • Stupor to noise: the ancient kinship of deafness with stunning prepares the verb’s great reversal — to deafen will mean to stun with sound.

To deafen (14th–16th c.): Middle English built the causative verb deafen — to make deaf — and with it flipped the word’s direction: where deaf had described a silent lack, deafen described an excess that inflicts lack, clamour so great it unmakes hearing. Brawls, bells, and the new gunpowder artillery supplied the experience; chronicles complain of soldiers deafened by ordnance, congregations deafened by rival preachers. The present participle followed naturally, and by the 1590s deafening stands in Elizabethan pages as the superlative of loud — cannon, tempests, and shouting armies.

Industry, amplification, and the paradox (19th–20th c.): The factory century made deafening a working word: steam hammers, boiler shops, locomotive sheds, and weaving floors kept whole towns at the threshold of pain, and novelists from Dickens to Lawrence reached for deafening whenever machinery entered the scene. Two modern refinements followed. First the paradox: deafening silence — the unanswered question in Parliament, the missing applause, the empty house — named the pressure of expected sound withheld, and became one of English’s favourite oxymorons. Then the compliment: mass democracy and mass entertainment taught crowds to measure love in decibels, so that deafening applause and deafening welcomes turned the wound into a wreath. Occupational medicine closed the circle with a warning: the shipyard riveter and the front-row guitarist learned that years of the figurative deafening collect, in the end, as literal deafness — the ancient root’s stupor returning as damaged hair-cells in the inner ear.

The dragon over Bywater: Tolkien, who knew the deep history of every syllable he spent, placed deafening exactly where five centuries of escalation demanded: at the climax. The show rises through roar and whizz and somersault to the one noise-word that admits no louder neighbour — the burst that does not merely sound but deafens, crowning the hobbits’ night with the ringing aftermath every reader remembers from standing too close to the bonfire. From fog to stupor to cannon to dragon, the word has travelled from the inside of a dazed head to the sky above a village — and it still means, at bottom, what *dheubh- meant: the world too much with us, until sense surrenders.
