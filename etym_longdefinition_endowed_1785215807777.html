endowed is the past participle of the verb to endow, and as an adjective it means provided or equipped with a faculty, quality, gift, or resource — whether by nature, by fortune, or by deliberate bestowal. It has two great provinces of meaning, and its distinctive genius is that it spans both with one idea: permanence. (1) In the personal or figurative sense, to be endowed with a quality is to have it conferred as a gift that lasts — "endowed with patience", "endowed with great intelligence", "endowed with a remarkable voice". (2) In the institutional or financial sense, to endow an institution is to provide it with a permanent fund or income — an endowed college, an endowed chair, an endowed hospital — money given to last, usually forever. Both senses descend from the word's origin in the dowry: Latin dōtāre, "to provide with a marriage portion", from dōs, "dowry", from dare, "to give". An endowment, like a dowry, is a gift that is settled, permanent, and meant to support its recipient over the long term.

In the passage from Mill's Autobiography from which this note is drawn — "Mill remained delicate throughout his life, but was endowed with that intense mental energy which is so often combined with physical weakness" — the word is used in its figurative, biographical sense. Mill's mental vitality is framed as a gift conferred upon him: a natural provision that coexists with, and seems almost to compensate for, his bodily frailty. The sentence quietly contrasts the two endowments — a weak body and a powerful mind — and presents the second as the deliberate gift of nature, permanent in the sense that it belonged to him for a lifetime. The choice of endowed rather than gifted or provided with is precise: it suggests a settled, durable, almost providential bestowal, the dowry that nature gives a man to compensate him for what she withholds.

The adjective's senses, past and present, may be surveyed point by point:

  • Provided naturally with a faculty or quality (personal sense) — "endowed with intelligence, courage, patience, grace, a fine voice": the most frequent modern use, always implying a gift that is durable and generally admirable.
  • Provided with a permanent fund or income (institutional/financial sense) — "an endowed university", "an endowed chair of philosophy", "a richly endowed library": the sense in which institutions are given capital whose income supports them in perpetuity.
  • The historical dowry sense — in Middle English, to endow was literally to provide a bride with her marriage portion, or to give a church, college, or monastery a settled income for its support; this is the etymological root of all the modern senses and survives in the law of property.
  • The theological or providential sense — to be endowed with a talent or mission by God or nature; frequent in 17th–19th-century religious and biographical prose, where endowment is a gift of the Creator.
  • Used of things — a landscape "endowed with rare beauty", a country "endowed with rich mineral resources": the word extends from persons to places and institutions, always with the sense of a bestowed, lasting asset.

Historical semantic bridge. All the senses flow from the dowry. Latin dōs (genitive dōtis), "dowry, marriage portion", was the property a bride brought to her marriage — a settled gift intended to support the new household. The verb dōtāre, "to provide with a portion", passed into Old French as douer, and with the prefix en- ("in, into") became endouer, "to endow", which English borrowed in the 14th century. The first English uses are legal and financial: to endow a bride with her portion, to endow a church with lands. From money the word extended, in the 16th–17th centuries, to qualities: to be endowed with intelligence is to receive from nature a portion as lasting as any dowry. The double life of the word — money for a college, patience for a person — is thus not a split but a single ancient idea: what is endowed is bestowed with permanence.

Boundaries, constructions, and register. Endowed differs from gifted (confined to personal talents and largely individual in application), from invested with (official authority or office conferred from outside), from furnished and supplied with (colourless, physical terms for providing what is needed), and from bestowed upon (which stresses the giver's act rather than the recipient's state). It is used chiefly with with: "endowed with X". In register it is formal and dignified, carrying the implication of generosity and permanence; in financial contexts it is a technical term of institutional finance (an endowed fund, the endowment of a university). It is the adjective of the verb endow and the noun endowment, and it belongs to the large Indo-European family of give — though, notably, the English verb give itself descends from a different root.
