The evolution of augury is one of the most instructive word-histories in the European lexicon, because it shows a single chain of sound and meaning running from an abstract idea of "growth" to the concrete, institutional machinery of Roman state religion, and from there to a general, slightly archaic English word for "a sign of things to come." It is a biography in four movements: the Indo-European seed, the Roman institutional flowering, the medieval clerical transmission, and the modern secular generalization.

1. The Indo-European seed: *h₂ewg- "to increase." Deep in prehistory, the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂ewg- denoted growth, increase, and the fostering of strength. In an agricultural and pastoral society, the ability to make things grow — crops, herds, flocks, family, and power — was the most valued ability on earth, and the root therefore carried prosperity as its natural shadow-meaning. It radiated across the Indo-European family: Greek aúxein "to increase," Sanskrit ójaḥ "strength, vigour," Latin augēre "to increase." None of these words had anything to do with prophecy. The semantic future of the word lay in a detour the root took in one particular corner of the ancient world: the city of Rome.

2. The Roman institutional flowering: from augēre to augurium. Latin augēre produced the noun augur, whose exact formation is debated (see the etymology note): most scholars read it as "one who increases or favours," a religious functionary who secures the growth and goodwill of the gods for the Roman people. Whatever the precise morphology, the historical reality is certain and remarkable. In the Roman Republic the college of augurs was a state institution of the first rank. No public assembly could be lawfully held, no magistrate legally inaugurated, and no war confidently begun until the augur had taken the auspicia — the signs — and pronounced them favourable. The augur worked by dividing the sky into quarters with his curved staff (lituus), marking out a sacred space (templum), and then watching. Signs might be the flight of birds (from the right or left, from front or rear), their cries, their feeding, or celestial portents such as thunder and lightning. This was not superstition in the modern pejorative sense; it was a scrupulous, regulated science of public decision-making, and even hard-headed politicians like Cicero, who privately mocked the whole apparatus, served as augurs and defended the institution on civic grounds in his treatise De Divinatione.

From the office came the abstract noun augurium, which carried two meanings at once: the practice of the augur, and the portent read by him. This double meaning is the pivot on which the whole later history of the word turns, because it allowed the word to slide, by the natural figure of metonymy, from the rite to the sign. Already in classical Latin one finds augurium used for the omen itself, and it is this transferred sense that the medieval vernaculars inherited.

3. Medieval transmission: from Latin to French to English. With the fall of the western Empire, the college of augurs disappeared, and the word survived mainly in the learned and clerical strata of the language — in Latin texts, in the Church's vocabulary of prophecy and portent, and eventually in the Romance vernaculars. Old French took the word as augurie, and Anglo-Norman carried it into England in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. The phonetic journey is compact: Latin augurium (with the diphthong au pronounced as a single rising vowel) became Old French augurie, with the final -um replaced by the vernacular -ie, and then Middle English augurie, appearing in fourteenth-century religious and didactic prose where omens and signs were discussed in the vocabulary of natural portent. In medieval and early modern usage the word was applied broadly to divination, to the reading of signs and entrails, and to any prognostic, keeping the grave, sacred tone of its Roman origin but losing all institutional specificity.

4. Modern generalization and metaphor. From the Renaissance onward, as classical learning revived, the word was freshly felt as a noble, archaic term. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it appears in serious literary and political prose for "an omen, a portent," and writers exploit its gravity: to call an event an augury is to claim that it is charged with destiny. In the nineteenth century, Romantic and Gothic prose made a specialty of the word, using it for signs that carry monumental, almost cosmic weight. In contemporary English the word lives at three registers: (a) the historical and scholarly sense, in discussions of Roman religion where augury remains the standard term for the art of the augurs; (b) a formal, literary synonym for "omen" or "sign," chosen precisely because it is not the everyday word; and (c) a figurative extension in which any indicator — a market signal, a poll, a first chapter, a weather pattern — can be "an augury" of what follows, importing a theatrical whiff of fate into secular prediction.

Key sound changes and semantic drifts, summarized:

  • PIE *h₂ewg- "increase" → Latin augēre: the laryngeal h₂ colours the following vowel, giving Latin au; no consonant cluster in the attested forms.
  • Latin augur → augurium: suffixation with -ium, shifting the concrete agent-noun to an abstract practice-noun.
  • Latin augurium → Old French augurie: loss of final -um, retention of the -ie abstract ending; the au diphthong is preserved.
  • Old French → Middle English augurie → Modern English augury [ˈɔːɡjʊɹi]: the stressed first syllable develops a long open-o pronunciation under the Great Vowel Shift era, and the -urie ending is simplified to -ury.
  • Semantic drift: "increase/favour" (PIE) → "the one who secures divine favour" (augur) → "the practice of reading omens" and "the omen read" (augurium) → "any portent or sign" (medieval and modern) → "any significant indicator of the future" (modern metaphor). The metonymic bridge at augurium, rite-to-sign, is the hinge of the whole history.
  • Register drift: from a technical term of a functioning state religion, to a clerical/learned word, to an elevated literary archaism used for dramatic weight in otherwise secular contexts.

Literary attestations anchor each phase. Cicero's De Divinatione (44 BCE) gives the fullest classical portrait of augural practice. Chaucer and his contemporaries in the late fourteenth century use the word in its omen sense. Shakespeare, though he prefers "omen" and "presage," exploits augural imagery throughout the history plays and in Julius Caesar, where the flight of birds and the sacrificial reading of entrails decide the mood of the action. In modern prose, the word's measured archaism makes it a favourite of narrators who want a sign to feel fated. The path from "to increase" to "a sign of your ascent" is, in the end, a single unbroken chain: the priest who made things grow became the priest who read the sky, and the sky's signs became the verses on a page.
