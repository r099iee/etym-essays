Derivation chain for heedlessly: 1. Proto-Indo-European *kadh- 'to cover, to protect, to guard' (traditional reconstruction, Pokorny; the Germanic words for both 'heed' (guarding attention) and 'hood' (a covering) are usually traced to this base. The reconstruction is NOT universally accepted — many modern handbooks record no secure PIE ancestor for the Germanic verb and treat the deeper origin as uncertain). 2. Proto-Germanic *hōdijaną 'to guard, to watch over, to protect, to take care of' (reconstructed verb; reflected in the Germanic daughters — Old High German huoten, Old Saxon hōdian, Middle High German hüeten, German hüten 'to guard, to tend'; the related noun *hōdaz / *hōdiz 'guard, protection, care' underlies the English noun heed). 3. Old English hēdan (verb) 'to heed, to attend to, to take care of, to guard, to observe' (attested; the noun hēd 'heed, care, attention, guard' is also attested, as in the phrase tō hēde 'in heed, in guard'). 4. Middle English heden (verb) 'to heed' and heed (noun) 'care, attention' (12th c. onward); from heed + the privative suffix -les (Old English -lēas 'devoid of, without') comes Middle English hedeles (adjective) 'heedless, careless, unmindful' (recorded from c. 1440). 5. Middle English / Early Modern English heedlesly (adverb) 'heedlessly, carelessly' (formed with the adverbial suffix -ly, from Old English -līce 'in the manner of, like'; recorded from the 16th c.; the standard spelling heedlessly settles in the 17th c.). 6. Modern English HEEDLESSLY 'in a careless, thoughtless, or rash manner, without regard to risks or consequences'. Note on the internal structure: the word is a transparent three-part compound — heed (guard, attention) + -less (without) + -ly (adverb-forming) — and each element is native English, inherited from Old English without foreign borrowing. The suffix -less derives from Old English -lēas, originally an independent word meaning 'free from, devoid of', so that heedless literally means 'devoid of heed'.
