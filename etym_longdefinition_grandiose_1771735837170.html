In the sentence on Gibbon, “grandiose” is applied to the subject of the Decline and Fall: “a multivolume work on such a grandiose subject.” The subject is nothing less than the decline and fall of the Roman Empire, and the adjective measures the sheer magnitude of that theme — an epic, civilization-wide arc of centuries, armies, emperors, faiths, institutions, and ruined grandeur. The tone is one of amused self-justification: the author knows it “may seem odd” to call so stately and enormous a work “intimate,” and grandiose supplies the scale against which that intimacy becomes a paradox. In this sentence the word is used in its admiring sense — it signals breadth, dignity, and historical weight — yet it also quietly admits that a subject on this scale risks seeming too large, too theatrical, too much a grand public performance; the rest of the sentence rescues Gibbon from exactly that suspicion. As a dictionary word, “grandiose” is an adjective carrying two connected families of meaning. The first is admiring and aesthetic: impressive, magnificent, and imposing in appearance or style, of large scale, splendor, or dignity — a grandiose scheme may simply be one of magnificent extent. The second is critical and moral: pretentiously grand, inflated, overblown, claiming more grandeur than the reality can support — a grandiose plan is one so big that it has come unmoored from what is possible or true. Both senses coexist in modern English, and the word’s whole history is the story of how the first slowly curdled into the second. Context decides which face is showing: applied to an empire, a cathedral, or the subject of Gibbon’s history, “grandiose” leans toward the admiring sense of monumental scale; applied to a person, an ambition, or a boast, it leans toward pretension and self-importance. Polysemy — the full range of senses:
  • Magnificent scale (admiring): describing something genuinely vast, splendid, and imposing — “the grandiose proportions of a Baroque palace” — where the grandeur is real and the word is praise.
  • Pretentious inflation (critical): describing plans, promises, or self-presentations that are grand beyond their warrant — “a grandiose scheme to build a city in the desert” — where the grandeur exceeds the reality and the word is a reproach.
  • Psychiatric and clinical jargon: “grandiose delusions” — the delusional overestimation of one’s own importance, power, knowledge, or identity, classically associated with mania and certain forms of schizophrenia; here the word names a symptom, fully technical and wholly negative.
  • Of persons and character: self-important, pompously magniloquent, ambitious in an overweening way — “a grandiose manner” or “a grandiose air” describing someone who behaves as though the world owed him a stage.
  • Of discourse and style: swollen, bombastic, deliberately elevated — grandiose rhetoric or prose that strains for an effect larger than its matter.
  • Extended and colloquial uses: loosely, anything impressively over-scaled or over-decorated — a grandiose cake, a grandiose entrance — often with affectionate irony.
The boundary words sharpen the profile:
  • “Grand”: Similar because both mean magnificent and imposing; differs because “grand” is simply positive — noble and impressive, with no warning attached — whereas “grandiose,” applied to persons or schemes, means inflated and overweening.
  • “Magnificent”: Similar because both praise splendor; differs because “magnificent” is unreserved praise for beauty and splendor, while “grandiose” keeps an eye on scale and on the risk of excess.
  • “Imposing”: Similar because both command attention; differs because “imposing” stresses the effect on the beholder — awe or intimidation — rather than intrinsic worth, while “grandiose” concerns the (often excessive) scale of the thing itself.
  • “Monumental”: Similar because both suggest vast size and importance; differs because “monumental” points to literal memorial scale and is usually approving, whereas “grandiose” can slide into pretension.
  • “Pretentious”: Similar because both claim more than is warranted; differs because “pretentious” is openly negative, attaching to display that exceeds merit, while “grandiose” may be admiring when the subject genuinely matches its scale.
  • “Ostentatious”: Similar because both involve conspicuous display; differs because “ostentatious” stresses showy display for effect and usually implies vulgarity, while “grandiose” concerns scale and ambition rather than mere showing off.
The historical bridge between the two main senses runs through proportion. A thing is “grand” when its size suits its purpose; it becomes “grandiose” in the pejorative sense when the size so exceeds the reality that the grandeur itself looks like an illusion or a boast. The literary context exploits this hinge: Gibbon’s subject is grandiose in the first, admiring sense of magnificent scale, and the author lets the newer doubt flicker at the edges precisely so that his claim of “intimacy” can overcome it. In short, “grandiose” has always meant “full of greatness” — but whether the greatness is real or self-important depends, as in this very sentence, on the context that frames it.
