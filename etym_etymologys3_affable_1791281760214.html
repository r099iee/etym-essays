Affable is a textbook Latin formation whose history shows how a word for speakability became a word for lovability, moving from the Forum to the drawing-room to the television studio.

The deepest root is PIE *bheh2-, to speak, say, tell. This prolific root yields Greek phemi, I say, Latin fari, to speak, fate, what is spoken, fable, fame, and English ban, boast, and phone via Greek. The core idea is authoritative utterance: to say aloud what shall be.

  • PIE utterance to Latin speech verb: *bheh2- with lengthened grade gives Latin fari, deponent verb to speak, prophesy, with past participle fatus. Initial bh becomes f by regular Italic devoicing.
  • Prefixing with ad-: Latin ad-, to, toward, assimilates before f to af-, producing affari, to speak to, address. The prefix adds direction and courtesy: not just speaking, but speaking to someone.
  • Adjectival formation: affabilis adds -abilis, worthy of, capable of, hence worthy of being spoken to, approachable in conversation. Cicero uses affabilis for courteous accessibility of patrons and orators; it is a democratic virtue in a hierarchical society, praising the great who listen to the small.
  • Old French mediation: Affable enters Old French by the 14th century with the same sense of gentle, courteous speech, especially for lords, ladies, and clergy. French softens pronunciation and generalizes sense toward kindly.
  • Middle English borrowing: Around 1480 English borrows affable from French in Caxton-era courtesy literature. Early uses describe princes and hosts who receive suitors graciously.

Phonetically the form is stable. Latin affabilis loses its ending -is in French, keeping af-fable with double f marking assimilation. English keeps French spelling and stress on the first syllable, RP /aefabel/. No Great Vowel Shift obscures it because the vowels are short and unstressed. The transparency of ad + fable remains visible to anyone who knows fable as spoken tale.

Semantically the shift is from permission to disposition. Latin affabilis meant one whom you may address without fear; modern affable means one who actively welcomes address with warmth. The locus moves from status, condescension graciously granted, to personality, friendliness naturally possessed. 18th-century novels praise affable manners as proof of true gentility versus proud reserve. Victorian advice literature makes affability a professional asset for clergymen, doctors, and shopkeepers. 20th-century politics and media make it an electoral asset: the affable candidate wins the affable key of public talk.

The source phrase in the same affable key preserves the oldest metaphor: conversation as music where speakers must stay in tune. To match key is to match mood, and affable names the major, major-key mood of good humour. That continuity from Roman affari to modern chat shows how deeply Western culture links speech and sociability.
