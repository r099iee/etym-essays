The history of implement is a textbook Latin-to-English abstraction turned concrete. It begins with Proto-Indo-European *pleh1- to fill, one of the best-attested roots, yielding Greek pimplemi I fill, Sanskrit prati, Old English full, and Latin plere to fill, plenus full, completus complete. Latin compounds this productively: ex-plere, com-plere, sup-plere supply, complete, supplement. Im-plere (in- + plere) means to fill up, fill full, satisfy, fulfil an office or expectation. Its past participle impletus and noun implementum in Late Latin mean a filling up, fulfilment, accomplishment, and by extension that which fills a need - stock, equipment.

Medieval scholastic and legal Latin uses implementum for appurtenances, necessary outfit, fulfilment of contract. From there it enters Scots and English in the 15th-16th centuries as implement: first in Scots law as implements of a contract, means of fulfilment, then concretely as furniture, stock, tools. Early attestations (c. 1440-1530) show agricultural and household implements - ploughs, carts, utensils - as goods listed in wills and inventories. Shakespearean and King James English still feels the fulfilment sense: to implement is rare as verb until the 19th century.

  • Phonetics: Latin imple-re, imple-mentum with stress on second syllable enters English with initial stress IM-ple-ment for noun by 17th century; verb retains variable stress but RP now /ˈɪmplɪment/ for both, American verb often /ˈɪmpləmɛnt/.
  • Morphology: English back-forms singular implement from Latin neuter, then adds regular -s for plural noun and 3rd singular verb, creating homographic forms.
  • Semantics: fulfilment to equipment (metonymy: result to means), then equipment to archaeological artefact (specialization), then re-verbalization to execute (19th-20th c. bureaucracy).
  • Literary attestations: 1538 Elyot dictionary implement furniture; 18th-century husbandry manuals farm implements; 19th-century anthropology stone implements; 20th-century management implement policy, computing implement interface.

Socio-cultural context explains the verb revival. Industrial administration, welfare-state planning, and later software engineering needed a neutral, formal word for turning paper specs into reality without the lethal overtones of execute. Implement filled that gap, becoming EU English implementing act and software implementation. The money-history passage preserves the older noun layer when implements were literally wealth - hoes and knives anyone would take - before the word became boardroom jargon. Both layers remain fully current, distinguished only by syntax.

Modern corpora show noun implements peaking in 19th-century farming and archaeology texts, while verb implement surges after 1950 with systems theory and computing. Dictionaries now list verb senses first in business usage, reflecting this reversal. The survival of both proves the root idea durable: human beings constantly need both tools that fill hands and actions that fill promises.
