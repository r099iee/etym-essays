- Old English — a ditch-side name: hemlic names the poison weed of waste places, a word already confined to English with no known kin.
- 399 BCE remembered — the Athenian cup: English readers learn that this weed's Greek cousin kōneion executed Socrates, and the plant carries judicial gravity ever after.
- Medieval — the witch's garden: herbals list hemlock among cold deadly herbs, good for ointments in tiny doses and for murder in large ones.
- 16th–17th c. — the stage poison: Shakespeare's witches and pamphlet murderers make hemlock the very type of secret killing, fair flower and foul stem.
- 18th c. — the name emigrates: American settlers christen the harmless Tsuga conifers hemlock for their smell, and tanners strip their bark for leather.
- Today — twin reputations: in Britain the poison weed of ditches and headlines about foragers, in America the great shade tree of old forests, one ominous name on two unrelated lives.
