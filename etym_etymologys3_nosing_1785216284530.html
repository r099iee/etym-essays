The biography of nosing is the story of a body part learning to be a verb. It opens in Proto-Indo-European with the root *nas-, "nose," an almost universal human word: Sanskrit nāsā, "nose," and its derivative nāsikā, "nostril"; Latin nāsus; Lithuanian nósis; Old Church Slavonic nosъ; and, in the Germanic branch, Proto-Germanic *nasō, which became Old English nosu — the direct ancestor of modern English nose. The root is one of the most stable in the entire Indo-European family because it names a body part that every human shares and every child points to; sound change has barely shaken it. In the Indo-Aryan branch the same root produced Sanskrit nāsā, which passed through the Prakrits into Hindi as nāk (नाक) and into Odia as nāka, so that the same ancient word for "nose" is still spoken across modern India exactly as it is across Europe.

The noun stands alone. For more than a thousand years of English the word was purely a noun: Old English nosu, "nose," the organ of smell. English used it for the body part, for the sense of smell itself (a dog's nose), and figuratively for the front of a ship (the "nose" or prow). But the language did not yet have a verb nose. The denominal verb nosen, "to smell or scent," appears in the sixteenth century — a hunter's and hound's word, first of animals reading the air, a dog nosing the ground or nosing out a scent. The step from noun to verb was natural and frequent in Early Modern English (compare to hand, to head, to elbow), and the new verb immediately carried the full image of its source: a nose in motion, pressed forward, scenting.

The 17th-century extension to prying. The crucial semantic widening followed in the seventeenth century. To nose came to mean prying and searching — thrusting one's nose into places, whether a beast rooting in a corner or a busybody nosing into another's affairs. The phrase "to nose about" solidified this roaming, questing sense, and the figurative application to people followed at once: to nose into something is to meddle, to investigate what does not concern one, and a "nosy" person (the adjective recorded from the 1880s) is one who cannot keep his nose out of other people's business. The historical bridge is transparent and bodily: the nose is the organ of curiosity — it leans forward, sniffs, probes — and synecdoche (the part standing for the whole person) turned smelling into spying. The word thus acquired its double life: literal scenting (the hound) and figurative meddling (the busybody), and it is precisely this doubleness that gives it its edge.

Further derivations and uses. From the same denominal verb came to nose out, "to discover by persistent scenting or investigation," used of both hounds and detectives, and the noun nose was pressed into new compounds: nose-dive (the aircraft's head-first descent), nosebleed, nosegay (a posy carried to smell). In nautical and aeronautical prose, to nose forward or nose down means to advance or descend with the front end leading, the body-part image fully generalized. Every one of these uses, from the dog nosing a trail to the airplane nosing down through cloud, keeps the primitive image of the protruding front end of a living creature feeling its way.

The participle in Tolkien's prose. The present participle nosing, formed with -ing, converts this verb of investigation into an ongoing activity, and that is exactly its function in J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937): the cave-creatures are "slinking and nosing about," the participle catching them mid-probe, endlessly, in odd corners, heads down, quartering the darkness with a curiosity that is at once animallike and faintly meddlesome. The pairing with slinking is deliberate: slinking describes how they move — low, furtive, guilty — and nosing what they do while moving — probing, scenting, searching. The two participles together render these original owners as patient, scenting presences forever quartering the corners of the goblin tunnels, alive in the dark.

Sound and meaning. The word's sound is itself significant: the long vowel and the voiced -s- (a "buzz") give nosing a drawn-out, sniffing quality that matches its sense. Its semantic career is a textbook case of synecdochic transfer: nose (the organ) → to nosen (to smell, 16th c.) → to nose about (to pry, 17th c.) → nosy (inquisitive, 1880s) → nose out (to discover). A single body part, the most protruding and most curious feature of the face, became the English image of investigation in all its forms — from the hound reading the air to the gossip meddling in the affairs of others.

Key sound shifts and forms.
  • *nas- → Proto-Germanic *nasō: the PIE root with the standard Germanic noun ending; vowel unchanged.
  • *nasō → Old English nosu: regular Anglo-Frisian lowering and addition of the weak noun ending -u.
  • nosu → Middle English nose: loss of the Old English inflectional ending, giving the modern shape.
  • nose (noun) → nosen (verb): 16th-century denominal formation, "to smell, scent."
  • nosen → nose about: 17th-century extension to prying, questing search.
  • nose → nosing: the -ing participle, used as adjective and verbal noun from the 16th century onward.
