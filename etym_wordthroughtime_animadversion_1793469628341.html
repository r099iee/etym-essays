- Mid-16th c. (c. 1560–1580) — Latinate importation: Borrowed into Elizabethan English from Latin animadversio, carrying dual meanings: cognitive mental perception and judicial punishment/reprimand.
- 17th c. — The golden age of theological and political pamphleteering: The term becomes the definitive weapon and title in ferocious religious and civil disputes; John Milton publishes his famous Animadversions (1641) against the Anglican hierarchy, solidifying the word as a weapon of polemical refutation.
- Late 17th c. — Philosophical cognition: John Locke and the Cambridge Platonists occasionally preserve the archaic cognitive sense, using animadversion for the pure mental act of noticing or paying attention.
- 18th c. — Augustan critical essays: Samuel Johnson, Alexander Pope, and Jonathan Swift establish animadversion as the supreme literary term for severe moral rebuke and polished critical reproof in periodical essays like The Rambler.
- 19th c. — Parliamentary and judicial discourse: Victorian orators, judges, and academic reviewers use the plural animadversions to deliver grave, authoritative rebukes against institutional corruption and scholarly incompetence.
- Today — Rare, elevated literary usage: Kept alive in high-level intellectual criticism, jurisprudence, and scholarly prose to denote articulate, devastating critiques of flawed reasoning.
