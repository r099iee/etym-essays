- 1300s–1400s: Middle English scalen (from Old French escaler): climbing by means of a ladder — siege usage above all; the past form is strictly military-architectural, the verb of the escalade.
- 1500s–1600s: extended from fortifications to natural heights and then to achievements — 'scaling the heights' begins to mean reaching the top of anything; the ambition metaphor is born and quickly becomes a moral commonplace.
- 1700s–1800s: mountaineering, exploration, and imperial reportage: 'scaled the peak' becomes the heroic verb of record for first ascents; in Romantic prose the scaled height is a site of sublime effort and self-overcoming.
- 1900s–today: the climbing sense yields ground to 'climbed' in speech but keeps a formal and adventure/fantasy niche; meanwhile the homonymous 'scaled' of proportional sizing explodes through business, computing, statistics, and manufacturing ('scaled up', 'scaled down', 'scalable'), so that many modern readers first parse 'scaled' as 'resized' — a perception shift driven by spreadsheets and servers, not by mountains.
