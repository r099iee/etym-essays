The name Polus is the Latinized form of the classical Greek personal name Πῶλος (Pôlos), an ancient Greek onomastic formation rooted in archaic Indo-European zoomorphic naming conventions and immortalized across classical historiography, dramatic theory, rhetoric, and philosophical aesthetics.

1. Indo-European and Greek Linguistic Roots:

  • The Greek substantive noun πῶλος (pôlos) literally means "a foal, colt, young horse," or by extension "a young animal, youth, boy, or maiden."
  • It derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *pōl- (or zero-grade variant of *pelh₁- / *pleh₁-), meaning "young animal, foal, offspring." This root is directly ancestral to Latin pullus ("young animal, chick"), Gothic fula, Old High German folo, Old English fola, and modern English foal and pullet.
  • In ancient Greek culture, naming children after animals—especially spirited horses (e.g., Philippos "lover of horses," Hippokrates "horse-power," Xanthippe "yellow horse," and Polus "the colt")—was a widespread aristocratic practice reflecting vitality, nobility, athletic vigor, and auspicious aristocratic breeding.

2. Classical Attestations and Historical Figures:

  • During the 5th and 4th centuries BCE, the name was famously borne by two prominent Hellenic figures:
    • Polus of Agrigento (fl. c. 420 BCE), the Sicilian sophist and pupil of Gorgias immortalized in Plato's dialogue Gorgias.
    • Polus of Aegina (fl. c. 320 BCE), the celebrated tragic actor mentioned by Aristotle in the Rhetoric (III.12), Demosthenes, Plutarch, and later Roman chroniclers.
  • Aristotle notes that in 4th-century Athens, the reputation of the star actor had grown so enormous that actors often carried more influence over theatrical audiences than the dramatic poets themselves, citing Polus and Theodorus as supreme masters of dramatic delivery and vocal power (*hypokrisis*).

3. Roman Transmission and Aulus Gellius:

  • In the 2nd century CE, Roman grammarian Aulus Gellius recorded the famous anecdote of Polus in Noctes Atticae (Book VI, Chapter 5), rendering the name into Latin as Pōlus. Gellius described him as histrio in terra Graecia fuit celebritate incluta ("an actor in the land of Greece of renowned celebrity").
  • Plutarch likewise recorded Polus' legendary physical discipline and devotion to tragic art, noting that at the age of seventy, shortly before his death, Polus performed eight full tragedies in four successive festival days without vocal exhaustion.

4. Enlightenment Aesthetics & Theatrical Legacy:

  • During the 18th century, the name Polus entered European aesthetic philosophy when Denis Diderot critiqued his method in Paradoxe sur le comédien, sparking centuries of debate between proponents of emotional immersion and emotional detachment.
  • In 20th-century theatre history, Russian director Konstantin Stanislavski and American Method Acting theorists cited Polus as the primary historical archetype for emotional truth and affective memory on stage.
  • Today, in drama history and classical scholarship, Polus endures as the quintessential historical symbol of authentic emotional immersion on the theatrical stage.
