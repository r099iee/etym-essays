buffeting is the present participle (and verbal noun) of the verb buffet (/ˈbʌfɪt/, to strike), meaning subjecting to repeated, heavy blows — literally of wind, waves, or fists beating against a person or structure, and figuratively of misfortune battering a life, economy, or reputation. In the source clause, “buffeting his face”, the hot gale does not merely touch the face but beats it again and again, in concert with tearing, whipping tears away, and stinging legs with pebbles: a full-body assault by air made solid. The -ing form marks ongoing, iterative action contemporaneous with the main past verb (tore): while the gale tore, it was buffeting.

Morphologically buffeting = buffet (stem) + -ing (progressive/nominaliser, from Old English -ung/-ing). It functions as progressive verb (was buffeting), attributive adjective (buffeting winds), and noun (the buffeting of the storm, to take a buffeting). Pronunciation is /ˈbʌfɪtɪŋ/ (three syllables, stress on buf-). Crucial homograph caution: buffet /bʊˈfeɪ/ (self-service meal, from French) is an entirely different stress and meaning; buffeting never refers to food service (*buffeting lunch is an error for buffet lunch).

  • 1. To strike repeatedly with wind, water, or weather (primary, intransitive/transitive): Waves buffeting the cliffs, winds buffeting the plane, gale buffeting his face. Implies rhythmic, inescapable blows rather than single impact. Bridge from fist-blow to weather-blow is personification: nature boxes as a pugilist boxes.
  • 2. To knock about, toss, or batter physically (of travel, sport, machinery): The small boat was buffeted by the storm; passengers were buffeted in the back seat; the boxer took a buffeting. Stresses disorientation and displacement, not just pain.
  • 3. To contend with adversity; to be knocked by fortune (figurative): Buffeted by rising prices, buffeted by scandal, the buffeting of fate. Bridge: life’s blows mapped to weather/fists. Very productive in journalism (“markets buffeted by fears”). Implies endurance without control.
  • 4. To box, slap, or cuff (archaic literal, of fists): Knights buffeting each other, to buffet with the palm. Now rare except in historical novels and the idiom to buffet about. The original medieval sense from which weather senses grew.
  • 5. Noun sense — a succession of blows; the experience of being battered: The buffeting of winds, to receive many buffetings. Pluralises readily. In engineering, buffeting is a term of art for aerodynamic oscillation (tail buffeting, transonic buffeting) — rapid irregular shaking that can fatigue structures.
  • 6. What it is NOT: Not buffet /bʊfeɪ/ (food counter, from French buffet “sideboard”); not to buffer (to cushion or store data). The spelling overlap causes persistent learner confusion, resolved by stress and context.

Domain extensions deepen the picture. In meteorology and oceanography, buffeting collocates with gale, gust, headwind, surf, swell and implies turbulent flow, not laminar breeze. In aviation, buffeting is diagnostic: pilots report light/moderate/severe buffeting as precursor to stall or flutter, and engineers instrument buffet onset. In boxing and martial history, buffet named a blunt blow (not a cut), hence to take a buffeting = to absorb punishment. In economics and politics, buffeted is almost cliché — currencies, charities, reputations are “buffeted” — because it dramatises helplessness while preserving dignity (one is struck, not foolish).

  • Collocations: buffeting winds / gales / gusts; buffeting waves / surf; buffeting his face / cheeks; buffeted by storms / markets / scandal / fate; take / receive / endure a buffeting; buffet onset / buffet boundary (aviation).
  • Register: Literary-vivid for weather, technical-precise in aviation, journalistic-formulaic for misfortune. Always dynamic and slightly formal; colloquial speech prefers battered, knocked about.
  • Boundaries: Unlike battering (heavier, destructive), buffeting suggests repeated but survivable blows. Unlike pummelling (fists, continuous), buffeting suggests environmental rhythm. Unlike lashing (whip-like), buffeting suggests blunt impact.

In the desert-gale vignette, buffeting completes a crescendo of verbs — tore, buffeting, whipping, bounding, stung — each adding a tactile channel: pull, pound, flay, pelt, pierce. To be buffeted here is to lose footing, vision (tears whipped away), and skin comfort at once, preparing the reader for shelter-seeking and the leeward archway that follows.
