Repertoire travels from Roman finding to French indexing to English staging. The Indo-European root *per-, to bring forth, to bear, yields Latin parere, to give birth, parent, parturient, and by compounding re- plus parire, to obtain, yields reperire, to find again, to discover by bringing forth. The Roman verb covers finding lost property, discovering truth and inventing arguments. Rhetorical inventio is reperire: finding things to say.

Late Latin repertorium, a place where finds are stored, means an inventory or list. Medieval lawyers keep repertoria of titles and fees. French répertoire by the fifteenth century means an alphabetical index or directory where one finds addresses, a desk tool. Diderot Encyclopédie lists répertoires of books. Theatrical use grows naturally: the sheets listing what a troupe has found and can play again.

  • Sound change: Latin repertorium loses -orium ending in French to -oire then -oire to -oire spelled -oire; English borrows French spelling repertoire with -oi- pronounced as wah, anglicized stress shift to first syllable.
  • Doublet repertory: English earlier adapts as repertory on Latin model, as in repertory of writs; later reborrows French repertoire for artistic stock, leaving repertory for the system and repertoire for the list.
  • Semantic drift: from finding to list of finds to performable list to performable ability to social role stock, each step narrowing to readiness.

English theatrical borrowing is dated to the 1840s-1850s when French opera companies tour London. Critics speak of the repertoire of the Théâtre Français. By the 1880s English repertory theatres at Manchester and Birmingham institutionalize rotation. Musical programs adopt the term: a violinist repertoire. Mid-twentieth-century sociology completes the metaphor. Kenneth Burke dramatism, Erving Goffman Presentation of Self 1959 and Victor Turner social drama treat interaction as staged, needing a repertoire of roles. Linguistics follows with verbal repertoire of multilinguals.

Literary and scholarly attestations cement prestige. Shaw reviews repertory. Stanislavski builds actor repertoire. Contemporary management speaks of leadership repertoire. The prompt sentence exemplifies the dramaturgic turn: society as dramatic text with recognizable repertoire, implying roles are scripted, finite and learnable, a hopeful structuralism against formlessness.

Concluding synthesis: from repertorium drawer to French index to opera season to sociological wardrobe, repertoire names the paradox of prepared spontaneity. Actors improvise because stock is deep; speakers codeswitch because repertoire is wide; immune systems survive because antibody repertoire is vast. To claim society has recognizable repertoire is to claim order without rigidity: roles recur enough to be legible yet allow performance variation. Learning culture is thus learning repertoire, and expanding repertoire is expanding freedom to play parts well.

Note on usage: British repertory theatre versus American regional theatre both trade on rotating repertoire to train actors and sustain audiences. Critics praise deep repertoire as versatility, mock thin repertoire as one-trick. Language teachers prize expanded verbal repertoire as communicative competence.
