Subterranean is an adjective meaning existing, situated, occurring, or taking place beneath the surface of the earth — that is, underground. In the literary context of this note, from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), it describes the lake whose existence the traveller can only guess at: "the brink of a deep dark subterranean lake." The word there does not merely locate the water; it opens a hidden geography. Pool and stream are ordinary surface words; subterranean pulls the reader down into the imagined architecture of the world beneath — caverns, buried waters, and the secrets of the deep. Working with deep and dark, it builds an atmosphere of vast, sunless, unknown space, and it quietly expands the setting from a passageway to an underworld, hinting that the mountain contains lakes no one has ever seen. The word is strongly learned, Latinate, almost geological in tone, and that is precisely its power in the sentence: it lends scholarly menace to water that might, at the other extreme, be just a pool in the path.

Primary sense (literal). The core meaning is straightforward and spatial: lying under the surface of the earth. A subterranean river flows through a cave system; a subterranean chamber is a room carved or formed below ground; subterranean water is groundwater held in rock. In this literal sense the word is a formal, near-technical synonym of underground, preferred in scientific, engineering, and journalistic prose when precision and gravity are wanted.

Secondary senses and modern uses. The word has widened well beyond simple location:
  • Geology and speleology: subterranean refers to the whole realm of caves, aquifers, karst drainage, and lava tubes — any structure below the surface, studied as its own world (subterranean reservoirs, subterranean passage systems).
  • Architecture and engineering: subterranean levels, tunnels, bunkers, and railways — structures built below grade, where the term implies deliberate excavation and the management of a hostile, dark environment.
  • Biology: subterranean fauna and flora — organisms adapted to permanent darkness (troglobites), often eyeless and pigmentless; the word names a whole ecological niche.
  • Figurative and psychological transfer: what is hidden below the surface of social life, politics, or the mind: "subterranean currents" of feeling, "subterranean forces" in history, a "subterranean economy" of undeclared transactions. Here the historical bridge is semantic: anything under the earth is by definition concealed from view, so the word became a metaphor for the hidden, the suppressed, and the secretly active.
  • Literary and mythic register: in fantasy and gothic fiction, subterranean denotes the imagined underworld — sunless realms, buried kingdoms, nameless lakes — the mythic underside of the world, which is exactly the effect Tolkien exploits.

Register and distribution. The word is unambiguously bookish and Latinate, sitting higher in register than plain underground. It entered English in the early seventeenth century as a direct learned loan from Latin subterraneus and has remained an educated, slightly ceremonial term ever since. It is the least common and most evocative of the underground words, lending scholarly menace to the deep dark lake, and it quietly expands the setting from a passageway to an underworld.
