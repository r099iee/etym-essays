Tempering is the present participle of temper, from Latin temperare meaning to mix in due measure, to combine in right proportion, to regulate, restrain. The deeper root is disputed but often linked to PIE *tem- meaning to cut, divide (as in cutting something into measured portions) or to *temp- meaning to stretch, span (as in time, temple, contemplation). Latin tempus meaning time and temperare meaning mixture may share the notion of measured interval or due season — to temper is to bring to the right time-state.

In classical Latin temperare covers a wide field unified by proportion: to temper wine by mixing with water, to temper iron by quenching, to temper the climate, to temper anger, to temper laws. Temperantia (temperance) is the cardinal virtue of self-restraint — keeping passions in due mixture. Vitruvius uses temperare for mixing mortar; Pliny for hardening steel; Cicero for moderating the state. The participle temperans means self-controlled; temperatura means constitution produced by mixture.

  • Medieval craft senses: Through Old French temprer (12th-13th c.), English temperen (Chaucer, c.1380) inherits both to moderate and the technical senses: to temper mortar by mixing, to temper steel by heating and quenching, to temper a musical instrument by tuning. A tempered sword is neither too brittle nor too soft because heated and cooled in due measure.
  • Early modern moralization: 1500-1700 fixes temper as character mixture: a good temper is a well-mixed humoural constitution; a bad temper is ill-mixture; to temper justice with mercy is to mix severity with mildness. Shakespeare plays constantly on temper as both hardening and softening — to temper is to bring to right hardness by controlled softening.
  • Industrial precision: 18th-19th c. metallurgy gives tempering exact scientific meaning: reheating quenched steel to 200-600°C to reduce brittleness while retaining hardness, producing straw, brown, blue temper colours. Tempering is distinguished from annealing (softening) and quenching (hardening): it is hardening moderated by softening.

Phonetically temper /ˈtempər/ is stable since Middle English; tempering /ˈtempərɪŋ/ adds regular -ing. Spelling retains -mper- from Latin, never *tempre. The noun temper (mood) and verb temper (moderate) and technical tempering (metallurgy) are the same word viewed from different angles of mixture.

Your context — softening them, tempering them, and combining them — uses the metallurgical sense in a prehistoric panorama: early man learns fire not merely to melt but to soften, harden in due measure, and alloy — producing bronze and iron stronger and suppler than native metals. The paradox that tempering both hardens and softens is the point: it brings metal to usable toughness by measured heating.

  • Modern extensions: Tempered glass (prestressed for safety), tempered chocolate (crystallized for snap and gloss), tempered expectations, tempered optimism — all preserve the logic of moderated intensity producing greater resilience.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer tempering mortar; Malory tempering swords; Milton tempering justice; Austen on tempered manners; modern materials science on tempering martensite.
  • Debate note: The PIE ancestor of temperare is debated: de Vaan treats temperare as possibly from tempus + -erare or from *tem-p- to cut, while others link to Greek temnein to cut. The Latin sense to mix in due measure is undisputed; only the pre-Latin laryngeal reconstruction is uncertain, so the chain above gives the *tem- hypothesis with caution.

To temper metal is thus, etymologically, to make time and mixture right — to rescue hardness from brittleness by judicious softening, just as to temper judgment is to rescue firmness from cruelty by judicious mercy.
