Priestly means of, relating to, or befitting a priest or the priesthood — belonging to the ordained clergy, their office, garments, duties, or manner. As an adjective it covers both the institutional and the personal: "priestly office", "priestly vestments", "priestly vocation", "a priestly bearing". It can describe the literal ministry of authorised religious leaders or, by extension, any solemn, consecrated, ceremonial quality in a person, act, or calling.

In Wordsworth's Prelude (the source context of this card), the word operates in the figurative register: "poetic numbers came / Spontaneously to clothe in priestly robe / A renovated spirit" — the poet imagines the verses themselves vesting his renewed spirit in sacerdotal dress, presenting the poetic calling as a sacred, ministerial vocation. It is a central Romantic move: the artist as priest of the imagination.

Principal senses and domains:

  • Ecclesiastical: of or belonging to the ordained clergy — priestly ordination, priestly celibacy, priestly robes and stoles, priestly functions (absolution, consecration); the word is neutral-to-reverent in Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican use.
  • Sacerdotal theory: in theology, "priestly" contrasts with "prophetic" and "kingly" as one of the three Old Testament offices (Christ as priest, prophet, king); "priestly religion" stresses sacrifice and mediation as against ethical or mystical religion.
  • Biblical criticism: the "Priestly source" (conventionally P) is one of the documentary strands of the Pentateuch (Genesis–Numbers), so called for its interest in cult, calendar, and genealogy — a technical, non-religious use of the adjective in scholarship.
  • Anthropology and sociology: "priestly class", "priestly caste", "priestly power" — the study of religious specialists as a social stratum; the phrase carries from neutral description to anti-clerical criticism depending on context.
  • Figurative and aesthetic: hieratic, solemn, ceremonial — "priestly silence", "a priestly gravity in his speech"; the Romantic use ("the priestly calling of the poet") and the generalised sense of reverent formality.

The historical bridge of the English word is a long one: Greek presbyteros, "elder" (a comparative), became the Christian term for the ordained elder; Latin presbyter entered Old English as prēost ("priest"); the adjective priestly is a native English formation from priest + the suffix -ly (Old English prēostlīc), attested from the tenth century. The word thus combines a Mediterranean chain of ordination with a purely English adjectival building-block.

Register: priestly is the ordinary, neutral adjective in religious use (more everyday than sacerdotal); in secular contexts it is formal and mildly marked, suggesting solemnity, authority, or — when used critically — the self-importance of a privileged class.
