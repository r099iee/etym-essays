Gurgling is the present participle of the verb gurgle, used here as a participial adjective and, in phrases like “a gurgling sound”, substantively as well. Its core sense is the low, bubbling, liquid noise produced when water or air churns through a throat, a drain, or a narrow channel — a sound that is at once fluid, repetitive, and wet. In the literary context of J.R.R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit, the word is brutally exact: Gollum has just lost the Ring, sits down in despair, and begins to weep, and his sobbing reaches Bilbo as “a whistling and gurgling sound horrible to listen to.” The gurgling captures the viscous, throat-choked quality of the cries — sobs half-drowned in phlegm and slobber, bubbling up from a creature whose whole physiology seems alien and amphibious. Where a human sob is a dry, convulsive gasp, Gollum’s grief gargles, and that single word makes the sound disgusting as well as pitiful, its wetness conveying both the wrongness of his body and the depth of his despair. The word is onomatopoeic from birth: its liquid, reduplicated syllables (gur-gul) mime the bursting bubbles themselves, so that the reader’s ear hears the noise at the very moment of reading it. All the modern senses radiate from this one echoic core — the sound of water and the sound of the throat are the same sound, and English has used a single word for both since the fifteenth century. Core senses and usages:
  • Bubbling liquid (primary physical sense): water or another liquid giving off a broken, burbling series of bubbles as it moves over an obstacle, through a narrow space, or out of an opening — a stream gurgling over pebbles, a brook gurgling, a bottle gurgling as it empties, a sump pump gurgling.
  • Throaty, choked sound (bodily sense): the wet noise of air passing through liquid lodged in the throat or windpipe, as in choking, drowning, or violent weeping; this is the sense Tolkien presses into service for Gollum, and it is also the sound of a gravely ill or dying person’s laboured breathing.
  • Infantile pleasure (emotional register): a baby “gurgling” happily in a crib — a soft, bubbling, throaty coo of contentment; the cheerful pole of the word’s double life.
  • Digestive rumbling: the stomach or gut is said to gurgle when gas and fluid move audibly through the intestines (what medicine calls borborygmus).
  • Clinical and medical jargon: in auscultation, “gurgling” or “gurgling sounds” denote bubbling, coarse crackling noises heard in the chest or airway, frequently associated with retained secretions or airway compromise; a “death rattle” is often glossed as gurgling.
  • Domestic and mechanical noise: drains, sink traps, radiators, boilers, and pipes gurgle as air and water struggle through them; a toilet or washing machine may gurgle before clearing.
  • Figurative and metaphorical extensions: a “gurgle of laughter” or a “gurgle of delight” transfers the sound to the emotion that accompanies it; the word is also applied loosely to any wet, bubbling, murmuring noise in nature — a hidden spring, a reed-bed, a marsh.
Polysemy and the historical bridge: the entire family of senses hangs on one phonetic fact — the reduplicative, guttural-plus-liquid pattern g-r-g / g-u-r-g, which in almost every language imitates the gulping and bubbling of the throat and of swirling water. Latin already split the base two ways: gurges “whirlpool, flood” named agitated water, while gurgulio named the gullet and windpipe. Modern English gurgle inherits both halves at once, which is why a word can describe a happy baby and a drowning man with equal accuracy. The cheerful infantile sense is a comparatively late flourish of the nineteenth century, when nursery literature annexed the word; the threatening sense is as old as the word itself. No sense has died: today’s dictionary entry still straddles the merry bubbling of a brook and the wet, horrible bubbling of a choked throat, and it is precisely this double life that lets Tolkien’s “whistling and gurgling sound horrible to listen to” land so hard — the reader hears, in one word, both the musical gurgle of water and the clinical gurgle of a body half-choked with liquid. Collateral family members that share the base but are not identical in meaning include gargle (a deliberate, controlled rinsing of the throat, from the same echoic family), gargoyle (a stone throat-spout that gurgles rainwater from a cathedral roof), and gurgle itself in its nominal use (“the gurgle of the stream”). In the passage, the participle’s adjectival placement after the linking verb — “a whistling and gurgling sound” — also lends it a continuing, durative quality: the sound goes on and on, bubbling and repeating, exactly like Gollum’s inconsolable weeping.
