- Late 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Introduced from Old French *diminutif* and Latin *dēminūtīvus* as a precise technical term in Latin and vernacular grammatical education (John Trevisa, Chaucer); used strictly as a noun or adjective to categorize grammatical suffixes indicating smallness (*-ling*, *-kin*).
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor Humanism & Rhetoric) - Preserved in English Latin grammars (William Lily's standard grammar); began expanding from purely formal morphology into the rhetorical realm of hypocorisms (pet names, terms of endearing intimacy) and polite diminutive speech formulas.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Restoration Generalization) - Crossed decisively from grammatical terminology into general literature and drama (Shakespeare, Milton); began being applied directly to physical bodies, describing tiny fairies, miniature animals, and small human figures.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment Science & Microscopy) - Embraced by natural philosophers, botanists, and satirists (Jonathan Swift, Alexander Pope) to describe delicate insect anatomy, microscopic specimens, and miniature clockwork; simultaneously acquired a pejorative rhetorical edge for belittling trivial arguments.
- 19th Century (Victorian Novel & Social Portraiture) - Flourished in Victorian fiction (Charles Dickens, William Makepeace Thackeray) as a fond, picturesque character epithet for petite, charming, or eccentric individuals living in quaint, cramped dwellings.
- 20th Century to Present (Linguistic Formalism & General Use) - Solidified into its modern dual status: an indispensable technical term in morphological linguistics and typology, and a polished, elegant literary adjective for anyone or anything remarkably petite, compact, or miniature.
