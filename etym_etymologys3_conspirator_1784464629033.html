Conspirator is a word built entirely from breath: Latin spirare "to breathe," the prefix con- "together," and the agent suffix -tor — literally "one who breathes together" with others. Its biography runs from the physiology of shared air, through the politics of republican Rome, into medieval law and the gunpowder plots of English history, and down to the mock-grand toast in which a dwarf inducts a hobbit into a conspiracy against a dragon. Few words carry such a direct line from the body to the state. 1. The breathing root: Latin spirare The stem is Latin spīrāre "to breathe, to blow," attested in classical Latin and already productive by the 1st century BCE — the source of the enormous English family spirit, inspire, expire, respire, transpire, aspire. Its own deeper origin is uncertain: the traditional guess connects it to a reconstructed Indo-European root *(s)peis- "to blow," comparing Germanic blowing-words, but the cognate set is thin and dictionaries mark spirare as "origin uncertain." For the story of conspirator the important fact is the Latin verb itself, not its disputed prehistory: to breathe, and hence to live, and hence — in the deep metaphor of ancient physiology — to share breath, to be of one spirit.
  • Latin: spīrāre "to breathe, blow; to be alive."
  • Origin: uncertain; a PIE *(s)peis- "to blow" is proposed but not securely established.
  • Family: spirit, inspire, expire, respire, transpire, aspire.
2. Breathe together: conspirare and the slide to plotting Latin joined spirare to the comitative prefix con- "with, together" to form cōnspīrāre "to breathe together." The first sense is innocent and almost mystical: to be in harmony, to agree, to be of one mind and breath — the shared breath of people who think as one. But in the political prose of the late Republic the word darkened: those who breathe together in secret are precisely those who plot together against the state. Cicero uses conspirare and conspiratio of political agreement and of plots, and the noun conspiratio came to mean "conspiracy, secret plot." From harmony to treason in one political generation — the semantic slide is the heart of the word's history, and it explains why conspiracy is essentially social: etymologically one cannot conspire alone. 3. The agent noun: conspirator, and the road to English Latin formed the agent noun cōnspīrātor "one who conspires" from the verb stem plus -tor (the suffix of the doer). The word passed into French as conspirateur and entered English in the Middle English period, attested from the late 14th century. The political darkness came with it: in Renaissance and early modern England a conspirator was a figure of real danger — the Gunpowder plotters of 1605 were conspirators, and the word carried the weight of treason, torture, and the state's terrible reply. The vocabulary of conspiracy is among the most charged in English: conspiracy, conspire, conspiratorial, conspirator all descend from the same Latin compound. 4. From treason to law and back again In criminal law, conspiracy acquired a precise technical sense: the agreement of two or more persons to commit an unlawful act is itself the offence, so a conspirator is defined by the shared design even if the plot never comes to anything. In the modern world the word also feeds the vocabulary of conspiracy theories, where alleged conspirators are named both literally and dismissively. The old double life persists — the innocent "breathe together" of harmony is long gone, but the word still names the dangerous meeting of minds in secret. 5. Tolkien and the mock-grand toast In The Hobbit, the toasting voice of the company names Bilbo "our friend and fellow conspirator, this most excellent and audacious hobbit." The word is half-serious elevation: the unexpected party becomes a cabal, the burglar-recruit a sworn plotter, and Bag End a conspirators' hall. For a respectable Baggins the label is comic overstatement; for the dwarves' real plan against a dragon it is nearly literal. Tolkien reaches for the Latinate legal-political word precisely because its dark charge makes the joke — and yet the plot is real, so comedy and treason hold together in one breath. The card keeps the singular agent noun of the speech: Bilbo named, formally and fondly, as one who breathes the plan with them — a word carrying two thousand years of shared breath, from Roman harmony to hobbit conspiracy.
