Anglers is the plural of angler, meaning people who fish with hook and line — rod, line, hook and bait — whether for subsistence, commerce or sport. In this passage it is used in the older generic sense of fishermen in general: the Tahitian fish, drunk with the huteo and hora mixture and floating leisurely, were gathered effortlessly at the anglers will, that is, at their pleasure and discretion. In short, anglers here means the fishermen who take the stupefied fish.

Strictly, an angler is distinguished from other fishers by technique. Net-fishers use seines and trawls, spear-fishers use harpoons and gigs, trappers use weirs, pots and lures, and poison-fishers use ichthyotoxins; anglers use a baited hook that the fish takes voluntarily. Modern sport refines this into coarse angling, game angling, fly-fishing, sea-angling and ice-fishing, each with its own tackle, licences and ethics. The passage stretches the strict sense, applying anglers to poison-gatherers, but the stretch is idiomatic and historically normal.

Exhaustive senses and nuances:

  • 1. Core agentive sense — hook-and-line fisherman: one who fishes with an angle (Old English angel fishhook); covers child with cane pole, Waltonian fly-fisher, charter-boat client and commercial liner alike; implies patience, stillness and individual skill rather than collective net-hauling.
  • 2. Sport-specific sense — licensed recreational fisher: in modern Britain and America an angler is a licence-holder subject to seasons, size limits and catch-and-release rules; connotes leisure, tourism and conservation stakeholding; contrasts with poacher and commercial trawlerman.
  • 3. Generic fishermen sense — as in this passage: loose literary use for any men who catch fish, regardless of method; licensed by the possessive construction at the anglers will meaning whenever and however they wished; the narcotic method makes hooking unnecessary yet the word still fits because the gatherers exercise the anglers privilege of taking.
  • 4. Biological transferred sense — anglerfish component: not the sense here but essential polysemy; anglerfish (Lophiiformes) is named for its illicium lure that dangles like bait before a cavernous mouth; the human technique becomes animal anatomy by visual metaphor; plural anglerfishes or anglerfish preserves the stem.
  • 5. Figurative elicitation sense — angling for something: the verb to angle extends to angling for compliments, votes, invitations or information, casting a conversational hook and waiting for a bite; the noun angler rarely follows into this metaphor, but the underlying hook schema is identical: concealment, temptation, sudden capture.
  • 6. Commercial and institutional senses: charter angler, match angler, coarse angler, specimen angler; each denotes a subculture with tackle shops, magazines, clubs and competitions; implies graded expertise from novice to specimen hunter seeking record fish.
  • 7. Connotational range: historically humble subsistence, then Waltonian contemplative virtue, then Victorian sporting masculinity, then modern eco-tourist and conservationist; can be affectionate (keen angler), satirical (all anglers exaggerate) or legal (licensed anglers only); the classic joke about the one that got away attaches permanently to the word.
  • 8. Grammatical note — regular plural: angler to anglers by simple -s, unlike fisherman to fishermen and fish to fish; attributive use as angler effort, angler pressure, angler survey treats the singular as modifier; possessive anglers will in the passage uses the plural possessive without apostrophe placement modernised as anglers will.

The semantic bridge from hook to person is straightforward agent formation: the bent implement (*angulaz) gives the action (to angle) which gives the actor (angler). Secondary bridges run by visual analogy (fish lure like rod), by legal privilege (rod allowed where nets banned) and by moral idealisation (patience as virtue). In the immersion sentence the operative bridge is the loosest: men who control floating fish exercise an anglers will — effortless discretionary taking — whether or not a hook was ever baited.
