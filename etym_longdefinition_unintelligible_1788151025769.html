Core meaning.Unintelligible means impossible to understand: speech so garbled that no meaning can be extracted, a text so obscure that the reader cannot tell what it says, a signal, sound, or pattern that defeats interpretation. It is the precise negation of intelligible ("able to be understood"), and it typically describes either a failure of the utterance (mumbling, jargon, corruption, noise) or a failure of the register (a language, code, or concept for which the hearer lacks the key). In the passage above, the "unintelligible whim of the elements" is nature's weather acting as a will that no human reason can read — the dryness of rain withheld meaning nothing that cities could interpret or bargain with.

The word's shape. The skeleton of the word tells its story: un- (Old English for "not") + intelligible (Latin intelligibilis, "that can be perceived by the mind", from intellegere, "to understand", itself inter "between" + legere "to gather, choose, read"). Understanding is, etymologically, a gathering: the mind collects the scattered signs and gathers them into continuous meaning. What is unintelligible cannot be gathered — the pieces refuse to assemble.

  • Of speech and sound: mumbling, static, a drowned voice, a language entirely unknown to the listener.
  • Of writing and script: a text that cannot be read — though note the boundary: illegible means the writing cannot be deciphered physically; unintelligible means the meaning cannot be arrived at even when the letters are clear.
  • Of logic and thought: an incoherent argument, a policy whose aim cannot be stated, a dream that makes no sense.
  • Of mathematics, code, and data: "unintelligible output", raw data without a schema — modern technical uses that extend the classical sense of a thing not readable by the untrained mind.
  • Philosophical and theological: the unintelligible as that which exceeds human grasp — the inscrutable, the ineffable; 17th-century philosophers used the word of mysteries that reason cannot penetrate.

Semantic boundaries and register. Unintelligible is stronger than hard to understand and more precise than confusing: it declares an absolute impossibility, at least for the intended audience. It is cooler than nonsensical (which implies that no sense exists) — an unintelligible message may have perfect sense once the code is found. Nor does it accuse: a murmur can be unintelligible without being foolish. In style it is the standard formal word for communication breakdowns of every kind, from a stuttering radio transmission to a paragraph of academic prose that collapses under its own jargon.

In practice: who fails, the speaker or the hearer? The word does not say where the fault lies, and that neutrality is one of its great conveniences. An utterance may be unintelligible because the speaker mangles it, because the medium corrupts it (a dead battery, a bad line, a smudged page), because it is written in a code the hearer lacks, or because the thought itself outruns language. This makes unintelligible the diplomatic word of miscommunication: it reports the failure without assigning the blame, and it can be applied as easily to a prophecy as to a stutter. In legal and administrative prose this neutrality is prized — a clause is struck down as unintelligible not because it is absurd but because no reading can be fixed to it; in ordinary speech it is often the first resort of the politely baffled. The related noun, unintelligibility, is rarer and more technical, favoured in philosophy of language and in translation theory for the systematic opacity between conceptual schemes.
