decisive. Macaulay's phrase — the crisis "which the administration of King James the Second brought to a decisive crisis" — deploys the adjective in its classic historical sense: the crisis that settles the question. It is the moment at which the long contest between the Crown and Parliament reached its point of no return, the point at which it was determined which side would prevail. In Macaulay's Whig narrative the Glorious Revolution is the decisive event; James's own overreaching administration, by driving the crisis to its conclusion, made the resolution inevitable. The tone is teleological and confident — the historian speaking as judge, pronouncing that events had reached a determining pass. The collocation has a quiet paradox: crisis itself comes from Greek krísis, 'a judgement, a decision', so 'decisive crisis' names a moment of judgement that decides — an almost tautological intensification. The word lends the narrative the sense of a verdict reached, of history settling an account. Core senses
  • Settling an issue. The primary sense: that which settles a dispute, question, or uncertainty and produces a definite result or resolution. A decisive answer removes doubt; a decisive meeting ends months of negotiation. The 'cutting' image of the Latin ancestor (dēcīdere, 'to cut off') is still faintly felt — the decision severs the alternatives like a blade.
  • Producing a definite result. Close to 'conclusive': a decisive victory ends a war; a decisive vote determines an election. The emphasis is on outcome and finality rather than on the act of choosing.
  • Of evidence and factors. Decisive evidence, a decisive factor, the decisive argument: that which tips the balance and compels a conclusion, used in law, logic, science, and business. A 'decisive factor' is the variable that accounts for the outcome.
  • Of persons: resolute and firm. A decisive person, a decisive temperament, a decisive leader: someone who makes up their mind quickly and acts on the choice. This personal sense, emerging in the 18th century and fully established in the 20th, shifts the word from events to character; its opposite is indecisive, irresolute, dithering.
  • Military and strategic. A decisive engagement, a decisive battle, decisive force: a clash whose outcome shapes the whole campaign; in 19th-century strategic theory (Clausewitz, Jomini) the decisive point was where the outcome of a war was determined.
  • Scientific and mathematical. A decisive experiment (the experimentum crucis), a decisive test, a decisive axiom: a procedure or proposition that settles a question one way or the other; in statistics, a decisive result is one beyond reasonable doubt.
  • Metaphoric and figurative shifts. Anything that 'settles' in a transferred sense: a decisive gesture, a decisive tone, a decisive move in chess or business. The underlying metaphor is always the cut — the single stroke that divides possibility from fact.
Historical bridge The whole family rests on Latin dēcīdere, 'to cut off' (dē- 'off, down' + caedere 'to cut, hew, strike, kill'). Cutting off a thing — a branch, a head, a knot — became the standard Latin image for settling a dispute: the decision is the cut that ends the argument, exactly as Alexander's sword severed the Gordian knot. From the participle dēcīsus came dēcīsiō 'a decision' and the Late Latin adjective dēcīsīvus 'decisive' (4th century), which passed through Middle French décisif into English around 1610. The same root caedere produced a vast English crop: concise, precise, incision, incisive, homicide, suicide, caesura, and even the imperial titles Kaiser and tsar (via the name Caesar, folk-etymologised as 'cut out from the womb'). Synonym boundaries
  • Conclusive stresses the removal of all doubt — conclusive evidence closes a case — and belongs to law and logic rather than to events.
  • Definitive means authoritative and final, setting the standard: a definitive edition, a definitive answer.
  • Deciding and determining are the neutral causal words ('the deciding factor'), naming whatever tips the balance without decisive's weight.
  • Critical marks a turning point but also carries urgent danger, so a 'critical crisis' would be acute rather than settling.
  • Crucial (from Latin crux, 'cross') denotes the decisive test — the experimentum crucis — and is near-synonymous with decisive importance.
  • Pivotal pictures events turning on a pivot; momentous stresses weighty consequence rather than the act of resolution.
  • Resolute, firm, determined are the personal-sense neighbours, describing the person who decides rather than the event that settles.
