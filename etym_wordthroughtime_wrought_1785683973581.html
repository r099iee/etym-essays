- [Old English] - Plain past tense and past participle of wyrcan ('to work'), covering every sense of work: labour, doing, making. At this stage wrought is the everyday form where we now say worked — no special gravity attached.
- [Middle English] - Becomes the characteristic form for crafted labour: 'wrought iron', 'wrought by hand'. It keeps its ordinary past-tense job but starts to acquire weight — work that has been hammered and shaped with deliberate skill.
- [16th-17th c.] - As 'worked' takes over as the regular past tense, wrought survives in elevated, biblical and poetic registers meaning 'made, fashioned or effected', and extends figuratively into emotion: 'wrought up', worked into agitation. Connotation grows grand, archaic and weighty.
- [Modern usage] - No longer a working verb form; chosen deliberately for stately, literary or archaic dignity and preserved in fixed phrases (wrought iron, wrought havoc, overwrought). Perception is formal and faintly biblical, carrying the force of patient forging rather than everyday labour.
