Essay on PORK (the pig-meat strand). The first half of the compound descends from a genuinely ancient Indo-European livestock word. Proto-Indo-European *pórḱos, “young pig, piglet”, was part of the basic agricultural vocabulary of the western Indo-European communities — the same root that reached Latin as porcus “pig, swine” and the Germanic branch (via Grimm's Law, PIE *p- to Proto-Germanic *f-) as *farhaz, the ancestor of Old English fearh “piglet” and of the modern English farrow (a litter of piglets; to give birth of a sow). English therefore holds two descendants of one PIE word: the native Germanic farrow and the borrowed Latinate pork — a classic doublet created by the Norman Conquest. Latin porcus itself produced the Romance family (Italian porco, Spanish puerco, Portuguese porco, French porc, Romanian porc), and it reached Slavic in a parallel formation (Russian porosënok, “piglet”). The root even appears in the word porcupine, a Latinate compound meaning literally “prickly pig” (“pig-thorn”), showing how deep the pig-word runs in the learned European vocabulary.

The history of the word also tracks the history of the animal. The Indo-European pig was a domestic animal of the woodlands — driven into forests to fatten on acorns and beechnuts (the practice of pannage) — and the root *pórḱos names precisely the young animal of the farmyard, not the wild boar. In Latin, porcus is the ordinary word for the farm pig, attested in Cato, Varro, and Pliny in agricultural and gastronomic contexts, and it generated a productive word family: porcārius “swineherd”, porcellus “small pig, porkling”, and the modern Romance words for the animal itself. The sound shape of the Latin word carried the root through the vowel shifts of late Latin into the Romance vernaculars, so that the Old French porc that reached England in the eleventh century was a direct, unbroken descendant of the word Pliny had used a millennium earlier.

The decisive turn for English came in 1066. After the Norman Conquest, the Old French word porc — which named both the animal and its flesh — entered Middle English as pork, and English made a famous semantic split: the living animal kept its native names (pig, swine, sow), while the cooked flesh on the noble table took the French word. The pair pig/pork (like sheep/mutton and calf/veal) encodes the social history of Norman England: the Saxon peasants raised the animals, the Norman masters ate them. From the 13th–14th century onward pork settled as the standard English word for pig-meat, and in the 18th century it entered the compound pork-pie, attested around 1732 — a cold, hand-raised, jellied pie that became a staple of markets, coaching inns, and later railway platforms and pub counters. The word also spun off figurative and political senses in American English: pork-barrel spending and pork for government patronage treat public money as meat to be sliced and handed out — an extension of the feeding metaphor.

Essay on PIE (the pastry strand). The second half of the compound is the pastry word pie, whose origin is the most debated part of the story. The standard scholarly etymology links English pie to the name of the magpie: Latin pīca, via Old French pie, the pied black-and-white bird famous for collecting a jumble of odds and ends. By this account, a pie — a crust enclosing a miscellany of meats, fish, or fruits — was nicknamed after the bird precisely because it too held a mixed hoard. Pie appears in Middle English from about 1300 in exactly this pastry sense, and the magpie itself continued to be called a pie in English, surviving in piebald (“pied, patchy”) and the Pied Piper. The etymology is not beyond dispute — some prefer a derivation from a word for “pasty, folded dough” — but the magpie-nickname remains the usual and most widely cited explanation, and no rival theory has displaced it. The deeper origin of the bird-name itself is likewise uncertain and not securely attached to any Proto-Indo-European root.

From that single fourteenth-century name, pie expanded to cover every sweet or savoury dish with a crust — apple pie, pork pie, shepherd's pie, mud pie — and into idioms (easy as pie, pie in the sky). In the medieval kitchen the pie was also a food technology: the tall, sturdy crust (the coffin, from Latin cophinus, a basket) acted as a sealed baking and preserving vessel, letting rich mixtures of meat and spice be cooked slowly and kept for days — the same pre-refrigeration logic that produced the raised cold pies of the English market town. The pork pie is the most complete survivor of that technology: its dense hot-water crust is a descendant of the medieval standing crust, baked blind and then filled and re-glazed, and the sealing layer of jellied stock is a direct inheritance from the medieval technique of pouring clarified broth through a hole in the pie's lid to exclude air and preserve the meat.

The compound pork-pie, joining the pig-meat word to the pastry word, crystallised in the early 18th century (attested c. 1732) and named a distinct class of cold raised pie: chopped seasoned pork and jellied stock sealed in a sturdy hot-water crust, the pie being raised by hand or moulded, and its jelly poured through a hole in the lid after baking. Its most famous regional form, the Melton Mowbray pork pie, earned Protected Geographical Indication status in the European Union in 2008 — a modern legal seal on a word whose roots reach back to Indo-European pig-breeding and to a nickname drawn from a scavenging bird. In the 20th century the word went slang and fashion: Cockney rhyming slang pork pies = lies (clipped to porkies), and the pork-pie hat — a flat-crowned felt hat with a turned-up brim — entered jazz, noir, and mod culture. In Tolkien's scene, Bombur's PORK-PIE draws on all of that: a word of ancient farming and medieval French, of English markets and picnic hampers, deployed as a homely emblem of the plain, hearty, thoroughly un-Elvish plenty that the dwarves demand from the hobbit's larder.

  • *pórḱos “young pig” → Latin porcus → Old French porc → Middle English pork (13th–14th c.) → English pork → pork-pie (c. 1732).
  • Germanic doublet: PIE *p- → *farhaz → Old English fearh → farrow — native twin of borrowed pork.
  • Latin pīca “magpie” → Old French pie → Middle English pie (c. 1300) → English pie → pork-pie.
  • Semantic split: pig/swine (native, the living animal) vs. pork (French, the meat) encodes Norman social history.
  • Debate: the magpie-nickname origin of pie is standard but not universally accepted; no secure PIE equation exists for pīca.
