Component 1: avant (in front, forward)

Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*apo- + *h₂enti
"off, away from" + "in front, facing, opposite"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*ab + *anti
"from" + "before, in front"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
ab + ante
"from" + "before, in front of"
↓
Late Latin / Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–6th c.)
abante
"from before, ahead, forward"
↓
Old French (c. 11th c.)
avant
"before, forward, ahead in space or time"

Component 2: garde (guard, watch, body of soldiers)

Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*wer-
"to perceive, watch over, guard, be wary"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*wardōną
"to watch, guard, look after, protect"
↓
Frankish (Germanic loan into Romance) (c. 5th–7th c.)
*wardōn / *warda
"to guard; a watch, guardpost, sentinel"
↓
Old French (c. 11th c.)
garde (earlier warde)
"protection, watching, body of watchmen"

Synthesis and Modern Derivation: avant-garde + -ist

Old French (compound) (c. 12th c.)
avant-garde
"the foremost body of an advancing army, vanguard"
↓
French (social/aesthetic shift) (c. 1825)
avant-garde
"cultural and political pioneers spearheading progress"
↓
French / International English (late 19th–early 20th c.)
avant-gardiste / avant-gardist
"an adherent or practitioner of the avant-garde"
↓
Modern English (present)
avant-gardists
"practitioners and champions of radical, experimental artistic innovation"

Note: Because avant-gardists is an English morphological derivation (agent suffix -ist plus plural -s) of the French loan compound avant-garde, its etymological lineage unites two distinct linguistic branches: the Romance/Latin component (avant < Latin abante) and the Germanic superstrate component (garde < Frankish *warda). In Old French, Frankish initial /w-/ systematically shifted to /ɡw-/ and then /ɡ-/, creating doublets with Norman English (compare garde/ward, guerre/war, and guarantor/warranty).
