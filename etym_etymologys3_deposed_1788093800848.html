The first ingredient: de- "down, away". The prefix de- comes from the Latin preposition dē "down from, away from, out of", which in turn descends from the Indo-European deictic particle *de "from, away" — a tiny word with a huge career, since it is also the ancestor of the Germanic "to" and of Greek dé. In Latin compounds de- consistently adds the idea of downward or awayward movement: descendere "to climb down", deponere "to put down", deicere "to throw down".

The second ingredient: pōnere "to put, place". Latin pōnere is a contraction of the Old Latin posinō, itself *po-sinō: the prefix po- (from PIE *apo "off, away") plus sinō "to let, leave, allow" — so literally "to let off, set down". (The Indo-European ancestry of sinō is debated; the Latin formation itself is secure, and this gap is flagged rather than filled.) Classical Latin made from posinō the great verb pōnere "to put, place", whose past participle positus gave English "position", "positive", and "deposit".

The curious detour: pausāre takes over. Here Latin played its most famous trick on the Romance languages. A separate Late Latin verb pausāre "to rest, halt, cease" — from the noun pausa "pause", borrowed from Greek paûsis "a stopping" (verb paúein, "to make to stop") — began, in the spoken Latin of Gaul, to take over the meanings of pōnere "to place, set". The two verbs had grown phonetically similar in their participles, and the result was a merger: Old French poser means "to put, place, set", but it is the descendant of pausāre, not of pōnere. This is why English has two families where Latin had one: pose, repose, depose, compose (via French poser) alongside position, deposit, positive, composition (via Latin positus).

The compound: deposer → deposen. Old French joined de- to poser to make deposer, "to put down, lay down, set down" — used of things and burdens from the 12th–13th centuries and, very early, of persons: to put a lord down from his seat of power. Middle English borrowed the verb as deposen around 1300, and both great senses arrived together. The political sense — removing a king, pope, or bishop from office — is attested from the start: English chronicles use it of the depositions of medieval kings (Edward II in 1327, Richard II in 1399). The legal sense, "to give sworn testimony", rests on the same image: the witness "lays down" his evidence before the court (Latin deponere testimonium); English law reports show it from the 15th century, and "deposition" carries both meanings to this day.

The word in culture. Deposition is one of the oldest dramas of power. Medieval canon law developed careful procedures for deposing bishops and popes: the Council of Constance (1414–1418), called to end the Great Schism, actually deposed two rival claimants to the papacy — a juridical act that shaped European ideas of legitimate authority. In England, the deposition of Richard II (1399) became the founding political trauma of the Lancastrian dynasty, and Shakespeare made it the subject of Richard II (1595), where the deposed king — "Must he be deposed? The king shall be contented" — turns the act of being put down into the most moving poetry of fallen majesty in the language. From the 18th century on, "deposed" became the newspaper word for overthrown rulers everywhere, and in American courtrooms the legal sense grew routine: millions of witnesses have been "deposed" under oath, their testimony recorded for trial. Both senses remain alive, and both still mean, at bottom, what the Latin meant: laid down.

  • Phonetic notes. deposer → English depose: the French infinitive ending -er was dropped in the Middle English borrowing; the past participle deposed adds the native -ed. The participle is pronounced with the full diphthong of "pose" /dɪˈpəʊzd/.
