The name has three histories folded into it: the Latin and French history of dame, the French history of the particle-and-place surname du Barry, and the short, spectacular history of the woman who made the combination famous. Carlyle’s English form is a fourth layer, superimposed in 1837.

Dame: from household to honour

Latin domina was the feminine of dominus, “master of a household”, itself built on domus “house” from the Indo-European root *dem- “to build, to house” — the same root that gives Greek démein “to build” and dêmos, the district or people. A domina was therefore not merely a woman but the woman in charge of a house: a mistress in the original, non-sexual sense. Old French reduced the word to dame, and Middle English borrowed it in the thirteenth century with a cluster of senses — lady, mother, wife, woman of rank — that survive in fossil form in dame school and in the personifying Dame Nature.

  • Medieval and Renaissance: a courtesy title for a woman of standing, and a habitual prefix for allegorical figures in morality plays and pageants.
  • French resonance: Notre-Dame, “Our Lady”, keeps dame in its oldest religious sense; the English title and the French cathedral name are the same word at different social altitudes.
  • 19th century: the British honours system creates the Dame as the female equivalent of Sir in 1917, formalising what had been a loose courtesy.
  • American slang: from the 1880s, dame becomes a colloquial word for a woman, often with a dismissive edge; the usage is now dated and reads as noirish or rude.
  • Pantomime: the Dame is the comic female role played by a man, a tradition that has made the word theatrical and slightly camp in British English.

Du Barry: particle, place and family

The French du is a contraction of de and le, and in surnames it marks possession of a place: du Barry means “of Barry”, the seat of the family. The particle is not in itself a proof of nobility — many families adopted one — and the du Barrys’ claim to ancient descent was contested by genealogists even while they enjoyed court favour. The place name Barry is found in several French regions; its origin is usually referred to a Gaulish element *barr- “height, summit” (compare Welsh barr “top, tuft”), though a Gallo-Roman personal name with the estate suffix -acum has also been proposed, and the documentary chain between either etymon and the recorded name is missing.

The woman and the epithet

  • 1743–1768: born illegitimate at Vaucouleurs, educated at Sainte-Aure, employed as a milliner; her early Paris years are the raw material of later legend.
  • 1768–1769: marriage to Guillaume du Barry supplies the name and a fragile noble pedigree; presentation at court in April 1769 supplies the position.
  • 1769–1774: the last maîtresse-en-titre of Louis XV, at the height of a court already notorious for the Parc-aux-Cerfs and for the king’s appetite.
  • 1774–1793: exile at Louveciennes after the king’s death, then arrest and execution in the Terror; her reported last words to the executioner — “one moment more” — became one of the most quoted exits of the Revolution.

Carlyle’s coinage and its afterlife

Carlyle writes Dame Dubarry, not Madame du Barry. The substitution of the English honorific and the loss of the particle do two things at once: they naturalise her into an English chronicle, and they align her with the allegorical dames of medieval drama. In a paragraph that ends with Fortune’s inconstancy and the proverb that each dog has his day, the naming turns a living woman into a figure in a morality play. The device is characteristic of Carlyle’s method throughout The French Revolution, where persons are repeatedly reduced to emblems and then made to carry the moral weight of an epoch.

Later writing re-glamorised her. Nineteenth-century biographies and twentieth-century films restored the French form and the romance of the milliner who became a king’s mistress, so that “du Barry” now hovers between scandal and fairy tale. The Carlylean spelling survives mainly as a quotation, and the plain surname as a historical label for the last of the royal favourites.
