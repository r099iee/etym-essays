gong — a percussion instrument comprising a large, flat, circular or slightly convex disc of cast bronze or brass, suspended vertically by cords or chains and sounded by a single stroke of a padded beater. The defining physical fact of the gong is that it is a plate, not a cup: a bell is a hollow resonator tuned to a definite pitch and struck by a clapper, whereas a gong is a solid metal disc that the blow sets flexing in a low radial mode, sending a deep, sonorous tone of indefinite or shimmering pitch and a dense cloud of inharmonic partials out through the air. The sound does not stop quickly; it swells, shakes, and lingers for many seconds after impact, which is precisely why the instrument has always been used for summons, ritual punctuation, and ceremonial gravity rather than for melody. It does not sing a note so much as deliver a profound, absorbing stroke. In the passage from Mervyn Peake's Gormenghast, the gong is the acoustic fulcrum of the entire rite: it beats thrice from within the castle while the rejected carvings burn in the courtyard below Lord Groan's western balcony, and those three strokes divide the ceremony into before and after — the destruction of the failures and the emergence of the three consummate carvings "brought forth in the moonlight." The word here carries a tone of hieratic solemnity and fate: this is not an instrument of entertainment but the ritual heartbeat of a dynastic liturgy, a bronze voice that pronounces judgment without a word, its tripled stroke answering the three carvings, the three escapes from the flames, the threefold numerology of the rite. In the economy of the passage the gong is the hinge between destruction and revelation, and its resonance hangs over the scene like the castle's own pulse — a sound that has been waiting, as the mnemonic says, for the moonlight. The gong today is a richly polysemous word, and its senses radiate outward from the bronze disc through a series of metonymic and metaphorical bridges:
  • The instrument itself — the primary sense: the suspended bronze plate of Java, Bali, China, and the gamelan; also the single deep, disc-shaped instrument of the Western orchestra and of ritual.
  • The sound — by metonymy, the word names the stroke and its resonance: "the gong sounded," "the gong died away." The sound becomes a unit of time and ceremony.
  • A summons or signal — the gong as the voice of institution: the temple signal, the monastery's flat bronze cousin to the bell, the dinner-gong that summoned servants in the Victorian great house, the gong of the boxing ring that opens and closes rounds.
  • The orchestra's tam-tam — in classical music "gong" denotes the large flat untuned Chinese-style disc; the bossed, tuned gong of the gamelan is the gong ageng of Java, whose single stroke frames whole musical cycles.
  • To gong a performer — in British variety theatre and the TV talent-show tradition (the "Gong Show"), a gong was struck to cut off a bad act; the verb "to gong" means to dismiss or silence someone rudely.
  • A medal or award — in British armed-forces slang a "gong" is a military decoration, a medal; the sense is transferred from the sound of the award ceremony to the honour itself.
  • Modern meditative use — in contemporary wellness culture the gong (and the gong bath) is a sound-therapy instrument; the deep, long-sustained tone is used to induce calm and trance-like states, returning the word to its ceremonial origins.
Set beside its fellow percussion terms, the gong must be carefully distinguished. A bell is a cup- or bowl-shaped resonator tuned to a definite pitch and struck by a clapper; it sings, and it is tied to church, clock, and shipboard signalling. A cymbal is a small shallow disc beaten in pairs for a bright percussive clash; the gong is struck alone, for sustained depth rather than sparkle. A drum is a membrane stretched over a shell and gives a pitched, rhythmic thump; the gong is solid bronze and rings with a long sonorous decay. The tam-tam is technically the flat, untuned gong of the orchestra — "gong" in the strict sense denotes the bossed, tuned instrument of Southeast Asia. Etymologically the word is onomatopoeic, an imitation of the instrument's own deep, flexing boom, and it travelled from the Malay archipelago through the Portuguese and Dutch colonial trade routes into English in the early seventeenth century, so that the word itself, like the instrument, is a late and exotic arrival in Europe — a sound that had been waiting, for centuries of island ceremony, for the moonlight.
