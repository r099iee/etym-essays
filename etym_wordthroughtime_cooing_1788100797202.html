- [1660s England] - [pure ornithology: ‘coo’ names the dove’s murmur in natural-history prose; no tenderness yet — just the sound]
- [late 18th century] - [the verb transfers to human soft talk; lovers and mothers begin to coo in novels and verse — warmth arrives, and the dove-image travels with it]
- [early 19th century] - [the idiom ‘bill and coo’ (doves touching bills and murmuring) locks the word to courtship endearments — slightly coy, stagey, sentimental]
- [Victorian era] - [cooing is the standard tender word over cradles and sweethearts; ‘the cooing of doves’ is stock nature poetry — the pronunciation of domestic bliss]
- [20th century] - [journalism adopts ‘cooed’ as a speech-verb for gushing admiration; irony creeps in — to coo is now often to over-praise in public]
- [Today] - [cooing covers doting parents, fans, pets and marketing whispers; the word wavers between genuine tenderness and gentle mockery, but stays always soft and low]
