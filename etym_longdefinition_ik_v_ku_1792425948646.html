Ikṣvāku (proper noun, Sanskrit masculine; also written Ikshvaku; in Pali, Okkāka) — The foundational progenitor king and first mortal sovereign of the ancient Indian Solar Dynasty (Sūryavaṃśa), credited in Indian epic and Purāṇic historiography with establishing the imperial realm of Kosala and founding its legendary capital at Ayodhyā. As the son of Manu Vaivasvata (the lawgiver of humanity) and grandson of the Sun god Sūrya (Vivasvān), Ikṣvāku stands as the supreme dynastic patriarch from whom Lord Rāma descended in the Rāmāyaṇa, from whom Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) traced his royal Śākya lineage in the Buddhist canon, and from whom twenty-two of the twenty-four Jain Tīrthaṅkaras emerged.

Primary sense: Epic progenitor of the Solar Dynasty and the kingdom of Ayodhyā. In the Sanskrit epic tradition—most famously codified in the opening book of the Rāmāyaṇa (Bālakāṇḍa) and the genealogical chapters of the Mahābhārata and Purāṇas—Ikṣvāku is celebrated as the righteous monarch who inaugurated the golden age of post-diluvian earthly governance. When his father, King Manu, divided the terrestrial realm among his offspring, Ikṣvāku received the prime madhyadeśa (the sacred central plains of northern India), establishing his capital on the banks of the Sarayū river. His royal descendants—known collectively as the Aikṣvākavas or the "House of Ikṣvāku"—include towering figures of Indian legendary history such as Hariścandra, Sagara, Dilīpa, Raghu, Daśaratha, and Rāmacandra. The name serves as the ultimate synonym for nobility, steadfast adherence to dharma (cosmic and moral order), and unhesitating personal sacrifice for the welfare of the people.

Secondary sense: Ancestor of the Śākya clan in Buddhist canonical history. In the Pali Tipiṭaka of Theravada Buddhism, King Ikṣvāku is known under the Middle Indo-Aryan form Okkāka. In foundational canonical discourses such as the Ambaṭṭha Sutta of the Dīgha Nikāya, the Buddha responds to the proud caste assertions of a young Brahmin by reciting the royal pedigree of his own clan. According to the Buddhist narrative, King Okkāka, wishing to favor his beloved queen, banished his elder princes (Okkāmukha, Karaṇḍu, Hatthinīka, and Sinipura) into the Himalayan foothills. There, beside an ancient forest of teak trees (sāka), the noble princes established the city of Kapilavastu and founded the renowned Śākya clan. Thus, in Buddhist historiography, Ikṣvāku/Okkāka is revered as the distant royal ancestor who endowed Gautama Buddha with supreme, unblemished kṣatriya (warrior-aristocrat) nobility.

Tertiary sense: Cosmological patriarch in Jain universal history. In Jainism, the name Ikṣvāku is tied to the very dawn of human civilization. In the Ādi Purāṇa and universal histories (*Śalākāpuruṣa*), the first Tīrthaṅkara, Lord Ṛṣabhanātha (Ādinātha), is identified as the founder of the Ikṣvāku clan (*Ikṣvāku-kula*). When Ṛṣabhanātha was an infant, the king of the gods, Indra, offered him sweet sugarcane juice (*ikṣu-rasa*), leading to the name of the lineage. Through Ṛṣabhanātha, humanity was taught the foundational arts of agriculture, writing, statecraft, and social order. Incredibly, twenty-two of the twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras of the current cosmic epoch (avasarpiṇī)—including Parśvanātha and Mahāvīra—were born into this sacred Ikṣvāku lineage.

Quaternary sense: The Andhra Ikshvaku historical dynasty of Vijayapuri. In historical epigraphy and archaeology, the name refers to an illustrious royal dynasty that ruled the Krishna-Guntur valley in modern Andhra Pradesh during the 3rd and 4th centuries CE, following the decline of the Sātavāhanas. Ruling from their glorious planned riverine capital at Vijayapuri (modern Nagarjunakonda), kings such as Vāsiṣṭhīputra Chāmtamūla and Vīrapuruṣadatta proudly proclaimed themselves descendants of the epic Ikṣvāku. While the emperors performed classical Vedic sacrifices (including the *Aśvamedha* and *Vājapeya*), their royal queens (most notably Princess Chāmtisirī) served as the greatest architectural benefactors of Buddhism in southern India, funding magnificent stupas, chaityas, and viharas that made Nagarjunakonda an international center of Buddhist learning.

Quinary sense: Āyurvedic botanical and pharmacological nomenclature. In classical Sanskrit medical literature, specifically in the Caraka Saṃhitā (Kalpasthāna, chapter 3) and the Suśruta Saṃhitā, ikṣvāku is the technical botanical name for a variety of bitter bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*, also identified with *Trichosanthes cucumerina* or wild bitter cucumber). Ancient physicians utilized its flowers, leaves, and seed extracts as a powerful therapeutic emetic (*vamana*) designed to purge aggravated Kapha and Pitta toxins in patients suffering from respiratory congestion, toxic poisoning, and chronic dermatological ailments.

  • In epic and dynastic literature — House of Ikṣvāku, Ikṣvāku dynasty, Aikṣvākava: denotes the illustrious solar royal line of Ayodhyā, archetypes of truthfulness (*satya*) and dharmic governance.
  • In Buddhist canonical texts — King Okkāka: identifies the great solar progenitor whose royal scions established the Śākya clan at Kapilavastu.
  • In Jain sacred tradition — Ikṣvāku-kula: represents the foundational civilized lineage inaugurated by Lord Ṛṣabhanātha, giving birth to almost all Jain Tīrthaṅkaras.
  • In Indian archaeology & epigraphy — Andhra Ikshvakus: the historical 3rd–4th century CE southern dynasty of Nagarjunakonda renowned for grand Buddhist stupa architecture.
  • In Āyurvedic pharmacology — Ikṣvāku-kalpa: classical medical formulations utilizing the bitter bottle gourd as an emetic purification agent.
