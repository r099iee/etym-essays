Proto-Germanic *skag- / *skagg- (an expressive base meaning 'rough hair, coarse growth', probably 'that which juts out or stands up'; NO secure PIE etymon — the root is Germanic-only) → Old English sceacga (noun, 'rough hair of the head, coarse matted hair') → Middle English shagge (noun, 'rough matted hair or fur; also coarse cloth woven with a nap') → English shag (noun, 'a rough, thick mass of hair or fur') → English shaggy (shag + the adjectival suffix -y, 'having, full of'; first recorded in the 16th century) → SHAGGY Sister-form note (not the ancestor): the same Germanic base appears in North Germanic as Old Norse skegg 'beard', surviving in modern Icelandic skegg, Norwegian skjegg, Danish skæg, Swedish skägg, and in the Viking skeggøx 'beard-axe' (named for the jutting blade); the related Old Norse skagi 'a jutting point of land' survives in the place-name Skagen, Denmark's northern tip. These are siblings and toponyms derived from the root, NOT ancestors of English shaggy, and must not be merged into the English chain. The 'jutting, standing up' sense they preserve probably explains the hair: a shaggy mass is hair that stands out and up instead of lying flat. The noun shag also developed a separate, unrelated English word (the dance and the slang verb), which must not be confused with the hair-word.
