The target word mortified is a compound built on two Indo-European roots; the full linear chain of the compound is given first, then the second element in isolation. MAIN CHAIN (the "death" element *mer- running through to the whole word): PIE *mer- "to die, waste away, disappear by rubbing/dissolving" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin mors, genitive mortis "death" (attested 1st c. BCE; derived from *mer- via *mr̥-tis; the intermediate Italic forms are reconstructed, not attested; related verb morior "to die") → Latin mortificāre "to make dead, kill; to deaden" — Late Latin Christian coinage, mors "death" + facere "to make" (c. 3rd–4th c. CE, in patristic texts) → Old French mortifier "to kill, deaden; to subdue the flesh by discipline" (12th c. CE) → Middle English mortifien "to deaden, subdue, mortify" (14th c. CE) → English mortified (past participle / adjective) "deadened; humiliated, crushed in spirit" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) SECOND ELEMENT, ISOLATED (the "make" element *dheh₁-): PIE *dheh₁- "to put, place, set, make" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE) → Latin facere "to make, do" (attested 1st c. BCE; the verb that combines with mors in mortificāre) → (Latin also shows the same root as -ficāre in countless compounds: magnificare, glorificare, aedificare) Debate / nuance note: The compound mortificāre "to make dead" is securely attested in Late Latin Christian writers (e.g. the Vulgate tradition rendering Greek nekroun "to put to death"), so no stage is speculative. The deep root *mer- for "death" is one of the best-established PIE roots, with reflexes in Sanskrit mṛta (dead), mṛtyu (death), Greek brotos (mortal) / ambrotos (immortal), Germanic *murþrą (murder) and *marōn (nightmare).
