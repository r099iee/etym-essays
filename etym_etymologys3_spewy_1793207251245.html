The story of spewy begins five thousand years ago with an unsurprising human noise: the sound of spitting. The reconstructed PIE root *(s)pyēw-, meaning to spit, spew, or vomit, is one of the great imitative roots of Indo-European, and its sp- onset with a labial glide survives with remarkable fidelity across the whole family — Latin spuere, Greek ptýein, Lithuanian spiauti, and Germanic spīwan all spit from the same mouth, as it were. Such spit-words are among the most stable in language because the bodily act never changes.

In Germanic the root became Proto-Germanic *spīwaną, a strong verb of the first class, and passed regularly into Old English spīwan, attested in glosses and leechdoms for vomiting and emitting. The vowel shows the normal Germanic developments, and the verb kept its farmyard frankness through the Middle Ages: Middle English spewen is found for drunkards spewing, for wounds emitting matter, and — in a transferred sense already pregnant with Cobbett's adjective — for springs and fountains spewing water. Fourteenth-century writers use it of whales spouting and of pipes discharging, so that by 1400 spew already covered every kind of violent emission from every kind of mouth.

The adjective itself is a late provincial minting. English has possessed the characterising suffix -y since Old English (-ig), and applies it inexhaustibly to bodily discharges — runny, weepy, oozy, bloody — so that some southern speaker's formation of spew + -y for ground that discharges water was perfectly regular, needing no literary permission. What it needed, and got, was a great writer to overhear it. William Cobbett, the farmer-journalist riding through Middlesex gathering copy for the Political Register in the 1820s, had exactly the ear for such words, and his phrase a sort of spewy gravel remains the word's passport to the dictionaries.

  • PIE antiquity: *(s)pyēw- among the spit-words — Latin spuere (source of sputum), Greek ptýein, Sanskrit ṣṭhīv- — showing the root's pan-Indo-European spew of cognates.
  • Germanic regularity: Grimm's law and Verner's law leave the sp- cluster untouched (as in spit, spew, spew's cousin spout), carrying the verb cleanly into Old English.
  • Medieval widening: from mouths to springs to chimneys — the verb learns to emit anything from anywhere, preparing the ground, literally, for the adjective.
  • Dialect capture: Halliwell, Wright's English Dialect Dictionary, and county glossaries net the word for oozing soils and weeping sores across the southern counties.
  • Cobbett's immortalising: Rural Rides (published 1830, rides of 1822–26) fixes spewy gravel as the contemptuous verdict of a practical farmer on undrained Middlesex drift.

Socio-culturally the word is a small monument to the age of agricultural improvement. Cobbett wrote when draining marsh and heath was a patriotic obsession, and a farmer's vocabulary of wetness — spewy, hungry, cold, sour — was a working taxonomy of failure. The improving landlords tiled and ditched such land out of existence, and the word followed its referent into rarity: modern drainage leaves little spewy gravel to name. The OED keeps it marked rare and dialectal, modern corpora barely register it, and every new reader meets it, as you do, glistening in Cobbett's sentence — ground that spews, in prose that never stops discharging either.
