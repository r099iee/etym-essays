• Assembling: Similar because both mean bringing people or things together in one place; differs because assembling is the neutral, general process (parts, people, meetings) with no military or roll-call implication, while mustering retains the martial edge and the idea of formal accountability.
  • Gathering: Similar because both are acts of bringing scattered items together; differs because gathering is the everyday, unforced word (gather flowers, gather round the fire) without the discipline, purpose or head-count of a muster.
  • Rallying: Similar because both involve collecting scattered forces for a common effort; differs because rallying implies re-forming after dispersal or defeat and reviving flagging spirits (rally the troops, a rallying cry), whereas mustering is the cooler administrative act of assembling in the first place.
  • Summoning: Similar because both involve calling people to attend; differs because summoning is the act of issuing the authoritative call itself (a summons), while mustering covers the whole process of bringing the group together and accounting for it.
  • Convening: Similar because both mean formal assembly; differs because convening is procedural and civilian (convening a meeting, a court, a committee) — parliamentary-bureaucratic rather than military, and with no inspection.
  • Mobilising: Similar because both mean preparing forces for action (mobilising the reserves); differs because mobilising means activating and moving military resources to readiness — logistics and deployment — whereas mustering is the head-count assembly itself, which mobilisation presupposes.
  • Marshalling: Similar because both describe the orderly arrangement of people or things for a purpose (marshalling troops, marshalling facts); differs because marshalling emphasises arranging into correct order and formation (and has separate heraldic and legal lives), while mustering emphasises counting and collecting together.
  • Congregating: Similar because both are collective assembly; differs because congregating is spontaneous and often religious or social, with no authority, inspection or purpose of readiness.
  • Collecting: Similar because both gather scattered elements; differs because collecting is the general verb (stamps, data, children from school) and entirely lacks the military or emergency frame; register neutral.
  • Levying: Similar because both can mean raising troops; differs because levying is the official, legal act of conscription or imposition (levy an army, levy a tax) — the institutional authorisation — while mustering is the physical gathering of the troops thus raised.
  • Amassing: Similar because both build up a large accumulation (amassing forces); differs because amassing focuses on the quantity accumulated (wealth, evidence, troops) without inspection discipline, while mustering is person-centric, ordered and accountability-driven.
  • Canvassing: Similar because in Australia and New Zealand mustering overlaps with canvassing the bush for cattle during a round-up; differs because canvassing elsewhere means soliciting opinions or votes door-to-door — a completely different frame of reference.
