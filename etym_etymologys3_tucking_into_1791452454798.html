Tucking into is a phrase rather than a single word, and its history is the history of two ordinary English verbs drawing together into a fixed idiom. The first element, tuck, is a Middle English verb of pulling and folding; the second, into, is one of the oldest prepositional compounds in the language. Neither is glamorous, and that is precisely the point: the idiom's flavour comes from the collision of a homely physical verb with a plain directional particle.

The Germanic ancestry of tuck. The verb is recorded in Middle English as tucken, "to pull up, gather, fold", and it has cognates in Middle Low German tucken and Middle Dutch tucken, both meaning to tug, twitch or jerk. The common Germanic form is reconstructed as *tukkōną, an expressive verb imitating a pulling motion, and an Old English form *tūcian is sometimes posited to account for the Middle English evidence, though its exact status is uncertain. Whether the Germanic word descends from the PIE root *deuk-, "to lead or draw", is debated; the semantic link is plausible, since drawing and pulling are close, but the phonological details are not secure, and some scholars prefer to treat tuck as an independent onomatopoeic formation.

From cloth to body to food. The recorded senses unfold in a clear sequence. In the fourteenth century tuck is a tailoring verb: to gather cloth into a fold, and the derived noun tuck names the fold itself. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the verb widens to any act of thrusting something into a containing space: a dagger into a belt, a letter into a pocket, a child into bed. The domestic sense of tucking someone up warmly belongs to this period too, and it gives the word its enduring tone of cosy care.

  • Physical core: pull, gather, fold, thrust inward, cover snugly.
  • Body sense: draw the limbs close, as a diver or gymnast tucks; the noun tuck names the compact position.
  • Storage sense: put away safely, hence "tucked away in the country" and "money tucked away".
  • Eating sense: put food briskly into the body, hence tuck into, tuck in and tuck away.

The rise of the eating idiom. The phrasal verb tuck into is a nineteenth-century development of British informal speech, though the related tuck in and the noun tuck for food are attested from the middle of the century. The school slang sense is particularly revealing: at British boarding schools tuck meant sweets, cakes and other contraband delicacies, kept in a tuck box and bought at the tuck shop. Here the word for stowing things away became the word for the things stowed, a neat metonymy that explains why the eating senses feel so physical and so slightly guilty.

Eating as stowing. English has many verbs that treat a meal as a job of packing: put away, pack away, stow, get stuck into, polish off, demolish. Tuck into belongs to this family, and its particular shade is hearty rather than violent. It suggests a person settling down to a large plate with obvious pleasure, not a ravenous animal. That is why it suits comic fiction so well: it is affectionate, unpretentious and faintly ridiculous when applied to a duck.

The literary register. The idiom flourishes in the twentieth-century comic novel, in cookery columns, in letters and in journalism that wants to sound friendly. Wodehouse's use of it for a duck feeding on lake weed is a small masterpiece of register: the phrase humanises the bird, gives it an appetite and a personality, and makes the narrator's bewilderment funnier by contrast. The humour depends on our knowing exactly what kind of person usually tucks into something.

The second element: into. Into is a compound of Old English in and tō, both inherited from Proto-Germanic and ultimately from PIE *en, "in". It replaced the older simple preposition in the sense of motion toward the interior, and by the Middle English period it was fully established as the ordinary marker of inward direction. Its role in the idiom is crucial: tuck alone can mean fold or cover, but tuck into is unmistakably directional, aiming the action at a target. The food is the container into which the eater tucks.

  • Phonetic history: the Old English long vowel of tō and the reduced vowel of into reflect the general weakening of unstressed syllables in Middle English.
  • Morphology: into is one of the few English prepositions formed by compounding two prepositions, and it has no exact equivalent in many languages.
  • Idiomatic productivity: the pattern verb + into yields a large family of English phrasal verbs, of which tuck into is one of the homeliest.

Modern life. The idiom remains current in British English and is understood wherever British fiction and television travel. It has acquired a nostalgic, slightly cosy aura, associated with Sunday lunches, school stories and the plain-speaking world of mid-century comedy. At the same time it remains fully alive in speech: a parent still tells a child to tuck in, a reviewer still says that the diners tucked into their food. The phrase has outlived the tuck shop, the tuck box and the world of boarding-school treats that helped to create it.
