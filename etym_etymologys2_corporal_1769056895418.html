CORPORAL has two distinct root words, and each requires its own chain. ROOT WORD 1 — the rank noun (the card's sense), meaning "head": - *kaput- "head" (Proto-Indo-European, reconstructed; Pokorny; de Vaan; source of Latin caput and Germanic *haubudą) → Latin caput, capitis "head" (classical) → Vulgar Latin *capo "head, chief" (Late Latin) → Italian capo "head, chief, leader" (from Latin caput) → Italian caporale "leader of a squad; pertaining to a head" (capo + -ale, medieval/early modern Italian) → French caporal "non-commissioned officer" (16th century, borrowed from Italian) → English corporal "the lowest non-commissioned officer, head of a small body of men" (16th century, borrowed from French; the spelling later remodelled under the influence of the body-adjective — see Root Word 2) ROOT WORD 2 — the adjective "of the body", the spelling influence: - *kwrep- / *krep- "body, form" (Proto-Indo-European, reconstructed; Pokorny; source of Latin corpus) → Latin corpus, corporis "body" (classical) → Latin corporalis "of the body" (classical) → English corporal (adjective) "of the body" (14th century) — best known today in corporal punishment → this adjective's spelling, via its association with the "body" of men a corporal commands, remodelled the spelling of the rank word in the 16th–17th centuries → final node of the spelling line: corporal Note (debate and nuance): The rank noun truly descends from *kaput- "head" (through Italian caporale and French caporal), while the adjective and the ecclesiastical noun (the linen corporal of the Mass, from corpus Christi) descend from *kwrep- "body". The two unrelated roots converge in the single modern English spelling corporal, because the head-of-a-body rank was assimilated in writing to the existing body-word adjective. Etymologists keep the two trees strictly separate, and the convergence is described as an accident of spelling rather than of meaning.
