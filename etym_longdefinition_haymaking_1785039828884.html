HAYMAKING is the compound noun denoting the seasonal agricultural operation of cutting grass or leguminous crops, letting the mown growth dry and cure in the sun, and gathering it into heaps, cocks, or bales for use as winter fodder for livestock. It is a "weather-window" industry in the fullest sense: hay must be cut when the crop is at its nutritious peak, then dried until the moisture is low enough to prevent mould and spontaneous combustion in the stack. The traditional sequence is mowing, tedding (spreading and turning the cut grass to expose it to sun and air), conditioning or crimping, raking into rows, cocking into small stacks to shed rain, and finally loading, carting, and stacking in a barn or rick. In The Hobbit, ch. iv ("Over Hill and Under Hill"), the word carries a poignant, homesick tone: while the company toils through the cold, damp passes of the Misty Mountains, Bilbo thinks that "the summer is getting on down below" and that haymaking "is going on and picnics," that "they will be harvesting and blackberrying, before we even begin to go down the other side at this rate." The single noun summons an entire vanished world of sunlit, communal, cyclical country labour — the scythe-swing, the tedding of grass, the fragrant hayfield — and sets it in deliberate contrast to the violent weather and raw peril above. Its tone is wistful and nostalgic, a small piece of the comfortable domestic world that the quest has left behind. Senses of the word (exhaustive)
  • The process (primary): cutting, drying, and gathering grass or legumes to produce hay, as a seasonal chore of the agricultural calendar.
  • The season (metonymy): "the haymaking" or "haymaking time" can name the short summer period in which this work happens — a time of long days, heat, and haste.
  • Figurative opportunity (extension): the proverb "make hay while the sun shines" (attested 16th c.) turns haymaking into the type of seizing one's moment while conditions favour; "making hay" is still used of exploiting an advantage while it lasts.
  • Mechanical extension: in modern usage haymaking may also denote the industrialised production of hay and silage — swathers, mower-conditioners, balers — a widening of the old word to new machinery.
  • Loose extended uses: haymaking can appear in informal American English for a wild, vigorous burst of activity or celebration, and the related "haymaker" (a wild swinging punch, in boxing) belongs to the same word-family image of a swinging, scything blow.
Historical bridge Because haymaking is completely weather-dependent — the proverb insists on it — the word carried from the start the idea of opportunity that must be seized, and from that the figurative sense of profiting from a favourable moment grew naturally. The compound is transparent: hay, "grass cut and dried for fodder," from the Germanic root meaning "that which is cut," the same root as hew; and making, the gerund of make, "to create, construct, bring about." Haymaking is the verbal noun of the phrase "to make hay," which is older than the compound and which gave the proverb. The word is written either as one word, haymaking, or with a hyphen, hay-making; the one-word spelling is standard today. Register and near-synonyms Haymaking is a plain, workaday, rural word, but its modern tone is noticeably nostalgic, because the kind of haymaking it describes — scythes, tedders, cocks of hay in a meadow — has largely vanished from industrial agriculture. Among related terms, mowing names only the single act of cutting grass; reaping is the sickle-harvest of cereal grains; harvesting is the generic term for gathering any matured crop at season's end; tedding and cocking name individual stages of the work; and haying (chiefly North American) is the informal colloquial synonym for the same chore. Haymaking alone denotes the full composite operation — cutting, tedding, curing, raking, cocking, and storing — and it carries the pastoral weight that made it Tolkien's chosen image of the homely Shire world the quest has left behind.
