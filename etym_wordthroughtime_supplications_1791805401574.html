- Late 14th c. — the word enters English as a religious term: humble prayer, especially public prayer for a pressing need.
- 15th–16th c. — the written petition sense flourishes in Chancery, Parliament and the universities; the word acquires a procedural as well as a devotional life.
- 16th–17th c. — the Reformation and the Book of Common Prayer embed supplications in English liturgy, while Puritan and Anglican divines use it of intercessory prayer.
- 18th c. — still current in sermons and historical narrative; in polite prose it begins to sound solemn and slightly old-fashioned.
- 19th c. — largely restricted to religious writing, scripture and historical romance; everyday language prefers plea and petition.
- 20th–21st c. — a liturgical and literary word, often in the plural; outside church contexts it signals archaism, pathos or gentle irony.
