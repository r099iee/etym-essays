GNASHED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to gnash: to strike or grind the teeth together forcefully, convulsively, and audibly, in an outburst of rage, anguish, or menace. The word names the involuntary, grating collision of the jaws — teeth knocked against teeth with a percussive, rasping sound — and it is nearly inseparable in use from fury, grief, despair, or the threat of violence. Gnashing is not a controlled action (one does not gnash one's teeth thoughtfully); it is a reflex of the body under overwhelming emotion, the mouth's own way of expressing what speech cannot. Literary context — The Hobbit. The sentence describes the Great Goblin's reaction to Orcrist, the ancient elf-blade of Gondolin: "The Great Goblin gave a truly awful howl of rage when he looked at it, and all his soldiers gnashed their teeth, clashed their shields, and stamped." The verb sits in a triple drumbeat of percussion — teeth, shields, feet — so that gnashed acquires a distinctly animalistic, almost reptilian tone: not reasoned anger but an involuntary, mechanical grinding of the jaws, a sound-mirror of hatred too raw for speech. The word signals the mob's synchronised bloodthirst and its grotesque sub-humanity, the prelude to the dwarves' torture and death. Polysemy and related senses. Although gnash is a narrow verb, it carries a rich set of uses and echoes:
  • Literal — strike or grind the teeth together (core sense): "He gnashed his teeth in fury." Usually transitive with "teeth" as object, or intransitive with "with": "to gnash with rage."
  • Figurative — extreme rage or anguish (idiom): "gnashing of teeth" is a fixed formula for hopeless fury and torment, inseparable from the biblical phrase: the damned "weep and gnash their teeth." The idiom survives in secular prose for any helpless, speechless fury.
  • Archaic and extended senses of the verb: in older English, gnash could mean to tear or bite (of animals), to snap the jaws, or to clash with a grating sound; transitive extensions to things — "gnash the oars," "gnash the waves" — appear in 19th-century poetic diction.
  • Dialectal uses: northern English dialects preserved forms of the verb for grinding, crunching, and snapping well into the modern period, keeping alive the broader Germanic family.
  • Sound symbolism and spelling: the word's gn- spelling preserves a consonant cluster that was once pronounced (/ɡn-/), now reduced to /n-/ in standard English; the silent g is a fossil of the Old English onset and a famous spelling trap.
Semantic boundaries. Grinding the teeth is slow, sustained rubbing pressure, literal or figurative, with no snapping; gnashing is audible, convulsive impact. Clashing is external — hard objects struck together — whereas gnashing is internal, the body's own dentition in motion. Gritting suggests silent, controlled endurance (one grits one's teeth under strain, suppressing complaint); gnashing is the opposite — loud, involuntary, explosive fury. Snapping is a single quick bite; gnashing is repeated and grinding. The word is nearly inseparable from rage, sorrow, or menace — its most famous habitat is the King James Bible's "weeping and gnashing of teeth," and that scriptural resonance lends the goblins' gesture the flavour of the damned.
