Encapsulate carries two closely linked modern senses. The first is literal and technical: to enclose in or as if in a capsule — to sheathe, coat, or contain something within a small closed vessel, membrane, shell, or module. Pharmacists encapsulate drugs in gelatin; engineers encapsulate circuits in resin; biologists speak of encapsulated tumours, nerves, or bacterial colonies walled off by a membrane. The second, now more frequent in general prose, is figurative: to compress and contain the essence of something in compact form — to summarize, epitomize, or embody it in miniature, as when a slogan encapsulates a campaign or one novel encapsulates an era.

Your context — the new electronic expressive space “seems to encapsulate its long history and open out a new chapter” — uses both layers at once: the new medium is said to enclose and preserve the whole written past inside itself, like a capsule holding its contents, while simultaneously distilling and expressing that history in a new compact form. That double force, containment plus summary, is the signature of the verb.

  • Literal enclosure (pharmacy, biology, engineering): to put into a capsule or protective covering; e.g. “the tablet encapsulates the powder,” “the device is encapsulated in epoxy.” The semantic logic is transparent: en- (“in, into”) + capsule + -ate (verb-forming), so to capsule-ize inward.
  • Isolation / quarantine: to wall off, as an encapsulated abscess or an encapsulated module in software; the bridge is the capsule as a boundary that protects both sides, keeping the inside pure and the outside safe.
  • Compact summary / epitome: “her speech encapsulates the argument”; the bridge is metaphor from physical miniaturization to informational compression — a summary is a verbal capsule holding a large meaning in a small space.
  • Embodiment / typification: “he encapsulates the spirit of the age”; here containment shades into representation, where the person or work does not merely summarize but contains and displays the whole, as a capsule contains its dose.
  • Computing / systems: in object-oriented programming, encapsulation is the bundling of data with methods and the hiding of internals behind an interface; the bridge is again the capsule as a sealed unit whose interior is accessed only through defined openings.

Grammar and register: encapsulate is a regular transitive verb (encapsulates, encapsulated, encapsulating, encapsulation as noun). It is somewhat formal and favoured in academic, journalistic, and technical prose; in casual speech speakers more often say sum up or capture. It takes a direct object and often a prepositional complement (“encapsulated in a single image,” “encapsulated within resin”). Avoid overuse as a mere synonym of describe: true encapsulation implies both faithful containment and telling compression. In examination prose the verb earns its place only when the example genuinely holds the whole — a capsule that, once opened in the mind, releases the full dose of the larger history it carries.
