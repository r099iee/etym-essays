The history of deigned is a history of worth — of how a language turns the assessment of value into a social gesture and finally into an accusation. The verb's deepest ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *dek-, which combined two related ideas: the physical act of reaching out to take or accept what is offered, and the cultural notion that what is received must be fitting and proper. In ancient Greece the root split productively: dékhomai meant "I receive, accept," while dóxa ("that which seems right; opinion; glory") became the fountain of such English words as dogma, orthodox, and paradox. The root's dual life — receiving and fittingness — set the stage for the whole later career of the word.

In the Italic branch, *dek- produced the Latin verb decēre, "to be fitting" (source of English decent and decorum), and the adjective dignus, "worthy, deserving." From dignus the Romans formed the verb dignārī, "to deem worthy." In Roman society the word plugged directly into the deeply felt concept of dignitās — the accumulated public standing, honour, and moral credit of a citizen, the very thing a Roman politician spent his life acquiring and guarding. To exercise dignārī was to weigh persons and acts against one's own dignity and to accept only what measured up. This Roman idea of worth-as-standing is the historical bridge that later English, through the adjective dignified and the noun dignity, would inherit.

The critical transformation happened in medieval French. Old French deigner / daignier — born of Vulgar Latin *dignāre — kept the root's meaning of judging worth, but placed it in the rigid hierarchy of feudalism. In a society ordered by degrees of noble rank, for a lord to "deign" to do something was a public, performative act: he declared the task beneath his station and yet, magnanimously, consented to perform it. The word thus carried a built-in social asymmetry — it presupposed a superior and an inferior, and it dramatized the gap between them. This is the moment the word became inherently about hierarchy, and the modern sense is already implicit in it.

English received the word with the Norman Conquest. Anglo-Norman deignen appears in Middle English around 1290, and Chaucer uses it richly in the late 14th century: in the Canterbury Tales, noble and ecclesiastical characters "deign" to speak, to eat, to descend to the company of their inferiors, and the word registers the exact temperature of their condescension. The spelling deign settled in the 15th century, preserving the French -ei-, while the pronunciation lost the palatal glide: Middle English /deiˈdʒən/ became modern /deɪn/. The cognate dainty (also from dignus) shows the older form with the sound retained through a different channel.

The semantic career since the 16th century is a steady drift from neutral to critical:

  • Early modern sense: "to think worthy or fitting" — morally neutral. Tyndale's Bible and the Book of Common Prayer use "deign" (and its old synonym vouchsafe) to translate the humility of saints and supplicants: "deign to hear our prayer."
  • Courtly and liturgical use: God, kings, and patrons "deign" to grant favours — a gracious condescension from on high, still free of irony.
  • Modern dominant sense: by the 18th–19th centuries the word had become the property of novelists and satirists. In Jane Austen's era — and in the fiction of this very batch's contexts — "deign" is virtually always ironic: a character who "deigns" to notice someone of lower rank is being mocked for his or her self-importance. The verb now does the work of a verdict: it condemns the arrogance it reports.
  • Modern grammar: the present tense has withered; the word survives chiefly in the infinitive ("deign to...") and the past ("deigned to..."), frequently negated ("would not deign to").

The sentence on this card — "He deigned to speak to the common people only when it suited his interests" — sits exactly at the modern point: a superior descending conditionally, and the verb alone exposing the condescension. It is a one-word indictment of pride, and the two thousand years that led from *dek-, the hand reaching out to accept what is offered, to the modern verb of arrogant refusal, are the biography of how a culture came to judge the gap between rank and conduct.
