BATTERY enters English in the 1530s with the sense action of battering, in law the unlawful beating of another, borrowed from French batterie, from Old French baterie beating, thrashing, assault of the twelfth century, from batre to beat, modern battre, from Latin battuere, also spelt batuere, to beat, strike, pound. In Middle English bateri had meant only forged metal ware, vessels of beaten metal, showing the hammer already at work. The Latin verb is itself anomalous: rare in Cicero and Virgil, abundant in late and Vulgar texts, evidently popular and old. Etymonline, following Walde-Hofmann and de Vaan, reports it is said to be probably borrowed from Gaulish, citing Welsh bathu to beat and Irish and Gaelic bat, bata staff, cudgel. Pokorny extends this to a PIE root *bhau- to strike, also reflected in Old English beatan to beat and beadu battle. Both the Celtic-loan and PIE stages are flagged as provisional; Latinists stress that battuere could equally be onomatopoeic intensification of batuere. The honest etymology therefore begins securely at Latin battuere and marks earlier steps as debated.

French develops the crucial polysemy. Old French baterie first means blows inflicted, then collectively the apparatus that inflicts them. By the fifteenth century artillerymen speak of batterie as bombardment, heavy blows upon walls, then by metonymy the guns themselves deployed together, a sense recorded in English from the 1550s as battery of ordnance. Parallel extensions produce batterie de cuisine set of beaten-metal cooking vessels, attested 1294 as batherie in the Dijon toll, and musical batterie drumming and strumming. English inherits the whole cluster: legal battery as beating, military battery as guns, domestic battery as pans. Pronunciation anglicises French /batʁi/ to RP /batəɺi/.

  • Franklin's analogy: In 1748 Benjamin Franklin, experimenting with Leyden jars in Philadelphia, calls a row of jars wired in series an electrical battery by analogy with a battery of cannon, both being units assembled for concentrated discharge. The metaphor sticks globally: German Batterie, Spanish bateria, Italian batteria. What began as pounding walls becomes storing charge, preserving only multiplicity plus sudden release. Car, torch and lithium batteries all descend from this witty mid-century letter, not from physics alone.
  • From cannon to cages: Nineteenth-century poultry keepers, seeking the same concentrated output, arrange laying cages in tiers and call the assembly a battery by direct artillery metaphor: hens deployed like guns to fire eggs. The Oxford English Dictionary traces battery hen and battery cage to early twentieth-century trade catalogues, spreading after the Second World War with wire, vitamins and housing grants. Ruth Harrison's Animal Machines (1964), contemporaneous with the peregrine crisis, exposes barren batteries where each bird has less than 550 square centimetres, turning a term of power into one of pity. EU Directive 1999/74/EC bans barren batteries from 2012, requiring enriched colonies, while Britain debates full cage abolition.
  • Literary sting in the falcon sentence: To write that DDT makes the magnificent hunter as helpless as a spastic battery hen weaponises every prior sense. The hunter that stooped at 200 miles per hour now cannot make shell; the bird of kings shares wire, tremor and cracked eggs with the cheapest layer; things that batter have become battered. Baseball's battery of pitcher and catcher (1867, originally pitcher alone) and the examining battery of tests show the same massing logic turned benign, sharpening by contrast the hen's misery. The sentence therefore needs the full artillery-electrical-legal resonance to land: only readers who feel batteries as powerful can feel how far the falcon has fallen.

Battery thus travels from Celtic fists to Roman hammers to French cannon to Philadelphian sparks to Yorkshire egg sheds, keeping throughout the thud of repeated blows. Its spelling never changes after 1530; its referents multiply from fists to guns to jars to hens. To blank battery before hen is to hide that entire pounding column, and to mute the era when both falcons and farm birds broke their eggs from chemistry and wire.
