CHAIN: FROM THE ANATOLIAN RIVER TO MEANDERING (5 stages)

  1. The Carian river name Maiandros (Μαίανδρος) — the wildly winding river of Caria in western Asia Minor, the modern Menderes (Turkish), whose ultimate origin is PRE-GREEK / ANATOLIAN and is not securely reconstructed in Indo-European (see the debate note).
  2. Ancient Greek maiandros (Μαίανδρος) — from the river name to an appellative: 'a winding river-course; a winding, crooked line; the ornamental Greek fret pattern' (classical Greek; the name was already proverbial for anything labyrinthine).
  3. Latin Maeander / Maeandros — 'the Carian river; a winding course, a labyrinthine path; a meander ornament' (attested classical, borrowed from Greek).
  4. Middle French méandre — 'a winding course; an ornamental fret' (16th century, borrowed from Latin/Greek) → English meander (verb 'to flow or walk in a winding course', noun 'a winding course', first recorded 1570s–1590s, from the Latin stem with the sense of the Greek common noun).
  5. English MEANDERING — the present participle and adjective of meander, 'winding, circuitous, rambling' (recorded from the 17th century; the modern figurative senses for speech and thought develop from the 19th century).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The ultimate etymology of the river name Maiandros is UNKNOWN: it is a pre-Greek, likely Anatolian place-name, and no credible Proto-Indo-European derivation has been demonstrated. The Indo-European life of the word begins only when the name becomes a common noun in Greek; the PIE root claimed by some older authorities is speculative and should not be treated as established.
  • English meander is borrowed from Latin Maeander (and reinforced through French), not directly from Greek, but the productive common-noun sense was already Greek.
  • The modern spelling meander (with ea) settled in the 17th century; earlier English forms show mæander and maeander, and the modern Turkish Menderes is the modern reflex of the same ancient river name.
