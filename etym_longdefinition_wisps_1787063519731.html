Wisps are thin, delicate, loosely bunched or trailing strands of a light substance — most often smoke, vapour, mist, steam, cloud, or hair — that drift, curl, or cling lightly rather than hang heavily. The word carries an inherent sense of fineness, airiness, and fragility: a wisp is barely there, easily dispersed, and insubstantial by nature. It names something that exists at the very edge of perception, seen for a moment and then gone.

In the source context — “__________ of vapour floated up and past him and he began to sweat” — the plural wisps describes slender, curling trails of vapour rising off the hot, sweating body. The choice of the plural is significant: the vapour does not lift as a single solid cloud but as several delicate, separate curls that rise, waver, and drift past — exactly the effect a wisp, and not a cloud or column, captures.

Primary senses in present-day English

  • Thin trail of a vapour: a wisp of smoke from a dying candle, a wisp of steam over a kettle, wisps of cloud across the moon, wisps of fog over a field. This is the dominant modern sense. It emphasises faintness, transience, and a curling, almost living motion.
  • Small bundle of hay, straw, or grass: the original historical sense (c. 1300). A wisp of hay served as a handful for a horse, as material for thatching or wiping, as tinder, or as a makeshift torch. Now largely confined to rural, dialectal, or historical use, but still recorded.
  • A thin strand or lock of hair: wisps of hair escaping from a bun or cap. Emphasises lightness and unruliness, usually with a soft or affectionate tone.
  • A trace or sliver of the abstract: a wisp of a smile, a wisp of hope, a wisp of regret, a wisp of a rumour. The noun extends metaphorically to any faint, barely perceptible hint of an emotion, thought, or sign.
  • A slender, delicate person: “a wisp of a girl” — a person (often a child or a small, fine-boned woman) described as light, fragile, and nearly weightless, as if made of vapour.

Secondary, figurative, and specialist senses

  • Will-o'-the-wisp: from wisp in the old sense of a handful of straw used as a torch; the flickering marsh-light (Latin ignis fatuus) was imagined as a mischievous spirit luring travellers into the bog. Metaphorically it means anything elusive, deluding, or impossible to grasp — a false hope or phantom goal. The compound is first attested in the 16th century.
  • A small besom or brush: dialectally, a wisp is a bundle of twigs or straw bound for sweeping or for clearing an oven — a direct extension of the “bundle” sense.
  • A faint flicker of light or sound: by analogy with vapour, a wisp may describe a thin wavering beam of light or a thread of sound that is barely audible.
  • Meteorology and physics — wispy: the derived adjective wispy (mid-19th c.) describes thin, scattered, feathery cirrus cloud or any delicate, loosely gathered structure, and is standard in descriptions of cloud form and of thin hair or fabric.
  • The verb wisp: attested from the 19th century, meaning to emit or move in wisps, or to make into a wisp — e.g. smoke wisps from the chimney. It is rare and mostly literary.

Semantic boundaries and register

Wisps occupy the borderland between the concrete and the poetic. A strand has structure and tension; a wisp has neither. A streak has a defined edge and often a colour; a wisp has neither. A shred suggests a torn fragment of a solid, often with violence or neglect; a wisp carries no damage — only lightness. A waft is the motion, a wisp the object. Wisps are by nature evanescent, delicate, and often beautiful; in modern use the word is chosen precisely for its atmospheric, ghostly, tender, or nostalgic colouring. The plural wisps multiplies this effect across several faint, curling trails, as in the source sentence.

Etymology in brief

Wisp enters English c. 1300 from Old Norse visp (“a handful of straw”), with the plural wisps following the regular English plural. The deeper origin is uncertain and probably pre-Germanic contact; some scholars connect the root to words for “twist” or “bend”.
