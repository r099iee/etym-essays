claret: the deep red, ruby colour of claret wine — or, as in this passage, a brilliant beam of that hue among the jewel-colours sweeping over the observer. Claret is the traditional English name for the red wines of Bordeaux, and the colour-word carries with it the wine's associations: clarity, richness, a deep luminous red shot through with light.

topaz: a yellowish-gold or golden-brown colour, the colour of the topaz gemstone — a clear, brilliant yellow warmed toward orange, as if sunlight were trapped in the stone.

Sense 1 — CLARET: primary and secondary senses

  • The wine: the red wine of Bordeaux, the traditional English word for the great clarets — the primary, original sense since the 14th century.
  • The colour: the deep translucent ruby of that wine — a colour-sense that developed in the 18th-19th centuries and is now as established as the wine-sense.
  • Etymological paradox: claret means "clear" (from Old French claret, "clear little wine"); the colour claret is therefore a red the light passes through rather than one that absorbs all light — at once the deepest red and the most limpid.

Sense 2 — TOPAZ: primary and secondary senses

  • The gem: a translucent yellow to golden-brown crystalline gemstone, prized since antiquity — the original sense.
  • The colour: the clear golden yellow of that gem, warmed toward orange — the colour-sense that followed the scientific fixing of the stone in the 18th-19th centuries.
  • Figurative glow: a topaz light, a topaz sky — golden sunlight made solid, a jewel-hue of light and treasure.

Historical bridges

Claret descends from Latin clarus "clear, bright" through Old French claret "the clear little wine," so the word's own meaning of "clear" is built into its name — the colour claret is the clear red, the deep luminous red that light travels through. Topaz descends from Greek topazos, the ancient name of the golden gem, and the colour-word follows the gem: a clear, brilliant yellow warmed toward orange, as if sunlight were trapped in the stone. In the card's context the two words work as jewel-hues: "beams of claret, topaz, rich violet, radiant green" — the wine-red and the golden-yellow among the refracted rainbow of the enchanted sky.

Neighbours and boundaries

  • crimson: a cool, deep red with a hint of blue, from the kermes insect dye; claret is warmer, deeper, more translucent — a red with light in it.
  • scarlet: a bright, fiery red tending to orange, traditionally wool dyed with kermes; claret is darker, richer, and wine-derived.
  • ruby: the red of the gemstone, brilliant and hard; claret is the red of the wine, liquid and luminous.
  • vermilion: a vivid, opaque red; claret is deeper and translucent.
  • maroon: a brownish dark red, darker and flatter than claret; claret keeps its wine-clearness.
  • burgundy: the wine-red of Burgundy, cooler and sometimes paler than claret; the two are rival wine-reds.
  • amber: a golden yellow-brown like fossil resin; topaz is clearer and more brilliant, a yellow warmed toward orange.
  • gold: metallic yellow; topaz is the gem's clear golden tone rather than the metal's sheen.
  • honey: warm golden yellow with a soft brown undertone; topaz is brighter, clearer, more jewel-like.
  • saffron: a strong orange-yellow from the crocus; topaz is gentler, more translucent, a golden-yellow warmed toward orange.
  • tawny: a warm brownish-orange, darker and duller; topaz is clear and brilliant.
  • ochre: an earthy yellow, opaque and dull; topaz is a luminous crystalline yellow.

Modern and technical usage

In modern English claret remains the wine-word par excellence (the clarets of Bordeaux) and, in the palette, a deep translucent red used of cloth, glass, and the sky. Topaz is the gem-word and the colour-word: a clear golden yellow, beloved of poetry and fashion for its rarity and polish. In the card's context both are jewel-hues among the refracted rainbow — "beams of claret, topaz, rich violet, radiant green" — wine-red and golden-yellow sweeping over the watcher from a sky of vast ripples and cross-ripples.
