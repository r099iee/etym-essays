Bilge is primarily a nautical noun meaning the lowest interior part of a ship’s or boat’s hull — the rounded bottom inside, on either side of the keel, where seawater, oil, and filth collect — and by extension the foul water itself (bilge water). In the card, the steward is hurled into the boat’s black-ribbed bilge: the dark curved belly of the open boat with its ribs showing, the wettest, dirtiest, most undignified place to land. The image is visceral and precise: wood, water, bruises, stillness.

Core sense — ship’s belly: Every hull curves down to the keel; the bilge (often plural bilges) is that curve seen from inside, the sump where leaks, condensation, engine drips, and wash all drain. Small boats show open ribs there; large ships hide it under floor plates (bilge covers) with pumps beneath. Key compounds all serve this sump: bilge water (the slop itself), bilge pump (to expel it), bilge keel (external fins that reduce rolling, named for their position at the turn of the bilge), bilge alarm (warns of flooding). A sound bilge is dry; a neglected one stinks of diesel and rot. Sailors speak of sweet versus foul bilges as a badge of seamanship.

  • Bilge water and its stench: stagnant, oily, foul-smelling liquid proverbial for filth. Example: the bilge reeked after a week at sea. The bridge from place to contents is everyday metonymy — the container names what it contains.
  • Bilged and bilging (verb): to bilge or be bilged is to spring a leak or stave in at the bilge (a rock bilged her; she bilged on the reef). Technical, mostly in accident reports and classic adventure prose.
  • Boat anatomy in the card: black-ribbed bilge pictures a clinker- or carvel-built launch whose frames show dark with tar and bilgewater. Landing there means sprawling in the slop at the crew’s feet — maximum humiliation with minimum comfort.

Secondary and figurative senses:

  • Bilge as nonsense (slang, chiefly British): worthless, stupid, or hypocritical talk — “Don’t talk bilge,” “newspaper bilge,” “what a load of bilge.” Short for bilge water: dirty, useless, foul fluid becomes a metaphor for dirty, useless, foul speech. Attested from the 1920s naval slang, now mainstream informal. The bridge is sensory disgust transferred to intellect.
  • Bilge as belly or bulge (archaic/technical): the widest part of a barrel or cask is its bilge (or bulge); a bilge hoop binds it. This preserves the oldest sense of swelling curve, shared with bulge and belly.
  • Bilge keel, bilge blocks, bilge boards: naval-architecture terms where bilge simply locates: at the turn of the hull. No filth implied — pure geometry.
  • Figurative sinking: “the project sprang a leak and bilged” — rare, jocular extension of the verb to any foundering enterprise.

Register and collocation: Nautical-technical in its literal sense (manuals, surveys, sea stories), informal-contemptuous in its slang sense. Typical collocates: bilge water, bilge pump, bilge keel, bilge alarm, stinking bilge, pump the bilge, talk bilge. Grammar: countable, often plural (bilges); uncountable in slang (a lot of bilge). To call speech bilge is blunter than nonsense and earthier than rubbish — it smells.
