- [Heraldic and dyeing sense] - Entering English in the 14th and 15th centuries, 'tincture' first meant a dyeing or colouring - and in heraldry, the metals, colours and furs of a coat of arms, a technical sense still alive today.
- [Medicinal sense] - From the 17th century it named a solution in which a substance is dissolved in alcohol - the apothecary's tincture - concrete and practical.
- [Figurative trace] - Alongside these, from the 17th and 18th centuries, it came to mean a slight admixture or faint infusion of some quality - a 'tincture' of learning or of Roman arts - an elegant, bookish metaphor of staining.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today it survives mainly in the pharmacy (tincture of iodine) and as a refined literary word for a subtle trace or flavour, its painterly colouring metaphor intact.
