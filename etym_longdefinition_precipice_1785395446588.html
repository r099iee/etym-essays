PRECIPICE is a very steep rock face or cliff, especially one of great height, falling away almost sheer - any place where the ground breaks off abruptly into a drop. The Latin ancestry of the word, from praeceps, 'headlong, head-first' (prae 'before' + caput 'head'), is the key to its tone: a precipice is not merely a cliff but a cliff conceived from the standpoint of falling. In The Hobbit the eagles leave the travellers on a spur of the mountain from which there is no path down except by flying, and no path down off it except by jumping over a precipice: the word makes the drop the entire geography of the place - no path down, no path off, only the fall itself. Its register in the passage is grave and sublime, the vocabulary of epic heights and abysses, and Tolkien deploys it at the moment the adventure becomes vertiginous and inescapable. The two clauses are perfectly balanced - 'no path down on to it save by flying; and no path down off it except by jumping over a precipice' - and the repeated no path makes the precipice the single, inescapable fact of the spur, the wall that the whole company must leap or die. Since the sixteenth century the word has also lived metaphorically: to stand 'on the precipice of' ruin, war, or a fateful decision is to be balanced on the brink of catastrophe, and both senses - the literal cliff and the brink of disaster - are alive in the one sentence. CORE SENSES AND POLYSEMY
  • Primary sense (geographical): a very steep or perpendicular cliff or rock face, especially one of great height, falling away almost sheer - the precipices of the Alps; a castle built on the edge of a precipice.
  • Figurative / brink sense: a situation poised on the edge of catastrophe or of a decisive change - on the precipice of war, of ruin, of bankruptcy, of a great discovery; the word names the point just before a fall or a leap.
  • Sense of peril: a precipice is dangerous ground itself - the place where one would fall headlong - so the word carries a permanent warning, unlike the neutral cliff.
  • By-forms and relatives: precipitous 'resembling a precipice, very steep'; precipitately 'headlong, hastily'; precipitate 'to throw headlong; to act hastily'; precipitation 'a headlong fall; rain or snow falling'; all of these keep the 'headlong' idea inside them.
  • Homonym guard: precipice must not be confused with precinct (from Latin praecinctum 'enclosure'), principle, or principal - all different Latin roots.
  • Register: formal and literary; ordinary speech prefers cliff or drop, while precipice is reserved for writing that wants gravity and scale.
POLYSEMY - THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE FROM FALLING TO FOREBODING The whole life of the word turns on its Latin formation. Latin praeceps literally means 'head before' or 'head-first': it describes a person or thing that falls with the head foremost, and from that physical description it developed its moral senses - headlong, rash, hasty, violent - because a person who rushes 'head-first' into action is acting precipitately. The noun praecipitium, 'a steep place', is simply the place that makes one fall head-first: the ground where the head goes before the feet. That is why a precipice is never just a cliff. A cliff is a fact of geology, neutral and descriptive; a precipice is a cliff from the point of view of the fall. The word imports the faller into the landscape. From this core the figurative sense grew naturally: to be 'on the precipice of' something is to be already leaning forward, already head-first over the edge, so that only one direction of motion remains. The metaphor is so compact that it needs no explanation - a situation on the brink of disaster is felt as a body about to fall. DISTINCTIONS AND REGISTER Cliff is the neutral, general word for any steep rock face, whether twenty feet or two thousand; precipice insists on great height and on the danger of the fall. Crag suggests a rugged, broken projection of rock rather than a sheer drop; escarpment is the geological term for a long, steep slope or cliff formed by faulting or erosion; bluff is a broad, steep bank or headland, usually of less dizzying height; abyss and chasm name the depths below the edge rather than the edge itself. Precipice alone among these words carries the full sense of headlong danger, and it is the word of epic and sublime writing, of mountain literature and of the language of crisis. In Tolkien's sentence it is chosen precisely because it makes the geography menacing: the spur has no way down and no way off except a headlong fall, and the word concentrates the whole vertigo of the moment in a single noun.
