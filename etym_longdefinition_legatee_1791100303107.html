A legatee is a person (or institution) to whom money or property is left in a will; a beneficiary of a legacy. In the strict legal sense it denotes anyone entitled under a testator's will to receive a specific gift — a sum of money, an object, or a share of the estate — as opposed to an heir who inherits by blood or intestacy, or an executor who administers the estate.

Dickens's phrase "sole residuary legatee" refines this further. A residuary legatee is the person who receives the residue: everything left over after debts, funeral costs, taxes, and specific legacies have been paid. To be sole residuary legatee is to sweep up the remainder entire. In the novella, Scrooge is Marley's everything-man — executor, administrator, assign, legatee, friend, mourner — a bleak joke about a friendship consisting purely of paperwork and profit.

  • Core legal sense: One named in a valid will to take a legacy. Example: "The legatees under the will each received £10,000." Lawyers subdivide this into specific legatee (a particular item, e.g. "my watch"), general / pecuniary legatee (a sum of money payable from general assets), demonstrative legatee (money payable from a named fund), and residuary legatee (the remainder). A legatee must survive the testator and the legacy must not have been revoked or adeemed.
  • Distinction from heir, devisee, beneficiary: An heir inherits by intestacy or by heirship at law; a devisee (in traditional English usage) takes real property (land) by will while a legatee takes personal property, though modern statutes often merge them; a beneficiary is the broader modern term covering wills, trusts, insurance, and pensions. Legatee is thus narrower and more formal, tied to wills.
  • Residuary legatee in practice: The residuary legatee bears both upside and risk: if the estate is rich after debts, the residue is large; if debts consume it, the residue may be nothing. Draftsmen often name a spouse or child as residuary legatee to catch everything forgotten elsewhere. Dickens uses it to show Marley left no loose ends — and no other friends.
  • Corporate and charitable legatees: A legatee need not be human. Charities, colleges, and companies are routinely legatees. "The British Museum was residuary legatee" is perfectly normal legal English.
  • Archaic / literary extension: By metaphor, a legatee can be an inheritor of non-material things: "legatee of the Romantic tradition," "literary legatee." This is journalistic rather than legal, borrowing probate solemnity for cultural succession.

Register and connotation are formal, bureaucratic, and slightly Victorian. In everyday speech people say "he left me money in his will" rather than "I am his legatee." The word appears in probate notices, wills, law reports, and 19th-century novels, carrying an aura of mahogany desks, sealing wax, and mourning black. It is neutral — it implies neither affection nor greed — though Dickens colours it with miserliness by context.

Grammatically, legatee is a countable noun, plural legatees, pronounced with final stress. It takes of, under, or named in: "legatee of the late Mr Marley," "legatee under the will." Adjectival use is rare except in compounds like "legatee status." Do not confuse with legate (an envoy) or legator (obsolete for testator).
