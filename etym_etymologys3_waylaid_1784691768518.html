The biography of waylaid begins far from the trolls' wood, in two ancient Indo-European roots that grew side by side in the Germanic dialects and were fused together on the roads of medieval Europe. The first is the PIE root *weǵʰ-, 'to carry, transport, travel', which produced the Proto-Germanic noun *wegaz, 'a way, road, path'. In Old English this became weg, the everyday word for a road or a journey and the direct ancestor of Modern English way; cognates run right across the family, from Old Norse vegr and Gothic wigs to Latin vehere 'to carry' and Sanskrit váhati 'he conveys'. The second root is PIE *legh-, 'to lie down', whose Germanic descendant *ligjanan 'to lie' developed a causative, *lagjanan, 'to lay, to cause to lie', seen in Old English lecgan, Old Norse leggja, and Old High German legen; beyond Germanic the same root appears in Greek lékhos 'bed' and Latin lectus 'bed'. For centuries the two strands ran in parallel: the way itself, and the act of laying something down beside it.

The medieval compound and its formation. The welding together happened in the trading towns of the Low Countries and northern Germany in the age of the great pilgrim and merchant roads. Middle Dutch wegelagen and Middle Low German wegelagen — built from wech or weg, 'way', and lagen, 'to lie in wait, to watch for' — meant 'to lie in wait beside a road for travellers'. This was the era of the robber bands that haunted the highways of Europe, and the word named their trade with sinister precision. English, in the late fourteenth century, made its own form, wey-leyn, later waylay: to lie in wait for someone upon the way. Whether English calqued the continental word or formed it independently, the two are plainly the same creation, born of the same social reality — the dangerous road and the man hidden beside it. The past tense waylaid formed itself on the pattern of lay → laid, the old strong verb whose forms run from Old English legde through Middle English leide to modern laid; the verb thus inflects like a native English verb while carrying a compound of two native roots.

Sound changes and the passage of time. The vowels of the compound show the ordinary history of English: Old English weg became Middle English wey and then Modern English way with the Great Vowel Shift reshaping the vowel, while the second element passed through the same development as lay. By the early modern period the form waylay was settled, and the meaning was settled too: to waylay was to lie in wait on the road to stop, rob, or attack a traveller. The word belongs, in its first centuries, entirely to the world of highway robbery and its literary glamour — the highwayman, the ambush, the sudden seizure on the king's highway.

Semantic drift and literary life. As highway robbery declined in the nineteenth century, the word widened: it came to mean any unexpected and importunate stopping of a person on their way, and the menace drained out of it. To be waylaid by a creditor, a journalist, or an eager acquaintance is to be intercepted without a crime. Yet the word never quite lost its cunning. It remains the verb of the ambush, and writers reach for it precisely when they want surprise and secrecy: in The Hobbit, the trolls 'waylaid strangers' because they lay hidden and waited — the word tells us everything about the method and the menace of the attack. The history of waylaid is thus the history of a medieval crime-word that became a metaphor for every interruption of our journey, while retaining, under its modern politeness, the shadow of the highwayman in the trees.
