Lucca in the famous opening sentence of Leo Tolstoy's War and Peace (1869)—uttered by Anna Pavlovna Scherer to Prince Vasily Kuragin at her St. Petersburg salon in July 1805: 'Well, Prince, Genoa and Lucca are now no more than private estates of the Bonaparte family'—denotes the historic, fiercely independent Tuscan republic of Lucca. In June 1805, Napoleon Bonaparte transformed the centuries-old Republic of Lucca into the Principality of Lucca and Piombino, placing it under the rule of his ambitious younger sister, Elisa Bonaparte Baciocchi, and her husband Félix Baciocchi. To Russian and European aristocrats, this blatant dynastic privatization of an ancient sovereign commonwealth symbolized Napoleon's unchecked imperial tyranny and disregard for established European law.

In geography and history, Lucca is the capital city of the province of Lucca in Tuscany, central Italy, situated on the alluvial plain of the Serchio River near the Tyrrhenian Sea. Celebrated worldwide for its intact, monumental Renaissance city walls (now a scenic tree-lined pedestrian promenade), its Roman amphitheatre-turned-piazza (Piazza dell'Anfiteatro), its Romanesque churches (such as the Cathedral of San Martino and San Michele in Foro), and as the birthplace of opera composer Giacomo Puccini, Lucca holds a unique place in Italian history as one of the few city-states that maintained its republican independence virtually uninterrupted for over five hundred years (from 1160 until 1799/1805).

  • Historical Sense: The Republic of Lucca (Serenissima Repubblica Lucquese): The independent merchant commonwealth that preserved its sovereignty from the 12th to the late 18th century against the aggressive expansionism of powerful neighbours like Florence and Pisa (e.g., Lucca's motto 'Libertas' was emblazoned on its banners and city gates; the Republic of Lucca grew fabulously wealthy through its European monopoly on luxury silk weaving). Lucchese merchants and silk-weavers established commercial enclaves across London, Paris, Lyon, and Antwerp, funding their state's neutrality through diplomacy and impenetrable fortifications.
  • Napoleonic and 19th-Century Principality: The Principality of Lucca and Piombino: The Napoleonic puppet principality created by Napoleon in 1805 for his sister Elisa Bonaparte (the specific historical context of War and Peace). Under Elisa's enlightened despotic rule, the city underwent major neoclassical urban renovations (such as the creation of Piazza Napoleone). After Napoleon's fall, the Congress of Vienna (1815) established the Duchy of Lucca under the Spanish House of Bourbon-Parma until it was annexed to the Grand Duchy of Tuscany in 1847 and subsequently joined unified Italy in 1860.
  • Agricultural and Gastronomic Renown Sense: Globally renowned for producing some of Italy's finest extra virgin olive oils (Olio di Lucca), renowned for its light, fruity elegance, as well as Lucchese wines and culinary traditions (such as buccellato).
  • Cultural and Artistic Heritage Sense: Celebrated as the birthplace of great musical masters, most notably Giacomo Puccini (composer of La Bohème, Tosca, and Madama Butterfly) and Luigi Boccherini, and home to world-renowned cultural events like the Lucca Comics & Games festival.

Stylistically, Lucca evokes antique Tuscan elegance, staunch republican liberty (Libertas), and architectural preservation. In literary and historical texts, its pairing with Genoa in Tolstoy's opening lines establishes the dramatic backdrop of the Napoleonic Wars.
