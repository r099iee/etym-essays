Sanskrit / Dravidian or Semitic Root (ancient substrate)
*maru- / *marwa-
"fragrant plant, sweet marjoram"
Note: Sanskrit maru (मरु) denoted marjoram or sweet-scented herb. Arabic marw / marjamiyya and Hebrew marror share Afroasiatic roots denoting bitter or aromatic herbs.
↓
Ancient Greek (c. 5th–4th c. BCE)
σάμψυχον (sámpsykhon) / ἀμάρακον (amárakon)
"marjoram, sweet aromatic herb"
Note: Borrowed into Greek from an eastern Mediterranean/Near Eastern language; Latin inherited both sampsūcum and amāracus.
↓
Medieval Latin (c. 10th–12th c.)
majorana / madiurana / marjorana
"sweet marjoram herb"
Note: Folk-etymologically reshaped through association with Latin maior ('greater') or altered from amāracus with intrusive nasal /r/.
↓
Old French (c. 12th–13th c.)
marjorane / majorane
"sweet marjoram herb"
↓
Middle English (c. 1350–1400)
marjoram / margerum / majerone
"fragrant culinary and medicinal herb"
↓
Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)
marjoram / sweet marjoram
"aromatic garden herb used in cooking, medicine, and strewing"
↓
