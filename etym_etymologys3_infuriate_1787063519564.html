The etymological roots of infuriate lead back to the ancient Proto-Indo-European verbal root *dʰwes- or *dʰus-, which carried the primary sense of breathing heavily, panting, blowing in a gust, or being possessed by a divine or demonic vapor. In early Indo-European spiritual thought, violent emotional states—such as frenzy, madness, and battle-rage—were conceptualized as divine winds or vaporous spirits entering a person's body and possessing their breath (compare Greek thyō 'to rage, seethe', Old English dwæs 'foolish, dull', and Lithuanian dūsti 'to choke, gasp').

The evolutionary trajectory through Latin mythology, humanistic Renaissance scholarship, and modern English literature includes several decisive milestones:

  • Classical Latin 'Furere' and the Roman Furies: In Proto-Italic and Classical Latin, the root produced the basic intransitive verb furere, meaning to rage, rave, be wild with frenzy, or act in a state of madness. From furere, Latin derived the abstract noun furia ('rage, violent frenzy, furious passion'). In Roman religious mythology, the Furiae (the Furies, corresponding to the Greek Erinyes—Alecto, Megaera, and Tisiphone) were dread subterranean goddesses of vengeance with snakes entwined in their hair, sent from the underworld to torment mortals who committed heinous moral crimes, driving them to incurable madness.
  • Late Latin Prefixation and Compound Formation: In Late Latin and early Renaissance Neo-Latin, scholars formed the compound factitive verb infuriāre by attaching the prepositional prefix in- ('into, upon') to the nominal stem furia-, followed by the first-conjugation verbal suffix -āre. The literal meaning was 'to bring rage into' or 'to deliver someone into the power of the Furies'. From its past participle infuriātus, Italian adopted infuriare and French adopted infurier.
  • Adoption into English Epic Literature (17th Century): The word entered English in the seventeenth century, initially appearing as a participial adjective (infuriate, meaning 'enraged' or 'maddened'). John Milton made magnificent use of this participial form in his epic poem Paradise Lost (1667, Book VI), describing the cosmic battle in heaven: '...and the madded Ramme / Raught out the giant Angels, through the pure / Seraphick Flames, infuriate with Rage, / Pursu'd them to the bottomless Abysse.'
  • Transition to a Conjugated Transitive Verb (18th–19th Century): During the eighteenth century, following the productive pattern of English Latinate verbs ending in -ate (such as activate, frustrate, and exasperate), infuriate transitioned into a standard transitive verb ('to infuriate someone'). Novelists and essayists in the Georgian and Victorian eras, including Tobias Smollett, Walter Scott, and Charles Dickens, employed it to depict intense character conflicts and dramatic confrontations.
  • Literary and Tactical Usage in 20th-Century Classics: In modern classic fantasy, J.R.R. Tolkien employed infuriate in The Hobbit ('...dancing among the trees he began to sing a song to infuriate them and bring them all after him...'), showcasing Bilbo Baggins using psychological warfare and insulting songs (calling the spiders 'Attercop' and 'Lazy Lob') to provoke the monstrous arachnids into blinding rage, blinding their judgment and saving the dwarves.

Today, infuriate continues to serve as an evocative, precise verb for describing the deliberate or accidental ignition of intense, explosive anger across all levels of human and narrative conflict.
