• skinflint: Similar because both denote a person who begrudges expenditure; differs because a skinflint drives hard bargains in transactions and may live comfortably, merely disliking parting with cash, whereas a miser hoards and voluntarily deprives himself, dwelling in squalor amid plenty.
  • penny-pincher: Similar because both describe extreme reluctance to spend; differs because penny-pincher suggests small-scale meanness in daily purchases, a careful scrutiny of every penny, rather than the all-consuming, ritualized hoarding of wealth that defines a miser.
  • tightwad: Similar because both are colloquial words for someone unwilling to spend; differs because tightwad is a jokey Americanism stressing grudging small payments, whereas miser is the serious, moralistic, literary word for obsessive hoarding.
  • cheapskate: Similar because both name people who dislike spending; differs because cheapskate is a slang word for someone who shirks fair payment and is contemptible in small ways, whereas miser implies a grander, darker devotion to wealth itself.
  • frugal (person): Similar because both involve economical use of resources; differs because frugal is a virtuous, deliberate thrift aimed at sensible living, whereas miserliness is a compulsive, self-punishing love of money that drains the pleasure from life — frugality is a choice, miserliness a pathology.
  • parsimonious (person): Similar because both describe restrained spending; differs because parsimonious is a formal, neutral-to-mildly-critical word for sparingness, often with an intellectual flavour (a parsimonious explanation), whereas miser is an emotive, moralistic noun for the hoarder.
  • hoarder: Similar because both accumulate things compulsively; differs because a hoarder accumulates objects, clutter, and junk often without regard to value, whereas a miser hoards money or precious things with a single-minded devotion to wealth and deploys nothing.
  • usurer: Similar because both are figures connected with money; differs because a usurer actively puts money to work at interest, lending it out for profit, whereas a miser locks his wealth away and refuses even to spend it, let alone lend it.
  • money-grubber: Similar because both describe someone obsessed with money; differs because a money-grubber is an aggressive accumulator out to get more through any means, whereas a miser is defined by refusal to part with what he has and by self-imposed poverty.
  • Scrooge: Similar because both name the archetype of greedy miserliness; differs because Scrooge is a proper noun from Dickens that implies a specific cold-hearted, Christmas-hating curmudgeon, whereas miser is the generic word; the miser need not be mean to others, only to himself.
  • niggardly (person): Similar because both involve grudging unwillingness to give or spend; differs because niggardly is an adjective describing a grudging, stingy manner in giving, whereas miser is a noun for the systematic hoarder living in self-denial.
  • miserable (false friend): Similar because both descend from the same Latin root and share the "wretched" heritage; differs because miserable means "wretched, unhappy, pitiable" — the state of the sufferer — whereas miser names the grasping hoarder, and the two words' meanings have diverged across the centuries.
