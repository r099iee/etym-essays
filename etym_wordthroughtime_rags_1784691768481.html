- 14th century - "rags" came into English from Old Norse "rögg", a shaggy tuft of coarse wool, carried into northern England by the Danelaw settlers; by the early 14th century Middle English "ragge" meant a torn strip of cloth, the sense of a rough, tufted texture passing into that of a frayed, torn remnant.
- 16th-19th century - through the plural "rags" the word came to mean ragged clothing and so poverty itself: "in rags" has been the phrase of destitution since the 16th century, and "from rags to riches" completed the arc in the 19th.
- Modern English - "rags" still carries both the poverty and the texture — tattered cloth, rough edges, threadbare wispiness — and by metaphor any torn, shred-like remnant, from scraps of paper to the flying, wind-torn strips of cloud in a night sky.
