- [Late Middle English] - Adopted from Latin larix via German Lärche and French, naming the European larch; the sense is stable from the start — a tall, conical, deciduous conifer of the Alps and northern forests.
- [16th-17th c. natural-history sense] - Fixed as the conifer that sheds its needles; naturalists single the larch out precisely because it breaks the 'evergreen' rule, giving the word a note of curious, paradoxical distinction.
- [18th c. British plantations] - The word enters the landscape of British highland estates as the larch is widely planted for timber; the sense is unchanged, but the connotation gains hardiness, rot-resistant timber, and the wild northern edge of cultivation.
- [1753 Linnaean fix] - Larix becomes the botanical genus name; alongside its everyday use the word acquires a technical, scientific register.
- [Modern register] - Neutral and bookish; woodsmanship and forestry use it plainly, while literature makes it stand for alpine wildness, autumn gold, and resilient high-altitude beauty — the exact naming of a knowledgeable guide.
