corporal: (noun) A non-commissioned military officer, ranking below sergeant and above private, who leads a small squad or body of soldiers; the lowest rank of non-commissioned officer in most armies. The same spelling serves an unrelated adjective: corporal (adjective), "of or relating to the body" (as in corporal punishment). The two are different words that have collided in spelling (see the derivation chain). In the context passage. In "What about tying a stone to his feet, Corporal?" (C. S. Lewis, "Prince Caspian"), the word is used as a vocative, a title of address, and its literary effect is institutional and chilling. The speaker does not name his companion; he addresses him by rank, and that single word converts a proposal to murder a bound prisoner — drowning a Dwarf with a stone tied to his feet — into a matter of military routine. The nuance is cold, bureaucratic, hierarchical: command structure and orders have drained the scene of humanity, and the rank itself does the dehumanising work. The tone is flat and matter-of-fact; the sinister quality lies precisely in how ordinary the address sounds, as if discussing the disposal of cargo rather than a person. The word also signals scale — these are not great lords but low-ranking soldiers — which makes their casual cruelty both more chilling and more believable. Polysemy and modern senses. The word bears several distinct meanings:
  • The military rank (the card's sense): the lowest non-commissioned officer, commanding a squad or section; in many forces divided into corporal and lance corporal (a provisional or acting rank below full corporal). Different armed services fix the rank at different levels — in the US Army it is the E-4 grade, in the British Army and Royal Marines the junior NCO, and so on — but the common core is the first rank of command above the private soldier.
  • The adjective "of the body": from Latin corporalis, meaning "pertaining to the body" — corporal punishment (physical punishment), corporal works of mercy (in Christian teaching, bodily acts of charity: feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, etc.), corporal being (bodily existence). This adjective is a different word from the rank noun but shares its spelling.
  • The ecclesiastical noun: a "corporal" is the square linen cloth on which the consecrated Host and chalice are placed during the Mass, so called because it holds the Body of Christ (Latin corpus Christi) — an entirely different technical word, again the same spelling.
  • The family of corpus words: the rank shares its spelling-family with corps, corpse, corporal (adjective), corporate, corporation, corporeal — all from Latin corpus "body". The military noun's true ancestor is "head" (Latin caput), making corporal one of the great accidental homographs of English: a rank that means "head" but is spelled as if it meant "body".
Collocations and register. The rank collocates with promoted to, lance corporal, corporal of the guard (a supervisory duty in some forces), corporal's stripes or chevrons; the adjective with punishment and works of mercy. The register of the rank word is institutional, flat, and administrative; the adjective is formal, legal, and ecclesiastical. Synonym boundaries. The ranks around corporal are strictly delimited:
  • sergeant: a higher non-commissioned rank commanding a section and enforcing discipline; a corporal commands a smaller squad and is the lowest NCO.
  • private: the base rank with no command authority at all; the corporal is the first step above it — the first man with a little authority over others.
  • lance corporal: an acting or provisional step below full corporal, carrying a limited and often temporary authority.
  • lieutenant: a commissioned officer holding authority by commission and standing apart from the ranks; a corporal is promoted from the ranks and remains close to the men.
  • petty officer: the naval equivalent of a non-commissioned rank, in a different service culture and tradition.
  • corporal (adjective) "of the body": the homograph, related by spelling only, not by meaning or origin.
