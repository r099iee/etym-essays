- [c. 1500–1000 BCE Vedic Era] — formed in Vedic Sanskrit as an adjectival derivation from saras ("lake, marsh"), denoting lacustrine creatures and plants belonging to sacred water bodies — [perception: perceived as part of the pure, sacred aquatic topography of Vedic rituals]
- [c. 800–500 BCE Epic Era (Rāmāyaṇa)] — immortalized at the opening of Sage Vālmīki's *Rāmāyaṇa*, where the grief of a female crane mourning her slaughtered mate inspired the world's first metrical verse (*śloka*) — [perception: revered with sacred pathos as the immortal catalyst of poetic meter, compassion (*karuṇa*), and classical literature]
- [c. 4th–8th c. Classical Sanskrit Golden Age] — celebrated extensively by Kālidāsa, Bhāravi, and Bhavabhūti as the supreme living archetype of lifelong matrimonial devotion (*dāmpatya*) and unbearable grief in separation — [perception: universally idealized as an emblem of unshakeable romantic and marital fidelity]
- [Medieval to Early Modern India] — protected by rural peasant communities across the Gangetic plains as an auspicious harbinger of good monsoons and domestic peace, protected by strict religious taboos against hunting — [perception: cherished as a gentle, sacred avian neighbor and guardian of fertile paddy fields]
- [Late 18th–19th c. Colonial Era] — transliterated into English as *sārasa* and anglicized as *Sarus crane* by British naturalists (William Jones, Edward Blyth), entering international zoological taxonomy (*Antigone antigone*) — [perception: cataloged with scientific awe for its towering stature, while celebrated for its astonishing tameness around rural villages]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] — serves as a global flagship species for wetland and marshland conservation in South Asia, while remaining in world literature as the timeless embodiment of the Vālmīkian poetic genesis — [perception: treasured as both a vital ecological keystone and a profound cultural-literary archetype]
