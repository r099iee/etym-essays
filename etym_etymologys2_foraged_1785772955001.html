Earliest known root: Proto-Indo-European *peh₂- 'to feed, graze, protect' (also written *pā-), the root of Latin pāscō 'to feed, to drive to pasture' and pānis 'bread'. NOTE: the laryngeal-grade reconstruction (*peh₂- vs *peh₃-) is debated among specialists; the Germanic and Romance stages below are secure from the attested record. → Proto-Germanic *fōdrą 'fodder, animal food, provisions' (1st millennium BCE–CE; a derived noun on *péh₂-d-ro- / *peh₂-tro- 'that by which one feeds', with long *ō from the lengthened grade). Native English survival: Old English fōdor / fōddor → modern fodder. → Frankish *fōdar 'fodder, food, provisions' (West Germanic, 1st millennium CE), carried into the Gallo-Romance speech of northern Gaul during Frankish settlement. → Old French feurre / fuerre 'straw, fodder' (attested from the 9th–13th c.; the *ō of *fōdar gives the ue/eu diphthong). HOMONYM GUARD: Old French fuerre also meant 'sheath, scabbard', from a different Frankish word *fōdr 'case, lining' — unrelated to the forage family. → Old French fourrage / forrage 'fodder; forage; the act of collecting fodder and provisions' (12th–13th c.), formed on feurre with the noun suffix -age. → Middle French fourrager 'to forage, to collect fodder and provisions, to live off the land' (verb, from the 13th c.); the military sense — parties ranging to supply men and horses — crystallized in this period. → Middle English foragen / forragen 'to forage; to collect provisions by ranging the countryside; to ravage for supplies' (14th c., borrowed from Old French / Anglo-Norman during the Hundred Years' War). → English forage (noun and verb, from the 14th c.; also spelled forrage into the 17th c.). → foraged — the regular past tense and past participle (forage + -ed), the exact form on this card.
