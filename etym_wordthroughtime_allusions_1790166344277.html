- Metaphor and parable in Tudor humanism - perceived as learned allegorical comparison, serious and didactic.
- Word-play and jest in Elizabethan rhetoric - perceived as witty, sportive verbal play in the Latin sense of alludere.
- Passing or casual reference from the 1610s - shift to indirection as core; perceived as elegant economy, the gentleman-scholar's hint.
- Augustan critical term of taste - perceived as a mark of cultivation; Pope and Swift police good versus pedantic allusion.
- Modernist dense intertextuality - Eliot, Joyce and Pound make allusion structural; perceived as brilliant but demanding, even elitist.
- Contemporary broad and democratic usage - from political dog-whistles to internet memes; perceived as connective play, rewarding shared literacy across high and popular culture.
