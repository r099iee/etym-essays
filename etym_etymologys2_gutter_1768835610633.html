The root word gutter descends from a single PIE root meaning “to pour”, through the Latin word for “a drop” and the Old French word for “a water-channel”.

Stage 1 — PIE *gheu-, “to pour; to make a libation” (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE), with extended forms *gheu-d- and *gheu-t-.
Stage 2 — *gheu-t- → Latin gutta, “a drop” (attested 1st century BCE–1st century CE): the single bead of liquid, the smallest unit of pouring.
Stage 3 — Late Latin / Vulgar Latin *guttaria (or a formation on gutta), “water-channel” → Old French goutiere / gouttière, “channel for water” (attested 12th century); Old French goutte, “drop”, is the base, and goutiere is the trough that gathers a stream of drops.
Stage 4 — Anglo-Norman goutere → Middle English goter, “watercourse, channel for rain-water” (13th century).
Stage 5 — Modern English gutter (noun: roof-edge or street channel, 14th century onward; figurative “the gutter” of society, 19th century).
Stage 6 — Verb gutter, “of a candle, to burn unevenly so the melted wax runs down in channels” (18th century): an English-internal extension of the noun's “channel” sense.

Note on debate/nuance: The exact intermediate between Latin gutta and Old French goutiere is debated in detail — whether the French form continues a Latin *guttāria directly or was built on French goutte, “drop” — but the line from gutta, “drop”, through the Romance channel-word is standard (OED). The verb of candle-burning is not a separate borrowing but an English-internal reapplication of the noun's drainage sense. The same PIE root *gheu- also produced, via *gheu-d-, Latin fundō, “I pour, melt, cast” (source of foundry, fuse, infusion, confuse, refund), and, in Germanic, *geutaną “to pour” (source of Old English gēotan and probably of gush); these are sibling branches of the same root, not ancestors of gutter, whose own line runs exclusively through gutta and the drop.
