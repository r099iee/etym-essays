• Fluent: similar because it describes easy, flowing speech; differs because it is neutral or positive and says nothing about sincerity or depth.
  • Articulate: similar because it praises clear, effective expression; differs because it implies genuine command of ideas rather than superficial polish.
  • Voluble: similar because it describes a person who talks readily and at length; differs because it concerns quantity and speed, not truthfulness or shallowness.
  • Slick: similar because it suggests smooth, confident performance; differs because slick can be admiring and often refers to style, presentation, or commercial finish.
  • Smooth: similar because it uses the tactile metaphor of an easy surface; differs because smooth is often neutral or complimentary and lacks the explicit accusation of insincerity.
  • Oily: similar because it suggests a manner that is too smooth to be trusted; differs because oily is more visceral and often implies flattery or ingratiation.
  • Unctuous: similar because it describes excessive smoothness and insincere warmth; differs because it is more formal and stresses oily, fawning religiosity or servility.
  • Smarmy: similar because it criticises insincere charm; differs because it is more colloquial and suggests eager, ingratiating flattery rather than verbal facility.
  • Facile: similar because it describes something too easy and therefore shallow; differs because facile can apply to solutions, arguments, and writing, and often stresses lack of effort rather than deception.
  • Pat: similar because it describes a ready-made, conveniently neat answer; differs because pat can be neutral or positive and does not necessarily imply dishonesty.
  • Plausible: similar because it describes an account that sounds believable; differs because plausible concerns credibility, not the speaker’s fluency or character.
  • Specious: similar because it describes something superficially convincing but actually false; differs because specious is more formal and stresses logical deception rather than social smoothness.
  • Evasive: similar because glib answers often deflect scrutiny; differs because evasive concerns avoidance of the question, while glib concerns the style of the answer.
  • Disingenuous: similar because it implies insincerity; differs because it stresses pretended ignorance or innocence, not fluency.
  • Silver-tongued: similar because it describes persuasive eloquence; differs because it is often admiring or literary and does not automatically imply shallowness.
  • Honeyed: similar because it suggests sweet, soothing speech; differs because honeyed stresses flattery and tone rather than ready fluency.
