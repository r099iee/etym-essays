• mermaid: Similar because it denotes the half-human, half-fish sea-dweller; differs because mermaid is the singular, gender-specific, folklore-saturated figure of the lone romantic maiden on a rock or rock-luring sailors, while merpeople is a modern, gender-neutral collective—a whole society rather than one maiden.
  • merman: Similar because it is the male half-human, half-fish sea-dweller; differs because merman is the singular male counterpart of the mermaid, rare in storytelling, while merpeople includes all genders and sexes at once as a plural people.
  • merfolk: Similar because it is also a collective name for the sea-people, and for generations the two words were interchangeable; differs because merfolk keeps the older, broader folk-flavour of a tribe or kin-group (folk), while merpeople (from the neutral merperson) is the more recent, deliberately inclusive plural built on the modern civic word people.
  • merperson: Similar because it is the singular of merpeople, a half-human, half-fish being; differs because merperson is the gender-neutral singular coinage (back-formed from mermaid and merman), used when one being is meant or when sex is unknown, whereas merpeople is always the collective plural naming the community.
  • sirens: Similar because they are the most famous singers of the sea in mythology; differs because sirens are the dangerous enchantresses of Greek myth who lure sailors to their deaths, and in the original legends they were half-bird rather than half-fish—while Narnian merpeople sing for joy, a community rather than a trap.
  • tritons: Similar because they are sea-creatures of mythology with human and fish features; differs because tritons are the male Greek sea-gods, attendants of Poseidon armed with conch-shell trumpets—deities and individuals, not a people, and never part of a singing society.
  • nereids: Similar because they are sea-nymphs of Greek myth who live beneath the waves; differs because nereids are fully divine daughters of Nereus, beautiful but not half-fish at all—goddesses, whereas merpeople are mortal or semi-mortal hybrids.
  • selkies: Similar because they are supernatural beings of the sea in legend; differs because selkies are Celtic seals that shed their skins to become human—shapeshifters rather than natural hybrids, never half-fish by nature, and their story is about captivity and skin rather than community.
  • sea-people: Similar because it is the plain English paraphrase of merpeople, and the two are synonymous in folklore writing; differs because sea-people is the descriptive compound used in translation and folk-tale prose, while merpeople is the compact, coined form whose mer- prefix ties it to the mermaid family and to the old sea-root mere.
