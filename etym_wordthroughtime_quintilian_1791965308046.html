- 1st c. CE — the name of a celebrated teacher in Rome, holder of the first publicly salaried chair of rhetoric and author of the Institutio.
- Middle Ages — known only in fragments, excerpts and citations; Cicero remains the master, while Quintilian survives as a name in grammatical and rhetorical compendia.
- 1416 — Poggio Bracciolini finds a complete manuscript at St. Gall; the recovered text makes Quintilian a humanist celebrity and a model for school reform.
- 15th–16th c. — printed editions (Rome, 1470) and wide classroom use; Quintilian becomes the educator’s authority, cited alongside Cicero and drawn on by Erasmus and the Protestant schoolmen.
- 17th–18th c. — a fixture of grammar-school and university rhetoric; “Quintilian” and “Cicero” are paired as the theory and the model of eloquence, exactly as Bryant describes.
- 19th–21st c. — chiefly a historical and scholarly reference; the name now evokes humane, morally serious liberal education and the classical canon.
