RIDGES is the plural of RIDGE, which denotes a long, narrow, elevated crest or chain of high ground, a continuous line of a landscape with ground descending on either side of its topmost line. The word is fundamentally about linear extension: a ridge is not a point but a line, a backbone of terrain that runs for a distance, connecting or separating higher and lower ground. In The Hobbit, as the company leaves the Misty Mountains and heads toward Mirkwood, Bilbo looks forward and "could see before him only RIDGES and slopes falling towards lowlands and plains glimpsed occasionally between the trees": the ridges are the last backbone-like ramparts of the mountain world, already declining, the final high ground before the land folds away into open country. The tone is ruggedness and continuity; narratively the word marks the threshold of the descent, where heroic heights give way to the glimpsed plains. The plural pictures parallel crests, each one dropping the traveller's eye a little lower, so that the landscape reads as a series of descending bony ramparts rather than a single summit — a terrain of lines, not points, leading the gaze down toward the world of men.

The word is one of the most basic terms of geography and everyday description. Its core image is anatomical: the ridge of a landform is conceived as the backbone of a beast, the raised central line from which the flanks fall away — and this is no accident, because the English word itself first meant "back, spine" (Old English hrycg) before it was applied to hills. That double life persists in fossilised phrases: the ridge of the nose, the ridge of a roof, the ridge of a field. In the plural, the word accumulates the senses of parallelism, repetition, and descent that a single ridge cannot carry: ridges are what a traveller sees one after another, each lower than the last, as the mountains give way to the plains.

  • Sense 1 — a long narrow elevation of land (primary topographical sense): the crest-line of high ground with slopes falling away on both sides; a chain of hills or a mountain backbone. A ridge may be a watershed, the boundary between two drainage basins; a mountain ridge may connect peaks. This is the word in The Hobbit and in ordinary geography.
  • Sense 2 — the back or spine of an animal (archaic/anatomical root sense): the original meaning of the word, still preserved in fossilised phrases: the ridge of the nose, the ridge between the shoulder-blades. The historical bridge is the analogy between the raised spine of a creature and the raised crest of a hill — a shape the Anglo-Saxons recognised at once.
  • Sense 3 — any raised line on a surface (technical extension): the ridge of a roof (the apex where the two slopes meet), the ridge of a wave, the ridge of a field (the raised strip between furrows, as in "ridge and furrow" cultivation), the corrugations pressed into metal or the tread of a tyre. The bridge is the same image of a raised continuous line.
  • Sense 4 — geological and oceanographic jargon (modern technical sense): in geology and oceanography, a ridge is a long, narrow elevation of the earth's surface — a mid-ocean ridge, a ridge of fold mountains, a pressure ridge in ice. The mid-ocean ridge is the planet's longest mountain system, running beneath the oceans where tectonic plates spread apart. The bridge is the simple extension of the landform word to sub-oceanic and planetary features.
  • Sense 5 — military and sportive uses (minor modern senses): "ridge" appears in military terrain language (ridge line, a ridge held as a defensive position), in mountaineering (ridge walk, arête-like ridge traverse), and in place-names (Ridge Road, Blue Ridge). These are applications of the topographical sense, not separate senses, but they are part of the word's modern reach.

The semantic bridge in one paragraph: every sense of ridge is the same picture drawn at different scales. The earliest attested meaning, "back, spine" of a man or beast, supplied the governing image: a central raised line from which things fall away on both sides. Transferred to the land, that image names the crest-line of a hill; transferred to a roof, it names the apex of its slopes; transferred to a field, it names the raised strip between furrows; transferred to the ocean floor, it names the planet's submarine mountain chain. The plural RIDGES foregrounds the idea of a sequence — parallel crests descending one after another — and that is precisely what Tolkien's sentence exploits: the ridges are the receding ramparts that drop the eye and the traveller down toward the plains.

Register, grammar, and collocations: ridge is a standard word in every register of modern English — news, science, literary prose, and everyday speech — free of archaism and dialect colour, though its landform sense leans technical in geology and mountaineering. It is a countable noun (a ridge, two ridges), and the plural is regular. Common collocations include ridge line / ridgeline, ridge walk, ridge of a roof, ridge of the nose, ridge and furrow, mid-ocean ridge, mountain ridge, and Blue Ridge. In The Hobbit the plural is used as a landscape inventory, and that is a wholly literary but entirely standard use: the plural of a landform word collects the features of a terrain into a single sweeping view, which is why the sentence needs RIDGES rather than a single ridge.
