The warrant begins with a warrior keeping his word. Its root is PIE *wer-, to cover and guard — the ancestor of warden, guard, ward, and aware (being covered in watchfulness). In Proto-Germanic this grew into *warjaną, to ward off and defend, and among the Franks into *warand, the protector-guarantor: the man who stands surety, shield in one hand and bond in the other, answering for his follower's debts and deeds. When Frankish warriors took service and land in northern Gaul, their guarantee-word went with them.

French then split the inheritance in two. In the Norman north the Germanic w- survived, giving Old Northern French warant — guarantor and the paper he signs; in Paris and the centre the sound softened to garant, ancestor of modern guarantee. The Conquest of 1066 imported the northern form into England, where Middle English warant flourished in both senses at once: the man who answers for you and the sealed writ that empowers action. Medieval rolls are thick with them — merchants warranting the soundness of horses, lords warranting their tenants' titles, sheriffs acting by the king's warrant.

Stages of the journey:

  • PIE cover (before 2000 BCE): *wer- spreads the vocabulary of protection — covering, guarding, watching — across the daughter languages.
  • Germanic defence (c. 500 BCE–500 CE): *warjaną and its kin give ward, warden, aware — defence as a daily verb.
  • Frankish surety (5th–9th c.): *warand names the protector who answers for another — honour as collateral, the warband's social glue.
  • Northern French paper (10th–11th c.): warant joins man to document — the guarantor's promise made portable in sealed writing, while central French garant walks a parallel road.
  • Middle English double life (13th–15th c.): warant in England is at once personal assurance (I warrant you) and legal instrument — Chaucer and the law rolls use both without blinking.
  • Tudor statecraft (16th–17th c.): general warrants, search warrants, and death warrants make the word feared and formal — power exercised through paper, and therefore answerable to paper.
  • Modern justification (18th c.–today): the verb's centre of gravity slides from I promise to the facts authorise — evidence warrants conclusions — while the noun hardens into the policeman's knock and the shareholder's voucher, and phrases like warrant officer and dividend warrant carry the old surety into barracks and boardrooms.

Attestation is everywhere: Shakespeare's characters warrant each other a dozen times a play, the Bill of Rights generation argued bloodily over general warrants, and Jane Austen's neighbours still say I warrant with bluff ease — the exact note Tolkien gives his hobbit. From warband surety to search warrant, the word has never stopped meaning what it first meant: somebody solid, standing behind the claim, ready to answer for it.
