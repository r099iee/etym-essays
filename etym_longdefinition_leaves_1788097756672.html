1. The botanical sense. Leaves are the flat, thin, typically green organs of vascular plants — the plant's food factories — where chlorophyll captures sunlight and drives photosynthesis; their blades, petioles and veins vary endlessly from pine needles to palm fronds to great plate-like surfaces three metres across. Deciduous trees shed their leaves every autumn, when the green pigments break down into the golds, oranges and reds of fall foliage; leaf litter decomposes into humus; and a whole vocabulary of human uses surrounds them: leafy vegetables and leaf herbs, tea leaves, bay leaves, leaf buds, leaf mould, leaf vegetables in the kitchen gardens of the world.

2. The sheet sense — and the context sense. In the source sentence, "He was gilded all over with thin leaves of fine gold", leaves means thin sheets or laminae of metal: gold leaf and silver leaf, the foil of the gilder, beaten so thin that light passes through them and the gilded surface glows. This is the oldest extension of the plant sense: anything thin, flat and layered can be a leaf. The same logic gives us the leaf of a book (a single sheet or page, both sides), loose-leaf binders, to leaf through a volume, to take a leaf out of someone's book; the drop-leaf table and its fold-down leaves; leaf springs, the stacked steel strips of carriage and car suspensions; the leaves of a folding door or shutter; and tea leaves, read by fortune-tellers for the patterns they leave in the cup.

3. The hidden homograph: the verb. Leaves is also, completely independently, the third-person singular of the verb leave ("he leaves the room early"), from a quite different Old English verb lǣfan, "to cause to remain, to go away from". The two words — the plural of leaf and the inflected form of leave — are pronounced identically, /liːvz/, and spelled identically, though they share no ancestor at all: a perfect English homograph-convergence that learners must disambiguate by syntax alone.

  • Grammar note: the plural of leaf is leaves, with the historical voicing of final f to v before the plural ending, the same pattern as wolf/wolves, half/halves, knife/knives, loaf/loaves; the non-standard "leafs" survives only in a few fixed names, above all the Toronto Maple Leafs hockey team, whose owners deliberately chose the ungrammatical form in 1927 as a joke at the expense of schoolroom grammar.
  • Technical senses: in computing and mathematics, a leaf is a terminal node of a tree — a node with no children, in the botanical image of root, branch and leaf that structures databases, filesystems, decision trees and phylogenies; in markup and spreadsheets, leaf elements are the finest-grained data items at the end of a branch.
  • Figurative and idiomatic senses: "to turn over a new leaf" (a page-metaphor of moral reform), "to shake like a leaf", "a leaf out of the book of ...", "leafless" in winter, "leafy" suburbs, and the old proverb that "leaves of grass" name the poet's book in Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855).
  • Modern environmental senses: leaf-peeping (autumn foliage tourism in New England and Japan), leaf blowers and municipal leaf collection, leaf protein research after the Second World War, and the leaf as the universal green symbol of environmental movements.

4. The semantic bridge. Flatness and thinness bind the noun senses: a plant leaf, a sheet of gold, a page, a folded table wing and a steel spring leaf are all thin flat layers, and the name simply followed the shape from the field into the workshop. The verb sense is pure coincidence — two different words from two different roots colliding in one spelling — and it is precisely this collision that makes leaves one of the most instructive words in English spelling, pronunciation and grammar.
