Trifles, the plural of trifle, means in its central modern sense things of little value, weight, or importance — small matters, trivial details, or petty concerns that do not deserve serious attention, as when the Elven-smiths in the source sentence dismiss the lesser rings as mere trifles beside their greater craft.

The sentence shows the word doing characteristic double work: objectively the lesser rings are still perilous, yet from the lofty perspective of the smiths they rank as playthings, essays, practice pieces. That gap between lofty dismissal and real consequence is the living heart of trifle: what a great power calls a trifle may still crush a mortal, and much of the word's literary force comes from this irony of scale.

Core meaning in detail: A trifle is anything inconsiderable — a small sum of money, a minor fault, a brief delay, a passing fancy. The plural trifles overwhelmingly names accumulations of such small things: to quarrel over trifles, to be detained by trifles, trifles light as air in Shakespeare's famous phrase about jealousy. The word minimises by design; to call something a trifle is already to take a stance toward it, to refuse it dignity.

The verb to trifle: The noun cannot be separated from its verb, to trifle, meaning to act frivolously, to dally, to treat something carelessly or without due seriousness — to trifle with someone's affections, to trifle away one's time, not a matter to be trifled with. The semantic bridge is transparent: one who occupies himself with trifles trifles, and one who trifles produces trifles. The verb keeps the older moral colouring alive, since English still hears idleness and disrespect inside it.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Things of little importance (primary modern sense): petty matters, trivialities, small worries or pleasures. The bridge from the earliest sense of idle talk is generalisation: worthless words widened into worthless things of every kind.
  • A small amount, a little (quantifying use): a trifle tired, a trifle too long, meaning slightly or somewhat. The bridge is understatement through the noun: a trifle of something is literally a tiny portion of it, then bleached into a softener for adjectives.
  • A small gift or token (concrete social sense): to bring home trifles from a journey — souvenirs, knick-knacks, inexpensive keepsakes. The bridge is affectionate miniaturisation: valueless in price yet valued in feeling, sold in shops still called trifle and fancy dealers in older novels.
  • A layered dessert (culinary sense, from the 16th c.): sponge cake soaked in wine or sherry with jam, custard, fruit, and whipped cream, served in a glass bowl. The bridge is debated but usually told as smallness and ease: a light, easily assembled dish of odds and ends, a mere trifle beside great baked works — or else from the verb, a cream whipped into a frothy trifle, a thing frothed up out of nothing.
  • An idle or false tale (earliest sense, c. 1300, now archaic): a fable told to deceive, an empty story, mockery. This is the seed from which everything grew: talk that signifies nothing, then by extension anything that signifies nothing.
  • A person of no consequence (dismissive, usually with moral edge): a mere trifle of a man is rare now, but to treat someone as a trifle survives. The bridge carries the minimising stance from things to people, and it stings precisely because the word denies the victim weight.
  • Sport or dalliance with serious things (moralised verb sense): to trifle with danger, with death, with holy things. The bridge runs through the Elven-smiths of the source sentence: mastery that handles perilous craft as practice work is trifling in the oldest and most dangerous meaning of the word.

Register and connotation: trifles is cool, superior, faintly weary — the word of judges, elders, and immortals surveying mortal fuss. It soothes (never mind, it is a trifle) or wounds (you waste my time with trifles) depending on who holds the scales. Tolkien's use is deliberately double-edged: the smiths are right that the lesser rings are essays beside the Great Rings, yet the narrator quietly warns that even essays in such craft outgrow their makers, so the reader hears both the justice and the arrogance of the dismissal at once.
