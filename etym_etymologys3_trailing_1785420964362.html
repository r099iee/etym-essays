The participle TRAILING descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *tragh-, "to drag, draw, pull," a verbal core of physical effort whose descendants spread across several branches of the Indo-European family. In Germanic the root produced *draganą, giving Old English dragan and modern English drag, draw, and draught — words that keep the root's primitive sense of hauling something across a surface or toward oneself. In Greek it appears in trékho (τρέχω), "to run," where the sense of "drawing oneself along" is thought by some scholars to have shifted into motion at speed; the comparison is old but its details are debated, and no Greek word is required for the English chain. The decisive line for English runs through Italic: Proto-Italic *trag- produced Latin trahere, "to drag, draw, pull," attested from the first century BCE, and trahere is one of the most fertile roots in the English lexicon. Its past participle tractus gave tract, tractable, traction, and tractor; its compounds produced attract, contract, extract, subtract, and distract; its frequentative tractāre, "to handle, deal with," yielded treat, treatise, and maltreat. Even the French-derived trawl and trolling descend from the same family through Vulgar Latin *tragulāre, "to drag a net or sled," and Old French traille, "a drag-net." The word trail itself took shape in the Romance and Old French stages. Vulgar Latin *tragulāre developed the image of dragging a sled or a weighted net, and from this Old French built traille, the net dragged through water that is the source of English trawl, and the verb trailer or trailler, "to tow or drag along." It is this Old French verb that entered Middle English around the fourteenth century as trailen: to hang down so as to touch the ground, or to be dragged along behind when moving. From the start the participle TRAILING described the physical condition of a garment, branch, rope, or hem that dangles and sweeps in the wake of motion — a word of skirts and sleeves, of vines and brambles, of smoke and dust. The figurative career of the word then unfolded in stages.
  • Natural imagery. By the early modern period trailing described creeping plants and drifting smoke and dust, the long wake left by a moving body: trailing ivy, trailing clouds, the trailing train of a comet.
  • Pursuit and tracking. The noun trail, "a track or scent left by a moving person or animal," allowed to trail a suspect or quarry, a sense alive in hunting and detective vocabulary from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries onward.
  • Competition. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, to be trailing meant to be behind in a race, match, or election — the loser as the one who follows in the leader's wake.
  • The dueling idiom. The phrase "to trail one's coat" preserves a gesture of the age of honour: a gentleman dragged his coat or cloak along the ground so that an adversary might deliberately step on it, an insult that compelled a duel. From the eighteenth century onward, trailing one's coat came to mean deliberately provoking an argument, broadcasting an aggressive opinion in the expectation of a fight.
  • Wordsworth's glory. The same physical image of a child drawing a heavenly train is the basis of "trailing clouds of glory" in the Ode: Intimations of Immortality (1807), where the soul is said to come into the world trailing clouds of glory from God.
  • Technical extensions. The twentieth century added trailing edge in aeronautics, trailing stop and trailing twelve months in finance, and trailing comma or trailing whitespace in computing, all preserving the core idea of "that which follows at the back."
Socio-culturally, TRAILING has always carried two tones at once: the neutral, descriptive register of physical dragging and the knowing, combative register of the idiom. Empson, writing the preface to the second edition of Seven Types of Ambiguity, exploits precisely this second tone when he jokes that "apart from trailing my coat about minor controversies" he had claimed the right to define ambiguity as he pleased. The word lets him appear at once provocative and self-aware, the dueling gesture translated into the polite warfare of literary criticism. The sound of the word itself — the diphthong /eɪ/ stretched through the liquid -l- and the velar -ng — matches its meaning: a long, drawn-out, lingering motion, exactly what is left behind as something moves forward.
