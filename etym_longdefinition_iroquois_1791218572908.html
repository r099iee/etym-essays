Iroquois is a proper noun denoting a member of, or collectively the peoples of, the Haudenosaunee Confederacy — the alliance of Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and later Tuscarora nations of north-eastern North America — and by extension their languages, culture and longhouse architecture. In the source context it identifies the builders of sprawling five-hundred-foot bark edifices sheltering many families, i.e. the classic Iroquois longhouse village.

As an ethnonym it is an exonym: the people call themselves Haudenosaunee, People of the Longhouse. Iroquois is what French colonists called them in the early 1600s and what English then borrowed. It is capitalised, invariant in the plural — one Iroquois, many Iroquois — and used both as noun and adjective: an Iroquois warrior, Iroquois diplomacy, the Iroquois League.

  • Core ethnic sense: a person of Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca or Tuscarora descent. Example: she is Iroquois on her mother's side. This is the primary modern sense and the one intended in historical and anthropological prose.
  • Collective political sense: the Confederacy itself, often with definite article — the Iroquois — meaning the League as a diplomatic and military power. Historians write of Iroquois neutrality during the Anglo-French wars, Iroquois expansion during the Beaver Wars, Iroquois influence on American federal thought.
  • Linguistic sense: Iroquois or Iroquoian as a language family. Northern Iroquoian includes the Six Nations languages plus Huron-Wyandot and Susquehannock; Southern Iroquoian is Cherokee. To say a word is Iroquois may mean it belongs to Mohawk or Onondaga vocabulary rather than that a person is ethnically Iroquois.
  • Cultural-architectural sense: Iroquois longhouse, Iroquois village, Iroquois wampum, Iroquois False Face society — denoting material culture. The source sentence uses it this way: the Iroquois, for example, raised sprawling edifices of bark-covered timber.
  • Hairstyle sense (chiefly British): an iroquois haircut — what Americans call a mohawk — a strip of upright hair with shaved sides. This fashion term has only a stereotyped link to actual Haudenosaunee warriors and is now often avoided in favour of mohawk or crest.
  • Place-name and institutional survival: Iroquois persists in toponyms, school names and titles — Iroquois County Illinois, Iroquois Park, Iroquois Theatre — even where the living nation is now officially styled Haudenosaunee. Such uses are historical fossils rather than live ethnic descriptions.

Usage notes and register: in contemporary Canada and the United States, official and respectful usage prefers Haudenosaunee for the people and Iroquoian for the language family, reserving Iroquois for historical contexts, quotations and established proper names. Using Iroquois for a living person is not a slur but is increasingly marked as dated or outsider usage, much like Eskimo versus Inuit and Yupik. Anthropology and linguistics textbooks typically gloss on first mention: Haudenosaunee — Haudenosaunee, formerly called Iroquois by outsiders.

Polysemy bridge: all secondary senses radiate from the original 17th-century French tribal label. League → language family is a normal metonymy from speakers to speech. Tribe → house type comes from the salient longhouse noted by every European observer, including the source passage. Tribe → haircut comes from romantic frontier imagery of the scalp-lock warrior, later punk reappropriation. None of these is a separate word; each is a contextual extension of one exonym whose semantic centre remains the Six Nations people.
