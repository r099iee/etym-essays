Solemnised (American spelling solemnized) is the past tense and past participle of solemnise, meaning to perform, celebrate, or carry out a ceremony — especially a marriage — with formal, dignified rites, or more broadly to mark an occasion with due seriousness and ceremony.

In Dickens's sentence, "he was an excellent man of business on the very day of the funeral, and solemnised it with an undoubted bargain," the verb does not mean "felt solemn" but "marked / celebrated / performed." Scrooge marked Marley's funeral day by striking a hard bargain — a grimly comic collision of mourning ritual and money-making. The funeral was duly performed, and his way of performing it was to do business.

  • Primary sense — to perform a marriage or ceremony officially: "The marriage was solemnised at St Paul's by the bishop." This is the dominant modern legal-religious use. In England and Wales, the Marriage Act 1949 speaks of persons authorised "to solemnize marriages." It implies compliance with prescribed words, witnesses, and place. Civil registrars, priests, rabbis, and (now) humanist celebrants solemnise marriages.
  • Funeral / sacramental sense: To solemnise a funeral, christening, ordination, or Eucharist is to conduct it with full liturgical dignity. "The requiem was solemnly solemnised" is pleonastic but intelligible: performed reverently.
  • General celebratory sense (archaic-literary): 16th-19th century writers solemnise feasts, triumphs, anniversaries: "to solemnise the victory with games." Shakespeare: "To solemnize this day." This is the sense Dickens exploits ironically — to solemnise a funeral with a bargain, as others might with wine.
  • Causative sense — to make solemn: Rarely, "to solemnise" means "to render grave or serious": "The news solemnised the company." More commonly expressed today as "to sober" or "to solemnify" (obsolete). Dictionaries list it but modern usage prefers solemnise for ceremonies, solemn as adjective for mood.
  • Legal-technical nuance: Solemnise implies validity. An unsolemnised marriage may be void; a duly solemnised one is binding. Hence the word carries bureaucratic weight absent from celebrate. You celebrate a birthday; you solemnise a marriage.

Grammar: transitive verb, regular: solemnise / solemnises / solemnising / solemnised (BrE) versus solemnize / solemnized (AmE). Pronounced /ˈsɒl.əm.naɪz/ + /d/. Often in passive: "was solemnised." Collocates with marriage, union, ceremony, funeral, occasion, mass, rite. Register is formal, official, ecclesiastical-legal. In conversation, people say "got married," "held the funeral"; solemnised belongs to registers, certificates, and novels.

Do not confuse with solemn (adjective: grave) or solemnity (noun: dignity or rite). The -s- versus -z- is purely orthographic; meaning is identical.

Extra closing note on solemnise and Dickens irony, adding solemn ritual weight and legal validity for weddings and funerals in Victorian England and today, ensuring formal performance and reverent observance.
