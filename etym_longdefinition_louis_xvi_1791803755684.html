What the name denotes. Louis XVI is the regnal name of Louis-Auguste de Bourbon (1754–1793), King of France from 1774 until the monarchy was abolished in September 1792, and the only French king to be executed. The compound has two parts: the personal name Louis, borne by fifteen kings before him, and the regnal numeral XVI, which means “the sixteenth of that name.” English reads the numeral as an ordinal and says “Louis the Sixteenth”; French reads it as a cardinal, “Louis seize.”

The name as a title. A regnal number is not part of a personal name but a disambiguator, added after the fact so that kings sharing a name can be distinguished. Louis XVI therefore means “the sixteenth king called Louis,” not “Louis, sixteenth son” or anything of the kind. The numeral became necessary because Louis was the most common royal name in French history: from Louis I, the Pious, son of Charlemagne, to Louis XVIII, the restored brother of the executed king, the name carried a dynasty's sense of itself.

The man. Born at Versailles on 23 August 1754, Louis-Auguste was a grandson of Louis XV. He married Marie Antoinette of Austria in 1770, became dauphin on the death of his father in 1765, and inherited the throne in 1774 at the age of nineteen. Contemporaries found him pious, well-meaning, physically strong and politically indecisive. His reign opened with reformist ministers—Turgot, then Necker—and with French intervention in the American war, which deepened the debt that would force the monarchy to summon the Estates-General.

The Revolution. The Estates-General met at Versailles in May 1789; the Third Estate broke away, the Bastille fell in July, and the royal family was brought to Paris in October. Louis accepted the constitutional settlement of 1791 and became “king of the French,” but in June 1791 he had attempted to flee to the frontier and was arrested at Varennes—an act that destroyed his credit with the Revolution. War with Austria and Prussia began in 1792; on 10 August the monarchy was suspended after the storming of the Tuileries; in September the Republic was proclaimed. Tried by the National Convention in December 1792 under the name Louis Capet—a deliberate republican re-naming that invoked the dynasty's founder and stripped him of royal legitimacy—he was convicted of treason and guillotined on 21 January 1793 in the Place de la Révolution.

Why he matters. Louis XVI is the hinge on which the Revolution turns from reform to republic. His execution made regicide irreversible, divided Europe, and gave the Revolution its central martyr and its central crime, depending on who was telling the story. Royalists remembered a good king badly advised; republicans remembered a tyrant who had plotted with foreign armies. Modern historiography has moved away from both caricatures, stressing his genuine piety and his intermittent political calculation, while still holding him responsible for the disastrous timing of his decisions. In traditionalist Catholic circles he is honoured as a martyr, though he has never been canonised.

Attributive sense. Louis XVI also functions as a period and style label. “Louis XVI furniture” denotes the French neoclassical style of his reign—straight lines, fluted legs, mahogany, classical ornament, marquetry—and “a Louis XVI chair” or “a Louis XVI salon” is a standard phrase in the antiques trade. The style outlived the king and is now a purely decorative designation, morally neutral in a way the name itself can never be.

In this reading. Carlyle's history invites the reader to stand “on the streets of Paris at the fall of the Bastille or the public execution of Louis XVI.” The paired images are the Revolution's beginning and its point of no return. By writing in the present tense and the first-person plural, Carlyle makes the execution a scene the reader witnesses rather than a fact the reader is told—and the name Louis XVI, heavy with seven centuries of dynasty, is what makes the scene feel like the end of an age.

Key facts at a glance

  • Born: 23 August 1754 at Versailles, as Louis-Auguste, duc de Berry.
  • Married: Marie Antoinette of Austria, 1770.
  • Reigned: 1774–1792; “king of the French” under the constitution from 1791.
  • Deposed: 10 August 1792; the Republic was proclaimed in September.
  • Tried and executed: December 1792 – 21 January 1793, under the name Louis Capet.
  • Afterlife: royalist martyr, republican tyrant, and a period label for neoclassical furniture.
