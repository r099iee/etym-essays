The verb flattered began as a gesture of the hand, not of the tongue. Its whole history is the history of how a physical act — smoothing, stroking, patting — was gradually transferred to speech, and how the transferred sense carried with it a permanent charge of suspicion and calculation. To trace the word is to watch an image of the palm moving across a surface become one of the most morally loaded verbs in the language. The Indo-European root: flatness and spreading.
  • At the foundation lies the Indo-European root *plet-/*plat-, meaning "flat, broad; to spread out." It is a root of surfaces and extension, and it appears across the family: Greek platys, "broad, flat" (the source of plateau, "a flat tableland," and platypus, "flat-foot"); Sanskrit prathati, "spreads out, extends," and prathus, "broad"; Slavic forms meaning "flat"; and the Germanic word *flata-, "flat, level."
  • In the Germanic branch this root produced *flata-, which survives in the English adjective flat ("level, even"), in Old Norse flatr, Old High German flaz, and Middle Dutch vlat. From *flata- the West Germanic languages formed a verb meaning "to make flat, to smooth" — in Frankish, the reconstructed *flatjan.
  • This is the crucial point of the word's biography: flatter is not a Latin word at all. It entered the Romance world as a Germanic loan, and its Indo-European ancestry runs through the Germanic line of *plat-, not through Latin. The English word flat and the French verb flater are therefore blood relatives, both descended from the ancient root of flatness.
From hand to tongue: the Old French shift.
  • When the Franks settled in Gaul, they carried *flatjan into the speech of the land, and Old French took it as flater. Its earliest attested meaning was entirely physical: "to smooth, to stroke, to caress, to pat." One flattened a surface, smoothed a garment, stroked an animal's coat.
  • The leap from hand to tongue took place within medieval French, and the logic is easy to follow. To stroke an animal — a horse, a dog — is to soothe and please it; to stroke a servant or a subject is to please and calm them. From "stroking a person" it was a short step to "pleasing a person with words," and so flater came to mean "to coax, to soothe with pleasant speech, to praise in order to please." By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries both the physical and the figurative senses were alive in French, and the figurative one was winning.
  • From the same verb Old French built flaterie, "cajolery, insincere praise," and flatteur, "one who flatters" — the agent noun that already, in medieval usage, named a species of parasite and deceiver. The moral disapproval solidified early.
Into English: flateren and the medieval inheritance.
  • English borrowed the verb in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, in the Anglo-Norman and Middle English form flateren, meaning "to caress with words, to coax, to praise insincerely." Chaucer uses the family — flaterye, flatterers — in The Canterbury Tales, where flattery is already the stock tool of the dishonest and the ambitious, condemned from the pulpit and mocked on the stage.
  • The moral tradition the word entered was ancient and powerful. Classical moralists from Plato to Plutarch had made the flatterer (Greek κόλαξ, Latin adulator) a fixed type: the man who mirrors your opinions, agrees with everything, and lives by pleasing the great. The Mill sentence that anchors this card — that Mrs. Mill "may have flattered her husband's vanity by echoing his opinions" — is directly in this two-thousand-year-old line: flattery as the echo that gratifies, the agreement that flatters, the power won by pleasing.
  • The grammar of the word settled early too. The regular English past tense flattered (verb + -ed) is the form on this card, and the passive participle became an adjective: a "flattered" person is one whose vanity has been pleased. From the sixteenth century on, "to flatter oneself" developed as a reflexive idiom meaning "to deceive oneself with pleasing hopes or opinions."
The widening and softening of the modern word.
  • In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word acquired a purely aesthetic sense: clothes, light, and angles "flatter" a person when they make them look better than they are. A "flattering" portrait, a "flattering" dress, "flattering" lighting — here the suspicion vanishes and the word becomes a compliment about appearance.
  • The passive also softened: "I'm flattered" came to mean genuinely pleased, honoured, even when no manipulation is intended. The native suspicion is retained when the word is aimed at the flatterer's motives, but dropped when the word records the receiver's pleasure. The verb thus became double-edged in a new way: to flatter is still suspect, but to be flattered is often simply delightful.
  • The noun and its family grew in parallel: flattery, flatterer, flattering, flatteringly. The proverb "flattery will get you nowhere" and the phrase "empty flattery" kept the critical core alive, while the psychological vocabulary of "flattering someone's vanity, pride, or ego" kept the word precise and clinical.
The word in this card.
  • In the sentence "Mrs. Mill may have flattered her husband's vanity by echoing his opinions, or she may have indeed been an Egeria, full of inspiration and intellectual helpfulness," the word is used at full historical pressure. Flattering here is the suspected tactic of a wife gaining influence by mirroring her husband — the ancient strategy of the flatterer who agrees with everything — and the sentence sets it against the genuine inspiration of Egeria, the nymph who truly guided the Roman king Numa. The opposition is exactly the classical one: the flatterer who echoes versus the inspirer who contributes.
  • The word thus does what it has always done: it names praise whose sincerity is in question, a pleasing of vanity that may be a bid for power. It is at once a compliment and an accusation, and its long history from the stroking hand to the echoing tongue is compressed into its use here.
Conclusion. Flattered carries an Indo-European root of flatness and spreading, a Germanic verb of smoothing, a French shift from the stroking hand to the coaxing tongue, and a medieval English career in which the flatterer became the archetype of the insincere. In the modern language it splits into the suspect act and the innocent pleasure — but the suspicion is never far away, and a sentence like Mill's shows how much moral history can be compressed into a single word.
