The evocative literary adjective unshed describes that which has not been shed, poured out, dropped, or cast off. In its dominant and most poignant literary application—as encountered in the source sentence where Bertie Wooster observes a heroine whose eyes were “wet with __________ tears, but I rather think they were, certainly she clasped her hands in piteous appeal”—the word specifically designates tears that have gathered, brimmed, or glistened within the tear ducts and across the cornea due to intense emotion, grief, or distress, yet have been held back by willpower, shock, pride, or stoic composure before rolling down the cheeks.

Formed by prefixing the native Germanic negative marker un- to the past participle shed, the word possesses a dual character in the English language: a deeply emotional, elevated poetic register on the one hand, and a precise biological and agricultural sense on the other:

  • The poetic / sentimental sense — tears restrained in the eyes: By far the most celebrated collocation in English literature: “eyes bright with unshed tears”; “a burning ache of unshed tears.” It conveys profound emotional tension, capturing the precise threshold between inner agony and outward self-control. Unlike tears that fall freely, unshed tears represent grief trapped within the self.
  • Comedic and mock-heroic inversion: In twentieth-century humorous prose, particularly in the works of P. G. Wodehouse, the phrase is frequently invoked with affectionate irony. Wooster's sentimental foils (such as Madeline Bassett or Honoria Glossop) habitually brim with theatrical “unshed tears” while striking melodramatic attitudes, deflating the Victorian sentimental trope into high farce.
  • Martial, biblical, and moral sense — unspilled blood: Applied historically to blood that has not been shed in combat, murder, or sacrifice: “a peaceful revolution bought with unshed blood”; “the guilt of unshed blood.” In historical and diplomatic prose, it signifies the preservation of human life and the avoidance of slaughter.
  • Zoological and anatomical sense — uncast skin, hair, or antlers: In natural history and wildlife biology, unshed describes animal structures that have not been moulted or shed according to seasonal cycles: “the stag still carried its unshed winter antlers well into May”; “a reptile struggling with patches of unshed skin (dysecdysis).”
  • Botanical and agricultural sense — unfallen leaves or seeds: Used in botany and horticulture to describe autumnal leaves, petals, or grain kernels that fail to undergo natural abscission and cling to the parent stem: “the unshed leaves of the beech tree rustled through the winter frost.”
  • Psychological resonance: The word carries immense expressive weight because of the physical sensation it denotes: the burning, heavy fullness in the eyes and throat when a human being suppresses the impulse to weep. It is the definitive adjective of emotional restraint.
  • Semantic bridge: Deriving from Old English scēadan (“to divide, separate, part, scatter”), the base verb shifted from dividing physical space to scattering drops or casting off bodily coverings. Negated by un-, the adjective signifies that which remains united to the source—tears retained in the eye, blood within the veins, or leaves upon the bough.

Collocating primarily with tears, blood, drops, antlers, and leaves, unshed remains an indispensable stylistic touchstone of emotional and descriptive English.
