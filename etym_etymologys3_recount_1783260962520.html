The story of recount is the story of how the Romans learned to count by cleaning, and how cleaning became thinking, and thinking became telling. Its deepest root is the Proto-Indo-European base *pewH-, “to cleanse, purify,” a root whose physical sense is plainly preserved in its other Latin offspring: purus “clean” (English pure), purgāre “to cleanse” (English purge, expurgate, purgatory), and purificāre “to make clean” (English purify). From this cleansing base the Romans formed the verb putāre, whose earliest attested sense is agricultural: to prune a tree, to trim, to make clean by cutting away. The genius of the semantic development lies in the metaphor that followed. To putare an account was to “clear” it, to settle it, to wipe it clean; from clearing accounts the verb glided into “reckoning” and then into “considering, thinking, esteeming” — for the Roman, to think was to tidy the mind. The English language is still full of the deposits of that metaphor: reputation is “the way one is reckoned” (reputāre), impute is “to reckon to a person's charge” (imputāre), dispute is “to weigh against” (disputāre), and amputate preserves the original pruning sense (amputāre, “to cut around”).

The counting line. With the prefix com- (“together”), Latin built computāre, “to reckon together, sum up,” and this is the immediate ancestor of our word. As the empire dissolved into the Romance vernaculars, computāre became the ordinary verb for counting in the west: French compter, Italian contare, Spanish and Portuguese contar. In Old French the verb was written conter, and — critically for our word — it carried two senses side by side: “to count, number” and “to tell, relate, narrate.” The conceptual link is older and deeper than the French: to tell a story is to give an account, to run through the items in order, to number them; the very word account preserves the double meaning, and the English phrase “to give a full account of” still moves between the counting-house and the chronicle. So in medieval French, conter meant both to count coins and to tell tales, and reconter meant “to tell over again” — a retelling, a full narration, the ancestor of modern French raconter.

Into English. Old French conter gave Middle English counten, our count; Old French reconter gave Middle English recounten in the fifteenth century, with the sense “to relate, narrate in detail.” The word was a favourite of chroniclers and historians; a chronicle was precisely a recounting, an ordered telling of events year by year. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the verb was used in the grand narrative registers of history and sermon, and it carried into those uses the old Latin association of thinking and ordering. Macaulay, writing in the 1840s, reaches for the word at the head of his great narrative, and the choice is deliberate: “I shall recount the errors...” — he promises a connected, chronological, complete narration, the chronicler's word dignified into the historian's pledge. The Whig philosophy of history — that a candid recounting of errors teaches the nation its lessons — is written into the very word he chose.

The homograph. Meanwhile the counting line went its own way. Modern English re-count, “to count again,” is a nineteenth-century formation built directly on count with the prefix re-; the noun recount, above all in the electoral sense — the second counting of ballots in a disputed election — is a twentieth-century fixture of democratic politics. The two words, the chronicler's and the pollster's, now share a spelling and a sound, and the mnemonic on this card teaches exactly this doubling. Etymologically they are two different words that converged; semantically they remain as close as numbering and narrating always were, for the recount of a ballot is, in the end, a fresh and careful telling of the votes.

  • PIE: *pewH- “to cleanse, purify” → Latin purus “clean” (pure, purge), and putāre “to prune; to settle; to reckon, think.”
  • Counting: computāre “to reckon together” → Old French conter “to count; to tell” (both senses).
  • Narrating: Old French reconter “to tell over again” → Middle English recounten (15th c.) → recount “to narrate in detail.”
  • Homograph: re-count “to count again” is a separate 19th-century formation; the election recount (noun) follows.
  • Macaulay: “I shall recount the errors...” — the chronicler's word as the historian's pledge (1840s).
