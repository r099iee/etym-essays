ivy (noun) — the evergreen climbing plant of the genus Hedera, especially Hedera helix (common, English, or European ivy), a woody vine that clings to walls, trees, and other supports by means of aerial rootlets and remains green through the winter. In this passage the word designates the climbing, woody evergreen plant that has overrun the old walls and that Peter wants to "clear away." The specific nuance of the word here is not botanical description but its role as the emblem of nature quietly reclaiming human architecture: ivy clings, spreads, and slowly erases masonry, so that to speak of "clearing it away" is to speak of reversing the work of time itself. The plant's distinctive habit—gripping stone with aerial rootlets, remaining green through winter, and maturing into dense mats—makes it the perfect shorthand for a place that has been abandoned long enough for vegetation to begin blotting out the traces of its builders. Within the context the tone is plain and matter-of-fact: Peter treats the ivy as a practical nuisance to be removed, yet the very familiarity of the word carries an undertone of neglect and the passage of the years, since an ivy-clad wall is the traditional sign of a ruin that man has left behind. 1. The primary botanical sense
  • Botany: ivy is any member of the genus Hedera (family Araliaceae), of which Hedera helix is the type species. It is an evergreen woody vine or ground-creeping shrub with two distinct leaf forms: the lobed, five-pointed juvenile leaf produced on climbing and trailing stems, and the unlobed, diamond- or oval-shaped adult leaf produced on the erect flowering stems of mature plants. Greenish-yellow flowers open in late summer and autumn, and the dark blue-black berry-like fruits ripen through the winter.
  • Climbing mechanism: unlike tendril-climbers such as the grape or clematis, and unlike stem-twiners such as honeysuckle or bindweed, ivy clings by aerial rootlets—short adventitious roots that press against the support and hold fast, allowing a single plant to scale a wall or trunk to a great height and to spread into a dense, impermeable mat that can eventually pull masonry apart and block windows and gutters.
  • Hardiness and name: ivy is native across Europe, western Asia, and northern Africa; it is famously shade-tolerant and frost-hardy, which is why it stays green through every winter—in Hindi it is the सदाबहार बेल (sadābahār bel), the "evergreen climber," the green that holds fast. This winter-constancy is the source of nearly all its symbolism.
2. The architectural and ruin sense (dominant here)
  • The ivy-clad wall is the stock image of the picturesque ruin: ivy is what grows on a place man has abandoned, the green that erases masonry and takes a building back from its builders. In the passage Peter's matter-of-fact desire to "clear this ivy away" quietly reverses that symbolism—he is the rightful restorer re-taking the castle from the plant that claimed it during the kingdom's absence.
  • From the 18th century the Romantic movement made ivy on ruins beautiful: poets and landscape painters used the ivy-mantled tower as the emblem of noble age, and the phrase "ivy-clad" became a literary stock formula. Ivy thus carries, in a single word, both neglect (a wall nobody has tended) and a melancholy beauty (the plant that makes abandonment picturesque).
3. Secondary, symbolic, and modern senses
  • Sacred and festive symbol: in antiquity ivy was sacred to Bacchus/Dionysus; in medieval England a bush of ivy hung over the alehouse door as the sign that wine was sold within, and ivy was twined with holly into the midwinter garland. Its ever-green habit made it, in Christian tradition, an emblem of everlasting life and of the soul that endures. In Victorian flower language (floriography) ivy signified fidelity and friendship, because it holds fast and clings true.
  • Institutional sense — Ivy League: since the 1930s the word "Ivy" (and the phrase "Ivy League") names eight prestigious American universities in the northeastern United States—Brown, Columbia, Cornell, Dartmouth, Harvard, the University of Pennsylvania, Princeton, and Yale—so called because of the ivy-grown collegiate architecture (with a folk etymology from the Roman numeral IV of the early athletics league). By extension "Ivy," "Ivy League," and "ivy-covered" are adjectives for anything elite, venerable, East-Coast-academic, and old-money.
  • Loose plant-name transfers: the name is applied to several unrelated plants: poison ivy (Toxicodendron radicans), a North American sumac-family climber with three-leaved foliage whose resin causes a severe contact rash; Boston ivy (Parthenocissus tricuspidata), an unrelated grape-family creeper grown on brick walls; ground ivy (Glechoma hederacea), a creeping low mint; and the garden "ivy-leaved" plants such as ivy-leaved geranium and ivy-leaved toadflax.
  • Derivatives and uses: ivied (adjective, "covered with ivy"), ivy-clad, ivy-covered, ivy-leaved; "ivy green" as a colour name; and "Ivy" as a modern given name drawn from the plant, a botanical-name revival of the late 19th century.
4. The semantic bridge
  • The thread that joins every sense is tenacity and permanence: ivy stays green when other plants die, holds fast to whatever it touches, and outlasts the structures it climbs. From that single idea flow fidelity (Victorian flower language), constancy and everlasting life (Christian and midwinter symbolism), institutional age and prestige (the Ivy League), and the elegiac image of the wall it slowly erases. In every register the word is about time and the refusal to let go.
