- (1300s–1500s) - The a- state-word pattern (on + noun: asleep, abed, afoot, alive) is a living part of English, but astir itself does not yet exist; the raw materials — stir as “movement, bustle, commotion” — are already ready.
- (1822) - Astir first appears in print meaning “up and about; on the move”: a purely physical, collective sense of households, camps, and armies rousing.
- (1830s–1850s) - Quickly spreads to whole communities and to news: cities and nations are “astir with” rumour, alarm, and excitement; the word gains a second, figurative sense of agitated expectancy.
- (1900s) - As plain speech shifts to up and about and on the move, astir becomes a literary and journalistic favourite for dramatic rousing scenes.
- (1937) - Tolkien’s The Hobbit plants it in fantasy: “In the camp all was now astir, as if for battle” makes astir the signature word of a war camp coming alive.
- (today) - Rare in speech, normal in epic and historical prose; perception: dignified, slightly archaic, evoking collective bustle on the edge of action.
