The biography of “slung” begins with a Proto-Indo-European root that described not throwing but winding. *sleng-/*slenk- meant “to turn, wind, twist,” a motion-language of vines, creepers, and pliant things. This is why the family looks oddly double in meaning: things that wind can also be thrown, because a thrown object rotates as it flies — the arm, the wrist, and the projectile all trace a turn. From the very beginning the root split across dialects into two rhythmic shapes: the velar *sleng- feeding the Germanic verb “sling,” and the dental *slenk- feeding “slink,” the stealthy winding of a creature that creeps rather than strides. That early fork is the first semantic shift in the story, and it determines everything that follows: one branch stayed winding and secretive, the other wound its way toward throwing.

In the Germanic world the root grew a strong class III verb, Proto-Germanic *slingwanan, “to wind, twist, swing, fling.” Its most influential daughter for English was Old Norse slyngva, which sailed into English with the Viking settlers around 1200 as “slingen” — the record’s first senses are all about hurling with a swing. From that came the weapon called a “sling” (a strap whose whirling motion, a continuous winding in the hand, hurls a stone) and the carrying loop that lets a rifle or a belt hang from one shoulder. Meanwhile the native Old English verb “slingan” (“to turn, twist, wind”) continued quietly, and its dental cousin “slincan” hardened into “slink,” the movement of something that winds along near the ground. The Continental sisters developed in parallel — Dutch “slingeren” (“to hurl, swing”), German “schlingen” (“to wind, twist, weave”) and its noun “Schlinge” (“noose, loop”) — each preserving the original winding sense while the English branch leaned ever further toward throwing.

The past-tense form “slung” follows the archaic strong-verb pattern of the “sing/sang/sung” type (sling — slang — slung, later levelled to sling — slung — slung), the same ablaut rhythm that drives most of English’s most native vocabulary. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the old past “slang” began to yield to “slung” for both the past tense and the participle, a process of levelling that regularized the verb; “slang” escaped into an independent life as a noun and verb meaning thieves’ cant. Shakespeare, writing at the moment of the change, gave the word its most famous literary witness in Hamlet (1600): “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” — here the weapon-sense itself, the whirling stone-caster of the root, stands for every mischance that fate can hurl.

The semantic drift continued in the centuries after Shakespeare. The hurling sense stayed alive but softened; the suspension sense — what hangs loosely from a point of support — grew steadily, so that by the 17th–19th centuries “slung” was the verb of habitual, easeful carrying among soldiers and travellers: a musket, a pouch, a knapsack slung over the shoulder in one practiced swing. The word shed force and kept only motion and looseness, exactly the value it has in the Narnia passage, where Lucy “slung the belt over her shoulder and once more felt the bottle at her side where it used to hang in the old days.” The twentieth century added the arm-sling of medicine, the baby-sling of parenthood, and the street idiom “slung low,” while “slung hash” kept the old cook-shop sense of rapid service.

Timeline:
  • pre-3rd millennium BCE — PIE *sleng-/*slenk- “to turn, wind, twist”; the root forks into Germanic “sling” and “slink.”
  • c. 500 BCE–500 CE — Proto-Germanic *slingwanan “to wind, twist, swing, fling,” a class III strong verb.
  • Viking Age — Old Norse slyngva “to sling, fling, hurl.”
  • c. 1200–1300 — “slingen” enters English with the Scandinavian settlers; the sling weapon and the carrying loop follow.
  • 16th–17th c. — the ablaut past levells to “slung”; Shakespeare’s “slings and arrows of outrageous fortune” (1600).
  • 17th–19th c. — the suspension sense dominates: musket, pouch, knapsack slung over the shoulder.
  • 20th–21st c. — arm-sling, baby-sling, “slung low,” and the casual modern slinging of bags and coats.
