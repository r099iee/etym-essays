Stems, the plural of stem, names in its plainest sense the slender stalks that hold up plants: the green shoots bearing leaves and flowers, the woody trunks rising into trees, the upright supports that lift living tissue toward light. In the source sentence it carries exactly this woodland sense: looking back, the travellers see the dark line of the Hedge through the stems of trees already thick about them, a lattice of vertical trunks between which the familiar boundary glints and fades as the forest closes round.

Core meaning in detail: A stem is the axis of a plant, the central line from which everything else branches. Herbaceous stems stay soft and green, collapsible at summer end; woody stems harden with lignin into trunks and boughs that endure for centuries. Functionally the stem does three jobs at once: it holds leaves and flowers up to sun and pollinators, it pipes water and food between roots and crown, and it stores strength against wind and weight. Tolkien's forester eye, trained on real woods, sees stems first as architecture: not foliage or fruit but the pillars of the green hall, dark, close-set, and increasingly impassable.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Plant stalks and tree trunks (primary sense, from Old English, the source sense): flower stems, the stems of young oaks, vine stems. The bridge is the original noun stefn, stemn, a staff or trunk: the upright that bears, whether rose or oak, held in common by gardener and forester.
  • The main upright of a thing shaped like a plant (extended physical): the stem of a glass, a tobacco pipe, a wine glass. The bridge is shape analogy: the slender foot that lifts the bowl mirrors the stalk that lifts the flower, and craftsmen borrowed the garden word for the table.
  • The prow or stern-post of a ship (nautical, from Old Norse influence): from stem to stern. The bridge runs through timber: the great curved timbers that form a ship ends were called stems as tree-trunks set upright in water, and sailors still measure vessels stem to stern as gardeners measure beds row to row.
  • Origin, stock, or lineage from which things branch (figurative): the stem of a family, the stem of a language. The bridge is the plant as family tree: as leaves branch from the stalk, so peoples, words, and customs branch from a common stock, and genealogists draw literal stems.
  • To stop, check, or dam a flow — to stem the tide (verb, separately derived but convergent): to stem bleeding, to stem decline. The bridge is debated: partly from stemming as damming with a stake, partly from stemming as making headway against a current bow-first, the ship stem breasting the tide until the verb means resistance itself.
  • Word-stems in grammar (linguistic technical): the stem to which affixes attach. The bridge is again the plant: strip the inflectional leaves and the lexical stalk remains, the form that bears meaning as the stalk bears flower.
  • Stems in science and technology (modern): brain stems, stem cells. The bridge carries the origin sense into the laboratory: the brain stem is the stalk that bears the brain flower, and stem cells are the undifferentiated stock from which specialised tissues branch like leaves.

Register and connotation: stems is cool, clean, and observational, the naturalist word rather than the poet flower-word. It looks past blossom to structure, past colour to column, and therefore suits moments of anxiety and orientation: lost travellers count stems, foresters thin stems, children hide among stems. In the source sentence the plural matters: one stem is a detail, but stems thick about them are a palisade, the Hedge glimpsed through bars that will soon become walls.
