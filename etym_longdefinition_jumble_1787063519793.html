Core meaning. A jumble is a confused, disorderly mixture of things thrown together without arrangement. In the supplied passage, the ledge and its landmarks — scorched grass, the thrush's stone, the snail-covered walls — disappear "in a jumble of smithereens," that is, into a chaotic, tumbled heap of shattered fragments in which nothing can be told apart. The noun always carries the sense of confusion and mixed-upness: a jumble is not just a pile, it is a pile whose order has been lost.

Primary sense (noun): a confused mixture. The core noun sense is "a disorderly mixture, a muddle": "a jumble of papers on the desk," "a jumble of voices," "a jumble of half-remembered facts." The word is used of physical objects, of sounds, of ideas, and of memories. It stresses that things which perhaps once had order have been shaken, tumbled, or thrown together until no structure remains — the semantic root idea of things "jumbling" against one another. It is a countable noun: one jumble, a jumble, a jumble of things.

Sense 2 (verb): to mix or tumble in disorder. As a verb, to jumble means "to mix up in a confused mass" or "to move about in confusion": "the earthquake jumbled the shelves," "her thoughts jumbled together." The verb is usually transitive (to jumble things) or reciprocal (things jumble together), and it feeds directly into the adjective jumbled ("a jumbled pile," "jumbled thoughts," "jumbled letters"). The physical image is of objects being jostled, tumbled, and rattled against each other until they lose their order — the same tumbling motion that gives the word its sound.

Sense 3 (British): jumble sale and jumble of second-hand things. In British English, jumble has developed a strong domestic sense: a "jumble sale" is a charity sale of second-hand clothes, books, and household oddments, and "jumble" alone can mean the donated clutter sold there. This sense (recorded from the late nineteenth century) grew naturally from "a confused heap of old things" — the sort of pile people donate to a sale, thrown into boxes and church-hall corners. In the US the equivalent event is a rummage sale, and the jumble word-family has stayed distinctly British.

Sense 4 (historical): the jumble cake. An older, now-archaic sense of jumble is a kind of thin, crisp, sweet cake or cookie (also spelled jumbal), recorded in English from the seventeenth century. This is very likely a different word — its origin is uncertain and possibly connected to earlier words for cakes shaped as knots or twists — but it survives in dialect and older cookbooks, a fascinating dead end in the word's history that shows how even a humble word can accumulate accidental senses.

Sense 5 (modern cultural): the Jumble puzzle. Since 1954, Jumble is also the registered name of a famous American newspaper word puzzle in which readers unscramble sets of letters to find a punning answer. The puzzle's name exploits exactly the word's meaning — letters "in a jumble" to be sorted out — and has made jumble familiar to millions who never think of piles of old clothes at all.

Grammar and style. Jumble is a countable noun ("a jumble of...") and a regular verb (jumble, jumbled, jumbling), with the adjective jumbled and the idiomatic phrasal verb jumble up. It is a friendly, everyday, mildly humorous word: unlike chaos, mayhem, or pandemonium, a jumble suggests mess that is disorganised but not terrifying — a muddle rather than a catastrophe. In Tolkien's sentence, though, the word is doing darker work: the ledge vanishes into a jumble of smithereens, a chaos of broken stone whose disorder is total and final, and the friendly word is made to serve annihilation.

Register and collocations. Informal to neutral: "a jumble of," "jumbled up," "in a jumble," "jumble sale," "jumble of ideas/thoughts/memories." The word sits comfortably in conversation about untidy rooms and in journalism about complicated situations alike, and its mildness is what lets writers use it for everything from a child's toy-box to the wreckage left by a dragon.
