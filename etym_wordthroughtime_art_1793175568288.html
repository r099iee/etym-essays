- Old English — everyday grammar: þū eart is the ordinary way to say you are to one person, familiar and unmarked.
- Middle English — thou art settles: Chaucer's pilgrims art each other constantly, while ye are grows polite and plural.
- 16th–17th c. — glory and doom: Shakespeare and the King James Bible lift thou art to poetry's height even as London speech starts dropping thou for universal you.
- 18th c. — the Quaker holdout: almost everyone says you are, but Quakers keep thou (often with are) as plain witness against flattery.
- 19th c. — hymns freeze it: How Great Thou Art and a hundred wedding vows embalm art in worship and romance.
- Today — pure time-travel: art survives only as deliberate archaism, in fantasy like the source sentence, jokes, tattoos, and liturgy.
