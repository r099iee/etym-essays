TOSSING is the present participle of the verb toss, used in the source sentence — from Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince, "The boy was tossing feverishly on his bed, and the mother had fallen asleep, she was so tired" — in its most affecting sense: the restless, convulsive, side-to-side movement of a feverish or suffering sleeper who cannot find rest. The word is at once a verb form, an adjective, and a gerund, and its range of senses radiates from a simple physical core: to throw or fling something lightly and casually upward.


Primary senses of the verb toss.



  • To throw lightly: to fling or pitch something with a quick, easy motion — to toss a ball, toss a coin, toss a coat onto a chair; the defining nuance is lightness and casualness: a toss is deliberate but unlaboured, neither a careful lob nor a violent heave.

  • To move or be moved violently back and forth: of a ship on rough seas ("tossed by the waves"), of a storm-tossed tree, of a body in fever or agitation — the sense of the Wilde sentence, where the sick boy's body is thrown from side to side by the force of his fever.

  • To toss and turn: the fixed phrase for a sleepless, restless night, in which the body turns over repeatedly; "tossing and turning" is the standard collocation of insomnia and anxiety, and the source sentence's "tossing feverishly" is a close cousin of it.

  • To toss one's head: to throw the head back sharply, expressive of disdain, impatience, or haughty dismissal; a horse or a person tosses the head in pride or refusal.

  • To toss a salad: in cookery, to mix salad ingredients by lifting and turning them so the dressing coats every leaf; likewise "to toss pancakes", flipping them in the pan.

  • To toss up / toss-up: to toss a coin is to decide by chance; a "toss-up" is a question too close to call, an even chance — the word appears in sporting, political, and academic idiom for anything decided by luck or nearly balanced.


Secondary and extended senses.



  • In sport, "toss" is an informal synonym for throw — the cricket coin toss, a baseball toss, a rugby line-out throw-in; in gymnastics and dance, "tossing" describes throwing a partner or object into the air.

  • In nautical and weather language, "tossing seas" and "tossed by the gale" describe violent vertical agitation; the idiom "toss the caber" (Scottish Highland Games) names the feat of hurling a heavy tapered log end-over-end, a famous specialisation of the word.

  • In slang, "toss one's cookies" (or "toss one's lunch") means to vomit, and "toss off" means to do something quickly and carelessly, or to drink down in one draught.

  • As an adjective or participle, tossing describes the restless sleeper, the heaving sea, the whipping branch, or a head thrown back: "a tossing night", "tossing waves", "tossing the mane".

  • As a noun (gerund), "a toss" is the act itself — "a toss of the head", "a coin toss", "with a toss of her hair" — and the word also yields the colloquial "toss!", meaning "no matter".


Semantic bridges. Every sense derives from one mental image — a quick upward throw — applied to four domains: (1) real projectiles (balls, coins, cabers); (2) the body's own agitated motion (a head thrown back, a fevered body thrown about, a ship thrown by waves); (3) the culinary flip (salads, pancakes); and (4) chance itself (the coin toss that decides). The register is uniform: toss is informal, kinetic, and slightly playful even in its grim uses — the feverish boy in Wilde's story is "tossing" because his body has become a small storm, thrown about by forces he cannot control.
