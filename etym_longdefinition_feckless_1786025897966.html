FECKLESS is an adjective denoting a person, action, disposition, or undertaking that lacks initiative, strength of character, effectiveness, or a sense of responsibility. The feckless person does not necessarily mean harm — that is what makes the word distinctive — but drifts, defaults, and fails, leaving obligations unmet and consequences unconsidered. The word condemns not malice but incapacity of will: the feckless squander what they have, forget what they owe, and abandon what they start. In the source passage a mother's grief is aimed at "your feckless son," the fecklessness being precisely the reason he has failed or is beyond help — a son too weak-willed, careless, or useless to be relied on.

The word is a Scots formation. It combines feck, an aphetic shortening of effect meaning "force, vigour, value, substance, the greater part," with the privative suffix -less. To be feckless is literally to be "without effect," "devoid of vigour and value." First attested in the 16th century in Lowland Scots, the word entered general English chiefly through Scottish literature and the novels of Sir Walter Scott in the 18th and 19th centuries, spreading as a vivid, half-humorous, half-contemptuous term for helpless incompetence.

Primary and Secondary Senses

  • 1. Lacking initiative and drive (core modern sense). The feckless person has no internal motor: they do not plan, persist, or push things forward. "his feckless management let the firm slide into ruin."
  • 2. Lacking strength of character and a sense of responsibility. Careless of duties, obligations, and consequences; unreliable and prone to defaulting. "a feckless father who never paid child support." This is the sense with moral weight.
  • 3. Ineffective, futile, without practical effect (the original force). Tools, arguments, and efforts that achieve nothing: "feckless attempts to repair the roof." Here the word keeps close to its root meaning, "without effect."
  • 4. Passive and helpless (extended or pitying sense). Lacking the ability to look after oneself or one's affairs — used with rueful sympathy as much as contempt. "the feckless dreamer, always expecting someone else to pay."

Semantic Bridges

The word's force comes from its root. Feck in Scots meant more than "effect": it meant the strength and worth that make an action count — vigour, substance, value, and even the greater part of a thing ("the feck of the work" = the bulk of it). To be feckless was therefore to lack the very substance that gives a life traction. The physical sense of being weak and useless gave way, over the 18th and 19th centuries, to the moral and behavioural sense: a feckless person is not merely weak but improvident, careless, and irresponsible. The shift mirrors a broader cultural movement in which self-discipline and diligence became the cardinal virtues of the industrial age, so that the word for "lacking effect" became the word for "lacking character."

Register and Tone

  • Register: Informal-to-formal, always evaluative. It is a word of character criticism, at home in literature, journalism, and family complaint alike.
  • Intensity: Contemptuous but rarely furious; it condemns weakness rather than wickedness, and so is often used with a sigh rather than a shout.
  • Scots identity: The word remains tinged with its Scottish origins — it carries a slightly dialectal, colloquial warmth even in formal writing.
  • Distinctions: Feckless sits between incompetent (lacking skill), irresponsible (deliberately careless of consequences), and shiftless (lazily without ambition) — but only feckless names the whole defect of character at once.
  • Nominal and adverbial forms: fecklessness (the state) and fecklessly (the adverb); the positive adjective feckful exists in Scots but has never entered general English.
