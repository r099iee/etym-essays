Behind the stately noun opulence stands one of the oldest ideas of the Indo-European world — that wealth is the product of work, and that plenty and power are the same thing. The root is PIE *op-, 'to work, be busy, produce', and the oldest texts of the family bear this out: in the Rigveda, Sanskrit ápas means 'sacred work, the ritual labour of the priests', while ápnas means 'possessions, goods, property' — work and wealth as a single concept. The root appears with the same double face in Iranian (Avestan apah- 'work') and, in a distant but generally accepted set of cognates, across the Indo-European family; the truly productive branch for this word, however, is the Italic one, which carried the root into Latin with exceptional richness.

Latin developed the root in two parallel directions. Toward opus, 'work' — source of operate, opera, opifex 'work-maker' (the exact compound that also produced officium, our office). And toward ops, 'power, might, plenty, wealth, resources'. The second direction gave Latin its great word for abundance: the adjective opulentus, 'rich, wealthy, powerful', built with the suffix -ulentus meaning 'full of' — the same suffix as in turbulentus and succulentus — so that opulentus is literally 'full of plenty'. From opulentus came the abstract noun opulentia, 'wealth, abundance, magnificence', a word of the Roman historians and moralists, used of the riches of cities, provinces and empires. The goddess Ops herself, wife of Saturn and patroness of the harvest, embodied the same equation of plenty and power, and her festival, the Opalia, was one of the great ceremonies of the Roman year.

The root's collateral descendants show how wide its net was. From ops Latin formed copia, 'abundance, plenty', literally 'a bringing together of plenty' (co- + ops); from copia English got copious, 'abundant', and — through a famous medieval shift — copy, since a 'copy' was originally the abundant stock, the exemplar, from which transcriptions were taken. Cornucopia, the horn of plenty, joins cornu 'horn' to the same copia. The negative inops, 'resourceless, poor', and its noun inopia, 'poverty, scarcity', complete the pair: the family can express both the fullness and the emptiness of resources. Where opulentia is wealth at its fullest, inopia is want at its barest — the same root measuring both poles of fortune.

Old French transmitted the noun as opulence 'wealth', and English borrowed it in the sixteenth century together with the adjective opulent. For the first two centuries of its English life the word kept the Latin sense of power and resources: the 'opulence' of a state meant its strength and plenty, not its chandeliers. The eighteenth-century historians — Gibbon, and then Macaulay — deployed it exactly so: Macaulay's 'her opulence and her martial glory grew together' treats opulence as the material engine of national power, the twin of military fame, springing from the 'auspicious union of order and freedom' after 1688. In the nineteenth century, in the age of great fortunes, plate-glass and gilded interiors, the word tilted decisively toward display: opulence became the name for sumptuous, ostentatious, on-view wealth, often viewed with a faintly admiring or faintly critical eye. That is the modern flavour — luxury flaunted — though the older sense of sheer national might survives in historical prose.

  • Literary attestations. Livy and Pliny for Latin opulentia; Shakespeare's opulent (early 1600s); Gibbon and Macaulay for the Augustan national sense; 19th-century novelists (Dickens, Thackeray, James) for the display sense.
  • Socio-cultural factors. The semantic drift from 'power' to 'display' tracks the rise of consumer capitalism: wealth became visible, domestic and interiorised in the 19th-century home and the 20th-century luxury market, so that a word that once described the strength of an empire now describes the splendour of a drawing-room.
  • Sound changes. Negligible across the chain — the Latin -ent-/-entia forms passed nearly unchanged through French into English; the story is almost entirely semantic, the same root supplying both the 'power' of the state and the 'show' of the palace.
  • Semantic summary. 'work' → 'plenty, power' (Latin ops) → 'full of plenty' (opulentus) → 'wealth, magnificence' (opulentia) → 'national abundance' (English, 17th–18th c.) → 'lavish display' (English, 19th c. onwards).
