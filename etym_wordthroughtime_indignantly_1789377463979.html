- Classical Latin (Antiquity) - Rooted in the verb *indignārī* ("to deem unworthy, to be offended/provoked"), representing the Roman ethical framework of aristocratic honor and rhetorical passion (*indignatio*).
- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Era / Renaissance) - Borrowed into English as the adjective *indignant* and adverb *indignantly*, appearing in historical chronicles and translations to depict noble wrath against tyranny or treason.
- 17th to 18th Century (Augustan & Enlightenment Literature) - Widely employed in neoclassical poetry and satire (John Milton, Jonathan Swift, Alexander Pope) to voice righteous moral castigation of political corruption and societal hypocrisy.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Thoroughly naturalized in Victorian narrative prose (Charles Dickens, William Makepeace Thackeray, Oscar Wilde), functioning both as an emblem of authentic social protest on behalf of the poor and as a comic instrument to mock narcissistic vanity.
- 20th to 21st Century (Modern & Contemporary Usage) - Prevalent in investigative journalism, human rights advocacy, psychological discourse, and everyday speech to describe swift, principled resistance to injustice, discrimination, or personal slights.
