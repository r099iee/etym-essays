Unremitting means not remitting — not letting up, slackening, pardoning, or sending back. In ordinary use it describes anything that continues without pause, rest, or decrease in intensity: unremitting pain, unremitting rain, unremitting effort, unremitting attention. Your context’s “unremitting abstraction” is characteristic: abstraction that never relents, never softens into the concrete signals of everyday life, and therefore feels lonely and exhausting.

The force of the word comes from the buried metaphor of remit: Latin remittere meant to send back, release, relax, or forgive. To remit a debt is to send it back cancelled; to remit a punishment is to release it; to remit one’s efforts is to slacken them. Unremitting denies all of these at once: nothing is sent back, nothing is forgiven, nothing is loosened. That is why it suits both outer persistence (“unremitting bombardment”) and inner rigour (“unremitting logic”).

  • Ceaseless / incessant (temporal): lasting without interruption in time; e.g. unremitting noise through the night. The bridge is negation of remit as “to pause or abate,” so unremitting time never abates.
  • Relentless / unrelenting (intensive): refusing to soften in degree; e.g. unremitting pressure, unremitting scrutiny. The bridge is negation of remit as “to forgive or pardon,” so unremitting force never pardons.
  • Sustained / untiring (agentive): describing a person’s dedication that does not flag; e.g. “her unremitting care for her mother.” The bridge is negation of remit as “to slacken one’s exertion,” so the agent never slackens.
  • Financial / legal fossil: in older use, debts or sentences “unremitted” were unforgiven; unremitting preserves this theology of forgiveness in secular form — to be unremitting is to grant no remission.
  • Medical / meteorological: unremitting fever, unremitting drizzle — symptoms or weather that show no remission; here medicine’s technical term remission (abatement of disease) is directly negated.

Usage notes: unremitting is a formal, slightly literary adjective, stronger and more inexorable than continuous and more dignified than nonstop. It typically modifies abstract or mass nouns — effort, attention, hostility, rain, pain — rather than countable events. It is gradable only awkwardly (“very unremitting” is rare); its absoluteness is part of its rhetoric. The adverb is unremittingly and the abstract noun unremittingness, though both are less common than the adjective itself. In your sentence the adjective modifies abstraction with surgical precision: not merely abstract thought, but abstraction that never descends, never illustrates, never rests — and therefore drives readers back toward shapes, sounds, and gestures as relief from its beautiful but airless constancy. Collocationally it favours nouns of pressure and patience alike — unremitting efforts, unremitting criticism, unremitting gloom, unremitting vigilance — and in each case the compliment or complaint is the same: whatever is so described does not know how to stop, and will still be there tomorrow.
