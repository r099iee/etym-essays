- 8th c. BCE - In Homeric epic (The Odyssey), celebrated as an unproblematic, valiant hero whose slaying of Aegisthus is held up by the gods to Telemachus as an inspiring model of filial duty and honor.
- 5th c. BCE - In classical Athenian tragedy (Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides), deeply complicated into a tortured protagonist torn between divine command and horrific matricide, pursued by the Furies and rescued through the birth of institutional Athenian law.
- Roman Imperial & Renaissance Eras - Reimagined in Seneca, Virgil, and early modern drama as an archetype of harrowing guilt, ancestral curse, and theatrical madness.
- 19th & Early 20th c. - Reinterpreted by Goethe (Iphigenia in Tauris), Nietzsche, and Sartre (The Flies) as a symbol of existential responsibility, individual liberation from superstition, and the agonizing weight of moral autonomy.
- Mid-20th c. to Present - Coined in psychoanalysis (the 'Orestes complex' by Wertham) for unconscious maternal resentment, while continuing in literary theory and legal philosophy as the archetypal bridge from private blood feud to civilized rule of law.
