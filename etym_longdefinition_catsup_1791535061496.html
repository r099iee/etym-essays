catsup (noun / verb)

1. Primary Culinary Sense: Spiced Tomato Table Condiment
A thick, smooth, seasoned table condiment and dipping sauce prepared primarily from concentrated tomato purée, distilled vinegar, sweeteners (such as sucrose, high-fructose corn syrup, or cane sugar), salt, and a proprietary blend of aromatic spices and herbs (typically including allspice, cinnamon, cloves, onion powder, celery seed, and garlic). Characterized by its vibrant red hue, high viscosity, and distinctively balanced sweet-and-sour flavor profile with deep umami undertones, it is traditionally served cold or at ambient temperature as an accompaniment to fried, roasted, or grilled foods—most notably french fries, hamburgers, hot dogs, meatloaf, and hash browns. In modern American food law (specifically under FDA Standards of Identity, 21 CFR § 155.194), the terms catsup, ketchup, and catchup are designated as legally synonymous, governing a product that must meet strict density, soluble solid, and acid thresholds without artificial colorings or thickeners.

2. Polysemy, Historical Senses, and Domain Usages

  • Historical Non-Tomato Culinary Condiment (Georgian & Victorian Kitchen Liquor): Long before tomatoes were accepted as edible in the Western world, catsup designated an entire class of thin, shelf-stable, highly seasoned seasoning extracts produced by salting, fermenting, and boiling various savory bases. Prominent historical varieties included:
    • Mushroom Catsup: A dark, savory, thin liquor created by salting fresh field mushrooms to draw out their moisture, boiling the pressed liquid with allspice, black pepper, and mace, and bottling it as an all-purpose umami enhancer. It served as the primary savory liquid seasoning of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century English and American kitchens, functioning essentially as modern soy sauce or Worcestershire sauce.
    • Walnut Catsup: A pungent, dark condiment prepared from crushed green, unripe English walnuts steeped in spiced vinegar, wine, or brine, revered by historical cooks like Hannah Glasse and Isabella Beeton for enriching game, beef stews, and brown gravies.
    • Seafood & Marine Catsups: Early maritime varieties brewed from pickled anchovies, oysters, or fish brine, mixed with shallots, white wine, and cloves, representing the direct Western imitation of East Asian fish sauces.
  • Verbal Usage ('to catsup'): Used transitively in informal or culinary contexts to mean smothering, seasoning, or dressing food lavishly with catsup (e.g., 'he catsupped his burger before taking a bite'), often implying the masking or overwhelming of an inferior dish's natural flavor through excessive condiment application.
  • Commercial and Dialectal Branding Identity: Within commercial marketing and regional American English, catsup was historically the preferred trade spelling adopted by major packaging corporations—most famously the Del Monte Corporation, which labeled its flagship product 'Tomato Catsup' until 1988, as well as Hunt's in specific geographic distributions. While identical in chemical formulation to ketchup, catsup retains a cultural nuance of mid-century Americana, diner nostalgia, and distinct midwestern or southern regional preference.
  • Metaphorical and Idiomatic Usages: In theatrical, film, and cinematic jargon, catsup (or ketchup) has long served as an informal metonym for theatrical fake blood used in low-budget stage productions or classic slapstick and grindhouse films ('the fight scene was drowning in cheap catsup'). Culturally, it also serves as an emblem of industrial standardization, working-class culinary convenience, and democratic mass consumption.

3. Semantic Bridges and Evolutionary Logic
The historical bridge connecting an ancient Chinese fermented fish sauce (kê-tsiap) to an American bottle of thick tomato sauce lies in the European quest for shelf-stable umami. Seventeenth-century British sailors in Southeast Asia prized native fermented sauces because they preserved vital savory qualities across grueling multi-year voyages. When English cooks attempted to replicate this imported seasoning at home without native Asian ingredients, they turned to indigenous mushrooms and green walnuts that yielded similarly dark, fermented, glutinous juices. When the tomato—native to the Americas and belonging to the nightshade family—gradually overcame its European reputation as a poisonous ornamental plant in the early nineteenth century, American cooks recognized its high natural glutamic acid and pectin content. By simmering tomato pulp with vinegar and sugar, they created a stable, sweet-and-sour emulsion that preserved tomatoes through the winter. This American innovation proved so universally appealing that it eclipsed all earlier mushroom and walnut formulations, permanently redefining the word catsup from a thin, salty fermentation liquor into a thick, sweet tomato purée.
