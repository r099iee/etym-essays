Souping is the present participle / gerund of the hot-rod and custom-culture phrasal verb soup up — to tune, boost, or otherwise modify an engine (and by extension any machine, system, or thing) for greater power, speed, or showiness than stock. In the source sentence — “souping it up, ‘personalizing’ it” — teenagers tinker with the family car, raising compression, fitting louder exhausts and wilder paint, turning uniform factory product into personal statement.

The spelling souping (with -ing) covers both the progressive (“they were souping the Chevy all summer”) and the nominal (“souping was his religion”). American orthography also writes souping-up or souping it up; the particle up marks completion and intensification, as in soup something up.

  • Core automotive sense: to hop up, hot-rod, or tune an engine — boring out cylinders, adding superchargers or dual carbs, advancing timing — so a sluggish sedan becomes a street racer. “A souped-up Ford” is the canonical collocation from the 1940s–1960s.
  • Generalized mechanical sense: to upgrade any device beyond specification — soup up a moped, a lawnmower, a computer — by swapping parts, overclocking, or removing governors. Implies shade-tree ingenuity rather than factory engineering.
  • Figurative intensifying sense: to enliven, exaggerate, or sex up anything non-mechanical — to soup up a story, a résumé, a party, an advertising campaign — by adding punch, color, or scandal. Here it overlaps with jazz up, hype up, sex up, but keeps a greasy-hands, masculine tang.
  • Reflexive / middle use: intransitive or adjectival — “the car soups up nicely,” “a souped-up plot” — where the result (louder, faster, flashier) matters more than the spanner work.
  • Noun-adjacent gerund: souping as subculture activity — night-time garage work, drag-strip preparation, cruising ritual — connoting adolescence, rebellion, and decorative play rather than transport need.

Why soup should mean power is famously murky, which is part of the word’s charm. Two rival stories compete: (a) the horse-doping theory — early-20th-century American slang used soup for a narcotic or stimulant injection given to racehorses (to make them run faster than honest), transferred to fuel additives and engine work; (b) the super-theory — supercharger clipped and reshaped to super → souper → soup in racing patter, so “souped up” originally meant “fitted with a supercharger.” Both have period plausibility; neither is proven, and most dictionaries present the origin as uncertain with these two candidates. The “soup as fuel-mixture” folk gloss — pouring in rich mixture like soup — is a later rationalization.

Connotation is affectionate-mischievous: resourceful, loud, slightly illicit, adolescent-masculine. To soup something up is to refuse the manufacturer’s intentions, to choose noise over silence and show over restraint — exactly the “decorative play” the essay contrasts with practical purpose. Register is informal American, standard in journalism since the 1950s (“souped-up economy,” “souped-up prosecution”). Synonyms shade by domain: hot-rod, hop up, tune, mod, pimp (out), trick out, juice up, beef up for machines; spice up, jazz up, hype for stories.
