- 8th-9th c. - pund enters written English in laws and charters as a weight of silver and a legal unit of account; it sounds official, Roman and Christian rather than folkloric.
- Late 9th-11th c. - The Danelaw and the unified English kingdom fix the pound in royal law; it becomes a unit of tribute, taxation and minting, associated with royal authority.
- 12th-14th c. - Middle English pound becomes the everyday mercantile word; local pounds proliferate and the word acquires a practical, commercial, slightly variable flavour.
- 14th-16th c. - Tower and avoirdupois systems compete; "pound" is increasingly a technical term of trade, while the monetary pound stabilises at 240 pence.
- 17th-19th c. - The pound sterling becomes a global currency of empire and trade; "pound" carries prestige, solidity and British financial power.
- 20th c. - Devaluation, decimalisation (1971) and inflation strip the pound of some mystique; "pound" becomes a mundane unit again, though "sterling" retains diplomatic weight.
- 21st c. - "Pound" survives in metric Britain as a stubborn everyday measure, in "pound shops", in psi and pound-force, and in idiom; it feels traditional, slightly nostalgic and distinctly British.
