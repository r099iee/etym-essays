Suflice represents the exact source-text orthographic entry for the verb suffice (intransitive and transitive verb; /səˈfaɪs/ or /səˈfɪs/), meaning to be enough, adequate, competent, or satisfactory to fulfill a given requirement, end, standard, or purpose. While the entry preserves the verbatim spelling suflice as supplied in the note data (an OCR or typographical artifact where the ligature 'ff' was misread or transcribed with an 'l'), the lexicographical and semantic reality of the lemma is that of standard English suffice. In both philosophical and everyday usage, the verb marks the precise threshold of adequacy—signifying that a condition, resource, argument, or quantity satisfies a demand without necessitating excess.

The verb operates within an ancient intellectual framework distinguishing necessary conditions from sufficient conditions. To state that something does not suffice—as Rudolf Arnheim writes in the context passage: "The mere exposure to masterworks does not suffice"—is to assert that while exposure may be beneficial or even necessary, it falls short of achieving the desired outcome (in this case, cultivating genuine visual literacy and artistic understanding) unless supplemented by active, disciplined analysis.

Polysemy and Modern Specialized Senses:

  • Intransitive Adequacy and Threshold Competence: In its primary modern sense, suffice signifies being quantitatively or qualitatively adequate to answer a need, purpose, or occasion (e.g., "two hours will suffice to complete the exam"; "modest resources sufficed for their survival"). It emphasizes sufficiency rather than abundance.
  • The Idiomatic / Impersonal Formula ("Suffice it to say..."): In rhetorical and literary discourse, the phrase "suffice it to say" (an elliptical subjunctive construction meaning "let it suffice to say") is employed by speakers and writers to indicate that a full, exhaustive explanation is being deliberately omitted because a concise summary is adequate for the listener's understanding.
  • Formal Subjunctive and Impersonal Constructions: In elevated prose, the verb frequently takes an impersonal subject with a sentential complement: "it suffices that..." or "it suffices to note that...", serving as an economical transition in legal briefs, mathematical proofs, and philosophical treatises.
  • Transitive Sense (Archaic / Elevated): Historically, suffice functioned as a transitive verb meaning to satisfy, content, or supply the needs of someone (e.g., "Lord, show us the Father, and it sufficeth us", John 14:8). While largely archaic in casual modern speech, this transitive usage persists in poetic, liturgical, and classic literature.
  • Logic, Mathematics, and Formal Analytics: In formal deductive logic, mathematical analysis, and computer science, the concept of sufficiency is formalized: condition A is sufficient for condition B if the truth of A guarantees the truth of B. While mathematics typically employs the adjectival form sufficient, analytical prose frequently utilizes the verbal predicate (e.g., "to establish convergence, it will suffice to demonstrate bounded monotonicity").
  • Typographical and Lexical Status of Suflice: In historical bibliography and digital corpus linguistics, forms like suflice represent classic OCR (Optical Character Recognition) misreadings or 16th–18th century ligature typesetting errors, where the historical long 's' (ſ) combined with 'f' or 'i' (ſf, ffi, fl) caused scanning software to misinterpret 'ff' as 'fl'. Preserving the verbatim headword satisfies strict textual-reconstruction standards while illuminating the underlying standard lemma.

Semantic Evolution and Historical Bridges:

The semantic lineage of the word derives from Latin sufficere, a compound of sub- ("under, from beneath, secretly, in support") and facere ("to make, do"). The original physical meaning in early Rome was "to put or build under, to lay a foundation from beneath, to substitute, or to supply from below." From this tactile notion of supplying materials from beneath to support an overarching structure, Latin developed the abstract sense of providing an adequate supply, being equal to a task, or meeting an emergency. Old French inherited this as suffire, developing an inflected stem suffiss- that Middle English borrowed in the 14th century as suffisen, ultimately stabilizing in Early Modern English as suffice.
