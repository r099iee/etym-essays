The evolution of allay begins with a hand setting something down. Its deepest root is PIE *legh-, to lie, to settle, the great resting root that also gives English lie, lay, lair, and litter. Germanic built upon it a causative verb, *lagjaną, to cause to lie — to lay — and Old English prefixed it with a-, away or down, to make alecgan: to lay down, to put away, to diminish what stands raised.

In Old and early Middle English the verb still shows its hands. One aleys a burden from the shoulder, a weapon from the fist, anger from the breast. Feeling is figured throughout as a thing that rises — swelling dread, mounting wrath, climbing fever — and remedy is the reverse motion, laying flat again what reared up. The metaphor is so bodily that early readers need no bridge: the storm laid, the dust laid, the ghost laid, the fear allayed are one gesture in different weathers.

By the fourteenth century the spelling drifts toward aleyen and then allayen, pulled by the orbit of plain lay, and the sense narrows toward its modern dignity: to quell, to pacify, to relieve. Poets and prayer-makers adopt it for the heaviest comforts — tumults allayed, torments allayed, thirsts allayed — precisely because the word promises mastery without violence. To allay is never to crush; the trouble is persuaded, not broken, into rest.

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries tangle the spelling with a French stranger. Old French alaiier, to combine metals, gives English alloy and a second verb allay, to temper gold with baser metal. The two verbs share letters and nothing else — the one lays down, the other lays together — yet printers and scribes confuse them for a hundred years. Lexicographers ever since print the warning as a duty: the comforter from Saxon lay, the temperer from French mixture, homographs by accident.

Phonetically the journey is the familiar southern levelling: Old English alecgan with its hard guttural softens through Middle English palatalisation to aleyen, then the Great Vowel Shift and spelling standardisation fix allay with the same vowel as lay and day. The double l is the fossil of the prefix plus stem, al- meeting -lay, kept by printers long after speech had simplified it.

Stages and bridges at a glance:

  • Resting to laying: PIE lying becomes Germanic causing-to-lie, rest turned into the act that grants rest.
  • Laying to laying-down: the Old English prefix aims the gesture downward, turning placement into appeasement.
  • Hands to hearts: burdens and weapons laid down become fears and fevers laid down, the bodily metaphor carrying the whole emotional career.
  • Quelling to comforting: medieval pacification softens into modern relief, force yielding to persuasion across five centuries of sermons and sickrooms.
  • Confusion to clarification: the alloy homograph tangles then untangles, leaving dictionaries to guard the boundary between laying down and laying together.
  • Eloquence to silence in Tolkien: the source sentence stages the word's ultimate test, where even Saruman's voice, the great allayer of counsels, cannot lay a fear that has passed into knowledge.

So allay today is the gentleman of the calming verbs: older than calm in this sense, gentler than quell, more dignified than ease, carrying twelve centuries of laid-down burdens inside every reassurance it offers.
