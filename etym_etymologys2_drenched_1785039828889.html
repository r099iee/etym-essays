PIE root *dhreg- "to draw, drag; drink" (hypothesized; exact shape and distribution debated — see note below) → Proto-Germanic *drinkaną "to drink" (strong class III verb; c. 500 BCE–500 CE; the ancestor of Old English drincan, Old Norse drekka, Gothic drigkan, Old High German trinkan) → Proto-Germanic *drankijaną "to cause to drink, to give drink to, to steep, to drench" (causative formation built from *drinkaną by vowel mutation, the same pattern as sit/set, fall/fell) → Old English drencan "to give drink to, make drunk, soak, saturate, submerge, drown" (attested from the 8th century) → Middle English drenchen "to submerge, sink, drown; to soak" (c. 1200) → Early Modern English drench "to wet thoroughly by throwing liquid over" (c. 1550) → Modern English drenched "soaked, saturated, overwhelmed by liquid" (past participle of drench) Note on the debate: A secure Indo-European ancestry for the Germanic drinking root is not established. The widely repeated suggestion of PIE *dhreg- "to draw, drink" is speculative — the root has no certain cognates outside Germanic — and some authorities leave *drinkaną as a word of unclear origin, possibly related to a nasal-infixed formation from a root meaning "to draw in" (compare Latin dregum "lees," Old Irish derc "eye"). The causative *drankijaną, by contrast, is fully attested across Germanic (Old Norse drekkja "to give to drink, to drown," Gothic dragkjan "to give to drink," Old High German trenken "to water," Dutch drenken, German tränken) and is a Germanic innovation rather than an inherited PIE formation.
