- (c. 1000–) - Old English dell: a plain word for a hollow, pit, or deep place in the ground — no romance yet, just topography, recorded mostly in glosses.
- (1200s–1500s) - Middle English del(le): "a small valley, a dell" — the word settles into its modern shape as a minor but ordinary word for valley land, a sibling of dale.
- (1500s–1600s) - Pastoral poetry adopts it: Elizabethan verse makes the dell a fixture of the idyllic landscape — the word begins to glow with sheltered rural charm.
- (1600s–1700s) - The idyllic register hardens: in pastoral and elegy the dell is the green retreat of shepherds, lovers and mourners; its perception shifts from neutral "hollow" to "pleasant hidden valley".
- (1800s) - Victorian nature poetry and travel prose cement the word's archaic-idyllic tone; in speech it retreats, surviving in dialect and in place names (the Wisconsin Dells).
- (1937) - Tolkien founds Rivendell ("riven + dell", translating Elvish Imladris "deep dale of the cleft"): the dell becomes the archetypal fantasy refuge — the hidden valley of safety — and gains a new global audience.
- (today) - In ordinary speech dell survives mainly in place names and in literature, where it still means "a small, sheltered, green valley" — a word of pastoral peace, slightly old-fashioned on purpose.
