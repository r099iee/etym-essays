• Galloped: Similar because it describes a horse running fast; differs because galloping is a controlled, rhythmic gait the rider chooses, whereas bolting is a sudden, terror-driven breakaway beyond the rider’s control.
  • Ran away: Similar because it denotes fleeing at speed; differs because “ran away” is the general, colourless term for any hasty departure, while “bolted” specifies a sudden, explosive, unstoppable rush, especially of an animal.
  • Fled: Similar because it means to have escaped by running; differs because “fled” usually implies a deliberate decision to escape (often from danger or capture), whereas bolting is instinctive, panicked, and out of conscious control.
  • Stampeded: Similar because it is a sudden, uncontrollable rush of animals in panic; differs because a stampede involves a whole herd surging together, while a single animal bolts alone.
  • Spooked: Similar because it names the state of a startled animal (a “spooked” horse shies and may bolt); differs because “spooked” describes the frightened state or the sudden start, not the resulting full-speed flight itself.
  • Shied / shied away: Similar because a startled horse “shies,” jumping sideways in alarm; differs because shying is a sudden defensive jerk or swerve, not a headlong running-off; a horse may shy and then bolt, but the words name different moments.
  • Dashed: Similar because it means to move with sudden speed; differs because “dashed” is used of deliberate, energetic movement by a person (or a “dash” to a place), without the image of panic or loss of control.
  • Sprinted: Similar because it denotes running at maximum speed; differs because sprinting is a deliberate athletic effort under control, whereas bolting is involuntary and unstoppable.
  • Charged: Similar because it denotes a sudden, forceful forward rush; differs because charging implies a deliberate attack or onslaught toward a target, whereas bolting is blind flight away from something.
  • Made a break for it: Similar because it describes a sudden escape attempt; differs because it implies a conscious plan (a prisoner or person choosing the moment to flee), not the panic reflex of an animal.
  • Took off: Similar because it means to leave suddenly and quickly; differs because “took off” is colloquial and general (of people, events, careers), lacking the specifically equine panic sense of bolted.
  • Absconded: Similar because it means to have fled secretly or suddenly, especially to avoid justice; differs because absconding is a calculated, secretive flight, the opposite of the public, explosive panic of a bolt.
