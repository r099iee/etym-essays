ATTENUATED descends through a single Latin family built on the root of ‘thin’; the chain runs from the Indo-European base to the modern adjective. CHAIN — ‘thin / stretch’ element: - PIE *ten- ‘to stretch, extend, draw out’ (reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Latin tenuis ‘thin, fine, slender, delicate’ — literally ‘stretched out, drawn fine’ (attested from the 1st c. BCE) → Latin tenuāre ‘to make thin, reduce, weaken’ — a denominative verb built on tenuis (attested from the 1st c. BCE, e.g. in Lucretius) → Latin attenuāre ‘to make thin, lessen, weaken, reduce’ — a compound of ad- ‘to, toward’ + tenuāre, with the d of the prefix assimilated to the following t (attested from the 1st c. BCE onward, e.g. in Cicero) → Latin attenuātus (past participle of attenuāre) ‘made thin, weakened, reduced’ (classical) → English attenuate (verb) ‘to make thin, weaken, reduce in force’ (first attested c. 1520, borrowed from the Latin participle; possibly via Middle French atténuer) → English attenuated (past participle and adjective) ‘thinned, weakened, reduced in force, intensity, or thickness’ (adjective from the 1590s) → ATTENUATED NOTE ON DEBATE: the exact route into English is slightly uncertain — the verb could have been borrowed directly from Latin attenuātus or mediated by Middle French atténuer (attested at about the same date), and OED notes both possibilities; the adjective attenuated is securely attested in English from the 1590s. The classical verb attenuāre is itself straightforwardly built from tenuis, but the prehistoric shape of the root *ten- (with possible laryngeal effects in the full grade) is reconstructed rather than attested. The modern scientific senses (attenuation of signals, attenuated vaccines) are semantic extensions made within English and its European sister languages in the 19th–20th centuries, not part of the Latin chain itself.
