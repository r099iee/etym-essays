- 16th Century (c. 1540s–1570s) - Coined in Early Modern English as an expressive, onomatopoeic verb and noun mimicking the whistling, rushing noise of arrows, crossbow bolts, or cannonballs flying through the air.
- 17th to 18th Century - Solidified across military, nautical, and descriptive literature; writers used *whizz* to evoke the deadly acoustic proximity of projectiles in siege warfare and violent storms whistling through ship rigging.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Broadened to describe the exciting, non-lethal velocity of steam engines, mechanical spinning looms, and civil pyrotechnics; Oscar Wilde deployed the isolated exclamation *Whizz!* in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888) to herald the dazzling ascent of fireworks.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Developed a major colloquial offshoot in American English: shortened to *whiz*, it came to signify a virtuoso, child prodigy, or master of intellectual craft ("a math whiz"), derived from the lightning speed of their cognitive processing.
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Extended into domestic culinary technology; "to whizz" became the universal British and Commonwealth culinary verb for running ingredients through an electric food processor or blender.
- 21st Century - Retains universal vitality across all registers: as an auditory onomatopoeia for high-speed flight, a colloquial term for technical brilliance, and a playful exclamation of sudden motion.
