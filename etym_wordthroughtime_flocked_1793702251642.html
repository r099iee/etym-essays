- Old English (c. 800-1150) - Noun (animals) - The earliest attested use of 'flock' referred primarily to a group of animals, especially sheep or birds. This usage was deeply embedded in the pastoral and agrarian societies of the time, reflecting the daily realities of animal husbandry and observation of nature. The connotation was neutral, descriptive of a natural grouping.
- Middle English (c. 1150-1500) - Noun (people, religious) - During this period, the metaphorical extension of 'flock' to human groups, particularly religious congregations under a spiritual leader, became prominent. This shift reflected the growing influence of Christianity and the analogy of the 'Good Shepherd' tending his 'flock.' The connotation here became more communal, protective, and spiritual.
- Early Modern English (c. 1500-1700) - Verb (movement) - The verb 'to flock' emerged, directly derived from the noun, signifying the act of gathering or moving in a large group. This development allowed for a dynamic description of collective human movement, often driven by common interest or attraction. The connotation remained largely neutral, descriptive of collective action.
- 18th-19th Centuries - Noun (material) & Verb (application) - A distinct, unrelated sense of 'flock' as a fibrous material (e.g., waste from wool processing) and the corresponding verb 'to flock' (to apply this material to a surface) developed. This usage arose from industrial processes and textile manufacturing, representing a significant semantic divergence from the original animal/human group meanings. The connotation here is purely technical and descriptive of a manufacturing process.
- Modern English (20th Century-Present) - All senses active - All primary senses of 'flock' (animal group, human group, collective movement, and the material/application) continue to be used. The verb 'to flock' for collective human movement remains very common, often used to describe crowds drawn to events, places, or ideas. The connotation is generally neutral, though it can sometimes imply a somewhat unthinking or herd-like behavior when applied to people, depending on context.
