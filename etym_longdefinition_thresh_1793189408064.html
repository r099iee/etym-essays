Thresh is, in its primary and founding sense, the verb for beating harvested grain — wheat, barley, oats, rye — so as to separate the edible kernels from the husks, chaff, and straw that surround them. It is one of humanity's oldest food-processing verbs, naming the indispensable middle step between harvesting a crop and milling flour: the sheaves are cut, then threshed, then winnowed, then ground.

In the source sentence the word appears in exactly this ancient agricultural sense. The horse lays out the whole bread-making chain as a boastful measure of time: sow the wheat, wait till it is grown, harvest and thresh it, grind the grain, bake five ovens of bread — only then, says the horse, could his rival overtake the quarry. The verb sits at the hinge of the sequence, the hard physical labour between field and mill, and the fable uses the slowness of the whole process to make its comic point about speed.

Core meaning in detail: Traditional threshing meant spreading sheaves on a hard threshing floor and beating them with a jointed hand-flail, or driving oxen or a stone roller over them, until the grains shook loose from the ears. The loosened mixture was then tossed into the wind (winnowing), which carried off the light chaff and left the heavy grain. The modern combine harvester still performs all three operations — reaping, threshing, and winnowing — inside one machine: a rotating drum rubs the ears against a concave grate, threshing the grain mechanically at acres per hour. So the verb survives fully alive in modern farming, even though the flail is now museum furniture.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To beat grain from husks (primary sense, Old English onward): ‘to thresh wheat’. This is the sense every dictionary lists first and the one the fable uses — heavy, rhythmic, dusty work, the sound of flails giving the verb its onomatopoeic snap.
  • To beat or strike repeatedly in general (extended sense, shared with its doublet thrash): ‘threshing flails’, ‘the branches threshed the windows’. The bridge is the motion itself: once a word means rhythmic pounding of sheaves, it naturally extends to any repeated striking, and in this sense thresh and thrash blur into one word.
  • To toss or flail about violently (intransitive, of limbs and bodies): ‘the swimmer threshed about in the water’, ‘threshing in his sleep’. The bridge runs from the flail's wild arc to any wild thrashing movement — grain, arms, legs, and whole bodies all move the same way under the same verb.
  • To thresh out — to discuss exhaustively until resolved (phrasal, 19th c.): ‘to thresh out a problem’, ‘thresh it out in committee’. The bridge is metaphorical but vivid: just as threshing beats the grain until every kernel is out of the husk, so a committee beats a question until every grain of truth is out of the chaff of talk.
  • To re-thresh old straw — to rehash stale material (figurative): ‘threshing over old straw’. The bridge is the farmer's contempt for going over already-threshed sheaves: beating straw that has no grain left is the perfect image of pointless repetition.
  • Thresh as noun (rare, archaic): a threshing, or the separated grain itself. The bridge is simple conversion of verb to noun, now almost wholly replaced by threshing and thrash in this role.

Register and connotation: thresh is the plain, workmanlike twin of the pair; thrash took the wilder senses (beatings, defeats, rock music), while thresh kept the farm. It smells of barns, dust, and harvest — honest labour rather than violence. Its figurative child thresh out belongs to committee rooms and newsrooms, patient and thorough where hash out is hurried. Note too the famous cousin threshold, whose first element is very likely this same threshing/treading word: the threshold is the boards where grain was threshed or trodden at the granary door — the farm's hardest-wearing floor giving its name to every doorway in the language.
