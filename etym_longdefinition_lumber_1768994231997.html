In the phrase "the air smells of salt and newsawn lumber," the word carries the raw materiality of a working port. The smell of freshly cut wood — newsawn, damp, resinous — is set against the salt of the sea, and together they compose the atmosphere of a frontier economy: salt is the world of arrival, trade, and the ocean; lumber is the world of building, expansion, and the land being settled. The tone is sensuous and economic at once, the sentence registering the port through its odors rather than its sights, and the freshness of "newsawn" makes the lumber vivid — not a finished plank in a shop but wood just cut, still carrying the forest into the town. In a narrative of a boy moving through southern nights, the word quietly signals the world he is entering: a place of piers, planks, and construction, of rough labor and raw growth.

Primary sense: lumber (American English) is timber that has been sawed into boards, planks, and beams for sale and construction — the commercial building material of the New World, priced by the board-foot and stacked in yards.

Polysemy and related senses:

  • Regional distinction: In American usage wood is the general material (natural or worked), timber often means standing trees or unprocessed logs, and lumber is the sawn, saleable product. In British English, timber often does the work that American lumber does, and "wood" remains the everyday word; lumber in Britain historically meant disused furniture and stored junk.
  • Industry vocabulary: lumber yard, lumber mill/sawmill, lumberjack (the logger), lumber industry, lumber trade, lumberman; types: rough-cut lumber, dimension lumber, seasoned lumber, green lumber.
  • The verb: to lumber "to cut and market timber" (American), and the separate homophone to lumber along "to move heavily and clumsily" — a different word of obscure origin, meaning the mass and weight, not the wood.
  • Historical clutter sense: Middle English lumber (1550s) meant "disused household goods, pawned junk, stored clutter," and a lumber room was the attic or cellar where such cast-offs were heaped. This older sense survives in British English and is the semantic bridge to the American timber sense.
  • Figurative uses: "to lumber someone" (in British and Australian idiom) is to burden them with an unwanted task or responsibility; "to lumber a place" is to fill it with useless weight.

Synonym boundaries:

  • Wood: the general natural or worked material, no commercial sense; lumber is wood as a trade good.
  • Timber: in Britain roughly hewn or structural wood; in America standing trees or unprocessed logs; lumber is the sawn boards cut from them.
  • Planks / boards: the individual sawn pieces, not the commodity heaped in yards.
  • Logs: raw trunks before sawing.
  • Beams: large structural members, a category within lumber rather than a synonym for it.

Register: commercial and industrial — the vocabulary of sawmills, yards, railways, and the great log drives of the Pacific Northwest. In the sentence it is the precise American word for the raw material of settlement, carrying the whole economic history of the frontier in a single smell.
