WELL-FLOGGED is a compound of two independent elements, each with its own derivation; the chains are given in isolation below.

--- ROOT 1: WELL ---
PIE *wel- / *welh₁- "to wish, to want, to will" (reconstructed root of the will/wish family; c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE) → Proto-Germanic *wel- / *wela- "as desired, well, in a good manner" (adverbial formation from the wish-root; reconstructed) → Old English wel "well, thoroughly, properly" (attested from the earliest Old English texts, 8th c. CE) → Middle English wel / well "well, thoroughly" (12th–15th c.) → Modern English well "thoroughly, completely" as the intensifying first element of compound adjectives (e.g. well-done, well-bred, well-read), including well-flogged.

--- ROOT 2: FLOGGED ---
English verb flog "to beat with a whip, rod, or stick" (attested c. 1670s; origin uncertain and debated) → past participle flogged "beaten" (17th c. onward) → compounded with well- as well-flogged "thoroughly beaten; (fig.) rigorously trained" (attested in 18th-century usage and in Gibbon's memoirs).

Note on the debate/nuance for FLOGGED: The origin of English flog is not securely established. The leading theories are (1) that it is a slang or cant shortening of Latin flagellāre "to whip" (from flagellum "little whip," the source of English flagellate and flagellation), possibly via school or criminal cant; and (2) that it is imitative, echoing the crack of a whip. Both remain hypotheses. What is secure is the social history of the word in naval, penal, and school registers from the later 17th century onward. The Latin flagellum family (flagellate, flagellation, biological flagellum) is a distinct learned line and should not be presented as a proven direct ancestor of flog; Germanic words for striking (beat, thrash, whip) are parallel vocabulary, not proven sisters of flog.
