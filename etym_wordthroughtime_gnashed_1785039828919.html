- 15th century - 'Gnasten' enters Middle English, likely from Norse, for the convulsive grinding of the teeth in rage or grief; the harsh, grating word mirrors in its own sound the act it names.
- 17th century - The King James Bible's 'weeping and gnashing of teeth' fixes gnash in the language with apocalyptic force: it becomes the signature gesture of the damned, of helpless fury and torment.
- Contemporary sense - Gnashed is still the precise word for furious, involuntary teeth-grinding; it reads as biblical and almost animalistic, used almost exclusively for rage too raw for speech.
