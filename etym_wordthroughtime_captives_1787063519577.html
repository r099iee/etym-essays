- 14th c. - Entered Middle English (*captif / captive*) from Old French and Latin, denoting prisoners taken in warfare, slaves, or hostages.
- 14th–15th c. - Adopted into courtly romance poetry (Chaucer) as a metaphor for lovers hopelessly enchanted by romantic devotion (*love's captive*).
- 16th–17th c. - Standardized in Renaissance English (Shakespeare, King James Bible) to depict both geopolitical prisoners and spiritual subjugation.
- 18th–19th c. - Broadened into zoological registers (wild animals kept *in captivity*) and rhetorical analysis (*captive audience*).
- 20th–21st c. - Formalized in modern international humanitarian law (Geneva Conventions), while retaining rich figurative potency.
