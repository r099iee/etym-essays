Legislated is the past tense and past participle of the regular English verb legislate, "to make laws; to bring something about by legislation". The paradigm is entirely regular and weak: legislate — legislates — legislating — legislated, with the dental suffix -ed and no vowel change. The same form therefore serves as finite past tense ("Parliament legislated in 1833") and as past participle ("a legislated minimum wage", "the rate was legislated"). In the reading — "Hammurabi legislated extensively in such terms" — it is a finite past tense: the Babylonian king is said to have made law on a large scale, and to have done so in the vocabulary of fixed compensations.

The verb is intransitive in that sentence: Hammurabi legislated, he did not legislate a particular object. English allows both patterns. Intransitive use normally takes a preposition — "legislate on abortion", "legislate for small business", "legislate against monopoly" — while transitive use, commoner in American than in British English, takes the thing created as object: "to legislate a tariff", "to legislate a right of appeal". Some conservative stylists object to the transitive pattern, preferring enact or make law providing for, but it is long established in legal and political writing.

Meaning in the source passage

"Hammurabi legislated extensively in such terms" means that the king's law-making was extensive and that its substance was framed as a tariff: a schedule stating what must be paid for an eye, a tooth, an arm or a life. The verb points to three things at once — the authority of the lawgiver, the deliberate act of promulgating rules, and the written, systematic character of the resulting body of law. It is not the language of custom, adjudication or judicial precedent; it is the language of a sovereign publishing statutes.

Core senses

  • To make or pass laws. The central sense: to exercise the legislative function, whether as a parliament, a congress, a monarch or a council. "The assembly legislated for the whole province."
  • To bring something into existence or regulate it by statute. "The city legislated a ban on single-use plastics"; "the working week was legislated down to forty hours". Here the verb records the effect of law rather than the activity of a legislature.
  • To prescribe by law (of a rule or provision). A statute itself may be said to legislate: "the Act legislates a minimum notice period". This impersonal, institutional use is frequent in legal commentary.
  • Participial adjective: fixed by statute. "Legislated rates", "legislated targets", "a legislated monopoly", "legislated morality" — the participle describes something imposed by law rather than produced by custom, market or choice.

Figurative and polemical extensions

  • "You cannot legislate morality." A long-standing aphorism claiming that laws may restrain behaviour but cannot change conscience. Here legislate implies futile or overreaching compulsion.
  • "Legislating from the bench." A phrase of American political criticism, accusing judges of making policy rather than applying existing law. The verb is transferred from legislatures to courts and carries a strong pejorative charge.
  • "Legislate for" in social policy. To make provision for a group or contingency by law — "to legislate for the disabled", "to legislate for every emergency". The phrase can imply paternalism: the state deciding in advance what people may do.
  • "Legislated equality" or "legislated fairness". A sceptical formula suggesting that legal requirement produces only the appearance of a virtue, not the reality.

Grammar, register and collocations

Legislate is formal and institutional. It belongs to parliamentary reporting, constitutional law, political theory and history; it is out of place in casual speech, where "make laws" or "pass a law" is normal. Typical collocates include extensively, directly, nationally, against, for, on, and into existence. The agent nouns are legislator and legislature; the activity is legislation; the quality is legislative. Note that legislated is not a synonym of legal: a legislated rule is one created by statute, whereas a legal rule is any rule recognized by law, including common law and custom.

Relation to the tariff card

The two notes in this batch are designed as a pair. The tariff is the instrument — the published schedule of payments; legislated is the act that gives it force. Together they describe the transition from private revenge to public law: a ruler converts compensation into a written scale and promulgates it. The participle also carries the passage's implicit claim about Hammurabi's code, namely that it was deliberately enacted and systematically arranged rather than merely recorded custom.
