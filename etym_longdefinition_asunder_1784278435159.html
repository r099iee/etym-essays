ASUNDER (adv., also used predicatively as adj.) — Into parts or pieces; apart from one another; so separated that the parts no longer hold together. The word describes the result of division, rupture, or tearing: "the lightning split the oak asunder," "the war tore families asunder," "what God hath joined together, let not man put asunder."

Core senses

  • Physical rupture (primary sense): into separate parts or pieces, as a result of force — things are torn, rent, split, burst, hewn, or dashed asunder. This is the oldest and most literal sense: an object violently divided into fragments or halves. "The storm rent the ship asunder," "the bomb blew the wall asunder."
  • Separation of persons or groups: apart from one another, divided from each other — families torn asunder by war, lovers parted asunder, friends sundered from each other. Here the separation is of people once joined, and the word carries strong emotional and often violent weight.
  • Complete separation, in two directions from a point: the adverb marks the endpoint or result of an action of dividing — things that were together are now apart; it almost always follows a verb of division (break, cut, tear, rend, split, burst, cleave, heave, pull, divide).

Polysemy, register, and usage

  • Liturgical and biblical sense: the word's most famous occurrence is the marriage formula of the Book of Common Prayer and of Matthew 19:6 — "What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder." Here "put asunder" means to separate or divorce; the word names the rending of a union that divine authority has made. This fixed, solemn register — the word of vows, of the indissolubility of marriage, of divine law against human separation — is the sense most English speakers know.
  • Archaic and literary register: outside its formulaic pairings, asunder is archaic and poetic. In ordinary modern speech it is virtually confined to the fixed collocations "torn asunder," "rent asunder," "split asunder," "burst asunder." It belongs to elevated, dramatic, or biblical diction, and modern writers deploy it deliberately for grandeur or violence.
  • Intellectual / abstract separation: in the card's context — "it is surely the stupidest of losses to confuse things which right reason has put asunder" (Matthew Arnold's Culture and Anarchy, on Hellenism) — the word is used abstractly: right reason has divided and distinguished concepts that confusion would blend. Here asunder names the analytical separation of things that ought to be kept apart — a fully figurative, intellectualised use of the physical sense of division into parts.
  • Degree of separation: asunder is stronger than apart. Apart can mean merely "separated in space" (standing apart) or "with a distance between" (torn apart by war can be a euphemism); asunder insists on separation into distinct pieces or parts — a state of being divided or broken, not merely distant.

Connotation and grammatical notes — Asunder always implies that the separated things were once joined or whole, and that the separation is complete or violent. It is almost always used predictively after a verb, describing the result-state of the action: "torn asunder" (the tearing is done, the parts are apart), "burst asunder," "put asunder." The word is grammatically an adverb of result, though it often behaves like an adjective ("the wood lay asunder"). It never takes an object of its own and never appears in attributive position (*"asunder pieces"). Its connotation is of force, finality, and pathos: what is torn asunder is destroyed as a unity.

Morphology and related words — Asunder is the fused descendant of Old English on sundran, "in a separate state," the preposition on ("in") + the dative of sundor, "separate, apart." Its family: the verb sunder ("to separate, to divide"), the adjective sundry ("various, separate," originally "separate, distinct"), and the words of the same root in other Germanic languages — German sondern, "to separate," and sonder- in sonderbar, "strange, particular." The initial a- is the reduced Old English preposition on- (as in abed, afoot, ashore), which fused with the following noun-phrase to form the adverb.

Historical semantic bridge — From the PIE root *sen(i)- / *sane-, "apart, separate, without," through Proto-Germanic *sundrō, "apart, singly," to Old English sundor and the phrase on sundran, "in a separate state." As the unstressed preposition eroded to a-, the phrase fused into the single adverb asunder (by the 13th century), and the neutral "separate state" of the Old English phrase specialised toward violent physical separation — "into pieces." The word then rode the Bible and the Prayer Book into the high solemn register where it survives today, while its everyday successors apart and in pieces carry the modern language of separation.
