Deep stage: no secure PIE root can be reconstructed for the Germanic verb *hlaupaną. Its origin is uncertain — it is confined to the Germanic branch, with no convincingly agreed Indo-European cognates — and an honest chain therefore begins at Proto-Germanic. [DEBATED — see note below] → Proto-Germanic *hlaupaną “to run, leap, spring” (reconstructed; a class VII strong verb with the reduplicated past *hehlaup — attested in Gothic as haihlaup — continued by Old Norse hlaupa “to run, leap,” Gothic hlaupan “to leap, run,” Old Saxon hlōpan, Old High German hloufan / loufan, modern German laufen “to run”) → Old English hlēapan “to leap, spring, run” (class VII strong verb; singular past hlēop, plural hlēupon, past participle hlēapen; attested from the 8th century CE) → Middle English lepen / leppen / leapen “to leap, spring, run” (12th–15th centuries CE; past forms lēop, later lepte / lēpte) → Early Modern English leap, with both past forms in use: the strong leapt (with the -pt spelling established in the 16th century, formerly often written lept, leapte) and the regularized weak leaped → Modern English LEAPT — the card's target form: the strong past tense (and, in British usage, past participle) of leap, pronounced /lɛpt/ (this card) NOTE on the debate: the deeper origin of Germanic *hlaupaną is genuinely uncertain. It has been compared with Latin labō “to slip, glide, fall” and with Greek forms, but most specialists reject or doubt the correspondence, and the verb is often treated as Germanic-internal with no securely established Indo-European etymon. Within English the facts are secure: hlēapan (Old English) → lepen / leapen (Middle English) → leap, with the strong past leapt coexisting with the weak leaped since early modern times; leap year is so called because the feast days “leap” forward over the extra day, and LEAPS in finance is a modern back-formation acronym, unrelated to the etymology.
