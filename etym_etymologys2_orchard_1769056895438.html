Two root words combine to form orchard: 'ort' (the plant) and 'geard' (the enclosure). Their chains are complete and separate, given in isolation. CHAIN 1 — ort ('plant'): 1. PIE *wréh₂d- 'root, branch, stub' (c. 4500–3500 BCE; zero grade *wṟh₂d-, extended *wrad-) → Proto-Germanic *wurtiz 'root, plant, herb' (c. 500 BCE; the sibling form *wrōts gave Gothic waúrts and Old Norse rót, whence English 'root' as a loan c. 1100 CE) → Old English wyrt 'plant, herb, root' (attested 8th c. CE) → metathesized ort 'plant' (late Old English; the w- of the wr- cluster was lost before r and the vowel shortened, so wyrt became ort) → compound ortgeard 'orchard' (Old English, attested c. 1000 CE) → Middle English orchard / orcherd (13th–14th c., with the palatal -ch- developing from the -g-) → Modern English orchard /'ɔːtʃəd/ 'a piece of land planted with fruit trees.' CHAIN 2 — geard ('enclosure'): 1. PIE *gʰerdʰ- 'to enclose, fence in' (c. 4500–3500 BCE; the same root gives Latin hortus 'garden', Greek khórtos, Sanskrit gṛhá, and the Slavic -grad 'city') → Proto-Germanic *gardaz 'enclosure, yard, house' (c. 500 BCE) → Old English geard 'enclosure, yard, garden' (attested 8th c. CE) → compound ortgeard 'orchard' (Old English, c. 1000 CE; geard is the second element) → Middle English orchard / orcherd (13th–14th c.) → Modern English orchard /'ɔːtʃəd/. NOTE — the two chains meet in the Old English compound ortgeard 'plant-enclosure'; the narrowing from 'any plant' to 'fruit tree' is the only semantic change the compound has undergone in a thousand years.
