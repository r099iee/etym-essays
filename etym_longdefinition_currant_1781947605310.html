In the closing domestic beat of The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), Mrs. Rabbit "bought a loaf of brown bread and five currant buns" for her family. Here currant is the small dried seedless grape — the Zante or Corinth raisin — baked into sweet buns, not the fresh Ribes berry of the garden. The word is warm, ordinary, and restorative: after Peter's ordeal in Mr. McGregor's garden, the currant buns stand for home, provision, and motherly care. Potter's choice is culturally precise — an Edwardian English bakery staple — and narratively comforting, an edible counterweight to the dangerous lettuces and radishes of the forbidden garden.

Primary sense (dried fruit). A currant in the culinary sense is a tiny, dark, intensely sweet dried grape, seedless, produced from the small grapes of the eastern Mediterranean — historically the Zante grape of the Ionian island of Zakynthos and the Corinth region of Greece. It is distinguished from other dried grapes by size (the smallest of all), color (almost black when dried), and concentrated, slightly sharp sweetness. It is the standard fruit of British "currant buns," currant cakes, currant loaves, and Christmas puddings.

Secondary sense (fresh berry). From the 1570s the same name was transferred to the small, tart, fresh berries of the genus Ribes — blackcurrant, redcurrant, whitecurrant — which are botanically unrelated to the grapevine. The transfer was pure visual analogy: the clustered round berries of Ribes resembled the imported Corinth raisins. This is why currant now names two entirely different plants, an ambiguity that persists in every modern dictionary and in everyday speech.

Polysemy and usage inventory:

  • Currant bun: a sweet bun studded with dried currants; in British Cockney rhyming slang, "currant bun" is the traditional rhyme for "sun."
  • Currant cake / currant loaf / currant bread: baked goods enriched with dried currants.
  • Currant jelly: a preserve made from fresh redcurrants or blackcurrants (also, loosely, from dried-fruit jam).
  • Blackcurrant / redcurrant / whitecurrant: the fresh Ribes berries, used in jams, cordials (Ribena), and sauces.
  • Zante currant: the trade name for the original dried Corinth grape of Zakynthos.
  • Flowering currant: a garden ornamental (Ribes sanguineum), a horticultural extension of the name.
  • Homophone caution: currant (fruit) is unrelated to current (flowing, present, a stream of electricity or water); the two are etymologically distinct and only accidentally identical in sound.

Jargon and metaphoric shifts. In British baking and food labeling, currant is semi-technical, specifying a particular dried fruit; in horticulture it names Ribes cultivars. The metaphorical reach is small but real: the Cockney rhyming slang "currant bun" (sun) shows the word entering London street idiom, and "currants" sometimes stands, by metonymy, for the festive baking world of the English tea table. The word's historical bridge is a place-name: "raisins of Corinth" was shortened over centuries to currant, making it a rare example of a city's name becoming a grocery item.

Register and collocation. The register is everyday culinary English, slightly old-fashioned and British in the "currant bun" phrase, with a botanical register in Ribes contexts. Collocations: currant bun, currant cake, dried currants, blackcurrant, redcurrant, whitecurrant, currant jelly, flowering currant. In Potter it reads as cozy marketplace English — the language of the baker's shop and the tea table, grounding the tale's fantasy in the warm, real world of Edwardian domesticity.
