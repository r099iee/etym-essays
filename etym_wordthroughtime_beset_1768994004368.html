- Old English - the verb besettan means "to set about, surround, place around"; its earliest English senses are military — laying troops or a siege about a fortress — a verb of tactical encirclement.
- Middle English - besetten serves both literally (armies beset a city) and figuratively (dangers beset a traveller); the siege image is already fading into general trouble.
- 16th–17th c. - the figurative sense strengthens decisively: souls are beset by temptation, paths beset with dangers; the word becomes the standard language of moral and practical trial, with the battlefield only faintly behind it.
- 18th–19th c. - the passive construction "beset by" hardens into the norm for persistent threat and trouble; the word keeps an architectural gravity that makes even abstract pressures feel encircling.
- 20th–21st c. - almost always passive, followed by by for abstract agencies (beset by doubts, scandals, problems); a formal, literary word that journalism favours for institutions under pressure, its buried siege-sense still converting strain into something strategic and hemmed-in.
