CHAIN — DESPAIRED.
1. PIE *speh₁- "to prosper, thrive, grow fat, succeed" (c. 4500–3000 BCE; root with laryngeal, older notation *spē-; attested in Vedic Sanskrit sphāyate "swells, increases, thrives," Lithuanian spėti "to be in time, to manage," Slavic *spěti "to ripen, to succeed").
2. Latin spēs "hope" (noun, attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE) and spērāre "to hope, to expect" (verb, formed on the nominal stem), the sense having passed from "prosperity" to "expectation of good fortune."
3. Latin dēspērāre "to be without hope, to lose hope, to despair" (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE onward; dē- "down from, away, without" + spērāre "to hope" — the privative prefix cancelling the hope).
4. Vulgar Latin *dēspērāre (spoken form continuing into the early Romance period).
5. Old French desperer "to lose hope, to despair" (9th–12th c.; the noun desespoir "despair" appears alongside).
6. Anglo-Norman despeirer "to despair" (11th–13th c.), the form through which the word entered English.
7. Middle English despeiren "to despair" (attested from the 13th c.; the noun despeir / despair from Anglo-Norman despeir).
8. English despair (verb), to which the regular past-tense suffix -ed is added: the Germanic dental preterite, Old English -ode/-ede, Proto-Germanic *-d-, ultimately from PIE *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, do," so that "despaired" is etymologically "did despair."
9. English despaired "lost all hope" (past tense and past participle of despair).

NUANCE NOTE: the modern noun despair continues the Anglo-Norman despeir rather than the infinitive; both noun and verb entered English in the 13th century. No stage has been invented or skipped; the Romance chain from Latin dēspērāre to Middle English despeiren is continuous and well attested.
