ARRAS is a noun denoting in its strict historical sense a rich tapestry wall hanging woven with pictures or patterns, hung on walls of castles, churches, and great houses for warmth, decoration, and concealment. Named for the city of Arras in Artois, whose weavers dominated the luxury cloth trade in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, an arras typically depicted hunting, biblical, or mythological scenes in wool, silk, and gold thread, suspended a little forward from the wall so that courtiers, spies, and in Shakespeare's Hamlet Polonius himself could hide behind it. The register is antiquarian-literary: to say arras rather than tapestry or curtain is to summon draughty stone halls, torchlight, and eavesdropping.

In the immediate context, ARRAS is used figuratively and ecologically: endlessly listening and stabbing through the ______ of grass, the fixed eye blind to what it does. The thrush hunting worms stands before a dense vertical screen of grass stems that functions exactly as a hanging tapestry: a patterned curtain that both reveals and conceals, through which the beak must stab blindly on the guidance of hearing. Just as a courtier stabs through an arras without seeing, the bird stabs through grass arras with eye fixed yet unseeing. The metaphor dignifies a ditch-edge with palace furniture while stressing blindness, repetition, and cold detachment.

  • Primary sense: tapestry wall hanging from Arras. Museum examples survive at Hampton Court, the Cloisters, and Hardwick Hall. Phrases include behind the arras, the arras hangs, figured arras. Semantic bridge to the present sentence: functional analogy of a hanging screen that hides bodies and is pierced by weapons. Grass stems hang vertically like warp threads; the beak is the dagger through the cloth.
  • Secondary sense: any curtain-like screen or covering that conceals. An arras of ivy, of rain, of trees, of words. Modern poets and nature writers freely extend arras to vegetation, weather, and language that veils while displaying pattern. Bridge: generalisation from material to function. Once arras means patterned veil, any patterned veil qualifies, especially one stabbed or peered through.
  • Tertiary senses: attributive and allusive uses. Arras counterpane, arras-work for embroidery imitating tapestry, arras-eyed for peeking. In Shakespeare studies arras-scene denotes Hamlet Act 3 where Hamlet stabs Polonius crying How now, a rat. Bridge: cultural allusion. To invoke arras is to invoke concealed listeners and mistaken stabbing, exactly the thrush's blind stab, giving the grass image a tragic-eavesdropper undertone.

The choice of ARRAS over neutral curtain or thicket matters. Curtain is domestic, thicket is botanical, but arras is woven, storied, and pierced. It implies that grass is not mere background but a figured textile with warp and weft, that nature is furnished like a hall, and that predation repeats courtly murder on a miniature scale. The coldness noted in the sentence chills both bird and tapestry: endless listening without pity, stabbing without sight, the fixed eye blind like a woven figure's eye.
