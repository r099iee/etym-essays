- Late-16th-century entry - pedant came into English from French pedant and Italian pedante (teacher, schoolmaster), and at first named a schoolmaster or teacher neutrally, even approvingly.
- 17th-18th-century pejoration - during the age of the new science and criticism the word hardened into an insult: one who makes an ostentatious display of learning, fussing over rules and details instead of grasping substance or spirit.
- Macaulay's usage - in the 19th century the plural pedants became the damning label for the Byzantine grammarians who could read and interpret Sophocles, Demosthenes, and Plato yet were destitute of taste, sense, and spirit.
- Contemporary sense - pedant is strictly derogatory, naming the hair-splitting lover of minor rules, and the adjective pedantic is its everyday form.
