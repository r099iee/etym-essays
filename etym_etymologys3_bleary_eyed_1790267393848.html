1. Etymological Architecture of a Compound Adjective:
Bleary-eyed is a vivid compound adjective formed from two ancient Germanic linguistic elements: the adjective bleary (derived from Middle English blerien / blere, "dim, watery, rheumy-eyed") and the parasynthetic denominal adjective eyed (from Old English ēaged, "having eyes"). To fully trace the historical evolution of the compound, each component must be examined along its distinct historical trajectory.

2. Evolution of Component 1: bleary (The Ocular and Deceptive Element):
The root of blear / bleary is an ancient sound-symbolic and onomatopoeic Proto-Indo-European root *bʰle- ("to blow, swell, weep, babble, cry out"). In the Germanic branch, this gave rise to Proto-Germanic *blērjanan, which developed in Middle Low German and Middle Dutch as blerren / blēren ("to weep, bleat, shed tears, or blur").

The word entered Middle English in the 13th century as the verb bleren and adjective blere, describing eyes that were watery, dim, or running with mucous discharge (rheum), commonly caused by infection, smoke, weeping, or old age. In medieval England, a famous idiomatic phrase arose: "to blear someone's eye" (Middle English: bleren someone's eye), meaning to hoodwink, deceive, or blind someone to the truth by clouding their vision. Geoffrey Chaucer employed this idiom in The Canterbury Tales (e.g., in The Manciple's Tale: "And yet this Manciplic had bleared here aller eye"), and William Langland used it in Piers Plowman.

By the 16th century, the adjectival suffix -y was appended to create bleary, emphasizing a persistent physical quality of watery blurriness and irritation.

3. Evolution of Component 2: eyed (The Organ of Vision):
The second element, eyed, descends from the primary Proto-Indo-European root for the organ of sight, *h₃ekʷ- ("to see, look, eye"). This prolific root yielded Greek ὄσσε (ósse, "two eyes") and ὀφθαλμός (ophthalmós, source of ophthalmology), Latin oculus (source of ocular), Sanskrit akṣi, and Proto-Germanic *augô. In Old English, *augô evolved into ēage ("eye"), which in Middle English became eie / eye. By attaching the Germanic past-participle / possessive adjective suffix *-ōdaz (Old English -ed), English formed the parasynthetic descriptor ēaged / eyed ("possessing eyes of a designated characteristic").

4. Synthesis and Elizabethan Dramatic Attestation:
The compound first appeared in Early Modern English during the 16th century in the un-suffixed form blear-eyed. William Shakespeare frequently employed blear-eyed to evoke vivid sensory and moral imagery. In The Merchant of Venice (Act III, Scene 2), Bassanio speaks of "bleared sights" and "blear-eyed democracy," and in Coriolanus (Act II, Scene 1), Brutus describes how "the bleared sights are spectacled to see him." In these Jacobean contexts, the term captured both physical eye irritation from drunkenness or weeping and figurative moral blindness.

5. Phonetic Smoothing, Victorian Realism, and Modern Idiom:
During the 18th and 19th centuries, the form with the softening vowel suffix—bleary-eyed—supplanted the older blear-eyed in popular usage. Victorian novelists embraced the term to describe the physical toll of industrial labor, late-night academic study, and the morning-after consequences of heavy drinking. In the 20th century, humorists like P. G. Wodehouse elevated bleary-eyed to an art form, capturing the comic struggles of Bertie Wooster squinting through morning light with eyelids "more or less glued together." In contemporary life, bleary-eyed remains the definitive universal descriptor for sleep deprivation, morning disorientation, jet lag, and digital screen exhaustion.
