• lampoons: Similar because it denotes sharp, biting satires aimed at ridiculing an individual or institution; differs because lampoon is a broader, more common literary term, whereas pasquinade specifically preserves the historical connotation of an anonymous, publicly posted squib or placard.
  • squibs: Similar because it refers to short, witty, and cutting satirical writings; differs because squib emphasizes brevity, crackling wit, and sudden explosive impact (like a small firecracker), while pasquinade emphasizes public posting and political or ecclesiastical subversion.
  • satires: Similar because it utilizes irony, humor, and ridicule to expose vice; differs because satire is a broad, overarching literary mode and genre that can be philosophical, gentle, and extended (e.g., Horatian or Juvenalian satire), whereas a pasquinade is always sharp, personal, targeted, and aggressive.
  • libels: Similar because it involves damaging written attacks upon someone's reputation; differs crucially because libel in modern usage is primarily a legal concept concerning false, defamatory statements, whereas a pasquinade is an artistic and witty form of satirical protest, often exposing real truths.
  • pasquils: Similar because it is an exact etymological variant and synonym (from Latin pasquillus); differs because pasquil is an older, somewhat rarer sixteenth-century Anglicized form, while pasquinade (via French) became the standard international literary term.
  • caricatures: Similar because it distorts traits for humorous or critical effect; differs because caricatures are predominantly visual illustrations or exaggerated physical characterizations, whereas pasquinades are textual, poetic, or rhetorical lampoons.
  • broadsides: Similar because it refers to sheets of paper printed on one side and posted or sold in public streets; differs because broadsides can be ballads, news reports, or official proclamations, whereas pasquinades are strictly satirical and subversive attacks.
  • parodies: Similar because it employs humor and imitation for comic effect; differs because parody specifically mimics the style of another work or author to produce humor, whereas a pasquinade directly attacks a person, policy, or institution without necessarily imitating an artistic style.
