Embody in current English centres on two complementary actions, both turning on body as vessel. The first and most familiar is to give bodily or concrete form to what is abstract, to incarnate, as in she embodies grace, the screen can embody intonation, a flag embodies a nation. Here an idea, quality, spirit, or pattern, which has no limbs, is lent a perceptible body through person, image, diagram, or performance, so that it can be seen, heard, and touched. The second is to include, comprise, or incorporate as part of a larger body, as in the charter embodies rights, the volume embodies essays, where many parts are gathered into one corpus. Both share the image of body as container and manifestation.

In the source sentence, the computer screen can embody and dramatize what diagrams pointed to, the verb takes the first sense with a media twist. The screen does not merely display or illustrate intonation, timing, and voice, it gives them a body, a spatial layout, movement, colour, and timing that lets abstract prosodic structure become sensuously present and actable. To embody here is thus stronger than to show, it is to incarnate analysis into experience, turning pointing diagrams into inhabitable performance where readers see voice move. The pairing with dramatize reinforces theatrical embodiment, ideas put on stage in bodies that speak.

Historically embody moves between spiritual, legal, and aesthetic domains. Theologians speak of souls embodied in flesh and virtues embodied in saints, lawyers of clauses embodied in statutes and precedents embodied in judgments, artists of tensions embodied in characters and rhythms embodied in line-breaks. It governs direct objects of abstraction, principles, values, fears, hopes, and collective nouns of texts, and it appears in passive and adjectival embodied, as in embodied knowledge, embodied cognition, where mind is understood as lived through the body rather than floating above it. Contemporary theory of embodied mind gives the verb fresh prestige, to embody is not to illustrate from outside but to realize from inside fleshy practice.

  • Incarnate-manifest sense: to give concrete form to abstraction, e.g. to embody an ideal, the screen embodies voice. Polysemy bridge: from em- in plus body as flesh to putting spirit into flesh, transferring theological incarnation to general manifestation, where any medium can serve as flesh for meaning.
  • Include-incorporate sense: to comprise as parts of a whole, e.g. the code embodies safeguards, the anthology embodies poems. Polysemy bridge: via body as corpus or collection, where corpus of law or writing is figured as a body with members, so to bring into the body is to include.
  • Personify-exemplify sense: to be a living example, e.g. she embodies resilience. Polysemy bridge: via narrowing of incarnate sense to human exemplar, where person becomes textbook case, embodiment as moral shorthand.
  • Cognitive-embodied sense: to ground in bodily experience, e.g. embodied cognition, knowledge embodied in gesture. Polysemy bridge: via phenomenology and linguistics where meaning is not abstract symbol but sensorimotor enactment, embodiment marking resistance to disembodied formalism.

Stylistically embody is formal but not archaic, preferred in criticism, law, and philosophy where exemplify sounds dry and personify sounds mythic. It is transitive, often with abstract object and human or medial subject, and it favours present and modal can embody, will embody for capacity rather than single act. To say what the screen can embody is to claim not technical display but ontological hospitality, the screen can lend a body to voice itself, letting suppressed oral powers live again in visible space rather than remaining pointed at from outside.
