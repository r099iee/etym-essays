ELAPSE is an intransitive verb of time: "to pass away, go by, slip away (of a period or interval)." In the passage about Dai-yu's upbringing, Hawkes writes that her parents "had always insisted that after a meal one should allow a certain interval to elapse before taking tea in order to avoid indigestion." The verb elapse describes time passing — but with a distinctive quality. Where the plain verbs pass and go by merely register duration, elapse pictures time as something that slips or glides smoothly away, like water through fingers; its Latin ancestor ēlābī means literally "to slip out, glide away" (ex- "out" + lābī "to slip, glide"). The word therefore suits this sentence perfectly: the interval is not endured or filled but gently allowed to glide by, a deliberately unhurried span that must be let alone. The tone is calm, methodical, hygienic — the language of a disciplined nursery routine, of bodies governed by careful habit. It is the quietest word in the sentence, yet it does the moral work: the regulated sliding of time is itself part of the good health her parents have taught her, a small proof that even minutes are managed in the Dai household with measured patience.

Polysemy and the full range of uses:

  • The passing of time — the sole living sense: "several years must elapse before the results are final"; "allow a reasonable interval to elapse".
  • Of periods, terms, and deadlines in formal and legal registers — "before the period of limitation elapses"; the phrase "elapsed time" in science and sport.
  • Of life or opportunity, figuratively — poetic and archaic: "his hours elapse," "the chance elapses"; time slipping out of reach, close to the original Latin image of gliding away.
  • Specialised modern compounds — "elapsed time" (measurement), "elapsed run time" (computing); the adjective elapsed is common in technical English.

Its synonym family is small but precise. Pass is the neutral, all-purpose member — time passes, passes by, passes quickly — and is the word of ordinary speech; it is also hugely ambiguous (to pass a law, pass an exam, pass away), which elapse never is. Go by is the informal, conversational twin of pass and would deflate the sentence's stately rhythm. Expire refers to the end of a period — a term, a deadline, a licence expires — and concentrates attention on the terminal moment rather than on the flowing of the interval. Lapse is the close cousin to watch: lapse can mean time passing, but it carries the additional implication that a right or condition is lost when time passes (a contract lapses, a subscription lapses), whereas elapse is purely descriptive of duration, with no sense of forfeiture. Intervene names the time that comes between two events (in the interval that intervened) and emphasizes the gap itself, not its passing. Glide and slip away are the poetic, image-bearing equivalents that foreground the smooth, effortless motion latent in elapse's etymology. In sum, elapse is the formal, dignified, unambiguous verb for the passing of an interval — time that flows without being marked, endured, or lost.
