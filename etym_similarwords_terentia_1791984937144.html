• Fulvia: Similar because she was a Roman matron whose political importance is known mainly through the careers of the men she married; differs because Fulvia (d. 40 BCE) acted openly in the streets and in armies, raising troops and controlling factions, whereas Terentia's influence stayed inside the household and the letter.
  • Clodia: Similar because she belongs to the same late-Republican aristocratic world and is likewise known through a man's writings; differs because Clodia Metelli is the scandalous figure of Cicero's Pro Caelio and Catullus' poetry, a byword for sexual notoriety, while Terentia's reputation turns on money and domestic loyalty.
  • Cornelia: Similar because she is the archetypal Roman matron, mother of the Gracchi, defined by motherhood and lineage; differs because Cornelia belongs to the second century BCE and to legend, honoured as a public monument, whereas Terentia is documented in intimate, often unflattering detail.
  • Livia: Similar because she was the wife of the most powerful man of her age and a visible presence in dynastic politics; differs because Livia Drusilla (58 BCE – 29 CE) held the position of Augusta and shaped the succession, whereas Terentia had no title and no formal power at all.
  • Calpurnia: Similar because she was the wife of a dominant statesman, Caesar, and is remembered chiefly through his career; differs because Calpurnia is a shadowy figure known for one vivid anecdote, the dream before the Ides, while Terentia appears across decades of correspondence.
  • Tullia: Similar because she is the other woman of Cicero's household and shares the same documentary base; differs because Tullia is his daughter, loved and mourned in the letters, not his partner in the management of the estate.
  • Publilia: Similar because she too was Cicero's wife and is defined by that marriage; differs because Publilia was a young heiress whose brief union lasted only months and left almost no trace beyond the scandal it caused.
  • Servilia: Similar because she was a great aristocratic woman who advanced her family through alliances; differs because Servilia (mother of Brutus) moved in the highest political circles and helped plan a tyranny's end, whereas Terentia's sphere was property and obligation.
