In the Gibbonian sentence, sovereign qualifies the authority the Roman senate only appeared to possess: ultimate, undivided supremacy over the state — the prize of legitimacy that no subordinate magistrate could claim. The adjective is majestic and absolute; it does not mean merely "important" or "official," but "highest of all, answerable to none." Gibbon's irony is that this supreme quality is attributed as appearance, not substance: the word's full force of final power is precisely what has slipped from the senate to the emperor.

Core meaning: possessing supreme or ultimate power; not subject to any higher authority. As a noun, sovereign names the supreme ruler of a state — a monarch or, in modern political theory, the people or the state itself conceived as the ultimate locus of power.

Senses and uses:
  • Adjective, supreme power — sovereign authority, power, will, right, or state: power that is final, undivided, and answerable to no earthly superior.
  • Noun, a monarch or supreme ruler — "the sovereign" as the crowned head of a state; historically a king, queen, or emperor whose rule was not subject to another's.
  • Sovereign state (international law) — a polity with full independence, territorial integrity, and the legal capacity to conduct foreign relations; the technical foundation of modern international law.
  • Sovereign immunity and sovereign debt — legal and financial phrases: a state's immunity from suit in its own courts or abroad; the debt obligations of a state in finance.
  • Numismatics: a gold sovereign — the British gold coin of one pound (first struck 1489 under Henry VII, re-issued 1817), so named because it bears the image of the sovereign.
  • Figurative / archaic intensifier — "sovereign remedy," "sovereign contempt," "sovereign virtue": meaning excellent, supreme in degree, powerful beyond the ordinary; now literary or archaic.
  • Sovereign contempt (historical) — a legal or rhetorical phrase for supreme disdain or a jurisdiction's ultimate punitive authority.
Polysemy and semantic bridges:
  • The core image is spatial: the sovereign is the one who stands "above" (Latin super "over, above"). The vertical metaphor of height is the bridge from "over-ness" to "supreme-ness" to "having final command" — the same metaphor that produced superior, supreme, and paramount.
  • The noun use grows out of the adjective: from "supreme (power)" it is a short step to "the one who holds supreme power," and the medieval French soverain functioned as both from the start.
  • The modern legal senses (sovereign state, sovereign immunity, sovereign debt) are technical consolidations of the early modern political theory that located final, undivided authority in a single ruler or, later, in the people or the state.
  • The coin sense is a metonymy: the gold piece bears the monarch's image, so the coin becomes "the sovereign." The decorative and numismatic uses are later offshoots of the political sense.
Boundary contrasts:
  • Supreme — marks the top of a scale but can describe courts, commanders, or qualities without implying political independence.
  • Absolute — stresses freedom from legal restraint and can sound despotic.
  • Paramount — "above others in rank," often in feudal or comparative contexts, not a claim to undivided state power.
  • Autonomous — emphasizes self-rule and independence from external control, not majesty or the right to command subjects.
  • Preeminent — first in standing or excellence, without any implication of governing power.
Collocations: sovereign power, sovereign authority, sovereign state, sovereign right, sovereign will, the sovereign; in law, sovereignty, sovereign immunity, sovereign debt; historically, a gold sovereign. Register is formal, legal-political, and historical; in international law "sovereign" remains a technical term, and in literary historiography it carries exactly the ceremonial weight Gibbon needs when he describes what the senate seemed to hold and no longer truly did.
