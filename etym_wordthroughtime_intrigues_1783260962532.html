- [17th-century French loan] - 'Intrigue' entered English in the 1600s from French (from Italian intrigare, 'to entangle'), first as a verb meaning to trick or perplex, then as a noun for secret plotting.
- [Courtly scheming] - In the 17th and 18th centuries 'intrigues' named the secret schemes of courts - whisper, letter and private audience - a word of backstairs diplomacy and faction.
- [Romantic sense] - By the 18th and 19th centuries it also meant a secret love affair - amorous intrigue - adding a seductive, French-flavoured glamour to the noun.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'intrigue' means both secret scheming and fascinated curiosity (something that intrigues us), the old courtly plots surviving in historical prose.
