Yarmouth joins a mysterious Celtic river name to a transparent Germanic harbour word, and its history is the history of England learning to say a foreign river with a Saxon mouth.

The river we now call the Yare, flowing from near Dereham through Norwich to the sea at Gorleston, bore a Brittonic name before any English was spoken in Norfolk. Saxon settlers in the 5th-6th centuries heard it as something like Gerne, Yerne and kept it, as newcomers usually keep river names. Antiquaries have guessed at meanings: Old Celtic babbling, eddying water, yew-tree river, or roaring stream, comparing Welsh iar (babbling), Gaelic garbh (rough), or the Yare's winding marshes. None is proven by inscription or early gloss; the Cambridgeshire school marks Yare as of obscure Brittonic origin. What is certain is adoption: English did not rename the river, it learned it, then regularised Gerne to Yare under the influence of spelling and of the Yar on Wight.

  • Germanic mouth (secure): Proto-Germanic munthaz (mouth, opening, entrance) gives Old English muth, mutha (mouth, river mouth, harbour mouth). The sense narrows in place-names: -mouth means harbour town at a river's sea-gate (Plymouth, Dartmouth, Exmouth, Teesmouth). Phonologically Germanic u plus nth gives Old English u, modern reduction to muth in compounds (YAR-muth). This half of Yarmouth is textbook Germanic hydronymy.
  • Compounding and Domesday fixing (c. 1000-1086): Saxon fishermen build huts on the spit and call the haven Gernemutha, the Yare's mouth. Domesday scribes latinise it Gernemutha, Gernemua, noting herring renders and salt-pans. The spelling Gerne- shows metathesis or vowel lowering still unsettled; Norman ears hear G- where later ears hear Y-.
  • Medieval standardisation (13th-15th century): borough charters (1208), town walls, and the herring fair make the port famous. Rolls write Jernemuth, Yernemuth, Yarmouth interchangeably; initial J- and Y- mark Anglo-Norman spelling for the palatal glide. Great appears to distinguish the Norfolk haven (Great Yarmouth) from the Wight haven (Yarmouth) and from Little Yarmouth (Gorleston). By Caxton the modern form wins.
  • Maritime and literary afterlife: the Roads (offshore anchorage) shelter Crusoe's first voyage (Defoe himself knew the coast), Colliers, and the Dutch herring busses; Defoe's Tour praises the quay; Dickens stages the great storm; Borrow preaches on the sands. Each re-inscription keeps the transparent etymology alive: every visitor hears Yar plus mouth and understands harbour.

Together the name compresses conquest and continuity: a Celtic river that refused renaming, a Saxon mouth that named its harbour, and a Norman clerk who wrote both down, leaving Ruskin's sailor-grandfather a doorway word through which to vanish like Crusoe and return delightful.

Conservation today lists the Rows, the walls, and the curing works: the mouth that fed England with herring now lands wind-farm crews, yet the transparent Yar-plus-mouth etymology still guides every visitor from river to sea.
