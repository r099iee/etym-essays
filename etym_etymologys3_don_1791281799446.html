The verb don is one of the most transparent contractions in English, preserving in a single syllable a whole syntactic phrase: do on. Its history is therefore the history of two of the oldest Germanic function words fusing under stress and frequency, and later surviving as a literary archaism while its opposite, doff, faded even further.

In Old English, dōn was a high-frequency verb meaning to do, act, make, put, or place, descended regularly from Proto-Germanic *dōną and ultimately PIE *dheh₁- to put, place. It combined freely with the preposition-adverb on, from Old English on, an, from Proto-Germanic *ana and PIE *h₂eno- meaning on, upon. To do on a garment was simply to do or put a cloak on oneself. The phrase do on clothes, do on armour, do on a crown is common in Middle English manuscripts. Because do and on were both unstressed in rapid speech before a stressed noun, do on coalesced phonetically to don, just as do off coalesced to doff and do of to dof.

By the 14th century don is written as one word with the specific sense to put on, dress oneself in. Chaucer and his contemporaries use don in this fused sense, and the past forms don, donned, donning are established by the 15th century. The Great Vowel Shift did not alter its short vowel /ɒ/, and spelling stabilised as don, donned, donning to mark the short vowel before inflection. Semantically it narrowed: while do remained general, don specialised almost exclusively to clothing, armour, insignia, and later abstract attire such as to don a disguise, to don an attitude.

  • Old English phrase stage: dōn + on as two words, fully compositional, used for putting on helmets, rings, crowns, and for placing things on tables or altars.
  • Middle English contraction: phonetic fusion in unstressed position creates don alongside do on; meaning narrows toward personal dress and soon competes with put on.
  • Early Modern literary retention: put on becomes the neutral everyday form, while don survives in elevated, ceremonial, and military registers — to don uniform, to don robes — giving it a formal, deliberate flavour.
  • Modern revival: safety and technical writing revives don for precision — to don PPE, to don a life-jacket, to don a spacesuit — where put on feels too casual and wear describes state rather than action.

Socio-culturally, don acquired dignity from its association with ritual dressing: judges don robes, graduates don gowns, priests don vestments. This ceremonial colour explains why Wodehouse and other comic writers use commenced to don for humorous elevation of a trivial act of getting dressed. The opposite doff, to take off, became rare, making don feel even more bookish by contrast.

Literary attestations are continuous: Malory has knights donning harness, Shakespeare has characters donning vizards and night-gowns, the King James Bible has to put on but later commentaries paraphrase as to don. In the 19th century, antiquarian and Oxford usage reinforced it, and the homograph noun don — a university fellow or Spanish gentleman from Latin dominus master — created puns but no etymological merger. The verb never derives from Latin; it is purely Germanic.

Phonetically, the development is reduction: /doːn ɒn/ to /dɒn/, loss of vowel length distinction, gemination in inflected forms donned /dɒnd/ to preserve shortness. No borrowing intervened. The debated point is only the exact PIE reconstruction of on — whether *h₂en- or *ano- — which does not affect the English story. The core fact is secure: don equals do + on, fossilised.
