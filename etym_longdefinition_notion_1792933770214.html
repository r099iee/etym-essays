Notion is English's lightest word for thinking: an idea caught in mid-flight, before it has hardened into knowledge or earned the dignity of a theory. Where concept labours in philosophy and plan marches toward execution, a notion flits — half opinion, half hunch, half whim — and its charm is precisely that it promises nothing it cannot lightly abandon.

In the source sentence — But now I've a notion — the speaker is struck mid-conversation by a fresh thought, still warm from the mind's oven. Tolkien gives the line no object and no explanation, which is exactly the point: a notion announces itself before its content is ready, a flag run up the pole while the message is still being written. Every reader recognises the moment — the lifted finger, the brightened eyes, the sentence that begins before the thought has finished arriving.

Core meaning in detail: A notion is a mental representation held lightly: an idea (a notion of justice), an opinion (my notion is that he will refuse), an understanding (she has no notion of the cost), or a sudden impulse (he took a notion to sail around the world). The word governs the whole spectrum from the nearly philosophical to the nearly frivolous, and context alone decides whether the notion deserves respect. Grammar marks the lightness: one has a notion, takes a notion, or gets a notion into one's head — verbs of possession and catching, never of building or proving.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • An idea or opinion (primary modern sense): his notion of a holiday is a week in a tent. The bridge from Latin notio (something known) is direct: what the mind has noted down for itself, however sketchily, is its notion.
  • A conception or understanding (of something): children's notions of time are wonderfully elastic. The bridge generalises the idea-sense into mental models: a notion is the mind's working sketch, accurate or not, of how anything is.
  • An inkling or vague awareness: I had no notion you were ill. The bridge thins the idea toward mere acquaintance: to have a notion of a fact is to have brushed against it, not grasped it — the faintest possible knowing.
  • A sudden whim or impulse (colloquial): she took a notion to dye her hair green. The bridge runs through suddenness: notions arrive uninvited and abruptly, so the word for the thought became the word for the urge that rides in with it.
  • Small wares and sewing odds and ends (plural notions, chiefly American): the shop sells buttons, ribbons, and notions. The bridge is the famous Yankee joke turned trade term: 19th-century pedlars sold clever little inventions — Yankee notions — trifles born of someone's bright idea, until the bright ideas themselves became the stock in trade.
  • A mistaken or foolish idea (with disapproval): wherever did you get that notion, romantic notions. The bridge is the word's built-in lightness turned against it: because notions are unproven by definition, calling something a notion can dismiss it — mere notion, not knowledge.

Register and connotation: notion is conversational and unpretentious — the thinking word of kitchens, pubs, and hobbit-holes rather than seminar rooms. It flatters the thinker with spontaneity (to have notions is to be lively) while reserving the right to be wrong (it was only a notion). Only the commercial plural has left the mind for the marketplace, where notions counters still sell the small clever things that someone's notion once dreamed up.
