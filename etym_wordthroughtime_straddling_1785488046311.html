- Physical posture (c. 1560) - formed from stride plus the frequentative suffix -le; meant standing astride with legs wide, one foot on each side of a horse, fence or ditch; purely concrete.
- Figurative hedging (18th-19th centuries) - 'straddle the fence' in American English comes to mean avoiding commitment or hedging; the word acquires a mildly evasive overtone.
- Specialised technical senses (19th-20th centuries) - a stock-market straddle is an options position bestriding both a call and a put, a poker straddle a voluntary blind bet; the root idea of holding two sides at once turns professional.
- Modern intellectual register - academic prose uses straddling for a thinker's double competence, one foot in each discipline held in effortless balance; the tone is admiring, even athletic, rather than evasive.
