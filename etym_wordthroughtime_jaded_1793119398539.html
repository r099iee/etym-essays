- Late 14th c. — the worthless nag: Middle English jade names a worn-out horse, and by extension a disreputable woman, a term of stable contempt.
- c. 1600 — to ride hard: the verb to jade means to tire a horse by overwork, then to tire anyone or anything by extension.
- 1630s — ridden out: jaded first describes exhausted horses and travellers, pure physical weariness.
- 1770s — dulled by pleasure: the sense shifts from muscles to appetite, and jaded begins to mean sated and bored by overindulgence.
- 19th c. — the fashionable complaint: Byron, Thackeray, and the novelists make jaded palates and jaded beaux the signature of sophisticated urban life.
- Today — the default word for overexposure: jaded viewers, jaded voters, jaded consumers, naming the peculiar modern fatigue of those who have seen everything and felt little.
