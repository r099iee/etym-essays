- Early 16th Century (Tudor Era, c. 1520–1535) - Emerges in Early Tudor English as an expressive onomatopoeic noun (*squibbe*), designating a handheld paper tube filled with gunpowder that hissed and spat fire before popping; popular in public street festivals and Lord Mayor pageants.
- Late 16th to 17th Century (Elizabethan & Stuart Eras) - Metaphorically applied to literature and politics: a *squib* came to mean a short, witty, biting satire, anonymous broadside, or sarcastic pamphlet flung into public debate like a mini-firework.
- 18th Century (Georgian Era & Legal History) - Immortalized in common-law jurisprudence through the landmark 1773 tort case *Scott v. Shepherd* ('The Squib Case'), establishing proximate causation; widely cultivated by Fleet Street satirists.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Gave rise to the iconic idiom *a damp squib* (mid-19th c.), denoting an anti-climactic failure; lovingly characterized in Oscar Wilde's fairy tale *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888) as an innocent, cheerful firework.
- 20th to 21st Century - Diversified into modern technology: practical film special effects (blood squibs), firearms mechanics (*squib load* barrel obstruction), aerospace engineering (pyrotechnic actuators), and American football (*squib kick*).
