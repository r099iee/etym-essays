Afford is in everyday modern use above all the verb of means: to have enough money, time, strength, or opportunity to meet the cost of something without painful sacrifice or loss, as in we cannot afford a new car or she can hardly afford an hour of sleep. It measures capacity against cost, and its negative is often more common than its positive, since speakers reach for it exactly when limits press.

In the source sentence the word carries its older literary sense of yielding or supplying: the Surrey Hills are famous for the prospects they afford, meaning the views they offer, present, and lay open to the walker who climbs them. This is the formal afford of landscapes, books, and opportunities, not the financial afford of shops and budgets, and it is the key that unlocks the word's history, because the sense of providing came before the sense of being able to pay.

Core meaning in detail: Grammatically afford takes a direct object of the thing obtained or borne (afford a car, afford time, afford a mistake) and very often pairs with can, could, cannot, or with a to-infinitive of the risked action (we cannot afford to be late). The subject is usually a person, household, or institution with limited resources, and the sentence quietly names both the desire and the constraint. To say I can afford it is to say both that the thing is wanted and that the means are sufficient.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To have the financial means to pay for something (primary modern sense, from late 14th c.): they can afford private school. The bridge runs from the older sense of supplying: what a household can supply from its own purse without ruin it can be said to bear, and by extension the purse itself bears the purchase.
  • To have enough time, energy, or opportunity to spare (extension of the financial sense): I cannot afford another delay, we can afford ten minutes. The bridge is metaphorical accounting: time and strength are treated as budgets exactly like money, with spending, saving, and waste, so the verb of purchase becomes the verb of scheduling.
  • To provide, yield, or supply something, especially an opportunity or experience (formal, literary; the source sense): the hills afford fine prospects, the archives afford rich material. The bridge is the word's oldest surviving logic: the Middle English verb meant to accomplish and put forth, hence to bring something into reach, and this giving sense is the trunk from which the financial branch grew.
  • To give, grant, or spare something to another person (formal, slightly old-fashioned): afford shelter to travellers, afford him every assistance. The bridge narrows the yielding sense toward generosity: the subject does not merely produce the thing but bestows it, and the construction often names the recipient with to.
  • To be able to bear or risk something without serious consequence, usually in the negative with a to-infinitive (very common): we cannot afford to lose this client, she could not afford to smile. The bridge runs from cost to consequence: just as money spent is gone, so a mistake made cannot be undone, and the verb treats reputation, safety, and goodwill as funds that careless action would bankrupt.
  • To manage or sustain a particular appearance or behaviour, often with reputation at stake (journalistic): the minister could hardly afford that tone. The bridge extends the risk sense into manners: demeanour itself has a price in public life, and the wrong note is figured as an expense beyond one's station.
  • To accomplish or put forth, to carry out (archaic, Old and Middle English, now obsolete): to afford a deed in the sense of performing it. This is the etymological root sense, preserved only in dictionaries, from which every later meaning of supplying and then being able to supply descends.

Register and connotation: afford is sober and practical in its financial uses, the vocabulary of budgets, housing, and careful households, but elevated and almost poetic in its literary use, where hills, rivers, and ruins afford views and reflections. The negative cannot afford carries warning and pathos by turns: the missed holiday, the uninsured risk, the luxury glimpsed and set aside. Few verbs so quietly join economics and emotion, since nearly every cannot afford names something the speaker would dearly like.

Collocations and grammar: typical frames are afford something (afford rent, afford staff), afford to do something (afford to wait, afford to ignore), and the literary something affords something (the walk affords pleasure). Note the double-object flavour of the giving sense (afford us a glimpse) and the fixed warnings cannot afford to fail and can ill afford, where ill sharpens the shortage.
