SHINGLES is a famously polysemous English word with three quite different meanings and three separate etymological histories. In the source sentence it means loose rounded pebbles or stones — the shingle of a beach or riverbank, the mass of water-worn stones "piled" at the feet of the great gates. The same spelling names thin overlapping roof tiles and the painful viral disease herpes zoster.

Sense 1 — loose stones (the source meaning):

  • Rounded pebbles, especially on a seashore or riverbank; a stretch of shore covered with them is a "shingle beach" or "shingle bank".
  • Used collectively as a mass noun: "the crunch of shingle", "built on shingle". The plural form shingles is the usual word for scattered stones in quantity.
  • In the source text, the river has piled shingles — water-worn stones — against the feet of the clifflike gates, describing a gravelly beach.

Sense 2 — roof tiles:

  • A thin, flat piece of material (wood, slate, tile, asphalt) laid in overlapping rows on roofs and walls to shed water.
  • By extension, a covering built of such pieces; "to shingle" a roof or wall is to cover it with shingles.
  • Also a short-cropped woman's haircut of the 1920s-30s (the "shingle bob"), named for its layered overlapping look.

Sense 3 — the disease:

  • Herpes zoster, a viral infection causing a painful blistering rash along a nerve, so named because it often forms a belt-like band around the torso.
  • Plural in form, singular in use ("shingles is painful"), from Latin cingulum "girdle, belt".

Semantic reasoning and bridges:

  • The pebble sense comes from a dialectal Germanic word of uncertain origin, likely related to Norwegian singl "gravel, small stones" and to the Old Norse verb singla "to jingle, clink" — pebbles are what crunches and tinkles as they shift underfoot. It is a mass noun describing loose, water-worn stone.
  • The roof-tile sense is a different word: Old English scindel, from Late Latin scandula/scindula, from Latin scindere "to split, cleave" — a shingle is literally a thin piece of wood split from a log, hence "splinter". Overlap and layering gave the name to roofs and to the 1920s haircut.
  • The disease is a third word entirely: medieval Latin cingulus, from Latin cingere "to gird, surround" — the rash wraps the body like a belt. The English plural form follows medieval medical Latin cingula "girdles".

Usage notes:

  • In geography and building the two physical senses can collide in place names: "Shingle Street" (a shingle beach in Suffolk) beside "shingle roof".
  • "Shingles" (stones) is a collective or mass noun; "a shingle" (one stone) is rare outside dialect.
  • "Shingles" (disease) is grammatically plural-looking but singular: "shingles is caused by the varicella-zoster virus".
  • The roof sense has spawned "shingled" (covered with shingles) and the adjective "shingle-style" for American wooden architecture.

Register: the pebble sense is literary and coastal, common in British English and in nautical writing; the roof sense is everyday technical; the disease sense is standard medical. In the source text, the word's gritty, water-worn associations make it exactly right for the mouth of a river.
