- 15th-century English entry — literally "a leaping or springing up for joy": the physical body jumps, the visible eruption of gladness, the act of bounding upward with the whole frame.
- 16th–17th centuries — the leap becomes metaphor: exultation widens from bodily springing to any intense, triumphant rejoicing, always carried by outward cries and shouts, and it takes on a biblical, ceremonial elevation as the lofty word for public joy.
- 18th–19th centuries — the register hardens: exultation settles as the most Latinate, formal member of the joy-family, set against the calm interiority of elation, the communal carnival of jubilation, and the public narrative of triumph — a word for orators, poets, and scripture.
- Contemporary — consciously archaic and literary: exultation is chosen precisely because it sounds classical, the loud upper register of the soul; it is rarely applied to ordinary happiness and always keeps the shout of a victor's cry, the acoustic signature of triumph.
