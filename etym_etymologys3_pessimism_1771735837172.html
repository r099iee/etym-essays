The biography of “pessimism” begins, improbably, at the human foot. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *péd- meant “foot,” and it is one of the best-attested words of the entire family, surviving in Hittite pata-, in Tocharian pe, in Greek poús, in Sanskrit pád, in the Germanic *fōt- that gives English foot, and in Latin pēs — the ancestor of Italian piede, French pied, Spanish pie, Portuguese pé, and Romanian picior. Nothing in this oldest stratum suggests evil: a foot is simply the lowest part of the body, the part that touches the ground. But Latin seized on that geography of “lowness” and built from it a moral vocabulary. The adverb pessum meant “to the ground, downward” — to be cast pessum was to be sunk, ruined, hurled to the bottom — and from the same base Latin constructed a pair of graded forms: peior, “worse,” literally “lower,” and pessimus, “worst,” literally “lowest,” built as *ped-ios and *ped-semos. These two words were then drafted into the most irregular, suppletive adjective-paradigm of Latin, standing beside malus, “bad,” exactly as melior and optimus stand beside bonus, “good”: bad, worse, worst — malus, peior, pessimus. The “worst” was thus, etymologically, “that which lies at the very bottom,” the foot of all things. In Italian this ancient superlative survives almost unchanged — pessimo still means simply “very bad, awful” — while French pire, Spanish peor, Italian peggiore, and Portuguese pior keep the sister form peior alive. The modern noun, however, is much younger, and it was born in the philosophical wars of the eighteenth century. When Leibniz and his followers argued that God, being perfect, had necessarily chosen the best of all possible worlds, their school was named from Latin optimus, “best,” as optimisme — English optimism, attested from 1737 onward. The word cried out for an opposite, and in 1759 — the very year of Voltaire’s Candide, which savaged Leibnizian optimism with the Lisbon earthquake and the horrors of war — the French coinage pessimisme appeared, built on pessimus, “worst,” as the formal antonym of optimisme. The two words were born as a pair, philosophical weapons in the debate over providence and the problem of evil. English took the noun over as pessimism in 1794, at first purely as the name of the doctrine that this is the worst of all possible worlds. The word then began its long walk from the philosopher’s study to the market square. In the early nineteenth century pessimism remained a technical doctrine, associated above all with Arthur Schopenhauer, whose philosophy held that existence is fundamentally suffering and that the world is as bad as it could possibly be — a metaphysics elaborated in The World as Will and Representation (1818) and made notorious by his famous pessimism about life, desire, and history. From Schopenhauer the term passed into German philosophy (Pessimismus) and entered the wider European discussion through the pessimism controversy of the 1860s and 1870s, when Hartmann’s Philosophy of the Unconscious revived it, and when the specter of “the decline of the West” began to haunt intellectual discourse. The decisive semantic shift — the one the Gibbon passage relies on — came when pessimism generalized from doctrine to temperament and then to cultural mood. By the later nineteenth century the word no longer named only a metaphysical position: it named the disposition to expect the worst and to see the worst side of things, a recognizable character type of the modern age, cultivated in the melancholy of the fin de siècle. Writers from Baudelaire to Hardy were read as pessimists; the age itself was diagnosed as pessimistic. In the twentieth century the term broadened again into collective and systematic registers — economic pessimism on the trading floor, strategic pessimism in politics, cultural pessimism among intellectuals, the “pessimism bias” studied by psychologists — and into clinical contexts where persistent hopelessness appears as a symptom of depression. Everywhere the ancient image survives: the worst is, etymologically, the lowest, the thing that lies at the bottom, and to be pessimistic is to expect the ground to fall away. The socio-cultural dynamics of the shift are worth spelling out:
  • The theological frame: pessimism was born as the negative half of the optimism controversy — a doctrine about providence and the problem of evil, answered by Voltaire’s satire of “the best of all possible worlds.”
  • The philosophical frame: Schopenhauer gave pessimism its classic systematic form, making it a metaphysics rather than a mood, and German philosophy in turn made Pessimismus a term of high intellectual debate.
  • The temperamental turn: as religion’s hold loosened in the nineteenth century, the cosmic question shrank into a personal psychology — pessimism became the character of the one who expects the worst, respectable and even fashionable in a melancholy age.
  • The civilizational turn: in the twentieth century, pessimism attached to societies and empires — the conviction that a civilization is decaying, the very reading habit the Gibbon passage describes, in which Roman history becomes “a handy guide to the sources of decay in other empires and civilizations.”
  • The technical registers: economics, finance, and psychology each adopted the word for a measurable expectation — investor pessimism, the pessimism bias, defensive pessimism — completing its journey from metaphysics to metric.
Literary attestations trace the same arc. Candide (1759) dramatized the optimism it mocked and so gave pessimism its occasion; Schopenhauer’s aphorisms made the word a philosophy of life; in the twentieth century, Spengler’s Decline of the West (1918) gave civilizational pessimism its most famous book, and commentators from Churchill to Orwell invoked the word for the mood of the century. In the passage on Gibbon the word stands at the very top of this ladder — no longer a doctrine of the study nor a private gloom, but a fashionable, systemic conviction that Western civilization is in decline, a conviction so general that an ancient historian’s story of Rome has become the contemporary reader’s mirror. The irony the essayist turns on the word is precisely the irony of its whole career: pessimism began as a solemn philosophy and has become, as the sentence says, fashionable — a costume worn by a civilization contemplating its own ruins, with Gibbon’s pages as the handbook.
