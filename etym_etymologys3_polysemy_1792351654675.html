The historical, philological, and conceptual evolution of the noun polysemy exemplifies how late nineteenth-century European linguistics broke away from classical prescriptive grammar and evolutionary biology to establish semantics as an autonomous scientific discipline. Although formed entirely from classical Ancient Greek roots, polysemy is not an ancient Greco-Roman term; it is a deliberate, highly successful modern scholarly coinage conceived in Paris in the final years of the nineteenth century to name a universal cognitive phenomenon that classical rhetoricians had observed for millennia but had never rigorously systematized as an essential property of language architecture.

Ancient Greek Etymological Roots and Morphological Architecture:

  • The First Element (*polus*): The prefixal combining form poly- derives from Ancient Greek polús (πολύς, neuter polú, stem polv- / poll-), meaning "many, numerous, large in quantity or extent." This descends from Proto-Indo-European *polh₁- or *pelu- ("abundant, much"), which generated a vast family of cognates across the Indo-European world, including Sanskrit purú ("much, abundant"), Old Persian paru, Gothic filu, Old High German filu (modern German viel), and Old English feolu / fela (modern English many / full via related nasalized formations).
  • The Second Element (*sēma*): The root element derives from Ancient Greek sêma (σῆμα, stem sēmat-), signifying "a sign, mark, token, grave-mound, omen, signal, or military emblem." In Hellenistic philosophy and early grammar, the derivative verb sēmaínō (σημαίνω, "to signify, indicate, give a sign") and the abstract noun sēmasía (σημασία, "signification, meaning") became the standard vocabulary for analyzing how verbal utterances express underlying thought. Comparative linguists traditionally trace sêma back to Proto-Indo-European *dʰyeh₂- ("to notice, see, observe"), which also produced Sanskrit dhyāyati ("he meditates, reflects") and dhyāna (the source of Chinese Chan and Japanese Zen).
  • Classical Antecedents: In Hellenistic Greek, the compound adjective polúsēmos (πολύσημος) existed in a descriptive capacity, meaning "having many meanings," "portending many omens," or "having varied significance." Aristotle, in his Poetics and Rhetoric, cautioned against the rhetorical pitfalls of ambiguous words, noting that nouns with multiple meanings are useful primarily to poets seeking grandeur and sophists seeking to confound their interlocutors, whereas the philosopher should aim for precise clarity.

The 1897 Coining by Michel Bréal and the Birth of Semantics:

  • Michel Bréal's Masterwork: The formal introduction of the abstract noun into modern science occurred in 1897 with the publication of Essai de sémantique (Science des significations) by the eminent French comparative philologist Michel Bréal (1832–1915). Bréal had already coined the very word sémantique (semantics) in an 1883 article to describe the historical study of meaning change, deliberately creating a counterpart to phonetics and morphology.
  • Bréal's Theoretical Breakthrough: In Chapter XIV of his 1897 treatise, titled "La Polysémie," Bréal challenged the prevailing nineteenth-century biological metaphor that treated languages as living organisms whose words underwent a "struggle for life" where older senses died when new ones appeared. Bréal observed that when a word acquires a new meaning, the ancient sense does not instantly vanish; rather, the word expands its scope, harboring both senses simultaneously without conflict because surrounding context immediately disambiguates the intended meaning for the listener: "Le sens nouveau ne met pas fin à l'ancien. Ils subsistent tous deux côte à côte... Cette multiplication de sens s'appelle la polysémie."
  • Transmission into English: Bréal's foundational work was translated into English in 1900 by Mrs. Henry Cust under the title Semantics: Studies in the Science of Meaning, with an introduction by John Pentland Mahaffy. The English Anglicized noun polysemy was rapidly adopted across British and American linguistic circles, establishing itself within the lexicographical lexicon over the first two decades of the twentieth century.

Twentieth-Century Structuralism to Cognitive Semantics:

  • Structuralist Systematization: During the mid-twentieth century, structural linguists such as Stephen Ullmann (The Principles of Semantics, 1951) and John Lyons codified the precise methodological distinction between polysemy and homonymy, analyzing how semantic fields maintain equilibrium through polysemic shifts.
  • The Cognitive Turn: Beginning in the 1980s, cognitive linguists (George Lakoff, Mark Johnson, Claudia Brugman, Dirk Geeraerts) revolutionized the understanding of polysemy. Rather than viewing polysemy as an accidental collection of divergent definitions that clutter a dictionary, they demonstrated that polysemy is direct, empirical evidence of how the human brain uses metaphorical mapping from embodied physical experience to construct abstract thought.
