- 14th–15th c. — Italian craft polish: in Renaissance Italy, lustro emerged as a workshop term among armorers, goldsmiths, and painters for the refined sheen given to polished metals, marble, and varnishes.
- 1520s–1580s — French and English adoption: borrowed into Middle French as lustre and entered Elizabethan English (c. 1580s), denoting both physical gloss and figurative personal renown (used by Shakespeare in Troilus and Cressida).
- 17th c. — decorative arts and lighting: expanded in England to denote cut-glass chandeliers and crystal candelabra that multiplied candlelight through faceted prisms.
- 18th c. — mineralogy and ceramics: formalized in natural history as a diagnostic property of minerals, while English potters developed Staffordshire lustreware using metallic glazes.
- 19th c. — victorian romantic aesthetics: John Ruskin and Walter Pater celebrated lustre as the gentle, non-aggressive optical radiance of great art and historical memory, contrasting with modern industrial glare.
- 20th c. — cultural and commercial idioms: preserved in mineralogy and luxury commerce, while giving rise to widespread idiomatic expressions such as ‘to add lustre’ or ‘to lose one’s lustre’.
- Contemporary — prestige and finish: remains standard for high-end materials (lustre finish in photography, hair care, cosmetics) and elevated descriptions of institutional reputation.
