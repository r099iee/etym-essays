Wearisome is an adjective meaning “causing weariness” — that is, producing fatigue, tedium, or boredom. It describes things that exhaust the body by sheer effort or exhaust the patience by sheer dullness: a wearisome climb, a wearisome journey, a wearisome argument, a wearisome day of waiting. In Tolkien’s sentence, “lonesome and wearisome” describes Bilbo’s state at the door of the Mountain — the adjective points to the draining, dreary monotony of long anxious waiting.

Primary senses

  • Causing physical fatigue: tasks, journeys, or labours that tire the body — “a wearisome march over the hills”.
  • Causing mental tedium or boredom: long, dull, repetitive activities or words — “a wearisome lecture”, “wearisome formalities”.
  • Irksome, trying the patience: people or circumstances that grate because they drag on — “a wearisome companion”, “wearisome delays”.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Of the person affected (older, looser use): dictionaries also record “wearisome” in the sense of “weary, tired” describing a person’s own state; this is now felt as archaic or poetic, and modern standard usage restricts the word to the thing that causes the weariness.
  • Moral and existential nuance: in religious and philosophical writing the wearisome is the burdensome — the world-weariness of Ecclesiastes, “all things are full of weariness” — so the word can shade into “oppressive, depressing”.
  • Humorous hyperbole: in conversation, “wearisome” is often used with mild irony of trivial annoyances — a wearisome meeting, a wearisome neighbour — where “tedious” would be plainer and “exhausting” too strong.

Composition

  • The word is a transparent compound of weary (tired, exhausted — Old English wērig) and the adjective-forming suffix -some (Old English -sum, “having the quality of, tending to”).
  • It therefore means, almost literally, “having the quality of causing weariness”, just as tiresome means “causing tiredness” and loathsome “causing loathing”.
  • Unlike weary (which describes how you feel) or wearied (what has been done to you), wearisome names the quality in the cause: it is the objective, outward counterpart of the subjective, inward weary.

The weary / wearied / wearisome triangle

  • Weary = the feeling: “I was weary after the march”.
  • Wearied = the state produced in someone: “they were wearied by the wait”.
  • Wearisome = the quality of the cause: “the wearisome wait”. A wearisome thing makes you weary; it is not itself tired.

Register and usage

  • Standard, slightly literary; more elegant than “boring”, more precise than “tiring”.
  • Common collocations: wearisome task, wearisome journey, wearisome repetition, wearisome wait, wearisome arguments, wearisome years.
  • Often found in narrative prose for atmosphere; in Tolkien it doubles the dreariness of “lonesome” — loneliness and tedium together drain the patience of the waiting hobbit.
