FEVERED is both the past tense and past participle of the rare verb fever ("to affect with fever") and, far more commonly, an adjective meaning (1) affected with or suffering from a fever; (2) accompanied by, caused by, or resembling a fever - hot, flushed, agitated; and (3) figuratively, intensely excited, frenzied, or at a heightened emotional or imaginative pitch ("fevered imagination", "fevered excitement"). In the supplied context - "a more fevered shaking was in his legs" - the adjective qualifies the trembling of Bilbo's legs: trembling so violent and involuntary that it resembles the shaking of a person in fever, the body's reaction to terror and exertion combined.

Primary senses

  • Literally, affected with fever: suffering from an abnormally high body temperature and its symptoms. "The fevered child tossed in the sheets." Now more literary and emphatic than the everyday "feverish".
  • Resembling or suggesting fever: of symptoms, states, and appearances - a hot forehead, flushed cheeks, glittering eyes, trembling limbs. "Fevered shaking" in the source context.
  • Figuratively, of intense mental or emotional states: "a fevered imagination", "fevered dreams", "fevered ambition" - imagination, desire, or fear working at a pitch so high it feels like an illness.
  • Figuratively, of activity and atmosphere: "fevered haste", "fevered activity", "fevered pitch" - urgent, hectic, frantic action, often in journalism and narrative prose.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Of climates, air, and places: "fevered air", "fevered swamps" - air or ground that causes fever (a 17th-19th century medical-geographical sense tied to the miasma theory of disease). "The fevered jungle."
  • Of discourse and creativity: "fevered prose", "fevered imagining" - writing or thought produced at a peak of excitement, sometimes implying slight irrationality.
  • Of markets and crowds: "fevered trading", "fevered speculation" - speculative manias; the borrowed heat of financial journalism.
  • As a participle of the verb "fever": "The summer sun fevered the sick man" (rare, archaic-sounding). The verb is attested from the 16th century but is now literary.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridges

The semantic history is short and simple. The noun "fever" (a bodily heat) was turned into a verb "to fever" in the 16th century; from the verb came the participle-adjective "fevered" (literally "made hot with fever"), attested from the mid-17th century. Once the adjective existed meaning "in a fever", the bridge to metaphor was immediate: anything running at a heat out of control - an imagination, a market, a crowd, a pair of legs shaking - could be called "fevered". The word differs from "feverish" in that "fevered" carries a stronger sense of something actively heated to excess, whereas "feverish" describes a general condition of being fever-like. The figurative sense ("fevered pitch", "fevered excitement") became especially prominent in 19th-century journalism and Victorian Gothic fiction.

Register and nuance

  • Literary and emphatic: "fevered" is rarer and more vivid than "feverish", which is the everyday adjective.
  • Figurative uses dominate modern usage: in contemporary texts "fevered" is more often figurative ("fevered imagination") than medical.
  • The word nearly always implies excess, intensity, and a loss of control - heat, trembling, and haste.
  • In the source sentence the word is exactly right: Bilbo's legs shake as if with fever - beyond his control, hot with terror and exertion, utterly unlike the deliberate trembling of the climb down.
