Commenced is the past of commence, whose ancestry runs from the simplest PIE verb of motion, to go, through Latin metaphors of entering, to French storytelling and English law. It shows how going in came to mean beginning.

The foundation is PIE *h1ey-, to go. This root supplies Greek eimi, I go, Latin eo, ire, to go, Sanskrit eti, he goes, and English past went via suppletion. Motion is its essence.

  • Go to enter: Latin in + ire gives inire, to go into, enter upon. Its noun initium, a going in, entrance, soon means beginning, as entering a new space is starting a new state. Religious language of initiation, initiare, to initiate, admits the novice by leading him in.
  • Initiate to begin together: Vulgar Latin prefixes com-, together, to initiare, creating *cominitiare, to initiate together, to begin jointly. The com- adds collectivity and completeness typical of late administrative Latin. This unattested but securely reconstructed form is required to explain French forms.
  • Old French comencier: By the 12th century comencier means to begin, in epics, romances, and charters. Chanson openings ci commence, here begins, are formulaic. Phonetic change palatalizes c before e and simplifies nasal vowels, giving Norman and Central French variants that English borrows.
  • Middle English commensen: Borrowed around 1300, commence at first competes with native begin and start. It wins the high register: chronicles, statutes, and liturgy prefer commence for dignified inception, leaving begin for ordinary action.
  • Modern commence / commenced: The verb regularizes as weak commence, commenced, commencing. American English preserves it most visibly in commencement ceremony for graduation, where ending school is framed as beginning life. British legal and railway usage keeps works commenced, proceedings commenced.

Phonetically the chain shows Romance lenition and English stress shift. Latin -ti- before vowel becomes -ci- /ts/ then French -c- /s/, hence initiare to comencier. English restores Latin spelling with -mm- and -ce, pronouncing /komens/ with stress on the second syllable, RP /kamens/. Past -ed adds /t/, giving /kamenst/.

Semantically the drift is upward in formality. What was once ordinary begin in French becomes marked formal begin in English because English already had begin. This is classic borrowing stratification after 1066: native words for everyday, French words for law and ceremony. To commence to don therefore sounds deliberately grand, turning dressing into a rite. Court records, university charters, and military logs keep the solemn sense alive, while comic writers exploit it for bathos. The tiny fragment He did so, and I commenced to don captures that perfectly: obedience and robing narrated as if a campaign had begun.

Additional note: the persistence of commenced to plus infinitive in memoirs and minutes long after begin to became normal shows deliberate archaism as a resource. Speakers reach for commenced when they want distance, dignity, or deadpan humour, proving that etymology lives not only in sound change but in register choice preserved across centuries.
