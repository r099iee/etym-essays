- Mid-15th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1440–1475) - Coined in late medieval England as a compound of *nose* + *gay* (in the archaic sense of "a pretty ornament, toy, or source of joy"); designated a small bunch of sweet-smelling flowers and herbs carried to delight the nostrils.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Stuart Eras) - Crucial sanitary and medical adoption under miasma theory; carried by nobility and urbanites to repel foul street stenches and ward off bubonic plague; presented to High Court judges at the Old Bailey to protect against "jail fever" (typhus).
- 18th Century (Georgian Era) - Widely carried at public assemblies, theaters, and masquerades; figurative usage flourishes as a title for published anthologies and moral miscellanies (*A Nosegay of Verses*).
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes an indispensable accessory of Victorian courtly romance and floriography; carried in ornate silver tussie-mussie holders; immortalized in Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend* as the Miller's casual trophy of exploitation.
- 20th to 21st Century - Retained in contemporary bridal floristry as a prestigious, technical term for a tight, symmetrical, dome-shaped handheld bouquet, while preserved in British royal and judicial ceremonies.
