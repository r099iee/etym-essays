The evolution of commotion is the history of an idea of movement slowly accreting noise, politics, and psychology. The chain begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *mewə- / *meuə-, 'to push away, to thrust off', a root whose exact vocalism is debated but whose semantics — the idea of setting something in motion — are among the most widespread in the family. In Latin this root produced movēre, 'to move', one of the language's great verbal trunks: from it come English move, motion, momentum, mobile, and automobile. The step that matters for commotion is the compound commovēre, built with the prefix com- ('together, thoroughly'). In Roman usage commovēre meant not merely to move but to move with force, to shake up, to stir into action — Cicero speaks of commovēre animum, 'to agitate the mind', and Caesar of armies being stirred up. The Latin noun-formation machinery then did its work: from the supine/participle stem commōt- plus the suffix -iō, Latin produced commōtiō, 'a violent moving, an agitation', a word that in classical prose could describe anything from an earthquake's shudder to the stir of a crowd to a tremor of the emotions. The phonetic journey from Latin commōtiō to English commotion is typical of the Romance transmission. Latin long ō in the first syllable was borrowed into Old French and Anglo-Norman and shortened and slackened in pronunciation; the sequence -tiō became -cion, -cioun in French and then in Middle English (commocioun, commotion), where the Latin spelling -tio was progressively restored under learned influence while the pronunciation kept the sibilant (/ʃən/), producing the modern written -tion. The prefix com- kept its form in the written language; the English spelling 'commotion' was fixed by the 16th century. Semantically the word travelled along a striking arc. In Middle English (c. 1380–1400) commotion still meant primarily violent physical agitation and shaking — Chaucer-era English uses it of the stir of the elements and of bodies. By the 16th century a decisive extension had taken hold: commotion became the standard term of chronicles and state papers for insurrection and civil disorder — 'the commons made a commotion', 'commotions in the realm'. The key semantic bridge is that rebellion literally sets the body politic in motion: the 'movement' of a people is the political analogue of the physical shaking of a thing, and the noise is the clamour of protest. Shakespeare exploits precisely this register in his history plays, where 'commotion' names the turbulence of civil war and popular rising that threatens the throne. In the 17th and 18th centuries the word then turned inward as well as outward. Alongside public commotion, writers used commotion for the agitation of the passions and the mind — 'a commotion of the spirits', 'the commotion of grief' — transferring the crowd's visible turbulence to the hidden churning of feeling. At the same time, medicine kept the original 'motion' sense alive in the technical phrase commotio cerebri, 'shaking of the brain', used for the jarring of the brain in a blow, the condition later renamed concussion; even today commotio appears in neurological Latin alongside contusio. This technical survival is a beautiful fossil of the word's first, purely kinetic meaning. By the 19th century the gravity had begun to erode. Dickens could use commotion of street brawls and parliamentary uproar, Conrad of the mutinous stirrings of a ship's crew, and the novelists of empire of the tremors of native unrest — but in ordinary domestic speech the word was steadily lightening toward general hubbub. In modern English a commotion is most often a sudden noisy fuss: children upstairs, a dropped crate in a market, a row in a shop doorway. The political sense remains available (news reports still speak of 'commotions' in foreign capitals), but its register has shifted from the grave to the everyday. A final sociolinguistic note: commotion has remained a remarkably neutral word — it is not slang, not vulgar, not markedly formal — which is precisely why it has survived so long across such different registers. Its power lies in the fossilized Latin 'motion' at its heart: however lightened by daily use, the word still promises that something is moving, restlessly and out of control, and that the noise we hear is only the surface of a disturbance in progress. It is this promise of motion, inherited unchanged from commōtiō, that makes 'the commotion they made filled all the forest' so effective: the forest is not just loud, it is set in turmoil by a moving, raging force.
