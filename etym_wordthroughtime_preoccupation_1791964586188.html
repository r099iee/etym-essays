- 1550s — preoccupation enters English in the literal sense “occupation beforehand, prior possession”; legalistic and rare.
- 17th c. — transferred to the mind: a prepossession or bias, and in rhetoric the anticipating of an objection; French préoccupation adds the colour of anxious concern.
- 18th c. — used for the state of being already engaged or taken up; still formal, still close to the literal sense.
- 19th c. — the modern psychological sense crystallizes: absorption, absent-mindedness, a mind elsewhere; Victorian psychology and fiction make it familiar.
- 20th c. — extended to collective and political life (“a national preoccupation”) and to clinical descriptions of anxious or obsessive states.
- 21st c. — the dominant sense is a ruling concern, often mildly critical (“our preoccupation with celebrity”), while the adjective preoccupied has softened into everyday “distracted”.
