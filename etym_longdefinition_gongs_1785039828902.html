**GONGS (plural noun) — at its plainest core** A **gong** is a percussion instrument consisting of a broad, flat, or slightly bossed disc of hammered bronze (or other metal) that is hung from a frame and struck with a padded mallet to produce a deep, swelling, reverberating tone. The plural **gongs** denotes the instruments themselves as a set, and in the goblin chant of the Misty Mountains the line "Knocker and gongs!" names the instruments of the goblins' own deafening racket, beating brutal time with their underground march. The word is a relatively late arrival in English — a loanword from the Austronesian world — and that foreignness is part of its nuance. **Primary sense and the literary context** In the goblin verse, the gong is at once exotic and ominous. Because the word entered European vocabulary only in the age of exploration, as a loan from Southeast Asia, it carries to English ears a suggestion of the foreign and the barbaric — of alien temple rites and war-signals — which Tolkien exploits brilliantly: the goblins beat gongs not for music but for terror, and the deep, rolling, doomsday resonance of struck bronze seems to issue from the very rock of the mountain. Paired with the "knocker," the line conjures a subterranean din of iron against bronze, a percussive assault that keeps brutal time with the goblins' march. **The musical instrument itself** As an instrument, a gong is distinguished by its material and its method of tone production. Unlike bells, which are cup-shaped, carry their own internal clapper, and are rung rather than beaten, a gong is a flat or bossed metal disc struck externally with a mallet. The struck disc vibrates over its whole surface, producing a complex, throbbing, enveloping sound that swells, hangs in the air, and slowly decays. In the gamelan orchestras of Java and Bali — the gong's ancestral home — tuned gongs and gong-chimes mark the phrase-structure of the music with long, resonant strokes; in the West, the gong entered the orchestra in the nineteenth century as a single, dramatic instrument of climax and menace. **Polysemy and secondary modern senses** Beyond the instrument, the word has accumulated a distinctive set of secondary meanings:
  • The signalling instrument: from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the gong became a household and institutional object — the dinner gong, the tea gong — struck to summon servants or announce meals; a gong is any struck metal disc used as a call-signal.
  • The verb "to gong": attested from 1853, meaning to summon or signal with a gong, and later, in show-business slang, to dismiss a failing act by striking a gong (the "gong-show" of twentieth-century variety theatre).
  • Military slang "gongs": in British armed-forces slang of the World War I era, "gongs" means medals or decorations worn on the chest — the shiny bronze discs recalling the instrument.
  • Jazz and film usage: in jazz, film scores, and radio drama the gong is the standard sign of exotic menace, suspense, and impending doom — a sound that swells and hangs, which is exactly the register Tolkien taps in the goblin chant.
**Distinctions from related instruments**
  • Bells: similar because both are metal percussion producing loud, resonant sound; differ because bells are cup-shaped, carry their own internal clapper, and are rung, whereas gongs are flat or bossed discs beaten externally with a mallet.
  • Cymbals: similar because both are struck metal; differ because cymbals are two thin plates clashed together for a bright, metallic crash, while a gong gives a sustained, throbbing hum.
  • Tam-tam: similar because both are large struck metal discs; differ because the tam-tam is a gong of indefinite pitch used for an engulfing roar, while a tuned gong has a definite, pitched tone.
  • Drums: similar because both are percussion instruments; differ because drums produce sound from a stretched membrane, while gongs sound from the resonance of solid metal.
  • Chimes: similar because both are struck metal; differ because chimes are tuned tubes or rods struck individually for melody, while a gong is a single disc producing one massive, complex tone.
**Semantic reasoning and historical bridge** The whole semantic history of the word is a study in loanword assimilation. Entering English as a foreign curiosity in travel-writing (c. 1600), the gong passed from an exotic temple instrument to a domestic summoning device to a military slang term for medals, each step widening the context while keeping the core idea of a struck, resonant bronze disc. The figurative "dismissal by gong" of the gong-show is a late, theatrical twist: the sound that once called people together now sends them off. In Tolkien's line, all these layers are available at once — the deep doomsday resonance, the alien origin, and the function of keeping brutal, mechanical time in the dark.
