A gaggle is most familiarly a collective noun for geese, specifically a flock of geese when on land or water as opposed to a skein or wedge when in flight, and by extension any noisy, chattering, disorderly cluster of people, especially one that moves or talks together in a distracting, giggly, or intrusive way. The word is inherently auditory and slightly pejorative, evoking gabbling cackles, overlapping voices, and lack of discipline, as in a gaggle of journalists, a gaggle of schoolchildren, or, as in the source passage, a gaggle of computer magazines and ecology newsletters piled together in miscellaneous chattering plenty.

As a noun, gaggle belongs to the medieval tradition of venery terms, fanciful collective nouns for animals codified in 15th-century hunting manuals, alongside a murder of crows, a pride of lions, and an exaltation of larks. While many such terms were literary inventions, gaggle had genuine onomatopoeic grounding in the cackling of geese. Modern usage preserves both literal ornithological sense and human transferred sense, with the human sense now dominant in journalism. To say a gaggle of tourists swarmed the lobby is to suggest not just number but noise, gawking, and mildly comic disorder.

  • Ornithological collective: a flock of geese on ground or water, e.g. a gaggle of geese crossed the green. Polysemy bridge: directly imitative of geese cries gag-gag-gag, naming the group by its characteristic sound.
  • Human collective pejorative-humorous: a noisy crowd, e.g. a gaggle of reporters, a gaggle of teenagers. Polysemy bridge: via metaphor transferring goose-cackle to human chatter, mapping animal noise onto social noise and implying silliness or intrusiveness.
  • Verb to gaggle: rare or dialectal, to cackle like a goose, to chatter noisily. Polysemy bridge: via zero-derivation back from noun to sound verb, reviving original imitative verb sense.
  • Extended inanimate collective humorous: as in source, a gaggle of magazines, a gaggle of emails. Polysemy bridge: via further humorous extension where even voiceless objects are imagined as chattering crowd because of miscellaneous piled abundance.

Register is informal, vivid, and faintly mocking, never neutral like group or flock. It carries connotations of femininity in older sexist use such as a gaggle of girls, though modern style guides discourage that, and of media scrum in political reporting such as a gaggle of press following a candidate. Unlike herd, which implies mindless following, or crowd, which is neutral about sound, gaggle specifically foregrounds high-pitched gabbling and lack of order. Unlike skein, which is poetic and airborne, gaggle is earthbound, waddling, and comic.

Literary and journalistic nuance exploits this comedy: P. G. Wodehouse, tabloids, and political blogs love gaggle for its alliterative punch in gaggle of hacks or gaggle of groupies. In children's books geese remain a gaggle to teach collective nouns, preserving medieval wordplay. Grammatically it takes of plus plural, behaves as singular collective with plural agreement variable, and rarely appears without its of-phrase. Its charm lies in sound symbolism, the hard g repeated suggesting throaty honks, making it memorable long after more neutral collectives fade.
