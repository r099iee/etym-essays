- 6th c. BCE (c. 522 BCE) — Borne in Old Persian as Dārayavahuš, signifying sovereign virtue, righteous rule, and cosmic alignment with the truth (arta) under Ahuramazda.
- 5th c. BCE — Recorded in Classical Greek historiography (Herodotus) and theater (Aeschylus's tragedy The Persians) as Dareîos, depicted as an awe-inspiring, wise Oriental monarch whose hubris nevertheless brought ruin to his army at Marathon.
- 4th c. BCE — Darius III became the classical archetype of tragic imperial ruin, fleeing before Alexander the Great and assassinated by his own treacherous satrap Bessus.
- Hellenistic & Roman Era — Venerated in Jewish Scripture (Ezra, Nehemiah) as an enlightened patron of the Temple in Jerusalem; adapted into Latin as Dārīus.
- Middle Ages & Renaissance — Celebrated in medieval romance and literature (such as Chaucer's Monk's Tale) as an exemplar of worldly majesty overturned by Fortune's wheel.
- Modern Era — Permanently established in international historiography as one of history's greatest imperial administrators, and widely popular across the globe as a classic given name.
