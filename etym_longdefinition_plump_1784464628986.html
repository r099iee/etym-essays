PLUMP — a word that straddles several word classes and two related but distinct branches of meaning. As an adjective it means 'pleasingly full and rounded'; as a verb it means 'to drop or set down heavily and abruptly' (and, in a separate development, 'to make full and soft'); as an adverb it means 'heavily, bluntly, directly, without ceremony'; and in the idiom 'to plump for' it means 'to choose or vote decisively'. The card's context uses the verb and adverb sense: 'Bilbo plumped down the beer and the cake' — set them down heavily, abruptly, unceremoniously. Primary Sense 1 — Verb: to drop or set down heavily
  • Definition: to fall, or to set something down, with a heavy, abrupt, blunt impact; to deposit unceremoniously.
  • Core examples: 'she plumped her bag on the table', 'he plumped himself down on the sofa', 'Bilbo plumped down the beer and the cake'.
  • Character: kinetic and acoustic — the sense encodes weight, suddenness, and a dull contact; no elegant serving, no careful placing.
Primary Sense 2 — Adjective: full, rounded, pleasantly fleshy
  • Definition: having a full, round, pleasingly plump shape; well-filled and soft rather than thin or angular.
  • Core examples: 'a plump fruit', 'plump cheeks', 'a plump figure', 'plump cushions'.
  • Register: generally affectionate or neutral, of persons, animals, fruit, and soft objects; not the harsh word for obesity.
Secondary and extended senses
  • Verb 'to plump up': to make something soft fuller — 'plump up the pillows', 'plump up the cushion' — fluffing it back to roundness by shaking or patting.
  • Adverb: directly, bluntly, heavily — 'he fell plump into the water', 'she came plump against the wall'; meaning 'straight, without hesitation'.
  • Idiom 'to plump for': to choose or vote decisively for one person or option, 'to come down heavily on one side' — 'he plumped for the cheaper option'; originally political, of dropping one's whole vote decisively on a single candidate.
  • Sound-symbolic use: the interjection 'plump!' for a heavy, dull splash or thud.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridges
  • The drop-verb, the adverb, and the interjection belong to a North Sea Germanic family of sound-symbolic words for heavy, blunt falling (Middle Dutch plompen, Middle Low German plumpen 'to plunge, fall flat'); the word's shape — the initial pl- cluster and the short -ump — mimics a heavy soft mass striking something.
  • The adjective 'plump' is a related but distinct branch of the same Low-Countries expressive family: Middle Dutch plomp meant 'blunt, thick, lumpy, dull', and the sense of a full, rounded, pleasantly heavy shape developed from the idea of something thick and soft.
  • The idiom 'plump for' grew out of the drop-verb's decisive weight: to 'come down' on one side of a question, then to let one's vote 'drop' on a candidate — the physical thud of commitment becoming a decision.
  • The two branches — dropping and roundness — are usually treated by dictionaries as separate words of identical spelling (plump v.1 / plump adj.), though both draw on the same expressive, imitative wellspring; there is no classical Latin or PIE root to unite them.
Usage notes
  • Register: plump (drop) is informal and vivid, old-fashioned but alive; plump (adjective) is everyday and generally kind; 'plump for' is standard idiom.
  • In The Hobbit, 'plumped down the beer and the cake' captures Bilbo's fraying patience: the refreshments hit the table with the blunt energy of a heavy, irritated fact, the very opposite of a hobbit's usual careful hospitality.
