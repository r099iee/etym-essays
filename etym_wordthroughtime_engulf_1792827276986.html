- 1550s — swallowed by the waters: born as a literal verb of drowning and plunging into a gulf or whirlpool, used of ships, shores, and sinners in sermons.
- 17th century — sermons and seas: preachers warn that hell and the deep engulf the ungodly, while travel writers describe coasts and reefs engulfing vessels; a grave, physical word.
- 18th century — feelings learn to drown: the novel of sensibility turns the sea inward, so grief, melancholy, and passion begin to engulf heroines as surely as tides engulf rocks.
- 19th century — empire and geology: lava, jungle, desert, and fog engulf explorers and outposts in travel literature, while cities fear being engulfed by slums, smoke, and the crowd.
- 20th century — the headline devourer: war engulfs continents, flames engulf hotels, inflation and debt engulf savings; the verb becomes the journalist's favourite for any total disaster.
- Today — menace and occasional beauty: still overwhelmingly grave — fear, fire, flood, war — but nature writers also let light, music, and blossom engulf, keeping a softer edge of wonder inside the drowning.
