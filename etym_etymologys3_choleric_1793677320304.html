1. Proto-Indo-European Origins and the Chromatic Conception of Bile

The deepest etymological origin of choleric traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰel- (or in laryngeal reconstruction *gʰelh₃-), an ancient verbal and adjectival base signifying "to shine, gleam," which developed into an extensive family of color designations specifically denoting vivid yellow, yellowish-green, or bright golden hues. In the ancestral Indo-European worldview, brilliant light and gleaming vitality were chromatically associated with the bright green shoots of springing vegetation and the vivid, bitter yellowish-green secretions of the digestive viscera. From this singular root arose an immense spectrum of cognates across Indo-European branches, including English yellow, gold, and green (via Proto-Germanic *gelwaz), Latin galbus ("yellow"), Slavic žьltъ ("yellow"), and the Germanic words for digestive secretions such as Old English gealla and modern English gall.

In the Proto-Hellenic dialectal continuum (circa 1500–1200 BCE), this root underwent regular sound changes, shifting the Proto-Indo-European voiced aspirated velar stop *gʰ into the voiceless aspirated velar plosive *kʰ (written in the Greek alphabet as chi, χ), yielding the Proto-Hellenic nominal form *kʰolā́. This noun specifically denoted the bitter, greenish-yellow fluid produced by the liver and stored in the gall bladder—what modern biology terms bile.

2. Classical Greek Medicine and the Genesis of Humoral Theory

By the time of the Homeric epics and the Archaic period of Greece (c. 8th–6th centuries BCE), the noun had crystallized into Ionic-Attic χολή (kholḗ), signifying "bile" or "gall." In Homeric psychology, emotions were intensely somaticized and physically localized in bodily organs; hence, bitterness, resentment, and vindictive anger were envisioned as pooling directly in the gall bladder or burning within the liver. From kholē, Greek speakers derived the adjective χολερικός (kholerikós), formed with the productive adjectival suffix -ικός (-ikos), denoting "pertaining to, suffering from, or characterized by bile."

The decisive intellectual transformation of the term occurred in the 5th and 4th centuries BCE within the Hippocratic Corpus, particularly in the foundational treatise On the Nature of Man (De Natura Hominis), attributed to Hippocrates' son-in-law Polybus. In this text, humoralism was systematically formulated as a comprehensive physiological model. The human organism was conceived as being composed of four humors: blood (haima), phlegm (phlegma), yellow bile (xanthē kholē), and black bile (melaina kholē). Each humor possessed distinct elemental qualities:

  • Yellow Bile (choler): Naturally hot and dry, matching the cosmic element of Fire, the season of summer, and qualities of dryness, ambition, and swift combustion.
  • Black Bile (melancholy): Cold and dry, corresponding to Earth and autumn, producing quiet despondency and contemplation.
  • Blood (sanguine): Hot and moist, corresponding to Air and spring, generating optimism, social warmth, and cheer.
  • Phlegm (phlegmatic): Cold and moist, corresponding to Water and winter, inducing apathy, calm, and sluggishness.
Simultaneously, Greek physicians coined the noun χολέρα (kholéra) to describe a catastrophic, acute medical affliction characterized by the violent evacuation of bile through vomiting and diarrhea. In classical Greek nosology, therefore, kholerikos referred both to a patient suffering from this torrential intestinal disorder and to someone afflicted with an excess of yellow bile.

3. Galenic Systematization and Late Latin Transmission

In the 2nd century CE, the Greco-Roman physician Galen of Pergamon synthesized Hippocratic humoralism into an overarching theory of constitutional temperaments (complexiones). Galen argued that individual character, physical appearance, and disease susceptibility were determined by the prevailing mixture (krasis) of the four humors. An individual in whom yellow bile predominated was labeled kholerikos. Galen observed that such persons were lean, dry, hot-blooded, easily excited to fury, swift in action, and prone to inflammatory fevers.

As Greek philosophical and medical scholarship was assimilated into the Roman Empire, the Greek adjective was borrowed directly into Late Latin during the 3rd and 4th centuries CE as cholericus, alongside the noun cholera ("yellow bile" or "the disease cholera"). Latin authors, including Caelius Aurelianus and later Isidore of Seville in his 7th-century encyclopedic Etymologiae, preserved the dual diagnostic meaning: cholericus designated both the patient racked by bilious cholera and the irascible individual dominated by fiery bodily bile.

4. Medieval Arabic Scholarship, Salerno, and Old French Adoption

Following the decline of the Western Roman Empire, classical humoral medicine was preserved, refined, and expanded by Byzantine and Islamic scholars, most notably Hunayn ibn Ishaq, Rhazes (al-Razi), and Avicenna (Ibn Sina) in his monumental Canon of Medicine. During the 11th and 12th centuries, these Arabic texts were translated back into Latin at the medical school of Salerno and in Toledo, reigniting scholastic interest across Western Europe.

Through Gallo-Romance phonological evolution, Latin cholericus passed into Old French during the 13th century as colerique (frequently variant-spelled cholerique). In Old French, the initial Latin voiceless velar stop ch- (/k/) was represented as c-, and the word gained immense currency in courtly and philosophical literature to describe both a medical imbalance and a haughty, quick-tempered aristocratic bearing.

5. Middle English Ingestion and Chaucerian Literary Attestation

Following the Norman Conquest and the pervasive bilingualism of Anglo-Norman England, the word entered Middle English during the late 14th century as colerik or cholerik. One of the earliest and most celebrated literary attestations occurs in the works of Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1387). In the General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales, Chaucer presents an exquisite, textbook portrait of the humoral archetype in his description of the Reeve:

"The Reve was a sclendre colerik man. / His berd was shave as nygh as ever he can; / His heer was by his eres ful round yshorn..."
Chaucer emphasizes the Reeve's lean, dried-out physique, sharp temper, and dreaded administrative severity, all direct manifestations of his choleric constitution. Furthermore, in The Nun's Priest's Tale, when the rooster Chauntecleer is tormented by nightmares of a predatory beast, his hen Pertelote dismisses his fears as a purely physiological symptom caused by an overabundance of yellow bile, urging him to purge his red choler with digestive herbs.

6. Renaissance Humoralism, Shakespearean Drama, and Semantic Shift

During the Tudor and Elizabethan eras, humoral psychology reached the pinnacle of cultural and literary prominence. In the 16th century, the spelling was re-Latinized with the classical digraph ch- to produce early modern choleric. On the Elizabethan stage, playwrights drew heavily upon humoral temperaments to construct dynamic, instantly recognizable dramatic characters. Ben Jonson's "comedy of humours" (e.g., Every Man in His Humour, 1598) formalized this practice, while William Shakespeare employed the choleric temperament to generate psychological tension across both comedies and tragedies.

In The Taming of the Shrew (Act IV, Scene 1), Petruchio deliberately denies the famished Katherine a serving of roasted beef on the grounds that over-roasted meat produces yellow bile and will exacerbate their mutual bad tempers:

"I tell thee, Kate, 'twas burnt and dried away; / And I expressly am forbid to touch it, / For it engenders choler, planteth anger; / And better 'twere that both of us did fast, / Since, of ourselves, ourselves are choleric..."
Similarly, in Henry IV, Part 1, Hotspur's fiery impatience and unreflective drive for martial honor embody the textbook choleric soldier, whose excessive heat blinds him to political reality.

7. The Demise of Galenism and Modern Semantic Survival

The 17th and 18th centuries witnessed a radical scientific revolution that gradually dismantled Galenic humoralism. William Harvey's demonstration of blood circulation in 1628, followed by Robert Boyle's chemical investigations and 19th-century cellular pathology established by Rudolf Virchow, demonstrated that yellow bile was simply a hepatic digestive fluid aiding lipid emulsification rather than a mystical reservoir of emotional energy. Furthermore, Robert Koch's 1883 isolation of the bacterium Vibrio cholerae permanently separated the epidemic infectious disease cholera from the personality trait of irascibility.

Nevertheless, although humoral theory was completely discarded by modern medical science, the word choleric survived in the English language through lexical fossilization. Stripped of its literal physiological mechanisms, it retained its vivid psychological and behavioral connotations, standing today as an elevated, scholarly, and expressive descriptor for individuals characterized by fierce, combustible, and easily provoked anger.
