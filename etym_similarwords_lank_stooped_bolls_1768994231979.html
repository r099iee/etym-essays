• Lean (for lank): Similar in denoting thinness; differs because lean implies a hard, healthy thinness and is admiring — a runner is lean — while lank is thin and slack, thin without tone, drained of firmness.
  • Gaunt (for lank): Similar in marked thinness; differs because gaunt is a hollowed, haggard thinness from hunger, illness, or anxiety, carrying pathos, whereas lank is limp and toneless rather than hollowed — a sag more than a hollowing.
  • Scrawny (for lank): Similar in being thin; differs because scrawny is contemptuous and bony, the word for the underfed, while lank is neutral to mournful — the flat, unheroic slackness of a body emptied by labour.
  • Skinny (for lank): Similar in being thin; differs because skinny is casual, colloquial, and often slightly critical, describing a thin build in general, while lank adds the slackness and droop that skinny does not imply.
  • Wiry (for lank): Similar in being slender; differs because wiry describes a lean body that is strong, tough, and energetic — all sinew — whereas lank describes a body stretched and drained, without tone or spring.
  • Spindly (for lank): Similar in being long and thin; differs because spindly emphasizes long thin limbs that look weak and fragile, like an insect's, while lank emphasizes the limp, slack, sagging quality rather than the fragility of the bones.
  • Lanky (for lank): Similar in sharing the lank root and describing tall thinness; differs because lanky has softened into an affectionate or neutral word for the awkwardly tall adolescent, while lank keeps the limpness and the mournful, unheroic flatness.
  • Limp (for lank): Similar in the slackness, the lack of firmness; differs because limp describes any flaccid object or a person without energy, without implying thinness, while lank is specifically the thinness-plus-slackness of a body or of hair.
  • Bent (for stooped): Similar in describing a body curved from its upright line; differs because bent is the general word for any curvature — a bent finger, bent wire — while stooped specifies the forward fold of shoulders and back, the posture of the whole body.
  • Hunched (for stooped): Similar in a forward-curved back and shoulders; differs because hunched suggests the shoulders drawn up and together, often in cold, fear, or defensive withdrawal, while stooped is the forward-downward fold of wear and age.
  • Bowed (for stooped): Similar in a downward-forward curve of the body; differs because bowed is more formal and suggests a deliberate or dignified bending — a bowed head — while stooped is the involuntary, habitual posture of the worn body.
  • Crooked (for stooped): Similar in deviating from a straight line; differs because crooked is the general, often morally tinted word for anything twisted or bent, while stooped is specifically the bodily forward fold, with no moral colour in the participle.
  • Pod (for bolls): Similar in being a rounded seed-case; differs because pod is the generic term for the dry fruit of legumes, while boll is the specialized agronomic name for the rounded seed-capsule of the cotton plant — a word bound to the harvest and the picker's hand.
  • Capsule (for bolls): Similar in being a dry seed-case; differs because capsule is the botanical technical term for any dry dehiscent fruit, used in scientific registers, while boll is the practical, regional farm word specifically for cotton's rounded boll.
  • Seed-vessel (for bolls): Similar in being the case that holds seeds; differs because seed-vessel is a descriptive, non-technical phrase applicable to any plant's seed-holder, while boll is the fixed name of the cotton plant's round fruit in the cotton economy.
  • Husk (for bolls): Similar in being the outer covering of a seed or fruit; differs because husk is the dry outer shell of grain or nuts, rough and splitting, while the boll is the rounded swelling that holds the fibre itself, the crop's treasure.
