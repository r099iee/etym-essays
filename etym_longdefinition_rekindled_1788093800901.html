Primary sense — literal fire. Rekindled is the past tense and past participle of the verb rekindle, “to kindle again”: to set alight a fire, flame or glow that has gone out or died down. The literal use is attested from the word's first appearance in English in the 1590s (“the lamp was rekindled”, “he rekindled the embers”) and survives in poetry, in period prose and in accounts of hearth and campfire, though in everyday modern speech the literal job has largely passed to “relit” and “reignited”.

The figurative core — the modern dominant sense. Because fire is the oldest and most universal metaphor for emotion and energy, rekindle developed a figurative life in which the object is not fuel but feeling, and it is in this register that the word is now overwhelmingly used:

  • Passion and attachment: to revive a romantic, affectionate or friendly bond — “rekindled romance”, “rekindled desire”, “they rekindled their old friendship”; the phrase “rekindle the spark” is a staple of relationship writing.
  • Hope, enthusiasm, interest: “rekindled hope”, “rekindled interest in history”, “the victory rekindled national enthusiasm” — the sense of a light returning to something that had dimmed.
  • Conflict: to revive a quarrel, feud, war or debate — “rekindled hostilities”, “the controversy was rekindled by the leaked memo”. This is the sense of this card's context from The Hobbit: after the death of the Great Goblin of the Misty Mountains, the goblins' hatred of the dwarves “had been rekindled to fury” — an ember of ancient enmity flaring up again into full flame.
  • Creative and intellectual energy: “rekindled passion for music”, “a rekindled curiosity”; common in memoir, self-help and lifestyle registers for deliberately reviving a dormant pursuit.

Semantic nuance. Unlike “reignited”, which suggests a sudden spark or an explosion, rekindled implies slow warmth returning to something that still held embers — a gentler, more hopeful word, which is why it is preferred in romantic and inspirational contexts. It is almost always transitive in figurative use; of fire it may be intransitive (“the embers rekindled”). The ember image is essential: embers hold heat invisibly, so a rekindled feeling is one that was never quite dead.

Morphology and grammar. re- + kindle + -ed. The -ed participle is regular; the spelling preserves the -le of kindle, and the prefix carries a long vowel in careful speech: /ˌriːˈkɪndəld/. The -le ending is etymologically part of the Old Norse loan kynda, not the English frequentative suffix -le, though the two have long been associated in speakers' minds. The prefix re- is Latin (from PIE *wre-, an extended form of *wer- “to turn”), meaning “again, back”, and by the 1590s it was fully productive in English.

Register and distribution. Rekindled is a literary and rhetorical word: headline writers use it for peace talks, feuds, rivalries and anniversaries; novelists use it for feeling; scientists rarely use it at all. Its career illustrates a common drift — a concrete verb of fire-lighting becoming an abstract verb of emotion, with the literal sense receding to the margins.
