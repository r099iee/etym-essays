- Late 16th century - the soldier's wall - "parapet" enters English (by the 1580s, via French from Italian parapetto, literally "chest-warder") as a strictly military term for the low breast-high wall of earth or stone behind which defenders stood to fire and be sheltered.
- 17th-18th century - fortification and bridge - the word stays military but also names the low guarding walls along bridges and the edges of platforms; it remains a fortification term, the defensive railing of the engineer's trade.
- 19th century - civilian life - the defensive sense recedes and "parapet" becomes the architectural word for any low protective wall along roofs, balconies, and terraces, guarding against falling rather than against assault.
- 20th-21st century - the invisible origin - the word's literal heart, "breast-high," has faded from most users' awareness, so that a parapet is simply a low safety wall; freed of its military urgency, it can even take on an elegiac, poetic resonance as a relic of fortress glory.
