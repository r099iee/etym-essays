- 14th century - "clump" entered English as a continental Germanic loan meaning a lump — a compact, heavy mass of earth, metal, or dough — the same expressive family as clod, clot, cluster, and clog; for three centuries it named inert mass and nothing more.
- 17th century - the sense turned from the inanimate to the living: a "clump of trees", a dense, close-set cluster of growth standing together like a clot of vegetable matter, began to appear, distinguished from the orderly grove or deliberate planting by its accidental lumpishness.
- 19th century to modern - the verb followed the noun into motion: to clump is to walk with the heavy, dull tread of a mass of weight, and the sound itself is a clump — so that every sense of the word holds together by weight, denseness, and cohesion.
