- [Classical Greek & Hellenistic Era (c. 3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE)] - originated in the great Library of Alexandria (Zenodotus, Aristarchus, Didymus) where grammarians wrote hypomnemata (commentary rolls) to preserve classical Homeric and dramatic texts - [perception: revered as the ultimate guardians of Hellenic literary heritage and elite linguistic purity]
- [Byzantine & Medieval Codex Era (c. 5th–14th c.)] - evolved into the definitive form of the scholiast, as monastic scribes transferred commentary rolls directly into the generous margins of parchment codices surrounding the central text - [perception: regarded as indispensable intellectual bridge-builders preserving the wisdom of pagan antiquity within the Christian empire]
- [Renaissance & Early Modern Humanism (c. 15th–17th c.)] - borrowed into French and English (c. 1570s) by Renaissance humanists recovering Greek manuscripts, who celebrated scholiasts for unlocking forgotten classical dialects and historical allusions - [perception: valued by Erasmus, Scaliger, and Casaubon as vital keys to reconstructing classical antiquity]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Augustan Satire] - lampooned heavily by satirists like Alexander Pope (The Dunciad) and Jonathan Swift, who contrasted the genius of original poets with the dry, parasitic pedantry of marginal annotators - [perception: acquired a sharp pejorative secondary meaning as dry-as-dust, hair-splitting academic pedants obsessed with trivialities]
- [19th–21st c. Modern Philology & Digital Humanities] - restored to high scholarly esteem in modern critical editions (such as the Teubner series, Princeton Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, and digital hypertexts), where scholiasts are recognized as invaluable repositories of lost literature and primary sources of cultural history - [perception: universally respected as the foundational architects of textual commentary and guardians of literary transmission]
