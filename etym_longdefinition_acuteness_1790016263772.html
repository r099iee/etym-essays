Acuteness is the abstract noun for the quality of being acute — of being sharp in the literal sense of a point or edge, and by metaphor in the senses of intense feeling, severe crisis, keen perception, and shrewd intelligence. Where acute describes the knife, the pain, the ear, the mind, or the crisis, acuteness names the sharpness itself as a measurable or discussable property: the acuteness of a blade, the acuteness of distress, the acuteness of hearing, the acuteness of intellect, the acuteness of a shortage. It is a formal, slightly bookish word that allows a writer to dwell on degree — how sharp, how intense, how severe — rather than merely labelling a thing sharp.

The richness of acuteness comes from the extraordinary range of acute, one of English's most travelled Latin adjectives. From a single image — a needle point — Latin and then English derive a whole family of sharpnesses: physical, sensory, mental, medical, geometric, phonetic, and economic. Acuteness inherits all of them. To master it is to see how one metaphor of the point organises disparate domains, and why your sentence — the acuteness of the girl's distress — chooses acuteness over intensity or severity: it wants the image of pain as a point that pierces, not merely as a weight that presses.

  • 1. Physical sharpness (literal, oldest): having a fine point or keen edge. Example: the acuteness of a needle, thorn, blade. Bridge: direct from Latin acutus sharpened, past participle of acuere to sharpen. Today this sense is often ceded to sharpness, but acuteness survives in technical descriptions of tools, leaves, and architectural points.
  • 2. Sensory keenness: sharpness of sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch. Example: acuteness of vision, auditory acuteness. Bridge: the sense organ as a point that penetrates the world; a keen ear pierces silence as a needle pierces cloth. Highly current in psychology, audiology, and wine-tasting.
  • 3. Intellectual / perceptual sharpness: keenness of mind, insight, discernment. Example: acuteness of intellect, acuteness of observation. Bridge: mind as blade that cuts through confusion to distinction. This complimentary sense overlaps shrewdness and perspicacity and is central to praise of thinkers, detectives, and critics.
  • 4. Intensity of feeling or sensation (your context): severity and piercing quality of pain, grief, distress, joy, embarrassment. Example: the acuteness of her distress. Bridge: emotion felt as a stab rather than an ache; acute pain in medicine is sudden, sharp, severe. Acuteness here conveys both degree (very great) and quality (piercing, hard to bear).
  • 5. Severity / criticality of a situation: gravity and urgency of a crisis, shortage, conflict. Example: acuteness of the housing crisis, acuteness of famine. Bridge: from medical acute disease (sudden, severe, short) to general crisis; what comes to a point demands immediate decision. Current in economics, geopolitics, and public health.
  • 6. Medical-technical acute: In medicine acute means sudden-onset, severe, short-term (acute appendicitis, acute phase), opposed to chronic. Acuteness then means the property of being acute in that clinical sense. Bridge: Latin acutus morbus sharp disease — one that comes to a crisis quickly like a point.
  • 7. Geometric, phonetic, and prosodic extensions: an acute angle (less than 90°, sharp), an acute accent (é, a raised, sharp mark), acute sound. Acuteness in these fields means pointedness of angle or raised pitch. Bridge: visual and auditory metaphors of elevation and sharpness preserved from Latin acutus.

Register: acuteness is formal and analytic, at home in essays, medicine, criticism, and science. Conversation prefers sharpness, keenness, intensity. Its value is precision: it lets a writer specify which kind of sharpness is meant while retaining the prestige of Latin. Collocations — acuteness of mind, of hearing, of pain, of crisis — each select a different subdomain, disambiguated by the of-phrase.

For learners: acute does not mean cute, and acuteness does not mean cuteness. Acute versus chronic is a distinction of time-course, not of seriousness alone; a chronic disease can be severe but not acute. Acuteness versus acuity: acuity is almost exclusively sensory (visual acuity), while acuteness covers mental, emotional, and situational sharpness as well.
