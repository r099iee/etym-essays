brewing is the present participle and gerund of the verb brew, and it carries two families of meaning that pull in very different directions. In the passage from The Hobbit, Gandalf’s farewell speech uses the figurative sense: “There is news brewing” — news is in preparation, working its way up from below, like something fermenting unseen until it bursts to the surface. The image is of hidden activity maturing towards eruption: a storm gathering, trouble accumulating, events taking shape behind the curtain of the visible.

Primary literal sense — making beer and other drinks.

  • To brew beer or ale: to soak malt in hot water (mashing), boil the resulting wort with hops, cool it, and ferment it with yeast. “Brewing” in this sense is both the technical process and the industry — the brewing trade, a brewing company.
  • By extension, to prepare any hot drink by infusion: “brewing a pot of tea”, “brewing coffee”. The verb shifted in modern domestic usage from beer to tea and coffee, and the noun a brew now most commonly means simply a cup of tea (“fancy a brew?” in British colloquial speech) or a particular mixture (“a strong brew”).
  • To concoct or mix any liquid preparation (archaic and residual): medicines, potions, soups “brewed in the cauldron” — the witch’s-brew sense preserved in fairy tale and fantasy prose.

Secondary figurative sense — gathering and imminence.

  • Of weather: “a storm is brewing” — a storm is developing and will soon break; the meteorological sense is among the oldest figurative uses and remains vivid.
  • Of troubles, conflicts and crises: “trouble was brewing”, “a crisis is brewing in the east”, “war is brewing”. The event is imagined as a ferment working away beneath the surface, invisible now but certain to erupt.
  • Of news, plans and schemes: “there is news brewing” (as in the source sentence) — information is taking shape that will shortly be made public; also, archaic, “to brew mischief / treason / deceit” — to contrive or plot secretly, a sense directly parallel to to hatch or cook up.

Tertiary and technical senses.

  • Brewing as a noun (gerund): the business, craft or science of making beer — “brewing has been practised since antiquity”, “the chemistry of brewing”.
  • “Brew” as a countable noun: a drink, especially tea or beer; also any mixture or combination (“a curious brew of influences”) — a modern metaphorical extension in which disparate elements are “brewed together”.
  • Technical brewing jargon: gravity, wort, fermentation, conditioning — the vocabulary of the modern industry descended from the same verb.

Semantic bridge. The literal brewing process is one of hidden, bubbling fermentation: ingredients are combined, heat is applied, and something new slowly rises and transforms below the surface until it is ready and must be released. The figurative senses simply project this invisible, inexorable maturation onto events: trouble, storms and news are “brewing” because they are undergoing their own silent fermentation, accumulating force out of sight, bound to surface. That is why the word instinctively fits prophecy, warning and foreboding — as in Gandalf’s speech — and why it can never be used of something already visible and complete: what is brewing is by definition still to come.

Register and morphology. Brew is a regular verb (brew – brewed – brewed); brewing serves as present participle, gerund and attributive adjective (“the brewing industry”, “a brewing crisis”), and this versatility explains its frequency — every storm and every dispute in the news is “brewing” before it “breaks”. The word is equally at home in the homely kitchen sense and the ominous figurative sense, and the contrast between the two — a comforting pot of tea versus a gathering catastrophe — is one of English’s favourite small ironies.
