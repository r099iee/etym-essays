- c. 1000 — a carrying frame: Old English bearwe names a basket or stretcher-frame for bearing loads by hand between two people.
- 14th c. — it gains a wheel: Middle English barewe covers hand-barrows and the new wheelbarrows, the one-wheeled garden servant spreading through manors and monasteries.
- 16th–18th c. — the market takes it: costermongers' barrows become mobile shops, and barrow starts to mean street trade as well as soil.
- 19th c. — industry scales it: iron mortar barrows, rail barrows, and navvy barrows on every railway cutting, the garden tool grown muscular.
- 20th c. — gardens keep it: allotments and suburbs rescue the wooden wheelbarrow as the emblem of honest weekend labour.
- Today — modest and indispensable: barrows of compost, barrows of market flowers, and, on the downs, their ancient mound-namesakes, the two twins sharing one spelling.
