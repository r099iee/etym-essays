• valley: Similar because both name a depression between hills or mountains; differs because a valley is large and usually open, often carrying a river and forming a whole drainage system, whereas a dell is small, enclosed, tree-screened, and frequently dry, the valley seen through a magnifying lens.
  • dale: Similar because both are native English words, from the same Germanic root, for low-lying ground; differs because a dale is a broad valley, the northern and poetic word for a whole strath or valley floor, while a dell is its diminutive, small enough to be one pocket within a dale.
  • vale: Similar because both are literary words for low ground between hills; differs because vale is broad, romantic, and often ceremonial, a poetic synonym for valley, while dell is concrete, small, and topographically exact, a vale you travel through, a dell you stumble upon.
  • glen: Similar because both denote a small sheltered valley; differs because glen is the Gaelic-derived word for a narrow mountain valley, especially in Scotland, and implies steep sides and a highland setting, while dell is native English and suggests wooded lowland rather than mountains.
  • dingle: Similar because both name a deep, wooded, hidden hollow; differs because dingle is more archaic and even more emphatically literary and mysterious, while dell is the more general, slightly better-known word for a tree-screened hollow.
  • hollow: Similar because both name a sunken depression in the ground; differs because hollow is the plain, everyday, topographically neutral word, carrying no literary colouring, whereas dell adds seclusion, trees, and a pastoral or fairytale flavour.
  • combe / coombe: Similar because both name a small valley; differs because combe is a southern English word, from a Celtic root, for a short steep-sided valley or hollow on a hillside, especially in Devon, Cornwall, and the chalk country, while dell is native English and not regionally fixed in the same way.
  • dene / dean: Similar because both are native English words for a small valley; differs because dene originally meant a valley with a stream or a wooded vale and survives chiefly in southern place-names, whereas dell does not imply water and has kept a free-standing literary life.
  • swale: Similar because both name a low, sunken stretch of ground; differs because swale is a marshier, meadow-like depression, often seasonally wet, with no implication of trees or enclosure, while dell stresses dry, wooded shelter.
  • depression: Similar because both name a downward dip in the land; differs because depression is the neutral, scientific, cartographical term for any sunken area, stripped of the scenic, literary, and sheltering associations of dell.
  • basin: Similar because both name a hollow, container-like depression; differs because basin in physical geography implies a drainage catchment, river basin or ocean basin, of large scale and function, while a dell is tiny, purely local, and has no drainage role.
  • gorge: Similar because both can name a sunken, enclosed landform; differs because a gorge is a deep, narrow ravine with steep rock walls, usually river-cut and dramatic, whereas a dell is shallow, soft, wooded, and gentle, the opposite of vertical and violent.
  • nook: Similar because both can name a small sheltered place; differs because nook is architectural and domestic, a nook of a room or a chimney nook, and need not be a landform at all, while a dell is always a piece of landscape.
  • retreat: Similar because both can denote a sheltered, hidden place; differs because retreat is abstract and functional, a place of withdrawal usable of buildings and institutions, whereas dell is a physical landform whose seclusion is a property, not a purpose.
