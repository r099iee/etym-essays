Braces as clothing hardware is a late, specialized chapter in the much longer story of a word that means "the arms". The whole brace-family radiates from a single image: two arms, the human pair of supporting limbs, and anything that holds or clasps as arms hold. Modern English brace (support, prop), embrace (to take in the arms), bracelet (arm-band), and the plural braces (trouser straps) all descend from this stock.

Stage one: Greek and Latin. The story begins outside English, in the Greek word brakhíōn (βραχίων), "the upper arm" — attested in Homer and throughout classical literature. Its own deeper origin is uncertain: some handbooks connect it to the adjective brakhýs "short" (the upper arm as the "short" limb), but the etymology is debated and it may well be a word of obscure or pre-Greek ancestry; the honest statement is that brakhíōn has no securely reconstructed Indo-European source. Latin borrowed it as bracchium (also spelled brachium), "the arm, especially the forearm", attested from the second century BCE onward (Plautus uses it). The Latin plural bracchia names the two arms together — a paired, embracing unit — and it is that plural, not the singular, that feeds the later shift.

Stage two: Old French and the "pair / embrace" shift. When Latin passed into the Romance vernaculars, bracchia produced Old French brace, which carried two related meanings: the two arms, and an embrace — the measure of what two arms can hold, hence "a pair", "a span". From "a pair of arms" the noun generalized: a brace came to mean any pair (a brace of pheasants, a brace of pistols — still current hunting language), and any clasp or support that holds things together as arms might hold.

Stage three: Middle English. English borrowed brace from Anglo-Norman and Old French in the 13th–14th centuries. The noun appears with the senses "a pair", "a clasp", "a fastening", "a support or prop"; the verb to brace means "to fasten firmly, to support, to take in the arms". Chaucer and Middle English writers use both. It is important to note what this word is not: it is not from the Germanic root *bʰreg- "to break" — despite the identical spelling, English brace is a Romance loan (Latin arm-word via French), while break is native Germanic; the resemblance is coincidence.

  • 13th–14th c. — ME brace: "pair; clasp; support" (from Anglo-Norman).
  • 16th–17th c. — brace as a structural prop; embrace fully naturalized.
  • 19th c. — British braces for trouser shoulder-straps; orthodontic braces follow in the 20th century.

Stage four: the garment sense. The clothing sense is British and relatively recent. By the early 19th century the paired straps that take the weight of trousers from the shoulders were being called braces in Britain (they are essentially "supports" — the same word as wall-braces). American English developed a different name, suspenders, from the verb "to suspend" (hang up), and in Britain suspenders itself was reserved for other supporting straps — the garters that hold up stockings. So the same object has two names across the Atlantic, and the British word is the one Tolkien assumes.

Later extensions. The 20th century kept the support-verb productive: orthodontic braces are literally small appliances that "brace" the teeth into alignment; typographical braces { } group items; a musical brace joins staves. Every modern sense still answers to the ancient image of two arms holding firm.

The Tolkien context. Bilbo's thumb hooked behind his braces is character in a single gesture — comfortable, slightly pompous, utterly unready for adventure. The word's path from Greek "upper arm" through Latin and French to a hobbit's trouser-straps is complete long before Bag End; Tolkien simply plants the domestic British plural in the scene so the reader sees a settled body resisting the road. The plural itself is significant: braces are a pair, and the language keeps that duality, exactly as Latin bracchia kept the two arms together.
