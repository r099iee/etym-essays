lashed is the past tense and past participle of the verb lash, and it straddles two distinct families of meaning that share one sound: binding and striking. In the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — the barrels "lashed together" are bound fast with ropes, and the word evokes both the physical security of the lashing and the violence of the river that strains against it.

FAMILY ONE — BINDING, TYING, SECURING

  • Primary nautical sense: to tie or bind firmly with rope, cord, or twine — "the crew lashed the cargo to the deck", "he lashed the oars together to make a raft".
  • Lashing is the technical word for rope-binding on ships — lashings, lash up, lash down — and it connotes speed, firmness, and storm-preparedness: you lash things down precisely because you expect them to try to move.
  • Extended to everyday objects: lashed to a fence, lashed to a tree, lashed together into a bundle; the sense is always "secured so that it cannot shift".
  • Related noun: lashing = the cord or rope used for binding (or a beating with it — see family two); "lashings of" (British, 19th c.) = plenty, abundance — probably from the image of layer upon layer of cord.

FAMILY TWO — STRIKING, BEATING, FLOGGING

  • To strike or beat with a whip, thong, or flexible instrument: "the slave-driver lashed the prisoners", "the groom lashed the horses".
  • To beat against violently, of weather: "rain lashed the windows", "the wind lashed the sails" — nature wields an invisible whip.
  • "To lash out": (of an animal) to kick or strike suddenly; (of a person) to attack suddenly with words or blows — "he lashed out at his critics".
  • Figurative self-punishment: to lash oneself = to berate oneself harshly; religious self-flagellation is the extreme of the same image.
  • Noun uses: a lash = the thong of a whip (also the eyelash, so named because it flicks or whips); a lashing = a beating; whiplash = the cracking neck injury of sudden motion, or the thong itself.

TERTIARY AND TECHNICAL SENSES

  • In sewing and tailoring, to lash = to stitch or bind with thread (from the same Old French source as lace).
  • In rope-making, rigging, and climbing: a lashing is any binding that holds two spars together — square lashing, diagonal lashing, sheer lashing — precise technical vocabulary of sailors, scouts, and mountaineers.
  • In bookbinding and printing, a "lashed" edge is stitched or tied (rare).
  • In journalism and meteorology the storm image persists in headlines: "fury lashes the coast", "winds lash the city".

THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE BETWEEN THE TWO FAMILIES

  • The two lash verbs (bind vs. strike) are historically distinct in origin (see the etymology field), but speakers have long felt them as one word, and there is a genuine physical bridge: a lash — the flexible thong of a whip — is itself a piece of cord. A cord binds; a cord strikes.
  • The whip's "lash" is the same supple rope that, in other hands, lashes cargo to a deck. The shared image of a flexible cord doing violent or securing work has fused the families in perception even though their histories differ.
  • This fusion lets Tolkien's sentence work on both levels at once: the barrels are lashed together (bound), yet they creak and fret as if whipped by the river — security and violence in one word.

REGISTER AND STYLE

  • Lashed is vivid, physical, and slightly archaic-heroic; it belongs to sea stories, adventure narrative, punishment history, and storm journalism.
  • The binding sense is neutral and practical; the striking sense is violent and emotive.
  • Modern idiom favours the figurative: "lash out", "lashed by criticism", "whiplash reaction" (a sudden negative backlash in politics or markets).
  • 21st-century slang (UK/Ireland/Australia): "on the lash" = on a drinking spree — the word's energy keeps generating new informal senses.
