*PIE *bhreg-* —
to break, crack, burst
(Pokorny IEW; the root of Latin frangere and English break)

↓

Proto-Germanic *brekanan —
to break
(whence Old English brecan, English break)

↓

Proto-Germanic *braknō —
zero-grade *brak- + noun suffix *-nō
the brittle, easily-snapped fern

↓

Old Norse brakni —
fern
(poetic name; cf. Old Norse braka “to crack, crash”)

↓

Middle English braken —
fern; bracken
(14th century; borrowed from the Scandinavian stock)

↓

English bracken —
Pteridium aquilinum, the tall coarse fern
(spelling settled 15th–16th centuries)

↓

BRACKEN —
the present word

Notes: The clipped form brake (“fern thicket, brushwood”) descends from the same root and was reinforced by the homograph brake from break. Old English made a parallel native twin, bracce “fern”, from the same root, but it did not survive as the ordinary word. The derivation of the Germanic plant-name from *bhreg- via the zero grade is standard and not seriously disputed; the exact suffix (*-nō) is a reconstruction.
