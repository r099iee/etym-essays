- 1542 – Tudor translation: Udall renders Erasmus’s Latin literally; the phrase means “speak bluntly,” with a whiff of coarseness.
- 17th–18th c. – gentlemanly plain dealing: used approvingly of honest speech, sometimes with a shrug at its rudeness.
- 19th c. – imperial and sporting English: becomes a stock marker of no-nonsense British character.
- 20th c. – journalistic cliché: politicians and pundits invoke it to claim candour.
- Mid 20th c. – unrelated slang collision: “spade” as a racial slur (1920s, from cards) leads some to misread the idiom; the digging-tool phrase is centuries older and unconnected.
- 21st c. – rhetorical badge: “let’s call a spade a spade” signals antifashionable directness, often before an uncomfortable truth.
