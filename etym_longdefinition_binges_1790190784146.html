binges (verb, third-person singular present / noun, plural) refers to engaging in or denoting instances of excessive, unrestrained, and compulsive indulgence in an activity—most commonly eating, drinking alcohol, spending money, partying, or consuming digital media—over a concentrated period of time.

Core Senses and Semantic Domains:

  • Bouts of Heavy Drinking or Substance Use (Historical Slang Sense): The traditional colloquial meaning describes extended drinking sprees or carousing sessions where intoxicating liquor is consumed in massive quantities over hours or days. In informal British and American English, going on binges entails wild, unrestrained partying and drunken revelry.
  • Social Parties, Wild Celebrations, and Sprees (Comedic & Social Sense): In colloquial and comedic literature (prominently featured in P. G. Wodehouse's stories of the Drones Club and interwar high society), binges refers to lively, boisterous parties, social gatherings, nightclub outings, or celebratory excursions where wealthy young revelers let their hair down and engage in exuberant antics.
  • Clinical and Psychiatric Pathologies (Binge Eating & Dysregulation): In modern psychiatry and clinical medicine, binges constitutes a formal diagnostic symptom within eating disorders (such as Binge Eating Disorder and Bulimia Nervosa). It defines discrete episodes of consuming objectively large quantities of food accompanied by a profound sense of lack of control, followed by distress, guilt, or compensatory behaviors.
  • Digital Media and Entertainment Consumption (Binge-Watching): In contemporary 21st-century digital culture, binges describes marathon sessions of consuming multiple episodes of television shows, video content, or podcasts back-to-back via streaming platforms. This modern sense has largely shed the moral stigma of substance binges, becoming a widely accepted domestic leisure routine.
  • Financial, Shopping, or Behavioral Indulgences: Extended bursts of uncontrolled behavior in other domains, such as shopping binges, spending binges, or gambling binges, characterized by immediate gratification followed by financial regret.

Polysemy and Historical Semantic Bridges:

The etymology of binge is rooted in nineteenth-century English provincial dialect—particularly the dialect of Northamptonshire and Lincolnshire—where the dialect verb binge meant "to soak a wooden vessel (such as a beer cask, wooden tub, or butter churn) in water to cause the wooden staves to swell and make the container watertight."

Around the mid-nineteenth century (c. 1850s), this rustic craft term was adopted by British university students (notably at Oxford and Cambridge) and Royal Navy sailors as a vivid slang metaphor for heavy drinking. Just as a cooper soaked a wooden beer barrel until its timber was saturated, so too did a carousing drinker "soak his own internal barrel" with ale or spirits. By the 1880s and early 1900s, this slang broadened from drinking bouts into general celebratory sprees, parties, and shopping excursions.

During the latter half of the twentieth century, clinical psychologists co-opted the term to categorize compulsive, unregulated food and substance intake, creating a formal medical framework. Finally, with the advent of on-demand digital streaming in the 2010s, the metaphor underwent another technological shift into marathon media consumption, completing its evolution from wooden barrels to digital screens.

Modern Registers, Pragmatics, and Usage Notes:

  • Grammatical Flexibility: Operates both as a plural noun (e.g., "attending midnight binges") and as the third-person singular present form of the verb binge (e.g., "he binges on historical documentaries every weekend").
  • Tone and Register: Varies dramatically based on context: cheerfully roguish in social/literary settings, clinically serious in psychiatric and healthcare contexts, and casual/playful in streaming entertainment discussions.
