Dratting is the present participle and gerund of the colloquial verb to drat, and it denotes the act of uttering a mild curse or exclamation of annoyance, vexation, or frustration — cursing softly and inoffensively, usually under the breath, without real profanity or malice. The word is the -ing form of drat, and in the sentence from The Hobbit it functions as a verbal noun standing in parallel with grumbling: "a good deal of grumbling and dratting". The register is instantly recognisable: dratting is homely, old-fashioned, gently comic speech, the strongest language a pre-war English kitchen, a children's book, or a flustered schoolmaster allows himself, expressing sharp irritation with an almost apologetic mildness. The verb drat itself. Drat is used in several related functions, and an exhaustive account of the root word requires them all: - As an interjection: "Drat it!", "Drat the man!", "Drat!" — the bare exclamation of annoyance, equivalent in function to "confound it!" or "blast!" but far milder. First recorded about 1815. - As a transitive verb: to drat someone or something, i.e. to curse or damn them mildly: "I drat the day I met him." - As an intransitive verb: to utter such curses, from which the -ing form dratting derives. - As the participial adjective dratted: "that dratted lock", "the dratted cat" — meaning confounded, annoying, wretched, a euphemistic stand-in for "damned" that carries the same comic mildness. Recorded from the nineteenth century, and if anything even more common in everyday usage than the verb. - In the fixed formulae "drat it" and "drat the thing / the boy / the lot of them". The origin and the semantic bridge. Drat is a minced oath, a euphemistic corruption of the genuine imprecation "God rot (him, it, or them)!" — a curse that wished upon its object the decay and putrefaction of the grave. English, from the Renaissance onward, developed an elaborate machinery of such softened oaths to keep the force of swearing while avoiding the blasphemy of taking God's name in vain, and drat belongs to the same family as zounds ("God's wounds"), egad ("by God"), gadzooks, gosh, darn, and dash, and the American doggone and goldarn. The full phrase "God rot" was whittled down through the reduced "'d rot" (and the variant "od rot", with the divine name already masked as od) to the monosyllable drat; the sense remained that of wishing something to rot — to decay, spoil, become worthless — a rustic, almost agricultural curse that by the nineteenth century had been worn smooth of everything except comical vexation. This historical bridge explains everything about the word's tone: it is a curse with the teeth drawn, anger without blasphemy. Usage, register, and geography. Drat was dialectally and socially a homely, colloquial word when it first appeared at the turn of the nineteenth century, and it quickly became a stock property of comic and children's writing. It is now distinctly dated: to say "drat it" today is to imitate a period voice, and modern speakers mostly encounter drat, dratted, and dratting in Victorian and Edwardian fiction, in pre-war comics, and in archaising children's literature. The word is lexicographically notable as one of the clearest documented examples of the minced-oath process, and its -ing form dratting is the rarest of its derivatives, occurring almost exclusively in humorous or literary narrative. In the passage from "Roast Mutton", Tolkien sets the word with comic precision. As the dwarves and the hobbit climb the dark hill, "do what they could they made a deal of rustling and crackling and creaking (and a good deal of grumbling and dratting)". Grumbling gives the low, continuous, rumbling complaint; dratting gives the sharp, intermittent expletive — each unseen root, thorn, and puddle calling forth a small explosion of mild profanity. The word humanises the dwarves: thirteen blundering figures, cursing quietly at the furniture of the Wild like irritated farmers or flustered housewives, heroic and absurd at once. It also contributes to the chapter's running comedy of noise, in which the party's attempts at stealth fail noisily at every level of the senses. Key distinctions from near-synonyms:
  • Cursing and swearing: similar because both name the utterance of abusive or profane language; differ because cursing and swearing imply strong, profane, or obscene language, while dratting is a mild, inoffensive imprecation drained of offence.
  • Grumbling: similar because both voice discontent; differs because grumbling is diffuse, low-voiced, continuous complaint, while dratting is sharp, intermittent, exclamatory cursing.
  • Muttering: similar because both involve low, indistinct speech; differs because muttering describes any murmured speech, while dratting names a specific mild oath of vexation.
  • Complaining: similar because both express displeasure; differs because complaining is articulate and addressed to someone, while dratting is an exclamation of irritation with no real addressee.
  • Imprecating: similar because both are forms of calling down a curse; differs because imprecating is formal, solemn, and often genuinely hostile, while dratting is comic and affectionate in its mildness.
  • Damning: similar because both are oaths of condemnation; differs because damning is severe and literal, while dratting is a euphemistic substitute that avoids the word and the deed.
