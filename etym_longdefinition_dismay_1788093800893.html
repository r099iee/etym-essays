Dismay is both a verb and a noun. As a verb it means “to fill with sudden consternation, loss of courage and discouragement; to alarm and dishearten at once” — the scale of the losses dismayed the generals. As a noun it names the resulting state: a sudden loss of courage or confidence in the face of alarming or disheartening news — consternation tinged with dejection — to my dismay, the door was locked. The feeling it names is precise and worth pinning down: dismay is stronger than mere disappointment, weaker than terror; it combines the shock of alarm with the droop of discouragement — the wind knocked out of confidence, the sudden sag after a blow.

1. The verb. To dismay is to strike with consternation — to rob of spirit. It acts on people, plans and hopes: the news dismayed the whole town; she was dismayed to find the bridge gone. Historically it was a strong word, close to “terrify, rout, unman”: in the Bible and Shakespeare, dismay is what happens to armies and to sinners (“fear not, neither be dismayed” — Deuteronomy 1:21; dismayed hosts in the history plays). Modern use has softened toward “greatly disappoint, with alarm thrown in”, but the core remains: a dismayed person has had their courage suddenly knocked down. Derived forms: dismayed (adjective — the commonest form in speech), dismaying (adjective — “a dismaying result”), dismayingly (adverb).

2. The noun. The noun is a back-formation from the verb (Middle English dismai from dismaien). It appears chiefly in the fixed phrases that make it one of the most serviceable emotion-words of English: to one’s dismay, much to my dismay (“contrary to one’s hopes; to one’s disappointed alarm”), in dismay (the sense of the source sentence: the messengers “hastened back in dismay”), with dismay, a cry of dismay. It shades into horror (dismay at a massacre), despair (dismay at total failure) and surprise (dismay at an unexpected defeat), and its register is formal-neutral — common in journalism, fiction and polite speech, rare in slang.

3. Semantic bridge (etymological memory). The word’s deep structure explains its unique flavour. In Old French esmaier — from the Germanic root *magan “to be able, have power”, with the intensive prefix es- (“out of”) — meant literally “to deprive of strength”, to un-power someone. Dismay is thus the feeling of having one’s power taken away: not mere sadness, which sits quietly with you, but the sudden collapse of capability — the wind out of the sails. This is why dismay carries both alarm (the event) and helplessness (the state) at once. The French compound desmaier later reinforced es- with des-, and English took the reinforced form c. 1300, re-analysing the prefix as the familiar negative/away dis-.

4. Usage notes. The noun takes: in dismay (state), to my dismay (reacting formula), with dismay, and dismay at + noun/gerund. The adjective collocates with to + infinitive and that-clauses: “dismayed to learn that…”, “dismayed that the funds had vanished”. Spelling is dis-may (no z; the French-influenced “desmay” died in the 16th century), pronunciation /dɪsˈmeɪ/. The noun of physical rout is now literary-historical (“the enemy broke in dismay”), but “hastened back in dismay” — as in the source sentence — remains perfectly idiomatic and is the word’s grandest living register.
