Of all the words in this batch, dreading guards the strangest secret: nobody knows where it comes from. Its Old English ancestor ondraedan — to fear, to be afraid — stands nearly alone in Germanic, without the respectable family of cognates that words like rind and dike can muster. It is an island word for an island emotion: the cold wait before the blow. And from that obscure beginning it grew into one of English's most psychologically precise instruments.

From terror to reverence and back: Middle English dreden did double duty that modern English has forgotten — it meant to fear and to revere. One dreaded God, one's king, and the plague with neighboring shades of the same verb: trembling was worship's body language, and awe was fear in its Sunday clothes. The great dread sovereign of chronicles and the dread disease of bills of mortality are the same adjective — power that makes the knees bend, whether crowned or microbial.

  • The participle's career: dreading as a continuous form let writers stretch fear across time — had been dreading all week, dreading the winter, dreading to tell — and the construction proved so useful that it outgrew terror entirely. By the 19th century letters and novels dread examinations, visits, Mondays, and dentist chairs: fear's grammar applied to nuisance, with the flinch intact but the danger negotiable.
  • Sound and spelling: Old English ondraedan shed its prefix to give Middle English dreden; the vowel then followed the regular path to modern /drɛd/ while the spelling kept its instructive ea — the same vowel letter that in bread and thread marks the short modern vowel. The participial -ing was added without drama, giving dreading /ˈdrɛdɪŋ/, rhyming honestly with treading but no longer related to it in any speaker's mind.
  • The abandoned etymologies: Victorian philologists, embarrassed by an orphan, proposed parents: links to drear (gloomy), to a root meaning “to deceive,” even to confusion with tread. Modern handbooks set these aside for lack of evidence, and the honest entry — the one given here — begins the chain at Old English and marks the deeper origin unknown rather than inventing one.
  • Literary witnesses: the Authorised Version dreads the Lord in hundreds of verses; Shakespeare's kings are dread sovereigns and his cowards dread tomorrow; Austen's heroines dread balls and proposals in equal measure; and Tolkien's travellers, hearing what they had all been dreading, gave the past-perfect-progressive its most quoted night — fear confirmed by hoofbeat.
  • The dreadlocks boundary: Rastafari dreadlocks (1940s–50s Jamaica) reuse dread in its awe-terrible sense — hair that inspires holy fear — but the coinage is independent of the participle's history; it borrows the finished word, not the Old English root, and belongs in a note rather than the chain.

What remains is a small masterpiece of emotional grammar: where fear names the state and fright the shock, dreading names the wait — the mind's rehearsal of suffering before the event. An orphan word for the most universal of vigils, it has needed no ancestry to become indispensable.
