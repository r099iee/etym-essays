- 19th c. — military and colonial term for a fixed station of observation; appears in army manuals, travel narratives, and frontier fiction; strictly concrete and military
- Late 19th–early 20th c. — enters adventure literature (Kipling, Haggard, and later Tolkien's The Hobbit, 1937), acquiring a romantic, imperial-adventure flavor; also used of naval lookouts and signal stations
- Mid-20th c. — wartime usage: watch-posts, observation posts, and picket lines fill war reportage; the word is workaday military jargon but still concrete
- Late 20th–21st c. — recedes from everyday speech, displaced by "observation post," "lookout," and "guard post"; now reads as literary, historical, or archaic, and its two transparent parts keep it instantly comprehensible
- Present — also used figuratively for any role demanding vigilance; a semi-archaic, mostly literary word, prized in historical and fantasy prose for its concrete, old-fashioned clarity
