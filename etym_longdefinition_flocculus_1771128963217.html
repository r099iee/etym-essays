flocculus is a Latinate noun meaning "a small tuft, a little flock or lock of hair, wool, or fiber; a small fluffy mass". It is the diminutive of Latin floccus ("a tuft of wool, a flock"), formed with the diminutive suffix -ulus, and it carries the double connotation of smallness and soft, fibrous, woolly texture. In modern technical use the word has three main specialized senses: an anatomical one (a small lobe of the brain), an astronomical one (a bright or dark patch on the sun), and a general scientific one (a small tufted aggregate, as in chemistry). In the card's literary context, "an flocculus of humor" is a wonderfully precise metaphor: a small, woolly, stray wisp of humor that has broken off from a man's usually balanced nature—light, faintly comical, slightly unravelled.

Sense 1: The literal sense—"a small tuft, lock, or flock". At its root, flocculus denotes a tiny tuft of wool, hair, or fiber—a small clump of soft, fluffy material. It differs from plain tuft or flock in its diminutive force: the Latin suffix -ulus shrinks the already small floccus ("a tuft of wool") into something still smaller and finer. It can describe a lock of hair, a wisp of wool, a tuft of down or lint. This literal sense is the semantic core from which all technical senses descend: a flocculus is always a small, soft, tufted mass.

Sense 2: The anatomical sense—the flocculus of the cerebellum. In anatomy and neuroscience, the flocculus is a small, lobulated structure on the underside of the cerebellum, part of the vestibulocerebellum, which is involved in the control of balance and eye movements (the vestibulo-ocular reflex). It is so named because its lobed, tufted appearance resembles a small tuft of wool or a flock. Anatomists, working in the Latin scientific tradition, simply Latinized the metaphor: the little woolly lobe of the brain is the flocculus cerebelli. This sense is standard in medical terminology (attested in English since the 19th century).

Sense 3: The astronomical sense—a solar flocculus. In astronomy, a flocculus (plural flocculi) is a bright or dark patch seen on the surface of the sun, especially in spectroheliographic images taken in the light of hydrogen (Hα) or calcium (K). These fluffy, tufted-looking regions of the chromosphere are named for their resemblance to tufts of wool. The term dates from the early 20th century, when spectroheliography first revealed the sun's mottled, fleecy appearance: bright flocculi around sunspots, dark flocculi elsewhere.

Sense 4: The chemical and general scientific sense—a flocculent aggregate. In chemistry and materials science, a floccule (or flocculus) is a small, loosely aggregated clump of particles that forms when a colloid or suspension flocculates—when fine particles clump together into soft, woolly masses that settle out. The adjective flocculent ("woolly, tufted; forming soft clumps") and the noun flocculation (the process) belong to the same family. This usage is frequent in water treatment, brewing, and soil science.

Sense 5: The literary/metaphorical sense—a small stray wisp. In literary prose, flocculus can be used with a wink toward its literal meaning to name any small, soft, faintly comical wisp that has come loose from a larger whole. The card's phrase, "an flocculus of humor that seemed to have strayed from the man's usually balanced nature", uses the word as a miniature metaphor: the man's character is a fleece of balanced humors, and this particular "flocculus" is a stray tuft of humor that has drifted away—light, woolly, incongruous, almost endearing in its waywardness. The word's scientific gravity and its woolly smallness combine to create a precise, ironic, and slightly whimsical image.

Synonym spectrum and boundaries.

  • Tuft: Similar because both mean a small clump of hair or fiber; differs because tuft is generic and can be larger or firmly rooted, while flocculus is a diminutive—smaller, softer, more woolly.
  • Flock: Similar because both denote a tuft of wool; differs because flock (in the textile sense) is a mass or waste of wool fibers, not a tiny singled-out tuft.
  • Wisp: Similar because both mean a small, light, loose piece; differs because wisp suggests smoke, cloud, or a few thin strands, while flocculus implies a soft, woolly, clumped texture.
  • Lock: Similar because both can denote a tress of hair; differs because lock is a longer, gathered portion of hair, while flocculus is a small fluffy tuft.
  • Nodule: Similar because both denote a small round mass; differs because a nodule is hard and solid, while a flocculus is soft, fibrous, and woolly.
  • Clump: Similar because both mean a small mass; differs because clump is generic and implies stickiness or density, while flocculus implies dry, airy fluffiness.

Conclusion. Flocculus is a diminutive of a diminutive: a small tuft made smaller and softer by Latin grammar. From sheep's wool it traveled to the brain, the sun, and the chemistry laboratory, and in the card it returns to poetry as the image of a stray tuft of humor drifting from a balanced mind—proving that a word for wool can be pressed into the most delicate metaphorical service.
