DOCTRINAIRE — the biography of a teacher's word turned into an insult. The word is the youngest member of one of the oldest families in our vocabulary, a family whose reconstructed ancestor, Proto-Indo-European *dek-, meant "to take, to accept". The crucial semantic step was taken in Latin. Where most branches of the family simply kept the root as "take", Latin developed a causative from it: docēre is literally "to cause to take (into the mind)" — to make something acceptable — and hence "to show, to teach". Out of this single verb grew an entire learned civilization: doctus "taught, learned", doctor "teacher" (the ancestor of our doctor and doctorate), documentum "a lesson, an example, a proof" (our document), and the twin learning words discere "to learn" and discipulus "pupil" (our disciple and discipline), which come from a lengthened *dek-sk- form meaning literally "to take up knowledge". On the Greek side of the same root, dékhomai "to accept" produced dokeîn "to seem, to think" and, through it, the nouns dógma and dóxa that lie behind the DOGMA card — a true sibling, since doctrinaire and dogma are distant cousins of one root, not one word, and the two lines must be kept distinct. At the heart of the family stands the abstract noun doctrīna — "teaching, instruction, learning" — which gave Old French doctrine and English doctrine, recorded in the 1380s. For centuries doctrine was a neutral word for a body of teaching, religious or philosophical, and its derivative adjectives were equally neutral: doctrinal means merely "pertaining to doctrine". The pejorative turn was a political accident. The formation doctrinaire was created in France, not as a general adjective but as the nickname of a party. In 1815, after Napoleon's fall and the Bourbon Restoration, a group of constitutionalist politicians — Pierre-Paul Royer-Collard and François Guizot among them — sought to reconcile the monarchy with the Revolution through abstract, general principles: parliamentary sovereignty, the balance of powers, the rights of reason. Their opponents, the ultra-royalists, mocked them as les doctrinaires: men who ruled by doctrine rather than by experience, who applied a theory to France as a professor applies a syllabus to a class. The word was born as an insult, coined from doctrine with the person-marking suffix -aire (the same suffix as in millionaire and concessionaire), and the contempt of its birth has never washed out of it.
  • The party itself was real and influential: the Doctrinaires dominated moderate politics in the Restoration, and Guizot went on to serve as prime minister under the July Monarchy.
  • The nickname spread: French doctrinaire was borrowed into English around 1840, exactly as Guizot's reputation peaked in the 1830s and 1840s.
  • From a party label it generalized to any person or work that fits reality to a fixed theory — the sense the Gibbon passage uses.
English took the word eagerly because it needed it: no native word named the vice of ruling by abstract principle, and the English had been arguing for two centuries about whether their constitution was a body of practice or a set of doctrines. Doctrinaire gave the language a precise, contemptuous adjective for the theory-monger. In the nineteenth century it described political dogmatists, economic system-builders, and religious formalists; in the twentieth it attached itself to the great ideologies — doctrinaire communism, doctrinaire liberalism, doctrinaire socialism — and became a standard term of historical and political analysis, always pejorative, always cool. The word's peculiar value is that it names a specific intellectual vice with surgical precision. A dogmatic person asserts truths without proof; a doctrinaire person imposes a doctrine on every case regardless of consequences. The doctrinaire is not inflamed like a fanatic — he is cool, systematic, and principle-driven — and he is not merely theoretical, for theory as such does not insist on being applied. The doctrinaire's signature move is the imposition: he fits evidence to doctrine rather than doctrine to evidence. In the Gibbon passage the essayist claims that few works in the social sciences have had "so small a dogmatic or doctrinaire ingredient" as the Decline and Fall — that Gibbon neither asserts unproved truths nor forces a favorite theory onto the past — and the pairing of the two adjectives exhausts both forms of intellectual rigidity at once.
  • Semantic drift: to take → to cause to take (into the mind) → to teach → doctrine → (1815) the party nickname → (c. 1840 English) rigid application of theory to reality.
  • Register: always critical; essayistic, political, and scholarly; cool rather than hot; the noun and adjective both pejorative.
  • Modern state: a standard term for ideology-driven rigidity, still carrying the ghost of the French professors of the Restoration whom the ultras mocked.
The evolution of doctrinaire is thus a study in how a neutral word for teaching hardened, through one political quarrel, into the English word for the intellectual vice of preferring a system to the evidence — and how a teacher's language became the name of its own worst habit.
