- 14th c. - 'lasshen' enters English; 'lashing' names violent whip-strokes - the standard language of punishment and flogging
- 16th-17th c. - naval and legal floggings make 'a lashing' an everyday, brutal reality; 'lash' also develops the separate sense 'to bind with rope'
- 17th-18th c. - the nautical lashing (rope binding cargo and masts) becomes professional and technical; the beating sense remains dominant in justice and discipline
- 19th c. - British colloquial 'lashings of' (1835) is born meaning abundant quantities, especially food and drink - the word splits into one violent and one cosy personality
- 20th-21st c. - judicial flogging fades, weakening the punishment sense; 'lashings of' survives as a warm, old-fashioned idiom of plenty; lashing keeps its stormy life in 'lashing rain' and 'lashing waves'
