Derivation chain for elms (single root word, with a parallel Latin branch): 1. Proto-Indo-European *el- (extended form *elem-) "red, reddish-brown" — applied to the elm tree, named for the reddish hue of its bark and wood (c. 4500–3500 BCE) — note: whether the root primarily meant "red-brown" or named the tree directly is debated (see note). 2. Proto-Germanic *elmaz (also reconstructed *almaz) "elm tree" (reconstructed, c. 1st millennium BCE–CE) — cf. Old Norse almr, Old High German elmboum, Middle Dutch olm. 3. Old English elm (masculine noun) "elm tree" (attested c. 8th–11th c.). 4. Middle English elm (attested c. 12th–15th c.) — unchanged in form. 5. Modern English elm; plural elms (regular -s plural, established by Early Modern English) — target root word. Parallel branch (from the same PIE *el-): • Latin ulmus "elm tree" (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) — source of the botanical genus name Ulmus, and of Italian olmo, French orme, Spanish olmo. Note on debate/nuance: the PIE root *el- is reconstructed on the basis of the closely agreeing Germanic, Latin, Celtic (Old Irish lem, Welsh llwyf), and Balto-Slavic (Russian il’m, Czech jilm) forms, and is generally accepted; whether it originally meant "reddish-brown" (a color-term origin) or was already a tree-name is debated. The English chain is remarkably short and stable — elm names are extremely conservative, and the plural elms adds only the regular plural suffix. No attested stage has been invented, merged, or skipped.
