• Crack: Similar because both name a narrow break in a surface; differs because crack is far broader and more everyday, covering wood, glass, plaster and even sound (a “crack of thunder”), whereas crevice is restricted to narrow openings, usually in rock or masonry, and has no verb use.
  • Chink: Similar because both denote a small narrow opening; differs because chink suggests an opening between or through things (as between boards or in armour) that lets light through, while crevice implies a split within a solid mass.
  • Fissure: Similar because both are narrow openings in rock or the earth; differs because fissure is the more technical, geological term for a fracture, often longer and deeper, whereas crevice is smaller and more ordinary.
  • Crevasse: Similar because both derive from the same French source and name an opening in rock or ice; differs because crevasse is a deep, open crack, specifically in a glacier, far larger and more dangerous than a crevice.
  • Interstice: Similar because both name a small intervening gap; differs because interstice is formal and technical, used of the gaps between closely packed objects (bricks, cells, atoms), not typically of rock faces.
  • Slit: Similar because both are narrow openings; differs because slit implies a cut or incision made by something sharp and usually applies to fabric, paper, or membrane, whereas a crevice is a naturally formed split.
  • Cleft: Similar because both are splits in rock or land; differs because cleft is broader and often formed by splitting or chopping, and can be used of a chin or a hoof, which crevice cannot.
  • Gap: Similar because both are openings or breaks; differs because gap is generic and can be any size or in any medium, whereas crevice specifically names a narrow crack in a hard solid.
  • Fault (geological): Similar because both relate to breaks in rock; differs because a fault is a large-scale fracture along which rock masses move, a strictly technical geological term, whereas crevice is a small everyday fissure.
  • Aperture: Similar because both are openings; differs because aperture is deliberately neutral and technical (used of optical and mechanical openings) and carries no sense of rupture or breakage.
  • Rift: Similar because both are openings caused by separation; differs because rift implies a forcible, large splitting (of rock, clouds, or relations), far grander than a small crevice.
