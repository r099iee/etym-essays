• Flood: Similar because both denote water overflowing land; differs because flood is the neutral, generic term — any overflow of water onto dry land, from a river flood to a burst pipe — and lacks deluge's biblical scale, its sense of total catastrophe, and its elevated literary register.
  • Inundation: Similar because both denote the covering of land by water; differs because inundation is the formal, technical word of hydrology and official reports, stressing the extent of coverage — land being covered — rather than the violence of the event; a deluge is violent and overwhelming, an inundation merely extensive.
  • Torrent: Similar because both involve rushing water; differs because torrent emphasizes speed and violence in a narrow, raging stream, whereas a deluge is a general, all-consuming flood covering everything — a torrent is a fast river, a deluge is the sky coming down.
  • Downpour: Similar because both suggest great quantities of water falling at once; differs because downpour and cloudburst name the heavy rainfall itself, a weather event, whereas a deluge is the resulting flood — the catastrophe on the ground, not the rain in the air.
  • Cataclysm: Similar because both denote an overwhelming disaster; differs because cataclysm is a great upheaval or disaster of any kind — geological, social, or cosmic — not necessarily watery, whereas a deluge is specifically a watery catastrophe; a deluge is one species of cataclysm.
  • Overwhelm (as verb-to-noun parallel): Similar because both convey being buried under a great quantity; differs because overwhelm is a verb of pure volume and pressure, with no water imagery, whereas deluge always keeps a trace of its watery, drowning origin even in its most figurative uses.
  • Spate: Similar because both denote a great onrush of water or things; differs because spate suggests a sudden, brief surge — a river in spate — and in British English a burst of activity, whereas a deluge is larger, total, and slower-moving, a catastrophe rather than a surge.
  • Surfeit / glut: Similar because both denote an excessive quantity; differs because surfeit and glut stress harmful excess or overabundance of goods or food, a matter of supply and appetite, whereas a deluge stresses overwhelming arrival that buries the recipient.
  • Stream / flood-tide: Similar because both denote flowing water in quantity; differs because stream is small and continuous and lacks the element of catastrophe, whereas a deluge is sudden, total, and destructive.
  • Antediluvian (family relative): Similar because it shares the deluge's biblical reference to the Flood; differs because antediluvian is an adjective meaning 'belonging to the period before the Flood' and, by extension, 'hopelessly old-fashioned,' while deluge names the Flood and its metaphorical overloads.
