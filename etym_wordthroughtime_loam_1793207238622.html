- Old English — workable mud: lam is clay, mud, mortar — the potter's earth, the dauber's earth, whatever holds shape when wet.
- Middle English — bricks and pots: lome feeds the brickyards and tile-kilns of growing towns; loam-pits scar the commons outside London.
- 1500s — husbandry notices it: Fitzherbert and Tusser sort good earths from bad; the fruitful mixture begins to earn a name of praise.
- 1700s — improving landlords: Tull, Townshend, and Young preach texture and tilth; loam becomes the scientific ideal of the Agricultural Revolution.
- 1800s — Cobbett's evidence: black loam over Thames gravel proves good land abused by tax-eaters; soil-reading becomes political journalism.
- 1900s — the texture triangle: soil science formalises loam as the balanced centre of sand, silt, and clay; the brickfield sense fades as London clay and concrete take over.
- Today — the gardener's grail: every seed packet dreams of well-drained loam; foundry loam survives in bell-casting and art casting, the old mud still taking impressions.
