Elk-teeth is the plural of elk-tooth: the two vestigial upper canine tusks (ivories) of the North American elk or wapiti (Cervus canadensis), and by extension the Eurasian elk/moose (Alces alces). Unlike molars for chewing, these small, creamy, slightly curved teeth - about 2-3 cm long - are remnants of ancient tusks, composed of ivory-like dentine with a glossy enamel cap. In Indigenous Plains and Plateau cultures and later among frontier traders, elk-teeth were drilled, polished, and sewn onto dresses, pouches, and jewellery as display of hunting prowess and wealth. In the source passage, two elk-teeth equaled one pony because they were durable, portable, countable, and universally admired - classic commodity money before coinage.

Zoologically, the term sits at the intersection of dentition and trophy. Adult elk have 34 teeth; the ivories have no chewing function but persist evolutionarily. Hunters call them ivories, whistlers, or bugle teeth, saving them from a first kill for rings, tie tacks, and watch fobs, especially in the Elks fraternal order (Benevolent and Protective Order of Elks) whose emblem uses elk-teeth. Dentists and wildlife biologists age elk by molar wear, not ivories, but ivories signal maturity. Scrimshaw artists carve them like miniature walrus ivory, though modern trade is regulated where elk are protected.

Anthropologically, elk-teeth exemplify ornament-money. Like cowrie shells, wampum beads, and boar tusks, they bridge adornment and currency: sewn in rows on a woman's elk-tooth dress (sometimes hundreds of teeth representing many hunts and great value), they are simultaneously savings account, status display, and bridal wealth. Early ethnographers record dresses valued at many horses. Their value derives from costliness - each elk yields only two, hunting is dangerous and seasonal - plus beauty, durability, and divisibility by count. When the passage says eight ponies equaled a wife, it places elk-teeth in a bridewealth system where teeth, ponies, and blankets interconvert.

  • Biological sense: vestigial upper canines, ivory composition, present in both sexes but larger in bulls; also called tusks or ivories.
  • Ornamental sense: polished, drilled, sewn regalia; elk-tooth dresses, necklaces, earrings; Elks lodge jewellery.
  • Monetary sense: countable units of value on frontiers; rate two teeth to pony in cited account; comparable to beaver pelts, dentalium shells.
  • Craft sense: scrimshawed or gold-mounted keepsakes; tie pins, rings; modern imitation resin teeth for reenactors.
  • Linguistic note: hyphenated plural elk-teeth from elk-tooth; singular rarely used alone except by jewellers; alternative spelling elk teeth open form common.
  • Conservation sense: legal harvest only, shed teeth never (they do not shed); trade in imitation versus real ivory rules under marine-mammal versus ungulate law.

The bridge from anatomy to economy is direct: rarity plus beauty creates desire, desire plus countability creates money. Unlike gold, elk-teeth cannot be melted or divided, but they can be accumulated visibly on clothing, making wealth public. Their decline as money follows overhunting, reservation economies, and coin introduction, but their prestige as ornament persists among hunters and Native artisans. To understand the source equation, picture not loose teeth but gleaming rows on hide - portable, shining proof of value anyone would take.
