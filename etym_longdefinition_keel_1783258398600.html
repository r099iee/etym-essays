In the sentence "their weight made the __________ sink deep in the water," the noun keel names the lowermost longitudinal timber of a vessel, the single member that runs from stem to stern along the very bottom of the hull and serves as the ship's structural backbone. The word is deliberately physical and specific: it is not the whole boat that is described but the deepest, most hidden timber of it, the part normally invisible beneath the waterline. That precision is the point of the ghost-story detail. The weight of the dead, though invisible and only betrayed by "the speech of the dead" that the boatmen hear, presses the keel "deep" down into the water, so that the spectral burden is registered in the most concrete, engineering way possible. The eerie power of the sentence comes from this collision of the supernatural with the mechanical: the dead are bodiless, yet their mass has the exact, measurable effect of ballast. The tone is solemn, hushed, and ominous, with the old nautical word keel lending the vessel a gothic, seafaring antiquity.

The full polysemy of keel. Like many ancient nautical words, keel has grown a wide family of senses around its central timber.

  • 1. The structural timber (the card's sense). The lowermost longitudinal member of a ship's hull, running stem to stern, from which the ribs rise like the vertebrae of a spine. Extended compounds: false keel, bilge keel, fin keel.
  • 2. By synecdoche, a ship itself (poetic and archaic). Old English cēol and Old Norse kjǫlr could name the whole vessel: the word for the timber stood for the ship, and in Anglo-Saxon poetry 'the keel' is simply 'the ship'.
  • 3. Figurative — the base, foundation, or central principle of a thing. To be on an even keel is to be steady, balanced and stable; to keep something on an even keel is to keep it running smoothly; the keel of a project is its foundation.
  • 4. Verb senses. To keel (of a ship) is to keel over — to capsize; of a person, to keel over is to collapse or faint; to keelhaul is to punish by dragging under the keel.
  • 5. A flat-bottomed barge on the Tyne (historical dialect). In north-eastern England the keel was a broad-beamed coal barge, and its crew the keelmen — a separate borrowing from Middle Dutch kiel 'ship, barge' that happens to share the same spelling.
  • 6. Biology and anatomy — a ridge or carina. The projecting ridge on the breastbone of birds where the flight muscles attach; the sharp dorsal ridge of certain shells; the fused lower petals of a pea flower; a raised ridge on a leaf. All are called a keel because they run like the ship's spine.
  • 7. As a combining element. Keelboat, keelhaul, keelson (the inner keel), keel-block, keel-laying.

Historical bridge. The modern sense of 'timber' was fixed by the Viking age. The native English word, Old English cēol, meant 'ship' rather than 'timber'; it was the Norse borrowing kjǫlr, reinforced by the Scandinavian seafarers who settled and traded in England from the 9th century, that supplied the precise engineering sense of the lowermost timber, recorded in English from the 14th century. The old 'ship' meaning survives only in poetry and old sea-speech.

Boundaries. The hull is the whole body or shell of the vessel, the entire frame and planking; the keel is only the central spine at the bottom from which the ribs rise. The bottom or floor is a vague, non-technical word for the lower part. The ribs (or frames) are the transverse timbers attached to the keel that give the hull its shape. The stem and stern-post are the fore and aft end members. Figuratively, keel gives the very base or foundation of a thing, but with a distinctly structural, nautical colour — it is the spine, not merely the ground.

Collocations. On an even keel, keel over, lay the keel (start building a ship), the keel of the ship, deep-keeled, false keel, bilge keel, keelhaul. In the sentence of the card, the collocation 'made the keel sink deep' is deliberately physical: the supernatural burden registers as ballast pressing the very spine of the boat down into the water.
