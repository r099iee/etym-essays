- [Mid-15th century: the door on the turn] - the phrase "on char(e)" describes a door literally 'on the turn', caught in the act of swinging — it names a mechanical moment, a hinge arrested mid-motion, with no hint yet of secrets or suspense.
- [16th–17th century: narrowing to the gap] - the phrase contracts to mean simply "turned a little way", the small physical result of the turn; the sense fixes on a door slightly open, and the word begins its life as a state rather than an event.
- [1718: fusion into ajar] - "on a jar" and then simply ajar; the compressed word specializes purely to 'neither open nor shut', and its everyday register is plain and domestic — the door, the window, the gate.
- [19th century: the storyteller's crack of light] - ajar becomes the writer's word for suspense and invitation: a door ajar is a recently opened door that may close at any moment, a sliver of light or danger, the threshold of interruption; the small domestic adjective acquires a frisson.
- [Contemporary sense] - ajar is still used almost only of doors, windows, and gates, and still carries that suspended, precarious note — caught between two states — which is exactly what it lends the hobbit's narrowing escape route in the dark passage.
