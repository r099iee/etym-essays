The biography of pretension is a biography of stretching. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ten-, "to stretch, extend," one of the most productive roots in the entire family, and it ends with a word that today means an unearned claim to status or knowledge — the empty gesture of the showy and the shallow. Between the root and the modern sneer lies a chain of metaphors, each recorded in the historical record, that show how a verb about bodily posture became a noun about social pretence. Stage one: the stretched root. The PIE root *ten- was a verb of extension whose descendants span Europe and India: Sanskrit tanóti "he stretches," already in the Rigveda; Greek teínein "to stretch" and its nominal tónos "tension," the ancestor of English tone; Latin tendere "to stretch"; and in the Germanic branch an adjectival formation that yielded English thin — the idea being "stretched out, attenuated." Even the name of the illness tetanus preserves the root, since tetanos literally meant "stiff, stretched." The root carried no moral value whatever: it described the physical act of pulling something out to length. Stage two: Latin splits the root in two. In Latin, *ten- developed into two living verbs. Tendere, "to stretch," gave tense, tension, tensile, extend, contend, attend, distend, intend, and the tent, a canvas stretched over its poles. Tenēre, "to hold," arose from the same image — to stretch something is to keep it taut, to hold it out — and produced tenacious, tenure, tenant, tenable, contain, content, and continue. The branch that matters for pretension is the compound praetendere, built with the preposition prae-, "before, in front." Stage three: the crucial leap from body to speech. Praetendere first meant physical display: a soldier stretches a shield in front of his body; a man holds a cloth before a door to conceal what is behind it; a runner holds out his hand to fend off an opponent. From this bodily act the verb was extended to language: to put something forward before others as an excuse, an allegation, a screen — so that praetendere acquired the meaning "to allege, to use as a pretext." The great Roman advocate Cicero uses the verb in exactly this forensic sense, of pleading a false reason in place of a true one. The step is the hinge of the whole word-history: stretching a shield in front of oneself and alleging an excuse are the same gesture, one physical, one verbal. From the past participle praetēnsus, medieval scholastic Latin formed the noun praetensio, "a putting forward, an allegation, a claim," and English borrowed it in the mid-fifteenth century as pretensioun. Stage four: the word enters English innocent. The earliest English meanings of pretension are startlingly neutral: "assertion, allegation, objection, intention, signification." The word was born as an innocent filing of claims, the technical vocabulary of lawyers and rhetoricians. It had no more moral colour than the modern word "submission" or "declaration." The souring came later and is recorded step by step in English lexicography: around 1600 pretension takes the sense of a claim regarded as unearned or doubtful; by the eighteenth century it is fully pejorative — Johnson's Dictionary registers the claim sense with examples that assume the speaker's distrust of the claimant. Stage five: the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The eighteenth century made pretension a social word. The age of the connoisseur, the Grand Tour, and the new middle classes was also the age of the sham — the man who claimed taste he did not have, gentility he had not inherited, learning he had not acquired. The Stuart Pretenders of 1688–1745 (from the same family via pretender "one who lays claim") kept the word in the public ear as a political claim to a throne; the social satirists turned it on the aspirational classes. When Ruskin writes in the mid-nineteenth century of "the ignorant sarcasms of pretension," he is using a word already loaded with a century of social suspicion — and he is giving it a new, aesthetic edge: the pretension he attacks is the claim of critics to artistic authority, an assertion of standing in the republic of taste that their ignorance exposes. Sound and form. Phonetically the word has been stable since its adoption: Latin praetēnsio passed into English as pretensioun, with the initial cluster simplified and the Latin suffix -io naturalized as English -ion. The spelling later settled as pretension, matching French prétention, from which English also re-borrowed the word in the fifteenth century. The adjective pretentious is a much later, purely English formation (1852, on the model of French prétentieux), and it has almost entirely displaced the noun in everyday talk — people call a film or a restaurant "pretentious," while "pretension" has retreated to the register of literary and social criticism. Today. The modern word remains double-faced. The plural pretensions is often a mere record of ambition — "his pretensions to gentility" — softened almost to "aspirations." The singular keeps the accusatory charge: a pretension is a claim stretched beyond its true reach, a false front held up to be seen. The word's social career — from neutral legal term, to political claim, to aesthetic slur — is a small history of how a culture learned to distrust the gap between what people claim and what they are. In Ruskin it does its final work: the "ignorant sarcasms of pretension" are dismissed with a phrase that does not bother to argue, because a claim stretched too far falls of its own weight.
