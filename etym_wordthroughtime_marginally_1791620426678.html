- Mid-19th c. - Formed in bibliographical and scholarly printing to describe textual annotations or glosses placed in the literal margin of a folio or book; entirely literal and descriptive.
- Late 19th c. - Adopted into technical economic theory (the Marginalist Revolution of Jevons, Menger, and Marshall) to describe incremental shifts occurring at the margin of production or consumption; perceived as rigorous, high-level scientific terminology.
- Mid-20th c. - Broadened into sociology and politics to describe the precarious existence of disenfranchised classes on the societal fringe; carried serious, empathetic socio-political connotations.
- Late 20th c. to Present - Universalized across everyday English, journalism, and business reviews as a common hedging adverb of degree meaning 'slightly' or 'barely'; tone shifted to conversational, mildly dismissive, or mathematically cautious.
