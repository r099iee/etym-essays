- Classical Rome — what is furnished: Latin praebere means to furnish or supply, and its gerundive praebenda names whatever must be supplied — the due, the allowance.
- Early Middle Ages — the Church’s allowance: cathedral chapters pay each canon his praebenda in food, lodging, and coin; the system feeds the clergy of Europe.
- 14th c. — England borrows twice: the Church keeps prebend for the canon’s stipend while the stable-yard takes provender for the horse’s ration — one Latin mother, two English children.
- 15th–16th c. — horses and soldiers: provender is overwhelmingly animal and military — oats for cavalry, hay for garrisons, measured by the quartermaster’s bushel.
- 17th–18th c. — the table joins the manger: travellers, inns, and country houses speak of good provender for men; plenty, not mere ration, becomes the note.
- 19th c. — the mind is fed: essayists and divines discover intellectual and spiritual provender; books become fodder, sermons become feed.
- Today — the Shire’s word: half-archaic, wholly affectionate, surviving in farms, fantasies, and jokes about teenagers — Tolkien’s hobbit-parents its fondest employers.
