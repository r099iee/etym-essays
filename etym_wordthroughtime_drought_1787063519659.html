- Old English drūgaþ "dryness, a dry condition" - a plain abstract noun from "dry", describing any dryness of ground, air, or mouth.
- Middle English droughte - increasingly tied to weather: "a time of drought" = a period without rain, with serious agricultural consequences in the old rural world.
- 16th–19th c. - the word becomes a standard meteorological and economic term; droughts are chronicled as disasters causing famine, migration, and ruined harvests.
- 20th–21st c. - the scientific meaning sharpens (meteorological, agricultural, hydrological, socioeconomic drought); the word enters climate policy and disaster reporting.
- Modern - also figurative: "a drought of ideas", "a goal drought"; the archaic dialect form "drouth" survives in Scottish and American speech.
