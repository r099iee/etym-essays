Alliterative is an adjective meaning characterised by alliteration — the conspicuous repetition of the same initial consonant sound (and sometimes vowel) in a sequence of stressed words: "wild and woolly", "safe and sound", "Peter Piper picked". An alliterative phrase, line, slogan, or style deliberately exploits this echo for emphasis, memorability, musicality, or comic effect. In prosody it can denote verse built on this principle (alliterative metre); in rhetoric it denotes ornament added to prose (alliterative isocolon, alliterative pairs); in everyday criticism it notes any noticeable tongue-twister texture.

The device is among the oldest in Germanic poetics. Old English Beowulf, Middle English Piers Plowman and Sir Gawain are written in alliterative long lines where two or three stressed syllables share an initial sound, bound by caesura rather than rhyme. The fourteenth-century Alliterative Revival consciously opposed French rhyming fashion with native stalk-and-echo music. Later, alliterative doubling became the backbone of English idiom and branding: "bells and whistles", "dead as a doornail", "kit and caboodle", "Coca-Cola", "Best Buy", "PayPal". Teachers of style therefore speak without surprise of an alliterative isocolon — balanced parallel clauses further tied by matching initials — as the showiest tool of a hypotactic nominal style.

Senses fan out by domain. In phonetics, alliterative describes onset repetition irrespective of spelling ("cynical citizens" alliterates though c differs). In child language and speech therapy, alliterative play builds phonemic awareness ("silly Sammy snake"). In journalism and advertising, alliterative headlines ("Brexit Blunder", "Marvellous Monday") are criticised as cheap but irresistible. In onomastics, alliterative names (Marilyn Monroe, Ronald Reagan) are remembered as euphonious. The evaluative charge varies: admiring for poetry and oratory, affectionate for nicknames, scornful for purple prose and tabloid punning.

  • Poetic — in alliterative metre: composed in the Germanic stress-and-initial system; alliterative verse, the Alliterative Revival.
  • Rhetorical — using initial echo: alliterative pairs, triplets, slogans, and isocola for balance and punch.
  • Descriptive — having that texture: noting accidental or habitual echo, whether praised or mocked as tongue-twisting.
  • Branding — mnemonic doubling: product and headline language chosen for sticky initial music.
  • Collocations: alliterative verse, alliterative prose, alliterative headline, alliterative nickname, alliteratively paired; antonym is non-alliterative, with assonant and rhyming as neighbouring devices.
  • Classroom note: not mere rhyme or repetition: strict alliteration concerns stressed onsets, so "proud and proper prince" qualifies while "ship and champagne" does not in most analyses.

To call a style alliterative is to hear it before judging it — to notice the drumbeat of initials that makes language festive, childlike, tribal, or tediously overworked, depending on dose and ear.
