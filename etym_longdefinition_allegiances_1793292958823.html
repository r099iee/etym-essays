Allegiances, the plural of allegiance, names the loyalties, devotions, and binding commitments that tie a person to a sovereign, nation, party, faith, institution, or — as in the source sentence — even to an intellectual theory. Where the singular often denotes the abstract bond itself, the plural stresses multiplicity and division: rival claims on one heart, loyalties that can clash, shift, or be weighed against each other.

In the source sentence the word is used in its transferred intellectual sense: impassioned allegiances to theories are partisan devotions to schools of thought, the kind of fierce camp-following that makes scholars defend a framework as soldiers once defended a lord. The author, writing on orality and literacy, warns that such study does not normally produce hot partisanship but rather calm, wide-ranging reflection — the plural matters because theories compete, and each solicits its own following.

Core meaning in detail: At its centre allegiance is a duty of fidelity acknowledged as obligatory: the citizen owes allegiance to the state, the vassal once owed it to the lord, the believer to the creed. It is owed rather than merely felt, which separates it from affection or admiration — one can admire many things but owe allegiance only where a bond of duty, oath, birth, or enrolment exists. Dictionaries define it as loyalty or commitment to a superior, group, or cause, and the plural extends this to the whole set of bonds a person carries at once.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Duty of loyalty to a sovereign or state (primary legal-civic sense, from c. 1300): the obligation of a subject or citizen to the crown or country, whose breach is treason. This is the founding sense, born in feudal homage and carried into modern citizenship oaths and pledges.
  • Feudal homage of vassal to lord (earliest historical sense): the sworn fidelity of the liege man, sealed by oath and service. The bridge to the modern civic sense is direct institutional descent: the personal oath to a lord became the impersonal duty to the state.
  • Party, tribal, or factional loyalty (modern political sense): as in shifting party allegiances or tribal allegiances in a divided electorate. The bridge runs through the civic sense narrowed to voluntary association: the voter is treated as a liege man of a party, free to transfer homage at each election.
  • Devotion to a cause, faith, or movement (extended sense): religious, ideological, or charitable allegiances. The bridge is metaphorical elevation: the structure of feudal duty is mapped onto belief, so that converts are said to transfer or renounce allegiance exactly as subjects once did.
  • Intellectual or aesthetic partisanship (the source sense): allegiances to theories, schools, or styles — Freudian versus Jungian allegiances, modernist allegiances. The bridge carries the language of feudal war into the seminar room: rival doctrines become rival lords, and scholars their sworn followers.
  • Brand, club, and sporting loyalties (colloquial contemporary sense): football allegiances, brand allegiances in marketing surveys. The bridge is commercial and tribal analogy: consumer researchers borrow the gravity of civic duty to dignify repeat purchase, while supporters embrace the warrior register gladly.
  • The oath or pledge itself (metonymic sense): to swear or pledge allegiance, where the word names the vow rather than the bond. The bridge is act-for-state metonymy: the ceremony that creates the duty lends its name to the duty created.

Register and connotation: allegiances is formal, weighty, and faintly martial — courtrooms, citizenship ceremonies, manifestos, and editorials rather than chat. In the plural it often carries a note of tension or appraisal, since divided or shifting allegiances imply conflict, conscience, or betrayal. The source sentence uses it with mild irony: theories, unlike nations, should not command impassioned troops, and the scholar who treats them as liege lords has mistaken reflection for warfare.
