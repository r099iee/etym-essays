In Gibbon's sentence DEVOLVED is the key verb of the constitutional transfer: "The Roman senate appeared to possess the sovereign authority, and devolved on the emperors all the executive powers of government." The word pictures the executive powers of government as rolling or flowing downward from the Senate onto the emperors — a slow, quasi-gravitational, and almost passive transfer rather than a violent seizure. Gibbon's choice of devolved is precise: it conveys a legal and customary handing-down of power, the formal (if partly fictional) republican grant by which the Senate surrendered its executive role to the princeps, while the physical image of something rolling downhill quietly hints at loss of height, dignity, and practical control. What looks like orderly delegation is also the Senate's gradual surrender of real power; the word holds both the formal fiction of constitutional grant and the historical fact of imperial accumulation. The core sense of DEVOLVED (past tense and past participle of devolve) is: "passed down or transferred to another person, body, or level, especially power, authority, or responsibility"; and more specifically "to roll or flow downward." In Gibbon's usage it means "handed over (executive powers) to the emperors" — transferred downwards from the sovereign Senate to the imperial office. The verb's family includes the noun devolution ("the transfer of powers from central to local or regional government") and the adjective devolved (as in "devolved administrations," "devolved powers"), terms now central to the constitutional vocabulary of the United Kingdom, Spain, Italy, and other states.
  • Transfer of powers: the core modern sense — to transfer power, responsibility, or authority to a lower level or another body, as in "devolved on the emperors," "power devolved to local government," "devolved administrations."
  • Legal/hereditary transfer: in law, to devolve on (someone) means to pass by legal succession or hereditary transmission, as property, rights, or duties devolve on heirs or successors.
  • Rolling/flowing downward (literal): the original physical sense — to roll down, flow down, or descend, as a river or an object rolling downhill.
  • Degeneration sense: "to devolve into" (chaos, violence, a state) means to degenerate or sink into a worse condition — a late semantic extension in which the rolling-down image becomes moral and political decay.
  • Devolution (noun): the institutional term for the transfer of central-government powers to regional assemblies or devolved parliaments (e.g., Scottish Parliament, Welsh Senedd, Northern Ireland Assembly).
  • Adjectival "devolved": in modern constitutional and administrative English, "devolved powers," "devolved responsibilities," "devolved government" describe matters handled below the central level.
The historical bridge from the literal to the constitutional sense is the image of rolling. The Latin verb devolvere means "to roll down, roll away" (de- "down, away" + volvere "to roll"), and Roman law used the idea of property or rights "rolling down" to an heir — the legal notion that on a person's death, their rights and obligations devolve on their successors by the natural course of succession. Medieval and early modern legal Latin developed the transitive sense "to cause to pass on, to transfer," and the word entered English from Middle French dévoluer / devolver in the 15th century. Gibbon applies this legal transfer sense to the Roman constitution: the Senate "appeared to possess the sovereign authority" — the verb "appeared" is deliberate, since by the imperial period the Senate's sovereignty was a legal fiction — "and devolved on the emperors all the executive powers of government." The rolling-down metaphor lets Gibbon describe the transfer without open conquest: power was not torn from the Senate but rolled down onto the emperors as if by its own weight. Register: DEVOLVED is formal, legal, and political. It belongs to constitutional history, administrative law, and political science, and it is equally at home in Gibbon's high Augustan prose and in modern news reports about devolution. Collocations include "devolved on/upon," "devolved to," "devolved powers," "devolved government," "devolved administration," "power devolved to local authorities." The word is not slang, not archaic, and not colloquial; its participle form carries a slightly stately, Latinate weight. Pronunciation is /dɪvˈɒlvd/ (di-VOLVD), with the stress on the second syllable.
