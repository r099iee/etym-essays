- c. 1300: the verb besegen enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman (be- + sege “siege”); besieging is expressed through verbs and participles (besieged) — the attackers who sit around a town.
- 14th–15th c.: the Hundred Years War and its chronicles (French sieges retold in English prose) make the encircling army a stock figure of narrative.
- 16th c.: the agent noun besieger is attested and immediately thrives in the plural — Shakespeare’s histories show the besiegers of Orléans and of Harfleur; epic diction (Spenser, Milton) adopts the word.
- 18th–19th c.: grand-narrative history — Gibbon on the besiegers of Constantinople (1453), Scott, Macaulay — neutral, dignified tone; first figurative uses (besiegers of a heart, of fortune).
- 20th–21st c.: journalistic and historical; mildly ominous colouring from modern siege wars (Leningrad, Sarajevo); the plural remains the rule, the singular rare; transferred uses (besiegers of privacy, of reputations, of markets) keep the military image.
