• Mob: Similar because both refer to disorderly, potentially violent crowds; differs because mob (short for Latin mobile vulgus, "the fickle commonalty") emphasizes sudden, active, and dangerous kinetic fury and violence, whereas rabble emphasizes disorder, low social status, noise, and worthlessness.
  • Riffraff: Similar because both are contemptuous terms for disreputable, low-class people; differs because riffraff (from Old French rif et raf, "sweepings") focuses on moral worthlessness, petty criminality, and trash-like social refuse, while rabble emphasizes crowd volume and clamor.
  • Canaille: Similar because both denote the lowest stratum of the populace; differs because canaille is a formal French loan-word (literally derived from Italian cane, "dog/pack of dogs"), carrying an explicit aristocratic, continental register.
  • Multitude: Similar because both refer to a massive gathering of the general population; differs because multitude is neutral, biblically dignified, or grandly demographic, lacking the inherently derogatory and chaotic venom of rabble.
  • Populace: Similar because both describe the common people of a region; differs because populace is a neutral demographic, sociological, or political term for the entire body of citizens.
  • Horde: Similar because both denote vast, unorganized groups; differs because horde (originally from Turkic orda, "camp/army") emphasizes immense numbers, aggressive military swarming, and nomadic invasion.
  • Scum: Similar because both are intensely disparaging class insults; differs because scum is a visceral moral metaphor for filthy surface residue, denoting the most vile and untrustworthy individuals.
  • Ragtag: Similar because both imply disorganization and lack of pedigree; differs because ragtag is an adjective describing a motley, improvised, and poorly equipped assembly.
  • Herd: Similar because both dehumanize a crowd into animals; differs because herd emphasizes dumb, passive, unthinking obedience and conformity rather than noisy agitation.
  • Crowd: Similar because both denote a gathering of people; differs because crowd is the entirely neutral, basic descriptive term with zero intrinsic connotation of chaos or class contempt.
