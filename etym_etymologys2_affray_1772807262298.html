PIE *priH- "to love, to be kind to, to be pleased" (reconstructed; the e-grade underlies Sanskrit prīyáte "is pleased," Gothic frijōn "to love") → Proto-Germanic *friþuz "peace, protection, inviolability" (reconstructed, a tu-stem; cf. the parallel *frijōndz "friend," literally "one who is loved") → Gallo-Romance borrowing of Germanic *friþu + Latin ex- "out of" → Vulgar Latin *exfridāre "to take (someone) out of peace, to disturb, to startle" (reconstructed, 5th–8th c. AD) → Old French esfreer / effreer "to frighten, to disturb" (attested 11th–12th c.); nouns effrei / esfroi / affrai "alarm, disturbance, terror" (12th c.) → Anglo-Norman / Old French affrai "a disturbance, a public commotion" and affrayer "to frighten, to disturb the peace" (13th–14th c.) → Middle English affray (noun) "fright, alarm; a public disturbance or brawl" (14th c.; also the verb "to frighten," whence afraid) → Modern English AFFRAY (a fight in a public place disturbing the peace; legal sense).

Debated / nuanced note: the Vulgar Latin stage *exfridāre is reconstructed, not directly attested; the exact shape of the Germanic element borrowed into Gallo-Romance (the "peace" word) is inferred from the Old French outcomes. The modern French effroi "terror, dread" preserves the "fright" sense on the same base. No letter labels are used; every stage is a real attested or explicitly reconstructed form.
