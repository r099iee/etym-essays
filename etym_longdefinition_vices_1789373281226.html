Vices is the plural noun designating habitual moral failings, wicked practices, corrupting personal defects, or entrenched behaviors that violate ethical principles, virtue, and social order. In ethical philosophy and psychological analysis, a vice is not an isolated, accidental transgression or momentary lapse in judgment; rather, it represents a deeply rooted disposition or acquired character trait that consistently inclines an individual toward harmful, self-destructive, or unjust actions. To speak of an individual's vices is to identify the chronic flaws and dark inclinations woven into their behavioral fabric.

Etymologically derived from the Latin neuter noun vitium ("fault, defect, physical blemish, moral failing, crime"), the word initially signified a concrete, tangible flaw or imperfection—such as a crack in a marble pillar, a defect in an animal's leg, or an error in ritual augury. Over centuries of Roman jurisprudence and Stoic moral philosophy, this physical blemish was internalized as an ethical deformity of the soul. When transmitted into Old French and Middle English, vice stood in direct binary opposition to virtue (Latin virtus, "moral excellence, courage, strength"), becoming one of the fundamental organizing categories of Western moral and theological discourse.

The term displays rich and diverse polysemy across moral, legal, psychological, veterinary, material, and theatrical domains:

  • Moral Philosophy and Theology (Primary Ethical Sense): In classical ethics and Christian doctrine, vices are habituated moral corruptions that distort the human will. Aristotle, in his Nicomachean Ethics, conceptualized vice as the departure from the "golden mean": for every virtue, there exist two opposing vices of excess and deficiency (for example, courage is flanked by the vice of cowardice on one side and rashness on the other). In Christian theology, the Seven Deadly (or Cardinal) Vices—pride (superbia), greed (avaritia), lust (luxuria), envy (invidia), gluttony (gula), wrath (ira), and sloth (acedia)—are identified as the root corruptions from which all individual sins germinate. In Marcel Proust's literary philosophy (as highlighted in his critique of Sainte-Beuve), an author's personal vices, social habits, and domestic failings belong strictly to the superficial, worldly self and provide no meaningful insight into the deeper, creative self that writes a masterpiece.
  • Criminology, Law, and Law Enforcement: In criminal jurisprudence and municipal policing, vice designates illegal or regulated commercial activities involving moral taboo, self-indulgence, or exploitation—most notably prostitution, illicit narcotics distribution, illegal gambling, and human trafficking. Municipal police departments maintain specialized "vice squads" (or vice divisions) tasked specifically with investigating, infiltrating, and suppressing these underground economies.
  • Colloquial Indulgences and Minor Guilty Pleasures: In contemporary vernacular and psychological self-reflection, vices is frequently softened through affectionate irony to describe minor, non-criminal guilty pleasures, mild addictions, or unhealthy habits that a person enjoys despite knowing they are bad for their health or discipline. Smoking cigarettes, an insatiable craving for dark chocolate, binge-watching television dramas, or spending too much money on vintage wine are routinely referred to as "my little vices."
  • Veterinary Science and Equestrian Husbandry: In veterinary medicine and horse training, a vice (specifically a stable vice) is an abnormal, repetitive, and destructive stereotypic behavior exhibited by domesticated animals, particularly horses confined to stalls. Equine vices include crib-biting (gripping a wooden fence with incisors and swallowing air), wind-sucking, stall-walking, weaving (rhythmic swaying from foot to foot), and chronic kicking or biting. These vices arise from boredom, isolation, or chronic stress and can severely degrade the animal's physical health, digestion, and commercial value.
  • Material Science and Structural Integrity (Archaic / Technical): In metallurgy, timber grading, and architectural craftsmanship, vice historically retained its classical Latin meaning of a physical defect, knot, fissure, or blemish that compromises the strength or utility of a material. A piece of timber marred by internal rot or an iron casting containing hidden air pockets was described as having a vice.
  • Medieval and Renaissance Theatrical Personification: In 15th- and 16th-century English morality plays, the Vice was a wildly popular stock character who personified evil, sin, and mischievous temptation. Leaping about the stage with a wooden dagger, cracking bawdy jokes, and directly mocking the audience, the Vice functioned as the comic yet sinister tempter who lured the protagonist away from virtue. This theatrical archetype directly shaped Renaissance drama, serving as the dramatic ancestor of Shakespeare's most mesmerizing villains, including Richard III and Iago in Othello.

The semantic bridge connecting these varied senses is the foundational concept of deviation from an ideal state of soundness: whether an ethical soul crippled by corrupt habits, a horse displaying neurotic stall vices, or a board weakened by a structural knot, vices consistently signify deep-seated imperfections that mar the integrity of the whole.
