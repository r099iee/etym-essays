prismatic (adjective) — /pɹɪsmˈatɪk/ — (1) of, relating to, resembling, or shaped like a prism; (2) in optics, produced by or involving the refraction of light through a prism; (3) by extension, displaying or having the rainbow of colours into which white light is split — many-coloured, rainbow-hued, iridescent; (4) in mineralogy, of a crystal form having prism-shaped faces with parallel sides.
  • Primary senses. In geometry and optics the adjective stays close to its noun: a “prismatic surface,” a “prismatic refraction,” the “prismatic colours” of the spectrum. Newton’s experiments of 1666–1704 fixed the sense of “rainbow-coloured”: white light passed through a triangular glass prism is split into its constituent hues, and “prismatic colours” became the scientific name for the spectral band.
  • Extended and figurative senses. The rainbow sense generalised in the 19th–20th centuries: anything “prismatic” is dazzlingly many-coloured, showing a play of separated hues — “prismatic light,” “a prismatic landscape,” “prismatic prose.” A figurative nuance of brilliance and separation underlies it: the world seen “through a prism” is analysed, dissected, shown in its constituent colours. The word can therefore mean both literally spectral and metaphorically kaleidoscopic.
  • Technical senses. In mineralogy, “prismatic” describes crystal forms with parallel faces (a prismatic habit); in engineering and optics, “prismatic” names lenses, compasses (the prismatic compass), and other instruments using prisms; in chemistry and physics, “prismatic” appears in the names of instruments that split or analyse light.
  • Semantic bridge. Greek prisma meant “a sawn block” — a solid with parallel faces cut from a larger mass — which Euclid adopted for his geometric solid; the optical prism is a glass triangle cut (sawn) from a block; and “prismatic colours” are colours “sawn apart” from white light. The bridge from carpentry to geometry to optics to ordinary rainbow-vividness is one of the most elegant in English.
mantle (noun, also verb) — /mˈantəl/ — (1) a loose, sleeveless cloak or shawl worn over the shoulders, historically the everyday and ceremonial outer garment; (2) by extension, any enveloping covering that wraps or conceals, from a layer of snow to the earth’s rocky shell; (3) in geology, the thick layer of the earth between the crust and the core; (4) figuratively, the “mantle” of an office or authority — the responsibility or role taken up from a predecessor.
  • Primary sense — the garment. The mantle is the ancient cloak: a broad, loose covering of cloth over the shoulders, worn by kings, priests, scholars, and travellers. Its breadth made it the natural metaphor for anything that wraps and hides — and for the robe of office, so that “to take up the mantle” of a role means to assume its authority.
  • Secondary senses — enveloping layers. Science and poetry widened the word to any wrapping layer: geologists’ “mantle” (the earth’s great rock shell beneath the crust), the incandescent gas-lamp “mantle” (the mesh bag that glows over a flame), the ornithologists’ feathery “mantle,” and poets’ “mantle of snow,” “mantle of night,” “mantle of silence.” The bridging idea is always the same: a covering that wraps, protects, conceals, or dignifies.
  • Figurative and leadership sense. From the garment of office came the idiom of succession: Elijah’s mantle passed to Elisha; “to assume the mantle of leadership” is to inherit a role’s responsibilities. This is now one of the most common modern uses, entirely metaphorised but still carrying the dignity of cloth.
  • Semantic bridge. Latin mantellum, “a cloak, a covering,” moved from the physical garment (Old English mentel, Old French mantel) to any enveloping layer (geological mantle, gas-mantle) to the abstract mantle of authority. Each extension keeps the image of something broad spread over something beneath.
In the card’s passage — “the Excellent Prismatic Spray, Phandaal’s Mantle of Stealth” — both words do their modern jobs at once: the Spray promises rainbow-refracted, dissected colour (prismatic), and the Mantle promises a covering that conceals the wearer (mantle).
