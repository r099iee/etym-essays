The history of pelting is the history of an English verb of uncertain birth that became the perfect meteorological metaphor for assault. Unlike words with a clean Latin pedigree, pelt appears suddenly in the early 16th century, and scholars have been arguing about its parentage ever since. Its present-participial form pelting inherits all of that mystery and then adds a second history: how a word for throwing stones became the standard Dickensian image for merciless rain.

The dominant textbook story derives pelt from pellet, a small ball or stone used as a missile. Pellet itself is secure: Old French pelote, pelete, from Vulgar Latin *pilotta, diminutive of Latin pila, a ball. In Middle English pellet meant a stone ball, a shot, a projectile. The theory holds that to pelt was originally to assail with pellets, by a natural shortening or back-formation, first recorded around 1500 in the sense to strike with missiles. The semantic bridge is transparent: pellets are thrown things; pelting is the act of throwing them repeatedly. Phonologically the loss of the diminutive ending is plausible in rapid speech and is paralleled by other English shortenings.

A rival view treats pelt as imitative or as a variant of older dialect verbs meaning to drive or thrust, possibly ultimately connected to Latin pellere, to drive, push, expel, from PIE *pel-, to drive or strike. Supporters point to the sense to hurry, rush, which survives in to pelt along, to pelt down the street, suggesting an older sense of driving forward rather than throwing. Others note Middle English pelten, pulten, to thrust, and the echoic quality of pelt, pelt-pelt, like patter and batter, well suited to rain noise. In this reading the resemblance to pellet is folk-etymological reinforcement rather than true parentage. The Oxford English Dictionary leaves the origin as uncertain, which is itself the honest conclusion: the pellet hypothesis is plausible and widely repeated, but not proven.

  • 1500–1600: assault with missiles. Earliest attestations mean to strike with stones, mud, insults. Shakespeare has pelting, petty, pelting villages, where pelting already shades toward paltry, and crowds pelt offenders, criminals are pelted with rotten eggs. The core is repeated blows from many small impacts.
  • 1600–1750: scolding and rushing. By extension to pelt with words, to pelt with questions, reproaches. Parallel intransitive sense: to pelt along, to go at full speed, as if driven. Both keep the idea of uninterrupted force.
  • 1750–1900: weather sense fixed. Pelting rain, pelting hail, pelting storm become stock phrases. The metaphor is exact: raindrops are countless small missiles striking skin, roofs, windows. Dickens uses it in A Christmas Carol to deny Scrooge even the pity granted to bad weather: wind, snow and pelting rain are relentless but honest, while Scrooge is colder still.
  • Sound change: No great vowel shift drama here; pelt keeps a short e throughout. The -ing suffix is Old English -ung, -ing, forming verbal nouns and participles, fully productive in Modern English. Pronunciation settles as /pɛltɪŋ/ with clear t release.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare, Coriolanus and Measure for Measure; Defoe on pelting storms at sea; Dickens, A Christmas Carol opening; 19th-century newspapers full of pelting showers. Each use reinforces the moral overtone: nature pelts without malice, men pelt with intent.

In contemporary English pelting survives chiefly as a modifier of precipitation and as a verb of bombardment. We still say pelted with stones, pelted with emails, pelted with criticism, preserving the 16th-century military image in digital dress. As an adjective before rain, hail, sleet it signals more than heaviness: it signals hostility, sting, inability to plead for pause, exactly the Dickens sense of rain less open to entreaty. The word thus travelled from doubtful origins through human violence to meteorology and back to metaphor, and its very uncertainty fits its meaning: a shower of small blows whose source you cannot quite trace.
