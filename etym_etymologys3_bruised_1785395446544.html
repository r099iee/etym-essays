FROM A VIOLENT ROOT TO A HOUSEHOLD MARK

The story of bruised begins with a violent root. The Indo-European base *bhreus- carried the sense "to break, shatter, crack" — the raw, explosive sound and action of something brittle giving way — and from it the Germanic peoples formed the verb *brusjanan, "to crush, to pound", the action of grinding something to fragments under repeated pressure. Old English inherited this as brȳsan, which could still mean "to crush" in the fullest sense: a pounder brȳsde herbs in a mortar, a smith brȳsde metal beneath the hammer. The word described the deliberate, rhythmic destruction of a thing's structure — not yet the accidental mark on a shin.

THE NARROWING TO THE SKIN

The narrowing to a flesh injury happened at the meeting of two languages. In Middle English the old Germanic verb lived on as brusen ("to bruise, crush"), recorded around 1300; and at about the same time the root entered the vocabulary again by a different door. Old French bruisier "to break, to shatter" was itself a Germanic loanword in Romance — carried south by the Franks — and it reinforced the English verb, helping to fix its spelling and its sense. Around 1300 the specifically physical sense of the English word appeared: to injure the flesh by a blunt impact that ruptures the vessels beneath the skin while leaving the surface whole — the mark that darkens slowly from red to blue to purple-green. From this concrete core the word has never really moved, though it has widened in three directions:

  • As a noun, bruise (17th century) named the mottled mark itself, the visible evidence of the invisible hurt.
  • As a participle, bruised extended to fruit and food — a bruised apple is a fruit with a soft discoloured patch where it has been knocked — the same hidden damage transferred from flesh to plant tissue.
  • From the 19th century the participle and the noun reached for the inner life: one could be bruised in spirit, carry bruised pride, feel emotionally bruised — hurt, but not broken, lighter than wounded.

SOUND SHIFTS AND FORMS

  • The Old English verb brȳsan had the long vowel ȳ (from Germanic *ū); in Middle English this shortened and lowered as brusen.
  • The modern spelling bruise, with -ui-, reflects the French-influenced spelling bruiser, so the written form tells the story of the double transmission — Germanic root, Romance spelling.
  • The regular past tense and past participle bruised is recorded from the 14th century and has been stable ever since.

LITERARY AND CULTURAL ATTESTATIONS

Bruise is a word of wounds, not of hospitals: it appears in Middle English verse, in the Authorised Version of the Bible (where the serpent's head is promised to be "bruised"), in Shakespeare and Milton, always in the homely physical sense. By the 17th century the noun was firmly established ("a bruise on the shin"), and in the 19th century the figurative sense entered the emotional vocabulary — Dickens can write of a character's "bruised" feelings. In modern English the word is firmly everyday and domestic: a kitchen word and a nursery word ("black and blue", "a bruised apple"), whose homeliness is exactly its point even in epic contexts — as in The Hobbit, where dwarves are subject to bruises like anyone else. The same ancient root travelled separately through Romance: French briser "to break" and its derivative débris, "broken fragments", and brisure, "a break, a joint", all descend from the Germanic breaking-word, so the scattered fragments of the root lie across half of Europe.

SOCIOCULTURAL NOTE

Bruise has never lost the note of the hidden and the inward: unlike a cut, which declares itself, a bruise is private, surfacing slowly and fading slowly. That is why it became the natural word for damage to pride and spirit — a mark you carry inside, unseen by others, that aches for days. Tolkien's use of "bruised and damaged legs and feet" leans on exactly this: the travellers are not spectacularly wounded, just invisibly battered, and the word asks the reader to feel the dull ache rather than admire the scar.

SUMMARY

From a PIE root meaning "to break, shatter", through a Germanic verb of crushing and pounding, narrowed in Middle English to the blunt blow that marks the flesh beneath unbroken skin, and extended from fruit to feelings, bruised carries four thousand years of fracture in a word as homely as a shin-knock.
