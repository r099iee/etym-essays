Chain for UNCOUTH
1. Proto-Indo-European *gneh₃-, 'to know' — reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE (one of the most productive roots in the family; cognates: Sanskrit jānāti 'he knows,' Greek γιγνώσκω gignṓskō 'to know,' Latin (g)nōscere 'to come to know')
2. Proto-Germanic *kunnaną, 'to know, to know how' — c. 500 BCE–500 CE (reconstructed; the PIE *g became Proto-Germanic *k by Grimm's Law)
3. Old English cunnan, 'to know, to know how' — 8th–11th c. CE (the ancestor of the modern modal verb can)
4. Old English cūþ, 'known, familiar, well-known' — 8th–11th c. CE (the past participle of cunnan; also the source of kith, in 'kith and kin,' via the abstract cȳþþ, 'acquaintance, knowledge')
5. Old English uncūþ, 'unknown, unfamiliar, strange' — 8th–11th c. CE (the negative prefix un- + cūþ)
6. Middle English uncouth, 'unknown, strange, unfamiliar' — 12th–14th c. CE
7. Early Modern English uncouth, 'rude, uncultured, unrefined' — 15th–17th c. CE (the social sense gradually replaces the epistemological one)
8. Modern English uncouth, 'lacking good manners, refinement, or grace' — present day

Note: English later coined the back-formation couth, 'refined, cultured,' in 1896, extracted from uncouth — an ironic reversal, since the negative once contained the positive: what is 'couth' is only 'known.' The semantic migration (known → unknown → strange → foreign → unpolished → rude) is one of the best-documented examples of a word moving from epistemology to etiquette.
