octogenarian — the mathematics of a long life. The numeral 'eight' goes back to Indo-European *oḱtṓ, a word so stable that it has survived almost unchanged across the whole family: Sanskrit aṣṭá, Greek oktṓ, Latin octō, and Proto-Germanic *ahtō, the ancestor of Old English eahta and modern English eight. Unlike the numerals 'one' and 'two', which have diversified, 'eight' has proved extraordinarily conservative — it is one of the oldest intact sounds in the language. The second component of the word is the Indo-European decimal system itself. Latin formed octōgintā, 'eighty', by joining octō to the ten-fold suffix -gintā, a collateral form of *déḱm̥, 'ten' — the root of Sanskrit dáśa, Hindi das, Greek déka, Latin decem, and English ten. The suffix is the same one that gives the series vīgintī, trīgintā, quadrāgintā, and so on up to nōnāgintā — 'eighty' was, etymologically, 'eight-times-ten', the arithmetic of counting itself written into the word. From octōgintā, Latin built the distributive numeral octōgēnī, 'eighty each' — a form used to count people or things taken in batches of eighty — and from it the adjective octōgēnārius, 'belonging to a person of eighty'. English borrowed the word directly from Latin in the seventeenth century, adapting the ending to -ian on the pattern already established by sexagenarian and septuagenarian. It is worth insisting on the directness of that borrowing: octogenarian has no French or Germanic intermediate, and the Latin stem arrives in English almost untouched, a learned word adopted by scholars and clerks rather than grown in the mouths of the people. The word's career is the career of bookkeeping applied to human life. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as censuses, insurance tables and actuarial science took shape, the age-classifications became standard demographic instruments. Obituaries and tributes then dignified them: 'the grand old octogenarian' became a Victorian formula of respect, turning a statistical category into a monument of survival. The register is dual — coldly statistical on the census page, warmly eulogistic in the tribute — and octogenarian is the only word in the series that holds both tones comfortably, because eighty is the age at which mere survival begins to look like achievement. In Peake's castle all that survives of Nettel is that arithmetic. He is introduced not by character but by decade, his eighty years counted like the steps of a tower, and the ceremony he remembers is older than his count. The word's genealogy is thus a genealogy of counting itself: eight, ten, eighty, each carved from the same Indo-European numerals that still count the world today.
armoury — from the joint to the jousting-hall. Everything in this family begins with the Indo-European root *h₂er-, 'to fit or join together'. In Sanskrit the root produced ṛtá, the cosmic order — that which is 'fitted' and therefore right — and artha, 'purpose, meaning', the fitting aim of action, which survives in Hindi as arth, 'meaning'. Greek built from it the verb ararískō, 'to fit', and the noun harmónia, 'a joint', which became English harmony — agreement imagined as the perfect fitting of parts. Latin exploited the root with extraordinary fertility: artus, 'a joint'; ars, 'skill, craft' — the 'fittedness' of hand to material — which gave English art; and arma, 'tools, gear, weapons', the neuter plural that named everything 'fitted' for use, in peace and war alike. The very etymology of arma thus imagines weapons not as instruments of violence but as things fitted, joined, made ready — a readiness that is the soul of the word armoury. The building word came next. From arma, Latin formed armāre, 'to equip', and from the equipping-room the Romance languages built their storehouses: Late Latin *armaria, 'a store of equipment', produced Old French armerie and Anglo-Norman armorie. Middle English took the word in the fourteenth century as armerie or armorie, 'a storehouse for arms, an arsenal'. The -our- spelling is the British retention of the French form, preserved by the same conservatism that keeps colour and honour; the American armory dropped the 'u' under nineteenth-century spelling reform. Meanwhile, a second sense had flowered: because coats of arms were the 'arms' — the distinguishing fitted equipment — of a noble house, 'armoury' in the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries also named the science of heraldic bearings, the systematic description of shields and crests. The College of Arms was, in that old phrase, the armoury of the nobility: a storehouse not of steel but of identity. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word's centre of gravity shifted. As professional armies replaced castle stores, the armoury became an institutional depot, and in American English the armory added a civic sense — the drill hall and assembly building of the National Guard. The figurative extension had meanwhile been active all along: an 'armoury of arguments', of resources, of rhetorical devices — any store held ready for use. The whole career runs from 'fitted things' to 'a fitted room' to 'a stock of ready means'. Peake's phrase 'the rusting armoury' turns that career against itself: a store of readiness that is never drawn upon, arms corroding because they are never used. The word's ancient sense of being fitted, joined and ready is betrayed by the rust, exactly as Nettel's eight decades of counted memory are betrayed by the ceremony's indifference to him. The armoury and the octogenarian are the same monument twice over: readiness and age, both kept, both rusting, both outliving their use.
Together — the two monuments of neglect.
  • Parallel arithmetic: octogenarian counts decades; armoury stores arms; both are measures of how long the rite has endured.
  • Parallel decay: Nettel's age is only counted, never honoured; the armoury's arms are only stored, never drawn — readiness and memory corrode together.
  • Etymological contrast: octogenarian is a pure Latin numeral-borrowing, the language of the census; armoury is a French-rooted craft-word, the language of the workshop; Peake pairs the bookkeeper and the smith to build a single image of the ceremony's cold eternity.
  • Register: both words are formal and slightly archaic in flavour, lending the prose a ceremonial weight that matches the unchanging rite they describe.
