Derivation chain for RAFTERS (single root word; reconstructed stages are labelled as such, attested stages carry dates):

1. Proto-Indo-European *rep- (reconstructed, uncertain) — traditionally glossed "stake, beam"; the primitive sense is the hewn pole, the raw timber of early shelter-building. Handbooks caution that the exact reconstruction is debated: some compare *rebh- "to cover, roof" for the roofing senses, and the Germanic base below is marked by the OED as of uncertain or disputed origin.

2. Proto-Germanic *raftra- / *rafta- (reconstructed) — "beam, rafter", a pole-hewn roof timber; the etymon of the whole Germanic raft-family. Cognates include Old Norse raptr "rafter; long beam or log" (sagas, 9th–13th c.) and Middle Dutch raefter / rafter "rafter", and, by semantic transfer, the English float-word raft (via Middle Dutch rafte "beam").

3. Old English raefter / refter — "rafter, roof-beam", attested by the 10th century in Anglo-Saxon building records; the direct Old English member of the family.

4. Middle English rafter — "rafter", 13th–15th centuries; the word passes into the modern period with the same form and meaning, a roof-beam on the slope.

5. Modern English rafter — the sloped roof-timber; the card's exact inflected form is the plural RAFTERS.

Note on the debate: the OED records the Germanic base as of disputed origin; the equation with PIE *rep- is traditional rather than certain, and no Italic or Hellenic reflex directly feeds the English word (Greek erepho "to roof", from a different root, is a parallel formation only). English raft (the float) is a sibling of the same Germanic beam-word, not a separate source.
