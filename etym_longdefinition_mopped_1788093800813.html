MOPPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb mop — most basically, 'wiped or cleaned with a mop, or with an absorbent cloth,' and by extension 'wiped away (moisture) from a surface, especially from one's face or brow.' The flashcard's line — 'He _____ his face with a red silk handkerchief' — shows the sweat-wiping, 'wipe one's brow' sense, a thoroughly ordinary use of the verb since the fifteenth century. But the verb's territory is wider than floors and foreheads.

1. Core senses (verb).

  • To clean a surface with a mop: 'mopped the kitchen floor' — the domestic, industrial, nautical and janitorial sense; the object of the verb is the surface cleaned.
  • To wipe moisture from body parts: 'mopped his face / brow / forehead' — the object is the person or the part wiped, and by transferred object the moisture itself: 'mopped the sweat from his brow.' This sense is as old as the verb and is the one in the source line.
  • To wipe up / absorb: 'mopped up the spilled milk' — the liquid as object.

2. The phrasal verb 'mop up.'

  • Literal: to wipe away liquid.
  • Military (idiom, twentieth century): to eliminate remaining pockets of resistance after the main fighting — 'mopping-up operations' became standard dispatch language in both world wars ('the last enemy pockets were mopped up').
  • Economic / financial (idiom): to absorb surplus — central banks 'mop up excess liquidity,' markets 'mop up' surplus supply. The bridge from wiping floors to draining reserves is the shared idea of removing what remains.
  • General: to finish off, dispose of, complete the remnants of any task ('mopped up the last details').

3. The noun and its satellite senses.

  • Mop, n. — the implement: a bundle of coarse yarn or cloth (now often a sponge or microfiber head) on a handle, for washing floors, decks and the like. The word is first attested in English in the fifteenth century.
  • Mop of hair: a thick, loose mass of hair like a mop-head — 'mop-headed,' and, in the twentieth century, 'mop-top' for the Beatles' haircut (a coinage of the 1960s).
  • Mopboard: the American word for a baseboard — the skirting board against which a mop runs when washing a floor.
  • Mop fair (obsolete regional sense): an old English hiring-fair in which servants stood with a mop or badge of their trade (the mop for household servants); the name survives in the mop fairs of the West Midlands.

4. The obsolete grimace sense. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries English also had to mop 'to make a wry face, grimace' — of uncertain origin, possibly imitative, possibly from Dutch moppen 'to pout' — with the rhyming pair 'mopping and mowing' for apish grimacing current in Elizabethan writing (Shakespeare's Caliban says his tormentors come like apes that 'mow and chatter,' The Tempest). This is a different 'mop': not the cloth verb, though its past tense is also 'mopped.' The sense is now obsolete, but it is why 'mopped and mowed' still surfaces in archaic pastiche.

5. Grammar and spelling. 'Mopped' is a perfectly regular weak past formed by doubling the final -p-: English doubles a single final consonant after a stressed short vowel before -ed/-ing (mop → mopped, hop → hopped, stop → stopped). The doubled spelling is what preserves the short vowel /ɒ/ in RP /mɒpt/. Nothing about the form is irregular; its interest is orthographic and lexical, not grammatical.

6. Register. 'Mopped' is unmarked, everyday English in all its active senses — domestic, nautical, military, financial — with no elevation and no vulgarity. In the flashcard's fantasy setting its homeliness is exactly the point: the high archaic world of the narrative is grounded by a man wiping his face with a borrowed silk handkerchief.
