Brooded is the past tense of the verb brood. Its oldest, literal meaning concerns birds: a bird “broods” when it sits on its eggs to keep them warm so that they hatch. Out of that homely image of a hen motionless over her nest grew the two great figurative senses of the word: (1) to sit absorbed and silent in troubled thought, nursing worry or resentment over a long time — “to brood over a grievance”; and (2) to hang over or dominate a scene in a threatening, oppressive way — “storm-clouds brooded over the mountains”, or, in Tolkien, “danger brooded in every rock”.

Primary senses

  • Of a bird — to sit on eggs: the literal, earliest sense; a brooding hen incubates her clutch by bodily warmth.
  • Of a person — to think moodily and at length: to dwell anxiously, gloomily, or resentfully on a single subject, shutting out distraction; this is the dominant modern sense.
  • Of an atmosphere or threat — to hang over oppressively: clouds, silence, danger, or fate “brooding” over a place implies a heavy, watchful, lowering presence.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Transitive, protective use: older literature uses “to brood one’s young” in the sense of sheltering and nurturing, the hen guarding her chicks.
  • Adjectival uses: “brooding” (of a person or look) = darkly thoughtful, melancholy, or ominously silent; “brooded” can also appear adjectivally in such compounds as “cloud-brooded”.
  • Of emotions: a “brooding resentment” or “brooding jealousy” is one fed silently over time, grown heavier and more dangerous.

Semantic bridge

  • The figurative senses all rest on one vivid picture: the hen who sits perfectly still over her eggs for days on end. That stillness — long, patient, immobile, turned inward — became the model for the human who sits wrapped in troubled thought, and for a sky that seems to press down on the land.
  • Because the hen’s sitting is also protective, a faint undertone of watchfulness and care survives in the word: to brood is not to panic or rage but to keep one’s trouble warm and close, almost as if nursing it.
  • In “danger brooded in every rock” the verb is used intransitively with an abstract subject — danger itself sits motionless, patient, and ominous in the landscape, exactly as a hen sits hidden in her nest.

Register and usage

  • Literary and standard rather than slang; the thinking-sense usually takes “over”, “on”, or “about” (“she brooded over the insult”), or stands alone (“he brooded all evening”).
  • The oppressive atmosphere-sense is common in fiction, especially in descriptions of weather, mountains, forests, and menace.
  • Synonyms exist but none fully replaces it: pondered is neutral and intellectual, moped is weakly self-pitying, ruminated is slightly formal and even medical — brooded alone carries the full weight of silent, prolonged, slightly angry gloom.

Grammatical notes

  • Weak verb: brood – brooded – brooded; the preterite is identical in spelling and pronunciation (/ˈbruː.dɪd/) to the past participle.
  • It may be transitive (“brooded his resentment”), intransitive (“he brooded in silence”), or followed by prepositions “over”, “on”, “about” for the object of the thought.
  • In Tolkien the bare intransitive use with an abstract subject (danger) is the most atmospheric of all: no object, no preposition — danger simply sat and brooded in the rocks.
