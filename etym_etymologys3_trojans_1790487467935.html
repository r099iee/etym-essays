Trojans is the English plural of Trojan, meaning originally a native or inhabitant of Troy (Troia, Ilios), the Bronze-Age city in north-west Anatolia besieged by the Achaeans in the Trojan War. The word's deep history begins outside Greek, in the Late Bronze Age archives of Hattusa, and its modern history ends in condoms, football teams, and computer viruses — a striking case of an ancient ethnonym becoming global metaphor.

Hittite cuneiform tablets of the 13th century BCE mention Wilusa and Taruisa as western Anatolian polities in treaty and letter contexts (the Alaksandu treaty, the Tawagalawa letter). Since the 1920s most Hittitologists (Forrer, Güterbock, Bryce) have equated Wilusa with Greek (W)ilios (Ilios, another name for Troy) and Taruisa with Troia, via regular sound correspondences: Hittite w- lost in Greek, -u- vocalism, and Luwiandigest. The equation remains the best hypothesis but is not proof: critics note geographic vagueness and the absence of the exact form Troia in Hittite. Greek itself offers no internal etymology — Troia, Troes, Tros (the eponymous king) form a closed circle with no verb or adjective to explain them — which Beekes takes as a sign of Pre-Greek substrate origin. Myth made Tros son of Erichthonius explain Troes, but linguistically this is back-formation: the people gave the ancestor, not vice versa.

  • Homeric ethnonym: In the Iliad, Troes (Trojans) and Dardanoi, Ilieis alternate for Priam's people. Homer treats them with remarkable sympathy: Hector, Andromache, Priam, Polydamas, Sarpedon and Glaucus (allies) are noble, pious, and doomed. The Achaeans kill them twenty-to-one in a boy's counting, but the poem honours their defence. Greek Troikos gave the adjective Trojan.
  • Latin appropriation: Romans claimed Trojan descent through Aeneas (Aeneid), turning Troiani from enemies into ancestors. Virgil's Troiani are pious exiles destined to found Rome. Hence Late Latin and medieval chronicles use Trojanus with pride: Franks, Britons (Brutus of Troy), and Turks all at times claimed Trojan origins. The ethnonym becomes a charter-myth.
  • Medieval to early-modern English: Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, Lydgate's Troy Book, Caxton's Recuyell, and Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida keep Trojans as chivalric pagans — brave but infidel. Early-modern English fixes spelling as Trojan / Trojans, pronounced with initial stress. The plural in Ruskin's sentence is purely Homeric: the mass of ordinary Trojan soldiers mown down by heroes.
  • Metaphoric explosion (19th–21st c.): Three modern metaphors dominate and must be distinguished. (1) Trojan Horse: the wooden horse of guile (Virgil, timeo Danaos), now any deceptive gift or infiltration — Trojan horse bill, Trojan horse malware. (2) Work like a Trojan: Victorian slang for tireless labour, from the idea that Trojans defended heroically against odds; Ruskin's whole kingship moral inverts this — his kings out-work Trojans. The phrase is first attested c. 1820s–1840s. (3) Trojan as brand and team: Trojan condoms (U.S., 1920s, implying reliability and virility), USC Trojans (athletic teams, 1910s), Troy weight, etc. Computer Trojan / Trojan horse virus (1970s–80s, Ken Thompson) fuses (1) and (3): software that looks benign but hides attack.
  • Phonetics: Greek Trōia with long o gives Latin Trōia, Trōiānus with penultimate stress; Old French Troien diphthongises; Middle English Troyan re-Latinised to Trojan in the 16th century under humanist spelling. Modern RP /ˈtrəʊdʒən/ shows regular palatalisation of -i- + vowel to -dʒ- and reduction to schwa. Plural adds regular -s /z/.

Thus Trojans runs from a debated Bronze-Age Anatolian toponym through Homeric ethnonym, Roman ancestor-name, medieval romance people, and finally global metaphor for deception, hard work, and commercial strength. In Ruskin's boyhood sentence it still holds its oldest sense: the brave eastern host slaughtered in heaps by Achaean kings.
