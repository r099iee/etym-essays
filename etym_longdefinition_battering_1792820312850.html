Battering is the verbal noun and present participle of batter: the action of striking something again and again with heavy blows, and by extension the damage those blows leave behind. Rain batters windows, waves batter cliffs, fists batter doors — wherever force falls repeatedly on one target, there is battering.

In the source sentence the word appears attributively in the great siege compound battering ram: the heavy beam, capped with iron, that armies swung or drove against gates and walls until they gave way. Bilbo's host bars the door against visitors even if they bring a battering ram — hyperbole for absolute refusal, since no respectable caller arrives with siege artillery. The joke needs the reader to feel the ram's ponderous menace and then to picture it deployed for a morning call.

Core meaning in detail: To batter is to beat continuously rather than once: a single blow hits, a shower of blows batters. The noun therefore carries duration and relentlessness in its bones — a battering is what remains when beating will not stop. Engineers speak of battering loads on bridges and hulls; doctors of battered faces; generals of battering trains (the siege artillery of old armies). The cooking batter — pancake batter, fish batter — comes down the very same road, for it is the mixture beaten in the bowl, and shares its etymology.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Repeated heavy striking (primary sense, from 14th c.): the battering of the door, hail battering the roof. The bridge from beat is iteration: battering is beating that refuses to stop.
  • Siege assault, especially with the ram (historical military sense): the battering of the walls, a battering train before the fortress. The bridge is scale: the ram concentrates the repeated blows of a hundred men into one iron-capped rhythm.
  • Sustained natural assault (meteorological sense): gales battering the coast, surf battering the pier. The bridge personifies the elements as besiegers, with cliffs as city walls and storms as artillery.
  • A heavy defeat or sustained criticism (figurative journalistic sense): the team took a battering, the minister took a battering on the morning shows. The bridge carries the bruises from flesh to reputation: polls and headlines do to standing what fists do to doors.
  • Domestic and criminal violence (modern social sense): wife-battering, baby-battering — now usually replaced by battering compounded or by abuse, but current from the 1970s. The bridge is grim and direct: the home becomes the besieged place, and the word's relentlessness names exactly what makes such violence distinct.
  • A sporting onslaught (colloquial, especially cricket): the batsman gave the bowling a battering. The bridge returns the word to play: runs rain down like ram-blows, and the fielding side stands where the city wall stood.

Register and connotation: battering is visceral — noise, impact, and endurance in one package. Applied to weather it awes, to armies it terrifies, to reputations it gloats, to victims it grieves. Few words travel so far while keeping their bruises so visible: whether the ram is iron-capped oak or a breakfast-show interview, the reader hears the same repeated thud. Tolkien, lover of siege-craft from the Gondolin tales onward, deploys the ram as the summit of unwelcome knocking.
