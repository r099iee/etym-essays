- Late 14th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French and Latin *defectus*; initially used to denote a complete lack, deficiency, or absence of an essential quality, resource, or natural faculty (e.g., "defect of sight").
- 15th to 16th Century - Broadened from total absence to an active fault, blemish, physical deformity, or moral weakness in human character and craftsmanship.
- Late 16th to 17th Century - Verbal usage emerges and bifurcates; *to defect* comes to mean deserting one's allegiance, country, religion, or military post (apostatizing), while the noun settles firmly as a flaw in legal instruments, arguments, and material goods.
- 18th to 19th Century - Institutionalized in jurisprudence, political science, and philosophy (e.g., the proverbial French translation "the defect of a virtue," *le défaut d'une vertu*), highlighting how excessive adherence to a positive quality yields an unintended flaw.
- 20th Century - Extensively developed in industrial manufacturing (quality control, patent law, "latent defect"), solid-state physics (crystal lattice defects: Frenkel, Schottky, dislocations), and Cold War espionage (intelligence defectors).
- 21st Century - Central to software engineering (bug tracking, code defects), semiconductor fabrication, and materials science, designating any failure of a system to meet designated specifications.
