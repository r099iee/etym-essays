• dress: Similar because both are garments worn by women; differs because “dress” covers all one-piece female garments from casual to formal, while “gown” is reserved for the long, formal or ceremonial end of the range.
  • frock: Similar because both are woman’s garments (and “frock coat” for men); differs because “frock” is now quaint or childish — a little girl’s dress, a smock — and lacks the ceremonial gravity of “gown”; historically the two overlapped, then diverged in register.
  • robe: Similar because both are long loose garments of ceremony; differs because “robe” foregrounds the ceremonial and religious (robes of state, choir robes, judge’s robes) and is rarely used in modern English for a woman’s fashion dress.
  • nightgown: Similar because it is a compound of “gown”; differs because it names the specific bedtime garment, and in ordinary speech is shortened to “nightie” — while plain “gown” has moved up-market to formal and institutional dress.
  • smock: Similar because both are loose coverings; differs because a smock is a simple, utilitarian or working garment (a painter’s smock, a woman’s overall), at the opposite social pole from the gown.
  • kirtle: Similar because both were medieval long garments; differs because “kirtle” is extinct outside historical fiction and named a tunic or gown worn under the outer robe — the gown’s companion rather than its rival.
  • habit: Similar because both are institutional garments; differs because “habit” is specifically the religious or equestrian uniform (nun’s habit, riding habit), while “gown” covers far broader ground of office and fashion.
  • caftan: Similar because both are long loose garments; differs because the caftan is the Middle Eastern and North African robe adopted by western fashion as exotic leisurewear, without the western ceremonial history of the gown.
  • chemise: Similar because both are simple under- or nightwear; differs because the chemise is the close-fitting undergarment or shift worn beneath the gown — older, simpler and never formal.
  • mantua: Similar because it was the fashionable woman’s gown of the eighteenth century, the gown’s ancestor; differs because “mantua” names a specific historical cut and era (named from the Italian city via its fabric), obsolete since the 1780s in living usage.
