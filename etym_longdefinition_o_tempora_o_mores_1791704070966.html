O tempora, O mores (pronounced /oʊ ˈtem.pər.ə oʊ ˈmɔː.reɪz/ or /oʊ ˈtem.pɒ.rə oʊ ˈmɔː.riːz/) is a world-renowned classical Latin exclamation meaning literally "Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!" (often translated into English as "Oh, what times! Oh, what morals!"). Originally voiced by the Roman statesman, orator, and philosopher Marcus Tullius Cicero, the phrase has resonated across two millennia as the quintessential rhetorical apostrophe to express deep moral outrage, sorrowful exasperation, or satirical lamentation over the corruption of public life, the decay of traditional standards, and the general decline of social, political, or intellectual decency.

The expressive genius and enduring vitality of the phrase reside in its sophisticated rhetorical architecture and semantic dualism:

  • The Interjection *O*: The opening particle O is the classical exclamatory vocative/accusative interjection, expressing spontaneous affective distress, elevated rhetorical passion, and an appeal to cosmic justice. It lifts the utterance out of dry legalistic analysis into high oratorical tragedy.
  • The Diptych of *Tempora* and *Mores*: The phrase couples two distinct but intimately interdependent realities:
    • Tempora (plural of tempus): Represents the objective, macroscopic external world—the historical epoch, the political atmosphere, the institutional environment, and the current state of public affairs.
    • Mores (plural of mos): Represents the subjective, internal moral fabric—the character, ethical habits, civic virtues, and cultural decencies of the citizenry.
    By pairing them under a parallel exclamatory structure, the speaker diagnoses a catastrophic reciprocal degeneration: corrupt times undermine and pervert moral character, while debased morals in turn destroy institutional order and produce dark, corrupt times.
  • The Historical Origin (Cicero vs. Catiline, 63 BCE): Cicero uttered this thunderous exclamation on 8 November 63 BCE in the Temple of Jupiter Stator before a tense Roman Senate during his First Oration against Catiline (In Catilinam I). Confronted with the traitorous aristocrat Lucius Sergius Catilina—who had plotted to assassinate elected consuls, burn Rome to the ground, and overthrow the Republic—Cicero marveled in despair that despite the Senate possessing full knowledge of the conspiracy, Catiline brazenly sat in the Senate chamber among the very men he intended to slaughter: "O tempora, O mores! Senatus haec intellegit, consul videt; hic tamen vivit. Vivit? Immo vero etiam in senatum venit!" ("Oh, the times! Oh, the customs! The Senate knows these things, the consul sees them; yet this man lives. Lives? Nay, he even enters the Senate!").
  • Literary Context of the Source Passage: In the provided text, the author—a veteran professor of English reflecting on the controversies of his discipline, the rise of post-structuralist "theory," and the alleged decline of "good taste"—wryly alludes to Cicero: "(He is me, of course—or I, I suppose. O grammar! O tempora, O mores! as my friend Tully once remarked.)" The reference to "my friend Tully" is the traditional Anglophone humanist name for Marcus Tullius Cicero (commonly called "Tully" by Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton, and 18th-century English essayists). The author playfully juxtaposes a trivial lament over grammatical correctness with Tully's grand historical lament over national treason, capturing the quintessential self-aware irony of educated English prose.

In contemporary English discourse, O tempora, O mores functions across two opposite stylistic poles:

  • Earnest Moral & Political Jeremiad: Deployed in serious editorial writing, philosophical critique, and legal oratory to convey genuine grief and moral indignation over systemic corruption, brazen institutional deceit, or the collapse of democratic norms.
  • Ironic, Mock-Heroic Self-Deprecation: Used with deliberate hyperbole by intellectuals, academics, and columnists to mock their own reactionary impulses or pedantic frustrations—such as lamenting modern youth slang, vulgar pop culture, sloppy punctuation, or bureaucratic absurdity by summoning the grand oratorical ghost of the Roman Republic.
