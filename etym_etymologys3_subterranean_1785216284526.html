The word subterranean is a Latin compound, and its full biography must therefore follow two roots that only met in Latin: the spatial particle sub- and the earth-word terra. Each has its own deep history, so the essay is told in two movements.

First movement: sub-. The prefix descends from the PIE spatial particle *upo, "under, below, up from under" — one of the great orientational words of the prehistoric language, which radiated into nearly every branch of the family. Greek turned it into hupó (source of English hypo-, as in hypodermic); Sanskrit made of it úpa, "near, towards, under," still alive in Hindi उप (up) and Odia ଉପ (upa) as a prefix meaning "sub-, deputy, near," as in Hindi उप-राष्ट्रपति (up-rāṣṭrapati) "vice-president"; and Germanic made of it *upp, the ancestor of Old English upp and modern English up. In Latin, *upo became sub by a regular sound change in which the voiceless *p between vowels voiced to b. Latin sub, "under, below," is the celebrated prefix of suburb, subway, submit, and subordinate. To sub, Latin added an old comparative element *-ter-, producing subter, "underneath, below"; and to subter, Latin joined terra with the adjectival suffix -aneus, yielding subterraneus, "situated beneath the earth." English borrowed the whole word directly in the early seventeenth century, first in learned works of geography and natural history. The branching of the same particle is one of the most instructive demonstrations in Indo-European linguistics: a single prehistoric word for "under" produced sub-, hypo-, up, and upa, each carrying the original sense into a different culture and era.

Second movement: terra. The second element has an etymology that surprises: Latin terra, "earth," comes from the PIE root *ters-, "to dry." Terra thus literally means "the dry [land]," the firm ground defined against the sea — a semantic shift (land defined by dryness) found independently in languages across the world. The root *ters- also produced Latin torrere, "to parch" (source of English torrid and torrent), Greek térsomai, "to become dry," and in Germanic Old English þurst, "thirst," the ancestor of modern English thirst. In the Indo-Aryan branch it yielded Sanskrit √tṛṣ, "to thirst," with tṛṣṇā, "thirst, craving," surviving today in Hindi तृष्णा (tṛṣṇā) and Odia ତୃଷ୍ଣା (tṛṣṇā). When Latin combined this "dry-land" noun with subter and -aneus, it created a word whose literal value is "pertaining to what lies under the dry land," and the semantic force of subterraneus was from the start architectural and geographical: the realm beneath the firm ground.

Assembly and English career. English took subterraneus whole and unchanged in form, and the borrowing settled immediately into the register it still occupies: scientific, elevated, slightly alarming. From the 17th century on it was used of cave systems, underground water, and buried structures, and in the 19th century it gained its great modern companions: the subterranean railway (the London Underground and its peers) and, in biology, the study of subterranean fauna. In literature the word acquired a mythic charge — the imagined underworld of fantasy and gothic romance — and J.R.R. Tolkien, a professional philologist steeped in exactly these older registers, chose it in The Hobbit to transform a guessed-at pool into a deep dark subterranean lake, an entrance into the sunless world beneath the mountain. The word thus carries, in a single adjective, three thousand years: a prehistoric particle, a Latin coinage on the model of the dry land, and a modern fantasy of the world underfoot.

Key sound shifts and forms.
  • *upo → Latin sub: voicing of intervocalic *p to b; loss of final syllable by regular Latin apocope in prefixed use.
  • sub → subter: addition of the comparative/adverbial element *-ter-, giving the sense "underneath" with emphasis on position below.
  • *ters- → Latin terra: *ters- lost s and lengthened the vowel, giving the attested terra of Classical Latin.
  • terra + -aneus → subterraneus: the suffix -aneus forms adjectives meaning "belonging to, made of," as in mediterraneus, "midland."
  • Latin subterraneus → English subterranean: direct borrowing; the English spelling -ean reflects the -eus/-ea adjective pattern (cf. mediterranean).

Parallel borrowings. Latin subterranus (without -e-) produced Old French souterrain, "underground," which English re-imported in the 18th century as a specialist archaeological term for an underground chamber; Romance languages built their own regular descendants — Italian sotterraneo, Spanish subterráneo, Portuguese subterrâneo — each meaning "underground," each still visibly built from the same sub + terra.
