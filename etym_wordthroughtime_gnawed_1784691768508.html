- Old English literal sense - gnaw (gnagan) is the strong verb of persistent biting: to bite at something again and again, scraping and wearing it away with the teeth, as a dog gnaws a bone or a rat gnaws through a sack; a word of destruction by degrees
- Middle & Early Modern figurative extension - gnaw spreads to intangible, corrosive processes: care, disease, grief and pain 'gnaw' at a person from within, eating away like teeth at a surface
- 18th-19th century standardising - the figurative senses settle into fixed literary form: gnawing hunger, gnawing doubt, a gnawing pain; the word becomes the stock image of inner erosion
- Contemporary sense - the physical sense survives for animals and vermin, while the commonest modern use is figurative — a gnawing worry or anxiety that worries and wears at the mind
