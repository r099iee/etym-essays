CHAIN — a single root family, beginning at attested Latin (the base word has no secure PIE ancestor):
Latin finis "end, limit, boundary" (attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE; of no agreed Indo-European etymology — de Vaan classes it as without a certain PIE pedigree, so no reconstructed PIE stage is given or invented here)
→ Latin finīre "to end, finish, set bounds to" (attested 1st c. BCE)
→ Latin fīnītus "finished, brought to an end, completed" (past participle of finīre; attested 1st c. BCE)
→ Old French fin "end" and, in the transferred sense, "fine, refined, pure, of high quality" — something carried to its finished limit is excellent (adjective fin; attested from the 9th–12th c.)
→ Middle English fine "of high quality, delicate, pure, excellent" (borrowed from Old French fin; attested from the 13th c.)
→ English fine + suffix -ery (from Old French -erie): finery "showy, expensive clothes and ornaments" (attested c. 1670s)
→ English finery ◄ TARGET (present day)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The base Latin finis has no certain PIE etymology. Earlier attempts to attach it to a root meaning "to fix, set, put" (and to PIE *dʰeygʷ- "to thrust in, fix," whence stake, pale) are judged uncertain or folk-etymological by modern handbooks (de Vaan: "no good etymology"). The chain from Latin finis onward is fully attested. Nuance: the metallurgical finery ("a hearth for refining pig iron into wrought iron," attested 17th c.) is the same word-family applied to the process of fining/refining metal — English refine itself is re- + fine — so the two senses of finery (fine dress vs fine iron) belong to one word-family, not to two accidental homophones.
