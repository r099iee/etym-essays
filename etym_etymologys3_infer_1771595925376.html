From carrying to concluding: the evolution of infer The biography of infer is the biography of a very old verb of carrying, pressed into the service of the mind. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰer-, "to carry, to bear, to bring" — a root older than any word for thinking, a word for the physical work of transporting things from one place to another. In Sanskrit this root gave bhárati, "he carries," and from it the noun bhāra, "a burden," which survives in Hindi भार (bhār), "burden, weight," and Odia ଭାର (bhāra), "burden, load." In Greek it gave φέρω (phérō), "I carry," the root of metaphor (a word literally "carried across"), periphery ("carried around"), anaphora ("a carrying back"), and amphora (the two-handled jar carried from both sides). In Germanic it gave the ancestor of English bear, birth, and burden; in Celtic it gave Old Irish berid, "he carries." The root is so ancient that it appears in the oldest layers of every branch of the family, always with the same physical image: something heavy, something lifted, something moved. It was Latin that turned this root toward the mind. The Latin verb ferre, "to carry," formed a vast family of prefixed compounds — transferre, referre, conferre, offerre, preferre, proferre, afferre, differre — each a physical carrying given a metaphorical job. The one that matters for our word is īnferre, formed with the prefix in- "into": literally "to carry in," to bring a thing across a threshold into a place. In classical Latin īnferre already carried intellectual senses — to bring in an argument, to adduce a reason — and Cicero in the first century BCE uses it precisely of carrying reasons into a discussion. The logical sense developed further in late and medieval Latin, where the scholastic philosophers made inferre the standard verb for the drawing of conclusions: a conclusion is "brought in" from premises, as a traveller is brought into a city. It is this school-Latin usage, not the simple physical verb, that the fifteenth century passed to English. Middle English borrowed the Latin base as inferre/infer around 1500 with the concrete meaning "to bring in, to import" — a meaning recorded for goods brought into the country — and within a century the logical sense had hardened and displaced the physical one. By the sixteenth century to infer was specifically to derive a proposition by reasoning, to carry a conclusion over from premises, and Shakespeare and the Elizabethan theologians use it exactly in this sense. The word has been the ordinary English verb for drawing a conclusion ever since.
  • Sound changes: the Latin prefix in- assimilated and the first syllable shortened, giving English infer (stress on the second syllable); the family preserves the spelling -fer- of the Latin stem in all its compounds (transfer, refer, confer, offer).
  • Semantic drift: from physical "to carry in" (a load, an argument) to logical "to conclude from premises" — one of the clearest cases in English of a concrete verb of motion becoming an abstract verb of thought, in the same way that comprehend (to grasp) and conclude (to shut together) moved from body to mind.
  • Socio-cultural context: the rise of the empirical method in the seventeenth century (Bacon, then Locke, then the Royal Society) made "inference" a key word of English intellectual life; the nineteenth century added statistics, where inference became a formal technique; and the Victorian controversy over "implying" versus "inferring" reflects the age's new scrupulousness about what exactly a writer had said.
  • Literary attestations: Ruskin's "if the reader chooses, thence, to infer that they were not masters at all, it is his conclusion, not mine" is the masterpiece of the word's use as a rhetorical shield; the imply/infer distinction is enshrined in Fowler's Modern English Usage (1926) and remains the most cited usage note of the language.
In modern English infer is everywhere: in ordinary speech, in science ("inferring a mechanism from the data"), in statistics ("inferential statistics"), and in reading pedagogy ("inference questions"). Its two living edges are the strict logical use and the courteous conversational use, and its whole career is a single, almost allegorical movement — the root's ancient image of a strong back carrying a burden gradually became the modern image of a careful mind carrying a conclusion. When Ruskin hands the inference to the reader and then disowns it, he is exploiting the deepest structure of the word: an inference is always something carried in by someone, from somewhere, and its origins and its bearer can be held to account.
