The biography of hallowed begins at a root that meant, not holiness, but wholeness. The Proto-Indo-European root *kailo- signified “whole, uninjured, of good omen” — the concept of a thing intact and unbroken, hence sound, healthy, and bringing good fortune. The root is attested across the family in words of wholeness and health: Sanskrit kevala, “entire, alone” (a proposed cognate), Old Prussian kailas, “healthy, whole,” and Old Church Slavonic cělъ, “whole,” the ancestor of Russian tselyy, “whole, entire.” The oldest mental equation beneath all of them is that the intact is the good: what is whole is sound, what is sound is healthy, what is healthy is fortunate. Germanic: from whole to holy In the Germanic branch the root became *hailaz, “healthy, whole, sound; of good omen,” the ancestor of English whole, hale, and heal, and of the greeting hail — the wish for soundness. From *hailaz the Germanic peoples formed the derived adjective *hailagaz, “holy,” literally “belonging to the sound, intact order of things”: the quality of the divine was conceived as completeness and inviolability, the unbroken wholeness that the profane world lacks. The word’s forms spread across the whole Germanic world — Old Saxon hēlag, Old High German heilag (German heilig), Old Norse heilagr (whence the Scandinavian helgi, “saint”), Gothic hailags — and Old English hālig, “holy,” the adjective of divine sanctity, recorded from the earliest English texts. It is one of the oldest and most fundamental religious words of the language, yet its root is entirely physical: the holy is the whole, the unbroken, the inviolate, set apart from the broken and the profane. The sound history here is instructive: the PIE *k- shifted to Germanic *h- by the First Sound Shift (Grimm’s Law), giving the h- that English still pronounces; the PIE diphthong *ai survived into the oldest Germanic and then monophthongised in different directions in the branches — to ā in Old English (hālig), to ei in Old High German (heilag, heilig). Old English: the denominative verb From hālig, Old English derived a denominative verb hālgian, “to make holy, to consecrate,” the physical act of setting something apart for God by ritual — a church, an altar, ground, a person, bread and wine. The verb is recorded in the earliest Old English texts and continued through Middle English as halwen, halowen, “to consecrate, to honour as holy,” whose past participle halowed hardened into the adjective hallowed, “consecrated, sanctified.” The word is fixed in the liturgy of the Lord’s Prayer — “hallowed be thy name” — the line in which the petitioner asks that God’s name be treated as holy, and through the Bible (Wyclif c. 1395, Tyndale 1526, the Authorised Version 1611, and the Book of Common Prayer) the adjective carried the full weight of divine sanctity into every household that prayed in English. All Hallows’ Eve The same Old English hālgian produced the noun halwe, “holy one, saint,” the plural of which — hallows — survives to this day in the festival name All Hallows’ Eve (the eve of All Saints’ Day), contracted in modern spelling to Halloween. The survival is remarkable: the verb to hallow is now archaic outside liturgy, but its sister noun lives on in one of the most popular festivals of the English-speaking world, a fossilised relic of the old saint-word that most speakers no longer recognise. The figurative transfer The decisive semantic step for the adjective came in the early modern period. From the sixteenth century hallowed began to loosen from the altar to anything treated as sacred by tradition: hallowed customs, hallowed memories, hallowed institutions, hallowed ground (later of battlefields and memorials). The transfer is a movement of reverence — what is treated with the awe owed to the divine becomes, by long custom, “hallowed,” with no claim of divinity at all. The OED tracks the sense precisely: “made holy,” then figuratively “made the object of reverential awe or regard.” By the nineteenth century the word had become a staple of elevated prose for quasi-religious veneration — the reverence paid to precedent, canon, and tradition — and it is in this transferred, almost priestly register that the prosodic treatise can speak of the “hallowed example of great poets” with no religious intent at all. Sound changes and transmission summarised
  • PIE *kailo- → Proto-Germanic *hailaz: the PIE *k- became Germanic *h- (the First Sound Shift, Grimm’s Law), and *ai remained a diphthong in the oldest Germanic, monophthongising differently in the branches (OE ā, OHG ei).
  • PGmc *hailaz → *hailagaz: the adjectival suffix *-aga- (of belonging, having the character of) added “having the character of,” giving “belonging to the sound/intact order of things.”
  • PGmc *hailagaz → Old English hālig: the *ai monophthongised to ā, and the medial *-ag- cluster simplified to -i-, giving hālig; the same development produced German heilig from heilag.
  • OE hālig → hālgian → Middle English halwen → Modern hallowed: the denominative -ian suffix, the Middle English weakening of the root vowel, the regularisation of the weak past participle to -ed, and the -ow- spelling that settled in the modern form.
The word’s arc runs from bodily intactness, through sacred rite, to the quiet awe of a literary canon. In the passage, the “hallowed example of great poets” is the transferred sense at full strength: the metrical practice of the ancients, consecrated by veneration and precedent — sanctity without utility, a canon too venerable to question but incapable of providing the “minutely detailed rules” that the same passage says the English language’s syllables lacked.
