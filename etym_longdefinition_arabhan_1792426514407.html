Śarabhan (proper noun / lexical stem; Sanskrit: शरभन् / शरभङ्-, representing the stem of Śarabhaṅga, compounded of śarabha + aṅga; in text editions appearing as Śarabhan̄ga or stem Śarabhan-) — In ancient Sanskrit epic literature, particularly the Araṇyakāṇḍa of Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa, the venerable forest sage, ṛṣi, and master ascetic whose hermitage Lord Rāma, Princess Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa visit immediately after slaying the rākṣasa Virādha. Revered throughout Hindu tradition as an archetype of supreme ascetic mastery (*tapas*), self-transcendence, and voluntary spiritual departure, Śarabhaṅga famously postpones ascending to the highest celestial world in order to meet Rāma in person before consigning his mortal body to a blazing sacrificial fire.

Primary epic episode — the visitation of Indra and meeting with Rāma. In sarga 5 of the Araṇyakāṇḍa, as Rāma approaches the hermitage, he witnesses an awe-inspiring supernatural spectacle: Lord Indra, the king of the gods, has descended in a radiant, sun-like celestial chariot (*vimāna*) drawn by green horses, hovering above the hermitage while accompanied by effulgent deities. Indra has come personally to escort the sage Śarabhaṅga directly to the supreme realm of Brahmā (*Brahmaloka*), which the sage had won through countless centuries of unyielding austerities. However, perceiving that Lord Rāma is nearby, Śarabhaṅga declines to board Indra's chariot, informing the king of heaven that he will not abandon the mortal world until he has gazed upon Rāma, his honored guest and the upholder of cosmic righteousness.

The voluntary fiery ascension (Agni-praveśa and Mahāprasthāna). When Rāma enters the hermitage and prostrates before him, Śarabhaṅga greets the prince with fatherly warmth. The sage offers to bestow upon Rāma all the sublime heavenly realms and spiritual merits he had accumulated through his lifelong penances. Rāma gracefully declines the generous gift, stating that he must earn his own worlds through righteous duty, requesting only that the sage instruct him on a safe, auspicious location in the forest to build a hermitage. After directing Rāma south toward the hermitage of the sage Sutīkṣṇa, Śarabhaṅga declares that the time for his earthly departure has arrived:

  • Preparation of the sacrificial fire — The venerable sage consecrates a sacred sacrificial fire (*havya-vāhana agni*) with ghee and Vedic oblations while chanting sacred mantras.
  • The casting off of the mortal sheath — In the direct presence of Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa, Śarabhaṅga serenely steps into the blazing pyre, offering his decrepit, aged mortal body as a final oblation to the flame.
  • Metamorphosis and celestial ascent — As the mortal flesh burns away, a radiant, youthful divine body of luminous splendor emerges from the fire. Resplendent as the morning sun, Śarabhaṅga ascends beyond the realms of the gods directly to the unconditioned world of Brahmā.

The skeletal witness and Rāma's sacred vow. Following Śarabhaṅga's ascension, a multitude of surviving ascetics (*vānaprasthas*, *vaikhānasas*, and *vālakhilyas*) approach Rāma. They lead him to the vicinity of Śarabhaṅga's hermitage and reveal terrifying mounds of human bones and skulls—the ghastly remnants of saintly ascetics who had been butchered and devoured by cannibalistic rākṣasas throughout the Daṇḍaka wilderness. This gruesome sight moves Rāma to tears of sorrow and righteous fury, prompting his historic solemn vow: with his bow and arrows, he swears before the hermits that he will completely annihilate the demonic forces from the forest, an oath that sets the stage for the destruction of Khara, Dūṣaṇa, and ultimately King Rāvaṇa.

Philological, morphological, and onomastic analysis:

  • The constituent *śarabha* — A mythical eight-legged beast of ancient Indian lore (celebrated in the Atharvaveda and Purāṇas) that dwells in the snowy mountains, said to possess such terrifying strength and speed that it can overpower lions and elephants. In Śaiva mythology, Lord Śiva assumes the avian-leonine form of Śarabheśvara to subdue the furious half-man, half-lion incarnation Narasiṃha.
  • The constituent *aṅga* — Signifies "limb, body, member, constituent part." Compounded together, Śarabha-aṅga denotes "He whose limbs possess the strength of a Śarabha" or "He whose mortal bodily frame is shattered/sacrificed like a beast," symbolizing supreme ascetic resilience.
  • The orthographic variant *Śarabhan-* — In Sanskrit manuscripts and typographical transliteration of class-nasals, the guttural nasal consonant ṅ (which regularly precedes the voiced guttural stop g in Śarabhaṅga) is frequently represented with a macron or tilde (n̄ or n), yielding the compound division Śarabhan̄-ga. The stem form Śarabhan captures this lexical base in Sanskrit compound morphology.

Spiritual and philosophical legacy. Śarabhaṅga embodies the ancient Indian concept of *ichhā-mṛtyu* (voluntary death at will) and the sublime completion of the four life stages (*āśramas*). Having attained the highest pinnacle of earthly knowledge and witnessed the living presence of the divine avatāra, he casts off the physical body without fear or regret, illustrating that the true sage treats mortality as merely a worn-out garment discarded in the fire of spiritual liberation.
