Herring is a small, silvery, shoaling sea fish of the North Atlantic and North Sea, principally Clupea harengus (Atlantic herring), with close kin in Pacific herring and sardines, sprats, and shad. Deep-bodied, blue-green above and silver below, with a single dorsal fin and weakly toothed jaws, it travels in vast schools that darken the sea, feeds on plankton, and spawns on gravel banks. In the source sentence, John Ruskin in Praeterita suspecting his sailor-grandfather had something to do with the herring business but his mother never being communicative, the word means the commercial fishery itself: catching, curing, barrelling, and selling herring from Yarmouth and the east-coast ports, the 18th-century oil-and-salt economy in which a seaman could rise, speculate, and fall silent.

As food, herring is oily, bony, and rich, eaten fresh, salted, smoked, pickled, or fermented across northern Europe. Curing vocabularies distinguish grades: white (fresh or lightly salted), red (heavily salted and long-smoked to russet and hard), bloater (lightly smoked whole, pale and soft, Yarmouth's fame), kipper (split, salted, and smoked), buckling (hot-smoked), matje (Dutch young virgin herring), surstromming (Swedish fermented), rollmop (pickled fillet rolled round onion). Before refrigeration, salt herring in barrels was Lenten protein for millions, armies, and plantations; Yarmouth, Lowestoft, Aberdeen, and Great Grimsby lived by the autumn drift-net season, with Scottish fisher lasses following the fleet to gut and pack. The fish is also bait, oil, and meal: herring oil for lamps and leather, guano and fishmeal for fields.

Ecologically, herring is a keystone forage fish: the bridge between plankton and cod, haddock, seals, porpoises, gannets, and man. Stocks boom and crash with overfishing, sea temperature, and North Atlantic oscillation; the 20th century saw repeated collapses and recoveries under quotas and marine science. To call a shoal a herring run is to name one of the sea's great pulses, visible from cliffs as oily calms and wheeling birds.

  • Core zoological sense (countable and mass): the fish itself, alive or as catch; for example a shoal of herring, three herrings for supper, herring gulls follow the herring. Plural herring or herrings; young called whitebait (mixed), sile, or sild. This is the dictionary head sense.
  • Commercial fishery sense (the herring business): the industry of catching, curing, and selling; for example Yarmouth herring business, herring season, herring fleet, herring lass. The bridge is product to trade: the fish names the work, capital, and season around it. This is the source sense.
  • Food and curing senses: prepared forms; for example red herring (hard-smoked, also a false trail), bloater, kipper, pickled herring, herring salad. Each names a recipe: salt plus smoke plus time. The bridge is preservation: the perishable shoal must be stabilised within hours.
  • Figurative red herring (idiom): a false scent or diversion dragged across a trail, from cured fish used to train hounds; for example a red herring in a mystery, a budget red herring. The bridge is smell: strong smoked fish distracts dogs and readers. The sense is now fully lexicalised and far from the sea.
  • Dead herring and idioms of cheapness: dead as a herring (utterly lifeless), neither fish nor flesh nor good red herring (useless muddle). The bridge is abundance: herring were so common they became the measure of worthlessness.
  • Attributive and ecological senses: herring gull (the silver-grey gull that follows fleets), herringbone (cloth or masonry pattern like the fish's spine), herring run, herring stock. Here herring modifies to evoke shoaling plenty, silvery slant, or marine dependence.

Grammatically herring is both countable (a herring, two herrings) and mass (a barrel of herring, eat more herring), with zero plural common in trade. Pronounced in Received Pronunciation as HERR-ing, with short e and velar nasal. It must not be confused with shad (deeper river spawner), sardine (smaller Mediterranean pilchard), or anchovy (smaller, saltier). In Ruskin the word smells of salt, smoke, and reticence: a grandfather's sea capital his daughter will not discuss.
