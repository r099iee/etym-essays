Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*snu- / *sne-
"to sniff, breathe, mucus"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*snuf- / *snip-
"to draw in air through the nose"
↓
Middle English (c. 1350 CE)
sniften
"to sniffle, snort, inhale sharply"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1740 CE)
snifter
"a sharp gust of wind; a sniff"
↓
Modern English (industrial) (c. 1790 CE)
snifter (valve)
"engine valve that admits air with a sniffing hiss"
↓
Modern English (slang) (c. 1840s)
snifter
"a small sip, tot, or dram of spirits"
↓
Modern English (glassware & Wodehousian) (early 20th c.–present)
Snifters
"brandy glasses; convivial liquor drinks"

Etymological Note: Lexicographers debate whether the liquor sense arose primarily from the nasal effect of strong spirits (a quick nip clearing the nasal passages, causing a sniff) or from the physical act of connoisseurs holding balloon glasses to their noses to inhale aromatic bouquets before sipping. Both associations reinforced each other during the 19th and 20th centuries.
