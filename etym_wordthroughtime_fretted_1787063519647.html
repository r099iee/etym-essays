- Old English fretan — "to devour, eat up" — a strong verb for animals eating greedily (German fressen still means animal-eating); purely physical, almost savage.
- Middle English freten — "to gnaw, wear away, consume" — the eating sense transfers to corrosion: rust frets iron, water frets stone; moralists love the word for decay.
- 14th–16th c. — "to vex, irritate, annoy" — what gnaws at the body now gnaws at the mind; to be fretted is to be harassed and tormented by cares.
- 17th–19th c. — "to worry, be anxious" becomes the dominant sense; fretful = peevish and anxious; "don't fret" enters everyday consolation — the word shifts from external gnawing to internal churning.
- 19th c. — of inanimate things: ships and timbers fret = creak, groan, complain under strain — Tolkien's barrels creak and fret, an animate metaphor for wood under pressure.
- 20th–21st c. — fret about, fret over — fret becomes almost exclusively psychological; the physical "eating away" survives only in fossil phrases ("fret the stone") and technical chafe-of-rope; meanwhile the unrelated decorative fret (fretwork, guitar frets) keeps the same spelling alive in entirely different workshops.
