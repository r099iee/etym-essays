1. The striking root. To be impelled is, at root, to be driven as if by a thrust. The reconstructed base PIE *pel- gathers meanings of striking, thrusting and setting in motion — the physical grammar of force applied to a body. It is the same stock that underlies Latin pellere and its enormous family, Greek pállein "to poise, sway, brandish", and a range of Germanic and Celtic "drive / strike" verbs. Even before the word reaches English, then, the vocabulary of force is fully formed: the root does not describe gentle encouragement but the impact that starts a thing moving. 2. Latin pellere and the drive-family. - Latin pellere "to push, drive, strike, beat, banish, expel" is attested from early Latin (Plautus, c. 3rd–2nd c. BCE). Its participle stem puls- yields English pulse and, via Old French poulser, push; the compounds build the force-vocabulary of English: compellere → compel, expellere → expel, propellere → propel, repellere → repel, and impellere → impel. - Sound note: the alternation between the present stem -ll- (pellere) and the participial -s- (pulsus) is the classic Latin root pattern; English borrows from both streams — impel from the present stem, impulse from the participle. 3. The compound impellere. - impellere adds the preverb in- / im- "into, against, upon" to pellere, giving "to strike against, drive into motion, urge on". It is attested in classical prose and verse (Cicero, Caesar, Lucretius, 1st c. BCE), where it covers both physical shoves and political, moral and emotional urgings. - The image is a push applied at a point of contact that sets something going — pressing upon something until it yields and moves. That image of the applied force is what later transfers so cleanly to the conscience. 4. English: a learned bookish loan. - English impel was not inherited from the Germanic side; it entered as a learned borrowing from Latin (partly via French) in the early 16th century, first recorded c. 1520–1533. - Because it was a bookish loan, impel kept an elevated, Latinate register beside the native push and drive, and it was suited from the start to moral and psychological rather than purely physical contexts. Its noun partner impulse, from impulsus, adds the sense of a sudden inward shove — the momentary spark that precedes action. 5. Semantic drift: from hand and weapon to conscience. - The physical sense — driving a body forward by force — survives only in technical language, most visibly in the noun impeller: the rotating blade that drives fluid in a pump, fan or turbocharger, where the root's mechanical force still operates in plain engineering terms. - The dominant modern sense is psychological and ethical: conscience, duty, curiosity, love or fear "impels" a person to act. The resistance overcome is hesitation or reluctance, not an enemy's will; the drive is felt as coming from within, even when it is set in motion from outside. - Bullets on the drift: hand and weapon (thrust) → machine (propulsion) → mind (motive). The -ed form reports that the shove has already taken effect: the subject has been moved into action, and the grammar freezes the moment of decision. 6. The passage and the word's force. In the novel, Aroma's "simple devotion frequently impelled her to remonstrate" with Bao-yu. Her devotion performs the Latin work of impellere: she is not merely advised or inclined but driven to speak — affection as a pulse striking against hesitation. Because the remonstrances "invariably" fail and Bao-yu takes "not the least notice", the word carries tragedy: propulsion without result, effort that is spent into silence. Among the sister words one can map degrees of agency — compel binds from outside, propel drives forward in space, impulse names the shove, and impel sits at the moral-psychological middle where an inner drive presses a person into speech or deed, whether the world listens or not.
