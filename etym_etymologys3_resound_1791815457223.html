The Indo-European root. Latin sonāre “to sound” and the noun sonus “sound” belong to a small but widespread family built on the reconstructed PIE root *swen- “to sound, rustle, make a noise”. The root is recovered from Sanskrit svánati “sounds” and svaná- “sound, noise”, and it has a Germanic reflex in Old English swinn “melody, music”, a word that survives only in poetry and in the derivative swinsian “to sing”. The reconstruction is standard, but the root is not attested as a word; the topmost node of the chain is a comparative formula, not a text.

Classical Latin. sonus is ordinary Latin for any sound, from a footfall to thunder, and sonāre is its verb: to make a sound, to sound, to utter. From the first century BCE Latin also has resonāre, a compound of re- “back, again” and sonāre, used of places that return or hold a sound — caves, shores, halls, bronze — and of voices that ring out. The prefix is not simply “again”: in resonare it has the sense of a return, a sound thrown back from a surface, and hence of a space so filled with noise that it seems to answer. The verb is at home in epic and didactic poetry and in prose of spectacle and public noise.

From echo to report. Already in Latin the verb slides from acoustics to reputation: resonare can mean “to be talked of, to be celebrated”, because fame is imagined as a name ringing through a whole country. This is the same metaphor that gives English “renowned” from Old French renomer, “to name again”, and it explains why resound in English has an archaic sense “to be much spoken of, to be famous”. The bridge is not arbitrary: a sound that fills a space is public, and a name that fills a country is famous.

Old French and Middle English. Old French resoner, resonner (twelfth century) continues the Latin verb with the same double range, and Anglo-Norman carried it into England after the Conquest. Middle English resounen is recorded from about 1400 in the sense “to echo, ring out”; earlier the same verb appears in the form resownen, and the verb is common in fourteenth-century alliterative and devotional verse. The word was thus established in English as a bookish, slightly elevated borrowing, not as a native inheritance — a fact that still colours its register.

Phonology and spelling

  • Old French resoner had stress on the stem and a close o; Middle English kept the stress on the second syllable, which English still has: /rɪˈzaʊnd/.
  • The Middle English vowel /uː/ (spelled ou) was diphthongised to /aʊ/ in the Great Vowel Shift, giving the modern sound; the same change produced sound, house and out.
  • The final -d is not in the French etymon. It appears in late Middle English, almost certainly by analogy with sound (from Old French son), which acquired its own unetymological -d in the same period. The spelling resound is thus a sixteenth-century fixing of an analogical form.
  • The infinitive ending -en was lost in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, leaving the bare stem, and the modern spelling drops the Middle English ou in favour of the ou of sound.

Reformation and Renaissance. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century writers use resound in both the acoustic and the celebratory senses, often of bells, organs, trumpets and cannon, and often in religious verse where the vault of heaven replaces the church roof. The Authorized Version and the Prayer Book favour simpler verbs, but the poets exploit the word’s capacity to make a scene public: a resounding place is a place with an audience.

Science and the split from “resonate”. The noun resonance enters English around 1500 from Latin resonantia “echo”, and in the seventeenth century it is taken up by acoustics for the sympathetic vibration of a body at a particular frequency. Resonant and resonate follow. From the nineteenth century the technical sense dominates resonance, and in the twentieth century resonate develops a psychological sense — an idea “resonates with” a person or audience, meaning it strikes a chord — which has largely taken over the figurative work that resound once did. The result is a division of labour: resonate for sympathy and significance, resound for literal noise and for the set phrase “resounding victory”.

Modern English. The “famous” sense is now archaic; the transitive “extol” sense is obsolete outside historical prose. What remains is the literal resound with/through, still common in writing but rare in speech, and the adjective resounding, which is fully alive in journalism and politics. Carlyle’s usage belongs to the older, fuller verb: acoustic on the surface, moral and public underneath. When he writes that the churches “resound not with excessive groanings”, he is using the word at its most physical, and letting the reader hear the absence of the crowd.
