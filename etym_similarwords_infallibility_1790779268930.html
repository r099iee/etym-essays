• inerrancy: Similar because both denote total freedom from error; differs because inerrancy (from Latin errare, "to wander/err") is used predominantly in Protestant theology to describe the absolute factual and doctrinal accuracy of the original biblical manuscripts, whereas infallibility describes the broader capability or authority of an entity (such as the Pope, the Church, or human intellect) to not fail or mislead.
  • impeccability: Similar because both describe perfection; differs because impeccability (from Latin peccare, "to sin") refers specifically to the inability to commit sin or moral wrongdoing, whereas infallibility refers strictly to the inability to make errors of truth, doctrine, or factual judgment.
  • omniscience: Similar because both describe supreme cognitive capability; differs because omniscience (from Latin omnis "all" + scire "to know") means knowing all things infinite and complete, whereas infallibility merely guarantees that whatever knowledge or judgment is expressed is free from error.
  • certitude: Similar because both involve the absence of doubt; differs because certitude is a subjective mental state of total conviction and psychological confidence felt by an individual, whereas infallibility is an objective property or structural guarantee of being actually impossible to be wrong.
  • indefectibility: Similar because both represent theological protections against failure; differs because indefectibility refers to the Church's capacity to endure to the end of time without losing its essential identity, apostolic structure, or core faith, whereas infallibility focuses on specific doctrinal definitions.
  • unerringness: Similar because both denote never making a mistake; differs because unerringness is a native Germanic-derived descriptor frequently applied to mechanical precision, archery, instincts, or animal navigation, whereas infallibility carries profound theological, philosophical, and institutional gravity.
