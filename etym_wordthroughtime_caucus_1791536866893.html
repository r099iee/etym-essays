- Mid-to-Late 18th Century - Applied to secret colonial political clubs in Boston (the Caucus Club) where revolutionary leaders like Samuel Adams met in private to plan town-meeting strategy and resist British rule.
- Early 19th Century - Institutionalized in national American politics as 'King Caucus,' referring to closed congressional meetings that selected presidential nominees, carrying connotations of oligarchy and backroom elitism.
- Late 19th Century - Exported across the Atlantic to British politics as a pejorative term used by Disraeli and critics to denounce Joseph Chamberlain's rigid, centralized Birmingham party machinery.
- Mid-to-Late 20th Century - Re-established as a legitimate democratic institution through the Iowa presidential caucuses and formal congressional voting blocs (e.g., Congressional Black Caucus).
- Contemporary Usage - Neutral, standard political and legal term denoting party strategy meetings, legislative factions, and private side-sessions in professional conflict mediation.
