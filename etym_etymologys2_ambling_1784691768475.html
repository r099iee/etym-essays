Proto-Indo-European *h₂el- "to wander, to roam" (the root of Greek aláomai "I wander, roam") + *h₂m̥bʰi "around, on both sides" (the preposition-adverb behind Latin ambi-/amb- "around" and Greek amphí "on both sides") → Latin ambulāre "to walk, to go about, to walk around" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. in the comedies of Plautus) → Old French ambler "to walk; (of a horse) to move at an easy, smooth pace" (attested from the 11th–12th c.) → Middle English amblen "to move at an easy pace, to amble" (c. 1300) → Early Modern English amble (verb and noun; the noun is also applied to the horse's lateral gait, from the 16th c.) → Modern English amble + -ing (present participle and verbal noun) → ambling (walking or riding at a slow, relaxed, easy pace). Note on the debated stage: the element that follows ambi- in ambulāre is disputed. The older and still popular derivation from Proto-Indo-European *h₂eǵ- "to drive" (the root of Latin agere, Greek ágein, Sanskrit ájati) is not accepted by modern handbooks (de Vaan 2008; Beekes 2010), since the phonology and morphology fit poorly; the usual current analysis takes the base from *h₂el- "to wander", giving literally "to go about, to wander around". Some scholars regard the base as simply obscure and do not commit beyond "a verbal formation built on ambi-". The chain above follows the *h₂el- analysis and notes the alternative explicitly.
