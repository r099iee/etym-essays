noblesse is French for nobility, and in English it survives almost entirely as a literary borrowing meaning the noble class, noble birth, or noble character. In the source sentence — John Ruskin in Praeterita recalling how, as a boy, he had formed his notion of kinghood chiefly from the FitzJames of the Lady of the Lake, and of noblesse from the Douglas there, and the Douglas in Marmion — it means chivalric lordliness as Walter Scott imagined it: the Douglas earls of Angus and their kin, proud, loyal, valiant, courteous, dangerous, dwelling in castles like Tantallon and Stirling that the child later found painfully empty. For the boy Ruskin noblesse was not an abstraction but persons — old Angus, Ellen Douglas, her father — and a code: to fight hard, to keep faith, to protect the weak, to speak plainly to kings. The wondering question that follows, why the castles should now be always empty, is the child discovering that the social order that housed such noblesse has vanished, leaving only stone shells.

In ordinary French noblesse simply means the nobility as a social estate: la noblesse française, la noblesse de robe, la noblesse d'épée. English borrows it in three overlapping uses. First, as a straight synonym for aristocracy, usually with a French flavour: the French noblesse, the noblesse of old Europe, the provincial noblesse. Second, as a moral quality, nobleness of mind and conduct: there was a noblesse in his bearing, she received them with true noblesse. Third, and most durably, in the maxim noblesse oblige — nobility obliges — meaning rank imposes duties, privilege demands generosity, honour, and self-sacrifice. The phrase, first formulated in French by the Duc de Lévis in 1808, became a Victorian commonplace and remains the chief reason English speakers meet the word at all.

  • Head sense: the noble class, the aristocracy (countable collective): e.g. the noblesse fled during the Revolution, the lesser noblesse of the provinces, the Scottish noblesse of Scott's ballads. Always with an overtone of old-regime hierarchy, castles, titles, coats of arms. Historians distinguish the noblesse d'épée (nobility of the sword, old military families), the noblesse de robe (nobility of the gown, judicial and administrative families ennobled through office), the noblesse de cloche (municipal nobility of mayors and échevins), and the Napoleonic noblesse d'empire. Ruskin's Douglas noblesse belongs imaginatively to the first kind.
  • Moral sense: nobleness, dignity, elevation of character: e.g. a countenance full of noblesse, he bore his ruin with noblesse, the noblesse of her forgiveness. Here the word sheds rank and keeps bearing: upright posture, generous speech, refusal to stoop. It is near-synonymous with nobility, magnanimity, grandeur of soul, but keeps a French elegance that the plain English nobleness lacks.
  • Maxim sense: noblesse oblige (rank binds): e.g. noblesse oblige — he paid the debts of his tenants, she felt noblesse oblige to receive the stranger kindly. The sense is that to be noble, whether by birth, wealth, office, or talent, is to owe more, not to take more. Victorian public schools, the officer corps, and Ruskin himself preached it as social doctrine against mere money-power.
  • Historical-technical sense: the Second Estate of the ancien régime: e.g. the cahiers of the noblesse in 1789, the abolition of the noblesse on the night of 4 August. French history textbooks divide the kingdom into clergy, noblesse, and Third Estate; the noblesse paid no taille, held seigneurial rights, wore the sword, and was in turn swept away or restored in 1789, 1814, 1830, and 1848. English writers keep the French word precisely to mark Frenchness.
  • Scottish-Romantic sense (source sense): Douglas noblesse: the chivalric ideal of the border lords — hard riding, hard fighting, fierce loyalty to kin and king, hospitality to strangers, defiance of fortune. Scott's Douglas in The Lady of the Lake (the exiled father of Ellen) and in Marmion (Archibald Douglas, Earl of Angus, Bell-the-Cat) embodies it, and the boy Ruskin took it as documentary truth about what lords were.
  • Modern figurative extensions: journalists write of the noblesse of the professions, a scientific noblesse, the noblesse of football, meaning any entrenched elite imagined as having duties as well as privileges. The tone is usually half-ironic: the new tech noblesse praises and mocks at once. The fashion world calls elegant simplicity noblesse; wine writers praise noblesse in a Bordeaux, meaning breed and balance.

Grammatically noblesse is a mass or collective noun, rarely pluralised in English (noblesses is found but pedantic); French plural is noblesses. It takes the definite article when naming the class (the noblesse) and no article when naming the quality (with noblesse). Pronunciation in England is anglicised French /nəʊˈbles/ with silent final e, though purists keep nasal /nɔbles/. Spelling is stable; the older English form nobless (Chaucer) is obsolete. Register is consistently high-literary: to say noblesse instead of nobility signals that one has read Scott, Balzac, or Burke on the French Revolution.
