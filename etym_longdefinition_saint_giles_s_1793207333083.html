Saint Giles’s is, in Cobbett’s mouth, not a saint at all but a slum: shorthand for the parish of St Giles-in-the-Fields in London, by the early nineteenth century the most infamous rookery in Britain — a warren of courts and alleys round Seven Dials notorious for gin, beggary, and dirt. When Cobbett writes that the labouring people of Middlesex look to be about half Saint Giles’s, he means they look half-way to being St Giles’s people themselves: dirty, gin-drinking, and sunk in the capital’s lowest poverty.

Core meaning in detail: The form is a possessive ellipsis: the church (and then the parish, and then the district) of Saint Giles, i.e. St Giles-in-the-Fields, the ancient parish church standing where Oxford Street meets Tottenham Court Road. Founded beside a twelfth-century hospital for lepers outside the city walls, the parish was engulfed by London’s westward growth, subdivided by speculators into teeming tenements, and by 1700 had become the classic rookery — a maze thieves and beggars could vanish into. Half Saint Giles’s is Cobbett’s grim fraction: Middlesex villagers, sucked dry by London’s taxes and rents, sliding toward the condition of the capital’s poorest.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The London parish and district (primary, from 12th c.): St Giles-in-the-Fields, its church, hospital, and streets. The bridge is simple naming: the place takes the dedication of its church, as St Pancras and St Bride give theirs.
  • A byword for slum squalor (figurative, 18th–19th c., Cobbett’s sense): a Saint Giles’s for any rookery; half Saint Giles’s for people sinking toward it. The bridge is metonymy through fame: the worst slum in England lends its name to wretchedness everywhere, as a nadir or a Bedlam does.
  • The saint himself — Giles the hermit (hagiographic): the Athenian hermit of Provence (d. c. 710), patron of cripples, beggars, and the disabled, whose arrow-pierced hind gave painters their emblem. The bridge is the dedication: the hospital for lepers, the outcast sick, stood under the patron of outcasts.
  • St Giles’s Fair and parish institutions (historical): the parish fair, workhouse, and burial ground that organised local life. The bridge is administrative: the parish was the unit of poor relief, festivity, and burial, so its name stamps every institution.
  • The modern place-name (surviving): St Giles Circus, St Giles High Street — today a busy West End quarter of offices and the Centre Point tower. The bridge is clearance and rebuilding: New Oxford Street (1847) drove through the rookery, and commerce buried the slum while keeping the name.

Register and connotation: In Cobbett’s sentence Saint Giles’s drips with contempt — for the place, for the gin economy that filled it, and for the system that spread its likeness across Middlesex. Eighteenth-century Londoners used it with a shudder and a joke: Hogarth set Gin Lane (1751) in its streets, Gay begged his readers to avoid its alleys, and every moralist knew what a St Giles’s character meant. Today the horror has evaporated and only the address remains, but in historical prose the name still summons the smell of gin, sewage, and despair.
