- Old English (pre-11th c.) - [Idiomatic Phrasal Construct: *þȳ lǣs þe*] - Originated as an Old English instrumental phrase *þȳ lǣs þe* ('by the less that / whereby less that'), meaning 'so that less' or 'in order that not.'
- Middle English (12th–14th c.) - [Phonetic Fusion into Single Conjunction] - The phrase contracted into *les þe*, *leste*, and *lest*, losing the instrumental demonstrative and becoming a standalone subordinating conjunction governing the subjunctive mood.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.) - [Biblical & Constitutional Solemnity] - Flourished in Tudor and Jacobean prose, notably the King James Bible (*'Lest thou forget the Lord thy God'*) and Shakespeare, cementing its high rhetorical register.
- 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment to Victorian) - [Poetic Warning & National Remembrance] - Solidified as the hallmark of cautionary moral poetry, immortalized in Rudyard Kipling's *Recessional* (1897) with the refrain *'Lest we forget—lest we forget!'*
- 20th–21st Century (Contemporary) - [Elevated Register & Formal Caution] - Retained in formal, legal, literary, and diplomatic writing; triggers the present subjunctive (*lest he be*) or modal *should* (*lest they should find*).
