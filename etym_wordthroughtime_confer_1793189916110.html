- Ancient Rome — the bringing-together verb: Latin conferre means to carry together, compare, consult, contribute, and bestow — all branches present in one supremely useful verb.
- Middle Ages — the scholars' tool: conferre in its compare-sense organises marginal cross-references and manuscript collation; monks confer passages as naturally as breathing.
- 16th c. — English adopts it whole: confer enters with both great branches — to consult together and to bestow — plus the comparing-sense for scholars.
- 17th–18th c. — institutions take it over: universities confer degrees, crowns confer honours, courts confer rights; the bestowing branch becomes ceremony's own verb.
- 19th–20th c. — the conference age: the noun conference multiplies — peace conferences, press conferences, parent-teacher conferences — while the verb keeps its dignity.
- Today — counsel and ceremony split the work: doctors confer at bedsides, chancellors confer degrees, and every footnote's cf. still whispers the Latin imperative.
