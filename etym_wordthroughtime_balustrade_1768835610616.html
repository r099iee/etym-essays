- A word of the new classical architecture (17th c.) - borrowed from French balustrade, the term enters English with the taste for Italianate and Palladian building, naming the open rail of small turned posts that lines staircases, balconies, and terraces of formal houses
- The mark of Georgian gentility (18th c.) - in pattern books and townhouse facades the balustrade becomes the standard furnishing of stairs and parapets, its vase-shaped balusters read as a row of little urns supporting the rail - proportion and symmetry made walkable
- Spread and domestication (19th c.) - Victorian terraces, railway bridges, and public buildings multiply balustrades everywhere, and the word broadens from high architecture to any railing of posts carrying a rail, shedding some of its Versailles glamour
- Everyday architectural furniture (contemporary) - balustrade is now the plain technical name for balcony and staircase railings - ornamental heritage intact, but the word has quietly lost the aristocratic air it wore in the age of Georgian staircases
