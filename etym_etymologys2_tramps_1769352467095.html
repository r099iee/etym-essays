Single root word: tramps (the plural of the English noun tramp, formed from the English verb tramp, "to walk with heavy steps"). The whole family is built on a Low German and Dutch base for stamping the feet, and its deeper Indo-European connection is debated and therefore stated explicitly rather than forced. Derivation chain: 1. Expressive / imitative Germanic base *tramp-, "to stamp, tread heavily, tread the ground with a firm foot" — a sound-symbolic base of the North Sea Germanic area. NO Proto-Indo-European root can be securely reconstructed for it. NOTE: some scholars have tried to link the base to PIE *trem-, "to tremble, vibrate," but the semantic bridge (trembling —> heavy stamping) is weak and is not accepted by the OED; the OED treats the word as of Low German or possibly Scandinavian origin. This is the central debate in the etymology. 2. Middle Low German trampen and Middle Dutch trampen, "to stamp the feet, to tread heavily, to trample" — attested in the medieval Low Countries; the base also appears as a frequentative formation, compare German trampeln, "to stamp, trample." 3. Middle English trampen, "to walk heavily, tread with heavy steps, to stamp" — attested c. 1377 (in William Langland's Piers Plowman: "trampe" in the sense of treading); borrowed into English from Low German or Middle Dutch through North Sea and Anglo-Dutch contact. Some accounts allow a native English development on the same expressive base; the borrowing route is the OED's position. 4. English tramp (verb), "to walk with heavy, regular steps; to tread or press heavily; to walk or hike over a long distance" — continuous from Middle English. 5. English tramp (noun), "the sound or act of heavy walking; a long walk or hike" — attested from the mid-17th c.; then "a person who tramps, a wanderer on foot" — the person-sense attested in England from the late 17th–18th c., and in American English as "a vagrant, an itinerant worker or homeless wanderer" from the 1860s–1870s (the era after the American Civil War). 6. English plural tramps —> tramps, "vagrants or wanderers who travel on foot from place to place." Final node: tramps. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: The only genuinely uncertain stage is stage 1. The Low German / Dutch base is solidly attested, and the Middle English borrowing is well documented, but the deeper Indo-European ancestry of the base is unknown. The old linking of the word to PIE *trem- "to tremble" is rejected by the OED; the safest statement is that the word is of expressive Germanic origin with no reconstructable PIE ancestor. No other stage is in dispute.
