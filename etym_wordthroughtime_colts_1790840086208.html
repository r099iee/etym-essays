- c. 800–1100: Attested in Old English as *colt*, designating the young offspring of an ass, horse, or camel.
- c. 1100–1400: In Middle English (*colt*, plural *coltes*), narrowed primarily to young male or female horses, while developing its famous figurative sense of an exuberant, foolish, or wanton youth.
- c. 1400–1600: In Early Modern English, increasingly specialized within horsemanship to mean specifically a *male* horse under four years old (contrasted with the female *filly*), while Shakespeare and contemporaries frequently used *colt* as a metaphor for an unbroken, frisky young man.
- c. 1600–1900: Firmly established in equestrian, racing, and veterinary nomenclature for young intact male equines; also adopted in the 19th century as a famous American brand name for Samuel Colt's revolving firearms.
- c. 1900–Present: Universally used in equestrian breeding, thoroughbred racing (e.g., the Kentucky Derby for three-year-old colts and fillies), sports team naming (e.g., the Indianapolis Colts), and sports journalism for raw, talented rookie athletes.
