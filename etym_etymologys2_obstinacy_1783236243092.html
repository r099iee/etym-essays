PIE *steh₂- "to stand, make stand, set firm" — reconstructed (laryngeal form; zero grade *sth₂-, traditional spelling *stā-); source of Latin stāre, Greek hístēmi/stásis, Sanskrit sthā-, Germanic *stāna- → Proto-Italic *stā- "to stand" — reconstructed → Latin stāre "to stand, stand still, remain" — attested classical Latin; a derivative stem *stin- (nasal/ti-formation seen in compounds) gives the base of obstināre and dēstināre → Latin obstināre "to stand firm against; to persist, hold fast to one's purpose" — attested; ob- "towards, against, in the way of" + *stināre "to make stand" → Latin obstinātus (past participle → adjective) "resolute, unyielding, stubborn" — attested; source of English obstinate (14th c., via Old French obstiné) → Late Latin obstinātia (abstract noun) "stubbornness" — attested late Latin; formed with -tia as in audācia from audāx → Old French obstinacie "stubbornness" — attested → Middle English obstinacie — attested c. 1380s → English OBSTINACY — current Note on the family: Latin obstāre "to stand in the way" (ob + stāre) → English obstacle; dēstināre "to fix, appoint" → destine, destiny, destination; cōnstāre "to stand firm, to cost" → constant, cost; status "a standing, condition" → state, station, statue, statute, status, estate; sistō "I set, place" (reduplicated *sti-sth₂-) → assist, consist, persist, resist, exist, insist. The root also yields Greek hístēmi, stásis → English stasis, and sýstēma → system; Sanskrit sthā- → Hindi sthā; Germanic *stāna- → English stand. English obstination (15th c., from Latin obstinātiō) is a rare doublet of obstinacy.
