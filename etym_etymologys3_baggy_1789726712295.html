The evolution of BAGGY is the biography of a sack that learned to criticise. Its base, bag, enters Middle English around 1225 as bagge meaning a small sack, recorded in Ancrene Riwle. The Oxford English Dictionary, followed by Etymonline, gives it multiple origins: partly a borrowing from early Scandinavian — compare Old Icelandic baggi bag, pack, burden, large bundle, even plump person, Norwegian bagge bundle, pack, plump animal, Swedish bagge ram — and partly from Old French bague, bagage pack, bundle, sack (fourteenth century), itself ultimately from the same Germanic stock that yielded bag. Crucially, OED explicitly rejects connection to other Germanic belly and bellows words as without evidence and dismisses a proposed Celtic origin as untenable; the further etymology of Scandinavian baggi itself is uncertain, with no secure Proto-Indo-European root claimed. The borrowing is thus lexical and commercial — packs carried by traders and armies — not inherited through regular sound laws from PIE.

Phonetically the word is almost unchanged for eight centuries: bagge to bag with short /æ/ and hard /g/, plural bags, combining freely (game bag fifteenth century, bean bag 1871, brown bag 1970). The adjective baggy, formed with the regular English -y suffix meaning having the quality of (as in bossy, leafy, roomy), is first securely attested in 1743 in R. Brooks for loose clothing (Merriam-Webster, OED, and Dictionary.com agree on 1743, with Dictionary.com noting productive bag + -y). Pronunciation /ˈbæɡi/ preserves the base vowel; comparative baggier and superlative baggiest double the g regularly. No Great Vowel Shift or French palatalisation complicates the chain; the sack keeps its shape in sound while losing it in sense.

  • From sack to sag: eighteenth-century tailoring gives baggy its classic sense: hanging loosely because too big or stretched, as in baggy pants, baggy sweaters. Nineteenth-century medicine extends it to flesh (baggy eyes 1867 for folds under eyes, baggy skin), nautical use to blown sails, and student slang to Oxford bags — the extravagantly wide trousers of 1920s undergraduates that turned ill-fit into fashion. OED records seven subsenses, all circling pouch-like looseness.
  • From cloth to criticism: twentieth-century reviewers map tailoring onto texts: a baggy novel, symphony, or argument is loosely constructed and inflated with inessentials, needing the editorial equivalent of taking in. Henry James's famous complaint about the baggy monster of the Victorian novel fixed the metaphor. The present sentence — predator baggy with misuse — is the lexical version: a word overstuffed with wrong applications sags like trousers overstuffed with legs, losing definitional crease.
  • Cultural logic: bags are humble, capacious, and shapeless by design; to call trousers, prose, or predator baggy is to accuse them of sack-virtues in a tailored world. Conservation writing loves the charge because precision is protection: a hawk miscalled predator by baggy habit can be killed by baggy logic. Bagginess is thus entropy — of wardrobes, books, and meanings — always acquired through wear, never intended.

Eight hundred years from Norse pack to predator's sag, BAGGY never leaves the sack behind; it merely finds new things to put in it, until language itself hangs loose.
