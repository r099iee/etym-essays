PIE *sed- "to sit" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) → Latin sedēre "to sit" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) → Vulgar Latin *sedicum "seat, resting-place, position" (reconstructed Late Latin nominal formation from sedēre; c. 5th–8th c.) → Old French sege, siege "seat, throne; (military) encamping of an army before a town" (attested 11th–12th c.) → Middle English sege "seat; siege, blockade of a town" (attested 13th c.) → English siege (form sege/seage earlier; spelling normalized 16th–17th c.) → SIEGES (plural) ◄ TARGET Note: the military sense developed within Old French, where the verb assiéger "to besiege" literally means "to seat oneself before" a town; the Germanic cognate branch (Proto-Germanic *sitjaną, English sit) shares the same PIE root but is a sister line, not an ancestor of siege.
