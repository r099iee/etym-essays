The biography of zealous begins, as far as the standard scholarly account can recover it, with a root meaning "to boil, seethe, or foam." Ancient Greek preserved this in the verb ζέω (zéō, "to boil, seethe"), and classical lexicographers together with most modern etymological references have traditionally derived the noun ζῆλος (zēlos) — "ardor, eager rivalry, emulation, jealousy" — from this image of liquid heat. The connection is intuitive: the earliest Greek sense of zēlos is a passionate heat that rises and bubbles over, transferred from the physical boiling of a pot to the spiritual ferment of desire and ambition. Modern scholarship is more cautious: Robert Beekes and others note that the deeper origin of zēlos is not entirely secure, some proposing a Pre-Greek or substrate source, while an old but unproven attempt links the same root with Sanskrit yáśas-, "glory, renown." The Indo-European reconstruction *yes- ("to boil, seethe") therefore stands as the traditional, textbook account rather than a certainty, and it is the one adopted by the major etymological dictionaries. From Greek the word traveled outward in several directions at once, and those sister developments are instructive. Within Greek itself zēlos produced the agent noun ζηλωτής (zēlōtēs, "emulator, zealous follower"), which the Greek New Testament applied to the first-century Jewish nationalists who resisted Roman rule; Latin took it in as zelōtēs, and English has used zealot since the sixteenth century, its history veering steadily toward fanaticism. In the Latin West zēlos was borrowed as zēlus, and from this stem two separate English words eventually descend — the pair we now know as zealous and jealous. The first route passed through Old French. Latin zēlus entered Old French as zel (modern French zèle), giving Middle English zel and modern zeal by the late fourteenth century. But the same root, with the Vulgar Latin z- becoming the Old French affricate j-, produced Old French jalous and gelos — the ancestors of English jealous, attested from the thirteenth century. Thus zealous and jealous are historical doublets: both descend from Greek zēlos, yet jealous drifted toward "possessive suspicion, envy," while zealous kept the older sense of devoted ardor. The pair remains one of the most striking etymological twins in the language — the same boiling Greek noun, split by two centuries of French pronunciation. English zealous itself was a bookish coinage of the sixteenth century, formed directly from Medieval Latin zelosus (recorded already among the Church Fathers) with the native suffix -ous. It appears in English texts from the 1530s onward, above all in Reformation-era religious controversy, where Catholics and Protestants alike accused each other of misplaced zeal. In the passage under study, Macaulay deploys the word to characterize Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, and Alboin — the founders of Germanic royal houses on Roman soil — as zealous Christians: passionate converts for whom the new faith was still a novelty and therefore a burning devotion. That historical resonance is exactly right, because zealous in the nineteenth century still carried the Reformation's ambivalence: admiration for fervor, suspicion of excess. Modern English retains both poles in "zealous advocate" (laudatory) and "overzealous" (reproachful), while zealot and fanatic have hardened into words of censure — a semantic journey of nearly twenty-five centuries from boiling water to burning conviction. KEY STAGES IN ONE LINE
  • Sound shift: Greek z- retained in Latin zēlus and the learned Old French zel (English zeal), but shifted to j- in the popular Old French jalos -> English jealous; the adjective adds the native suffix -ous.
  • Semantic drift: 'boiling heat' -> 'eager rivalry/emulation' -> 'devoted ardor' (zealous) vs. 'possessive suspicion' (jealous) — a classic doublet split.
  • Socio-cultural factors: Reformation controversy sharpened the religious sense and the ambivalence between admired fervor and censured excess; modern law institutionalizes 'zealous representation'.
  • Literary attestations: the word enters English texts from the 1530s; the Greek New Testament's Zēlōtai fix the militant 'zealot' line; Macaulay's 'zealous Christians' (19th c.) uses the full Reformation resonance.
