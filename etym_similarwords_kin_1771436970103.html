• Family: Similar because both denote one's blood relations; differs because family today means the narrow household unit of parents and children, while kin is wider and older, spanning uncles, cousins, and the far-flung branches of the clan.
  • Relatives: Similar because both name persons connected by blood or marriage; differs because relatives is the plain, modern, slightly bureaucratic catch-all, while kin carries an archaic, formal, almost legal dignity.
  • Kindred: Similar because both denote the body of one's blood relations; differs because kindred is the full noun of the same root, now distinctly archaic and literary, while kin remains in living legal and idiomatic use.
  • Kinfolk / Kinsfolk: Similar because both name one's family members; differs because kinfolk adds a folksy, regional, storytelling warmth, while kin is the leaner and more formal member of the pair.
  • Kith: Similar because both occur together in the fixed pair "kith and kin"; differs because kith means one's friends, acquaintances, and known people rather than one's blood relatives.
  • Clan: Similar because both denote a group related by descent; differs because clan names a unilineal descent group with a strong territorial or chiefly identity (especially Scottish), while kin is intimate, genealogical, and blood-precise.
  • Tribe: Similar because both can denote an extended group of related people; differs because tribe is a larger social unit usually seen from outside and often carries an anthropological or historical frame, while kin is personal and familial.
  • Relations: Similar because both name people connected to one by blood or marriage; differs because relations is everyday and covers both kin and in-laws, while kin carries a more formal, collective, and archaic weight.
