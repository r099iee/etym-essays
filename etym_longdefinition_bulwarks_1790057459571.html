bulwarks is the plural of the noun bulwark: strong walls, ramparts, or protections built or standing against attack, danger, or the sea — literally fortifications and ships’ sides, figuratively any person, law, or principle that holds a line: the bulwarks of a city, the bulwarks of freedom, a ship’s bulwarks. In your sentence — “an island raised basalt bulwarks against the ocean” — the sense is the coastal-defensive, quasi-architectural one: the island’s black cliffs act as sea-walls, ramparts thrown up by geology doing a fortress’s work against the waves. The plural matters: a system of walls, not one stone.

The image behind every sense is plank-work made into a wall — the etymological core of bole-work (timber rampart). From siege engineering the word climbs onto ships (the timber wall above the deck), onto cliffs (nature’s ramparts), and into politics and morals (institutions as walls). The constant is mass interposed against force: cannon, sea, tyranny, doubt. A bulwark never attacks; it endures, shelters, and defines an inside worth keeping.

  • Fortification rampart (oldest and core): the bulwarks of Antwerp; earthen bulwarks; to man the bulwarks; storming the bulwarks. A bastion, outwork, or town wall of timber, earth, or stone. Early modern siege warfare owns the singular and plural: Vauban’s bulwarks, Civil War bulwarks around London and Bristol. Implies massive solidity and perimeter defence. Near-synonyms rampart, bastion, breastwork; bulwark is the most monumental and the most likely to survive into metaphor. Collocations: raise / erect / storm / defend / breach the bulwarks.
  • Ship’s side above deck (nautical specialisation): to lean on the bulwarks; spray breaking over the bulwarks; the lee bulwarks. The solid timber or steel wall rising from deck level to rail, sheltering crew and cargo from sea and shot. Nelson’s navy lives behind oak bulwarks; modern sailors still call the solid side a bulwark and its freeing-ports bulwark ports. Implies weather-protection and fighting cover. Your sentence borrows this seaward orientation: cliffs as a hull’s walls turned inside out, island as ship.
  • Breakwater and sea-defence (your context’s branch): basalt bulwarks against the ocean; chalk bulwarks; the cliffs stood a bulwark against the Atlantic. Natural or built masses that take the sea’s force: cliffs, reefs, moles, jetties. Victorian voyage literature — Darwin, Melville, Verne — loves exactly your collocation: new land announced by black bulwarks. Often attributive-metaphorical rather than engineering-literal; the island did not build, it raised them by volcanism. Register literary-scenic, monumental.
  • Moral, legal, and political defence (dominant modern figurative sense): a bulwark against tyranny / communism / inflation / despair; the press as a bulwark of liberty; the family as society’s bulwark; NATO as Europe’s bulwark. Edmund Burke’s and 19th-century parliamentary rhetoric fix the pattern: bulwark of the constitution, of the faith, of freedom. Implies principled immovability; often mildly grandiose or editorial. The plural generalises: the bulwarks of democracy = its interlocking defences (courts, press, votes). Opposite: breach, fifth column, Trojan horse.
  • Personal and psychological shelter (intimate extension): she was his bulwark in grief; a bulwark of calm; his bulk a bulwark for the children. A person or habit that absorbs shock for others. Warmer than the political sense; implies steadfastness rather than masonry. Collocations: bulwark against loneliness / fear / chaos.
  • Grammar and usage notes: countable noun, freely pluralised; the plural bulwarks favours systems and perimeters (the bulwarks = the defences as a whole) while the singular favours single principles (a bulwark against). Governed prepositions: bulwark against (the threat) and bulwark of (the thing defended). Maintain the u after b (bul-, not bull-) and the w (-wark, kin to work). Adjective rare (bulwarked = furnished with bulwarks).

In short, bulwarks are walls that keep a world intact — timber, oak, basalt, or principle — and your island keeps its world the oldest way: black stone standing up to say the ocean comes no further.
