- 16th c. (1580s–1590s) — The Celtic noun bog was borrowed into Early Modern English from Irish/Gaelic bogach to describe Irish peat bogs; rapidly converted into a transitive and intransitive verb (to bog, to sink into a quagmire).
- 17th c. (1600s–1650s) — Attested in Jacobean literature and agrarian reports describing horses, pack-mules, and carriages sinking into marshlands; occasional early metaphorical uses describing theological disputes trapping the soul.
- 18th–19th c. (1700s–1880s) — Established across military dispatches and frontier travel literature to describe troops or wagons bogged in mud; the phrasal verb construction bogged down crystallized as an idiomatic expression for being overwhelmed by difficulties.
- Mid-20th c. (1940s–1970s) — Ubiquitous in bureaucratic, political, and educational discourse; notably immortalized in computer science classics like Kernighan & Ritchie's C manual (1978) to warn programmers against pedantic stagnation.
- 21st c. — Retains vibrant literal use in off-road motorsport and agriculture alongside universal figurative deployment in software development (sprint backlogs), legislative analysis, and cognitive science.
