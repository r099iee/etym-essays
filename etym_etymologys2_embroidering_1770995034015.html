Single root word: embroidering — the present participle of English embroider, a loan from Old French embroder, itself formed from en- ("in, upon") + broder, "to work the edge, to ornament with needlework," a borrowing from a Germanic (Frankish) source meaning "point, edge, spike." Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root, debated: *bher- (variant *bhrend- / *bhrent-), "to project, stick out, protrude; a point, spike, edge" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. The reconstruction is uncertain: the Germanic "point/edge" words are assigned by some scholars to *bher- and by others to a separate root; Pokorny groups Germanic *brord- under the "point, border, projection" family. State explicitly: this PIE stage is inferred, not directly attested, and is debated in detail. 2. Proto-Germanic *brurdaz / *brordaz, "point, spike, edge" — reconstructed; attested descendants include Old English brord "point, spike, blade of grass, weapon's point" (c. 8th c. CE) and Old Norse broddr "spike, sting, arrow, shaft" (Viking-age texts). 3. Frankish / Old Low Franconian *brozd (variant *bruzd), "point, edge, border" — reconstructed Germanic form brought into Gallo-Romance by the Franks' settlement in Gaul (5th–9th c. CE). 4. Old French broder (also brouder, brosder), "to decorate the edge of a garment with needlework; to embroider" — attested from c. 1100 CE (early French texts; the noun brodure "embroidery, border" appears in the 12th c.). 5. Old French embroder / enbrouder, "to ornament with needlework," formed with the prefix en- ("in, upon") — attested from the 12th–13th c. CE. 6. Anglo-French enbrouder → Middle English embrouderen / embrouden / embrowder, "to ornament with needlework" — attested from c. 1380 CE (Chaucer's works, including the Troilus, use forms of the word). 7. Modern English embroider (verb) + present-participle suffix -ing → embroidering, "decorating fabric by sewing patterns with thread; figuratively, embellishing a story" — the figurative narrative sense attested from the 16th c. onward — final node. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: (1) The PIE shape behind the Germanic point-word is not settled; *bher- "to project" is the common suggestion, but the link is inferential. (2) The popular account that broder derives from French bord "edge" (the word behind English border) is only half right: both broder and bord descend from the same Germanic stock of "edge/projection" words, but broder is not formed from the noun bord; they are parallel Germanic loans. (3) The OED notes the Germanic base of broder is "identical with that of OE brord," the point/spike word — the chain given here.
