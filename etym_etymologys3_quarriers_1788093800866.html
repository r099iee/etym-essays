From "four" to "square". The whole family goes back to the Proto-Indo-European numeral *kʷetwóres "four". Latin inherited it as quattuor "four" and derived quadrus "square" (a figure with four equal sides) and quadrum "a square". On these Latin built the verb quadrāre, "to make square, to fit, to square off" — the source of English quadrate, quadrant, quadruple and, through a long detour, quarry.

Why a stone-pit is called a "square place". Roman and medieval building stone was delivered as squared blocks (quadrati lapides); a quarry was, in the first instance, the place where squared building stone was got. Medieval Latin formed quarreria (also spelled quarer(e)a, quararia) — "stone-digging place" — from quadrāre plus the place-suffix -aria. Old French took it as quarriere (12th century; modern French carrière), and Middle English borrowed it in the 14th century as quarre or quarry for the pit and — in a telling twist — for the squared stone itself, so that a medieval writer could speak of "quarry" as a product as well as a place. The name "quarry tile" for a square floor tile preserves the same "square" ancestry.

Phonetic development. Latin quadrāre /kwaˈdraːre/ lost its medial syllable on the way to quarreria: the cluster -dr- assimilated to -rr-, a classic Romance simplification also visible in Old French carrière. English quarry (Middle English quarre) settled on /ˈkwɒr-/: initial qu- kept the /kw/ cluster, and the stressed vowel followed the usual English lowering of short a before r (RP /ˈkwɒri/). The agent suffix -ier — a Romance suffix of occupation, as in collier "coal-miner", cashier, grenadier — then produced quarrier, "one who works in a quarry", recorded in English from the 16th century and in local and trade use ever since.

The trade and its world. For most of history quarrying was seasonal, dangerous and local. The quarrier won freestone (sandstone, limestone), granite or slate from open faces using picks, wedges, malls and the plug-and-feather technique of splitting by expansion; he worked in gangs at the foot of the face, raised blocks with shear-legs and cranes, and moved them by sledge and wagon. Cathedral, castle and city building depended on the quarrier more than on any other craftsman — medieval masons were bound to work ready stone, and the quarrier was the indispensable first link from hillside to spire. In modern Britain the great slate districts of North Wales and Westmorland and the stone districts of Portland, Bath and York kept "quarrier"/"quarryman" as a badge of identity: the Penrhyn quarry dispute of 1900–1903, one of the longest and largest labour disputes in British history, was fought by some 2,800 slate quarrymen, showing how numerous and how organized the trade had become.

Semantic drift and the game. Meanwhile the entirely separate Old French word cuiriee — "the entrails of the slain deer thrown to the hounds", from cuir "hide, leather" — entered English as quarry in the hunting sense: "the prey pursued". The two homonyms have coexisted since the 14th century, a standing trap for the unwary that only etymology can sort out. "Quarrier" always points back to the stone-pit word, never to the hunt.

Modern life of the word. Industrial and romantic usage carried the family forward: the "quarriers" of the 19th century appear in topographical writing, labour records and dialect glossaries; the 20th century shifted the standard noun to "quarrymen" and pushed the verb metaphorically forward — "quarrying data", "quarried from the archives". In the 21st century "quarriers" reads as consciously old-fashioned or epic — which is precisely the register Tolkien needs when he speaks of the ancient dwarves, "the miners and quarriers and builders of old", heirs of a people whose whole civilization was carved from living rock.
