OBSCUREST (/əbˈskjʊərɪst/) is the superlative form of the adjective obscure, meaning “most obscure”: the darkest, dimmest, most hidden, least known, or most difficult to understand. English forms the superlative either with -est (obscurest) or with most (most obscure); obscurest is the older, literary, slightly archaic-sounding choice, the one a storyteller or poet reaches for when elegance and antiquity of register are wanted. In the card’s source context (“no songs had alluded to him even in the obscurest way”), the word intensifies to an extreme: not the faintest, dimmest trace of allusion anywhere.

Core sense — darkness and concealment: At bottom, to be obscure is to be covered over and so not clearly visible — hence “dark, dim, shrouded”. The superlative obscurest names the maximum of that condition.

  • Sense 1 — most dark or dimly lit: the oldest sense, now chiefly literary; “the obscurest corners of the cavern” means the deepest, darkest recesses.
  • Sense 2 — most difficult to understand: the intellectual sense; the “obscurest passage” of a philosopher is the one hardest to make out, most convoluted or abstruse.
  • Sense 3 — least known or famous: the social sense; the “obscurest author” is the most forgotten, uncelebrated, or nameless figure in a tradition.
  • Sense 4 — most hidden or remote: physically or figuratively out of sight; the “obscurest hiding place”, the “obscurest hamlet” at the edge of the map.
  • Sense 5 — linguistic nuance: in phonetics, an “obscure vowel” is one reduced toward a neutral, indeterminate quality; “obscurest” in this technical frame would mean most reduced or indistinct.

Grammar and register: Monosyllabic and many disyllabic adjectives take -er/-est; longer adjectives prefer more/most. Obscure, a disyllable from French, has always straddled the two systems. Obscurest, formed with -est, carries a distinctly archaic and poetic flavour — the flavour of older verse and of prose that aims at a legendary, song-like register, as in Tolkien’s high style. Writers today would almost always write “the most obscure way”; obscurest is a deliberate archaism, lending dignity and remoteness. It belongs to the same archaic-forming habit that gives us eldest, farthest, and utmost in place of the more analytic most old and most far.

Historical bridge: Latin obscūrus combined the prefix ob- (“over, toward”) with a root meaning “covered” (from the Indo-European source *(s)keu-, “to cover, conceal”), so the literal value of the word is “covered over”, hence “not in the light”. From the physical darkness of being covered, the word travelled naturally to mental darkness (things “covered” from understanding) and then to social darkness (persons “covered” from fame). The superlative obscurest simply pushes that triple obscurity — light, meaning, and renown — to its furthest degree.
