elicited: Drawn out, evoked, or coaxed forth into the open through skillful inquiry, deliberate stimulation, tactful prompting, or reasoned inference. The verb implies that the thing drawn out—a fact, a response, a reaction, a confession, a reflex—was latent, hidden, dormant, or withheld beforehand, and that it required active agency and artful intervention to bring it to the surface. In the literary context of Dream of the Red Chamber ("Yu-cun elicited from him that the visitor was a scholar from the capital"), the word carries a distinct tone of diplomatic subtlety and strategic intellectual probing: Jia Yu-cun navigates the conversation so deftly that he lures out a valuable piece of intelligence—the true background of the distinguished visitor—without the appearance of interrogating his companion. 1. Core senses
  • To draw out information or a verbal response: The most frequent modern use. A lawyer, journalist, teacher, or diplomat elicits facts, answers, admissions, or testimony from a reluctant, guarded, or forgetful source by skilled questioning. The emphasis is on skill and indirection, not force.
  • To evoke a reaction or response (behavioral/psychological sense): In psychology and physiology, a stimulus elicits a response, reflex, or behavior (an unconditioned stimulus elicits an unconditioned response; a question elicits a smile). This is the standard technical verb of experimental psychology.
  • To bring out an emotional or aesthetic effect: A performance, scent, or memory elicits admiration, tears, laughter, or nostalgia. Here the word overlaps with evoke but keeps its connotation of being drawn forth by a specific cause or trigger.
  • To draw out hidden facts through inference (legal/logical sense): In formal legal and philosophical usage, to elicit can mean to deduce or bring to light what is implicit in the evidence, premises, or a person's account—an intellectual extraction from what is latent.
  • Rare physiological sense: In older medical usage, to elicit can mean to draw out a fluid or substance from the body (as a tincture or essence "elicited" from a plant by steeping), a sense close to the literal Latin image of drawing forth.
2. Register, collocation, and usage notes
  • Collocations: elicit a response, elicit information, elicit testimony, elicit a reaction, elicit a smile, elicit sympathy, elicit laughter, elicit a confession, elicit an answer, elicit feedback, elicit a reflex, elicit support.
  • Register: Formal to neutral; standard in journalism, law, medicine, psychology, and academic prose, and increasingly common in everyday educated speech. It carries a connotation of deliberateness: the elicitor is acting purposefully, and the elicited thing does not come out by accident.
  • Transitivity and collocation pattern: Elicit is transitive and commonly takes a direct object naming the response plus an optional "from" prepositional phrase naming the source ("elicit the truth from him"). It is never intransitive.
  • Common confusion: Elicit (verb) is frequently confused with illicit (adjective, "illegal") because the two sound alike; they are unrelated in origin and meaning, and this near-homophony is a classic spelling error.
  • Contrast with extraction: Unlike extract, elicit never implies coercion, pressure, or mechanical force; the drawing-forth is achieved by finesse, by the right question at the right moment, rather than by compulsion.
3. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge The word descends from Latin ēlicere, formed from the prefix ē-/ex- ("out, forth") plus lacere ("to entice, lure, trap")—literally "to lure out." The root image is that of a hunter or fisherman enticing a hidden creature out of cover with bait: the quarry is present but concealed, and skill (not force) brings it into the open. This original picture survives intact in the modern meaning: information, like a shy animal, must be coaxed from its hiding place. The historical bridge from baiting animals to questioning people was completed in classical Latin itself, where ēlicere described both drawing groundwater up by engineering and coaxing testimony, omens, or answers out of reluctant sources. The word entered English in the 17th century as a scholarly borrowing, and its deliberate, artful connotations have only strengthened with the rise of modern psychology, where a designed stimulus elicits a measured response. In the Dream of the Red Chamber context, the word is the perfect verbal portrait of Yu-cun's intelligence: he does not batter information out of his companion, he lures it into daylight.
