- Late 6th c. BCE (c. 522 BCE) — Immortally carved in Old Persian cuneiform on the Behistun cliff face as Vindafarnā, honored as a supreme imperial loyalist and bow-bearer who saved the Persian realm.
- 5th c. BCE — Recorded by Herodotus in Greek historiography as Intaphrénēs, remembered as a tragic cautionary figure of aristocratic insolence whose downfall inspired the famous moral tale of his wife's choice.
- Classical Athenian Era (c. 441 BCE) — Adapted by Sophocles into drama (Antigone, lines 905–912), embedding the argument of Intaphernes' wife into the core canon of Greek tragedy.
- Hellenistic & Roman Era — Studied in rhetorical schools and philosophical compendia (Valerius Maximus, Plutarch) as a standard moral paradox regarding fraternal duty versus marital affection.
- 19th c. — Rediscovered in cuneiform decipherment by Sir Henry Rawlinson at Behistun, confirming the historical authenticity of Herodotus's Persian account.
- 20th–21st c. — Cited continuously across comparative literature, legal philosophy, and anthropological studies of matrilineal kinship as the prime historical archetype of sibling solidarity over marital bonds.
