Obsolete as an adjective means no longer used, practised, or needed because something newer, better, or more fashionable has replaced it; out of date, disused, superseded. When Ruskin writes that his strange ideas about kings are much obsolete, he means they are unfashionable in the present — true perhaps to Homer but no longer believed by his contemporaries. The core is loss of currency: a word, law, machine, custom, or belief that once functioned now does not, except as history.

The emphasis differs from mere oldness. An antique chair is old but may be valued; an obsolete computer is old and useless for its purpose. Obsolete implies functional displacement: the telephone rendered the telegraph obsolete; email rendered the fax largely obsolete; rifled artillery rendered castle walls obsolete. It can be neutral-technical (an obsolete part number), regretful (an obsolete craft), or contemptuous (obsolete prejudices).

  • Lexical and cultural sense (oldest English use): of words, meanings, spellings, customs, rites, or laws fallen out of use; e.g. obsolete sense of gay as cheerful, obsolete spelling musick, obsolete law against witchcraft. Dictionaries label senses obs. or obsolete when no citation after c. 1700–1800 exists. The bridge from physical wearing to linguistic loss is metaphor: words wear out like clothes.
  • Technological and military sense (dominant today): of machines, weapons, systems, software, or formats superseded; e.g. obsolete mainframes, obsolete tanks, obsolete file formats, obsolete phones. Here obsolete is often a formal classification: armies declare equipment obsolete to authorise disposal; manufacturers declare parts obsolete to end support. Near-synonym legacy is softer (still supported); deprecated means discouraged but working; defunct means dead.
  • Biological sense (technical): vestigial or rudimentary; imperfectly developed and functionally useless compared with homologous structures in other species; e.g. obsolete wings, obsolete stamens, obsolete eyes in cave fish. Darwin's usage. The bridge is analogy: organs fall out of use like words. Modern texts prefer vestigial, but obsolete persists in floras and faunas.
  • Ideological sense (Ruskin's use): of beliefs, ideals, or social forms regarded as outmoded; e.g. obsolete ideas about kings, obsolete notions of honour, obsolete deference. Often modified by much, largely, now, sadly. Connotation depends on stance: progressives use it dismissively (obsolete prejudice), conservatives elegiacally (noble but obsolete chivalry). Ruskin uses it ruefully: he half-believes the old king-ideal yet concedes the present laughs at it.
  • Verb and noun extensions: transitive verb to obsolete (chiefly U.S. business): to make obsolete, to phase out; e.g. the new chip obsoleted last year's model. Nouns obsolescence (state or process) and obsolescent (adjective: becoming obsolete) and planned obsolescence (design for early discard). The semantic bridge is causative: to obsolete is to cause obsoleteness.
  • Degree and register notes: gradable (more obsolete, most obsolete, much obsolete, wholly obsolete) though purists prefer absolute. Register is formal-neutral; colloquial equivalents are outdated, outmoded, old-hat, past it, dead. Pronunciation RP /ˈɒbsəliːt/; verb sometimes /ˌɒbsəˈliːt/ with final stress.

In short, obsolete marks the moment when the past stops working in the present — whether a word, a weapon, a wing, or Ruskin's hard-working king.
