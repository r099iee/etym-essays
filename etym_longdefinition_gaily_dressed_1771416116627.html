Gaily-dressed is a hyphenated compound adjective built from the adverb gaily (“brightly, cheerfully, showily”) and the past participle dressed (“clothed, attired”), and it denotes a person, or group, clothed in bright, colourful, festive, conspicuous clothing — with an implied atmosphere of cheerfulness and display. In the literary context of this card (a translated scene of Dream of the Red Chamber), the word occurs in the sentence “Some gaily-dressed maids were sitting on the steps of the main building opposite.” The detail is social as well as visual: these are servant girls of a great Qing-dynasty clan mansion, and the narrator notices their bright clothing because the dress of even the lowliest serving-girls was supplied and supervised by the household — bright, well-made livery proclaims an establishment so rich and well-ordered that its cheerfulness is visible down to the figures sitting on the steps. The “gaily” element contributes both colour and mood: the clothing is not merely chromatic but festive, and the household’s ambient prosperity is written in its servants’ bright dress.

Because the compound is transparent (fully compositional), its senses are best understood through its parts and their interaction:

  • Primary (literal) sense: clothed in bright, colourful, ornamental garments — festive dress, showy in a cheerful and unselfconscious way, with no hint of disapproval.
  • Secondary (affective) sense: dressed so as to express or induce cheerfulness — the bright clothing reads as the outward sign of a happy, lively, or celebratory mood or atmosphere.
  • Register as period flavour: the compound is characteristic of 18th- and 19th-century literary English, where “gaily” was a standard intensifier for bright and showy appearance; today it sounds literary, old-fashioned, and faintly nostalgic.
  • Component senses — gaily: (1) brightly, with vivid colour (“gaily painted”); (2) cheerfully, merrily, joyfully (“gaily singing”); (3) showily, flashily, with display; (4) thoughtlessly, light-heartedly, heedlessly (an 18th–19th century extension: “gaily oblivious”).
  • Component senses — dressed: (1) clothed, attired; (2) formally or elaborately attired (“dressed to the nines”); (3) with a modifying adverb, specifying manner of attire (well-, smartly-, shabbily-); (4) (of food) prepared with dressing; (5) (of a wound) bandaged; (6) (of leather, stone, hair, etc.) prepared, finished, or styled. In the compound, only the clothing sense is active.

Historically, the compound rests on the older, now largely lost sense of gay. English gay, borrowed from Old French in the 12th–13th century, meant “joyful, light-hearted, splendid, brightly coloured,” and the adverb gaily (from gay + -ly, attested from the 14th century) carried “brightly, showily, cheerfully” into the 18th and 19th centuries, where phrases like “gaily dressed” flourished in poetry, fiction, and fashion writing with wholly positive force. In the 20th century, as gay acquired its primary association with homosexuality, the adverb and its compounds receded from ordinary use; “gaily-dressed” is now deliberately archaic or period-flavoured, and its cheerful colouring must be read in its historical register. The semantic bridge is important: the word’s brightness is not merely visual but moral — bright dress as the outward sign of joy and prosperity, a linkage of colour, mood, and display that modern usage, with its narrowed sense of “gay,” has largely severed.

Against near neighbours the boundaries are subtle but real. Brightly dressed is purely chromatic — it asserts vivid colour with no claim about mood. Colorfully dressed adds variety of hue but stays emotionally neutral. Showily dressed or flashily dressed introduces display, but with a hint of vulgarity or excess that “gaily” lacks. Gaily itself, in its historical sense, contributes exactly the missing note: cheerfulness, lightness, a festive animation that turns colour into mood. The compound thus occupies a precise niche — bright, festive, cheerful clothing — that none of its modern near-synonyms captures without adding either coldness (brightly), neutrality (colorfully), or censure (flashily).
