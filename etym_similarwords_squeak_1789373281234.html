• Close shave: Similar because both idioms denote a hair's-breadth escape from mortal danger, failure, or ruin; differs because close shave derives from the barber's razor passing millimeters from human flesh (implying terrifying proximity to a cutting edge), whereas narrow squeak evokes the tight physical compression and friction of barely squeezing through a deadly aperture.
  • Narrow escape: Similar because both characterize emerging safely from a perilous situation where catastrophe was nearly inevitable; differs because narrow escape is a formal, neutral descriptor suitable for serious historical and legal prose, whereas narrow squeak is a colorful, colloquial, and slightly informal idiom carrying a lighter or more breathless emotional register.
  • Creak: Similar because both designate frictional acoustic sounds produced by mechanical movement or unlubricated contact; differs because creak denotes a low-pitched, prolonged, grating, and rhythmic groaning sound (such as heavy wooden stairs or rusty iron gates under strain), whereas squeak denotes an abrupt, high-frequency, shrill, and piercing peep.
  • Squeal: Similar because both describe high-pitched cries emitted by animals or vibrating materials; differs because squeal implies a continuous, drawn-out, high-volume screech of agony, delight, or locked tires, whereas squeak is typically shorter, sharper, more muffled, or smaller in scale.
  • Peep: Similar because both describe diminutive, high-pitched vocalizations of small animals or weak beings; differs because peep connotes delicate, soft bird-chirping or tentative visual emergence, lacking the intense frictional or mechanical connotations inherent in squeak.
  • Peach: Similar because both function in criminal underworld slang as verbs meaning to inform against one's accomplices to the authorities; differs because peach (historically related to impeach) carries a formal historical pedigree of legal betrayal and accusation, whereas squeak emphasizes the physical loss of nerve, singing under duress, or ratting out peers like a trapped rodent.
  • Scrape through: Similar because both phrasal verbs denote achieving success or avoiding disaster by the barest conceivable margin; differs because scrape through evokes the abrasive physical friction of skin or hull dragging across a barrier, whereas squeak through highlights the breathless, high-pitched tight fit of the transition.
