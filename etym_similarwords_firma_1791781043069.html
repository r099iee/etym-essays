• firm: Similar because it is the direct English descendant of Latin firmus and shares the core sense of solidity; differs because firm is a living English adjective with moral and commercial senses, while firma is a Latin form confined to one phrase.
  • solid: Similar because both describe ground that does not give way; differs because solid stresses density and three-dimensional substance rather than the Latin word’s suggestion of dependable support.
  • stable: Similar because both imply resistance to upset; differs because stable is about equilibrium and the absence of sudden change, whereas firma is about firmness under pressure.
  • steadfast: Similar because both can describe loyal, unshakeable character; differs because steadfast is chiefly personal and moral, while firma in English is almost always physical or figurative ground.
  • secure: Similar because both convey safety from disturbance; differs because secure implies protection against attack or loss, a legal and military nuance absent from firma.
  • fixed: Similar because both suggest something set and not variable; differs because fixed can imply rigidity or immobility, while firma implies strength that supports.
  • sturdy: Similar because both describe resistance to strain; differs because sturdy is concrete and often applied to build or constitution, not to ground or foundations.
  • unwavering: Similar because both can render the figurative sense of constancy; differs because unwavering describes attention or resolve, never the physical earth.
  • ferm: Similar because it is the archaic English legal doublet of firma in its “fixed rent” sense; differs because ferm belongs to tenure and taxation, not to solid ground.
