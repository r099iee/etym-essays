• channel: Similar because it names a long hollow through which something passes; differs because channel is the general, often natural word (a river channel, a communication channel) and carries none of the mechanical precision of a cut groove.
  • furrow: Similar because it is a long narrow trench, originally one cut by a plough; differs because furrows belong to soil and agriculture, and the figurative furrow is on a brow, not in a mechanism.
  • rut: Similar because repeated passage wears a track that guides wheels; differs because a rut is always damage and always pejorative, whereas a groove may be deliberately cut and functionally good.
  • trench: Similar as a long narrow excavation; differs in scale and purpose — a trench is dug for drainage, burial or defence and is far larger than a groove.
  • notch: Similar because it is a cut made to receive something; differs in shape, being a local V- or U-shaped indentation rather than a continuous channel.
  • slot: Similar because it is a narrow opening that guides a part (a coin slot, a screw slot); differs because a slot usually goes through the material, while a groove is cut into the surface.
  • gouge: Similar as a deep cut; differs because a gouge is rough, unintended or violent, with no suggestion of precision or use.
  • sulcus: Similar because it is the anatomical term for a groove or furrow, especially in the brain; differs by being strictly technical and Latin-derived, used in medicine rather than in ordinary speech.
  • routine: Similar because both name a settled, repeated pattern of behaviour; differs because routine is purely temporal and behavioural, with no image of a physical track, whereas grooves keeps the spatial metaphor alive.
  • pocket: Similar because both name the shared, cyclic feel that makes a rhythm cohere; differs because pocket is jazz slang for the placement of notes relative to the beat, while groove can also mean the pattern itself and has a much wider popular use.
