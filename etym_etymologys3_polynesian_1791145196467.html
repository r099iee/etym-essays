Polynesian is a learned neoclassical coinage, not an inherited folk word. Its story starts with Greek polus many + nēsos island. French scholar Charles de Brosses in Histoire des navigations aux terres australes (1756) proposed Polynésie for the many-island seas beyond Asia, contrasting with Australasia. The adjective polynésien/polynesian followed in French then English within decades.

Stages:

  • 1756: French Polynésie: de Brosses coins Polynésie — all South Sea islands. Broad sense, purely geographical, from Greek roots via Neo-Latin.
  • 1830s: Tripartition: Jules Dumont d'Urville in 1832 divides Oceania into Polynésie, Micronésie (small islands), Mélanésie (black islands). Polynesian is thereby narrowed to the eastern Triangle (Hawaii–Rapa Nui–Aotearoa). Racialised undertones enter: Polynesians depicted as lighter, nobler than Melanesians in French naval anthropology.
  • 1840s–1900: English adoption: Missionaries, whalers, and writers (Melville, Stevenson, Pierre Loti) popularise Polynesian chief, Polynesian beauty, Polynesian cannibal. Loti's Le Mariage de Loti (1880, Tahiti) is exactly the source milieu: French naval officer romanticises/exoticises Tahiti. Meaning: noble savage / alluring isles.
  • 1900–1950: Anthropological precision: Rivers, Malinowski's successors, Peter Buck (Te Rangi Hīroa), and linguists define Polynesian culturally/linguistically: common Proto-Polynesian ancestor c.1000 BCE, Lapita origins, epic voyaging. Adjective gains scientific criteria (language cognacy, social structure).
  • 1950–1990: Tiki commercialisation: In the US, Polynesian becomes kitsch — Polynesian restaurant, Polynesian Village, Polynesian sauce. Semantic bleaching to generic tropical exotic. Simultaneously, decolonisation and revival (Hōkūleʻa voyage 1976) reclaim Polynesian as pride term.
  • 1990–present: Identity term: Polynesian as self-identifier in diaspora (Auckland, Honolulu, Salt Lake, Sydney), census category, and academic Pacific Studies. Qualified by specificity: Samoan, Tongan, etc. preferred where known; Polynesian retained as umbrella.

Form history: Greek polus (many) → poly- combining form in English/French + Greek nēsos (island, of unknown pre-Greek origin) + -ia (region suffix) → Polynesia → Polynesian (-an inhabitant/adjectival). No sound shift beyond anglicisation: French /pɔlinezjɛ̃/ → English /ˌpɒlɪˈniːzɪən/. Spelling stable since early 1800s.

Literary attestations track perception: Cook's journals, Bougainville's Tahiti as New Cythera, Loti's lush Tahiti, Gauguin's paintings, Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa (1928), Michener's Tales of the South Pacific. From Enlightenment geography through colonial romance to Indigenous voyaging revival, the word moved from outsiders' label to insiders' umbrella, now used with respect and precision.

Note on nuance: the tripartite scheme is now recognised as colonial-imposed and genetically/linguistically imperfect (Fiji straddles Melanesia/Polynesia; Micronesian diversity), but Polynesian as linguistic subgroup remains robustly supported. Modern scholars keep the term while rejecting its racial baggage.
