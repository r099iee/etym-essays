Edifices is the regular plural of edifice, and its story is the story of the singular plus the stable English plural -s. The singular descends without break from a Latin compound for house-making, through French ecclesiastical usage, into Middle English learned vocabulary, where it has remained a formal, slightly elevated synonym for building ever since.

Deep root: Latin aedes — dwelling, room, temple — is generally referred by etymologists to PIE *h2eydh- to burn, whose derivatives mean fireplace, hearth, then house. The semantic path fire → hearth → house → temple is cross-linguistically common: the place of the sacred fire becomes the sacred building. Latin aedes in the singular often meant temple or shrine, in the plural aedes meant house, dwelling. To this was joined -ficium, a combining form of facere to make, do — itself from PIE *dheh1- to put, place — yielding aedificium, literally a house-making, i.e. a building, edifice. Vitruvius, Cicero and imperial inscriptions use aedificium for any constructed work, sacred or secular.

  • Late Latin to Old French: aedificium simplified phonetically to edifici, edifice. The diphthong ae- monophthongised to e- everywhere in Gallo-Romance. By the 13th century Old French edifice meant church, temple, large stone building, carrying ecclesiastical prestige.
  • Middle English borrowing (c. 1380s–1400s): Wycliffite and Chaucerian English borrowed edifice directly from French and secondarily from Latin in Bible translation: edify the temple, edifice of the church. Early senses were almost entirely religious — God's edifice, the edifice of the Church.
  • Early Modern secularisation (1500s–1700s): with Renaissance architecture theory — Palladio, Alberti translated — edifice broadened to any large, stately building: public edifices, Roman edifices, the edifice of the state. The plural edifices becomes frequent in travel writing and antiquarian description: the stupendous edifices of Rome, sprawling edifices of the Iroquois as in the source passage.
  • Phonetic stability: spelling normalised to edifice / edifices by the 18th century; pronunciation settled to /ˈedɪfɪs/ with plural /ˈedɪfɪsɪz/ inserting an epenthetic vowel before /z/ as with all sibilant-final stems.

Literary attestations: the King James Bible uses edifice marginally, preferring building and temple, but 17th-century divines love the edifice metaphor — the edifice of faith, the edifice of grace. Gibbon's Decline and Fall (1776) repeatedly calls baths, basilicas and palaces edifices. Nineteenth-century anthropology, including the source text, applies the word even to bark longhouses, elevating native architecture by using a classical term — sprawling edifices five hundred feet long — rather than huts.

Modern range: edifices today is formal and slightly bookish. Newspapers write of government edifices, colonial edifices, crumbling edifices; architects of stone edifices versus glass towers. The metaphorical edifice — the edifice of democracy, the legal edifice, the edifice of lies — is actually more common in the singular, while the plural stays concrete. The word never fully vernacularised; it retains a Latinate dignity that building lacks, which is why writers reach for it when scale or solemnity is required.
