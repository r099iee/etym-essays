Corns (noun, plural; singular corn) designates localized, circumscribed areas of thickened, hyperkeratotic skin that typically develop over bony prominences on the toes or feet as a protective yet pathological response to chronic mechanical pressure, friction, or ill-fitting footwear. Characterized clinically by a hard, conical keratin nucleus or central plug pointing inward into the dermis, a corn can exert agonizing pressure upon underlying sensory nerve endings in the subcutaneous tissue whenever weight or lateral compression is applied. In podiatric medicine, corns are classified into distinct clinical categories, primarily hard corns (heloma durum) on exposed, dry surfaces like the tops of the toes, and soft corns (heloma molle) in warm, moist interdigital spaces between the toes.

In the source text—Oscar Wilde's satirical masterwork The Remarkable Rocket—the word is employed as part of a razor-sharp, comic definition of human misanthropy and psychological projection: “‘A person who, because he has corns himself, always treads on other people’s toes,’ answered the Roman Candle in a low whisper; and the Cracker nearly exploded with laughter.” Wilde's Roman Candle captures an astute psychological truth: an individual tormented by their own secret, agonizing vulnerabilities and irritable resentments ("corns") frequently behaves with malicious disregard toward the tender feelings and boundaries of others ("treads on other people's toes"). The physical agony of walking on foot corns is transformed into a brilliant metaphor for hypersensitive egotism and malicious social aggression.

The term corns demonstrates remarkable historical polysemy, medical categorization, homonymic convergence, and idiomatic prominence across English lexicography:

  • Clinical Podiatry and Dermatology (Pathological Hyperkeratosis): In modern medicine, corns are recognized as forms of focal hyperkeratosis. Unlike diffuse calluses, a corn features a distinct keratinous core that funnels pressure down like an inverted pyramid. Hard corns (heloma durum) form over rigid toe joints rubbing against shoes, while soft corns (heloma molle) soften from sweat between adjacent toes, often complicated by maceration or fungal infection. Seed corns (heloma milliare) present as small, painless grains on non-weight-bearing soles. Treatment involves scalpel debridement by a podiatrist, salicylic acid keratolytic plasters, and biomechanical offloading.
  • Homonymic Division and Botanical Cereal Grain (Historical Convergence): It is vital to distinguish podiatric corns from the homonymic noun corn denoting cereal grain. While podiatric corn derives from Latin cornu ("horn"), agricultural corn traces to Proto-Germanic *kurnam ("grain, seed"). In British and Commonwealth English, corn historically denoted the principal staple cereal of a region (wheat in England, oats in Scotland and Ireland, barley in Scandinavia), whereas in North American English, it specifies maize (Zea mays). Nevertheless, popular folk etymology historically conflated the two because a podiatric corn resembles a hard, conical kernel of dried cereal grain embedded in the flesh.
  • Veterinary Pathology (Equine Podiatry): In equine veterinary science, a corn denotes a painful, localized bruise or contusion of the sensitive sole at the "angle of the sole" between the wall and the frog/bars of a horse's hoof. Usually caused by ill-fitting horse-shoes, stones, or improper trimming, equine corns cause severe lameness, heat, and tenderness, requiring remedial shoeing and poulticing.
  • Culinary Preservation and Granulation (Corned Beef, Salt Corns): Historically, individual coarse grains or pellets of salt, gunpowder, or sand were referred to as "corns" (from the Germanic root). In culinary food preservation, "corning" (as in corned beef) refers to curing meat with large granular pellets ("corns") of rock salt rather than fine brine.
  • Idiomatic and Metaphorical Vernacular: Podiatric corns gave birth to vivid English idioms:
    • To tread on someone's corns / toes: To trespass upon someone's sore spots, offend their pet prejudices, or violate their personal boundaries.
    • Corn-weather / Weather-predicting corns: A folkloric belief dating to early modern times that sudden throbbing in foot corns reliably indicates falling barometric pressure and approaching rain.
    • Corny (Modern Slang): An adjectival derivative emerging in early 20th-century American jazz vernacular to describe trite, old-fashioned, or unsophisticated sentimentality.

The semantic bridge connecting the podiatric affliction to animal horns lies in the physical property of keratin itself: Latin cornu denoted the hard, horn-like material found in animal horns, hooves, and claws. When medieval physicians and commoners observed the dense, impenetrable, horn-like crust forming on chronically chafed toes, they borrowed the Old French term corne ("horn, horny callus"), perceiving the painful growth as a miniature, hardened horn embedded in human flesh.
