Sped is the past tense and past participle of speed: moved swiftly, travelled fast, or hastened something toward its end. Where speed names rapidity in the present, sped seals it in the past — the journey already flashed by, the errand already hurried home.

In the source line — Our journey done, our errand sped — the word closes a ballad mission with a flourish: the riders have both finished the road and hastened the business entrusted to them, and the archaic bare sped tolls like a bell over completed duty.

Core meaning in detail: Modern grammar keeps two past forms: sped for senses of motion and dispatch (he sped down the lane; the letter was sped on its way), and speeded for the motorist's offence and for helped-along processes (he speeded when fined; the drug speeded recovery). The distinction is usage rather than law, but careful writers honour it: sped flies, speeded merely accelerates or offends.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Moved swiftly in the past (primary modern sense): riders, arrows, rumours, and hours that sped. The bridge is the parent verb's motion-sense: speed as velocity, completed.
  • Hastened or dispatched a task (source sense): our errand sped — the business urged to its end. The bridge is the older causative speed, to send prosperously forward, surviving in formulae like God speed you.
  • Prospered or succeeded (archaic, Old English root sense): in Malory and the Prayer Book men pray their enterprise may speed. The bridge is the original meaning itself — Old English spedan meant to succeed — of which velocity is only the later shadow.
  • Driven beyond the limit (traffic, now speeded): fined for speeding. The bridge is regulation: the twentieth century's motor laws split the participle, reserving speeded for the offence.
  • Passed quickly away (poetic): the years sped; summer sped. The bridge is time-as-traveller: the calendar rides as fast as the horseman.
  • Sent forth with blessing or urgency (dispatch sense): the messenger was sped to the front; letters sped by every post. The bridge is the causative use — to speed someone is to launch them prosperously — surviving in farewells and orders alike.
  • Rushed headlong to an end, fair or foul (moral-literary): the spendthrift sped to his downfall; the plot sped toward disaster. The bridge turns velocity into fate: what moves fast toward an end, good or ill, is said to speed there.

Register and connotation: sped is crisp, literary, and faintly archaic — ballads, prayers, and novels prefer it where conversation says went fast. It lends any errand gravity: things merely done end, but things sped fly to their finish with purpose. It is the past tense of duty done at the gallop — equally at home in a medieval chronicle, a naval dispatch, and a modern fantasy epic.
