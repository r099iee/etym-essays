Derivation chain for scarce (single root word): 1. Proto-Indo-European *(s)kerp- "to cut, pluck, crop, harvest" (c. 4500–3500 BCE) — the verbal root behind "plucking"; a debated variant *(s)ker- "to cut" is sometimes cited. 2. Latin carpere "to pluck, pick, gather, seize" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) — cf. Horace’s carpe diem, "pluck the day." 3. Latin excerpere "to pluck out, pick out, select" — prefix ex- "out" + carpere (Classical–Imperial Latin, 1st c. BCE–CE); also the source of English excerpt. 4. Vulgar Latin *excarpsus "plucked out, stripped bare, rare" (reconstructed, Late Latin, 3rd–7th c. CE) — the spoken past participle; the more regular Latin form excerptus shows that *excarpsus is a Vulgar reshaping (debated, but standardly reconstructed on the evidence of the Romance descendants). 5. Old Northern French escars "rare, scanty, frugal, stingy" (attested c. 10th–13th c.) — the change of the Latin -rp-/-ps- cluster to Old French -rs- is regular in such borrowings. 6. Anglo-Norman / Middle English scars, scarse "rare, insufficient, sparing" (first attested c. 1200–1300). 7. Modern English scarce (adjective/adverb) — target root word. The final -e is a Middle English scribal lengthening marker; the adverb scarcely (with -ly) developed separately in the 14th century. Note on debate: the link between Latin excerpere and Old French escars requires the reconstructed Vulgar Latin *excarpsus, which is not directly attested in surviving texts; it is standardly inferred from Old French escars, Italian scarso, and Spanish escaso. The PIE stage is likewise reconstructed. No attested stage has been invented or skipped.
