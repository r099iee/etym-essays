The story of lances starts on the Iberian frontier of Rome. Latin lancea meaning light spear or Spanish lance is first explained by Varro (1st c. BCE) as a Spanish word — hence probably Celt-Iberian. De Vaan, Walde-Hofmann and the American Heritage Dictionary agree it is likely of Celtic origin, with a tentative and disputed comparison to PIE *pleh₂k- to strike, flat. What matters is that Rome borrowed a provincial weapon-name along with auxiliaries who threw it: Spanish and Moorish lancearii appear in imperial armies.

Phonologically Latin /lankea/ simplified to Late Latin lanceare to wield a lance and Old French lance (12th c.) spear, lance-length — also a measure of a spear's length. The French form spread pan-European: Italian lancia, Spanish lanza, German Lanze, Middle Dutch lanse, Danish landse. English acquired it twice: as surname Lance (Luke Lance 1198) then as common noun launce late 13th c. for the couched horseman's spear used in the charge rather than thrown. Verb launcen to pierce with a lance follows c.1300 from Old French lancier via Late Latin lanceare.

  • 1300–1500: Knightly weapon: The lance is the 3–4 metre ash shaft with steel head couched under the arm for shock combat, centrepiece of tournament and chivalric identity. To break a lance means to joust; to win with flying lances is honourable contest.
  • 1500–1800: Cavalry and surgery: Heavy lancers persist (Polish uhlans, Napoleonic lancers) while light lances become pikes. In parallel, diminutive lancet (Old French lancette small lance, late 14th c.) names the surgeon's double-edged blade for bloodletting; verb to lance a boil (late 15th c.) means to open with a lancet. Shakespeare, military drill-books and barber-surgeon texts fix both senses.
  • 1800–present: Reproduction and metaphor: Lance-corporal (1786) folk-etymologizes obsolete lancepesade from Italian lancia spezzata broken lance = veteran, hence lowest NCO rank. Lance as fish (sand-lance/sand-eel) transfers by shape; oxygen-lance in industry burns metal; U.S. MGM-52 Lance missile (1960s–90s) revives the name for a surface-to-surface rocket. Verb senses broaden to any piercing thrust or sudden dash (to lance forward).

Cultural context explains durability: the couched lance made feudal cavalry dominant for three centuries, then survived as ceremonial and colonial-police weapon into the 20th century (British Lancers in India, Plains Indians adopting the lance after the horse). Launch and élan also derive from lancier to hurl, showing how a weapon verb became motion vocabulary.

In the source sentence, African villages making boats or lances illustrate craft specialization — iron spearheads forged by smiths for hunting and war, exactly the ethnographic present in which lances means thrusting spears generally, not just European cavalry arms. The plural here is generic: spears of any culture.
