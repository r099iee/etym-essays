Note is one of the great polysemous words of English: a single syllable that means, among other things, a musical tone, a written symbol, a brief record, a short letter, paper money, attention, and a distinguishing quality of sound or character. In "the rumble of his snoring changed its note," Tolkien uses the sound-sense: the snoring's note is its characteristic tone — the pitch and quality that identify it — and when the note changes, Bilbo knows the dragon is waking. The word is the perfect choice: a "note" is what you recognise something by.

Primary senses.

  • A musical tone or sound: a single sound of definite pitch in music; also the written symbol that represents it ("a note on the staff," "middle C is a note"). By extension, any distinctive sound — "the note of a cuckoo," "a bird's note."
  • A distinguishing quality or character: "a note of urgency in her voice," "the note of a place" — the perceptible character by which something is recognised; also a trace or hint of an emotion ("a note of bitterness"). This is the sense in Tolkien's sentence: the snoring's note is its identifying tone.
  • Attention, notice, heed: "take note of," "worthy of note," "to note" (verb) = to notice or observe. From "mark" → "what you mark in your mind."
  • A brief written record: "to jot down a note," "lecture notes," "field notes" — a short written reminder of something worth remembering.
  • A short letter or message: "a thank-you note," "a note to the milkman" — a brief informal communication.
  • A banknote: paper money — "a five-pound note," "a fifty-dollar note" (US: bill). From "note of hand," a written promise to pay.
  • An annotation or commentary: a footnote, an editorial note, a scholar's note on a text; also a formal diplomatic note — a written communication between states.
  • A promise to pay: a promissory note, "note of hand" — the ancestor of the banknote.

Secondary and idiomatic senses.

  • Of note: important, distinguished — "a man of note" (a person marked out by reputation; the "mark" sense again).
  • To strike a note / strike the right note: to express a particular mood or quality appropriately; "to strike the wrong note" is to jar.
  • To compare notes: to exchange observations.
  • To note (verb): to notice, observe, or record — "note that..." in formal prose.
  • In music: note values (how long a note lasts), whole note, quarter note; "to play the notes" vs "the music" (the written vs the sounded).
  • In finance: debit note, credit note, bond note, Treasury note — written instruments of account.
  • In academia: "the notes" of a lecture; "a note on..." = a short scholarly article.

Register and usage notes.

  • Note is a workhorse word in every register: everyday speech (a sticky note, a banknote, take note), formal prose (a diplomatic note, a note of caution), music, finance, and scholarship.
  • The "quality or character" sense — "changed its note" — is literary and precise: it names the audible or felt character by which something is identified, and it is a favourite of prose writers for sound, mood, and atmosphere.
  • Derived forms: noted (adjective, "famous, well-known" — marked out), notable, noteworthy, notation, notary (one who records), notify (to make known), notice — the whole family descends from the same Latin "mark."

Why the word means all this. The root is Latin nota, "a mark, sign, distinguishing token" — originally a brand or stamp used to recognise and identify things. Every modern sense is a "mark": the musical note is a written mark for a sound; the banknote is a marked paper promising payment; the footnote marks the page; taking note is marking something in the mind; a man of note is marked out by reputation; and the note of a voice or a snore is the acoustic mark that identifies it. "Changed its note" therefore means, at the root: changed its identifying mark — no longer the same sound, no longer the same dragon asleep.
