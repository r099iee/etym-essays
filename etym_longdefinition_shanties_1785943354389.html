SHANTIES: The plural of shanty, and a word with two distinct, etymologically unrelated meanings that have merged in English spelling and pronunciation: (1) crude, makeshift huts or shacks, especially rough dwellings built of scrap material in settlements of the poor; and (2) sea shanties — the rhythmic work songs sung by sailors to coordinate heavy manual labour aboard sailing ships. Sense in this context. The card's sentence — "and sing sailors' __________." — uses the second sense, the musical one: shanties are the work songs of sailors, and "sing sailors' shanties" is the plainest possible picture of maritime folk-song. The word "sing" disambiguates instantly: one sings shanties, one does not sing huts. The context belongs to the world of seafaring narrative, and the sentence captures the communal, labour-coordinating function of the songs — the sailors sang as they hauled ropes, pumped bilges, and worked the windlass, and the shanty was both rhythm and relief for the crew. Primary sense 1 — the crude hut. A shanty is a roughly built shelter or hut, typically constructed of whatever scrap material is at hand — planks, corrugated iron, sacking, mud — and inhabited by the poor, by workers in remote camps, or by settlers on the frontier. The word appears in "shantytown," the settlement of such huts on the margins of a city, and historically described the log huts of lumber camps and the shacks of squatters and migrant workers. This sense is the "crude housing" meaning, used in sociology, journalism, and history for informal settlements, and it carries a connotation of poverty, impermanence, and rough hand-made construction. Primary sense 2 — the sea song. A sea shanty (also spelled "chantey" and "chanty," especially in American and nautical writing) is a work song sung by sailors aboard merchant sailing ships, especially in the 19th century, to synchronize and lighten heavy group labour: hauling halyards, heaving the capstan, pumping the bilge, and trimming sails. The form is a call-and-response: a solo "shantyman" leads the verses, and the crew answers with a repeated chorus, timed to the pull or the heave of the work. Shanties are classified by their work: "halyard shanties" for hauling, "capstan shanties" for walking round the windlass, "pumping shanties," and "short-haul shanties." Famous examples include "Blow the Man Down," "What Shall We Do with the Drunken Sailor?," "Leave Her Johnny," and "Shenandoah." In modern popular culture the genre has been revived — most famously in the viral 2021 performance of "The Wellerman" — and "shanty" is now a familiar musical term. Polysemy and secondary meanings.
  • Architectural/sociological: "shantytown" (or "shanty town") — a settlement of crude huts on marginal land, home to the poor, the displaced, and migrant workers; the word is a fixed term in sociology and urban history (cf. the "Hoovervilles" of the Great Depression, though that American word was more common).
  • Historical lumber-industry use: in Canadian and American lumbering, a "shanty" was the log hut or camp where lumberjacks lived; the term entered English from Canadian French chantier, which meant a lumber camp or its hut.
  • Nautical/musical (dominant modern sense): "sea shanty," "sailors' shanties," "shantyman" (the lead singer), "shanty chorus"; the word is used in folk music, maritime history, and popular culture for the work songs of the age of sail.
  • Spelling variants: "chantey" and "chanty" (reflecting the French source chanter "to sing") are used especially for the song sense, while "shanty" has become the dominant spelling for both senses in modern English; the two words are homonyms, not variants of a single word.
  • Metaphoric use: "shanty" as an adjective of rough, impermanent construction ("shanty-town," "a shanty economy") extends the hut sense; no such metaphor attaches to the song sense.
The historical bridge — two words, one form. The two senses are etymologically unrelated. The hut sense descends from Canadian French chantier, "a lumber camp, a lumberjack's hut, a building yard," borrowed into English in the early 19th century as "shanty" (first attested in the 1820s). The song sense descends from French chanter, "to sing," from Latin cantāre, and entered English in the mid-19th century as "chantey" or "chanty" — "a sailor's song" — first attested around the 1850s–1860s; under the influence of the identical hut-word, and because sailors' songs and lumber camps belonged to the same rough working world, the spelling "shanty" took over the song sense as well. The result is a classic homonymic merger: two words of completely different origin — one from a French noun for a lumber camp, one from a French verb for singing — now share a single form, and only context (such as the verb "sing" in this card) tells them apart. Collocations and register. For the song sense: "sea shanty," "sailors' shanties," "shantyman," "shanty chorus," "to sing a shanty," "a rousing shanty," "folk shanty"; for the hut sense: "shantytown," "a tin shanty," "a lumber shanty," "shanty dwellers." The song sense is currently the dominant one in ordinary speech and has been dramatically popularized by the folk revival; the hut sense is now chiefly historical, journalistic, or sociological. Both senses are fully standard English, and the plural "shanties" is regular.
