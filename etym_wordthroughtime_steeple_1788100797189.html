- Old English stīepel (c. 800–1000): "any lofty tower or pinnacle" — fortress towers, the tower of Babel, cathedral pinnacles; the word simply means "the tall thing" (stēap "lofty" + -el).
- 13th–14th centuries: the church captures the word — "steeple" narrows to the parish church tower crowned with a spire; the great age of English spires (Salisbury, Lincoln) fixes the modern meaning.
- 16th–17th centuries: symbol of the established church — Quakers coin "steeple-house" (1650s) as a wry name for other Christians' meeting-places; Wren rebuilds London with fifty-one new steeples after the Great Fire of 1666.
- 18th century: "steeplechase" — the cross-country horse race to the distant church steeple — sends the word beyond architecture into sport.
- 19th century: "steeplejack" names the daredevil chimney-and-spire workman; the Gothic Revival rebuilds steeples; the steeple becomes shorthand for the picturesque village.
- 20th–21st centuries: the church steeple is the landmark of nostalgia and film — the small-town silhouette in every establishing shot; in professional use the word is precise architecture, everywhere else a warm emblem of home.
