CLATTERING is the present participle of the verb "to clatter". It denotes the production of a rapid, continuous succession of short, sharp, percussive sounds made when hard objects strike together or against a surface. Where a single impact gives one noise, clattering describes a whole storm of impacts — a machine-gun chatter of knocks, rattles, and small crashes so closely spaced that they fuse into a single busy, hollow din. The word is strongly onomatopoeic: its explosive /kl-/ onset mimics the sharp click of something hard striking, and the light, flapping syllables that follow suggest repeated beating, so that the very shape of the word performs the sound it names. It reports not one crash but an ongoing, staccato bombardment, and it emphasizes continuous, percussive motion rather than a single impact. PRIMARY SENSES
  • Making a continuous rattling noise — the core physical sense, of hard objects striking together rapidly: dishes and cutlery, hooves on cobbles, machinery, tools, or falling stones.
  • Moving while producing such a noise — the verb used of a thing travelling as it clatters: a cart clattering down a lane, a train clattering over points, stones clattering down a slope.
  • The sound itself (noun use of clatter) — the din of repeated impacts: a clatter of plates, a clatter of hooves, a clatter of loose machinery.
  • Confused noise or uproar — the general sense of a loud, busy racket, especially of human activity and talk.
SECONDARY AND METAPHORIC SENSES
  • Of speech and voice — to clatter on or clatter away is to talk loudly, rapidly, and emptily, the mouth performing the same busy, hollow percussion as struck objects; the word converges with "chatter".
  • Figurative busyness — the clatter of a city, a market, a kitchen: the sound standing metonymically for bustling, noisy, energetic life.
  • Dialectal and regional senses — in some dialects a "clatter" is a great number or quantity (a clatter of children, a clatter o' sheep), and in Scottish and northern English usage the verb can mean to gossip or to scold.
  • Mechanical and technical use — a "clatter" in engineering and medicine denotes an abnormal rapid hammering of parts or joints (valve clatter, the clatter of a loose bearing), carrying the same percussive image into jargon.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES Clattering was coined to imitate a sound, and its senses therefore hang together through the ear rather than through logic. Because the core idea is purely acoustic, the same verb has always served both for the noise and for the motion that produces it: the movement sense is not a distant metaphor but a simple metonymy — the sound stands for the thing making it. From the physical clatter of struck objects the word easily slid to the busy chatter of human voices and then to the general uproar of crowded life, because a crowd of people is acoustically a crowd of hard, small, repeatedly striking things — feet, tools, crockery, voices. In modern usage the word keeps its plain, domestic, energetic register; it is informal enough for everyday speech yet precise enough for literary prose, and its very plainness is what gives it force in high narrative. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In The Hobbit, clattering appears at the height of the landslide in the Misty Mountains. The word is the auditory pivot of the sentence: it converts mere movement into a cascading, accelerating noise, each falling stone dislodging more, until the whole slope is on the move. The tone is one of mounting, uncontrollable disaster — a machine of rock and gravity feeding on itself. Because the register of clatter is ordinarily light and domestic, its application to collapsing mountain rock is all the more unnerving: the vocabulary of the kitchen and stable is suddenly turned loose upon a catastrophe. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Rattle — a thinner, more mechanical, more evenly spaced noise, and the toy that makes it; clatter is lighter, more hollow, and suggests mere disorder rather than menace.
  • Clank — a heavier, duller sound of metal on metal, lower and more ponderous.
  • Clang — a single loud, resonant, metallic ring, prolonged rather than rapid.
  • Clash — a sharper, more violent impact, often with conflict or opposition implied.
  • Clack — a single sharp dry sound, or the loose chattering of machinery, without clatter's sustained multiplicity.
  • Bang — one loud explosive noise, not a rapid series.
  • Crash — a louder, more shattering impact, the breaking of a large object rather than a light repeated percussion.
GRAMMATICAL AND FORMAL NOTES As a present participle, clattering works as part of the continuous verb (the stones were clattering down), as an adjective (clattering wheels, clattering slabs), and as a gerund (the clattering of hooves). The base verb clatter is both transitive and intransitive; transitively it means to cause something to make such a noise. The noun is countable in some uses (a clatter of plates) and uncountable in others (the clatter of the city). The word belongs to a large sound-symbolic family — click, clack, clank, clang, clash, clap, chatter — all built on the same kl-/kl- onset, and none of them descended from a reconstructable Indo-European ancestor.
