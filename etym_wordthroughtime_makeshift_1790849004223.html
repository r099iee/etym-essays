- c. 1560–1620: Coined in Early Modern English as a pejorative agent noun (*make-shift*), designating a shifty rogue, trickster, or needy person who lived by cunning shifts, stratagems, and deceitful dodges.
- c. 1620–1750: Shifted from describing a deceitful person to denoting an expedient contrivance, provisional measure, or temporary trick resorted to in an emergency or pinch.
- c. 1750–1850: Fully consolidated as both a noun (a temporary substitute) and an attributive adjective (improvised, provisional, crude) in domestic, naval, and military contexts.
- c. 1850–1950: Widely used in industrial, wartime, and pioneering settings (such as trenches, frontier settlements, and emergency repairs) to describe rough-and-ready inventions that kept operations running.
- c. 1950–Present: Broadly applied across technical, philosophical (e.g., Roland Barthes's semiotic texts), political, and everyday language to describe any improvised, stopgap measure that stands between functional survival and collapse.
