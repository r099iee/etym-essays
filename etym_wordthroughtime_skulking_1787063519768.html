- c. 1200: Middle English “skulken” — to lie in wait, lurk, sneak; a hunting/ambush word borrowed from Norse; stealth without any fixed moral judgement.
- 14th–15th centuries: appears in romances and moral literature; the secrecy begins to carry the flavour of deception; register is vivid and colloquial.
- 16th–17th centuries: Shakespeare and drama fix the cowardice association — “skulking in corners”; military slang: “skulker” = a soldier who shirks battle; the word becomes openly contemptuous.
- 18th–19th centuries: standard in adventure and crime fiction for thieves and villains; used in political insults; “skulking” firmly = sneaky + guilty + cowardly.
- 20th–21st centuries: lives on in crime, thriller, gothic and fantasy writing (Tolkien, Smaug's taunt); perception: vivid, pejorative, slightly literary — the eternal shadow-lurker's word.
