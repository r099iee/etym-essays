One root chain leads from the Frankish ethnic name to the noun; the earlier Proto-Germanic source is a debated reconstruction, noted at the end. Chain — from the Franks to ENFRANCHISEMENT: - Proto-Germanic *frankō “javelin, spear” (reconstructed, unattested in direct form; 1st millennium BCE) → the name of the Frankish confederation of the lower and middle Rhine, whose identity as a spear-bearing war-band is the usual (though debated) explanation of the ethnic name. - Latin Francus / Franci “a Frank; the Franks” (attested from the 3rd c. CE) — the Latin ethnic name for the Germanic confederacy that conquered Gaul. - Old French franc “free, exempt, not servile; noble; open, sincere” (9th–12th c.) — because only the Franks enjoyed full liberty in Merovingian and Carolingian law, the ethnic adjective became the adjective of the free man. - en- (from Latin in- “into, onto”) + franc → Old French enfranchir “to make free, to manumit, to grant the freedom of a town” (12th–13th c.). - Anglo-Norman / Middle English enfraunchisen / enfranchisen “to release from bondage; to admit to the freedom of a city or borough” (14th c.). - + -ment (Old French < Latin -mentum, nominal suffix) → Middle English enfraunchisement / enfranchisement “the act of making free; admission to civic freedom” (15th c.). - Modern English ENFRANCHISEMENT “the granting of a right or privilege, especially the right to vote or political citizenship.” Note on debate: The Proto-Germanic source of *frankō is not securely connected to any PIE root; a link to PIE *preg- / *perg- “to strike” (because a javelin is hurled) has been proposed but remains unproven and is rejected by several authorities. The sense “free” did not come from an abstract PIE word for freedom; it arose within Old French from the ethnic name of the conquering free class. The noun's modern political sense, “the right to vote,” is a development of the 18th–19th century.
