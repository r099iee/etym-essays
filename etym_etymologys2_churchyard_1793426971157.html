[THE COMPOUND: churchyard]
Old English (c. 10th c.)
ciricgeard
‘consecrated enclosure surrounding a church’
↓
Middle English (c. 13th c.)
chirche-ȝerd / chircheyard
‘parish burial ground, churchyard’
↓
Early Modern English (16th c.)
church-yard
‘consecrated parish burial ground’
↓
Modern English (current)
churchyard
‘enclosed burial ground surrounding a church’

Note: As a true Germanic compound noun, churchyard unifies two distinct Indo-European etymological branches: the ecclesiastical loanword church (ultimately borrowed from Byzantine/Koine Greek kyriakón along the Danubian or Rhine trade routes into West Germanic) and the native Germanic agricultural term yard (descended from PIE *gʰerdʰ-, cognate with English garden, gird, and court). In both components, Old English initial palatal consonants underwent fronting and assibilation: the velar stops /k/ in cirice shifted to the affricate /tʃ/ in church, while the palatal glide /j/ in geard yielded modern English initial /j/ in yard.
