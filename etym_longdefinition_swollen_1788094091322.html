Swollen is the past participle of the strong verb "to swell" — to grow larger from internal pressure — and, even more often, an adjective meaning enlarged, distended, or puffed up beyond the normal size. It applies to living flesh (a bruised ankle, an infected gland), to rivers and floods (water swollen by rain — the exact use in the source context: "Great floods and rains had swollen the waters that flowed east"), to wood that has absorbed moisture and jammed a door, and, figuratively, to anything inflated: pride, numbers, budgets, bureaucracies. Morphologically it is the o-grade participle (swell–swelled–swollen, beside the archaic/dialectal past "swoll" and the modern slang "swole"), and it has served as an adjective continuously since Old English.

Primary senses

  • Of the body: enlarged by fluid, inflammation, or injury — a swollen ankle, swollen glands, a swollen tongue; the medical register includes "swollen joints", "swollen spleen", and the pathological noun "swelling" (a lump, an oedema).
  • Of water: raised and increased in volume — a river swollen by rain or melted snow, swollen by floods; the source context belongs here, with the participle used as a perfect verb: the waters "had swollen", i.e. had become swollen and risen.
  • Of wood and objects: distended by absorbed moisture — a swollen door that sticks, swollen window frames; also of anything saturated and puffed, from a swollen book to swollen grain.
  • Of quantities: increased, augmented, inflated — swollen ranks, a swollen population, swollen profits; a purely quantitative sense common since the 19th–20th centuries.

Figurative and idiomatic senses

  • Swollen with pride / anger / vanity / importance: the classic figurative uses, biblical in lineage — internal emotion as a pressure that distends the self past its proper shape; "swollen with rage", "swollen with self-importance" follow the same pattern.
  • Swollen head (also swelled head): 19th-century idiom for conceit, with the adjectival "swollen-headed" beside it; the head swells because the ego does.
  • Swollen rhetoric / prose / sound: literary uses in which the word describes language or music loaded beyond their measure — usually with the undertone of bombast and excess.
  • Swole: the 1980s–90s slang of African American Vernacular English and then bodybuilding culture, a conscious reborrowing of the participle's old variant form meaning heavily muscled ("he's swole"); the modern slang is the same word wearing a badge of gym culture.

The semantic bridges

The word's whole range rests on one image — growth by internal pressure — and each extension moves outward in a straight line:

  • Body: flesh swells with blood and fluid; the medical sense is the literal root, and "swollen" remains its plain everyday word.
  • Water: a river swells with volume; the same word describing the same physics of pressure and expansion.
  • Emotion: the proud heart "swells" like a wound or a wave; the internal pressure becomes moral — an excess of self — and a "swollen" ego is a self distended past its proper limits.
  • Quantity: numbers, budgets, and bureaucracies "swell" like floodwater; the inflation is impersonal but keeps the old undertone of dangerous, uncontrolled excess.

Grammar and register

Swollen acts as both participle and adjective. In perfect tenses ("had swollen", "has swollen") it is a verb form, with "swelled" as the alternative participle in some uses and in the fixed phrase "swelled head"; in attributive and predicative position ("a swollen ankle", "the river was swollen") it is an adjective, and it does not normally take -er/-est — comparison is periphrastic ("more swollen", "most swollen"). The register is neutral and universal: the word belongs equally to medicine, flood reporting, and literature. Its family completes the picture — the noun "swelling" (a lump or protuberance; an increase), and the colloquial noun and adjective "swell" (a wave; a 19th-century dandy; American "swell" meaning excellent) — three descendants of one ancient verb, each with its own career.
