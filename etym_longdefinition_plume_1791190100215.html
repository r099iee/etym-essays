Plume in the source sentence is a reflexive verb meaning to pride, preen, or congratulate oneself: to plume oneself on or upon something is to take conspicuous pride in a distinction, often with a hint of vanity. More broadly plume is both noun and verb gathered around feathers. As a noun it is a large ornamental feather such as an ostrich plume on a hat or helmet, a bird plumage collectively, a feathery ornament or shape such as a plume of smoke or water, and figuratively an honour, a new plume in one cap. As a verb it means to preen or dress feathers, a bird pluming its breast, to adorn with plumes, and reflexively to boast or pride oneself. The source, distinctions on which we plume ourselves, uses the ironic reflexive: philosophers plume themselves on reason, speech, or tool-use as uniquely human, but the author insists the difference from animals is only of degree, so the pride is puffed-up like a pigeon breast.

The bridge from feather to vanity is animal behaviour: a bird that plumes itself swells, smooths, oils, and displays, and humans who plume themselves do socially what birds do physically. Hence the word is never neutral about pride; it pictures pride as display. Ornamental feathers explain the honour branch: from knightly helmet plumes to academic and military cockades, a worn feather advertised rank, so winning one meant winning distinction, and the metaphor survives in every prize called a feather in the cap. Feathery shape explains the smoke branch: anything rising, spreading, and soft-edged like down, from cigarette smoke to volcanic ash to deep mantle plumes in geology, borrows the noun. Writing explains the pen branch: quills were goose feathers, so French plume still means pen, and English keeps nom de plume, a pen name, as a fossil.

  • reflexive pride, source sense: to plume oneself on tools, intellect, ancestry; to vaunt and congratulate; mildly satirical.
  • literal preening: birds plume themselves, cleaning with beak, fluffing, oiling; falconry and ornithology term.
  • ornamental feather noun: worn as rank, fashion, military distinction; Prince of Wales feathers, shako plume.
  • plumose shape: feathery rising column: plume of smoke, ash plume, mantle plume; visual metaphor of lightness.
  • honour or prize: to win a plume, add a plume to one cap; achievement displayed like a feather trophy.
  • writing metonymy: nom de plume, gens de plume, lively plume for style; from quill-pen origin.
  • verb of adornment: to plume a helmet, a horse, a hearse; to deck with feathers for ceremony.
  • tone: faintly mocking for the reflexive; admiring for plumage; technical for geology and hydrology.

To plume oneself is therefore to be a little comical and a little bird-like, in need of deflation, which is why essayists love the verb for puncturing anthropocentrism: man plumes himself on being the tool-using, talking, reasoning animal, until beavers, birds, and chimpanzees are shown doing nearly the same. Franklin jest, reported by Boswell, that man is a tool-using animal becomes, in this telling, one more feather in which we dress ourselves, and the author gently removes it.
