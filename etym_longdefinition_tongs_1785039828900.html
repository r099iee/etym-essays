TONGS (noun, plural in form) is a two-limbed gripping instrument, usually of iron or steel, in which two long arms are joined at one end (by a pivot, hinge, or spring) and used to grasp, hold, lift, or move objects too hot, heavy, or awkward to be handled by hand. The two arms form a scissor-like grip, and the tool works by the leverage of long handles over a short jaw. Tongs are the blacksmith's and kitchen's essential instruments: the smith's tongs grip white-hot iron at the forge, the kitchen's tongs lift food, coals, sugar cubes, or ice, and domestic coal-tongs feed the fire. The word is a plurale tantum — it is almost always used in the plural, like scissors and shears — because the tool is conceived as a pair of limbs working together.

In the passage from The Hobbit, Chapter 4, the word appears in the goblins' chant "Hammer and TONGS!" The line does not describe a tool but swears by one, like an oath or a war-cry. The collocation is an ancient idiom of the forge: to go "hammer and tongs" at something (recorded since 1708) is to work with all one's strength and violence, exactly as a smith grips the white-hot iron with the tongs in one hand while pounding it with the hammer in the other. Spoken by goblins, the exclamation binds their cruelty to the blacksmith's elements — fire, iron, and the relentless rhythm of beating metal — and the pairing of two heavy instruments, each with its own clang, reinforces the percussive, industrial clamour with which the goblins march their prisoners. The tone is at once oath, boast, and threat.

  • Polysemy 1 — the blacksmith's / forge tongs (primary concrete sense): the long-armed iron tool for holding hot metal at the anvil; the oldest and central sense, going back to Old English tange/tonge.
  • Polysemy 2 — domestic and kitchen tongs: coal-tongs for the hearth, fire-tongs for the fireplace, sugar-tongs and salad-tongs for the table, and modern cooking tongs for the grill or pan; all share the two-armed grip for grasping without direct contact.
  • Polysemy 3 — the idiom "hammer and tongs": since 1708, "to go at it hammer and tongs" means to do something with great energy, force, and violence — to fight, argue, or work ferociously; the bridge is the smith's full-strength working rhythm at the forge.
  • Polysemy 4 — specialized technical tongs: crucible tongs, soldering tongs, curling tongs (for hair), and ice-tongs are all named for the same two-armed gripping form applied to different materials and trades.
  • Polysemy 5 — figurative and dialectal: in dialect, "tongs" can name a biting or pinching instrument broadly (even a snake's or insect's pincers), and the word's literal "biting" etymology (see the derivation) keeps resurfacing in metaphor.
  • Polysemy 6 — plurale tantum grammar: like scissors, shears, and pliers, tongs is grammatically plural even when it refers to one tool: "the tongs are on the mantel," "a pair of tongs." This is not a separate meaning but a defining grammatical trait worth noting.

Why the secondary meanings developed: the entire family of tongs-words — forge, kitchen, fireplace, curling, crucible — rests on one physical idea: two levers that grip from a distance, keeping the hand away from heat or sharpness. The idiom "hammer and tongs" transfers the smith's violent full-strength labour to any fierce activity. And the word's deep etymology (from "that which bites") explains why the grasping tool is thought of as a pair of jaws: the tongs bite the object exactly as teeth bite food, a metaphor preserved in the word's very shape.

Distinctions from near-synonyms: pliers are smaller, precisely pivoted tools with a joint near the jaws and often cutting edges, built for fine gripping and wire-work; pincers are hinged like pliers but stronger, used for pulling nails or gripping tightly; forceps is the medical and surgical twin of the pincers; tweezers are tiny hand-held nippers for delicate work; nippers are cutting pincers; clamps and vices hold work fixed rather than grasping it from a distance. TONGS alone describes the long, relatively simple arms designed for reach, leverage, and distance from heat rather than precision.
