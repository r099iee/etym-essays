• epithet: Similar because both serve as descriptive, characterizing substitute terms applied to a person, divinity, or object; differs because an epithet is typically an adjectival qualifier or characterizing title attached directly to a name (e.g., "swift-footed Achilles", "cloud-gathering Zeus", "Māruti the wind-born"), whereas a kenning is an autonomous, figurative compound noun substituting entirely for the target noun without necessarily stating the underlying referent (e.g., "sky-candle" for the sun).
  • periphrasis: Similar because both employ roundabout phrasing or circumlocution instead of a straightforward, direct simplex word; differs because periphrasis is a broad rhetorical term encompassing any multi-word circumlocution (often prosaic, bureaucratic, or euphemistic), whereas a kenning is an explicitly poetic, conventionalized, and compressed metaphorical compound grounded in Germanic alliterative verse.
  • circumlocution: Similar because both avoid uttering a direct concrete noun by circling around its attributes; differs because circumlocution frequently carries a negative connotation of evasiveness, prolixity, or verbal redundancy (speaking in circles), whereas kennings are deliberate, condensed, aesthetically elevated artistic devices.
  • metaphor: Similar because both establish a figurative conceptual equation between two dissimilar domains (e.g., comparing the ocean to a roadway); differs because metaphor is a vast, universal trope operating across all linguistic structures, whereas a kenning is a specific historical, morphological genre of compressed compound metaphor structured into a base-word and a determinant.
  • metonymy: Similar because both substitute an associated concept or attribute for the primary referent; differs because metonymy relies on real-world physical contiguity, causality, or spatial proximity (e.g., "the crown" for the monarch, "the White House" for the executive branch), whereas a kenning typically relies on imaginative metaphorical analogy or esoteric mythological narrative.
  • synecdoche: Similar because both substitute a part, material, or attribute for the whole entity; differs because synecdoche operates strictly on part-whole or material-object relationships (e.g., "all hands on deck", "fifty sails" for fifty ships), whereas a kenning creates a new synthetic, multi-element figurative riddle (e.g., "wave-steed" for a ship).
  • antonomasia: Similar because both substitute a descriptive phrase or epithet in place of a proper name; differs because antonomasia specifically replaces a proper personal name with an epithet or title (e.g., "the Bard" for Shakespeare, "the Iron Lady" for Thatcher), whereas kennings predominantly replace common concrete nouns (ocean, sword, sun, blood) as well as proper names.
  • heiti: Similar because both are specialized categories of Old Norse poetic diction (*skaldskaparmál*); differs because a heiti is an archaic, uncompounded poetic simplex or non-metaphorical synonym (e.g., using the rare archaic word jór instead of the everyday word hestr for "horse"), whereas a kenning is necessarily a multi-part, figurative compound expression.
  • trope: Similar because both denote figurative language deviating from literal denotation for rhetorical effect; differs because trope is the overarching master category of classical rhetoric encompassing all figurative shifts (metaphor, metonymy, irony, hyperbole), whereas kennings represent a specialized morphological subset.
  • sobriquet: Similar because both provide descriptive alternative names; differs because a sobriquet is an affectionate, humorous, or familiar nickname bestowed on a person by contemporaries, whereas kennings are formal, elevated, and mythologically charged poetic figures.
  • euphemism: Similar because both replace a direct word with an alternative designation; differs because a euphemism is motivated by a desire to soften harsh realities, avoid taboo subjects, or shield sensitive listeners from embarrassment, whereas kennings are motivated by aesthetic delight, poetic ornamentation, and intellectual challenge.
  • allegoria: Similar because both convey figurative meaning beyond literal phrasing; differs because an allegoria is an extended narrative or sustained symbolic discourse sustained over an entire poem or text, whereas a kenning is a compact, localized compound expression embedded within a single verse line.
