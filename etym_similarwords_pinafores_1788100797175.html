• Aprons: Similar because both are protective garments worn over the front of the body and tied or pinned on; differs because an apron is a general term for any such garment, in any size and material, while a pinafore is specifically the childhood or dress-like descendant of the apron, with sleeves or straps and often covering the bodice.
  • Pinny: Similar because 'pinny' is simply the affectionate diminutive of pinafore and denotes the same garment; differs in register — 'pinny' is nursery and kitchen slang, informal and cosy, while 'pinafore' carries literary and historical weight.
  • Pinafore dress: Similar because the pinafore dress is the modern descendant of the pinafore — the sleeveless dress worn over a blouse; differs because it is a fashion garment cut like a dress (bib front, straps, full skirt) rather than a simple pinned-on apron, and is worn by women and girls as an outer garment in its own right.
  • Smocks: Similar because smocks are loose protective garments worn over clothing, historically by children and workers alike; differs because a smock is pulled over the head and gathered (smocked) at the yoke, with sleeves, whereas the classic pinafore is pinned or tied at the back and sleeveless.
  • Overalls / dungarees: Similar because overalls are protective garments worn over clothes, and children's pinafores shaded into overall-like garments in the 20th century; differs because overalls are trousers-and-bib workwear with leg coverings, while a pinafore has no legs and no trousers.
  • Tabards: Similar because a tabard is a sleeveless garment worn over clothing and open at the sides, visually close to a full-front pinafore; differs because tabards are heraldic, ceremonial or high-visibility work garments for adults, with no bib-and-strap child's cut.
  • Bibs: Similar because bibs are small protective garments pinned on infants' fronts, the smallest member of the family; differs because a bib covers only the chest and is for feeding, while a pinafore covers the whole front (often the bodice and skirt) and is for playing, working and schooling.
  • Jumper (American usage): Similar because in American English a 'jumper' is a sleeveless dress worn over a blouse — functionally the pinafore dress; differs because in British English a jumper is a knitted pullover, so the word is regionally treacherous, while 'pinafore' means the same sleeveless dress in both dialects.
  • Tunics: Similar because tunics are loose garment over-clothes, and school tunics overlap with pinafore tradition; differs because a tunic is a defined outer garment with sleeves or a shaped body, not a front-fastened protective layer pinned on.
