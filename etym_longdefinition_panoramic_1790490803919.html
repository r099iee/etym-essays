panoramic means affording or relating to an unobstructed, sweeping view in every direction, as if one stood at the centre of a painted rotunda and turned slowly around. In the source sentence the child looks out “through the panoramic opening of the four windows of a post-chaise,” the four carriage windows combining into one continuous travelling picture of England, Wales, and lowland Scotland. The adjective captures both optics and delight: a wide, clear, all-round prospect, made “more panoramic still” because the boy’s little bracket-seat in front let him see forward past the horses as well as sideways.

The word is built transparently on the noun panorama (Greek pan- “all” + horama “sight, spectacle”), coined in 1787 by the Irish painter Robert Barker for his giant 360-degree canvases shown in circular rotundas. The adjective panoramic is first recorded in the 1810s–1820s and at first means strictly “of or like a panorama painting”: a panoramic exhibition, a panoramic view from a hill, a panoramic rotunda in Leicester Square. From that technical cradle it radiated into general use, so that by the Victorian period any broad landscape, seascape, or city prospect could be called panoramic, and by extension any account, survey, or display that takes in a whole field at a glance.

  • Core visual sense: wide unobstructed view. A panoramic view, panorama window, panoramic terrace, or panoramic vista takes in a vast expanse without interruption — the view from a mountain summit, a clifftop, a skyscraper observatory. E.g. “a panoramic view of the Alps from the hotel balcony.” The semantic bridge from Barker’s rotunda is direct: what a panorama painting simulated indoors, real high places supply outdoors.
  • Art-historical sense: of the panorama medium. Panoramic painting, panoramic rotunda, panoramic draughtsmanship describe the late-18th- and 19th-century entertainment of enormous circular canvases (Burford’s Panorama, Cinerama’s ancestor). Ruskin, who uses the word here, grew up when travelling panoramas and dioramas were the cinema of the day, and his childhood eye was trained by them; his later art criticism constantly judges paintings by their panoramic truth to nature.
  • Photographic and optical-technical sense. Since the 1840s–1850s: panoramic camera, panoramic photograph, panoramic format, panoramic head, panoramic radiograph (dentistry), panoramic sunroof (motoring). A panoramic image has a markedly elongated aspect ratio (roughly 2:1 to 4:1 or a full 360-degree stitch), capturing far more horizontal field than a standard frame. The bridge is functional equivalence: the camera does chemically and digitally what Barker’s brush did in oil.
  • Cinematic and display sense. Panoramic screen, panoramic shot, panoramic aspect ratio describe widescreen projection (Cinerama, CinemaScope, IMAX) and slow horizontal camera pans that survey a scene. A “panoramic opening shot” of a Western establishes the whole frontier before the plot begins. The verb to pan is itself clipped from panorama, a neat boomerang: panorama → pan (verb) → panning shot.
  • Figurative sense: comprehensive, all-embracing survey. A panoramic account, history, novel, or review covers a subject in its entirety — “a panoramic history of the nineteenth century,” “the novel’s panoramic cast of characters.” This is the dominant modern extended use: breadth of coverage figured as breadth of vision. The bridge is metaphorical mapping from space to knowledge: to see everything at once is to know everything at once.
  • Tourism, motoring, and property jargon. Estate agents and travel writers overuse panoramic as a prestige adjective: panoramic windows, panoramic balcony, panoramic sea views, panoramic sunroof. Here it shades into pure praise, sometimes applied to views barely wider than average. Learners should note the inflation: in brochures panoramic often means merely “large and pleasant” rather than literally 360 degrees.

Grammatically panoramic is a descriptive adjective, normally attributive (“a panoramic view”) but freely predicative (“the view from here is panoramic”). It gradates readily — more panoramic, most panoramic — exactly as the source shows (“made more panoramic still to me”), since width of view is scalar. The adverb is panoramically (“the restaurant is panoramically situated”). Register is neutral-to-elevated: at home in guidebooks, criticism, technical manuals, and motoring brochures, never slangy. Connotation is strongly positive — openness, mastery, freedom — except when used sarcastically of bloated surveys (“yet another panoramic 900-page biography”). To call the post-chaise windows panoramic is thus both literal (four windows ≈ all-round vision) and emotional: the child’s whole world arriving as one continuous delightful picture.
