The verb explicate carries an extraordinary etymological lineage rooted in the tactile imagery of weaving, papyrus scrolls, Renaissance scholarship, 20th-century literary theory, and analytic philosophy.

1. Proto-Indo-European Root and Latin Verb Family:
The ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *pleḱ-, meaning "to plait, fold, weave, twine, or braid." This root was exceptionally productive across the Indo-European languages:

  • Greek: *plekein* ("to plait, braid") and *plektos* ("twisted, complex").
  • Germanic: Proto-Germanic **flahtaz* (Old English *fleohtan*, modern English *plait*, *flax*, and *fold*).
  • Latin: The primary verb *plicare* ("to fold, roll up, bend"), which produced a vast family of English words including *apply* (*ad-plicare*, fold toward), *complicate* (*com-plicare*, fold together), *implicate* (*in-plicare*, fold within), *replicate* (*re-plicare*, fold back), *supplicate* (*sub-plicare*, bend down from under), and *duplicity* (*duo-plex*, twofold).

2. Prefixation and the Material Imagery of the Ancient Scroll:
Compounding *plicare* with the prefix ex- ("out, away from") yielded Latin explicare ("to unfold, unroll, spread out, disentangle"):

  • The Papyrus Scroll (*Volumen*): In ancient Rome, books were written on continuous papyrus or parchment rolls. To read a text, one literally had to *explicare* (unroll) the scroll. At the end of medieval manuscripts, scribes traditionally inscribed the colophon Explicit (short for explicitus est liber, "the book is completely unrolled/finished"), which became the origin of the term *explicit*.
  • Metaphorical Unfolding in Classical Authors: Roman writers such as Cicero, Virgil, and Livy transitioned the physical act of unrolling a scroll to the intellectual realm: *explicare rationem* meant to unfold and explain a line of reasoning or narrative.

3. Renaissance Entry into English:
The word was borrowed into Tudor English in the 1530s directly from the Latin past participial stem explicātus:

  • Scholarly and Theological Explication: During the 16th and 17th centuries, *explicate* became a favored term among humanist scholars, jurists, and theologians for the rigorous exposition of dense biblical passages, legal codes, and scientific treatises.

4. 20th-Century Pedagogical and Philosophical Climax:
In the modern era, the term achieved iconic status across two major intellectual traditions:

  • French Explication de texte & New Criticism: The French educational system established *l'explication de texte* as the standard rigorous method for teaching literature. This was adopted by the Anglo-American New Critics (Cleanth Brooks, I.A. Richards), who turned *explication* into the art of close textual reading. In Richard Lanham's *Analyzing Prose*, the term represents this rich tradition of unfolding rhetorical style.
  • Rudolf Carnap and Analytic Philosophy: In *Logical Foundations of Probability* (1950), Rudolf Carnap formulated the philosophical method of *explication*—the precise, formal transformation of ordinary, inexact concepts into scientifically rigorous logical tools.
