- 13th–14th c.: 'skirt' (from Old Norse for shirt) is the lower part of a tunic or robe hanging from the waist — first worn by men; plain, functional garment.
- 16th c.: the garment becomes chiefly a woman's outer piece, and the metaphor extends — 'the skirts of a forest/town' are its outer edges, like a hem fringing the body of land.
- 17th–18th c.: the verb arises — 'to skirt' an area means to move along its edge and avoid the centre; strategic and evasive.
- 19th–20th c.: hemline politics make the garment a cultural marker, while the geography sense is still vivid; the verb gains its modern dodge — 'to skirt an issue'.
- Contemporary: the garment sense dominates; 'the skirts of X' is now poetic or dated ('outskirts' preferred for towns), and 'skirting around' a problem is a common idiom for dodging the heart of a matter.
