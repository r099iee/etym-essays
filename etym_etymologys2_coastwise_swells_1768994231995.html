ROOT WORD 1 — coastwise (a compound of two independent chains, given in isolation) CHAIN A — the noun COAST: 1. PIE *kost- "bone, rib" (a root attested chiefly in Italic and Balto-Slavic; c. 4000 BCE). Note: Greek ostéon "bone" is from a DIFFERENT root, PIE *h₃est-, and must not be conflated. 2. Latin costa "rib, side, wall" (classical Latin, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE), the "rib" of the body. 3. Late Latin costa extended to the "side of the land" — the coast, as in the medieval phrase costa maris, "the coast of the sea" (Late Latin, 3rd–6th c. CE). 4. Old French coste "rib, side, coast" (9th–12th c.), carrying the secondary "seashore" sense into Romance. 5. Anglo-French coste / Middle English coste "rib, side; seashore" (14th c.) — borrowed into English by 1300. 6. English coast (noun) "the seashore, the margin of the land beside the sea" (14th c. onward), and the derived verb to coast "to sail along the shore; to move without effort" (17th c.). 7. English coast + -wise → COASTWISE "along the coast" (17th–18th c.; first recorded in the nautical and shipping sense). The etymology of the compound's second element is CHAIN B. CHAIN B — the suffix -WISE: 1. PIE *weid- "to see, to know" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of "seeing," hence of "knowing" (a thing known is a thing seen). 2. Proto-Germanic *wīsan "manner, way, custom" (c. 200 CE) — a "way of seeing/doing" generalized to "way, manner." 3. Old English wīse "manner, way, fashion, condition" (attested 8th–11th c.) — the noun that underlies the suffix. 4. Middle English -wise / -wīse (dative) — the instrumental case became the adverbial suffix of manner and direction. 5. English -wise, suffix meaning "in the manner of, in the direction of, in the sphere of" (productive from the 13th c.: likewise, otherwise, clockwise, lengthwise; and from the 20th c., "in respect of," as in money-wise). 6. coast + -wise → COASTWISE "in the direction of the coast, along the coast." (This is the same final node as CHAIN A step 7.) NOTE ON DEBATE: The link of the noun wīse to *weid- "to see" is standard and secure; the semantic bridge is "manner = way of seeing/going." The modern colloquial "in respect of" use (-wise as in weather-wise) is a twentieth-century extension of the old manner sense and is uncontroversial. ROOT WORD 2 — swells: 1. PIE *swel- "to swell, to expand, to rise in a rounded mass" (c. 4000 BCE). Note: many handbooks posit TWO distinct PIE roots written *swel- — one meaning "to swell," another meaning "to burn, to smoulder" (the source of sultry, swelter); the two are formally identical and are kept separate by meaning. Our word descends from the "swell" root. 2. Proto-Germanic *swellan- "to swell, to grow, to expand" (strong verb; c. 200 CE). 3. Old English swellan "to swell, to expand, to rise" (strong verb, class III; attested from the 8th c., e.g. in the Lindisfarne Gospels and Ælfric). 4. Middle English swellen "to swell" (11th–15th c.), with the past participle swoll(e)n. 5. English swell (verb) "to grow larger, expand, rise in a wave" (14th c. onward). 6. English swell (noun) "a rise, an increase, an undulation of the sea" — the noun is identical in form to the verb, a Germanic trait; the specific oceanic sense "long unbroken wave" is attested from the 16th–17th c. (Shakespeare uses swells of the sea and of feeling). 7. English swells (plural noun) "broad, rolling waves of the sea" — SWELLS (the form on this card).
