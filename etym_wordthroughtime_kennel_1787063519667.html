- 14th century - Middle English kenel borrowed from Old North French kenil (Latin canīle, 'dog-place'): the kennel is where a lord keeps his pack of hunting hounds - an aristocratic, hunting vocabulary
- 15th-17th centuries - widens from the lordly hunt to ordinary dog-keeping: any dog-house is a kennel; the pack itself is 'a kennel of hounds'; the contemptuous transfer begins - a mean human dwelling is a 'kennel'
- 18th-19th centuries - the kennel industry is born: dog breeding, dog shows, and boarding establishments make 'the kennels' a commercial place where dogs are kept, bred, and exhibited; the kennel-as-hovel sense fades
- 20th-21st centuries - the pet era: modern kennels are heated, staffed, and marketed with grooming and 'doggy daycare'; the word travels from the king's hounds to the suburban pet's holiday home, while 'forgotten in a kennel' still carries the old cruelty
