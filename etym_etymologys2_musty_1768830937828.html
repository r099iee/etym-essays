CHAIN FOR MUSTY: PIE *meug- / *mewk- — "slimy, slippery" (reconstructed; possible ultimate source of Latin mūcus "slime, mucus"; uncertain — see note) ↓ Latin mūcus — "slime, mucus, mold" (attested Classical Latin), with the related verb mūcēre "to be moldy, musty, slimy" and the adjective mūcidus "moldy, musty" ↓ Vulgar Latin *muscidus — "damp, moldy, musty" (altered variant of Latin mūcidus, possibly influenced by mūstum "new wine, grape must") ↓ Old French moiste — "damp, wet, soaked" (attested 12th–13th c., from *muscidus); and Old French moisi — "moldy, musty" (past participle of moissir "to grow moldy," from the same base) ↓ Middle English moiste — "moist, damp" (attested 14th c.), with moisty — "damp, wet" (moist + -y) ↓ Middle English musty — "damp, having a smell of damp decay" (attested early 15th c.; the form was reshaped from moisty under the influence of Old French moisi "moldy") ↓ Modern English musty — "smelling of damp, mold, or age; figuratively stale or outdated" (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE: The ultimate origin of Latin mūcus is uncertain — it may descend from PIE *meug- "slippery, slimy" (cf. Greek μύξα mýxa "mucus, slime") or be a loanword of non-Indo-European origin; some authorities do not reconstruct a PIE ancestor for it at all. The shaping of English musty from moisty under the influence of Old French moisi "moldy" is the standard account (OED, Etymonline), but the exact path of the vowel change (oi → u) involves some uncertainty, and a few authorities treat the word as a direct borrowing of the Old French moisi family rather than a reshaping of native moisty; the stage "moisty" itself is solidly attested, and no stage here has been invented.
