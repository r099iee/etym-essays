- Latin ūsurpāre (source perception in learned English) — “to take into use, appropriate, exercise a right/name/possession”; technical-legal flavor of using what one does not fully own by right.
- Anglo-French / Middle English entry — English speakers receive usurp(en) as a high-register verb of illegal seizure, especially of power and privilege; associated with feudal and royal conflict.
- 15th–17th centuries — peak political-tragic charge: usurping crowns, titles, and regencies; audiences of chronicle and drama hear moral condemnation and constitutional anxiety in the word.
- 18th–19th centuries — remains a staple of historiography and political theory; also used for institutional overreach (one body usurping another’s functions).
- 20th–21st centuries — core political sense intact; figurative boom in workplace, technological, and psychological prose (AI usurping roles; anxiety usurping logic). Public perception: formal, accusatory, legitimacy-focused—stronger than “taking over,” less physical than “seizing” alone.
