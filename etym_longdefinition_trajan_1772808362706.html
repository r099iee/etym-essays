In Gibbon's celebrated opening catalogue of the Roman empire at its height, TRAJAN appears as the second name in the sequence "Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, and the two Antonines" — the five rulers under whose "virtue and abilities" the public administration was conducted "during a happy period of more than fourscore years." In this sentence Trajan is not yet the conqueror of Dacia or the builder of forums and columns; he is one link in a golden chain of adoptive, principled emperors, cited as evidence that good character, not good luck alone, sustained the empire's happiness. The literary force of the name is honorific and structural: Gibbon uses it as a synecdoche for disciplined imperial greatness. Yet any educated eighteenth-century reader would instantly supply the military colour Gibbon leaves implicit — the Dacian Wars (AD 101–102 and 105–106), the annexation of Armenia and Mesopotamia, and the Senate's official acclamation of Trajan as Optimus Princeps ("best of princes"), the only emperor to be granted that title. The primary, dictionary sense of TRAJAN is therefore a proper noun: Marcus Ulpius Traianus, Roman emperor from AD 98 to AD 117, the second of the Five Good Emperors, born AD 53 at Italica in Hispania Baetica (modern Seville province, Spain), adopted by the childless Nerva in AD 97, and the first emperor of provincial (Spanish) origin. He is remembered simultaneously as the greatest soldier-emperor since Augustus — the empire reached its maximum territorial extent under him — and as a capable civilian administrator, founder of the alimenta (state welfare for children), builder of the great Forum and the Column of Trajan in Rome, and initiator of the harbour complex at Portus and of the Via Traiana in southern Italy. He died in AD 117 while returning from the East and was succeeded by his cousin and adopted son Hadrian.
  • Proper name of the emperor: the core sense — Marcus Ulpius Traianus, reigned AD 98–117, second of the Five Good Emperors.
  • Optimus Princeps: the senatorial honorific "best of princes," effectively a title-of-state attached to Trajan, stressing his idealised standing as the model ruler.
  • Trajanic (adjective): stylistic and administrative label — "Trajanic Rome," "Trajanic architecture," used by historians and art historians for the period, building programme, and column-composition style of his reign.
  • Monuments as metonyms: "Column of Trajan," "Forum of Trajan," "Trajan's Market," "Trajan's Bridge" — the name shifts from the man to his lasting built works, especially in Rome and the Balkans.
  • Trajan (typeface): the modern typographic sense — the Trajan typeface (1994, Carol Twombly, Adobe) is a capital-letter font modelled on the lettering of the Column of Trajan, the classic example of Roman square capitals; "set it in Trajan" is a standing instruction in print and film-title design.
  • Traian (Romanian given name): in Romanian (and the wider Danubian world) the name was revived as a national given name and toponymic label, celebrating the Roman conquest of Dacia; Traian is a common masculine forename in Romania.
  • Emblem of maximum expansion: metaphorically "the Trajan of our age" means a commander or leader who extends frontiers; "Trajan's ambition" stands for a programme of maximal conquest and glory.
  • Romanist/classical cultural reference: in rhetoric and historiography Trajan functions as the benchmark of the good soldier-emperor, the antithesis of Nero or Domitian.
Semantically, the historical bridge from man to monument is direct: Trajan built so conspicuously — the Forum, the Column, the Market — that his name attached to the works and then to the lettering style cut on the column's base. The bridge from man to typeface is the purest modern shift: twentieth-century graphic designers, needing the definitive Roman alphabet, lifted it verbatim from the column's inscription, so that the emperor's name now denotes a font. The bridge from man to Romanian given name is national memory: Roman Dacia was conquered and colonised under Trajan, and the Romantic revival of the nineteenth century made his name a banner of Latin identity in Romania. Register: TRAJAN is everywhere a proper noun, capitalized and almost never generic; the derived adjective Trajanic and the typeface name Trajan are its most productive extensions. Collocations include "reign of Trajan," "conquests of Trajan," "Column of Trajan," "Trajan's Forum," "under Trajan," "Trajanic building programme," "Optimus Princeps." The word does not normally appear as a common noun meaning "conqueror"; that sense is only achieved through the metaphor "a Trajan," with the definite or indefinite article doing the work of conversion. Pronunciation is /tɹˈadʒən/ (TRAY-jən), with the stress on the first syllable; in Romanian the given name Traian is pronounced roughly TRY-an, a separate phonological adaptation.
