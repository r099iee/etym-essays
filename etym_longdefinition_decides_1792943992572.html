Decides is the third-person singular present of the verb decide — the form taken with he, she, it, or any singular subject, as in she decides quickly, time decides everything, or one vote decides the contest.

In the source sentence the verb carries its settling, determining sense: the speaker weighs two reassuring facts — Fatty willing to hold the fort, and Gandalf certain to know the road they have taken — and declares that this combination decides him, meaning it fixes his wavering purpose, removes his hesitation, and settles his choice to press on.

Core meaning in detail: To decide is, at bottom, to cut. The Latin ancestor decidere meant literally to cut off, and every modern use preserves that buried image: alternatives are severed and fall away until a single course stands alone. Deciding may be instantaneous, a snap of judgment, or prolonged, weeks of turning a matter over before the cut is made. Grammatically the verb is unusually versatile: one decides on a plan, decides against delay, decides to leave, decides between rivals, decides a dispute in somebody's favour, or simply decides, absolutely, with the object understood. The third-person form here matters stylistically: decides presents resolution as something an outside power performs upon the world, or upon the speaker, rather than something the speaker performs himself.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To make up one's mind; to reach a resolution (primary sense, from the late 14th century): she decides to accept the post. This is the everyday core: deliberation converted into commitment. The semantic bridge runs straight from the Latin cutting image — the mind slices through dithering and a single intention remains standing.
  • To settle a question, dispute, or contest; to be the factor that determines an outcome (the source sense): that decides me, one goal decides the final. Here the subject need not be a person at all; a fact, event, or circumstance can decide, doing the settling work upon human agents. The bridge extends the agent from the chooser to the cause: whatever cuts off the alternatives counts as the decider.
  • To give a legal judgment; to adjudicate (law, from the 15th century): the court decides the case in the claimant's favour. Judges are professional deciders, and the courtroom preserves the oldest Roman flavour of the word, since decidere was already a term of Roman legal procedure. The bridge is institutional: private resolution formalised into public verdict.
  • To win or settle a sporting contest at its climax (sport, 19th century): to decide the decider, a penalty shoot-out decides the tie. The noun decider, a match that settles a series, grows directly out of this use. The bridge carries the legal verdict onto the field: the final whistle is the judge, and the score is the sentence.
  • To determine the character or consequence of something (formal, general): early investment decides later success, this clause decides the meaning of the treaty. No conscious choice need occur; causation itself is framed as decision. The bridge generalises deciding from human will to any determining force, so that geology, economics, and weather can all be said to decide.
  • To choose between stated alternatives with on, against, between (constructions, early modern): they decided on war, he decided against appeal. Each preposition shades the cut differently: on lands upon the survivor, against names the rejected, between holds both blades in view. The bridge is grammatical rather than semantic, but it shows how thoroughly English has mapped the cutting metaphor onto syntax.
  • To be decided or undecided; participial and adjectival shades (modern): the matter is decided, the undecided voter. The past participle freezes the cut as a state of the world, and the negative undecided has become a political noun in its own right. The bridge turns the completed act into a lasting condition, which polling and punditry then trade as a commodity.

Register and connotation: Decides is neutral, clean, and authoritative — at home in boardrooms, courtrooms, and commentary boxes alike. Unlike chooses, which savours preference, or resolves, which savours willpower, decides savours finality: the gavel has fallen and discussion is over. In narration it often marks the hinge of a plot, the sentence after which nothing is the same, which is exactly the work it performs in the source passage.
