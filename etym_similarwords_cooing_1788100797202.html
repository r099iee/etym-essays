• Murmuring: Similar because both describe soft, low, continuous sonorous sounds; differs because “murmuring” is generic — streams, crowds, wind and indistinct speech all murmur — and carries no dove-image, whereas “cooing” is the specific birdlike ‘coo’ plus a tender tone.
  • Crooning: Similar because crooning is singing or humming softly and sentimentally, and lovers croon as doves coo; differs because crooning is melodic music (lullabies, sentimental radio singers of the 1930s–40s), while cooing is speech-like or bird-like sound without a tune.
  • Purring: Similar because purring is a soft continuous low vibration of contentment — cats, engines, satisfied crowds — and is used exactly like “cooing” for pleased murmuring; differs because a purr is a throat vibration without vowels or syllables, while a coo imitates a rounded two-note call; and purring began with cats, cooing with doves.
  • Gurgling: Similar because gurgling is the soft bubbling contentment-sound of happy babies and streams, overlapping with “cooing” in nursery usage; differs because gurgling involves air and liquid bubbling in the throat (and drains gurgle too), while cooing is a dry, vowelled call.
  • Chirruping (chirping): Similar because both are small soft bird-sounds, and chirrup is the nearest general-singing synonym; differs because chirp/chirrup names the short, bright, high notes of sparrows and finches, while a coo is the low, long, mournful double-note of doves — brightness versus softness, treble versus bass.
  • Clucking: Similar because hens cluck and people “cluck over” children, fussing sympathetically in soft throat-sounds; differs because clucking is broken, consonant-rich and often disapproving or fussy, while cooing is smooth, vowel-pure and approving.
  • Trilling: Similar because trilling is another soft rapid bird-sound and people “trill” with delight; differs because a trill is a fast repeated alternation of notes (or a rolled ‘r’) — musical virtuosity — while a coo is one slow falling note; register: sparkle versus warmth.
  • Gushing: Similar because both describe effusive admiration — one can coo over or gush about a baby or a film; differs because gushing is a loud, abundant, uncontrolled flood of praise, while cooing stays hushed and soft; gush is an out-pouring, coo is a murmur, and coo keeps the touch of the dove.
  • Sighing: Similar because a sigh is a long soft breathy sound of longing, relief or tenderness, near “cooing” in love-scenes; differs because a sigh is wordless breath, while a coo is a voiced vowel-sound; and sighs are often sad or weary, whereas coos almost never are.
