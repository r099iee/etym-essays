The word pedestals is a Renaissance compound of two ancient roots, and its biography is really the biography of two words that met in Italy. Each root deserves its own essay. ESSAY ONE — THE FOOT ROOT (*ped-) The story of pedestal begins, appropriately, underfoot. The Proto-Indo-European root *ped- meant “foot, step,” one of the most stable and widespread roots of the whole family, attested in nearly every branch: Greek poús, podós (whence podiatrist, tripod, octopus, podium), Latin pēs, pedis (whence pedal, pedestrian, biped, quadruped, impediment), and Sanskrit pád, “foot,” with Hindi pad, “rank, step,” and pag, “footstep.” In Latin the word was ordinary and anatomical — the foot of a person, an animal, or a thing: the foot of a bed, the foot of a mountain, the foot of a page — and its derivatives spread through the whole Romance-speaking world. Italian piede “foot” is the direct descendant, through the accusative form pedem, with the standard loss of final -m and the reduction of the medial cluster that give the modern Italian form. The semantic history of the root is a long story of “foot” doing duty far beyond anatomy. The foot is the base of the body, and so *ped- words everywhere took on the meaning “base, foundation, support”: the foot of a column, the foot of a page, the foot of a mountain. In English the borrowed words show the same drift — a pedal is pressed by the foot, a pedestrian goes on foot, a pedigree (from French pied de grue, “crane’s foot,” from the shape of the branching lines in a genealogy) is the “footprint” of descent, and podium (Greek pódion, “little foot”) is a raised foot-platform. The root also carried the idea of measured motion — expedite, from ex-ped-, “to free the feet,” is literally to get one’s feet out of the way; impede, “to entangle the feet,” is to hinder. Every one of these senses is a metaphor built on the primitive fact that the foot is what stands beneath, supports, and moves us. What matters for pedestal is the step from “foot” to “base”: when Italian piede joined stallo in the compound piedistallo, it contributed exactly this foundation sense — the block “at the foot of” the statue, the foot beneath the figure’s feet. The root had already, in architecture, lent its name to the podium, the continuous base of a temple or platform, and to the piede of a column (its lower part). The transfer from body-part to architectural support is ancient and universal; pedestal is simply a late Italian formalisation of it, a word that says “foot-stand” in its bones. The Indo-Aryan branch preserves the same primacy of “foot”: Sanskrit pád is both the anatomical foot and the step or pace, and from it Hindi pad came to mean “rank, office, step” — the foot as the unit of position in a hierarchy, a striking parallel to the way pedestal came to mean a rank of honour. In both languages the same root moved from the physical base of the body to the metaphorical base of social standing — the step, the station, the eminence on which one stands. Pedestals, then, inherits from *ped- not only the image of a block underfoot but the whole ancient equation of “foot” with “foundation” and of “foundation” with “status.” The sound history of the root is likewise instructive: the PIE voiced stop *d- is preserved in Latin (pēs, pedis) and Greek (poús, podós), while the Germanic branch, had it retained the word, would have shifted it by Grimm’s Law — but the foot-root did not survive in Germanic, and the “foot” element of pedestal came in from the Italic side alone. ESSAY TWO — THE STANDING-PLACE ROOT (*stel-) The second half of pedestal came from the north. The Proto-Indo-European root *stel- meant “to place, put, stand; a standing place,” a root of placement and stability that produced some of the most serviceable words in the European languages: Greek stéllō “to set in order, send,” Latin stella “star” (the “one set/placed” in the sky) and stolo “a shoot,” Lithuanian stóti “to stand,” and, in the Germanic world, a whole family of “standing place” words. The Germanic form was *stallaz, “a standing place, a position, a station” — the word behind English stall (the standing place of an animal in a stable), install (to place in a stall, hence to invest with office), forestall (to take a stand before), and the market stall, the place where a seller stands. The concept is consistent: the root names the fixed point on which something stands, the place that holds a person, beast, or thing still. The word’s journey to Italy is a small chapter of Germanic-Romance contact. Frankish, the Germanic language of the Franks who ruled Gaul, carried *stal “position, standing” into Old French as estal, “place, station, stall” — the word of a standing place, a position held. From Old French (or from Germanic directly, through the Lombard and other Germanic settlers in Italy) it entered Italian as stallo, “seat, stall, position, support,” where it became the ordinary word for a fixed place or a support — the place where something stands. When the Italian architects and stonemasons of the Renaissance needed a name for the block under a statue, they combined piede, “foot,” with stallo, “standing place,” to form piedistallo — literally “foot of a stall,” the foot-stand on which a figure’s feet rest. The linguistic interest lies in the two roots meeting across two language families: an Italic (Latin) word for “foot” and a Germanic word for “standing place,” united in an Italian compound that then travelled to France (piédestal) and England (pedestal, 1560s, during the vogue for Italian architecture). The Germanic element is not Latin in origin: stallo is a loanword in Italian, a relic of the centuries when Germanic peoples — Franks, Lombards, Goths — ruled and settled in Romance-speaking lands and left their furniture and stall-words behind. The same root, meanwhile, descended straight into English through the native Germanic line: Old English steall gives stall and install, so that pedestal’s “-estal” is a distant cousin of English stall itself — two Germanic twins, one that went south into Romance and one that stayed home, meeting again inside a single English word borrowed from Italian. The semantic development of the whole word is a study in rising. Literally, a pedestal is a “standing place for the feet”: the low block of stone on which a statue stands, or the base of a column, which raises the column above the ground and protects it. In Renaissance architecture and art theory the pedestal became a studied, ornamented member — plinth, dado, and cap — whose proportions the treatises discussed at length, and the word spread from architecture to furniture (pedestal tables, pedestal desks), to machinery (pedestal bearings), and to sculpture (the pedestal of a bust). Then, in the seventeenth century, the physical base began to stand for social elevation: to place on a pedestal meant to exalt a person to a position of supreme honour, and the image — a figure raised, isolated, and glorified on a block — carried both the admiration and the warning that pedestals can be toppled. In the passage the metaphor is pressed to its highest degree: the verse of Greece and Rome is “set on the highest of pedestals,” an eminence from which the home-grown English product, judged against it, is found wanting. The word’s whole career — from a stable for a horse, through a support for a statue, to the top of a mountain of reverence — is contained in that one phrase.
