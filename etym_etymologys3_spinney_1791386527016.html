Spinney began as a word for thorns and ended as a word for a small wood. That shift — from the vegetation itself to the place where the vegetation grows — is a common pattern in landscape vocabulary, and it explains why the word still feels slightly prickly and enclosed.

Latin spīna. The root is Latin spīna, "thorn, prickle", and by extension "backbone" (because the spine is a line of bony points). Latin poets use spina for the thorns of a hedge or bush, and the plural spinae can mean "difficulties, perplexities" — a metaphor still alive in English "thorny question". The derived collective spīnētum meant a thorn hedge or a place overgrown with thorns; it belongs to a productive Latin pattern for places named after plants, as in quercētum "oak grove" or arborētum "tree plantation".

French transmission. Old French inherited the word as espine "thorn" and formed espinei (masculine) and espinaie (feminine), "a place full of thorns, a thorny thicket". Middle English borrowed these forms in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as spenné, spenney and related spellings. The earliest English evidence is place-name and personal-name material; the modern noun meaning "small wood, copse" is first recorded in the 1590s, though the thorny sense is older.

Sound changes. The initial e- of Old French espinei was lost in English, as often happened with initial es- clusters (compare state from estat, spy from espier). The French -ei ending was remodelled to English -ey, giving spinney. The medial consonant varies in the records between -nn- and -n-, and the spelling spinny is a common variant. The pronunciation /ˈspɪni/ has been stable since the early modern period.

Semantic drift. The path from "thorny place" to "small wood" is a shift of prototype. A thorny thicket is a small, dense, bounded patch of vegetation; once such patches were recognized as a kind of wood, the thorniness became optional and the size became defining. The word also acquired a functional sense in estate management: a spinney could be a covert planted for game, which reinforced its association with bounded, managed land rather than wild forest.

Landscape history. The English countryside of the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries was a patchwork of fields, hedges, copses and spinneys. Parliamentary enclosure, the expansion of game shooting and the fashion for shelter belts all created small woods. Many spinneys were planted with hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel and oak; others were remnants of ancient woodland. Place names such as Burthonspynay in thirteenth-century Yorkshire show the element in use long before the modern literary noun.

Literary attestations. The word appears in rural poetry, dialect writing and detective fiction, where it often marks a secluded or slightly sinister place. It is a favourite of British nature writers because it is precise without being technical: it says "small wood" and also says "dense, thorny, old". In Sir Gawain and the Green Knight the related Middle English spenne occurs in difficult passages that scholars have variously interpreted as "hedge", "fence" or "thorny thicket"; the debate is a reminder that the word's early history is partly obscured by spelling and dialect variation.

Modern usage. Today spinney is marked as British and slightly literary. It survives in place names, in the names of houses and streets, and in conservation writing about small woods and wildlife corridors. It has no productive derivatives beyond the plural, and it is rarely used figuratively. Its persistence is a small example of how a landscape word can outlive the agricultural system that produced it, carrying a memory of thorn hedges and game coverts into the age of the nature reserve.
