mule — The story of the mule begins not with a root but with a hybrid. The animal itself is an artificial creation — the sterile offspring of a jack donkey and a mare — and its name is a hybrid too, belonging to no single language family.

The word for the animal appears across the ancient Mediterranean without any Indo-European ancestry. Latin mūlus, with a probable Greek relative mýklos "she-ass," points to a pre-Italic substrate word that arrived with the animal itself, brought into the Mediterranean world along with the practice of deliberate hybrid breeding. Language preserves the record of the mule's artificial nature: this is one of the few common European animal-names that cannot be traced to Proto-Indo-European, and the handbooks state it plainly — no PIE root. The mule, like its name, was made, not born.

  • Substrate origin (pre-Italic): *mūlo-/*mūslo-, a loanword of the ancient Mediterranean; no PIE etymology (OED, de Vaan).
  • Latin mūlus (2nd c. BCE): attested from Plautus; the word (and animal) spread wherever Roman agriculture and military logistics went.
  • Romance continuation: Old French mul/mule, Spanish mulo/mula, Italian mulo — the mule's vocabulary followed the mule's trade routes.
  • Germanic loans: German Maultier, Dutch muildier ("mule-animal") — the Germanic languages borrowed both animal and word, adding a native noun to the loan.
  • Middle English mule (13th–14th c.): the word enters English through Anglo-Norman along with the beast.
  • English mule (modern): work-animal, proverb, machine, slipper, smuggler.

From Latin the word traveled everywhere the mule did. Old French mul and mule gave Middle English mule; French mulet "a young mule" and muletier gave English muleteer, the driver of mule-trains; Spanish mulo and mula carried the word to the American frontier, where the mule became the beast of colonial and then rural labor — and Spanish mulato, "a young mule," was turned into the racial term mulatto, an offensive colonial coinage that remains a scar on the word's history. In the Germanic north the animal was named with the loan plus a native noun — Maultier, muildier, "mule-animal" — proof that the mule was adopted by, but not native to, the Germanic world. English mule absorbed the whole journey and added its own senses: the spinning mule (the cotton-spinning machine that "hybridized" the spinning jenny and the water frame), the mule as a backless slipper, the mule as a smuggler's courier, and the mule as any hybrid.

The semantic center of gravity is labor. The mule's famous stubbornness — its refusal to be rushed or driven into danger — was read as simple obstinacy, giving the proverbs "stubborn as a mule," "mule-headed," and the adjective mulish, a folk misreading that stuck. But the mule's endurance, sure-footedness, and hybrid vigor also made it the engine of nineteenth-century American agriculture, the patient work-animal of the sharecropper, the frontier farmer, the Confederate army, and the wagon trains — displaced only by the tractor in the twentieth century. In the card's sentence, "a lone dark husbandman pursuing mule and harrow down the rain-blown bottomland toward night," the mule carries all of that history in a single syllable: the very image of thankless, unglamorous, indispensable labor, the biological workhorse of the poor, moving toward night.

Socio-cultural footprint. No animal is more identified with unglamorous endurance in American memory: the pack mule of the frontier, the mule skinners of the wagon roads, the mule-drawn ploughs of the depression-era South, the proverbially obstinate mule of fable. The word's register is deliberately humble — a barnyard noun — and that homeliness is exactly what the poem exploits. There is no romance in "mule"; there is history, toil, and survival.



harrow — The biography of harrow is a scholarly confession: the word has no securely established root, and its most dramatic chapter is a collision with a second, unrelated word.

The deep ancestry is genuinely uncertain. The OED marks harrow "origin unknown," and the most probable account is that it is a North Germanic implement-word related to Old Norse herfi, borrowed into Middle English as harwe along with the tool itself in the thirteenth century. A farming technology, it seems, travelled with its name: the frame of iron teeth that breaks the plough's clods and levels the seedbed was adopted by English farmers complete with its northern word, and the continental family — Middle Dutch harke, Dutch hark, regional German Harke — shows the same implement's name spread across the Germanic coastlands. Those who push deeper propose an Indo-European origin in *ker- or *sker-, "to cut, scrape, scratch," which would make the harrow literally "the scraper" — but the connection is not established, and honest scholarship refuses to force it. The tempting Sanskrit karkata "crab," a favourite of popular etymologists, derives from a different hard-and-scratch root and is not a reliable cognate.

  • Attested stage (13th c.): Middle English harwe, borrowed with the tool; the OED's "origin unknown" is the honest terminus of the chain.
  • Hypothesis (flagged): a PIE *ker-/*sker- "to cut, scrape, scratch" origin — proposed but not established; shown as a hypothesis, not a root.
  • Continental family: Old Norse herfi, Swedish harv, Danish harve, Dutch hark, German Harke — the implement's name spread across the Germanic world.
  • The collision: Old English hergian "to harry, ravage, plunder" merged with the tool-word in Middle English, giving the verb "to harrow" = "to wound, lacerate, torment."
  • The Harrowing of Hell: the medieval doctrine and mystery-play of Christ's descent to free the dead — the phrase that fixed the figurative sense forever.
  • Modern senses: the literal tool-word retreated to rural, technical vocabulary; the figurative harrowing ("a harrowing experience") became the common, strong, literary sense.

The word's most dramatic chapter is that Middle English collision. Old English had hergian, "to harry, ravage, plunder," the verb of war and raiding; the tool-word harwe merged with it so completely that "to harrow" came to mean "to wound, lacerate, torment" — the Harrowing of Hell, when Christ harrowed the souls of the dead, and a harrowing experience, which tears at the heart exactly as the iron tines tear at the earth. Modern English thus has two lives under one spelling: the toothed frame that breaks clods, and the verb of anguish that rakes the heart. In the card's line both are active — the husbandman harrows the rain-blown bottomland with the tool, while the poem's bleak, harrowing solitude tears at the reader. One word, two ancestries, one exhausted field.

Register and survival. As an implement-word, harrow survives in farming manuals and historical fiction; as a figurative word it remains a standard, powerful adjective for ordeal ("harrowing," "harrowingly"). The tool's very obscurity feeds the metaphor's force: because modern readers rarely see a harrow, the image of iron teeth raking a field arrives fresh and violent when applied to the emotions. The card sets the two registers side by side — a literal harrow in a figurative poem — and the word does the work of both.
