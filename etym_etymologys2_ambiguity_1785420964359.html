AMBIGUITY unites two roots in a single Latin compound; each root word has its own linear chain. === ROOT 1: AMBI- (prefix) === PIE *h₂embʰi- "around, on both sides, both" (a well-attested root of the "both/around" set; Sanskrit abhi- and Greek amphi- are the classic reflexes, Latin amb-/ambi- another) → Greek amphi- "on both sides, around" (attested throughout Greek literature; source of English amphibious, amphitheatre, amphora) → Sanskrit abhi- "to, toward, over" (attested in the Rigveda; source of Hindi abhi- in compounds, and of abhibhu "overcoming") → Latin amb-/ambi- "around, both" (attested from the earliest Latin; the form amb- before vowels and some consonants, ambi- elsewhere, with the old Italic variant am-, as in amens "out of one's mind") → the prefix element ambi- in Latin ambigere "to drive both ways, be in doubt" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE) — the first element of this card. Note: the reconstruction *h₂embʰi- is standard (Pokorny, de Vaan); the Latin amb- shows the regular loss of the laryngeal and the Italic treatment of the labial. === ROOT 2: AGERE (base verb) === PIE *h₂eǵ- "to drive, lead, set in motion" (a major root of driving; Sanskrit ajati, Greek agein, Latin agere are its chief reflexes) → Greek agein "to drive, lead" (attested from Homer; source of English agony, protagonist, pedagogue, strategy, demagogue) → Sanskrit ajati "he drives, leads" (attested in the Rigveda, of driving cattle) → Latin agere "to drive, do, act, perform" (attested 3rd c. BCE onwards; the verb of action par excellence, source of English agent, act, action, agile, agenda, agitate, cogent, exact) → Latin ambigere "to drive both ways, wander about, dispute, be in doubt" (compound ambi- + agere, "to drive this way and that"; attested 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Latin ambiguus "shifting, doubtful, double-faced, of double meaning" (adjective, from ambigere; attested 1st c. BCE) → Latin ambiguitas "double meaning, uncertainty, doubt" (abstract noun in -tas; attested 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE) → Old French / Middle French ambiguite (attested 14th c.) → Middle English ambiguitee (Chaucer, late 14th c.) → English AMBIGUITY (spelling modernised; c. 1580s — exact form of this card) Note on the semantic bridge: the image is of driving — agere — a herd or a walker around — ambi — so that ambigere literally means "to drive round and round, to wander between two directions," hence to hesitate, dispute, or be in doubt; the noun ambiguitas transfers that two-way motion from the walker to the meaning, which is driven toward two readings at once. The whole chain is attested; nothing after PIE is reconstructed.
