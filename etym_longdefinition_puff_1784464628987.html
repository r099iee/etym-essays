PUFF (n. and v.) — a short, sudden emission of air, smoke, or vapour; the act of breathing hard and fast; a light, inflated pastry; and, figuratively, exaggerated or inflated praise. The word's many senses all hang from one central image: a short burst of air that swells or inflates something, and the related experience of breathing in short bursts under exertion. Primary Sense 1 — Verb: to breathe hard and fast; to pant
  • Definition: to breathe in short, quick, audible bursts, typically from exertion — running, climbing, carrying — or from heat and agitation.
  • Core examples: 'he puffed up the steep hill', 'she puffed as she carried the groceries', 'the old man puffed and panted'.
  • Character: the breath is heard — each puff is an audible little blast; the word fuses movement with breath, progress measured in pants rather than strides.
Primary Sense 2 — Verb: to emit air or smoke in short bursts
  • Definition: to blow out air, smoke, or vapour in small sudden clouds — 'to puff on a pipe', 'to puff cigarette smoke', 'the engine puffed black smoke'.
  • Also intransitive of things: 'the locomotive puffed', 'the chimney puffed smoke'.
Secondary and extended senses
  • Noun 'a puff': a short emission of air, smoke, or vapour — 'a puff of wind', 'a puff of smoke'; also the visible cloud itself.
  • To swell with air: 'to puff up', 'puffed pastry', 'puffed sleeves' — anything inflated by air; a garment 'puffs' out; pastry 'puffs' with steam in the oven.
  • Culinary: puff pastry (a light, flaky, inflated pastry, from the mid-18th century), a cream puff (a filled choux pastry), a 'puff' as a small light cake.
  • Beauty: a powder puff — the soft pad used to apply powder, so named because it 'puffs' the powder onto the skin (attested from the 17th century).
  • Promotional: 'puff', 'puffery', 'a puff piece' — exaggerated, uncritical praise designed to inflate the reputation of a product or person (from the image of inflating like a balloon with air; puffery attested from the 18th century, 'puff piece' modern journalistic).
  • Phrases: 'to be out of puff' (out of breath), 'huffing and puffing' (the loud breathing of effort or indignation), 'puffed up' (swollen with pride), 'puffed' (short of breath).
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridges
  • The verb is essentially imitative: the shape of the word itself mimics a short expulsion of air through the lips — say 'puff' and you make the sound. Old English already had pyffan, 'to blow in puffs'; Middle English puffen broadened the sense to panting, swelling with air, and figurative inflation.
  • The 'swelling with air' branch explains everything else: pastry puffs with steam, garments puff with air, faces puff up, reputations are 'puffed' by empty praise — every modern sense of the word is the concrete breath-image extended one step further.
  • The promotional sense is the most thoroughly figurative: advertising 'puffs' a product, inflating its worth with air, so that 'puffery' means hollow, inflated, uncritical praise.
Usage notes
  • Register: puff is everyday and vivid, with a slightly comic or vigorous flavour in motion senses ('puff along', 'puff and pant').
  • In Tolkien's sentence — 'Gandalf for certain this time,' he thought as he puffed along the passage — the verb fuses locomotion with audible breath, marking the cost in air and dignity of Bilbo's repeated trips to the door.
