- (Old English) - lengþu: the word is born as a purely concrete noun — the measure of how long a thing is, built from “long” with the -th suffix of width, depth and strength.
- (Middle English) - lengthe: the sense extends to time — “the length of his life” — as the spatial metaphor for time becomes standard.
- (14th century) - The idiom “at length” crystallises: from “to the full extent” it develops both “in full detail” and “after a long time, at last” — the sense of this card's sentence from The Hobbit.
- (16th–17th centuries) - “Length” enters measurement systems: a unit in horse racing (“won by a length”), cable-length at sea, cloth-lengths in trade.
- (18th century) - “Go to great lengths” (great effort) and the American coinage “lengthy” appear; the word begins to describe discourse as well as things.
- (19th–20th centuries) - Technical senses spread into phonetics (vowel length), film (feature-length) and geometry; “at length” remains a dignified literary way of saying “eventually”.
- (Today) - Length is one of the most neutral, high-frequency words in English, still carrying both its spatial and temporal senses, with the idiom “at length” preserved in formal and narrative prose — exactly as it appears in Tolkien's sentence.
