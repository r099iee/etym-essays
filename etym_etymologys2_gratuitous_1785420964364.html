PIE *gʷerH- "to praise, welcome, favour" (reconstructed; the root underlies Latin grātus and the Indo-Iranian words for "praise," though its exact laryngeal shape — *gʷerh₁-, *gʷerh₃-, or unlabialized variants — is debated) → Proto-Italic *grātos "pleasing, agreeable, acceptable" (reconstructed) → Latin grātus "pleasing, dear, agreeable; grateful, thankful" (attested 2nd–1st c. BCE) → Latin grātīs / grātuītō (adverbs) "out of favour, without reward or payment" (attested; the adverbial base that generated the adjective) → Latin grātuitus "done freely, voluntarily, spontaneous; free of charge, without payment" (attested in classical Latin, e.g., Plautus, and in the Roman jurists) → Old French gratuit "free, unpaid" (attested 13th–14th c.) → Middle French gratuit (same sense) → English gratuitous "given freely, without charge, granted of goodwill" (borrowed c. 1650s; early use in legal, financial, and theological contexts) → English gratuitous, semantic shift (18th–19th c.): "done without cause, necessity, or justification; uncalled-for" (modern dominant sense) → GRATUITOUS (final node; the bold target rootWord) Parallel cognate family of the root *gʷerH- in Indo-Iranian: Sanskrit gīr (गीर्) "praise, invocation, song of favour" and the verb gṛṇāti "he praises, welcomes, celebrates" — sister evidence that the root meant voiced goodwill in the wider family. Family note: from Latin grātus the same root produced gratia "favour, grace, charm" (→ English grace via Old French), grātulārī "to congratulate" (→ English congratulate), and the modern English gratuity (a tip), gratis (free), and grateful / gratitude — all keeping the root's core idea of favour freely shown.
