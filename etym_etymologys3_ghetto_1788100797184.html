Venice 1516: the foundry island becomes a noun. The word's entire history is compressed in its birthplace. On the northern edge of Venice, the island of Ghetto Nuovo — "new foundry" — had once held the republic's copper foundries: ghèto in Venetian dialect meant "foundry", "casting house", or the slag produced there, from the verb gettare, "to cast metal", which descends from Latin iactāre, "to throw, fling, cast", the frequentative of iacere, "to throw". When the Senate decreed on 29 March 1516 that the Jews of Venice — mostly refugees from the wars of the mainland — must reside together on this island, the name came with the place: the Jews were locked into "the Ghetto", and within a generation the word had detached from the island and become a common noun for any quarter where Jews were compelled to live. The name's accidental aptness — a casting-house, a place where metal is thrown into moulds — was felt immediately in the notion of a people "cast" out of the city. A rival etymology, popular for centuries and still repeated, derives the word from Hebrew gēṭ (גֵּט), "a bill of divorce"; it is a folk etymology with no documentary support, rejected by all modern linguists, though its survival shows how powerfully Jewish tradition reached for the word's meaning. The English form arrived in 1611 with the traveller Thomas Coryat, whose Crudities describes the Jews of Venice confined in what he calls "the Ghetto" — the word's first attestation in English.


Spread across Europe (16th–19th centuries). The Venetian model was copied with papal authority: in 1555 Pope Paul IV's bull Cum nimis absurdum confined the Jews of Rome to a quarter in the Sant'Angelo district beside the Tiber — the Roman Ghetto, walled until 1870; Frankfurt's Judengasse (from 1462, closed as a gated street) and Prague's Jewish Town were parallel developments under municipal law. Everywhere the pattern was identical — walls or gates, nightly locking, restricted professions — and everywhere the word, or its translation, followed. For three centuries the ghetto was the standard European solution to what contemporaries called the "Jewish question", and it became a fixture of travel literature, guidebooks, and fiction: George Eliot's Daniel Deronda (1876) stages a Frankfurt synagogue scene that leans on exactly this geography of European Jewish urban life. The ghettos were abolished piecemeal in the nineteenth century — Napoleon's armies opened many gates — and the papal ghetto in Rome survived, remarkably, until 1870.


The Nazi century and the American migration. Between 1939 and 1944 the German occupation rebuilt the ghetto as an instrument of extermination in Poland and the occupied east: the Warsaw Ghetto held more than 400,000 people behind eight-foot walls from October 1940, and Łódź, Kraków, Lublin, and hundreds of smaller towns repeated the pattern of enclosure, starvation, and deportation to the death camps. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943 gave the word its fiercest heroic afterlife. Simultaneously, across the Atlantic, American social science seized the same word for a different enclosure: Louis Wirth's The Ghetto (1928), St. Clair Drake and Horace Cayton's Black Metropolis (1945), and above all Kenneth B. Clark's Dark Ghetto (1965) applied it to the segregated Black districts of Chicago, Harlem, and Detroit, districts created not by decree but by redlining, blockbusting, restrictive covenants, and white flight. By the 1960s the referent had migrated from religion to race; the mechanism — imposed enclosure — was identical. This is the sense Wilde's word, in its Venetian context, unknowingly anticipates: the Ghetto was already, in 1888, a place-name that meant confinement.


Slang, hip-hop, and the present. From the 1960s, Black vernacular began using "ghetto" with deliberate pride and irony — the adjective sense of resourceful survival under constraint — flowering in the hip-hop era with "ghetto blaster" (the boombox), "ghetto fabulous", and "ghetto chic", a usage at once badge of authenticity and instrument of critique. But the same decades saw white slang empty the word of its history — "ghetto" meaning "cheap, shabby, jury-rigged" — a usage now widely recognised as offensive, since it trivialises three centuries of confinement and genocide. In careful contemporary usage the word is reserved for historical quarters, Nazi-era sites, and analytically described segregated districts, while the neighbourhoods themselves are increasingly named by their residents without it. No other common noun in English carries such a complete arc — from a Venetian foundry to world history — in four centuries.
