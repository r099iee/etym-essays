lank — the biography of a bend turned into a slackness. The word begins with a bend. The Proto-Indo-European root *kleng- meant “to bend, turn, flex,” and it described the supple action of a joint or limb — the way a body part gives, hinges, and yields. In the Germanic branch this idea of flexibility hardened into *hlankaz, a word for what is slender and flexible, and the modern English adjective descends directly from Old English hlanc, which meant “loose, slender, empty, gaunt.” The crucial semantic drift is already visible there: a bent and supple thing, stretched over its skeleton, begins to read as thin and empty — flexibility slides into leanness, and leanness into the slight pathos of being unfilled. Middle English narrowed hlanc into lank, “slender, thin, meager,” and the word attached itself to exactly the bodily states the sentence needs: a lank frame, lank hair, lank limbs — thinness without tone, thinness that sags. The later adjective lanky (lank + -y) softened the word into something rangy and almost affectionate, the long-limbed adolescent, while the root's “bend” sense survived in a separate sally of the family: the same *hlanka- “side, flank” gave English flank, shipped through Frankish into French (flanc) and back into English, and probably gave link, the chain-ring that bends and joins. Each of these is the original root bending in a different direction — the flexible body, the flexible side, the flexible joint. By the modern period lank had settled into a literary word of the body's emptiness: we speak of lank, greasy hair and lank, labour-worn frames, never of lank health. Its evolution has been a slow fall from “supple” to “thin” to “limp,” and the card's sentence completes that fall — a worker lank and stooped, stretched by *kleng- and folded by *steup-, two bend-roots governing one exhausted posture.

stooped — the biography of a downward fold. The root *steup- did not begin as a posture at all; it began as the very idea of bending — the crouch of an animal, the bow of a body, the curving of something under weight — and Proto-Germanic *stūpōną carried that idea straight down into Old English stūpian, “to bow down, bend, stoop.” In the earliest English the verb described a single, often deliberate motion: a man stoops to the ground, an animal crouches, a body bends in submission. Middle English stoupen kept the physical act and added the moral one — to stoop was already to lower oneself, to submit — and by the sixteenth century the falconer's stoop (the hawk's swift downward swoop) and the moral stoop (to stoop to deceit) were both established in English. What made the participle stooped a word of its own was the slow grammatical shift from event to state: a body that stooped once became a body that is stooped, and by the modern period the participle had become the chronic posture — “stooped with age,” “stooped with work” — a habitual, involuntary forward fold of shoulders and back that is the body's settled condition. The word's whole evolution, in short, is the same bend moved from a momentary act to a permanent state, and from a neutral motion to a witness of wear and endurance — which is exactly the respect the passage pays it, folding the worker's whole history into a single participle.

bolls — the biography of a swelling. The word's ancestor is the Indo-European root *bʰel-, one of the great swelling-roots of the family, which produced on one side the mouth-filling words for blowing and breathing (bellows, bellow, blow) and on the other the round things that swell outward — ball, balloon, bowl, follicle, and boll. Proto-Germanic *bullōn- gave Old English bolla, “bowl, cup, round vessel,” and the crucial Middle English step came when the round vessel of the table was recognized as the twin of the round vessel of the plant: bolle / boll was applied to the swollen seed-capsule that holds a plant's seeds, the same globe-shape transferred from cup to pod. In the cotton fields of the American South the word found its enduring home: the cotton boll is the round, bursting seed-capsule that holds the fibre, and from the eighteenth century onward it anchored a whole agricultural vocabulary — boll weevil, the beetle that devastated the cotton South in the early twentieth century, bollworm, cotton-bolling time — a regional, agronomic word kept close to the picker's hand. In a literary sentence bolls authenticates the world with botanical exactness, and its roundness is exactly what the passage uses it for: the swollen globe the lank, stooped fingers reach among. Where the other two words track the body's emptying and folding, bolls supplies the full, round object of the harvest — the same root-image of swelling that made the earliest vessels, now grown into the crop itself.

  • lank: PIE *kleng- “bend” → PGmc *hlankaz “slender, flexible” → OE hlanc “loose, slender, empty” → ME lank → modern lank; the fall from supple to thin to limp.
  • stooped: PIE *steup- “bend, crouch” → PGmc *stūpōną → OE stūpian “bow down” → ME stoupen → modern stoop; from a momentary bend to a chronic posture.
  • bolls: PIE *bʰel- “swell” → PGmc *bullōn- “round object” → OE bolla “bowl” → ME boll “round seed-pod” → modern boll “cotton seed-capsule”; the globe-shape moved from cup to pod to the cotton economy.
