Scot-free means without suffering any punishment or injury — escaping entirely without penalty, harm, or reckoning. It is an idiomatic adjective or adverb used almost exclusively after verbs of motion or release: get off scot-free, escape scot-free, walk away scot-free, go scot-free.

IN THE PASSAGE

Yu-cun's rhetorical outburst — 'Am I to understand that a man can be beaten to death and the murderer walk off scot-free?' — deploys the phrase as a moral and legal indictment. 'Scot-free' does not merely mean 'unpunished'; it insists that not a single reckoning, fee, or satisfaction has been exacted. The buried metaphor is fiscal: in medieval England a 'scot' was a tax or contribution paid by householders, and to be 'scot-free' was to be exempt from the levy. Yu-cun's phrase therefore pictures the killer leaving court, street, and conscience as if no account were ever opened — a social debt left unpaid. The tone is indignant and slightly theatrical, suited to a magistrate who both condemns injustice and may be performing outrage before an audience of ushers and runners.

PRIMARY SENSES

  • Without punishment: escaping all legal or moral penalty for wrongdoing — the dominant modern sense ('the guilty party walked away scot-free').
  • Without injury or harm: emerging physically or financially unscathed from an accident, danger, or adverse event ('he came through the crash scot-free').
  • Exempt from payment (historical/literal): free of the scot, i.e. not liable for a levy or contribution — the original fiscal sense preserved in medieval records and in phrases like 'scot and lot'.

HISTORICAL BRIDGE

  • Literal fiscal sense (12th–14th c.): free of the municipal or parish tax called the 'scot'.
  • Extension to other costs (15th–17th c.): free of any imposed payment, fine, or amercement.
  • Generalization to all penalty and harm (16th c. onward): the fiscal image bleaches out and 'scot-free' simply means 'untouched by consequence'.

COLLOCATIONS AND REGISTER

  • Collocations: get off scot-free, escape scot-free, walk away / walk off scot-free, go scot-free, come off scot-free.
  • Register is standard to literary; the phrase is a staple of journalistic outrage and narrative fiction, and rarely appears in technical legal drafting (which prefers 'without penalty' or 'with impunity').
  • It is almost always predicative after motion or release verbs and rarely used as a premodifier ('a scot-free offender' is possible but unusual).

BOUNDARIES WITH NEAR-SYNONYMS

  • Compared with unpunished, scot-free adds the idiomatic sting and the buried ledger-metaphor of an unpaid account.
  • Compared with unscathed, it stresses moral accountability and penalty rather than physical survival.
  • Compared with with impunity, it is less formal and less legalistic, though both imply escaping consequence.

WARNING ABOUT THE SCOTS

The phrase has nothing to do with Scotland. The 'scot' in scot-free is the old tax-word (Old Norse skot 'contribution'), not the ethnonym Scot. The false folk-etymology is common and historically wrong.
