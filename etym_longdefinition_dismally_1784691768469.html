Dismally is the adverb formed from the adjective dismal, meaning "in a dismal manner" — gloomily, depressingly, cheerlessly, or in a way that is wretchedly discouraging. Underlying it is one of the strangest histories in the English lexicon: in medieval usage a "dismal day" was a day marked in the calendar as unlucky, one of the evil days of the old almanacs, and a faint flavour of ill omen clings to the modern word even after the calendar lore has been forgotten. As an intensifier the adverb is most at home with failure — "to fail dismally", "to come off dismally" — but its full range colours situations with a cold, leaden, disheartening quality: the mood of grey skies, bad news, hopeless odds, and tasks that depress by their sheer dreary weight. The sense-spectrum of dismally:
  • Manner sense — gloomily, cheerlessly. "The rain fell dismally all afternoon": an action performed in a manner that is gloomy, dreary, or dispiriting.
  • Manner sense — in a wretchedly discouraging way. "The project ended dismally": an outcome so bad as to be disheartening, with a hint of ill-fatedness inherited from the unlucky "dismal days".
  • Intensifier of failure. "To fail dismally" — the common modern collocation in which the adverb measures the completeness of the failure as much as the manner of it; the failure is total, humiliating, and somehow preordained.
  • Attributive to a state or quality — "dismally real". The striking Tolkienian collocation in The Hobbit, in which the adverb modifies an adjective rather than a verb: the washing-up is "so dismally real" — so depressingly, unavoidably, fatefully factual. The adverb here underlines the cruelty of plain fact: reality itself, in its most ordinary domestic form, is revealed as a verdict.
  • Residue of ill omen. Inherited from medieval astrology, a faint sense of the inauspicious: the word keeps a whisper of the "dismal days", the unlucky dates of the old calendars, so that what is dismally done is not merely sad but fated.
In the literary context of The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the adverb is applied with precise comic effect to the adjective real: "The washing-up was so dismally real that Bilbo was forced to believe the party of the night before had not been part of his bad dreams, as he had rather hoped." The collocation is deliberately surprising — reality itself is rarely the object of an adverb — and it captures Bilbo's deflation at the moment the previous night's adventure turns from thrilling memory into tedious fact. The word dismally carries the whole weight of his disappointment: the quest, the dragons, and the treasure have shrunk, by the light of morning, to the grim actuality of a filthy kitchen and a dozen unmet obligations. The gloom is not tragic but domestic; the adverb makes the mess almost a moral verdict upon his day, and its fossilised flavour of ill omen makes the verdict feel unavoidable — this, the sentence says, was written in the stars, or at least in the state of the washing-up. Near-synonyms each tilt the meaning differently. Gloomily suggests darkness and shadow, often physical; drearily suggests monotony and boredom; bleakly suggests a bare, exposed hopelessness; miserably stresses unhappiness itself; dejectedly stresses low spirits; and cheerlessly is a plain, colourless negation of cheer. Dismally, by contrast, retains that faint flavour of doom and ill omen inherited from the unlucky "dismal days" of the medieval calendars, and it is the word English reserves for failures that feel fated and for facts that feel like verdicts.
