Arrear names money that should have been paid and has not — a sum overdue, still owing, trailing behind its due date like a traveller late on the road. In the source sentence William Cobbett, settling accounts with the Scottish tax machine, records that FORDYCE, the late Duchess of Gordon’s brother, was Collector General of taxes in Scotland, and owed a large arrear to the public: revenue gathered in the nation’s name that had never reached the Exchequer, a great balance sitting in private hands while the public purse waited and the account stood open.

Core meaning in detail: The word is the noun of lateness applied to money. A debt becomes an arrear the morning after it falls due: rent unpaid past quarter-day, taxes unremitted past the reckoning, wages held back past payday, subscriptions lapsed past renewal. Modern English almost always reaches for the plural arrears — in arrears, arrears of rent, tax arrears — so that Cobbett’s singular a large arrear sounds antique to the modern ear, a survival of the older idiom in arrear that governed formal prose from Chaucer to Blackstone. The plural now does nine-tenths of the work, and the singular lives on chiefly in ledgers, statutes, and historical writing, with the derived noun arrearage naming the whole accumulated backlog as one mass.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • An overdue debt or unpaid balance (primary sense, from c. 1300): arrears of rent, tax arrears, mortgage arrears, arrears of wages. The bridge is the word’s birth itself: Old French arere meant behind or backward, and money that stays behind its due date is money in arrear — physical lateness converted into accountancy.
  • The condition of being behindhand — in arrear (from the 14th c.): his payments are in arrear, the work is in arrear, the correspondence is in arrear. The bridge narrows owing to mere lateness: not only money but any duty or task can lag, and the phrase lends financial gravity to ordinary delay.
  • The modern plural idiom in arrears (dominant from the 18th c.): three months in arrears with the rent, to fall into arrears, to clear one’s arrears. The bridge is frequency hardening into formula: landlords’ notices, bank letters, and court judgments repeated the plural until it swallowed the singular in everyday speech.
  • Arrearage — the accumulated sum (derivative noun, 14th c.): the arrearage of the estate, a heavy arrearage of taxes. The bridge adds the French collective suffix to the same stem, naming the whole backlog as one mass rather than counting it item by item.
  • Backward position in the field (obsolete military sense): the arrear or rear of a marching army, the hindmost ranks bringing up the tail. The bridge is the literal Latin root laid bare: that which comes behind — troops trailing the march exactly as debts trail the due date — before the financial sense drove the soldier’s sense out of the language.

Register and connotation: arrear is the language of ledgers, leases, and courts — formal, faintly stern, never slangy and never hurried. To say a tenant is in arrears is to open the first page of eviction with perfect politeness; to say a collector owes an arrear to the public, as Cobbett does, is to accuse him of keeping the nation’s money, with the gravity of a charge-sheet. The word implies process and patience exhausted: reminders sent, quarters tallied, interest accruing, the law waiting patiently at the end of the column with its costs to add.
