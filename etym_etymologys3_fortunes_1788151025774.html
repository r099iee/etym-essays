Fortunes: the career of a goddess in the grammar of everyday luck

I. Latin foundations: fors and fortuna

The Latin noun fors (genitive fortis) meant "chance, luck, hazard" — the accident that befalls one. Philologists derive it from the PIE root *bher-, "to bear, to carry, to bring": fortune is literally "that which is brought to one," the lot delivered by unseen hands. The nominative singular fors was already rare in classical prose, surviving mainly in set phrases such as forsitan, "perhaps," literally "it may be by chance"; the living paradigm ran through the oblique cases (fortem, forte, fortis). From fors Latin built the noun fortūna, "chance, luck, fate, good or bad destiny," and from the noun grew the goddess: the cult of Fortuna at Praeneste and Rome personified chance as a powerful, capricious female deity who turned her wheel, raising and dashing empires and individuals alike. It is a short, dramatic step from "the luck that befalls one" to "the goddess who deals the luck" — and both meanings travelled together inside the same word.

II. Romance and medieval English

  • Old French inherited fortune, "chance, fate, luck," and the Norman Conquest carried it into English: Middle English fortune is attested by the 13th century in both the philosophical sense (chance) and the personified sense (Lady Fortune).
  • Medieval literature made Fortune a central machinery of narrative: the Wheel of Fortune (rota Fortunae) raises and casts down heroes in Chaucer, Boccaccio and the romance cycles, while Boethius’s Consolation of Philosophy — translated across Europe — fixed the goddess as the emblem of the instability of earthly goods.
  • The plural fortunes already appears in medieval usage for "one’s lot in life": the word was pluralised early because a life has many turns, not one.

III. The secularisation: from fate to finance (16th–19th century)

  • As early modern Europe turned from providence to probability, fortune slid from "destiny" toward "chance" and then toward "opportunity": the fortune-hunter of the 17th–18th centuries pursues not fate but wealth, especially through marriage (Defoe, Fielding and the whole literature of the fortune-hunt).
  • The mercantile age completed the migration: to make one’s fortune (17th century) and to make a fortune (18th–19th century) recast fortune as money. The plural widened accordingly into "the financial circumstances of a person, firm or state": the fortunes of a merchant house, of a dynasty, of a nation.
  • By the 19th century the full modern array is in place: Lady Fortune (poetic relic), chance (philosophical), a fortune meaning wealth (everyday commercial), fortunes meaning vicissitudes (historical and journalistic). All coexist; context selects.

IV. The shape of modern usage

  • The dominant modern collocation is the fortunes of X: a neutral, almost statistical phrase tracking the rise and fall of an enterprise, an industry, a team or a party — "the fortunes of the Conservative Party," "the fortunes of the wine trade."
  • Near-synonyms have divided the ancient field: luck holds the pure-chance sense; fate and destiny hold the necessity sense; wealth and property hold the material sense; fortunes keeps the dynamic middle — luck as a developing situation.
  • The theological tension remains in fossils: "Fortune favours the bold" (secular), "God and fortune" (archaic pairing), "fortune-telling" (folk practice refusing the secularisation). The goddess has never quite died; she merely retired into the word’s less exalted corners.

V. Conclusion

Fortunes embodies the progress of Western thinking about luck in one plural noun: from a goddess who wheeled the stars, to a chance that befalls, to a balance sheet that fluctuates. When Durant writes of the "precarious fortunes of the chase," the whole ancestry is present — the hunter’s livelihood stands at the mercy of a luck as naked as the primitive fors, as fickle as Lady Fortune, and ultimately as measurable as a ledger of supplies.
