APPREHENDED (past tense and past participle of apprehend): perceived, grasped, or taken hold of, especially mentally. In this sentence the author distinguishes rhythm from metre: rhythm has "special reference to patterns apprehended through ordinary habits of perception", while metre depends on habits acquired through familiarity with a particular tradition of verse. Apprehended here means "perceived and grasped by the mind" — the patterns are caught hold of in the very act of perception, without conscious training or analysis. The nuance is that apprehension is a natural, pre-theoretical cognition, a seizing of structure before any formal understanding; it sits between mere sensation and full comprehension.

The word's root is a hand. Apprehend comes from Latin apprehendere, "to lay hold of, to seize", and the whole history of the word is a migration of the image of the closing hand from the body to the mind.

  • Physical sense: "to seize, to arrest, to take into custody" — the legal and law-enforcement sense ("the suspect was apprehended at dawn"). This is the dominant everyday use of the verb.
  • Mental sense (the sense of this card): "to grasp with the intellect, to perceive, to understand" — to take hold of a pattern or idea in the very act of perception, before systematic analysis.
  • Fearful offshoot: the noun apprehension developed the specialised meaning "fearful anticipation, dread", because to "catch hold of" a future event in the mind was felt as anxious foreboding; the verb is rarely used this way, but the noun colours the whole family.
  • Epistemology and philosophy: apprehension names the direct, immediate grasp of an object of thought, distinguished from comprehension, which is the full, systematic understanding of the whole.
  • Obsolete sense: "to learn, to acquire knowledge of" — now gone, but it shows the semantic bridge from seizing to knowing.

The historical bridge is the ancient metaphor of mental grasping: Latin prehendere "to seize" yields a family in which thinking is catching — comprehendere is "to grasp together", reprehendere "to hold back, to blame". English apprehend keeps both the physical and mental senses in play, so that in the present sentence the word carries an echo of its other sense, to arrest, intensifying the metaphor of the mind seizing its object: the patterns are caught, as a hand catches a falling thing. In register, apprehended is formal and literary in its mental sense and neutral-to-official in its legal sense. The mental sense is a favourite of philosophy, psychology, and cognitive writing, where it names the immediate, untaught taking hold of structure that ordinary perception performs.

In collocation, the mental sense of apprehend takes abstract objects — patterns, structures, rhythms, ideas, relations — and characteristically appears in formal, analytical prose, where it names the act of cognition that precedes analysis. It stands opposite to analyse: one apprehends a rhythm and then analyses it. The physical sense collocates with suspects, criminals, fugitives, and offenders, and dominates newspaper and police usage, so that an unwary reader of the present sentence may at first reach for the handcuffs and then find the hand of the mind. This controlled ambiguity is part of the word's power: it keeps the whole history of the closing hand available, so that "apprehended through ordinary habits of perception" reads as a catching hold of structure that is effortless, untaught, and complete in the instant — exactly the quality that separates rhythm, in this author's account, from the learned conventions of metre.
