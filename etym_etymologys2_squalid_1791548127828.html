Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*(s)kʷalo-
"large sea fish; rough, scaly"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*skwalē-
"to be rough, scaly, encrusted"
↓
Latin (3rd c. BCE)
squālere
"to be rough, dry, scaly,
stiff with dirt, or unkempt"
↓
Latin (1st c. BCE)
squālidus
"rough, scaly, uncultivated,
filthy, neglected, foul"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1590s)
squalid
"wretchedly filthy, foul from
neglect, sordid, degraded"

Note: Latin 'squalidus' is an adjectival derivative of the stative verb 'squalere' (to be rough, stiff, or encrusted with scale-like dirt), which shares an ancient root with Latin 'squama' (scale) and 'squalus' (a scaly shark or sea dog). The semantic pathway progressed from physical, scaly roughness to encrusted bodily filth, neglected agricultural fields, and ultimately the wretched environmental and moral squalor of urban life.
