Note on historical nuance: The Latin etymological ancestor recēnsiō was originally a technical civic and military term of the Roman state, designating the periodic inspection, auditing, and reviewing of citizen rolls, equestrian centuries, and property ratings conducted by the Roman censors. Its semantic migration into textual criticism occurred during the Renaissance and crystallized in late 18th-century German philology (notably through Johann August Ernesti and Friedrich August Wolf). In 19th-century classical stemmatics, Karl Lachmann formally separated the editorial process into two rigorous stages: recensio (the objective, mechanical collation of all surviving manuscripts to trace their genealogical descent and reconstruct the archetype without subjective guesswork) and emendatio (the critical conjectural correction of errors in the archetype). In English, the plural form recensions came specifically to signify the resulting divergent manuscript families and regional textual branches that emerged from this genealogical analysis. Modern Indo-Europeanists (de Vaan 107) trace Latin cēnsēre to PIE *ḱens- ("to speak with authority, proclaim"), cognate with Sanskrit śaṃsati ("he recites, declares, praises").
