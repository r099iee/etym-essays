CLOSETED — the past participle and adjective of the verb "to closet", meaning shut up in a small private room, especially for a private interview or confidential discussion. 1. The sense in this passage. In David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone (Hong lou meng), Lady Wang is found "closeted with Wang Xi-feng, deep in discussion of a letter which had just arrived from Nanking", attended by two women who have come as messengers from Lady Wang's elder brother and sister-in-law. The participle signals deliberate withdrawal into private conference rather than casual chat in an open hall. They are shut away — physically and socially — from the household's ordinary traffic so that a sensitive family and legal matter can be handled without eavesdroppers. The tone is serious and strategic: this is high-level Rong-guo management, intimacy of rank and trust, with the attendant women present only as authorized carriers of messages. "Closeted" marks the scene as confidential council, not leisure, and the word carries the weight of decisions that will steer the household's response to the news from Nanking. 2. Core meaning. To be closeted with someone is to be shut up together with them in a private room for discussion; the word insists on enclosure and on the exclusion of outsiders. Compared with simply "meeting" or "talking with", closeted adds the image of a closed door — a barrier that seals the conversation off from the world. 3. Full polysemy inventory.
  • Private conference: shut away with a person or persons for confidential discussion, as in "the minister was closeted with her advisers for hours".
  • Shut up in a small space (transitive, historical): to confine or enclose someone in a closet or narrow room; "to closet oneself" means to withdraw for study or thought.
  • Modern hidden-identity sense: "in the closet" or "closeted" — concealing one's sexual orientation or other private identity; a 20th-century metaphoric offshoot of hiddenness that coexists with the older conference sense.
  • Storage sense (via the noun): in chiefly American English "closet" means a cupboard or wardrobe, so "closeted" may loosely suggest something stored away or kept out of sight.
  • Literary and journalistic idiom: "closeted with" is used of politicians, executives, lawyers and lovers meeting in private negotiation, and remains common in political reporting.
  • Figurative withdrawal: closeted from reality — shut off or protected (or dangerously insulated) from the outside world, used of sheltered lives and secretive institutions.
4. Register and collocations. Typical collocations include "closeted with someone", "closeted together", "remain closeted", and "closeted for hours over a document or crisis". The register is literary and journalistic, slightly elevated, and sits comfortably in historical fiction, political biography and reported speech about elites. It is markedly more vivid than the flat phrase "in private", and more domestic than the legal Latin "in camera". 5. The semantic bridge. The noun "closet" (a small private room) gave rise to the verb "to closet", first recorded in 1604, and the past participle "closeted" describes the state of being shut away in such a room. The bridge to the modern identity sense is the shared notion of hiddenness: what is in the closet is sealed away from public view, just as a confidential meeting is sealed behind a shut door. The same old image of the barred, fastening root — the door that can be closed and locked — runs underneath all these senses. In this passage the conference sense is primary, and it perfectly fits elite domestic space: the closet as small private room becomes both setting and metaphor for decisions that will shape the family's fate.
