• practise: Similar because it can take a trade or craft as its object (“practise law”, “practise a craft”); differs because it is neutral and professional, without the suggestion of vigorous, repeated physical exertion that ply carries.
  • pursue: Similar in meaning “carry on an occupation or activity”; differs in aiming at a goal — one pursues a career or an aim — whereas ply emphasises the continuous doing rather than the object to be reached.
  • wield: Similar in the tool-and-weapon sense (“wield an axe”); differs because wield stresses grip, power and control in the hand, not the steady rhythm of repeated use.
  • apply: The closest etymological relative and the source of much of ply’s “busy oneself” sense; differs because apply means to put something to a use or to direct effort at a specific object, and it lacks the idea of continuing in a calling.
  • carry on: The plain-English equivalent of “ply one’s business”; differs in register — a neutral phrasal verb with no hint of craft, vigour or archaic dignity.
  • beset: Similar to ply with in the sense of pressing someone repeatedly; differs in hostility — to beset is to harass or surround, whereas to ply with drink or questions may be done hospitably.
  • importune: Similar in the nagging, persistent pressure of ply with; differs in being explicitly unwelcome and often verbal, without the concrete offer of food, drink or gifts.
  • supply: Similar in the “keep giving” element of ply with; differs because it is neutral about frequency and insistence — supply once, and the word is satisfied.
  • layer: The noun sense; similar as one thickness laid over another; differs in being a static measurement, where the verb ply is all motion.
  • strand: Similar in naming one twisted element of rope or yarn; differs in referring to the fibre itself rather than to the number of thicknesses counted together (“three-ply”).
