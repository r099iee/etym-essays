- Old English - “Wield” (wealdan) means to govern, command, and control — a word of mastery and rule, applied to kings and dominion.
- Middle English - Shifts toward the physical: to wield a sword, axe, or tool — to handle a weapon with the hand and use it effectively.
- 16th–19th century (figurative extension) - The political sense returns in figurative form: to wield power, influence, or authority, keeping the martial image of expert, commanding handling; in the 19th century, of a woman who “wielded influence” over a great philosopher — deliberate, sovereign agency.
- Contemporary - Common in journalism and rhetoric (wield power, wield influence, wield a pen); the literal weapon sense persists, and the word keeps its forceful, skilled-mastery flavour throughout.
