From the jolting of a cart to a small frown The history of vexed is the history of a word that has lightened — from a violent shaking to a polite frown. It begins in the prehistoric Indo-European verb *weǵʰ-, "to carry, convey, transport," a root whose descendants form one of the great transport families of the language: Sanskrit vahati "he carries, conveys," Greek okheo "to carry" and okhema "vehicle," Latin vehere "to carry," and Old English wegan "to carry, weigh, move" (whence our weigh and wagon). In Latin the root produced the verb vehere, and from its supine stem vex- the language built a frequentative, vexare, which meant "to shake, jolt, toss about." The frequentative was a standard Latin device: where vehere said "to carry," vexare said "to keep carrying back and forth," and from the image of a jolting cart-load the word passed naturally to the jolting of a person — vexare hominem, "to shake a man up," hence "to harass, torment, annoy." The physical picture is essential: at the root of all vexation is the idea of being shaken about against one's will, of being transported and jostled where one did not choose to go. The long middle age of vexation Vexare was a vigorous word in classical Latin. Cicero uses it constantly for harassment, persecution, and the rough treatment of people and provinces; Caesar speaks of lands vexed by war; and the sense of repeated, deliberate troubling is already fully present. This Latin word passed into the Romance world and reached Old French as vexer "to vex, annoy, torment," attested from the eleventh century. English borrowed the verb late in the Middle English period — the first English attestations of vexen date from c. 1410, in Lydgate — and for the next three centuries vexed carried the full Latin force: it could mean tormented, afflicted, persecuted, or distressed. Shakespeare writes of a vexed and distressed spirit; the King James Bible uses vex in this strong sense again and again ("vexed with devils," "vexed with unclean spirits"); and "the vexed Bermoothes" in The Tempest (Act I, scene ii) names the storm-churned Bermudas — a perfect image of the old jolting, tossing sea, the exact physical sense of the Latin ancestor. How the question became vexed Parallel to this strong personal sense, another developed in the intellectual sphere. From the late 16th century the verb came to mean "to debate, dispute, or trouble a matter," and from it grew the famous collocation "a vexed question" — a question so often shaken and worried by argument that it will not settle. The usage is found in law, theology, and scholarship from the 17th century to the present day ("the vexed question of the authorship of the plays," "a vexed question in economic theory"). Here the metaphor has shifted from the person to the issue: the question is harassed by controversy exactly as the traveller was harassed by the jolt of the road. The strength of the verb survived into the Victorian age — Darwin writes of "vexed questions" of natural history — so the intellectual sense is not a weakening but a sideways transfer. The modern lightening In the 19th and 20th centuries the strong personal senses steadily retreated. Vexed no longer means tormented or persecuted in ordinary use; it settled into the register of mild, rueful annoyance — the feeling of a small puncture of dignity rather than of rage. The reasons are social and rhetorical: in a culture that values understatement, a word that once meant "to shake and torment" became the perfect instrument of polite irritation, and its very strength could be played for comedy. This is precisely how Tolkien uses it in The Hobbit: Gandalf and Thorin are only "a bit vexed," the adverb and the "perhaps" doing the lightening, so that the dwarves' wounded pride reads as faintly comic rather than dangerous. The modern word thus preserves, compressed, four centuries of semantic change: the violent jolt of the Latin cart, the strong harassment of the Renaissance, the intellectual trouble of the disputed question, and the light, rueful frown of the present day. Key points
  • PIE *weǵʰ- "to carry" passed through Latin vehere into the frequentative vexare "to jolt, harass" — the root semantic bridge is physical shaking.
  • Old French vexer carried the word into English through Middle English vexen (c. 1410).
  • The archaic strong senses (torment, afflict, persecute) dominated English from the 15th to the 19th century, visible in Shakespeare and the King James Bible.
  • "A vexed question" (17th c. onward) is the intellectual offshoot, still in daily scholarly use.
  • The modern weakening to "mildly annoyed" is a 19th–20th century drift, and it is this light, rueful sense that Tolkien exploits for comedy.
