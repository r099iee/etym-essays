- [16th-century military sense] - 'Flank' enters English as a noun for the side of an army or body of troops; 'flanked' describes troops posted to guard the side, with a tactical, defensive, martial connotation.
- [18th-19th century: bodily and architectural extension] - The military word extends to the body, naming the fleshy side between ribs and hip, and to buildings; 'flanked' comes to mean having sides of a body or structure, still carrying a sense of ordered, purposeful arrangement.
- [Modern generalised sense] - 'Flanked' broadens into a neutral descriptive verb meaning situated on each side of something; connotation shifts from guarding to mere symmetrical placement - a table flanked by chairs - though the ghost of military protection persists when the flanked object is ceremonially central.
