WEIRWOOD is a rare example of a word whose etymology is both perfectly attested and wholly modern: the compound was coined by George R.R. Martin in 1996 for A Game of Thrones, yet it is built from two English words - weir and wood - whose individual histories stretch back unbroken to Proto-Indo-European. This essay treats each root separately, in full, before examining how Martin's coinage fuses their separate histories into one mythic object.

ROOT WORD 1 IN ISOLATION: WEIR

The word weir belongs to one of the most contested corners of Indo-European reconstruction. The Proto-Indo-European root is given as *wer-, but this is not a single root: the handbooks distinguish several homonymous *wer- roots, including "to cover, protect, ward off," "to turn, bend," and "to notice, perceive." The weir family is conventionally attached to the "cover/protect/ward" set, and the connection to Proto-Germanic *warjaną / *werjaną ("to defend, ward off") and Old English warian ("to guard") is widely accepted - the same family that yields ward, warden, wary, guard (via French), and warrant. Because the root is reconstructed from such a wide family, the *wer- reconstruction itself is secure in outline but the exact Proto-Indo-European semantics are debated.

In Old English the word was wer, meaning a dam, fish-fence, or enclosure built across a river - a barrier that both controlled the water and protected a fishery. It is a frequent element in Anglo-Saxon charters and boundary surveys, attesting to how central the river weir was to medieval economy: a weir could trap migrating fish, power a mill, or define a manor's fishing rights, and disputes over weirs are common in Domesday Book and later manorial records. Importantly, Old English wer is a homonym of the separate word wer "man" (as in werewolf, wergild), and it resonates in the cultural memory with wyrd ("fate"), giving the word an uncanny undertone of guardianship and destiny that Martin's fiction deliberately exploits.

In Middle English the form were / weir continued as the standard word for a dam or fish-trap. In early modern English the spelling settled as weir (with the variant wear in older texts), and the word acquired its modern technical range: an overflow weir in a reservoir, a measuring weir in hydrology, and the still-familiar fishing weir. Throughout its history the word has never lost its core sense of a barrier and a protection - precisely the semantic material Martin needed for a sacred boundary tree.

ROOT WORD 2 IN ISOLATION: WOOD

The word wood descends from a Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *widhu-, "tree, wood, forest" (some handbooks prefer *weydʰ-; the reconstruction is debated but the family is clear). In Proto-Germanic this became *widuz ("wood, timber, tree"), and in Old English it appears as widu and the standard wudu, meaning both the individual tree and timber and the collective forest. The word is everywhere in the Old English landscape: in placenames such as Scir-wudu (Sherwood, "the shire wood"), Wuduburna, and countless local names. Notably, Old English wudu escaped the Great Vowel Shift in its modern form: because its vowel was short u, it remained /ʊ/ ("wood") rather than becoming a diphthong like the long-ō adjective wōd "mad," which in modern English is also spelled wood (as in "wood (crazy)") - a charming homograph collision that continues to confuse and delight.

In Germanic and Celtic paganism the wudu was more than timber. Sacred groves - called nemeta by the Romans and weohs or holy places by the Anglo-Saxons - were protected stands where trees were never felled and where offerings, sometimes of blood, were made. Roman writers such as Tacitus describe the Germanic worship of trees and groves, and the Christianization of Anglo-Saxon England is studded with stories of missionaries felling sacred trees, such as Boniface's felling of the oak of Geismar. The deep cultural memory of the sacred, indestructible grove is precisely what wudu carried into Middle English wode and modern wood.

THE 1996 SYNTHESIS

When George R.R. Martin coined WEIRWOOD, he fused the barrier of the weir with the sacred grove of the wood into a single mythic object: a tree that is at once a protective boundary, a watcher, and a place of worship. The blood-red sap and weeping carved faces evoke the real-world tradition of blood sacrifice in sacred groves; the rot-proof petrified wood makes the tree an image of history that cannot decay; and the network of trees across the North becomes a memory palace that greenseers can enter. The compound is a masterclass in naming: it sounds ancient because it is built entirely from genuinely ancient words, and it feels inevitable because the two roots already carried, independently, the meanings Martin needed - enclosure, protection, fate, and forest.

Key Historical and Literary Milestones

  • PIE era (reconstructed): *wer- "cover/protect" and *widhu- "tree" establish the two families.
  • c. 700 (Anglo-Saxon charters): Recorded river wers (weirs) and wudus (woods) in boundary surveys.
  • c. 1086 (Domesday Book): Manorial disputes over fishing weirs and woodland rights.
  • c. 1386 (Chaucer): Feudal landscapes of fish weirs and dense ancient woodlands.
  • 1996 (George R.R. Martin, A Game of Thrones): First introduction of weirwood trees and godswood sacred groves in Westeros.
  • 2011-2019 (Game of Thrones TV series): The Heart Tree at Winterfell becomes a global pop-culture icon.
