DANGLING: a northern word that never stops swinging A LATE AND NORTHERN ARRIVAL The biography of dangling is short, obscure, and thoroughly northern. The word does not exist in Old English and has no native Germanic lineage in England; it enters the language only in the late sixteenth century, with the OED's first citation sitting in the 1590s. It arrives as a loan from Scandinavia: Danish dangle and Norwegian dangla both mean "to hang loosely, swing about," and the sudden appearance of the verb in Elizabethan English — precisely as English maritime and trading contact with the North was intensifying — fits a loanword profile exactly. Beyond Scandinavia the trail goes cold. No Proto-Indo-European root for the verb is securely reconstructable, and it would be an error to force one; the root may even be ultimately imitative, the repeated -ng- of the word echoing the pendular sway of the thing it names. Scholars have floated a link to Old Norse dengja, "to strike, hammer," on the grounds that a hammer swings and strikes, but the connection is speculative, and the dictionaries leave it unproven. The word simply is what it sounds like: a swinging thing. A RAPID, COMPACT SEMANTIC CAREER What is certain is the speed and compactness of the word's career once it reached English. It first meant, intransitively, "to hang so as to sway with one's own weight" — a vivid, almost playful word for loose-hanging things: keys, legs, trinkets, earrings. Almost at once it acquired the transitive use, "to let something hang loosely," recorded by the early seventeenth century; the present participle dangling, with the suffix -ing, follows immediately, and it is this participle that has become the word's most familiar shape. THE GRAMMAR BOOK TAKES OVER By the nineteenth century the -ing form had become a technical term of the classroom: a dangling participle (or dangling modifier) is a participle whose implied subject does not match the grammatical subject of its sentence — "walking down the street, the lamp-post loomed." The term turned the participle into a schoolmaster's byword for grammatically unanchored prose, giving the word a pedantic, faintly reproachful echo that still hovers over it. This is a rare case of a grammar label born from an ordinary physical verb, and it shows how far the word had travelled from the dock-side where a rope dangles. THE BAIT AND THE LOOSE END In the nineteenth century the verb also reached out figuratively, and here its modern psychological colour was set: to dangle a promise, a reward, or a carrot before someone is to hold the temptation out loosely, keeping it just out of reach — the eternal gesture of bait, drawn from the same image as a fisherman's lure. Dangling an offer, dangling hope, dangling a prospect: the connotation is coaxing and manipulative, the promise always withdrawable at will. In the twentieth century computing and management adopted the same image. A dangling pointer is a pointer that continues to refer to memory that has already been freed or reused — a reference that hangs there, pointing at nothing; a dangling thread is a task left unresolved; a dangling reference dangles into the void. In chemistry and materials science a dangling bond is an unsatisfied valence electron, an unpaired electron "hanging free" at a surface. Every one of these technical senses is the old physical image updated: something attached at one end, loose and unanchored at the other, waiting to be grabbed or waiting to break. SOUND AND FORM The word's shape has been stable since its arrival. The Scandinavian forms show the same nasal sequence -ng- that English adopted unchanged, and the English pronunciation has remained /dˈaŋɡəlɪŋ/ — the velar nasal, the lax vowel, and the -le and -ing suffixes all settled early. The -le ending is the same suffix seen in handle, dangle, and tangle, and the -ing participle form has been in use since c. 1600. Nothing about the word's sound has needed to change, because its sound was invented to match its meaning from the start. IN THE HOBBIT In Tolkien's sentence the word performs all its history at once. Gollum's feet dangle with loose, weight-driven slackness — the old Scandinavian image of keys and trinkets hanging free — yet the context makes that slackness sinister: the "large feet" of a "small slimy creature" over black subterranean water, held there with perfect, patient control, and never a ripple. The word also reaches forward into its figurative life: these feet are held out, dangled, as an unspoken provocation over the lake, the way a temptation is dangled before a fish. The childlike and the grotesque meet in a single participle, and the word does all of it by doing what it has always done — hanging loose, one end attached, the other free, ready to swing at the slightest movement. The derivation chain, from the earliest securely attested stage: Danish dangle / Norwegian dangla "to hang loosely, swing about" (Scandinavian, ultimate origin unknown) → English dangle, intransitive, "to hang loosely and sway" (c. 1590) → English dangle, transitive, "to let hang loosely" (early 17th c.) → English dangling, present participle and adjective (c. 1600; figurative "held out as temptation" from the 19th century).
