The etymological evolution of commencement represents an illuminating trajectory within the Romance and Germanic lexical confluence, demonstrating how an ancient Indo-European motion verb evolved through late Roman colloquial speech, medieval French bureaucratic culture, and scholastic guild rituals into the premier academic celebration of modern universities.

1. Proto-Indo-European Verbal Foundations and Classical Latin (c. 3000–100 BCE):

The ultimate morphological root of commencement is the Proto-Indo-European primary verb *h₁ey-, meaning "to go, walk, proceed." This foundational root gave rise to Sanskrit éti ("he goes"), Ancient Greek eimi (εἶμι, "I go"), Old Church Slavonic iti, and Latin ire ("to go").

In Classical Latin, prefixing the directional preposition in- ("into, upon") to ire formed the compound verb inire, meaning literally "to go into, enter upon, embark on, begin." From the past participial stem init-, Latin developed the abstract noun initium ("an entrance, beginning, origin, commencement") and the denominative verb initiare ("to initiate, consecrate into sacred mysteries, begin"). In formal Classical Latin prose (such as Cicero, Caesar, and Livy), the primary verbs for "to begin" were incipere and coepisse, while initiare was largely reserved for sacred rites, solemn inductions, and philosophical initiations.

2. Vulgar Latin Compounding and Gallo-Roman Shift (c. 300–800 CE):

During the decline of the Western Roman Empire, colloquial spoken Latin (Vulgar Latin) underwent widespread expressive remodeling. Speakers frequently reinforced basic verbs with the intensifying prefix com- (from cum, meaning "together, with", but functioning as a completive or intensive prefix signifying "thoroughly, completely"). Through this process, Vulgar Latin compounded com- with initiare to produce the hybrid verb *cominitiare, meaning literally "to initiate thoroughly, begin together, inaugurate."

As Vulgar Latin evolved into Proto-Romance across Gaul, *cominitiare completely displaced the classical verbs incipere and coepisse in popular speech. Through standard Gallo-Roman phonetic shifts—including vowel syncope, the assibilation of the dental plosive before the palatal glide (/ti/ > /ts/), and lenition—the unwieldy Vulgar Latin form *cominitiare contracted into early Old French as comencier (modern French commencer), meaning "to begin, start, undertake."

3. Old French Morphology and the Suffix *-mentum* (c. 11th–12th Century):

In Old French, abstract nouns of action and result were systematically formed by appending the suffix -ment (inherited from the Latin neuter nominal suffix -mentum, which denoted the means, instrument, or result of a verbal action, as seen in instrumentum and monumentum). Attaching -ment to the verbal stem comencier yielded Old French comencement, signifying "the act of beginning, an origin, inception, or first stage."

The term became prominent in medieval French administrative documents, chivalric romances, and historiography (such as the chronicles of Villehardouin and Froissart), where it denoted the formal commencement of military campaigns, legal treaties, and chivalric quests.

4. Anglo-Norman Conquest, Middle English, and the University Guild (c. 1250–1400):

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman French became the prestige language of the English court, the judiciary, and the emerging scholarly class. By the late 13th and early 14th centuries, comencement was borrowed into Middle English, coexisting alongside native Germanic terms like beginning (from Old English onginnan) and start (from Old English styrtan). Middle English legal and literary writers utilized comencement when they sought a register of elevated formality, dignity, and institutional gravitas.

Crucially, during the 14th century, the word acquired its specialized academic sense in the medieval universities of Oxford and Cambridge. These institutions were structured as corporate guilds of scholars (*universitas magistrorum et scholarium*). Just as a medieval blacksmith apprentice, upon producing their "masterpiece," was inducted into the guild of smiths and "commenced" business as a licensed master craftsman, a university student who passed their disputations was admitted to the degree of Master of Arts. The ceremony was called Commencement because the candidate officially commenced their statutory duties of teaching in the faculty. In 1387, John Trevisa's translation of Higden's Polychronicon attested this academic usage, describing the masters convening at the university commencement.

5. American Colonial Development and Contemporary Ritual (17th–21st Century):

When the English collegiate model was established in the American colonies—Harvard (1642), William & Mary (1693), Yale (1701), and Brown (founded as Rhode Island College in 1764)—Commencement Day was instituted as the intellectual and civic climax of the colony's calendar. As documented in Bronson's History of Brown University, the annual Commencement was an occasion of enormous social pomp: dignitaries, governors, donors, and alumni gathered to hear student orations in Latin, Greek, and English. In 1803–1804, Nicholas Brown Jr.'s donation of $5,000 "within one Year from the late Commencement" directly led to the renaming of Rhode Island College to Brown University. Over the 19th and 20th centuries, while British universities gradually reverted to terms like Congregation or Graduation, North American education universally preserved Commencement as the venerated symbol of academic achievement and personal renewal.
