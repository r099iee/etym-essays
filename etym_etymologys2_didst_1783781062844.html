Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, set, make" (c. 4500–3500 BCE), characteristically appearing in a reduplicated present *dʰi-dʰeh₁- (whence Sanskrit dadhāti "he places," Greek títhēmi "I put"). → Proto-Germanic *dōną "to do, act, place" (past stem *dēd-, from the reduplicated PIE perfect *dʰe-dʰeh₁- "he put, placed"). → Old English dōn "to do" with past tense dyde "did" (c. 8th–11th c. CE) and the second-person singular past dydest "thou didst" (þū dydest). → Middle English didest / diddest, contracted didst (13th c. CE). → Early Modern English DIDST (King James Bible 1611; Shakespeare; Milton, Paradise Lost 1667). Debated / nuanced: the PIE root is secure and one of the best-attested in the family; the Germanic past stem *dēd- is the direct reflex of the PIE reduplicated perfect, a pattern preserved in Gothic -dedum and Old Saxon -dun. There is no serious dispute about any link in the chain from PIE *dʰeh₁- to modern did. The only nuance is purely morphological: the Old English form shows the unrounded vowel (dyde) versus the Middle English rounding toward did, part of the ordinary early English vowel developments, and the contraction of dydest to didst is a regular loss of the unstressed vowel, exactly parallel to the contraction of satest to satst.
