The story of unassailed is the story of how a word for leaping became a word for siege, and how a word for siege became a word for an untroubled mind. It is a chronicle of three transfers: from the athlete's bound to the warrior's charge; from the warrior's charge to the lawyer's, satirist's, and moralist's attack; and finally from attack to the mere shadow of attack that never arrives - the purely negative state of having been left alone. The leaping root. The chain begins in reconstructed Proto-Indo-European *sel-, "to leap, spring, bound" - the abrupt, violent motion of a body leaving the ground. In the Indo-European diaspora this kinetic root scattered widely: in Sanskrit it flattened into sarati "to run, glide, flow" (whence the sacred river Sarasvati), in Greek it produced hallein "to leap", and in Italic it kept its spring: Latin salire "to leap, jump". The root's vitality in Latin is astonishing. From salire Roman speakers built an entire family of prefixed verbs: resilire "to leap back" (resilient), exsilire "to leap out for joy" (exult), insilire "to leap upon" (insult), dissilire "to leap apart" (dissilient), and, most important for our word, adsalire "to leap at". From leaping to assaulting. The step that made unassailed possible is the prefixed compound ad-salire: literally "to leap toward/at". In a pre-industrial world where combat often consisted of sudden bounding rushes, "leaping at" an enemy naturally meant "attacking" him. By Classical Latin, adsalire / assalire was a regular military verb meaning "to leap upon, attack, assault". When the Roman world dissolved into the feudal kingdoms of Europe, Vulgar Latin assalire ("to assault, make an attack") passed into Old French as assaillir - the verb of the siege: to assail a castle was to leap upon its walls. The meaning had narrowed and hardened from any spring to specifically hostile, armed attack. It is at this point that the English word enters: Anglo-Norman and Middle English assailen, attested from the 13th century, means "to attack with force, to set upon". The figurative turn. English then did something decisive: it widened assail from bodies to words. From the 16th century one could assail a person with arguments, accusations, doubts, temptations, calumnies, or flattery; a reputation, a faith, a conscience, or a judgement could be assailed. The siege of the castle became the siege of the mind. This figurative warfare is the semantic hinge of unassailed. If your judgement is assailed by flattery, flattery is an aggressor storming the walls of your discernment; if your reputation is assailed by slander, slander is an army at the gates. To be unassailed, then, is to be a fortress that no army has even reached - untouched not because it fought well, but because the enemy never came. Negation and its grammar. The negative prefix un- is Old English un-, from PIE *n- "not", the same privative that gives unbroken, unsung, unseen. English habitually negates participial adjectives with un- when no contrary adjective exists (unassailed, unremarked, untainted) rather than with in-/im-/il-. The participle assailed, a borrowing, nonetheless takes the native Germanic negative prefix, as English prefers with recent borrowings and verbs (unsolicited, unimpressed). The result, unassailed, is first attested in English in the 16th century, at the height of the figurative assail boom. Literary career. The word has lived most of its life in prose of moral and political assessment. It appears in 16th- and 17th-century theological writing for a faith untroubled by doubt; in 18th-century essayists for a reputation unbesmirched; and in 19th-century fiction for judgements left intact. Its most famous moment is Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice (1813), Chapter 4, where Elizabeth Bennet's "judgement too unassailed by any attention to herself" stands as the novel's first sketch of her psychology: she has never been besieged by vanity, so she sees clearly. The word here performs a cool, ironic praise - her discernment is intact precisely because she has not been flattered into folly, as her sisters and contemporaries so often are. It is a state of negative good fortune: she is wise because nothing has happened to her. Modern persistence. In contemporary English, unassailed survives chiefly in formal and journalistic registers: an unassailed record, an unassailed position, an unassailed assumption, a verdict that stands unassailed. In argument, an unassailed premise is simply one no one has refuted. The word retains its military ghost: it is always the negative of an attack that never landed. Bullet summary of the semantic trajectory:
  • PIE *sel- "to leap" - pure physical kinetics.
  • Latin salire "to leap" - springing retained.
  • Latin adsalire "to leap upon" - the jump becomes hostile.
  • Old French assaillir "to besiege, attack" - the feudal siege verb.
  • English assail (13th c.) - physical attack; from the 16th c. also verbal and moral attack.
  • English unassailed (16th c.) - the negative: never attacked, never breached, never even reached.
  • Austen (1813) - the psychological sense: judgement untouched by flattery.
The abiding image is the unbreached wall. Unassailed tells us nothing about the height of the wall, only that no one has come. It is the adjective of the untouched, the unswayed, the uncorrupted - security by non-occurrence, the quiet luck of having never been tested.
