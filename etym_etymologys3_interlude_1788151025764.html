1. The Latin skeleton. Interlude is a compound whose joints are visible to anyone with a little Latin: inter "between, among" and lūdus "play, game." The prefix comes from the ancient Proto-Indo-European adverb *h₁entér "between, inside," which also produced Sanskrit antár and Greek énther-forms; it is one of the most durable small words in the language family, still walking around in English "inter," "entrails," and "interpolate." The noun lūdus is older and shadier: it means "play, game, sport," but also "school" (the ludi of public entertainment and the ludus of gladiatorial training), and linguists admit its origin is uncertain — sometimes connected with the PIE root *leid- "to play," sometimes suspected of being an Etruscan loan. Neither origin changes the compound's meaning: what Latin built was simply "a play between plays," and everything the English word has ever meant is a variation on that image.

2. Medieval theater: the interlude as genre. Latin interlūdium passes into Anglo-French as entrelude and into Middle English as enterlude in the fourteenth century, and there it found an extraordinarily welcoming home: late-medieval English drama. Costly civic mystery cycles (York, Chester, Wakefield) were performed over days; court and manor feasts were punctuated by short, often wicked comic pieces; and the sixteenth century minted the term as a genre label for the little plays of John Heywood and his contemporaries — The Four PP, Johan Johan, and the rest, moralities and farces designed to be played "betwene" the larger business of a feast or a miracle cycle. The word in this period is solidly theatrical, and its association with lightness and jest is already fixed.

  • 3. The widening of the frame. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the noun had stepped off the stage. Prose and verse began to speak of "an interlude of peace," "a fortunate interlude" in a life, or an "interlude of calm weather" — an intervening period of time with a character of its own. The transfer is a natural metaphor: if a play is a structured whole and the interlude is the short, lighter piece between its acts, then any brief, self-contained episode cut into the flow of time is likewise an interlude. Dr. Johnson's Dictionary (1755) records both senses side by side, the theatrical first and the temporal second, and later editions quietly reverse the historical order as the temporal sense overtakes the theatrical one.
  • 4. The musical century. The nineteenth century gave interlude a third permanent home. Opera, oratorio, and the symphony developed instrumental pieces played between scenes, movements, or acts — the intermezzo in Italian usage, the interlude in English and French — from the entr'actes of Romantic opera (Massenet, Bizet) to the orchestral interludes of Britten's Peter Grimes in the twentieth century. Church music uses interludes between psalm verses; the ballad opera and variety stage used them as filler with a tune. The technical musical sense is the one most likely to appear in a modern concert program, and it is the direct descendant of the ancient intermission piece.
  • 5. Modern life and afterlife. In the twentieth century the temporal sense became dominant and emotionally rich: Eugene O'Neill's Pulitzer Prize-winning Strange Interlude (1928) made the word famous as a title for the strange, interior episodes of a life; film and television adopted "a musical interlude" for a set piece; and recorded pop music of the 1960s onward has used the short "interlude" track — a minute or two of atmosphere stitching the album together — as a standard formal device. Meanwhile earth sciences and climate writing rediscovered the word for deep time: the Holocene, an interglacial, is routinely called an interlude between glaciations, exactly as the reading context on this card has it. The word has thus completed a full circle: born in the theater as a play between plays, it now names the warm play of time between ice ages.

Conclusion. Interlude is a quiet lesson in how words widen without breaking: a compound about theatrical scheduling became a genre, the genre became a metaphor for time, the metaphor acquired a musical career, and all three senses still live comfortably in one short noun — something small, light, and well-timed inserted between the great works, whether they are acts, movements, wars, or ice ages.
