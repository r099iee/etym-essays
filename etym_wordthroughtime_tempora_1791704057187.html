- Classical Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE) — Ciceronian Political Apostrophe: cemented by Marcus Tullius Cicero in his First Catilinarian Oration as the definitive exclamation of despair over state corruption and political rebellion.
- Imperial Rome & Early Christianity (1st–5th c. CE) — Moral & theological critique: utilized by Roman satirists like Juvenal and early Church Fathers (such as Augustine in De Civitate Dei) to contrast the fallen, transient tempora Christiana with eternal divine peace.
- Medieval & Renaissance Humanism (12th–16th c.) — Proverbial crystallization: embedded in European scholarship through Latin maxims like Tempora mutantur, nos et mutamur in illis, reflecting on human impermanence and the wheel of Fortune.
- 18th to 19th c. — Enlightenment & Victorian Parliamentary Oratory: cited by British parliamentarians, essayists, and satirists (such as Jonathan Swift and Thomas Carlyle) as a recognized classical flourish to critique political degeneration.
- 20th to 21st c. — Ironic cultural shorthand: evolved in modern journalism and literary criticism into a sophisticated, often self-deprecating meme to express exasperation over rapid technological disruption, vulgarity in pop culture, or decaying linguistic standards.
