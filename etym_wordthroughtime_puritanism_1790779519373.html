- 1560s–1600s - Coined as a biting derisive epithet by Anglican traditionalists to mock reformist Protestants demanding radical "purity" in liturgical worship; viewed by the established church as factious, insolent, and seditious perfectionism.
- 1640s–1660s - Ascendant political and religious power under the English Civil War and Cromwellian Protectorate; perceived as zealous state-mandated moral reform, iconoclastic destruction of ecclesiastical art, and the legislative prohibition of theater, Maypoles, and Christmas festivities.
- Late 17th–18th Century - Transplanted to colonial North America (Massachusetts Bay Colony); associated simultaneously with pious, hardworking covenantal community-building and rigid, authoritarian theocratic intolerance (symbolized historically by the Salem witch trials).
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Reassessed in Anglo-American historiography as the foundational moral bedrock of civic virtue, democratic self-governance, commercial enterprise, and the "Protestant work ethic."
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Re-stigmatized by modernist intellectuals, satirists (notably H.L. Mencken, who famously defined it as "the haunting fear that someone, somewhere, may be happy"), and psychoanalysts as a pathological neurosis of sexual repression, artistic philistinism, and anti-intellectual dogmatism.
- Late 20th–21st Century - Broadened into a secular critique of ideological policing, corporate sanitization, algorithmic censorship, neo-prohibitionism, and cancel culture across both conservative and progressive spheres.
