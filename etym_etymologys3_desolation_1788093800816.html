1. Latin foundations: solus and the idea of aloneness. The history of desolation begins in Latin with the adjective sōlus "alone, only, solitary", a word of Roman semantic depth — it underlay solus locus "lonely place", legal phrases about persons acting alone, and the family of later nouns sōlitās and sōlitūdō (whence solitude). The adjective's own origin is debated: sōlus is usually analysed as *solwos, possibly built on the PIE reflexive stem *se- "self, apart" (seen in Latin sē "oneself" and sōc- in socius), which would literally mean "set apart by oneself". Many handbooks follow this, but the formation is not certain — a rare, candid caveat in what is otherwise a transparent chain.

2. dēsōlāre: to leave completely alone. From sōlus Latin formed the verb dēsōlāre "to leave alone, abandon, lay waste", with the intensifying prefix dē- "completely, utterly". The verb is attested from the 1st century BCE; Cicero's participle dēsōlātus "deserted, abandoned" is among the earliest evidence, and in the classical age the verb carried both a legal-political sense (of a place stripped of its people) and a rhetorical one (of a landscape made waste). The crucial semantic move is already complete in Latin: to "completely-alone" a place is precisely to ruin it by emptying it.

3. The noun dēsōlātiō and the Vulgate. The abstract noun dēsōlātiō "abandonment, laying waste" crystallised in Late Latin, and Jerome's Vulgate (completed c. 405 CE) turned it into a word of prophecy. The Vulgate applies dēsōlātiō to cities and lands that God has abandoned to destruction — Jerusalem, Babylon, Tyre — and coins the famous compound abōminātiō dēsōlātiōnis "abomination of desolation" (Daniel 9:27, 11:31, 12:11; taken up in Matthew 24:15). From the Vulgate onward, desolation in the Western literary tradition is inseparable from divine abandonment: the emptiness is a verdict.

4. Romance transmission and Middle English. Latin passed into Old French as desolacion (12th–13th c.) "desolation, ruin, grief" — note that the emotional sense is already present in Old French — and English borrowed the word around 1300 as desolacioun. Chaucer uses it of ruined, devastated things, and the Wycliffe Bible renders the Vulgate's dēsōlātiō as "desolacioun", keeping the prophetic register alive in English.

5. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: the emotional sense. The King James Bible (1611) fixed "the abomination of desolation" in the English ear. At the same time, in devotional and secular writing, the word attached itself to the inner life: "desolation of spirit" described the soul left dry, empty and far from God (a contrast sharpened by Ignatius of Loyola's Spiritual Exercises, where desolation and consolation are technical states). Shakespeare and the dramatists used desolate/desolation for both ruined kingdoms and grieving hearts, wedding the outer and inner senses in one breath.

6. The Romantic sublime: desolation becomes aesthetic. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, landscape and ruin poetry made desolation an object of awe: the picturesque ruin, the empty moor, the polar ice field and the desert all drew the word. Shelley's Ozymandias (1818) — "round the decay / Of that colossal wreck, boundless and bare / The lone and level sands stretch far away" — is the archetype of imperial desolation made sublime. Explorers' accounts of the Arctic and desert wastes used the word for regions of terrifying grandeur, so that by 1900 desolation implied not only sorrow but a vast, solemn beauty.

7. The twentieth century to today. Two modern lives of the word dominate. In war reporting and history, desolation describes devastated cities and scorched regions — a landscape emptied by violence (Dresden, Grozny, Aleppo). In literature and clinical prose, it names psychological ruin: the feeling of being utterly alone. Tolkien joins both strands: the dragon's "Desolation of Smaug" is a wasted, evacuated land, and in The Hobbit the same valley, "the desolation... now filled with birds and blossoms", dramatises the redemption of that emptiness. In its rarity and gravity, "desolation" remains one of English's most powerful words for what it is to be left alone.
