Arlen is, in this context, a proper name: the surname adopted by the novelist, essayist, playwright and scriptwriter Michael Arlen (1895–1956), born Dikran Sarkis Kouyoumdjian in Ruse, Bulgaria, to an Armenian family. It is not an inherited Armenian surname and not a conventional English surname in his case: Arlen invented it. According to his own account, he formed it from the first two syllables of Armenia, replacing the m with an l — Ar-men-ia becoming Ar-len. He used it for his first book, The London Venture (1920), and adopted it officially by deed poll in 1921–1922, when he also became a naturalised British citizen.

Why he changed his name. Arlen explained the decision in the "Apologia" prefacing The London Venture. His natal name, Kouyoumdjian, was, he said, "a feat of pronunciation which few of my English acquaintances have performed"; he had been told that some writers would have been famous if only their names could be pronounced familiarly. Changing it, he hoped, robbed his readers of their last excuse for his obscurity. The change was also strategic: as a Bulgarian national of Armenian origin in wartime and post-war London, he occupied a precarious social position, and an easily pronounced, faintly Celtic-sounding English name helped him move through literary and society circles.

Primary sense: the novelist and his world. Michael Arlen is chiefly remembered for The Green Hat (1924), a best-selling novel about Iris March, a beautiful, reckless society woman, which became a stage play and several films (including a 1928 adaptation with Greta Garbo). His other works include These Charming People (1923), May Fair (1925), Young Men in Love (1927) and collections of short stories such as The Gentleman from America. He wrote satirical romances of English "smart society", gothic horror and psychological thrillers, and later political commentary. His vivid, colloquial, slightly over-ornamented style was dubbed "Arlenesque" — a genuine lexical derivative of the surname.

Secondary sense: the surname as a cultural marker. In the 1920s "Michael Arlen" was a brand as much as a byline: elegant, exotic, faintly scandalous, associated with Mayfair, the Riviera, yellow Rolls-Royces and beautiful women. The name therefore carries connotations of Jazz Age glamour and of the immigrant outsider who remade himself as an English gentleman. It also carries the melancholy of vanished fame: Arlen is now little read, and his name often survives only as a footnote to the 1920s or as the answer to a trivia question about The Green Hat.

Homonyms and related names. The string Arlen also exists independently of Michael Arlen:

  • Irish surname: anglicised forms such as Arlen have been linked to Gaelic Ó hArlain or Ó hAirleáin, "descendant of Arlan", with airle meaning "pledge, surety". The connection is plausible but the spelling is unstable and the sources disagree.
  • English place-name surname: some genealogists derive Arlen from Harland or Harlan, "hare land", a habitational name.
  • Given name: Arlen became a modest American given name in the twentieth century, used for both men and women and often associated with Arlene.
  • Harold Arlen: the American composer of "Over the Rainbow" (born Hyman Arluck), whose stage surname is unrelated to Michael Arlen's coinage but is often confused with it.
  • Arlen, Texas: the fictional setting of the animated series King of the Hill; an independent pop-culture use of the name, unrelated to the novelist.

Literary and social significance. Michael Arlen is a case study in self-invention. His name change was not merely cosmetic: it marked his passage from an Armenian merchant family in Lancashire to the drawing rooms of literary London. D. H. Lawrence, a friend and early mentor, put him into Lady Chatterley's Lover as the playwright Michaelis. Aldous Huxley, Nancy Cunard and George Moore were part of his circle. The name "Arlen" thus stands at the intersection of immigration, assimilation, celebrity culture and the professionalisation of authorship in the early twentieth century.

Modern usage. Today the word is almost always a proper noun. It appears in literary histories, in film credits, in biographies of the 1920s, and in the adjective Arlenesque. As a search term it is ambiguous, because it collides with the Irish surname, the given name and the composer. In the source sentence — a list of fashionable and exotic objects noticed at Cannes — "Michael Arlen" functions as a cultural shorthand for the sophisticated, slightly scandalous Riviera set of the 1920s.
