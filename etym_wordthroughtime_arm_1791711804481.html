- [Old English / Early Middle English] - Denoting the human upper limb (as a Germanic anatomical term) while the Latinate military sense entered via Anglo-Norman to describe equipping knights with weaponry and plate armor - Utilitarian, martial, and chivalric connotation.
- [Late Middle English & Renaissance] - Expansion into collective weaponry ('arms'), heraldic coats of arms, and defensive mobilization ('a call to arms') - Patriotic duty, defensive nobility, and sovereign authority.
- [17th & 18th Century] - Metaphoric extension to branches of government, military divisions ('naval arm', 'infantry arm'), and geographical offshoots ('arm of the sea') - Bureaucratic organization, imperial expansion, and strategic administration.
- [19th & 20th Century] - Modern technological militarization ('arms race', 'nuclear arming'), legal codifications (the right to bear arms), and technical mechanical appendages (robotic arm, tone arm) - Industrialized warfare, geopolitical tension, and mechanization.
- [Contemporary Usage] - Extended metaphorically to informational leverage ('armed with data', 'arming consumers with facts') and bureaucratic power ('the long arm of the law') - Strategic preparedness, civic vigilance, and digital empowerment.
