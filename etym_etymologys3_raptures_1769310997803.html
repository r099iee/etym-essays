The biography of raptures is one of the most dramatic semantic climbs in the English lexicon: a word born in violence — the snatching, seizing, and carrying off of prey, plunder, and women — ends its career as the name of the highest human joy, the delight that sweeps the soul away. To trace the change is to watch a whole culture re-imagine the meaning of being taken.

The violent root: PIE and Latin
  • The chain begins with a Proto-Indo-European root reconstructed as *rep- (some scholars give *reup- or *rāp-), meaning "to snatch, seize, tear away, carry off by force." It is a verb of grasping and predation, felt across the ancient world: its Germanic cousins give words like bereave and reave, "to rob, plunder."
  • In Italic the root produced Latin rapere, attested from the third century BCE in the full violence of its meaning — to seize and drag, to plunder, to abduct. The famous "Rape of the Sabine Women" is a rape in the old sense: the forcible carrying off of the women of the Sabines by the men of early Rome. The English word "rape" itself is the same word, Latin rapere, and for centuries meant only "seizure, abduction."
  • From rapere Latin built a cluster of derivatives that kept the force: rapidus, "rapid" (literally "seizing along, sweeping"), rapax, "grasping, rapacious," rapina, "plunder, robbery," and the participle raptus, "seized, carried off." The noun raptus named the act of carrying off — a kidnapping, an abduction, a seizure.
The Christian turn: from abduction to ecstasy
  • The great change happens inside medieval Christianity. The Latin Bible described Saint Paul as being "caught up" into the third heaven (raptum in the Vulgate); the mystical tradition took this literally as the model of the soul's highest experience. In prayer and vision the soul is not lifted by its own effort but seized by God, snatched out of the body and carried up — raptura, the act of being rapt.
  • Thus the same word that described a wolf seizing its prey now described the soul seized by divine love. The violence did not disappear; it was sublimated. To be rapt was still to be taken by force, but the force was love, and the taking was salvation. The noun raptura in Christian Latin named this holy seizure and the ecstatic joy that flooded the soul in it.
  • In Middle French this became rapture, and around 1600 the word entered English, first in exactly this religious sense: a carrying up to heaven, a spiritual ecstasy. The earliest English uses are mystical — the rapture of the saints, of the soul, of divine contemplation.
Secularisation and the plural (17th–19th centuries)
  • In the seventeenth century (the age of Milton, Donne, and the metaphysical poets) rapture steps down from the altar into the world. Poets discover that music, beauty, love, and nature can also "rapture" the soul — carry it off as completely as grace does. The word begins to mean any intense, overwhelming delight.
  • The plural raptures emerges in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to name the outward explosions of that delight — cries, exclamations, transports of praise. In Jane Austen's England, to be "in raptures" over a picture, a view, or a concert is the mark of refined (or mocked) sensibility; the phrase could be genuine enthusiasm or gently satirised affectation.
  • By the nineteenth century raptures is a fully secular, aesthetic, and even social word, while the singular rapture retains both the everyday sense of ecstatic joy and, in religious discourse, its ancient mystical meaning.
The modern revival: the theological Rapture
  • In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries evangelical theology revived the capitalised Rapture — the doctrine that at the end of time believers will be caught up bodily into the air to meet Christ (1 Thessalonians 4:17). The modern theological term is a direct descendant of the medieval mystical raptura, and it shows how far the word can travel: the Latin image of being seized and carried off has now been used for two thousand years, from plundering legions to the Last Day.
  • Today raptures survives chiefly in the literary set phrase "in raptures," in poetic prose, and in the theological Rapture. Every modern use still carries, in its bones, the Latin verb of violent taking — a reminder that the highest joy was once imagined as being seized by an irresistible power.
  • The word's history is thus a perfect case of semantic sublimation: meaning has moved upward, from the predatory grasp of a raping wolf or soldier to the ecstatic transport of a soul swept off its feet by joy. The image never changes — being taken — only what does the taking and how it feels.
