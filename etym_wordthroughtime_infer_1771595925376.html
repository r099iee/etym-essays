- [16th century: to carry into a conclusion] - 'Infer' enters English meaning to bring in as a conclusion, to derive by reasoning from evidence; connotation is logical and neutral - the movement of the mind from premises to a judgment.
- [18th-19th century: the reader's act] - The word settles as the name of the reader's or hearer's own construction - to infer is to carry a meaning beyond the letter of what is said; connotation becomes attentive and slightly accusatory, the act of building a conclusion the speaker may disown.
- [Contemporary sense] - To deduce or conclude from evidence and reasoning, and (in a long-running usage note) distinct from 'imply'; connotation is precise and logical - the scrupulous inference that usually follows but is not compelled, 'his conclusion, not mine.'
