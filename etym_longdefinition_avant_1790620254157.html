Avant survives in English almost exclusively as the first half of the French loan avant-garde, “advance guard,” now the universal label for art, music, literature, and ideas that push ahead of the main body — experimental, radical, ahead of convention. Standing alone in your context (“an affair of the avant garde”), it carries that same charge: of the forward party, the scouts, the ones out in front. Dictionaries record independent English uses — “avant theatre,” “avant fashion,” clipped journalism for avant-garde — but the standalone form is best understood as a combining fragment whose meaning is completed by garde.

Etymologically avant is simply French for “before,” both in time (“avant la guerre”) and in space (“en avant,” forward). Its migration into English rode entirely on the prestige of French military organization and later French artistic prestige: first as a term for the vanguard of an army, then, from the mid-19th century, for the vanguard of art and politics. To call alphabetic counterculture “an affair of the avant garde” is thus to place it in a lineage of self-conscious advance parties — Dada, Surrealism, modernist typography — deliberately marching ahead of bourgeois taste.

  • Preposition / adverb in French (source sense): “before, in front of, forward”; e.g. avant tout, “above all,” aller de l’avant, “to move forward.” The bridge to English is direct loan: English speakers met the word inside the fixed phrase and rarely outside it.
  • Military vanguard (first English sense, 15th–19th c.): the avant-garde or vantguard as the leading division of an army, opposed to the rearguard; semantic logic is spatial — those stationed avant, before the main force.
  • Artistic and cultural vanguard (dominant since c. 1850–1920): artists and movements that see themselves as an advance party clearing the way for a new society; the bridge is Saint-Simonian and later Marxist metaphor transferring military advance to historical and aesthetic advance.
  • Clipped attributive avant (20th c.): “avant film,” “avant cooking,” meaning experimental and cutting-edge; the bridge is ordinary English clipping of a familiar compound, the way psychedelic clips to psychedelia’s neighbours.
  • Temporal “ahead of its time”: “an avant sensibility”; the bridge is rotation from spatial front to temporal front — what is spatially ahead of the column is temporally ahead of its age.

Form and register: in English avant keeps its French nasal vowel /æˈvɒ̃/ (often anglicized toward “av-ant”), and the full phrase is usually hyphenated (avant-garde) as noun or adjective, though your source prints it open as “avant garde.” The tone is admiring but can tip into irony: to call something avant-garde may praise its daring or gently mock its obscurity. As a lone word, avant is a deliberate Gallicism — writers use it to signal cosmopolitan cultural literacy. Paired with garde it recovers its full military grammar: one word gives the direction (forward), the other gives the body (the guard), so that even readers who know no French feel the advance-party logic simply by holding the two halves together.
