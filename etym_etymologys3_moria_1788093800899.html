Introduction. Moria is the only word in this batch that is not a natural-language item with a documented history in a speech community. It is a proper name invented by J.R.R. Tolkien (1892–1973), a professional philologist, for his fictional world, and first published in The Hobbit (1937). Its “evolution” must therefore be told on two planes at once: the internal, invented history of the word inside Middle-earth, and the external history of its reception, spread and semantic drift in the real world from 1937 to the present. Both planes are unusually well documented, because Tolkien left extensive notes on his languages and because the word's afterlife in popular culture is observable and datable.

1. The philologist's workshop (1910s–1930s). Tolkien began building his Elvish languages as a young man and refined them all his life. Sindarin, the language of the Grey Elves, was deliberately modelled on Welsh in its consonant system and word-formation, and its vocabulary was built from roots Tolkien designed for euphony and internal consistency. Within that system the name Moria is transparent: mor “dark, black” plus iâ “void, abyss, gulf” yields “Black Chasm” or “Black Pit”. Tolkien himself gave this analysis in Appendix F of The Lord of the Rings, where he notes that the Dwarves never use the name and that it is the Elvish name for their great habitation after its ruin — a sociolinguistic detail: the word is a foreign label imposed from outside, carrying the fear of those who named it.

2. First publication (1937). In The Hobbit, chapter 17, the raven Roäc cries out to Dáin: “whose father you slew in Moria.” This is the name's debut in print, and it is worth stressing how little it explains: the sentence presupposes the whole history of the War of the Dwarves and Orcs, the murder of Thrór by the orc Azog in Third Age 2790, and the Battle of Azanulbizar in 2799, where Dáin beheaded Azog before Moria's East Gate. The word thus enters English literature as a name of dread whose full meaning is deferred — the mark of Tolkien's technique of “applicability”, in which proper names act as loaded symbols.

3. The deepening (1954–55). The Lord of the Rings makes Moria a full narrative location: the Doors of Durin with their riddle-inscription, the dark water of the gate-pool, the tomb of Balin in the Chamber of Mazarbul, the endless stairs and galleries, and the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, where Gandalf's battle with the Balrog — “Durin's Bane” — ends with both falling into the abyss. The name's connotation shifts from “ancient blood-feud” to “the place where the Company is broken and the wizard dies”, and then, with Gandalf's return, to a place of sacrifice and rebirth. The film adaptations (Ralph Bakshi's 1978 animation; Peter Jackson's 2001 The Fellowship of the Ring, whose Moria sequence won the Academy Award for visual effects) have made the name visually iconic: vast pillared halls, a sunless lake, a chasm crossed by a narrow bridge.

4. The biblical echo and Tolkien's answer. Because Moria is spelled like the Latinised form of Hebrew Moriyyāh (“Moriah”), readers early on asked whether Tolkien borrowed it. His replies, preserved in his letters (notably Letter 297 of July 1967), state that the name is Sindarin “Black Chasm” and that the resemblance to the biblical mount of the binding of Isaac was not intended, while allowing that the Hebrew name — familiar to him as a devout Catholic — may have surfaced from memory. The episode is instructive for lexicography: it is a textbook “false cognate”, where an invented form and an ancient form converge in spelling while descending from utterly different systems.

5. The Greek coincidence. Independently, Greek μωρία (mōría) “folly, madness” (from μωρός, “dull, foolish”) gives the same Latin-letter string, most famously in Erasmus's Moriae Encomium (1511), a pun on his friend Thomas More's name. A medievalist like Tolkien certainly knew this word; whether it fed his coinage is unknown and unknowable, and he never claimed it. The homography is a permanent feature of the word's lexicography: three distinct etyma — Sindarin, Hebrew and Greek — share one spelling.

6. Cultural detachment and semantic drift (1975–present). In 1975 the roguelike computer game Moria (ancestor of Angband and its descendants) made the name the archetype of the procedurally generated dungeon; from gaming it spread into role-playing, fantasy fiction, engineering metaphors for underground infrastructure, and journalism (“the Moria of neglected tunnels”, “digging too deep”). Each reuse peels the name further from its fiction while preserving the core image — a vast, dark, inhabited underground that was once someone's home. The phonetic shape — /ˈmɒriə/, stress on the first syllable, light final schwa — is unusually easy for speakers of most European languages to produce, which explains its effortless international spread.

7. Conclusion. Moria shows how a single invented word can acquire real history: designed in a philologist's notebooks, glossed by its author, loaded with narrative meaning across two books, answered-for in letters, and finally released into the wild of popular culture, where it now functions as a generic noun for subterranean vastness and catastrophe — while still, for readers of Tolkien, naming a specific, lamented kingdom of the Dwarves.
