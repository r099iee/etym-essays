- 14th Century - Enters Middle English from Old French and Latin; applied simultaneously to physical boiling liquid, searing atmospheric heat, and burning theological devotion in Chaucerian and devotional texts.
- 15th to 16th Century - Prominent in Protestant Reformation polemics and Tudor spiritual literature; characterizes the fiery ardor of martyr faith, penitential prayer, and burning zeal for holy scripture.
- 17th Century - Literal physical senses begin to recede into elevated poetic language (notably codified in the 1611 King James Bible's "fervent heat"), while psychological senses broaden into solemn oaths, earnest friendships, and patriotic allegiance.
- 18th Century (The Enlightenment) - Cultivated in sentimental literature and epistolary novels; denotes refined moral earnestness, benevolent philanthropy, and deep filial or romantic affection, shedding some of its violent ecstatic overtones.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Becomes a defining virtue of Victorian high seriousness and moral earnestness; ubiquitously used to express high-minded philanthropic hope, domestic piety, and sincere commitment to public reform.
- 20th to 21st Century - Preserved as a high-register term of dignified emotional warmth and sincere yearning; universally recognized in set phrases such as "fervent hope" or "fervent prayer," denoting deep, unfeigned sincerity free from commercial cynicism.
