BY THE WAY (phrasal discourse marker / adverb) — used parenthetically to introduce a digression, an incidental remark, or a piece of additional information that stands beside the main line of the conversation or argument. It signals to the hearer that what follows is a side-note: important enough to be said, but set apart from the main road of the discourse, to which the speaker intends to return or has just returned.

1. Core senses and usage

  • Introducing a digression or aside: "by the way, did you call him?" — the speaker steps off the main topic to add a tangential remark, then commonly returns to it.
  • Introducing new, parenthetical information: "He mentioned, by the way, that he had run into an old classmate" — the information is additional, offered as an aside.
  • Signalling a change or resumption of topic: in modern speech the phrase is heavily grammaticalized as a discourse marker for topic shifts, often softened into "BTW" in texting and informal writing.
  • Literary and archaizing literal sense: "along the road, on the way, during a journey" — the original spatial meaning, still audible in poetry and in older prose ("they fell by the way," "things said by the way" in the sense of things encountered en route).
  • Incidental manner sense: "in passing, incidentally, not as the main business" — the bridge between the spatial and the conversational uses ("it was said by the way, not as the burden of the speech").

2. Historical bridge and semantic reasoning

The phrase is a fossil of a spatial image. Old English be þæm wege meant literally "along the path, beside the road," where be is the preposition "by, alongside" and weg "way, path" stands in the dative case with the definite article. A remark made "by the way" was a remark made beside the road, while travelling — something picked up or observed in passing, off the main journey. When the phrase transferred from travel to discourse, the geometry stayed the same: a remark made "by the way" is one made beside the main road of the conversation, a side-thought lying beside the argument. By the sixteenth century this figurative use was established in dialogue, and by the nineteenth the phrase had become the ordinary, fully grammaticalized marker of the aside that it remains today.

3. Modern jargon and register uses

  • Informal discourse marker: the single most common use — an apology-free way of interrupting one's own discourse with a new point ("By the way, what time is it?").
  • Texting and social media: the abbreviation BTW, dating to the early internet era (Usenet, 1980s), now universal in messaging.
  • Editorial and academic prose: "by the way" introduces genuinely optional, often intriguing asides; critics (as in the context of this card) prize the "suggestive and penetrating things said by the way" — the insights dropped incidentally in a writer's digressions.
  • Related but distinct phrases: "by the by," "in passing," "incidentally," "parenthetically," "speaking of which," and the prepositional "by way of" (as in "by way of introduction"), which preserves the same spatial grammar but means "as a means of."

4. Usage notes

"By the way" is now a pragmatic particle: it carries little or no literal meaning and works almost entirely to manage the flow of conversation. Its spatial metaphor — stepping off the road — is largely faded but still faintly audible, which is why the phrase can be used self-consciously to soften an interruption ("by the way — sorry to digress"). It differs from "incidentally" in being warmer and more conversational, and from "speaking of which" in not requiring a previous topic. In careful prose it is best used sparingly; in speech it is one of the most frequent connective phrases in the language.
