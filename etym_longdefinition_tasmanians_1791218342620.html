Tasmanians is the plural noun form of Tasmanian, denoting natives or inhabitants of Tasmania, the island state of Australia, and by extension things characteristic of that island. In its primary modern sense it means people who live in or come from Tasmania, e.g. Tasmanians are known for wilderness tourism, without regard to ancestry. In historical and anthropological use, especially in older books, Tasmanians often means Aboriginal Tasmanians, the Indigenous peoples of the island before and after European colonisation.

The distinction matters because Tasmania has two overlapping peoples: the Palawa and related Aboriginal nations who have lived on lutruwita for over 40,000 years, and the settler and later multicultural population established after British colonisation in 1803. Modern careful style says Aboriginal Tasmanians or palawa for the first and Tasmanians for all islanders, while 19th- and early 20th-century texts often used Tasmanians alone to mean the Aboriginal peoples, particularly when discussing extinction, hollow-tree shelter and Stone Age life.

  • General inhabitant sense: modern Tasmanians as islanders, e.g. Tasmanians voted in the referendum, Tasmanian hospitality. Neutral civic meaning.
  • Aboriginal peoples sense: historical Tasmanians as the nine Indigenous nations, e.g. the Tasmanians slept in hollow trees, the language of the Tasmanians. The source sentence uses this sense.
  • Attributive extension: Tasmanian as adjective for fauna, flora and products, e.g. Tasmanian tiger or thylacine, Tasmanian devil, Tasmanian oak, Tasmanian wine, with plural Tasmanians rarely used for animals.
  • Extinction-discourse sense: the Tasmanians as a supposed extinct race in Victorian anthropology, e.g. the last of the Tasmanians, a usage now recognised as mythologising and inaccurate.
  • Descendant-community sense: contemporary Aboriginal Tasmanians reclaiming identity, language and land, e.g. Tasmanians reviving basketry, mutton-birding rights.

Secondary nuances arise from this layering. To say the Tasmanians in a 1910 history of dwelling evokes primitive shelterlessness and isolation, part of a stadial ladder from tree to cave to hut. To say Tasmanians in a 2020 newspaper evokes island pride, wilderness, MONA art and AFL debate. The bridge is geographic continuity: the same island name now carries settler-regional identity rather than solely Indigenous reference. Readers must therefore date the sense by the text's age and surrounding parallels like Veddahs and Bushmen, which signal the older anthropological meaning.

In natural history Tasmanian also functions as a species epithet, as in Tasmanian devil Sarcophilus harrisii and Tasmanian tiger Thylacinus cynocephalus, extinct on the mainland and surviving only, or formerly only, in Tasmania. The semantic bridge is insular endemism: being confined to Tasmania becomes definitional. In commerce Tasmanian denotes provenance premium for apples, salmon, whisky and timber.

In the source context, some, like the Tasmanians, slept in hollow trees, the word illustrates a minimal-shelter stage using a living people as proxy for prehistory. Ethnographically there is a kernel of truth in seasonal use of hollow eucalypts and bark lean-tos, but the framing flattens a complex seasonal round of hunting, coastal shellfish gathering and fire management into a single picturesque trait. Recognising the historical sense lets the modern reader both learn the shelter fact and critique the evolutionist frame.
