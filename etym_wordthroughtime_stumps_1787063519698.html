- Early 14th c. - stumpe truncated portion of a tree - borrowed from Low German or Dutch; concrete and literal
- 14th-16th c. - stump extends to any short blunt end: of a limb (a stump arm), of a tooth, of a candle
- 16th-17th c. - to stump: to reduce to a stump, to stub out; in dialect, to stump also meant to walk heavily and clumsily
- 17th c. - cricketing: the stump is a wicket upright; to stump a batter is to dismiss them by breaking the wicket — a legal term born on the pitch
- Early 19th c. - to be stumped meaning perplexed or outwitted - from the idea of being caught or tripped up; also up a stump = in difficulty
- 1820s US - stump speech / stumping: campaigning by standing on a tree stump and addressing crowds - a stump becomes a natural political platform
- Modern - stumps retains tree remains, cricket wicket posts, and the figurative stumped (bewildered); to stir one's stumps (dialect) means to get moving
