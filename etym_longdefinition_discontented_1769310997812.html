discontented (adjective) — dissatisfied, unhappy, or restlessly displeased with one's circumstances; lacking inner contentment. caprice (noun) — a sudden, unaccountable change of mood, opinion, or behaviour; a whim; an unpredictable impulse. This card carries BOTH root words and their full definitions below.

PART 1 — DISCONTENTED

Discontented describes a state of mind, not a single grievance. A discontented person is not merely annoyed by a specific thing; the displeasure colours the whole disposition. In Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Mrs. Bennet "when she was discontented, fancied herself nervous": the word captures a temperament that converts every small discomfort into self-dramatized suffering. It is the chronic, restless mood, distinct from dissatisfied, which is the neutral word for displeasure with a specific result — dissatisfied with the service, discontented with one's whole lot.

  • Forms: the noun discontent (the state or feeling of dissatisfaction, especially with the existing order — "political discontent"); the archaic adjective and rare verb discontent; and malcontent, the stronger, more political word for a discontented person hostile to authority.
  • Political resonance: "Now is the winter of our discontent" (Shakespeare, Richard III, c. 1592–94) made the noun the emblem of public dissatisfaction; "the discontented" can name a restive class or faction grumbling against its lot.
  • Semantic bridge: content descends from Latin contentus, past participle of continēre "to hold together, contain": the content person's desires are "contained" within internal boundaries like water in a vessel. Adding dis- ("un-") signals the "uncontained" spirit — desires overflowing into restless, spilling complaint. Contentment is enclosure; discontentment is overflow.
  • In context: Mrs. Bennet, "a woman of mean understanding, little information, and uncertain temper," "when she was discontented, fancied herself nervous" — a mind without internal boundaries converting ordinary displeasure into nervous complaint.

PART 2 — CAPRICE

Caprice names a sudden and unaccountable change of mood or behaviour — a whim that arrives without reason and leaves without warning. In Pride and Prejudice, Mr. Bennet is introduced as "so odd a mixture of quick parts, sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice" that his wife cannot understand him after three-and-twenty years: the word here describes a man governed by unpredictable, mocking impulses rather than steady principle. Caprice is the noun of the capricious person.

  • Whim and fancy: the everyday sense — a passing, unreasoned desire ("the caprice of the moment"); a sudden change of mind without adequate motive.
  • Artistic and musical term: the capriccio (Italian) or caprice (French) is a short, free, virtuosic piece of music that follows the composer's fancy rather than a fixed form (Bach's Capriccio on the Departure of a Beloved Brother, Paganini's 24 Caprices); in painting, a capriccio is a fantasy landscape assembling real buildings in impossible combinations (Canaletto, Piranesi).
  • Fickleness and inconstancy: the word implies unreliability — a person or force that acts on impulse cannot be depended on.
  • By extension of impersonal forces: "the caprices of the weather," "the caprices of fate" — the unpredictable, unaccountable behaviour of nature or fortune.
  • Semantic bridge: from Italian capriccio, literally "head-hedgehog" — capo "head" + riccio "hedgehog" — an image of hair bristling like hedgehog quills in a sudden shudder of terror; the physical start was transferred to a sudden start of the fancy, a whim. (A rival theory connects the word to capra, "she-goat": a goat-like leap.)
  • In context: Mr. Bennet's "caprice" is the very quality that makes him opaque: his wife's "mean understanding" cannot track a mind governed by sudden, mocking, unpredictable impulses.
