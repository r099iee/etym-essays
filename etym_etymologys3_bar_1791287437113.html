The musical bar in I may even have hummed a bar or two descends, surprisingly, from a Celtic word for a bristling point. PIE *bʰar- (bristle, point, projection) gave Proto-Celtic *barros (top, point) and Gaulish *barra (pointed branch, rod, barrier). Vulgar Latin borrowed barra from Gaulish for a rod or barrier — a good example of conquered Celts lending builders' vocabulary to Roman soldiers — and Old French barre generalised it to any rigid rod, beam, toll-bar or barrier. Middle English borrowed barre in the 13th century for rods, prison bars, sandbars, the bar of a court (the rail separating judges from pleaders) and the barrier of a tavern counter.

From rod to line is a short visual step. Medieval musical notation, developing from neumes to staff notation in the 11th–16th centuries, began drawing vertical lines through the staff to guide ensemble timing and mark metrical units. English musicians called these strokes bars because they barred or divided the staff like rails. By the 17th century bar had undergone metonymy: not only the vertical stroke but the musical segment it enclosed — the measure between two bar-lines — was called a bar. To hum a bar or two is therefore to hum one or two such measures: a scrap of tune demarcated as if by little fences. American English preserves the older logic by preferring measure for the content and bar-line for the stroke, while British English happily calls both the line and the content a bar.

  • Phonetic path: Gaulish *barra → Vulgar Latin barra → Old French barre /baʀə/ → Middle English barre /barə/ → Modern English bar /bɑː/ with loss of final -e and, in RP, loss of rhotic /r/ compensated by lengthening.
  • Semantic chain: pointed branch → rigid rod → barrier or rail → vertical stroke dividing the musical staff → metrical unit between strokes → any short tuneful phrase (hum a bar).
  • Socio-cultural context: Celtic woodwork, Roman military barriers, medieval courts with bars, Tudor taverns with bar counters, Renaissance choirs needing ensemble coordination, 19th-century pub music-hall singing and 20th-century jazz bars where bars of music meet bars serving drinks.
  • Literary attestations: Chaucer on bars of doors; Shakespeare on prisoners at the bar; Pepys on bars of music; 18th-century theory manuals on bars and time; Wodehouse on humming a bar, propping up the bar, and being barred from clubs — ringing all changes on one monosyllable.

Wodehouse's line exploits the word's cheerful musical sense at its most casual. To hum a bar or two is to be so light-hearted — perhaps after a drink at the bar, behind bars of song — that melody leaks out involuntarily. The comedy lies in understatement: not an aria, not a chorus, just a bar or two, hummed possibly out of tune. The same monosyllable elsewhere in Wodehouse means the counter where drinks are served (a barrier between publican and customer), the legal profession (called to the Bar, behind the court's bar), exclusion (barred from the Drones Club) and obstruction (bar to progress). All are the same rod-barrier metaphor turned to different social uses.

Modern music theory distinguishes bar (British) and measure (American) for a unit of a given number of beats (a bar of 4/4, a twelve-bar blues), with bar-lines, double bars, repeat bars and upbeats organising notation. Jazz and pop speak of bars as units of invention (spit sixteen bars, a four-bar intro), while pubs named The Bar serve drinks at a counter that is historically the same barrier. The humble bar thus spans carpentry, law, hospitality and art — a rod that learned to keep time. Note on debate: the Celtic origin via Gaulish *barra is the standard account (Meyer-Lübke, OED), but some Romance scholars once proposed a purely Latin *barra from *barrus; Celtic priority is now consensus because of Old Irish barr (top) and Welsh bar (top, point).
