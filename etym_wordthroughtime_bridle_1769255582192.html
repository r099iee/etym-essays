- Old English brīdel: the headgear of the horse - from its first recorded uses it already leads a double life, serving in Old English homilies as the moral curb on unruly will (the brīdel of the tongue).
- Medieval through Early Modern: the concrete sense dominates a chivalric, equestrian world of 'bridle and bit' and the horseman's road, while the moral sense keeps pace - to bridle one's tongue, unbridled passion.
- 1700s-1800s: the word branches into landscape and manners - 'bridle path' and 'bridleway' name the narrow rider's lane that a cart cannot pass, and 'to bridle at' an insult (a horse tossing its head against the bit) becomes a fixed expression of offended dignity.
- Contemporary: the riding sense stays everyday, but the figurative uses are the living core - 'unbridled fury,' 'to bridle one's temper' - while British law preserves 'bridleway' as a right of way for riders, walkers, and cyclists.
