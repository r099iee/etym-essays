cosmography (noun): the science or art of describing, mapping, and representing the general structure of the universe — in its classical and Renaissance sense, a unified discipline that combined astronomy (the heavens), geography (the earth), and oceanography (the seas) into a single picture of the world and its place in the cosmos.

1. Primary sense — the unified description of the universe

Cosmography is, etymologically, the "writing/description of the cosmos" (Greek kósmos "world, order" + -graphía "description, writing"). In its golden age (15th–17th centuries) it was the master-science that held together all knowledge of the world's physical structure:
  • Astronomical component — the mapping of the heavens: the sphere of the fixed stars, the paths of the planets, the earth's place at the centre of the Ptolemaic cosmos, and the celestial coordinate system of latitude and longitude extended into the sky.
  • Geographical component — the description of the earth: continents, oceans, rivers, mountains, the climate zones (the classical five zones from the torrid equator to the frigid poles), and the steadily growing inventory of newly discovered lands.
  • Cartographic output — cosmographers produced maps (the medieval mappae mundi, the great world maps of the Age of Discovery), globes, celestial charts, and "cosmographical" treatises that joined text and image in a single encyclopaedic whole.
In C. S. Lewis's Narnia, "Cosmography" appears in the curriculum of the prince — exactly the encyclopaedic world-science of the medieval and Renaissance schools, one of the noble arts a Renaissance prince was expected to know, sitting naturally beside Rhetoric, Heraldry, and Astronomy.

2. Historical development

  • The term was coined in Hellenistic Greece (Greek kosmographía, used by Strabo, c. 1st c. BCE) and carried into Latin (cosmographia) and the medieval encyclopaedic tradition.
  • In the Renaissance it flourished as the proudest of the descriptive sciences: Sebastian Münster's Cosmographia (1544), Gerardus Mercator's Atlas (1595), and Peter Heylyn's Cosmographie (1652) are monuments of the genre.
  • As astronomy and geography became separate, mathematically grounded sciences in the 17th–18th centuries, cosmography's unified scope unravelled, and the word receded into antiquarian and poetic use.

3. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings

  • Philosophical / model sense — by metaphor, a "cosmography" can be any overarching conceptual map or model of reality: a worldview, a systematic account of how the universe is ordered ("a cosmography of the mind," "a moral cosmography"). The Renaissance mapping of heavens and oceans transfers to the mapping of abstract orders.
  • Literary / poetic use — the word survives as a reverent, grand name for the whole spectacle of the created universe and for the attempt to represent it.
  • Historical scholarship — in the history of science, cosmography names the early-modern genre itself and the modern discipline that studies it.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: because cosmography was by definition the total written picture of the cosmos, the word easily widens from the concrete mapping of the physical world to the abstract "mapping" of any complete system of thought — the secondary sense performs the same act of comprehensive description, transferred from stars and continents to ideas and worldviews.

4. Related vocabulary

Cosmography belongs to a family built on Greek kósmos (cosmos, cosmic, cosmology, cosmopolitan, cosmetic, cosmonaut) and on -graphía (geography, biography, cartography, oceanography, photography). Its closest relative, cosmology, is now the theoretical physics of the universe's origin and structure; cosmography remains the descriptive, mapping counterpart — the writing of the world rather than the theory of its beginning.
