• Molasses: Similar because both are dense, highly viscous, sugary liquid syrups derived from plant processing; differs because molasses (or treacle) is the dark, bitter-sweet byproduct left after sugar cane juice is boiled and crystallized, featuring an intense earthy mineral flavor compared to clean, neutral sugar sirup.
  • Honey: Similar because both are thick, sweet, golden liquids used interchangeably as natural sweeteners and throat demulcents; differs because honey is a natural organic substance produced by bees from floral nectar through enzymatic digestion, whereas sirup is generally an artificially prepared aqueous solution of sucrose or extracted sap.
  • Treacle: Similar because both are concentrated sugar solutions utilized in baking and sweet confections; differs because treacle is a specifically British culinary term referring to dark, uncrystallized syrup (black treacle) or clarified golden syrup, carrying deeper caramelized notes.
  • Nectar: Similar because both evoke sweet, viscous, pleasant liquids consumed for nourishment or indulgence; differs because nectar refers botanically to the raw sugar-rich secretion of flowers or commercially to thick, unclarified fruit juice containing pulp, lacking the high sucrose density of concentrated sirup.
  • Elixir: Similar because both historically functioned as pharmaceutical liquid preparations designed to deliver remedies pleasantly; differs because an elixir is an hydro-alcoholic solution (containing ethanol and water) formulated to dissolve water-insoluble medicinal compounds, whereas a sirup is an aqueous, sugar-saturated vehicle.
  • Linctus: Similar because both are viscous medicinal liquids formulated to soothe irritation in the respiratory tract; differs because a linctus is an exclusively medicinal preparation intended to be sipped slowly without water to coat the pharynx, whereas sirup encompasses broad culinary, industrial, and pharmaceutical categories.
  • Cordial: Similar because both denote sweet, concentrated fruit-based liquids often diluted before drinking; differs because a cordial in British usage is a non-alcoholic concentrate diluted with water, while in historical contexts it referred to an invigorating alcoholic medicinal tonic.
