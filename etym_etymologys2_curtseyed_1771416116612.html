1. PIE *gʰer- “to enclose, surround” — Pokorny records two homophonous roots, *gher- “to grasp, take” and *gher- “to enclose, surround”; the courtesy line descends from the enclosure sense (handbooks debate the exact membership of each). 2. PIE *gʰórtos “enclosure, fold, yard” — the nominal derivative of *gʰer-; cf. Greek χόρτος khórtos “enclosed feeding-yard, fodder” (Mycenaean ko-to, Linear B tablets). 3. Latin hortus “garden, an enclosed plot” — attested from the 3rd c. BCE; the enclosed garden of a Roman townhouse. 4. Latin cohors, cohort-is “enclosed yard; a throng, a retinue; (military) a cohort” — a compound of co- “together” + hortus, “the shared enclosure”; accusative cohortem (Caesar, De Bello Gallico, 1st c. BCE). 5. Vulgar Latin *cortem “yard, court” — from the accusative of cohors/cors; gives Old French cort (9th–11th c.) “royal court, residence, courtyard” (later cour, with court-yard and curtilage among its descendants). 6. Old French cortois / curtois “courtly, refined” (12th c.) and cortoisie / curteisie “courtly conduct, courtesy” — the chivalric adjective and noun built on cort. 7. Anglo-French curteisie → Middle English curteisie “courteous conduct” (13th c.) → English courtesy “polite, courteous behaviour” (13th c.). 8. English curtsey / curtsy “the feminine gesture of respect” (16th c.) — a contracted pronunciation of “courtesy” applied to the bodily courtesy — and its past tense English curtseyed (16th–17th c.), the root word. Note on the debate: the chain from Latin cohors to Old French cort is secure, but two points are debated — (a) whether PIE *gher- “enclose” and *gher- “grasp” are one root or two homophones, and (b) whether Latin hortus/cohort may show influence from the closely related Greek χόρτος; the mainstream analysis treats the Latin forms as straightforward reflexes of the “enclose” sense.
