PINED is the past form of the verb pine, itself composed of pine + the past-tense suffix -ed. The chain below follows pine to its PIE root, then shows the suffix. Chain for the verb PINE: 1. PIE *kwey- "to pay, compensate, avenge" (root, with the o-grade *kwoy- in the derived noun); reconstructed from cognates such as Greek poine "penalty, blood-price" and Avestan kaena "punishment, revenge"; c. 4th millennium BCE. 2. Greek poine "penalty, retribution, blood-money, compensation" (Homeric; e.g. in the Iliad, where the poine of a slain man is the blood-price to be paid). 3. Latin poena "punishment, penalty, pain, sorrow" (borrowed from Greek poine; attested 2nd-1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero, where poena is legal punishment; the spelling -oe- shows the Greek diphthong /oi/ Latinized). 4. Old English pin "pain, torment, punishment" (a borrowing from Latin poena into West Germanic; attested c. 900; cf. Old Saxon pina, Old High German pina). 5. Old English pinian "to torment, torture, afflict, cause pain" (weak verb; attested c. 1000; formed from the noun pin with the verb suffix -ian). 6. Middle English pinen "to torment, cause pain, suffer; (from the 14th c.) to waste away with longing, to long intensely" (attested 12th-14th c.; Chaucer uses pine and pinen of lovesick longing). 7. Modern English pine "to waste away with longing, grief, or confinement; to long intensely (pine for)" (verb) — past tense and participle pine + -ed → PINED. Chain for the suffix -ED: 1. Proto-Germanic *-d- (the dental suffix that forms the past tense of weak verbs; reconstructed). 2. Old English -ede / -ode / -ade (weak past endings; e.g. pin-ede "tormented," luf-ode "loved"). 3. Middle English -ed(e) / -d (past ending). 4. Modern English -ed, attached to pine → PINED (pronounced /d/ after voiced sounds, /t/ after voiceless, /Id/ after t and d). Crucial distinction (debate note): the verb pine is etymologically UNRELATED to the pine tree, whose English name comes from Latin pinus (via Old English pin and Old French pin). The popular image of a tree "pining away" is a folk etymology: the verb's "wasting away" sense arose because to be tormented (Old English pinian) came to mean to be consumed — body and spirit — by grief or longing. Both words converge on the spelling pine, but their histories are separate, and no stage between them should be conflated.
