- [Late 14th c. Middle English] — Entered via Old French fugitif and Latin fugitīvus, denoting almost exclusively runaway serfs, rebellious bondservants, and escaped felons evading sovereign feudal justice — [perception: a dangerous outlaw, rebel against divine feudal hierarchy, or stolen piece of bonded human property fleeing their rightful lord]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — Poetically broadened by Renaissance dramatists and metaphysical poets into an adjective for fleeting time, evanescent youth, passing shadows, and shifting spiritual states — [perception: celebrated in lyric verse as an evocative emblem of mortal fragility, earthly transience, and the tragic slipperiness of earthly delights]
- [18th c. Enlightenment & Industrial Era] — Adopted into specialized artisanal and printing terminology, naming volatile chemical spirits, fugitive pigments that faded under sunlight, and ephemeral pamphlets ("fugitive pieces") — [perception: technical marker of material instability, physical volatility, and fragile print ephemera lacking enduring durability]
- [19th c. Antebellum American Era] — Heavily politicized and charged with profound moral polarization surrounding the Fugitive Slave Laws (1793, 1850), pitting abolitionist resistance against Southern slaveholder legal claims — [perception: contested between a legalistic slur denoting escaped property and a heroic abolitionist badge of courage denoting a righteous freedom-seeker]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Codified in international criminal law (extradition treaties, Interpol "red notices") while simultaneously institutionalized in environmental regulations ("fugitive emissions") and modern media narratives ("the fugitive" as archetype of the wrongly accused hero) — [perception: dual perception as a standard legal designation for high-profile suspects on the run, alongside a clinical engineering term for uncontrolled industrial atmospheric leakage]
