The adjective GRATUITOUS reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷerH-, "to praise, welcome, or show favour," a root that describes the physical and social gesture of extending goodwill toward another person — speaking well of them or receiving them with favour. The same root appears in the Indo-Iranian family: Sanskrit gīr (गीर्), "praise, invocation, song of favour," and the verb gṛṇāti, "he praises, welcomes," preserve the underlying idea of voiced goodwill, as does Avestan gar-. The root also generated the Latin cluster that matters for English: grātus, "pleasing, beloved, grateful, thankful"; gratia, "favour, grace, charm, gratitude," the ancestor of English grace; grātēs, "thanks"; and grātulārī, "to congratulate." In the Italic branch the root yielded Proto-Italic *grātos, "pleasing, agreeable," which became classical Latin grātus, meaning "pleasing, dear, grateful, or thankful." Latin then built a small family on the word: the adjective grātuitus was formed on the adverbial base grātīs or grātuītō, "out of favour, without payment or reward," and it meant "done freely, spontaneously, voluntarily, without compensation." Its original value was entirely positive: something gratuitous was a gift given out of pure goodwill, with no obligation attached — a gratuitous service, a gratuitous act of generosity. This is the sense enshrined in the sibling nouns: a gratuity is a tip given freely, and Latin gratis means "free of charge." The word passed into Old French as gratuit and entered English in the mid-seventeenth century (c. 1650s), initially in legal, financial, and theological contexts, where it signified "given freely without charge": a gratuitous benefit, a gratuitous contract, gratuitous grace in theology, where God's grace is freely given, unearned, and unowed. For roughly two centuries this neutral-to-positive sense held sway. Then, during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the adjective underwent a striking semantic shift.
  • The mechanism of the shift. Something given gratuitously is given without obligation or necessity — and once the sense of "without necessity" emerged, the word could describe any addition made with no sufficient reason. A gratuitous remark, an unprovoked and unjustified remark, came to be seen as one added without cause; the free gift that needed no justification became the addition that lacked all justification.
  • The new dominant sense. By the twentieth century the pejorative sense triumphed: gratuitous violence, gratuitous nudity, a gratuitous insult, gratuitous detail. In media criticism, gratuitous is the standard accusation that a scene or image serves no dramatic or thematic purpose — it is there simply because it could be added for free, so to speak, and therefore should not be.
  • The register split. The older "free of charge" sense survives in technical and legal documents (a gratuitous bailment, a gratuitous agency) and in the everyday nouns gratuity and gratis, while in ordinary and academic speech gratuitous is overwhelmingly condemnatory.
In Empson's preface, "the gratuitous puzzles of definition" that he has cleared from his text are exactly the additions that lack all justification — self-imposed complications that obscure the real ambiguities, the ones that repay study. The word performs the precise critical gesture the sentence needs: it dismisses manufactured confusion while reserving the positive word "real" for the genuine puzzles that deserve attention. Phonologically, the word's triple rhythm — gră-tū-ĭ-tous, with the heavy first syllable, the long ū, and the light middle i — gives it a measured, slightly clinical delivery that suits its formal, evaluative register in scholarship, while the modern media sense has made it one of the most familiar words of aesthetic and moral censure in the language.
