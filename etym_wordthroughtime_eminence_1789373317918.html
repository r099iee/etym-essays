- Late 14th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French *eminence* and Latin *eminentia*; initially utilized in scholastic philosophy and translations (Chaucer) to denote high physical elevation, loftiness, or superior virtue and nobility.
- 15th to 16th Century - Spread through Renaissance humanist literature to describe supreme intellectual mastery, scholarly distinction, and artistic excellence standing above ordinary talents.
- 17th Century - Marked by monumental institutionalization: in 1630, Pope Urban VIII issued a papal decree officially reserving the honorific title "His Eminence" exclusively for Roman Catholic Cardinals; simultaneously, Cardinal Richelieu's shadowy advisor Father Joseph inspired the enduring political idiom *éminence grise* ("grey eminence").
- 18th to 19th Century - Consolidated in topographical surveys (denoting a hill, knoll, or military observation point) and medical anatomy (denoting physiological ridges and bony projections such as the thenar eminence).
- 20th Century - Widely adopted in modern literary criticism (e.g., Lytton Strachey's *Eminent Victorians*, Proustian scholarship) to dissect cultural canonization, institutional prestige, and artistic supremacy.
- 21st Century - Continues as an elevated formal term for supreme intellectual, artistic, or academic stature, as well as maintaining its precise anatomical, topographical, and ecclesiastical designations.
