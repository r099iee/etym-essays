Origins: two Old English verbs in one paradigm

Bade is the past tense of modern English bid, and any account of it must begin with a famous merger. Modern “bid” is the conflation of two distinct Old English verbs: biddan (a strong verb of the fifth class, meaning “to ask, pray, entreat; to command, require”) and bēodan (a strong verb of the second class, meaning “to offer, proclaim, announce, command”). The past tense bade descends specifically from biddan's past tense bæd (also bǣd); the modern spelling with final -e reflects the Middle English lengthened vowel bāde.

The *gʷʰedʰ- root: “to ask, to pray, to wish”

The verb biddan descends from Proto-Germanic *bedjaną “to beg, ask, pray”, from the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷʰedʰ- (also written *gʷhedʰ-) “to ask, to pray, to wish”. This ancient root — whose initial consonant is the labio-velar that the Germanic languages shifted to b- — is attested across the family: Greek thessasthai “to pray, ask”, Sanskrit bādh- / gadh- “to press, ask”, Old Irish guidid “to pray” (whence guide), and Germanic *bed- everywhere. The semantic core is petition: the movement of a lesser person toward a greater one, asking. This is visible in the English words built on the same Germanic stem: bead (originally “a prayer”, whence the rosary bead), bedesman “one paid to pray”, and beadle.

The *bʰewdʰ- root: “to announce, offer”

The second ancestor, bēodan, descends from Proto-Germanic *beudaną “to offer, announce, command”, from the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰewdʰ- “to wake, perceive, make aware, announce”. This root is the ancestor of English bode (“to announce, to foretell”, as in “bodes ill”), of the archaic noun bode “message, messenger”, and of English behest “command” (from Old English bēhǣs). The two roots differ beautifully in orientation: *gʷʰedʰ- faces upward, from supplicant to authority (asking, praying); *bʰewdʰ- faces downward, from authority to people (commanding, announcing, offering). Modern “bid” and its past “bade” straddle both: “bade them depart” commands; “bade farewell” announces; the archaic “bade for mercy” still asks. The dual ancestry explains why “bid” is so polysemous and why its forms are so irregular.

Old English stage

In Old English the two verbs lived separate lives. Biddan “to ask, pray, command” had past bæd and participle beden; it is common in the religious and legal prose of the period — people “bade” God for mercy and “bade” their servants to work. Bēodan “to command, offer, announce” had past bēad and participle boden; it is the verb of announcements, proclamations, and offers. Both are frequent in Beowulf: a lord bēad treasure (offered) and bæd his men (commanded or entreated).

Middle English stage: the merger

During the Middle English period the two paradigms collapsed. The infinitive forms bidde(n) and bēode(n) converged on bidden/beden; the pasts bæd and bēad merged on bad, bade, bod; and the participles merged on boden/bidden. Chaucer's Canterbury Tales use “bid” and “bade” in all four senses — ask, command, offer, proclaim — often in the same sentence, showing that the merger was complete in ordinary speech by the 14th century. The long vowel in “bade” (Middle English bāde, rhyming with “made”) reflects Middle English lengthening in open syllables and/or the influence of bēad.

Early Modern English: at home in Shakespeare and the Bible

In Early Modern English “bade” is fully alive and unmarked. Shakespeare uses it constantly — “I bade them take away the crown”, “bade me tell no one”, “bade good night” — and the King James Bible uses it in commands (“he bade them to sit down”). The greeting formula “bade farewell” is standard. At this date the word carries no archaism at all; it is the natural literary past of “bid”, pronounced /beɪd/ (rhyming with “made”).

Modern English: retreat into formula and literature

From the 18th century “bade” and “bid” in the command and ask senses begin to retreat before “told”, “ordered”, “asked”, and “said”. The auction sense of “bid” (“to offer a price”), which continues bēodan's “offer” sense, stays current — but its past is normally “bid” (“I bid fifty pounds”), not “bade”. The command sense survives in elevated and formulaic use (“bade him wait”, “bade her enter”), and the greeting sense survives robustly in the fixed formulas “bade farewell”, “bade good night”, “bade welcome”. In the 19th century, historical and Gothic novelists deploy “bade” deliberately for atmosphere; in the 20th century, fantasy and historical fiction — including Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) — use it to lend ceremony and archaic dignity to formal farewells. In contemporary English, “bade” is perceived as poetic, biblical, or old-fashioned; many modern speakers now pronounce it /bæd/ under the influence of the spelling, while conservative RP retains /beɪd/.

Phonetic and orthographic notes

  • Old English bæd → Middle English bade (lengthened /aː/) → Early Modern /beɪd/ (the Great Vowel Shift raising long /aː/ to /eɪ/).
  • The spelling bade was fixed in Middle English and has never changed; the final silent -e marks the historical long vowel.
  • Modern pronunciation splits: conservative RP /beɪd/ (continuing the /eɪ/ of the Great Vowel Shift) and common /bæd/ (a spelling pronunciation levelling the vowel to the modern short-a pattern).

Debated and nuanced points

  • The exact moment and mechanism of the biddan/bēodan merger is not perfectly documented; handbooks agree the conflation was complete by the 13th–14th century but differ on whether it proceeded through the infinitive or the past tense. Some grammarians analyse “bade” as continuing biddan's bæd; others allow contamination from bēad for the vowel. Both analyses agree on the form and on the dual ancestry.
  • The PIE root *gʷʰedʰ- “to ask, pray” is widely accepted, but the exact set of cognates is debated in detail (e.g., whether the Sanskrit forms belong); the Germanic derivation of *bedjaną from it is standard.
