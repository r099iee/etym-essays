The biography of the name Cerdic is unusual: a Germanic king who carries a Celtic name, and a name whose very spelling preserves the evidence of the culture-contact that produced it. The story begins in the reconstructed depths of Proto-Indo-European with the root *keh₂-, "to desire, wish, love," which generated the adjective *kāro-, "dear, beloved." This single root split into great branches. In the Indo-Iranian branch it produced Vedic Sanskrit kāma, "love, desire," the name of the Hindu god of desire, and it survives today in Hindi kāmnā, "wish, longing." In the Italic branch it produced Latin cārus "dear," and through Latin caritās ("dearness, high esteem") our English charity and cherish; French cher, Italian caro, and Spanish caro are all its descendants. But it was the Celtic branch that mattered for our word: Proto-Celtic *karā-, "to love," formed the common personal name *Caratācos, literally "beloved one," attested across the entire Celtic world and borne, in its Roman-period form Caratacus, by the great British chieftain of the Silures whose resistance to the emperor Claudius is told by Tacitus.

In medieval Welsh that name lived on as Caradog, and in a closely related derivative, Ceredig, it named a 5th–6th-century ruler of the territory still called Ceredigion (modern Cardiganshire). Into this Brittonic world came the West Saxons, and it is here that the fascinating linguistic evidence appears. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, recording the year 495, names the founder of Wessex as Cerdic. In native Old English, an initial c before a front vowel was automatically palatalized to the sound of English "ch" — ceorl became "churl," cinn became "chin" — but Cerdic is always written and presumably pronounced with a hard velar /k/. A native West Saxon would not naturally have produced that velar sound in that position; the un-palatalized spelling therefore functions like a fossil, showing that the name entered Old English already containing a hard k, i.e. that it was borrowed from a Brittonic source rather than coined within the language.

This small orthographic detail carries a large historical claim. Even the founders of the "English" royal house may have carried British names, testimony to the intermarriage and acculturation between incoming Saxons and native Britons. The West Saxon pedigree as it survives runs from Cerdic down through Cynric, Ceawlin, and on to Alfred and the later kings of England, and it was constructed to give the dynasty legitimacy and antiquity; a British name at the top of that pedigree was no embarrassment to the chroniclers, or was simply inherited without reflection. Modern historians are accordingly divided: some take Cerdic as a real, possibly British, warlord who founded a kingdom among the Britons of the south; others read the entire early genealogy as legendary back-projection. Either way, the name itself survives as a monument to the meeting of Celt and Saxon.

On this card, the name is pressed into a different service. In the 19th-century historiography of the passage, "the followers of Ida and Cerdic" stand for the pagan, pre-Christian world of the Anglo-Saxon settlers — the "superstitions of the Elbe," the old Germanic religion brought from the continental homeland — set against the zealous Christian dynasties founded by Alaric, Theodoric, Clovis, and Alboin in the continental provinces of the Roman empire. Cerdic, whose very name is a Celtic loan, here becomes the emblem of heathen Germanic custom; the irony is that the name and the culture it now represents belong to two different worlds fused by conquest and intermarriage. The word thus carries, in one compact form, the whole story of how Britain was made — Celtic name, Germanic bearer, and a later national myth woven around both.

  • Sound change: the crucial detail is the un-palatalized hard C- before e, a fossil of borrowing from Brittonic into Old English.
  • Semantic drift: "beloved" (Celtic praise-name) → royal ancestor-name of Wessex → historiographical symbol of pagan Anglo-Saxon conquest.
  • Literary attestations: Tacitus (Caratacus), the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (Cerdic, year 495), and the 19th-century historians (Macaulay and his contemporaries).
  • Cognate family: Latin cārus, charity, cherish; Sanskrit kāma; Hindi kāmnā; Welsh caru, Caradog.
