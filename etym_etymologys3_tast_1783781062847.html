The history of TAST, the Miltonic spelling of taste, is a textbook case of how a word can travel from the sense of touch to the sense of taste and then up into the abstract reaches of aesthetics and morality. Its journey runs from the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *tag-, "to touch, handle, grasp," through Latin, through the speech of early medieval France, into Middle English, and finally into the early modern spelling wars that produced Milton's deliberately archaic form.

From touch to tongue: the Indo-European and Latin foundations. The root *tag- is one of the most productive in the whole Indo-European stock. In Latin it produced tangō, tangere, "to touch," with its nasal-infixed present ta-n-g-ō, and from its past-participial stem tāct- grew a magnificent English loanword family: tact, tactile, contact, intact, tangent, contingent, contiguity, contagion, and integer (literally "untouched"). The sound change to notice here is the assimilation ct → st in the frequentative verb built on the tāct- stem: Latin *tax(t)āre / *tastāre, "to touch repeatedly, to try, to test." This frequentative, meaning "to handle or test by touching," is the direct ancestor of the whole Romance family.

The French pivot: taster means touch first, taste second. Old French inherited the verb as taster, and here the pivotal semantic shift occurred. In medieval French, taster primarily meant "to touch, to feel, to handle, to probe"; the modern French tâter ("to feel, to grope") preserves this tactile core almost unchanged, as does tâtonner ("to grope about"). But because tasting was conceived as a refined kind of touching — the tongue being the fingertip of the mouth — the verb acquired the specialised sense "to try by the mouth, to taste." This double life is not unique to French: Italian tastare likewise means "to touch, feel, probe," and the tastiera is the keyboard, literally "the thing that is touched," while Spanish tastar and the Romance branch generally carried both meanings.

English: the tactile sense dies, the gustatory sense reigns. English borrowed the verb tasten around 1300 with both meanings intact — to touch or handle, and to sample food or drink. Middle English medical and devotional texts still use "to taste" for "to touch" (as in feeling a wound), and the noun taste followed shortly after. But whereas French kept the two meanings apart with distinct verbs (tâter for touch, goûter for taste), English collapsed them: the tactile sense faded from the word by the end of the Middle English period, and taste became the name of the fifth sense. This was the decisive narrowing that made all later metaphor possible.

The rise of metaphor: from the palate to the mind. Once taste was fixed as the gustatory sense, it was ready for its great metaphorical ascent. Already in Middle English "to taste of" could mean "to experience, to have a share of" (Chaucer writes of tasting of "blisse"). The Bible intensified this: "taste and see that the Lord is good" (Psalm 34), and Christ's saying that he will not "taste" of the fruit of the vine until the Kingdom — where to taste is to experience death or joy. Milton exploits precisely this register: the "mortal tast" is simultaneously a physical act of eating, a sampling, and a bitter experience of mortality.

The decisive metaphorical transfer to aesthetics came in the late 17th century. Because a physical taste is judged immediately and intuitively as pleasant or unpleasant, writers began to speak of a "taste" for the beautiful — an immediate, intuitive faculty of aesthetic judgement. The French theorist Jean de La Bruyère and then, most influentially in English, the essayists of the 18th century (Addison and Steele in The Spectator, and later Alexander Pope in An Essay on Criticism) turned "taste" into the keyword of Enlightenment aesthetics: the refined, trained capacity to judge art, literature, and manners. This is the sense in which we say "good taste," "a man of taste," or "tasteless," and it is the historical bridge from the tongue to the salon: tasting the beautiful is the mind's version of tasting wine.

From aesthetics to morality and preference. From the aesthetic faculty the word extended further in two directions. First, into morality and social propriety: by the 19th century "in bad taste" meant not merely unaesthetic but tactless, indecent, offensive — a joke or remark "in poor taste" violates social decorum, not just artistic canons. Second, into subjective preference: "not to my taste" and "to each his own taste" democratise the faculty into mere inclination, so that taste now names whatever an individual happens to like, from music to interior design. In contemporary digital culture the word has been recharged again: having "taste" in curation, aesthetics, and media is prized as a mark of discernment, and "tastemaker" describes the influencer who sets standards.

Milton's tast in its historical context. Milton's tast (1667) is not a dialect form but a normal early modern spelling — the final silent -e was still optional in print, and taste/tast alternated in the same books. Milton chose the shorter form deliberately, giving the word an archaic, scriptural gravity and fitting it into his verse metre. The word sits at the hinge of the semantic history: it still carries the older "sampling, experience" register ("whose mortal tast / Brought Death into the World"), while the aesthetic and moral senses were even then rising around it in the prose of his contemporaries. The spelling tast has since been fossilised in the poem and survives in scholarly editions and literary allusion as a deliberate archaism — a small word that compresses within itself the entire movement from touch to taste to judgement.

Sound changes along the way. The chief sound change shaping the word is the late Latin/early Romance assimilation of ct to st in the frequentative, which produced the Romance stem tast-. In Old French the vowel was an open a, which in English passed through the Great Vowel Shift of the 15th–17th centuries to the modern diphthong /eɪ/: Old French taster /taˈster/ → Middle English tāsten /ˈtaːstən/ → modern taste /teɪst/. The final -e of the modern spelling is purely orthographic, a remnant of the Old English/Middle English inflectional system that Milton's tast dispenses with.
