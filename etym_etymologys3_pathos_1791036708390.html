Pathos comes directly from Ancient Greek pathos suffering, feeling, via Latin rhetorical borrowing into English in the late 16th century. Its history moves from metaphysics to rhetoric to tender criticism.

The deepest root is usually reconstructed as PIE *kwenth- to suffer, endure, though Beekes and others note Greek paskhein to suffer has an obscure, possibly Pre-Greek or complex labiovelar prehistory, so the precise PIE form is debated and should be flagged. What is secure is Greek morphology: verb paskho, paskhein (future peisomai, aorist epathon) yields noun pathos with -th- suffix marking result of suffering. In Homer pathos is misfortune befalling heroes. In tragedy it is the suffering scene itself. In Aristotle's Poetics and Rhetoric (4th c. BCE) pathos becomes technical: in drama the painful act, in rhetoric the emotional proof that moves judges, paired with ethos and logos.

Hellenistic Stoics moralize pathe as diseased emotions to be extirpated; Christian Fathers adopt the term for passions of Christ and martyrs, dignifying suffering. Latin borrows pathos unchanged as a Greek rhetorical term; Cicero and Quintilian discuss it while normally using affectus. Medieval scholasticism keeps it in Latin rhetoric.

  • 1590s English borrowing: humanists reviving Aristotle bring pathos as learned term for emotional appeal; early spellings pathos, pathetical.
  • 17th-18th c. aesthetics: sentimentalism revalues tears; pathos of a description becomes praise. Gray, Richardson, Sterne cultivate it.
  • 19th c. criticism: pathos versus bathos distinguished; true pathos restrained and noble, false pathos mawkish.
  • 20th c. everyday: advertisers, lawyers, journalists use pathos self-consciously; Wodehouse can joke that pathos gives him the pip.

Phonetics: Greek pa-thos with aspirated th becomes Latin /patos/ then English /peɪθɒs/ via Great Vowel Shift lengthening a to ei and th to voiceless fricative. Spelling unchanged for 2500 years, a mark of learned borrowing. Cognates sympathy (suffering-with), empathy (suffering-in), apathy (without-suffering), pathetic, pathology share the root, showing how suffering ramifies into feeling, disease, and care. Literary attestations run from Aeschylus to Aristotle to Sidney's Defence, to Wordsworth's plea for pathos, to modern film reviews.

Further historical depth: sound change here is regular and well attested in standard handbooks, while semantic change follows general principles of broadening, narrowing, amelioration, and pejoration driven by social attitudes toward training, suffering, and propriety. Print culture, schooling, law, and empire each leave fingerprints on frequency and connotation. Writers from Chaucer and Shakespeare through the King James translators, Johnson, Austen, Dickens, and Wodehouse reuse and reshape the stock, so that each century adds a layer without erasing the last. Consulting the OED historical quotations in chronological order makes the drift visible and prevents anachronistic reading of older playful or severe uses.

  • Phonology: regular Germanic consonant shifts, vowel reductions, and French-influenced spellings explain the modern form without ad hoc invention.
  • Morphology: affixation and compounding are transparent and productive, allowing hedged or intensified derivatives.
  • Sociolinguistics: class, gender, profession, and genre steer which sense dominates in a given decade.

Literary witnesses should be read in full context rather than as isolated quotations, since irony and narration often invert surface praise or blame. A final caution: where origins are labelled unknown or debated in the OED and Etymonline, responsible lexicography reports the gap instead of inventing a convenient proto-form. That honesty is itself part of the word history.
