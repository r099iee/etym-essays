Kindling is the present participle, gerund, and verbal noun of the verb kindle — to set fire to, to light, to ignite something from small beginnings (a spark, a flame, a hearth fire) — and, as an ordinary noun, it is the dry wood, twigs, or other material used to start a fire. In Bilbo's alarmed imagining in The Hobbit, "he thought of plundering dragons settling on his quiet Hill and kindling it all to flames," the -ing form does double work: it is the practical act of starting a blaze, and in hobbit panic it becomes apocalyptic, reducing home, hill, and comfort to fire. The word's root sense is concrete and domestic — one kindles a hearth or a candle — but here it is inflated by dragon-fear into total destruction, and the participle keeps the action ongoing and vivid, as if the Hill is already catching. Tone: homely at its core, catastrophic in this context; the word bridges the kitchen and the inferno. Core senses of kindle / kindling
  • Verb, transitive: to set fire to, to light, to ignite ("kindle a fire," "kindle the wood") — the act of starting a blaze from small beginnings.
  • Verb, intransitive: to take fire, to begin to burn ("the dry leaves kindled at once") — the flame catching.
  • Verb, figurative: to arouse, excite, or inflame ("kindle hope," "kindle desire," "kindle anger," "kindle war") — emotional or ideological fire, the same ignition image applied to feelings.
  • Present participle / gerund (this card): the act of lighting, in progress — "the dragons kindling the Hill" — and as a verbal noun, "the kindling of the fire."
  • Noun: dry sticks, twigs, and tinder used to start a fire ("a bundle of kindling," "wood kindling") — the fuel, by transfer from the act to the material.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern jargon senses
  • Neurological kindling (medicine and psychology): the process by which repeated, initially sub-threshold stimuli (electrical or chemical) progressively lower the threshold for a major response — especially seizures in epilepsy — so that eventually a full seizure or craving is triggered. The term was coined in the 1960s by the neuroscientist Graham Goddard; the bridge is the image of repeatedly striking sparks until a full blaze catches.
  • Psychological / psychiatric kindling: in mood-disorder research, the hypothesis that repeated episodes of stress or depression sensitize the brain so that later, milder triggers produce severe episodes — the same "small sparks build to a blaze" model.
  • Fire-safety and camping register: "kindling" is the standard term for the small-diameter wood laid beneath or beside larger logs to get a fire going ("tinder, kindling, then fuel") — a technical-sounding but everyday use.
  • Figurative "kindle" in literary and rhetorical use: "kindling passion," "kindling enthusiasm," "a kindling eye" (an eye beginning to glow) — the ignition metaphor remains productive.
Semantic bridges The entire family grows from the image of fire being brought into being — a small beginning (spark, tinder, twig) that grows into flame. The Old Norse verb kynda "to kindle, to set on fire" is the direct source of English kindle, with the candle-word kyndill beside it reinforcing the fire image. From "to start a fire" the verb extended naturally to "to arouse feelings as one rouses a fire," and the -ing noun kindling passed from "the act of lighting" to "the material that lights" by the ordinary transfer from action to instrument. The modern neurological sense transfers the same "small repeated stimuli producing a large blaze" image to the brain. Kindling in Tolkien's sentence preserves the exact -ing form of the text — the ongoing, catastrophic lighting-up of everything Bilbo loves.
