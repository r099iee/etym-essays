• Shrieking: Similar because both denote a sharp, piercing cry forced from the throat; differs because shriek is the standard, smoother, French-flavoured form, while skrike preserves the harder Scandinavian /k/ and sounds older, more provincial, and more guttural.
  • Screeching: Similar because both are harsh, grating, high-pitched cries; differs because screeching stresses disagreeable, undignified shrillness (of tyres, owls, and squabbles), while skriking carries an archaic, dialectal weight of northern speech.
  • Screaming: Similar because both are loud, intense vocal cries; differs because screaming is sustained, lower-pitched, and often terror-filled, while skriking is thin, high, and piercing, closer to a wail.
  • Squealing: Similar because both are high-pitched cries; differs because squealing is thin and reedy (of pigs, brakes, excited children), while skriking is grating and guttural, cut with a hard /k/.
  • Howling: Similar because both are loud outcries; differs because howling is deep, long, and mournful like a wolf, while skriking is sharp, high, and piercing.
  • Squeaking: Similar because both are shrill noises; differs because squeaking is the short, thin, mouse-like sound explicitly excluded by the shortDefinition, while skriking is a full, harsh, grating shriek.
  • Screaking: Similar because both are archaic imitative words for a harsh grating cry; differs because screak (c. 1500) is the softer parallel form, while skrike is the northern dialectal survivor with the hard Scandinavian /k/.
  • Cawing: Similar because both are harsh bird-cries; differs because cawing is the flat, crowing call, while skriking is the piercing, high-pitched cry of terror or complaint.
