CHAIN FOR ‘WHIRR’ (imitative; no PIE root is securely reconstructable):
1. Echoic base — Middle English whirren (also whirre, wirren) “to make a buzzing, whirling sound” (14th c.), imitative of rapid vibration or rotation; possibly reinforced by Old Norse hvirfla “to whirl” and related to the English whirl. No older attested stage survives, and no PIE etymon is reconstructable with certainty.
2. Early Modern English whirr / whir v. “to revolve or move with a rapid, low, continuous buzzing sound” (spelling whirr established from the 17th c.).
3. Noun derived from the verb — whirr n. “a low continuous buzzing or vibratory sound” (attested from the 18th c.) → WHIRR.
Note: the word is onomatopoeic, so the chain is short by nature; the only debated point is the degree of influence from Old Norse and the relationship to whirl, both of which share the same imitative sound-shape (w-h-r), rather than a demonstrable common ancestor.
