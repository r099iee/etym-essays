- 14th c. — Vernal botanical budding: The verb burgeon enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Old French, strictly describing trees, vines, shrubs, and flowers putting forth swelling buds and shoots in the spring.
- 16th–17th c. — Poetic and psychological transference: Elizabethan and Jacobean poets, including William Shakespeare, begin transferring burgeon to human beauty, virtue, and emotional states swelling within the human soul.
- 18th–19th c. — Agrarian and demographic growth: The term enters agricultural treatises and Romantic literature to describe crops thriving in rich loam, gradually extending to the physical growth of communities and towns.
- Mid-20th c. — Economic and industrial expansion: Post-war journalism, sociology, and economics adopt burgeoning to describe the explosive growth of consumer markets, middle-class populations, and urban metropolises.
- Late 20th c.–Present — Scholarly disciplines and emerging technologies: The participial adjective burgeoning becomes an academic and journalistic standard for describing nascent sciences, innovative tech sectors, and intellectual frontiers experiencing exponential breakthroughs.
