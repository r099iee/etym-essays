- Air and appearance (mid-16th c.) - Enters English meaning the outward aspect, look or bearing of a person; from the start it points at the whole figure, not a single feature.
- The visible signature of character (17th-18th c.) - Refines into the dignified language of character-reading: a person's mien is outer testimony of inner temper and rank, kept distinct from countenance, which names only the expressive surface of the face.
- Mien and countenance in the novel (19th c.) - In Austen's world the two work as a pair of instruments: Darcy's 'noble mien' advertises rank through his whole figure, while the same pride is read on his 'forbidding, disagreeable countenance' - mien the shell, countenance the surface.
- Modern usage - Recedes from speech into literary and historical prose; bearing, demeanor and expression now cover the ground, and mien survives chiefly in dignified collocations such as grave mien, haughty mien and imposing mien.
