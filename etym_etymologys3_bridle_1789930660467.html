Bridle as noun is Old English bridel meaning a bridle, a restraint, related to bregdan to move quickly, pull, braid, from Proto-Germanic *bregdilaz, with the etymological notion that which one pulls quickly, as Etymonline summarises. Cognates prove Germanic age: Old Frisian bridel, Middle Dutch breydel, Dutch breidel, Old High German bridel. The verb to bridle is c.1200 from Old English bridlian to fit with a bridle, then figurative to control, with head-tossing sense by mid-15th century. The deeper PIE connection to *bhrek- is tentative with phonetic difficulties, honestly flagged in scholarship.

  • Proposed PIE *bhrek- to Proto-Germanic *bregdan: jerking to braiding. Pokorny and Etymonline compare Sanskrit bhurati he moves quickly and Lithuanian bruzdus fast, reconstructing *bhrek- to jerk. From it Germanic forms strong verb *bregdanan to make sudden jerky movements from side to side, attested as Old English bregdan to pull, shake, brandish, braid, Old Norse bregda to move quickly, braid, Old Saxon bregdan to weave. Semantic bridge: quick sideways jerking of hands weaves threads and jerks reins; braiding and reining share wrist work. Phonetic difficulties remain, so de Vaan-style caution applies: the link is plausible but not airtight.
  • Proto-Germanic *bregdilaz: the pulled thing. Instrumental suffix *-ila- on bregd- yields *bregdilaz, that by which one pulls, the strap. Regular vowel reduction gives Old English bridel with i for e before dental plus liquid, and metathesis-resistant cluster br-. Gender is masculine-neuter for gear. Meaning is already both concrete strap and abstract restraint, as gear-words moralise early.
  • Old to Middle English bridel: stable and pulpit. In wills, charters and the Bayeux Tapestry’s world the bridel is war-gear alongside sadel. Homilists preach to bridle the tongue, translating Latin frenare. Verb bridlian gives Middle English bridlen c.1200 to control, dominate. By mid-15th century tossing the head as a reined horse does yields to bridle up for humans, pride made visible. Unbridled in late 14th century figurative unrestrained shows the negative already productive.
  • Modern拡散: paths, sports, machines. Bridle-path in 1806 enshrines riding rights against carriages; dressage refines double bridles; American West adds hackamores. Engineering borrows the Y-strap image for kite, parachute and gun bridles; French bride returns the Germanic loan as debridement unbridling a wound. The horse sense stays central: to carry saddle and bridle, as in Tolstoy, is to arrive ready to ride, not merely to stand.

Note on nuance: derivation from *bregdan is secure Germanic; projection to PIE *bhrek- carries a caveat about phonetic difficulties noted by Etymonline. No Beekes Greek cognate is claimed. The chain above therefore marks PIE as proposed, Germanic onward as attested.
