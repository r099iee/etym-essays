Scimitars (singular scimitar) are swords with a curved, single-edged blade, typically widening toward the point, traditionally carried in the Islamic world — Persian, Ottoman, Mamluk, Mughal and Arab lands — and, from the sixteenth century onward, the generic English name for any such "eastern" curved blade. The curve is functional, not decorative: a deeply swept edge lets a mounted rider draw a long slicing cut across an opponent without checking the horse's pace, the whole mass of the blade travelling behind a moving edge, while the widened point gives weight and biting force at the strike.

  • Primary sense — the weapon. A scimitar has a single cutting edge, a pronounced "belly" or sweep, and an unsharpened back; the hilt carries a cross-guard and often a pommel, and the blade may flare into a clipped tip (as in the Turkish kilij's yelman). Lengths vary widely, from short Mamluk blades to long Persian shamshirs nearly the height of the rider; classification in modern arms catalogues rests on the curve, the single edge and the widening tip, all three of which are needed for a blade to be called a scimitar rather than a sabre.
  • Historical and geographical range. From the 14th to the 19th centuries the scimitar was the signature sword of Ottoman sipahis, Persian savaran, Mamluk emirs and Mughal commanders. European travellers and chroniclers brought home one cover term for all of it, lumping the Persian shamshir, the Turkish kilij, the Indian tulwar and the Arab saif under the single word; in museum practice the word still labels the whole curved-sabre family of the East, and auction catalogues routinely describe any oriental curved blade as a "scimitar".
  • Shape-descriptor in modern domains. Anything curved "like a scimitar" now borrows the word without any reference to fighting: the scimitar-horned oryx (a Sahelian antelope whose long horns sweep back in one crescent curve), the scimitar babbler (an Asian bird with a strongly decurved bill), "scimitar winglets" and scimitar-blade propellers in aviation engineering, and scimitar-shaped dunes described in desert and planetary geology. The semantic bridge is pure geometry: one deep, uninterrupted curve.
  • Orientalist and literary symbol. From the 18th century the scimitar became the stock emblem of the Orient in European art and verse — the crescent blade of the Turk, naturally paired with the crescent moon. Byron's pirates and corsairs, Thomas Moore's Lalla Rookh and generations of orientalist painters made it a synecdoche of exotic menace and splendour; in 20th-century fantasy (Tolkien's The Hobbit, later role-playing games, film and video games) it is the default weapon of eastern and monstrous warriors — "goblins of huge size with scimitars of steel", exactly the register of our context sentence.
  • Residual ceremonial and rhetorical use. The scimitar appears in European heraldry as a charge commemorating campaigns against Islamic powers; janissary bands paraded captured blades; the phrase "the sword of Islam" keeps the word alive in religious-political rhetoric; colloquially, "a scimitar of a moon" or "of a beak" is a vivid metaphor for anything drastically curved.

Semantic bridges. The drift runs in four clear stages: (1) a specific weapon encountered in the Ottoman–Persian contact zone, first named in travellers' reports and military chronicles of the 1540s; (2) by the 17th century a generic class of curved eastern blades in European military taxonomy; (3) an orientalist emblem of Islamic power and exotic danger between the 18th and 19th centuries; (4) in the 20th–21st centuries a free-floating shape-metaphor and a fixture of fantasy fiction. Note the constant across all four stages: it is always the curve — never the point, never the hilt — that the word's figurative uses seize on. Note also that the plural form scimitars is the statistically dominant shape in narrative prose, and our card's context — a battle host of goblins "with scimitars of steel" — is thoroughly typical of the word's modern habitat.
