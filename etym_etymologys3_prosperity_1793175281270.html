The seed of Prosperity is hope itself. Latin spes, hope, is no thin abstraction but the farmer's word: it belongs to the reconstructed root *speh₁-, to thrive, to grow fat, to succeed, whose other children include the Sanskrit word for swelling grain and the ideas of speed and success that cluster round thriving things. To hope, in this deep sense, is to expect thriving the way a sower expects the shoot — and the Roman who called an undertaking prosperus, literally pro spes, according to hope, meant that events had grown the way hope had planted them.

The adjective prosperus appears in Plautus and Cicero for favourable winds, fortunate voyages, and obliging gods; from it Late Latin built the abstract noun prosperitas, the state of the man whose hopes are answered. Christian moralists seized it immediately as a spiritual danger-word: Augustine and Gregory warn that prosperitas softens the soul, that the thriving forget God, establishing the word's permanent double vision — blessing on the lips, warning in the margin. Old French took it as prosperite, and Middle English as prosperitee, where Chaucer and Gower use it for worldly success always shadowed by Fortune's turning wheel.

The King James translators gave the word its broadest English sense. Their in the day of prosperity be joyful, but in the day of adversity consider means no stock portfolio but the whole undivided favour of heaven: rain, harvest, children, peace. For two centuries English prosperity kept this capacious meaning — to wish a traveller prosperity was to wish him Godspeed in full — while political economy slowly narrowed a technical lane: by the eighteenth century writers on trade speak of the prosperity of manufactures, and by the twentieth the economist's prosperity is a phase of the business cycle, measurable, graphable, and gone by next quarter.

  • Hope as growth: PIE *speh₁- roots hoping in thriving, so that every prosperity still carries, six thousand years deep, the image of seed answering rain.
  • Rome's formula: pro spes turns expectation into a verdict on events — the voyage, the omen, the battle is according to hope, therefore good.
  • The pulpit's warning: late antique and medieval moralists fix prosperity as spiritually perilous, the cushioned slide from gratitude to pride, a suspicion never quite shaken.
  • The French passage: prosperite crosses the Channel with the Norman vocabulary of statecraft, joining the high French register of English success-words.
  • The twin modern lives: treasury prosperity, a statistic, and toast prosperity, a blessing, share one spelling and barely one meaning — the economist counts what the well-wisher sings.

So when the wonder-tale voice cries Prosperity to thee, Tsarevitch Alexei, it speaks the older, undivided sense: not a forecast of GDP but the whole hope of thriving packed into three syllables, seed, shoot, and harvest wished upon a hero at the road's beginning.
