Thrall is a Viking word, and its biography is the biography of the Viking slave class turned metaphor. Its story begins in the cold social world of the North and ends in the psychological and magical language of the modern novel and the fantasy game, a journey of nine hundred years from the slave-market to the phrase "in thrall to beauty". The Old Norse world: þræll, the unfree The word entered English as a borrowing from Old Norse þræll, the standard term for an unfree person in Viking society. Norse society was rigidly stratified: at the top stood the jarls (earls) and the hersir, then the free farmers, the karls, and at the bottom the þrælar — thralls, slaves — who might be captives taken in raids, children born into slavery, or debtors sold for non-payment. The thrall was chattel: bought, sold, branded, and bound, and the sagas record the trade in thralls across the North Atlantic from Scandinavia to Iceland, Dublin, and Byzantium. The Old Norse poem Rígsþula ("The Lay of Ríg") even offers a myth of the social classes, in which the first thrall, Þræll, is the ancestor of the slaves, with the coarse hands and bent back of the labourer — an early literary attestation of the very word. The etymology: a runner, or something else? The deeper origin of þræll is debated. The traditional account, still found in many handbooks, derives it from a Proto-Germanic *þrahilaz, "a runner, one who runs errands", built on the Proto-Indo-European root *traǵʰ-, "to run, to drag, to trail" — the slave conceived as the household errand-runner. The Old High German drahsil, "a turner (lathe-worker)", is cited as a cognate showing the regular þ-/d- correspondence, though its sense is far removed. But the reconstruction rests on inference, not on attestation: no Germanic language outside North Germanic preserves the noun in its old form, and some scholars have suggested the word may be unrelated to the "run" root altogether, perhaps connected instead with a stem meaning "to press, oppress". The honest summary is that þræll is of uncertain etymology, and the "runner" story is a plausible guess rather than a demonstrated descent. Borrowing into English: the Danelaw inheritance The word crossed into English with the Vikings themselves. From the late ninth century the Danes settled northern and eastern England — the Danelaw — and their language poured into Old English. þræll entered late Old English as þræl, and it is attested in the works of Ælfric around the year 1000, where it is used for the slave or bondsman. The native English words for slaves and servants were old and various, but þræl brought with it the specifically Scandinavian institution of the household thrall and quickly became the vivid, everyday word. In Middle English it appears as thral and thrall: as a noun, "a slave, a bondsman"; as an adjective, "enslaved, captive"; and in the verb to thrall, "to enslave". Chaucer and the alliterative poets use it freely, and the collocation in thrall, "in bondage", is already current. The great shift: from slave to spell The sixteenth century changed the word's centre of gravity. As English society moved out of the feudal world, the concrete person-sense of thrall began to recede, and the phrase in thrall — "in bondage" — began to be used figuratively of things that hold a person in their grip: tyranny, love, custom, disease, passion. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "held in thrall" was a stock rhetorical image, and the person had become a shadow in the word. The nineteenth century completed the transfer: in thrall to a passion, a creed, an obsession — the psychological sense is fully established, and the derived verb enthrall (which had meant "to enslave" in the sixteenth century) comes to mean chiefly "to captivate, to fascinate": the audience is enthralled, the lover is enthralled. Where the Middle English word described iron chains, the modern word describes silken ones — and the shift from the slave-market to the drawing-room is one of the cleanest examples in English of social reality driving semantic change. Modern revival: history and fantasy In the twentieth century the word split into two registers. Historians of the Viking age revived the concrete sense: the thrall is again a slave, the bottom of the Norse social order, and the word is standard in medieval history. At the same time, fantasy and horror fiction — from vampire lore to role-playing games — adopted thrall for the person bound by supernatural domination: the vampire's thrall, a servant under a magical compulsion, at once a slave and a convert. The word thus today carries all of its history at once: the Viking labourer, the feudal bondsman, the romantic captive of the nineteenth-century lyric, and the modern fantasy's mind-bound servant. It is hard to think of a short noun with more social history packed into it. Linguistic notes
  • The adjective thrall ("enslaved, captive") and the verb to thrall ("to enslave") are both attested in Middle English; both recede after the 17th century, leaving the noun phrase in thrall dominant.
  • The derivative enthral(ment) shows the prefix en- "in, into" converting the noun into a verb: "to put into thrall".
  • The spelling thralldom vs thraldom is a nineteenth-century orthographic variation; both mean "the state of being a thrall or in thrall".
  • In American English the spellings enthrall and thrall (without the doubled consonant) are preferred; British English shows enthrall/enthral variation.
