ranch is an English noun (and verb) of American English, meaning "a large farm, especially in the United States and Canada, where cattle, sheep, horses, or other livestock are raised on open land." The word arrived in English from Mexican Spanish and carries with it the whole world of the American West: cattle, cowboys, open range, and the vast livestock holdings of the frontier. In the context card, "ranches" are the big cattle-raising farms spread through the valley, from which boys come down to swim in the deep pool.

PRIMARY SENSE — the large livestock farm

  • A ranch — a large farm or estate, chiefly in the western United States, Canada, and Latin America, given over to raising cattle, sheep, horses, or other animals on open pasture or range land. The defining activity is livestock rather than crops, and the defining feature is extent: a ranch is typically vast, measured in acres or square miles.
  • Types of ranch — cattle ranch, sheep ranch, horse ranch, and (in the twentieth century) the "dude ranch," a working or former ranch run as a holiday destination for paying guests; the "game ranch" and "wildlife ranch" are modern variants that keep the word's livestock-management meaning.
  • The verb — to ranch is to work on, manage, or own a ranch; to run cattle on open range. The noun is also used attributively: ranch land, ranch house, ranch fence, ranch cattle.
  • The context use — boys "coming down from the ranches to swim in the deep pool" places the ranch in the American rural landscape of the 1930s: a working cattle spread whose boys are the next generation of the range.

SECONDARY SENSES — modern extensions of the word

  • Ranch house / ranch-style house — a single-storey house with a long, low, open-plan layout, developed in American domestic architecture in the twentieth century; the name transfers the wide, horizontal, spread-out character of the ranch landscape to the suburban home. Also "ranch-style" as a general adjective for the wide, low, single-level form.
  • Ranch dressing / ranch dip — a creamy American salad dressing and dip of buttermilk, sour cream, herbs (dill, chives, parsley), garlic, and onion, created in the 1950s at the Hidden Valley Ranch in California. The name of the ranch became the name of the dressing, a uniquely twentieth-century commercial sense.
  • Ranch in commerce and culture — "ranch" appears in countless modern compounds: ranch-style homes, ranch fencing, ranch mink, ranch land, ranch supply; the word functions as a wholesome, homespun, nostalgic Americana marker.

HISTORICAL AND GEOGRAPHIC SENSE

  • The Spanish inheritance — the word descends from Spanish rancho, which in Spain named a mess, a group of soldiers or workers eating together, and the camp or hut where they lodged; in the Americas it came to name a small rural settlement and then a livestock farm. The American "ranch" is the direct descendant of the Mexican rancho, the working cattle estate of colonial and republican Mexico.
  • The American West — from the mid-nineteenth century, as Anglo-American settlement moved into the Southwest and the Great Plains, "ranch" became the standard English name for the vast cattle-raising holdings of the frontier, the base of the cowboy culture that shaped American mythology.
  • Global spread — the word is used worldwide for large livestock operations in the style of the American West, and it has been borrowed into many languages; the Australian "station" and the Latin American "hacienda" and "estancia" are the neighbouring terms for the same institution in other regions.

REGISTER AND SUMMARY

  • Core meaning — a large farm for raising livestock on open land, especially in North America.
  • Cultural freight — the word carries the romance of the cowboy West: open range, horses, self-reliant outdoor life, and the frontier mythology of American identity.
  • Modern breadth — from working cattle operations to ranch houses, ranch dressing, and dude ranches, the word now names both a real institution and a homespun, nostalgic style of American life.
