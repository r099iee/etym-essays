Snarling, the present participle of the verb snarl in its growling sense, names the act of emitting a low threatening growl with bared teeth — the sound of a dog guarding its bone, a wolf driven into a corner, or, as in the source sentence, a captive creature surrendering its secrets unwillingly, hissing defiance between bouts of weeping.

In the source sentence the speaker recalls wringing the true story out of his prisoner bit by bit, together with much snivelling and snarling. The two nouns form a deliberate pair: snivelling is the whimper of self-pity, snarling the growl of resentment. Together they capture a prisoner who alternates between cringing submission and bared-tooth hostility, weeping one moment and threatening the next, and the participle form stresses that the menace is continuous — a sustained growl, not a single bark.

Core meaning in detail: To snarl in this sense is to produce the deep guttural warning sound animals make before biting: lips drawn back, teeth exposed, breath forced harshly through nose and throat. As a participle, snarling catches the sound in progress — simmering, unrelenting menace — and by extension it describes the facial expression that goes with it (a snarling lip, a snarling visage) and the tone of voice (snarling abuse, a snarling reply). Grammarians note three jobs for the form: progressive verb (he was snarling), attributive adjective (a snarling dog), and verbal noun (much snarling), and the source sentence uses that third, nominal job, pairing it with snivelling as twin miseries of the interrogated.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Growling with bared teeth (primary sense, from the 1590s): the literal animal sound — snarling dogs, snarling wolves, a cornered creature snarling over its secrets. This is exactly the source-sentence sense, and it remains the first picture every dictionary reaches for.
  • Speaking harshly and angrily (figurative, very early extension): a snarling tone, a snarling exchange, snarling commentators — any utterance delivered with audible hostility, as though the speaker were baring teeth. The bridge is acoustic and visual at once: angry human speech takes on the low harshness and curled-lip expression of the animal warning, so listeners hear the beast inside the bureaucrat.
  • Wearing a snarling expression (transferred epithet): snarling features, a snarling stone gargoyle, the snarling mask of a temple guardian. The bridge carries the sound across to the look that produces it — curled lip, wrinkled nose, exposed teeth — so the adjective can describe silent stone as vividly as living throats, freezing a moment of aggression into permanent guardianship.
  • Heraldic snarling beasts (specialised artistic sense): heraldry blazons lions and dragons as snarling or snarling guardant when shown with open jaws and lolling tongues. The bridge is emblematic: the frozen threat-posture becomes a badge of courage and protection, turning menace into heraldic virtue.
  • The other snarl, meaning tangle (homograph warning, distinct origin): phrases like snarling traffic in the traffic-jam sense belong strictly to snarl meaning a knot or entanglement, descended from snare, not to the growl word at all. The two verbs converged in spelling but never in blood, so snarled fishing line and snarled-up motorways must never be confused with the bared-tooth participle treated here.

Register and connotation: snarling is vivid, physical, and faintly contemptuous — headline writers love snarling rows and snarling exchanges because the word lets readers hear the quarrel. Applied to people it always accuses: a snarling official has abandoned civility, and the participle stresses persistence rather than a single lapse. Set against its twin snivelling, with which the source pairs it, snarling is the aggressive pole of misery — the sound defiance makes after it has already lost.
