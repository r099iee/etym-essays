• ouds: Similar because the European lute is the direct historical descendant of the Arabic ʿūd, sharing a vaulted pear-shaped body and angled pegbox; differs because ouds are fretless, strung with different courses, and plucked with a long plectrum (risha), whereas European lutes have tied gut frets and are plucked with bare fingertips to produce polyphony.
  • guitars: Similar because both are plucked stringed chordophones with fretted necks; differs because guitars have a flat wooden back with waist-indented ribs and a straight pegbox, whereas lutes feature a vaulted, rib-constructed bowl back and a neck with a sharply bent-back pegbox.
  • mandolins: Similar because both feature pear-shaped wooden bodies and fretted necks; differs because modern mandolins have metal strings, are tuned in fifths like violins, and are played with a plectrum at a much higher register, whereas lutes were historically strung with gut, tuned predominantly in fourths and a third, and played polyphonically with the fingers.
  • viols: Similar because both were prestigious aristocratic Renaissance chamber instruments utilizing gut strings and tied gut frets; differs because viols (viola da gamba family) are bowed chordophones played vertically between the legs, whereas lutes are plucked instruments held horizontally across the chest.
  • veenas: Similar because both serve as classical plucked instruments carrying profound spiritual and courtly prestige; differs because the classical Indian veena (such as the Saraswati veena or rudra veena) features large gourd resonators, raised brass frets set in wax, and drone strings, whereas the European lute is built of delicate wooden staves without gourds.
  • citterns: Similar because both were popular fretted stringed instruments in Renaissance Europe; differs because citterns featured flat backs, wire metal strings, fixed brass frets, and were often played with a quill or plectrum in tavern settings, whereas lutes occupied the highest art-music tier.
  • theorbos: Similar because the theorbo is an extended member of the lute family developed in the late Renaissance; differs because theorbos possess an elongated second neck supporting long, unstopped bass strings (diapasons) designed specifically for Baroque basso continuo accompaniment.
  • lyres: Similar because both are ancient plucked chordophones linked to poetic recitation; differs because a lyre has no neck—its strings extend between two curved arms joined by a crossbar across an open space—whereas a lute has strings running along a solid neck with a fingerboard.
  • harps: Similar because both are plucked acoustic instruments associated with celestial and courtly beauty; differs because a harp has open strings perpendicular to the soundboard with each string producing a single pitch, whereas a lute allows a few strings to produce dozens of pitches via stopped fingerboard positions.
  • pipas: Similar because the Chinese pipa is also known as a pear-shaped plucked lute; differs because the pipa has high raised frets along its soundboard, is held almost vertically, and is played with artificial fingernails utilizing rapid tremolo techniques unique to traditional Chinese music.
  • balalaikas: Similar because both are traditional folk and art chordophones; differs because the Russian balalaika features a distinct triangular wooden body and only three strings, whereas a Renaissance lute has a vaulted pear-shaped back and up to fifteen or more paired gut strings.
  • bouzoukis: Similar because both are long-necked chordophones derived from Middle Eastern and Mediterranean origins; differs because the bouzouki has a circular round bowl back, long metallic neck, steel strings, and is played with a plectrum for modal Mediterranean folk music.
  • sitars: Similar because both are celebrated plucked classical instruments of extraordinary harmonic beauty; differs because the Indian sitar utilizes movable curved metal frets, sympathetic resonance strings (taraf), a gourd resonator, and wide string-bending (meend), creating acoustic characteristics entirely unlike the intimate polyphony of European lutes.
