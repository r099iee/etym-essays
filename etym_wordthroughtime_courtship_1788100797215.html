- 1580s — "courtship" is coined in the age of Elizabethan courts: first meaning the state or behaviour of a courtier — courtly manners and intrigue — built from "court" + "-ship".
- 1590s — the wooing sense appears and quickly becomes the heart of the word: to court a woman (from "to pay court to a prince") is to attend her as courtiers attend royalty; Elizabethan drama and poetry take it up.
- 17th–18th c. — polite "courtship" is the socially managed path to marriage: visits, letters, chaperones, and "paying one's addresses"; conduct books regulate the art, and the word keeps the courtly sheen of ceremony and favour.
- 19th c. — Victorian courtship formalises: calling hours, Sunday walks, "keeping company", and the engagement that crowns it; fiction — and Wilde's tales — make the courtship plot the engine of romance: the Student's courtship of the Professor's daughter "lasted all through the summer".
- 20th c. — "dating" displaces "courtship" in everyday speech; the word retreats to formal and historical registers — and, with Darwin's legacy, becomes standard in biology: the "courtship display" of animals.
- 21st c. — "courtship" is literary, historical, and scientific; in politics and business it means the deliberate seeking of favour ("courtship of the voters"); in romance it carries a gentle archaism — the old court, still inside the word.
