PIE *weh₂dʰ- (conventionally written *wadh- / *wādh- in older handbooks) "to go, to walk, to wade, to advance through water" — reconstructed; securely attested by its Italic and Germanic descendants, but not certainly present in Indo-Iranian. 1. PIE *weh₂dʰ- / *wadh- "to go, to walk, to wade" (reconstructed; 5th–3rd millennium BCE) — the base of both the Italic verb of going and the Germanic verb of wading. 2. Latin vādere "to go, to walk, to march, to advance" (attested from c. 200 BCE, e.g. in Plautus). 3. Latin ēvādere "to go out, to get away, to escape" — ē- (from ex-, "out of, away from") + vādere (attested from the 1st century BCE, e.g. in Cicero and Caesar for escape from danger). 4. Old French evader (also esvader) "to escape, to slip away" (12th century), from Latin ēvādere. 5. Middle English evaden "to escape from" (borrowed via Old French / Anglo-French; attested from the 15th century). 6. English evade "to escape from; to avoid by a side-step a question, duty, or law" (early 16th century, c. 1510). 7. English EVADING (present participle / gerund) — this card. Related side-branches (not on the direct line, given for completeness): Latin ēvāsus "having escaped" (perfect participle) → ēvāsiō "an escaping" → English evasion (15th century); Latin ēvāsīvus "that escapes" → English evasive (1725); and the Germanic line *wadaną → Old English wadan "to advance, to ford, to wade" → English wade. Note on debate: the root is secure in Italic and Germanic but has no certain Indo-Iranian cognate; the superficially similar Sanskrit vadh- "to strike, to slay" belongs to an unrelated root (*gʷhen-), so no Indic cognate is claimed. The spelling *weh₂dʰ- with laryngeal is the modern notation; older handbooks (Pokorny) give *wadh- / *wādh-. The French relay (stage 4) is the standard account for English evade, though some sources allow a direct learned borrowing from Latin ēvādere in the 15th–16th century.
