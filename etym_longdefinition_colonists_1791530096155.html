Colonists is the plural of colonist, a noun meaning a person who settles in a colony, especially one who takes part in founding or occupying a new territory on behalf of a distant state. The word is neutral in form but heavily charged in use: it can describe the Roman veterans and traders who settled in Dacia, the English families who landed at Jamestown, the Boer farmers of southern Africa, or, in recent critical writing, the beneficiaries of settler colonialism whose presence dispossessed indigenous peoples.

Primary sense. A colonist is a settler in a colony: someone who moves to a new land and remains there as part of an organised, politically subordinate or chartered community. The word implies permanence and collective enterprise, and it usually implies a relationship with a metropolitan power, a charter company or an imperial government. It is therefore narrower than "migrant" (which implies no colony) and broader than "official" or "soldier" (which imply temporary service). The plural colonists is the normal way to speak of the settler population as a body, as in "the colonists petitioned the Crown" or "Roman colonists and soldiers carried Latin to the Danube".

Historical senses.

  • Greek and Roman antiquity: Greek apoikiai and Roman coloniae planted citizen-soldiers and traders in conquered or allied territory; English historians routinely call these people "colonists", though the Latin coloni were often farmers or tenants rather than settlers in the modern sense.
  • Early modern Atlantic: English, French, Spanish, Dutch and Portuguese colonists founded settlements from Virginia and New England to the Caribbean, Canada and Brazil; "colonist" competes with "planter", "adventurer" and "settler".
  • Revolutionary America: the "American colonists" became a political category in the 1760s-1770s, defined by their claim to the rights of Englishmen and then by their rebellion.
  • Nineteenth-century settler empire: British colonists in Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa, French colonists in Algeria, and Boer colonists in the interior became the core populations of settler colonies.
  • Postcolonial critique: twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship distinguishes colonists (the settler population), colonizers (the agents and structures of colonisation) and the colonised (the indigenous majority), and describes "settler colonialism" as a structure of land seizure rather than a one-off event.

Technical and scientific senses. In ecology and microbiology the word is used for organisms that establish themselves in a new habitat. A colonist species is an early arrival in a newly available environment, such as a pioneer plant on bare rock or a bacterium on a host surface; in island biogeography, "colonists" are the organisms that reach an island and found a population. In medicine, a colonist organism is one that lives on or in a body without causing disease, in contrast to a pathogen. These uses preserve the core idea of settling and establishing, stripped of the political and moral charge.

Modern and figurative senses. Space advocacy has revived the word: "Mars colonists" or "lunar colonists" are imagined settlers of extraterrestrial colonies. In computing and gaming, "colonists" may name units or populations in simulation games. In journalism, The Times Colonist of Victoria, British Columbia, preserves "Colonist" as a newspaper title. In everyday political speech the term is often avoided in favour of "settlers" when the speaker wishes to be neutral, or used deliberately when the speaker wishes to foreground the colonial relationship.

Grammar and usage. "Colonist" is countable and takes of or a possessive: "colonists of New England", "the colonists' grievances". The attributive use is common ("colonist families", "colonist society"), though "colonial" is preferred for institutions and styles ("colonial administration", "colonial architecture"). The plural is regular; there is no distinct collective noun, though "the colonists" functions collectively. The word is not pejorative in itself, but in postcolonial contexts it can be heard as an accusation, because it names a person whose presence depends on dispossession.

Semantic bridges. The Latin ancestor colere meant "to till, cultivate, inhabit, and worship"; a colonus was a cultivator, and a colonia was a settled agricultural community. From that root English drew not only "colony" and "colonist" but "cultivate", "culture" and "cult". The idea of settlement is therefore agricultural before it is political, which explains why "colonist" can sound almost cosy in a farming context and yet harsh in a context of conquest: the same word covers the hoe and the flag.
