• Split: Similar in dividing something apart; differs because split is neutral and often accidental (wood splits, seams split), with no necessary blade, force, or skill, while cleave implies a deliberate, clean cut with an edge.
  • Sever: Similar in cutting cleanly through; differs because sever means cutting one part off from the whole, precise but unheroic, as a limb from a body, where cleave divides through the middle of a resistant thing.
  • Rend: Similar in tearing apart by force; differs because rend is a violent, ragged tearing of cloth or flesh without a blade, while cleave implies a clean, sharp division.
  • Hew: Similar in cutting with a blade; differs because hew implies repeated chopping strokes and is used of shaping or felling, while cleave names a single decisive splitting stroke.
  • Sunder: Similar in parting or breaking asunder; differs because sunder is poetic and usually abstract — to part, to break apart — without the image of the blade and its decisive cut.
  • Chop: Similar in cutting with a blade; differs because chop is coarse, practical, and implies a blunt, hacking stroke rather than a clean division along a line.
  • Cut: Similar in dividing with an edge; differs because cut is the plain, general word for any incision, while cleave suggests splitting something resistant through and through, usually with force.
  • Rive: Similar in splitting apart forcibly; differs because rive is archaic and literary, closer to rend, implying a violent rupture rather than a clean blade-cut.
  • Part: Similar in separating into pieces; differs because part is gentle and general, with no edge or violence, while cleave implies force and a blade.
  • Dissever: Similar in cutting apart; differs because dissever is formal and rare, emphasizing complete separation of parts, without cleave's heroic and physical image.
