• Rogues: Similar because both brand dishonest untrustworthy men; differs because rogue is lively and current, often affectionate, while knave is archaic and wholly condemnatory.
  • Rascals: Similar because both denounce low scheming fellows; differs because rascal is softened by frequent jocular use for children and pets, while knave never plays and never forgives.
  • Scoundrels: Similar because both mark men destitute of honour; differs because scoundrel is the general Victorian villain-word, while knave adds the specific tang of trickery and low cunning.
  • Villains: Similar because both name deliberate wrongdoers; differs because villain suggests grand or criminal wickedness, while knave suggests petty deceit, cheating, and double-dealing.
  • Cheats: Similar because fraud is the knave's very trade; differs because cheat names the act and the actor plainly, while knave wraps the charge in historical contempt.
  • Swindlers: Similar because both live by defrauding others of money; differs because swindler is a precise modern term for financial fraud, while knave covers any baseness, from informing to flattery.
  • Blackguards: Similar because both are archaic terms of abuse for base men; differs because blackguard stresses brutal ruffianism, while knave stresses sly dishonest cunning.
  • Miscreants: Similar because both condemn the depraved and unprincipled; differs because miscreant originally meant a heretic or infidel and still sounds theological, while knave sounds political and worldly.
  • Reprobates: Similar because both write men off as morally lost; differs because reprobate pities even as it condemns, with a preacher's sorrow, while knave prosecutes with a pamphleteer's fury.
