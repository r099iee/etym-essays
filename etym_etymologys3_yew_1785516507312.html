Few trees have left a deeper imprint on the languages, religions, and military histories of Europe than the yew. Its name — so short, so simple in modern English — is one of the most ancient arboreal designations in the entire Indo-European family, reconstructible with confidence to PIE *h₁eyH-, alongside other deep-time tree names such as *bʰeh₂ǵos "beech," *bʰerHǵ- "birch," and *dóru "oak; tree generally." These tree names are among the oldest words that can be reconstructed because they designate species that defined the material culture of Neolithic and Bronze Age peoples: the beech and birch provided bark for writing and boats, the oak provided acorns and the hardest structural timber, and the yew provided the weapon that changed the balance of power in warfare — the bow. The Germanic branch is especially informative. The Old English word for the tree was īw or ēow, and from it the Anglo-Saxons named a rune: ᘋ, called ēoh in the Old English Rune Poem (8th–9th century CE). The poem's verse runs "Ēoh byþ utan unsmēþ trēow, heard hrūsan fæst, hyrde fyres, wyrtrumun underwreþud, wynn on ēþle" — "The yew is on the outside a rough tree, hard and fast in the earth, guardian of fire, supported by roots, a joy on the native land." This brief fragment encodes everything the Anglo-Saxons valued in the tree: its gnarled rough bark, its deep-rooted stability, its use as firewood ("guardian of fire"), and its power to inspire rootedness and belonging. The Old Norse rune ᙦ, named ýr "yew," stands in the final position of the Younger Futhark, and its name — identical to the word for "yew bow" — speaks directly to the tree's military function. In both traditions the rune is not merely a letter but a sigil charged with the tree's associations: death, endurance, the tension of the drawn bow, the release of the arrow. The military history of yew is, in large part, the military history of the English longbow. From the thirteenth to the sixteenth centuries the longbow was the decisive weapon of English armies, and it was made almost exclusively of yew: the wood's dense, elastic heartwood stored and released the enormous energy that let archers loose arrows that could penetrate plate armour. Yew won the great battles of Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356), and Agincourt (1415), which in turn shaped the political map of medieval Europe. The demand was so immense that England imported vast quantities of staves from Spain, Italy, and the Baltic, and by the sixteenth century the native English yew population had been severely depleted, prompting statutes to regulate its felling and export. The word "yew" thus carried, for several centuries, a distinctly martial connotation: the tree was the weapon, and the weapon was the tree. Alongside its martial life, the yew acquired a profound religious and funerary symbolism. Yews were planted in churchyards across Britain and France, and many of these trees are demonstrably older than the churches they shade — some are thought to be among the oldest living organisms in Europe. The reasons are partly practical (the toxic foliage discouraged grazing animals from entering the sacred enclosure) and partly symbolic: the evergreen needles promised resurrection, the enormous lifespan promised eternity, and the poison made the tree an image of death itself. By the early modern period the yew had become, in English poetry and folklore, the graveyard tree par excellence — a symbol of mortality, mourning, and the hope of life after death. In the twentieth century the word took an astonishing new direction. In the 1960s the Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia), once regarded as a worthless weed of the old-growth forests of the American Northwest, was discovered to contain paclitaxel, a compound with powerful anti-cancer properties. Commercialised as Taxol in the 1990s, it became one of the most important chemotherapy drugs in the world, and its taxane chemistry continues to yield pharmaceutical derivatives today. The yew had completed a full circle in Western imagination: the tree that poisoned warriors and guarded graves became a source of healing. The modern word "yew" therefore flickers between two poles — poison and cure, death and eternity, the graveyard and the pharmacy — and every one of these poles is encoded in the word's ancient, nearly unchanged Indo-European root.
