- [Latin root: to cast a shadow (16th c. entry)] - From Latin adumbrare, "to cast a shadow upon, overshadow" (ad + umbra, "shadow"), adumbrate entered English with the pictorial sense of shading in a drawing — an outline filled with shadow.
- [The sketch in advance (17th–18th c.)] - The word shifted from shading an outline to sketching a subject lightly and in advance: to adumbrate an argument was to give its shadow-picture before the thing itself arrives, provisional and suggestive rather than final.
- [Rare negative offshoot] - A much rarer strand kept the Latin force of "overshadow": to obscure or conceal. It survives only as an archaic corner of the word's range.
- [Modern register] - Today adumbrates is the scholarly, cautious verb for foreshadowing and preliminary outline: an introduction adumbrates the arguments to come. The shadow image persists — the reader is handed a silhouette, not a photograph — always forward-looking and tentative rather than definitive.
