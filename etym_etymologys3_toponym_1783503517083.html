The word toponym is a scholarly compound assembled in the nineteenth century from two Greek elements, and the two halves could hardly differ more in their linguistic depth and in the length of their documented histories. One element, -onym, descends from one of the oldest and most securely reconstructed words in the entire Indo-European family; the other, topos, has no Indo-European etymology at all and is judged by the leading authorities to be a relic of a vanished, non-Indo-European language of the Aegean Bronze Age.

Element one: *no-men-, the word for "name"

The second element of toponym continues the Proto-Indo-European root *no-men- (also reconstructed as *h₁nómn̥), "name," which appears, still recognizable, in Latin nōmen, Old English nama (whence modern English name), Sanskrit nā́man, Old Church Slavonic imę, Hittite lāman, Old Irish ainm, and Armenian anun. Because the root is shared by the word "name" in essentially every branch of the family, a speaker of Hindi saying nām, a French speaker saying nom, a Russian speaker saying imja, and an English speaker saying name are all continuing a single Proto-Indo-European word in unbroken use for over five thousand years. In Greek the root took the form ónoma in Attic, with the variant ónyma (found especially in compounds and in dialects such as Doric and Aeolic), and it is the -onym- stem that entered Greek compounding: epṓnymos "named after" (our eponym), anṓnymos "nameless" (our anonymous), pseudṓnymos "falsely named" (our pseudonym), homṓnymos "bearing the same name" (our homonym). Through Greek combining practice this element passed into the modern European scientific vocabulary. The European tradition of forming technical terms on the Greek model — which flourished from the Renaissance onward and reached industrial scale in the nineteenth century — took -onym as a productive suffix. Synonym and antonym entered English early (both sixteenth century); pseudonym and eponym followed in the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries; and in 1876 the English place-name movement, culminating in the foundation of the English Place-Name Society in 1923, created toponymy, with toponym following as its back-formation in 1899. Each of these compounds is a modern creation, but each rests on a root older than the earliest written records of Europe.

Element two: tópos, the Pre-Greek "place"

The first element, tópos, is a different story entirely. Unlike ónyma, Greek tópos has no secure Indo-European etymology: the best modern authority, Robert Beekes, judges it most likely a Pre-Greek substrate word — a borrowing from the older, non-Indo-European language (or languages) of the Aegean spoken before the Greek-speaking ancestors arrived in the second millennium BCE. Words of this class are recognized by unusual phonological shapes, by the absence of convincing cognates in other Indo-European branches, and often by the presence of typical Pre-Greek suffixal material; tópos fits this profile. It is thus one of those quiet words whose very opacity is evidence of deep history — a surviving relic, lodged in the core vocabulary of Greek, of the vanished tongues of the Bronze Age Aegean. Older handbooks sometimes tried to connect it to Indo-European roots (a claim linking it to a root meaning "to appear" or "to be visible" has been proposed and is now generally abandoned), and that attempted derivation should be noted as a historical curiosity rather than a live hypothesis. Whatever its origin, tópos proved enormously productive in Greek and in the European sciences that borrowed from it:
  • Greek topikós, "of a place, local," gave via Latin topica and the rhetorical tradition the English topic — the "commonplace" of argument, the general theme one talks about, showing how a place-word became a word for a subject of discourse.
  • The combining form topo- built topography (the mapping of places, from Greek topographía, "description of a place"), topology (the mathematics of "place"-preserving structure), and, in the nineteenth century, the naming sciences themselves.
  • As a standalone word, topos survives in English literary theory in the sense of a stock theme or convention (the plural topoi), a usage taken directly from Greek rhetorical vocabulary.

The modern compound and its cultural life

The study of place-names is ancient — Strabo and Pliny discuss regional names, and medieval charters preserve thousands of them — but the scientific term is modern. The nineteenth century, obsessed with origins and with Greek-based scientific nomenclature, created the vocabulary in which we now talk about names: etymology, philology, onomastics, toponymy. Toponym entered English in 1899 and has since travelled from the technical literature of geography and linguistics into archaeology, cartography, heritage writing, and cultural journalism, where it has acquired a distinctly reverent aura — the place-name as a fossil and a memory-vessel. In a cartographer's reverie, every toponym on an ancient scroll is a sacred vessel of history: the word itself encodes the two halves of that reverence, an Indo-European word for "name" five thousand years old joined to a Pre-Greek word for "place" whose own origin is a mystery of the Bronze Age.
