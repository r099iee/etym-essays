bale:
- 13th c. - Middle English takes bale from Old French balle: a round bundle of goods, tightly bound.
- 14th-16th c. - the word settles into the mercantile sense: the standard packed unit of trade - cotton, wool, hay, paper - compressed and corded, too heavy to open without ceremony.
- 17th-19th c. - bale belongs to the language of shipping, warehouses, and agricultural commerce; a thing of logistics and labour.
- 19th c. - the noun spins off the back-formation verb to bale, to pack into bales; the word doubles as cargo and as the act of making cargo.
- Contemporary - by extension any bulky bound mass of belongings becomes "a bale"; the mercantile ring quietly persists, making packed gear feel like merchandise - the travellers' very lives stowed as cargo.

prying:
- 13th-14th c. - Middle English prien first appears: to peer narrowly, to look closely at something; a simple verb of straining sight.
- 16th c. - pry simply means to look closely, without blame; the gaze is effortful but innocent.
- 17th c. - a second, unrelated pry, "to force open with a lever" (from prize, Old French prise), enters the language; the two homophones are thoroughly confused, and the prying gaze comes to feel like a lever prising secrets open.
- 17th-18th c. - the moral shading is acquired: prying eyes and prying questions stand for invasive curiosity that respects no privacy.
- Contemporary - the participial adjective prying is wholly negative, naming a curiosity that violates what wishes to stay concealed - a look that does work, wedging into the dark.
