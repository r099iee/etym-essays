- Classical Roman Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — In classical Latin, nasturtium referred exclusively to wild Eurasian watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*), derived by popular etymology from *nāsus* ("nose") and *torquēre* ("to twist") on account of the sharp, pungent grimace it provoked in diners — [perception: an invigorating medicinal tonic, digestive aid, and sharp culinary salad herb].
- Renaissance Exploration & Herbals (16th–17th c.) — Following Spanish exploration in the Andes of Peru, the vivid climbing plant *Tropaeolum majus* was brought to Europe; English herbalists such as John Gerard (1597) transferred the name nasturtium (or Indian cress) to the exotic newcomer because its leaves and seeds possessed the exact peppery pungency of watercress — [perception: a dazzling, exotic ornamental wonder and appetizing salad delicacy].
- 18th to 19th Century Rural England — Spoken English phonology naturally substituted the familiar vernacular suffix -ian (modelled after words like gentian or tertiary) for the foreign Latin neuter -ium, establishing nasturtian as the dominant folk name across English country villages, cottage gardens, and rural dialects — [perception: unpretentious, comfortable domestic floral ornament of working-class and cottage garden walls].
- Mid-20th Century (J.R.R. Tolkien & The Shire, 1954) — Tolkien deliberately selected the rustic form nasturtians in The Fellowship of the Ring to depict the floral embellishments of Bag End, reinforcing the agrarian, parochial, and linguistic warmth of Hobbiton — [perception: evocative philological marker of pastoral English heritage, innocence, and cozy cottage domesticity].
- Late 20th to 21st Century — While standard horticultural and botanical practice universally settled on nasturtium / nasturtiums, the variant nasturtians remains celebrated in literary and linguistic circles as an authentic historical dialectal relic — [perception: quaint, nostalgic literary archaism and philological gem].
