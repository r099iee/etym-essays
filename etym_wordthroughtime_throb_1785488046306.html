- [Native echoic coinage (c. 1400)] - Middle English throbbe(n), "to beat, palpitate," of obscure, probably imitative origin — the word was formed in English to sound like a dull, repeated, insistent beat, kin to thud and thump.
- [The heart and the wound] - The verb was used first of the heart and then of any body part that pulses with pain — a wounded limb throbs — the steady, insistent beat of life and of injury.
- [The noun takes two lives (1570s)] - The noun throb developed a concrete sense, "a pulsation, especially of pain," alongside a figurative one, "a pulse of emotion or vitality" — a throb of joy — and 17th-century poetry made it a word for the beating heart itself.
- [Extension to machines and cities (19th–20th c.)] - Throb spread to anything that pulses rhythmically — engines, music, the bass of a dance, the life of a city — and heart-throb grew from a beat of the heart into the 20th-century celebrity whose face makes hearts beat faster.
- [Modern register] - Today throb means a strong, steady, reassuring pulsation — deep, rhythmic vitality rather than violence; to say a prose tradition "throbs" with continuity is to grant it life, warmth, and loving constancy.
