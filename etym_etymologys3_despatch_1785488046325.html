The story of DESPATCH begins with the human foot. The Indo-European root *ped-, "foot", is one of the most securely reconstructed words in the whole family — it appears in Greek poús (genitive podós), Latin pēs (pedis), Germanic *fōt- (whence English foot), and many other branches. In Latin the root built pedica, "a fetter, a foot-shackle" — the hobble that slowed a slave or an ox. To unshackle, to take the fetter off the feet, was the physical act of release: the prisoner's feet are set loose, and instantly he can move, run, and carry a message away. It is from this small, concrete image that the whole modern family of "dispatch" descends: the freed feet are the emblem of speed and of sending.

The Romance relay and its debate. Latin produced, on the model of "unfetter", a Vulgar Latin verb *dispedicāre (dis-, "apart, away", + pedica), "to free from a foot-fetter", and from it the Romance languages built their verbs of haste: Old Italian dispacciare, "to send off hastily", Spanish despachar, "to expedite, to send off", Old French despeechier, "to hurry, to make haste", and Middle French despescher. Scholars are not wholly agreed on the road to English — the OED marks the word's origin "uncertain" — and the likely relays may have cross-borrowed from one another; some derive English dispatch from Italian, others from Spanish, and a minority theory derives it from Latin expeditāre, "to set free, to expedite". What is certain is that all routes agree on the core image: the unshackled foot, the released movement, the instant departure.

Entry into English. English took the word in the early sixteenth century, around 1510, with two meanings at once: "to send off a message or messenger with haste", and "to finish a piece of business promptly". Two spellings competed from the start — dispatch and despatch — the latter showing the influence of French despescher, and for much of the nineteenth century despatch was the commoner British form. The verb's speed infected the noun: from the later sixteenth century "dispatch" (or "despatch") meant "prompt settlement of business, expedition, haste" — the very quality later praised in Randolph Quirk. The deep image persists even in the abstract use: a man unshackled at the feet, and everything he touches gets finished and sent away at once.

Semantic expansion. From the sixteenth century the word spread in three directions. First, the concrete sending sense hardened into nouns: a dispatch was the message or orders sent off, especially military despatches borne by courier, and "mentioned in despatches" became a phrase of official honour. Second, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries journalism and diplomacy adopted the word for the formal report of a correspondent or ambassador, so that "a dispatch" became a news genre in its own right. Third, a sinister offshoot: to dispatch a person came to mean to kill or put out of the way, swiftly and efficiently — a euphemism that survives in literary prose ("he dispatched his enemy with one blow"). In the nineteenth century the abstract sense of promptness settled into formal and literary prose as the editor's virtue: "with great dispatch", "to act with dispatch". The pairing of speed with finality made it the natural counterweight to meticulousness in the tribute from which this card is taken — the editor who is both swift and exact.

Modern register and the digital turn. In the twentieth century the word bifurcated. In everyday speech "dispatch" remains formal and slightly old-fashioned — a news dispatch, a dispatch rider, a despatch box (the box on the table of the House of Commons from which ministers speak). In computing and logistics, meanwhile, the old "sending off" sense returned to vigorous life: to dispatch an event, a request, or a task; a dispatcher allocates work to handlers; dynamic dispatch is a core mechanism of object-oriented programming. The ancient unshackled messenger has become the modern message routed to its destination, and the editor's brisk "despatch" is still, at bottom, the speed of feet set free from a fetter — the whole history of a word pressed into a single syllable of promptness.
