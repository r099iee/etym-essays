• Antonius: Similar because he is the other principal orator in De Oratore, of the same generation and rank; differs because Marcus Antonius was a practical, forceful speaker who distrusted philosophical culture, whereas Crassus stands for the philosophically educated ideal orator.
  • Scaevola: Similar because Quintus Mucius Scaevola the Pontifex was Crassus's consular colleague and a fellow guardian of traditional Roman authority; differs because Scaevola's eminence was legal and religious rather than oratorical, and in the dialogue he voices scepticism about the civilizing power of eloquence.
  • Cicero: Similar because Crassus embodies the ideal orator-statesman that Cicero himself championed; differs because Cicero is the author and Crassus a character, and Cicero was an Arpinate new man while Crassus belonged to the old senatorial nobility.
  • Hortensius: Similar because Quintus Hortensius Hortalus was the leading orator of the next generation; differs because Hortensius cultivated a florid Asianic manner that Cicero contrasted with Crassus's weightier, more restrained style, and he belongs to a later era.
  • Marcus Licinius Crassus: Similar because the name is identical and the two men were close kin; differs because the triumvir was a soldier and financier notorious for avarice, not an orator, and he is the Crassus meant by the phrase "as rich as Crassus."
  • crass: Similar because it descends from the same Latin adjective crassus and preserves its sense of density; differs because the English adjective is a common pejorative meaning "grossly stupid or insensitive," while the cognomen was a neutral descriptive surname.
  • Licinius: Similar because it is the family name that Crassus bore, the nomen of the gens Licinia; differs because it identifies the clan rather than the individual and was shared by consuls, tribunes and poets, including the historian Licinius Macer.
