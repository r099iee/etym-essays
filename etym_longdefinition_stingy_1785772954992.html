STINGY — adjective meaning "unwilling to give, spend, or share; meanly reluctant to part with money, food, resources, praise, or affection; miserly." It describes a disposition of tight-fistedness that goes beyond careful economy into grudging refusal, and it carries a clear pejorative moral charge: the stingy person is felt to withhold what they could well afford to give. Core sense: meanness of giving and spending. The central meaning is an attitude of unwillingness to give or spend — money, tips, portions, help, praise, time, or affection. It implies that more is available than is offered and that the withholding is a defect of character, not a necessity. Unlike thrifty or frugal, which can be praised as prudent economy, stingy is always blameworthy in standard usage: it marks the subject as small-minded, niggardly, and socially graceless. In the card's sentence — "The village elders were known to be __________ with their praise, granting approval only after years of tireless devotion" — the word is applied metaphorically to praise, showing that stinginess can attach to intangible goods, not only to money. Polysemy and applied senses. The word spreads across several connected uses:
  • Financial stinginess: unwilling to spend or part with money — a stingy tipper, a stingy employer, a stingy allowance; the dominant sense in everyday speech.
  • Scant or inadequate portions: applied to quantities, meaning "disappointingly small, meager, grudgingly given" — a stingy serving, a stingy portion, a stingy amount of cream; the emphasis falls on the smallness that betrays reluctance.
  • Withholding of non-material goods: stingy with praise, with affection, with information, with help, with time — the metaphorical extension of financial tightness to generosity of spirit; highly productive in literary and moralistic prose.
  • Niggardly effort or quality: a stingy performance, a stingy gesture — the giving of less effort, space, or warmth than is owed.
  • Dialectal "sharp, biting, ill-tempered": in older and regional usage, the adjective could mean "sharp, pungent" (of things that sting) and, of persons, "mean-spirited, ill-natured"; this is the historical bridge to the modern sense, though it now survives mainly in historical or dialectal writing.
The unifying idea is the sting: refusal that smarts. The word is felt to be connected, by folk etymology and sound, with the verb to sting — the stingy person "stings" those who ask, pricking the expectation of generosity. That tactile metaphor gives the word a sharp, wounding quality that blunter synonyms like mean lack. Register and stylistic nuance. Stingy is standard informal English, comfortable in speech, fiction, and journalism; it is not archaic or technical. It is harsher and more colloquial than parsimonious (formal, can be nearly neutral), less pathological than miserly (which implies obsessive hoarding and self-denial), and plainer than penurious (which more often means "poverty-stricken"). In British English mean is the near-equivalent ("don't be mean" = don't be stingy), while in American English mean primarily means "cruel," leaving stingy as the everyday word for ungenerosity. Word boundaries and near-synonyms.
  • Miserly — both condemn tight-fistedness; miserly adds the picture of the pathological hoarder who lives wretchedly to guard gold, while stingy can describe a single act or an ordinary temperament.
  • Parsimonious — both denote extreme reluctance to spend; parsimonious is formal and can be technical (a parsimonious explanation, parsimonious model) without moral sneer, whereas stingy is colloquial and judgmental.
  • Niggardly — both mean grudgingly scant; niggardly stresses meager quantity and is now often avoided in speech because of its unfortunate phonetic resemblance to a racial slur (it is etymologically unrelated), while stingy is the safe, common alternative.
  • Tight-fisted / tightfisted — both image the closed hand that refuses to open; tightfisted is more vivid and idiomatic, while stingy is more general and extends to praise and portions.
  • Frugal / thrifty — contrasted: these are positive or neutral words for careful economy, while stingy is pejorative; frugality is virtue, stinginess is vice.
Grammar and derivatives. Adjective stingy (comparative stingier, superlative stingiest); adverb stingily (rare); abstract noun stinginess. Frequently used with the preposition with (stingy with money/praise/help). The word is almost always predicative or attributive of persons or their actions; applied to objects or portions, it carries the transferred sense of "meager." It is a genuinely English formation of the seventeenth century from sting + -y, and its sound and sense keep it permanently anchored to the idea of the smarting sting.
