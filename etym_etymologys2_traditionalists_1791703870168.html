Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*dō-
"to give, bestow"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*dō-
"to give"
↓
Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
dare
"to give, offer, grant"
↓
Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
tradere (from trans- + dare)
"to hand over, deliver across, pass down"
↓
Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
traditio (gen. traditionis)
"a surrender, handing over, teaching handed down"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
tradicion
"transmission of beliefs, customs handed down"
↓
Middle English (c. 1380–1400)
tradicioun
"ancestral custom, doctrinal heritage"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1550–1650)
tradition
"transmitted custom, generational knowledge"
↓
19th Century English (c. 1840–1860)
traditionalist (with suffix -ist)
"advocate of adherence to tradition"
↓
Modern English (present)
traditionalists
"adherents who uphold established customs, dogmas, and canons against innovation"

Note: Latin tradere also produced English traitor and treason (via the sense of 'handing over' secrets or surrendered prisoners to an enemy). Thus, traditionalist and traitor are morphological twin siblings in historical linguistics, diverging solely on whether that which is 'handed over' is a cherished ancestral legacy (tradition) or a betrayal of one's comrades (treason).
