FOURSCORE is not a single inherited word but a compound whose two halves arrived in English along entirely different roads, one of them native Germanic and the other a Norse loan. Understanding the word therefore requires telling two complete etymologies — the story of four and the story of score — and then seeing how they fused in the vigesimal counting system of medieval England. Essay 1 — The etymology of FOUR. The first element, four, is one of the most stable words in the entire Indo-European family. Its ancestor is the reconstructed root *kʷetwóres, the Proto-Indo-European cardinal for "four," a word so firmly established that it survives recognizably in every major branch of the family: Sanskrit catvāras, Greek téssares (Doric tetores), Latin quattuor, Old Irish cethair, and the Germanic forms that concern us. The PIE numeral has a distinctive initial cluster *kʷ- (a "kw" sound), which is exactly why cognates across the family begin with k-, t-, qu-, or hw- depending on the branch's sound changes. In Germanic, the *kʷ- developed into the *f- seen in Proto-Germanic *fedwōr: the regular Germanic change turned the labiovelar *kʷ- into *f- before the following vowel in this numeral (compare the same development in the numeral "five," PIE *penkʷe → Germanic *fimf). The medial portion of the word also shows the Germanic habit of reshaping the numeral's second half, so the attested forms across the old Germanic languages — Gothic fidwor, Old Norse fjórir, Old High German fior, Old English fēower — all descend from the same proto-form with the same meaning "four." Old English fēower carried the sense "four" unchanged from the earliest texts: it appears in the genealogies, the laws, and the poetry of Anglo-Saxon England. The word then moved through Middle English (four, fower, and dialectal variants) into modern English four with a sound-change that reduced the old two-syllable fēo-wer to a single syllable. Throughout this thousand-year span the meaning never wavered: four always meant four. The numeral is a member of the closed class of basic counting words, which are among the most resistant to borrowing, sound-bleach, and semantic drift in any language. When medieval English speakers wanted to express eighty, they did not invent a new word for "eight tens" in the modern decimal fashion; they said fēower and added a score-count — because their counting system was vigesimal, based on twenties, not on tens. Essay 2 — The etymology of SCORE. The second element, score, is a Norse loan into English, but its deeper ancestry is again Indo-European, and its earlier life in the language is a story of counting by notches. The ultimate root is PIE *sker-, meaning "to cut, shear, split." This root is enormously productive across the family: Latin caro "flesh" (that which is cut), Greek keírein "to cut," Sanskrit cutting-verbs, and in Germanic the verb *skeran- "to cut, shear," which yields English shear, sharp (via a different suffix), and score itself. In Old Norse the verb skera meant "to cut," and from it was formed the noun skor, meaning "a notch, an incision, a tally-mark" — the physical cut made in a piece of wood or bone to record a count. The Norse noun entered English during the Viking Age settlements and the period of intensive Norse–English contact (roughly 9th–11th centuries), when a large number of everyday Scandinavian words passed into the English dialects of the Danelaw. Middle English took over score with the sense of "a notch, a cut, a tally-mark." But the crucial semantic development happened in the counting culture of medieval England: because livestock, goods, and years were counted by notches on a tally-stick, with one notch for each unit, the practice of counting by twenties — vigesimal counting — made each full "score" of notches stand for twenty units. The word score therefore came to mean "twenty" itself, the quantity marked by twenty tally-notches. This is the same vigesimal habit that survives in French quatre-vingts "eighty" (literally "four twenties") and in the Danish numerals, and it is what makes the English compound fourscore transparently equal to eighty. From "twenty," the word score went on to develop a whole family of secondary senses in English: the "score" of a game (the running count), the "score" of points, the "score" of a musical composition (the written lines, from the idea of the lines of the staff — a modern sense of "cut/line" imagery), and "scores of" meaning "many." But the numeral sense "twenty" is the oldest English development, attested by the 14th century and firmly established by the time of the King James Bible and the English classical prose that Gibbon imitates. The fusion: four + score. The compound fourscore is a transparent multiplication: four (4) times score (20) = 80. It is formed by simple juxtaposition of the two numerals, exactly as threescore (60), twoscore (40), and fivescore (100) were formed in the same system. The compound is attested from late Middle English and became the standard literary and biblical form for eighty: "fourscore years," "fourscore and ten" (90), the Psalmist's "the days of our years are threescore years and ten," and finally the great modern echo in Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address of 1863 — "Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent a new nation." The phrase deliberately reaches back to the cadence of the King James Bible and the whole English tradition of score-counting, giving the number 87 the solemnity of scriptural time. Gibbon's use of fourscore in the sentence "a happy period of more than fourscore years" is exactly this kind of deliberate archaism. A modern statistician would write "more than eighty years"; Gibbon, writing in the high eighteenth-century classical and biblical tradition, uses the older vigesimal form to make the Antonine age feel monumental, providential, and closed — a sealed interval of civic virtue measured in the same arithmetic as Scripture. The word thus carries within itself the full history of European counting: the deep PIE numeral four, the Norse notched tally-stick, the medieval vigesimal habit of twenty-counting, the biblical cadence, and the grand historiographical prose of the Enlightenment. Its pronunciation /fˈɔːskɔː/ still shows the two-element compound four + score with the stress on the first element and a secondary stress on the second.
