The evolution of errant: from the flow of rivers to the roving knight and the escaped particle. 1. Proto-Indo-European: motion without judgment (c. 4500–3000 BCE). The story of "errant" begins at the reconstructed root *h₁ers-, whose earliest meaning was simply "to be in motion, to wander, to flow." In the oldest attested language of the family, Vedic Sanskrit, the verb "arṣati" describes the natural, rapid flow of water — a river rushing along its course. There is no moral content whatsoever in this primal sense: to wander is merely to move, unguided, across open space, as water does. The root spread across the Indo-European world, and its Germanic reflex preserved the idea of straying and confusion — Old High German "irri" means "wandering, confused, angry," giving modern German "irren" (to wander, to err) and "Irrtum" (error) — while the Italic branch developed it into the verb that would become the direct ancestor of our word. 2. Latin: the road and the error (3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE). In Latin the root produced "errare," "to wander, to stray, to roam," and — crucially — "to make a mistake." The Romans were the great builders of straight roads, surveyors of boundaries, and codifiers of law, and their culture valued the straight path above all. To wander off the road was not merely physical: leaving the established path usually led to being lost, and so "errare" quickly acquired the metaphorical sense of wandering away from truth, right, and correctness. Already in Plautus and Terence (3rd–2nd c. BCE) "errare" means both "to roam" and "to be wrong." From the participle "errans, errantem" ("wandering") Latin generated a rich family: "error" (a wandering, a mistake), "erroneus" (straying, giving "erroneous"), "erraticus" (wandering to and fro, giving "erratic"), and, with prefixes, "aberratio" (a wandering away, giving "aberration"). The dual semantic core — physical wandering and intellectual/moral error — was fixed in Latin and would shape the English word forever. 3. Old French and the chivalric collision (12th–13th c.). When the Latin participle passed into Old French as "errant," it encountered a second Latin source that transformed it. Old French had a separate verb "errer," "to journey, to travel," which descends not from "errare" but from Latin "iterare" (from "iter," a journey, a road). The two verbs, near-identical in sound, fused in medieval usage: "errant" could mean both "wandering/straying" (from errare) and "travelling/journeying" (from iterare). This collision is the origin of the famous "chevalier errant," the knight-errant of the chivalric romances — a warrior who has deliberately left the service of any single lord to journey in search of adventure, to right wrongs, and to prove himself. In this context "errant" was admiring and noble: to wander was to be free, adventurous, questing. The same word, however, retained its darker Latin sense of straying from truth, and both senses passed into English together when the word was borrowed in the fourteenth century. 4. Middle and Early Modern English: knight and sinner (14th–17th c.). English took "errant" in the fourteenth century, in the twin senses "travelling, roving" and "straying, erring." The chivalric sense dominated early: "knight errant" appears in the English romances by around 1300 and remains in use to this day, carrying the full glamour of the wandering hero — the model for Don Quixote (who in Cervantes' satire of 1605 is literally a "hidalgo" turned "caballero andante," the Spanish equivalent of the knight-errant). Alongside it, the moral sense flourished in sermons and moral literature: an "errant" person or "errant" belief is one that has strayed from right religion, right conduct, or right reason. Both uses coexist through the Renaissance and into the early modern period, and the word also appears in descriptive natural history and travel writing for anything that wanders — birds, clouds, thoughts. 5. The modern dispersion: from courts to crucibles (18th c. – present). From the eighteenth century onward, "errant" generalised far beyond knights and sinners. The spatial sense — an "errant" object that has missed its target — became standard for arrows, cannonballs, and (today) golf shots and baseballs that fly off course. The moral sense softened into a mildly censorious adjective for behaviour ("errant behaviour," "an errant son"). And, most interestingly for our literary sentence, the word entered scientific and technical prose to describe anything that has escaped the system meant to contain it: an "errant" particle, an "errant" current, an "errant" gene, an "errant" electron. This modern technical sense is the direct continuation of the Roman image of the traveler leaving the road — now applied to matter leaving the crucible. In the supplied context, an "errant flocculus of humour" escaping a heated alchemical crucible is exactly this: a tiny wandering clot of vapour that has broken containment and roams unpredictably. The word has thus come full circle — from the free flow of ancient rivers, through the Roman road and its errors, to the noble wanderings of the knight-errant, and finally to the straying particle of modern science — always describing the same thing: motion that has left the course.
