beset — the word in this passage and in the language. In the source sentence — "But all were beset by certain odd impressions" — the passive participle carries a quietly threatening weight. The men are not attacked in any armed, physical sense; rather they are beset by certain odd impressions, and the word makes the crowding of those impressions feel like a slow, tactical encirclement of the mind. The tone is one of mounting unease: the cave's darkness presses in on every side, and beset captures the sense of being simultaneously watched, hemmed in, and slowly worn down by pressures that cannot be located or answered. Because the verb carries an Old English military ancestry — its oldest senses concern laying siege to a fortress — the passive voice here quietly converts psychological strain into something almost strategic, as if the adventurers' own perceptions had thrown a cordon around them.

1. The full range of senses.
  • To surround and assail (military, the oldest sense): to set about on every side, to lay siege to, to attack from all directions at once — "the city was beset by the enemy." This sense, central in Old and Middle English, survives mainly in figurative and passive use.
  • To trouble or threaten persistently (the modern core): of difficulties, dangers, doubts, or problems that crowd in and press upon one — "beset by worries," "beset with difficulties," "a project beset by delays." The emphasis is on persistence and pressure from many sides, not on a single blow.
  • To hem in, enclose, or occupy (spatial): "a valley beset by mountains," "a path beset with thorns." The idea is of being set about, ringed, boxed in.
  • To set or fix ornaments on, to stud (ornamental, now rare): "a crown beset with jewels" — the object is set about with decorations. This is the literal Old English sense of "set things around or upon," preserved in poetry.
  • The participial adjective: besetting — "a besetting sin" is the fault that persistently dogs a person, the vice that surrounds and harries one; the collocation is one of the word's most famous survivals.

2. Grammar and usage. The verb is transitive; in modern English it appears overwhelmingly in the passive, followed by "by" for an agent ("beset by doubts," "beset by problems") or "with" for something surrounding ("beset with difficulties"). The present tense besets and the past/past participle beset are invariant in form — the verb shows the collapse of the older weak forms (Middle English bisetten, bisette) into a single form. The register is formal and literary; the word is rare in casual speech, which is precisely what gives it its weight in the source passage: a formal, almost ceremonial word for being surrounded by pressures.

3. The semantic bridge, stated plainly. "To set about on every side" → "to surround with an armed force, to besiege" → (by extension from the physical to the psychological) "to crowd in upon, to press, to trouble persistently." The military picture of a fortress ringed by an army became, over six centuries, the picture of a mind ringed by impressions — and the source passage sits exactly at that transfer, where the besieging army has become the odd impressions of the cave.

4. The synonym field. Against its near-synonyms, beset occupies a distinct position:
  • Besieged: implies an active, deliberate blockade by external forces, a defined attacker and a defined position; beset is vaguer, describing pressures arriving from all sides at once, often without a visible source.
  • Beleaguered: the closest in register — exhaustion under sustained pressure — yet it still carries the imagery of a military camp; beset is the quieter, more inward word.
  • Assailed and assaulted: more violent and more sudden, implying a direct strike, whereas beset suggests persistence rather than impact.
  • Harassed: petty, repeated annoyances — trivial by comparison.
  • Harried: pursued and pressed hard, implying an active pursuer; beset implies encirclement rather than pursuit.
  • Haunted: recurrent supernatural visitations, a personal, spectral presence; beset is impersonal and diffuse.
  • Plagued: afflicted repeatedly as by a plague — a disease metaphor; beset is the encirclement metaphor.
  • Tormented: active, deliberate infliction of suffering; beset is persistent pressure rather than deliberate cruelty.
  • Hemmed in, surrounded, encircled, encompassed: the spatial set, describing being ringed by things, but without the sense of hostile pressure and wear.
  • Overtaken: caught up with and overwhelmed, suggesting motion and pursuit; beset suggests being already ringed, static and pressed.
  • Dogged: followed and harassed persistently like a dog at the heels; beset is the surrounding, not the following.
In collocation, beset is typically passive and followed by "by" for abstract agencies (beset by doubts, fears, difficulties, scandals, temptations) and by "with" for concrete or surrounding things (beset with obstacles, with dangers, with thorns); it collocates strongly with "impressions," "problems," "delays," "confusion," "uncertainty." The word's tone is formal, slightly archaic in flavour, and quietly ominous — the perfect vehicle for a fear rising not from enemies but from odd impressions.
