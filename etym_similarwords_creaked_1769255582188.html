• squeaked: Similar because both name a thin, high-pitched, penetrating sound of friction or strain. Differs because a squeak is usually short, sharp, and small (a mouse, a hinge), while a creak is longer, drier, and heavier — the sound of rigid mass straining rather than small surfaces sliding.
  • squealed: Similar because both are high-pitched sounds of stress or friction. Differs because a squeal is more prolonged and shrill, often of brakes or of living things (a pig, a child), while a creak suggests dry, cracking timber or metal flexing rather than sliding.
  • screeched: Similar because both are harsh, grating, high-volume sounds. Differs because a screech is louder, more piercing, and usually vocal or metal-on-metal, while a creak is comparatively modest, tight, and "woody" — a sound of internal strain rather than a shriek.
  • shrieked: Similar because both denote a piercing, strained, alarming sound. Differs because a shriek is a human or animal cry of alarm or pain, a voice, whereas a creak is the sound of an object, not a throat.
  • grated: Similar because both describe a rough, rasping friction sound of surfaces in contact. Differs because grating is heavier, more continuous, and lower, like teeth or metal on stone, while creaking is lighter, higher, and more intermittently rhythmic — one stiff thing flexing.
  • scraped: Similar because both name the sound of surfaces rubbing together. Differs because scraping implies one surface dragged across another with a sliding motion, while creaking is the sound of a single stressed material flexing or turning within a joint.
  • rasped: Similar because both are dry, coarse, friction-based sounds. Differs because a rasp is deeper, rougher, and file-like, lacking the high-pitched, almost squeaky quality of a creak.
  • ground (as in grinding): Similar because both name harsh friction sounds of resistant material. Differs because grinding is heavier, lower, and continuous — stone or teeth on stone — while creaking is higher and more sparsely intermittent.
  • gnashed: Similar because both are harsh, grating sounds of stressed material (gnashing teeth). Differs because gnash is specific to teeth and expresses rage or pain, while a creak is impersonal, mechanical, and non-vocal.
  • groaned: Similar because both suggest material or a person giving way under strain with a low complaint. Differs because a groan is low, long, and quasi-human (and usually literally human), while a creak is high, dry, and the property of objects.
  • moaned: Similar because both convey an audible expression of strain or distress. Differs because a moan is vocal, human, plaintive, and continuous, whereas a creak is the non-vocal protest of rigid material.
  • whined: Similar because both are high-pitched, penetrating sounds of friction or complaint. Differs because a whine is a sustained, even pitch (a mosquito, an engine, a person complaining), while a creak is broken, dry, and pitch-varying — a series of strained starts.
  • crackled: Similar because both name dry, brittle sounds of stressed material. Differs because crackling is a rapid series of tiny snaps (a fire, static), while a creak is a single slow, drawn-out squeak of flexing wood or metal.
  • cracked: Similar because both are sounds of timber under extreme stress, and in the passage the crash follows the creaking. Differs because cracking is the sharp, decisive sound of actual fracture — the snap — whereas creaking is the tense, pre-fracture protest before it.
  • rattled: Similar because both name noise produced by hard material under agitation. Differs because a rattle is a fast series of hard clinks (a window, a chain, a snake), not the slow, sticky, high-pitched squeak of creaking.
  • jarred: Similar because both describe an abruptly harsh, unpleasantly grating effect. Differs because jar is chiefly a verb of impact and shock to the nerves (and a noun meaning a container), while creak is purely an acoustic quality of flexing material.
  • croaked: Similar because both words originally named the harsh cries of birds, and both are dry, guttural or grating sounds. Differs because a croak is deep, hoarse, and froglike or crow-like, while a creak is higher and mechanical; croak stayed with living voices, creak moved to objects.
  • cawed: Similar because both began as the cries of birds and share a harsh, raw timbre. Differs because caw is specifically the raucous call of a crow, still strictly vocal and avian, whereas creak has long been the sound of wood and machinery.
  • crepitated: Similar because both are words for dry, crackling, or creaking sounds, and both belong to the same family of friction noises. Differs because crepitate is a learned, medical or scientific term (the crackle heard in lungs or in grinding joints), while creak is the ordinary word for the same phenomenon in doors and timber.
  • scrooped: Similar because both name a dry, rough grating sound of friction (a scroop of stiff silk or a hinge). Differs because scroop is rare, dialectal or textile-specific (the rustle-grind of silk), while creak is common, general, and typically of wood or metal.
  • cark (obsolete verb): Similar because Old English cearcian "to creak, gnash, jar" is the direct etymological relative of creak, and the two share the same dry grating identity. Differs because cark is obsolete in the sound sense and survives only as a word meaning "to worry or burden," so it cannot be used for sounds today.
