supplications (/ˌsʌp.lɪˈkeɪ.ʃənz/) is the plural of supplication: the act of asking humbly and earnestly, or the prayer, entreaty or petition so offered. The word is formal, slightly archaic and strongly religious in colour. One makes supplications to God, to a sovereign, or to anyone with the power to grant or withhold; one does not normally make them to a shopkeeper or a colleague.

In the source, Paris is in terror at the news of the King's illness: "the churches resounded with supplications and groans". The pairing is exact. Supplications are articulate, ordered, often liturgical prayer — public, priest-led, perhaps chanted — while groans are the inarticulate noise of distress. The plural matters too: it suggests many voices and repeated petitions, an ongoing state of prayer rather than a single request. The word suits the eighteenth-century scene, in which a king's illness was a public calamity and the Church organised intercession on his behalf.

Primary sense: prayer addressed to God

  • Humble, urgent prayer, especially intercessory prayer on behalf of another: "supplications for the recovery of the King", "the supplications of the faithful".
  • Chiefly in religious and scriptural registers. The Authorized Version's "supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks" (1 Timothy 2:1) fixed the word in English devotional language, where it often appears in a list of prayer-types.
  • Liturgical and paraliturgical use: litanies, rogations and days of prayer are described as supplications; the plural is the norm because such prayer is corporate and repeated.
  • The word implies inequality between petitioner and addressee. That asymmetry is essential: a supplication is not a negotiation but an appeal from below.

Second sense: humble entreaty to a person in authority

  • A kneeling or abject request made to a ruler, patron or judge: "the supplications of the poor reached the Queen", "he listened to their supplications".
  • It implies ceremony and deference; in older narrative it often accompanies gestures — kneeling, bowing, clasping the knees, weeping.
  • In this use the word can be faintly ironic in modern prose, since the humility it names belongs to an older social order.

Third sense: a formal written petition

  • In medieval and early modern administration a supplication was a written request addressed to the Crown, to Parliament, to a bishop or to a court. Petitions of this kind were a recognised legal instrument, with formulae and registrars.
  • At Oxford and Cambridge the supplicat (Latin, "he prays") is the formal application for a degree, still used in the universities' Latin ceremony — a direct institutional descendant of the Roman petition.
  • In canon law a libellus supplex is a petition to an ecclesiastical court; the terminology survives in the Roman Curia.

Fourth sense: the Roman supplicatio

  • In the Roman Republic a supplicatio was a public act of prayer and thanksgiving decreed by the Senate, often for several days, during which the temples were opened and the whole citizen body was expected to attend.
  • It could be decreed for a military victory, for the averting of prodigies, or for the recovery of a magistrate; Livy records many such decrees, and the honour was politically charged.
  • This institutional sense explains why the Latin word denotes a public, authorised act rather than private devotion — a nuance English partly inherited.

Fifth, literal sense: the gesture of bowing

  • Etymologically the Latin verb means "to bend or fold oneself down"; the physical posture of kneeling is the root of the metaphor.
  • English rarely uses supplication for the posture alone, but the image survives in phrases such as "on bended knee in supplication".

Grammar, collocations and register

  • Countable in the plural (supplications) and usually abstract-singular in the set phrase "in supplication".
  • Verbs that collocate: offer, make, pour out, utter, raise, heed, reject. Adjectives: fervent, earnest, humble, piteous, ceaseless.
  • Register: elevated and devotional; in ordinary modern speech plea, appeal or request would be used instead. The word survives chiefly in liturgy, scripture, historical writing and deliberate archaism.
  • Emotional charge: pathos and humility, sometimes desperation; less shrill than begging, more abject than asking.
