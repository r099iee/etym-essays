- Roused (past participle of rouse) — early 15th century, falconry: a hawk "rouses" by shaking its feathers; the earliest English sense is purely physical, meaning "shaken, ruffled up", with no wakefulness implied.
- 16th century: the sense expands to hunting and the chase — game is "roused" (startled or driven from cover); the word enters the vocabulary of the hunt and of war, meaning stirred abruptly into motion.
- 17th–18th century: roused shifts to the human, everyday sense — woken from sleep, called from rest, summoned from idleness; "to rouse" someone becomes to shake them into alertness, increasingly with an urgent or inspiring tone.
- 19th century (Romantic and Victorian): heavily figurative — crowds are "roused" to fury, soldiers to courage, lovers to passion; the participle carries emotional electricity and often moral or patriotic weight.
- 20th century to present: slightly formal and literary; "roused" is retained in phrases like "roused from sleep", "roused to anger", and "roused the crowd", and in the adjective "rousing" (a rousing speech, a rousing cheer). It has largely ceded the purely sexual sense to "aroused", keeping the wakeful, energetic, and emotional register.
