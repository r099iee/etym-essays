- 5th–4th c. BCE — Transliterated into Classical Greek by Ctesias of Cnidus as Sardanápallos (Σαρδανάπαλλος); cited by Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics as the prime example of a life slavishly surrendered to animalistic pleasure.
- 1st c. BCE — Detailed by Diodorus Siculus in his Bibliotheca Historica, establishing the narrative of the two-year siege of Nineveh, the river flood, and the colossal self-immolating funeral pyre.
- 1st–2nd c. CE — Invoked in Roman Imperial literature by Cicero and satirized by Juvenal (Satire X: "the amours, feasts, and down cushions of Sardanapalus") as a proverb for imperial soft degeneracy.
- 16th–17th c. — Borrowed into Early Modern English through Renaissance humanist translations; used in Elizabethan and Jacobean political discourse to warn against effeminate monarchs.
- 1821–1827 — Revolutionized by Lord Byron's drama Sardanapalus and Eugène Delacroix's landmark Louvre painting The Death of Sardanapalus, transforming the cowardly voluptuary into a sublime, tragic Romantic hero.
- 20th–21st c. — Retained in high-register literary prose, art criticism, and political commentary as the definitive archetype of catastrophic hedonism and aesthetic self-destruction.
