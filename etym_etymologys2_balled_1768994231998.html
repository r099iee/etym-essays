ROOT WORD — balled (past participle of "to ball") 1. PIE *bʰel- "to swell, to blow up, to inflate" (c. 4000 BCE) — the root of roundness: a sphere conceived as something pushed out round from within. NOTE: the handbooks also record a separate root written *bʰel- "to blow" (source of Latin flare "to blow," English blast?), and Greek phallós "phallus" is sometimes compared as a "swollen thing" but is flagged as UNVERIFIED — these are kept separate from the "swell" sense that produced the ball-words. 2. Proto-Germanic *balluz "ball, round object" (c. 200 CE) — a "swelling," a round thing made by inflation or gathering. 3. Old Norse bǫllr "ball, round object" (attested from the Viking Age, 8th–11th c.) — the form through which the word entered English. 4. Middle English bal "ball, round object" (12th–15th c.) — borrowed chiefly through Norse contact in the Danelaw. 5. English ball (noun) "a round object, a sphere" (13th c. onward) — with the verb to ball "to form into a ball, to clench into a fist" (16th–17th c.). 6. English balled (past tense and past participle of to ball) "formed into a ball; clenched into a sphere" — BALLED (the form on this card). NOTES ON DEBATE AND HOMOPHONES: - The dance "ball" (a formal social dance) is a DIFFERENT word: Greek ballízein "to dance" → Latin ballāre → Old French bal → English ball (14th c.). It is not from *bʰel- "to swell." - English balloon (via Italian pallone "big ball" → French ballon) and English ballot (via Italian ballotta "small ball," the voting ball) descend from the same Germanic *balluz through Frankish and Langobardic loans — genuine kin of ball, not new roots. - Latin follis "a leather bag, bellows, an inflated ball" is a genuine Italic relative of *bʰel-, inherited by English as bellows and boll (a round seed-pod). - Sanskrit bāla "strength" is NOT a secure cognate of ball (it is usually linked to *bel- "strong"), and no Hindi or Odia reflex of *bʰel- "to swell" is accepted in the handbooks; none is forced here.
