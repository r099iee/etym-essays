- [Antiquity: PIE to Greek & Latin] - Greek týmbos originally denotes a raised earthen burial mound or barrow heaped over a hero's remains - [connotation: heroic honor, memorial tumulus in Homeric epic]
- [11th–13th c. Old French to ME] - Borrowed into Middle English via Anglo-Norman tumbe / Old French tombe, designating stone sepulchres and church monuments - [connotation: high aristocratic, feudal, and ecclesiastical prestige in Christian burial]
- [14th–16th c. Late ME to Early Modern] - Spelling is Latinized to tomb with a silent b; establishes rich metaphorical depth in Shakespeare and the English Bible - [connotation: solemn physical boundary of mortality and eternal rest]
- [17th–19th c.] - Central to Romantic, Gothic, and Victorian elegiac poetry (Gray's Elegy, Shelley, Keats, Wilde) - [connotation: melancholy beauty, the sublime finality of death, and the immortality of art and love]
- [20th–21st c.] - Retains formal, literary, and archaeological grandeur (King Tutankhamun's tomb, Tomb of the Unknown Soldier) - [connotation: timeless monument of national commemoration and archaeological awe]
