The history of burrow / burrows is short, murky and peculiarly English, with a debated link to hills and forts rather than to digging. The most widely accepted account connects it to Old English beorg (hill, mound, barrow) and burg, burh (fortified place, borough), both from Proto-Germanic *bergaz (hill, protection) from Proto-Indo-European *bhergh- (high, to rise, to protect). In Middle English southern dialects burgh developed a variant borow, borowe, barew meaning mound, tumulus or earthwork — the raised dry ground where rabbits characteristically dig. By c.1300 borow is attested in the specialised sense hole in the ground made by a small animal, rabbit-hole: the mound name slides to the hole in the mound. Early spellings wobble (borow, borowe, burgh, borugh) before 16th-century burrow with -u- stabilises, perhaps under influence of furrow or borrow.

The verb to burrow, to borowen appears almost simultaneously (late 14th century): to make a borow, to dig a hole. Noun and verb thus reinforce each other from the start, making it hard to say which is primary — dictionaries list the noun first by a hair. The plural burrows and third-singular burrows are regular modern inflections; no irregularity ever developed. Phonetics are stable: Middle English /bɔrou/ → modern /ˈbʌrəʊ/ with FOOT-STRUT lowering and smoothing, RP with non-rhotic final.

  • 1300s–1400s — emergence: borow as rabbit-hole in hunting and warren accounts; warrens are valuable seigneurial property, so the word lives in manorial rolls alongside coney, warren, ferret. No literary glamour yet.
  • 1500s–1600s — spelling fixation and semantic widening: burrow extends from rabbit-holes to any small-animal tunnel (fox, badger, mole) and to human dugouts (burrowed huts). Shakespearean contemporaries use to burrow for hiding (burrowed in earth). Surname Burrows, Burroughs (one who dwells by a mound or warren) becomes common.
  • 1700s–1800s — natural-history precision: Gilbert White, Darwin and warreners distinguish burrow, warren, earth, sett technically. Children's literature (Alice's rabbit-hole, Wind in the Willows Badger's sett) romanticises burrows as cosy underworld homes, adding warmth to a previously utilitarian word.
  • 1900s–present — metaphorical explosion: journalism, psychoanalysis and computing adopt to burrow into for deep research, repression and menu-diving. Ecology elevates burrowing animals to ecosystem engineers. The word stays phonetically frozen while its applications multiply.

Debate note: the OED explicitly marks burrow origin uncertain. The burgh/mound → rabbit-hole derivation is the leading hypothesis, supported by Middle English spelling overlap and by topography (warrens on mounds), but no unbroken textual chain proves it, and some scholars propose an independent imitative or pre-English substratum root for bor- (hole). The connection to borough is therefore plausible but not certain, and the Proto-Indo-European *bhergh- ancestry reaches burrow only if that hypothesis holds. What is certain is Middle English borow c.1300 as hole-dug-by-animal and continuous use thereafter; everything before that is reconstruction with a caution flag.
