peerless — without an equal; matchless, unrivalled, so far above its kind that no standard of comparison can reach it. The word is built from peer, "an equal, one of the same rank or standing," and the privative suffix -less, "without": the peerless thing is that which has no peer, that which stands outside the ladder of comparison altogether. In the passage from Gormenghast, "the latitude permitted to the most peerless" refers to those carvers whose skill has set them so far above the common run that the castle's rule — which otherwise forbids all contact between the inner and outer worlds — bends to admit them: once a year, the peerless few are permitted what no other within the walls may have. The nuance is social as much as artistic: in a world of rigid hierarchy, where every man is ranked by his craft, the peerless carver is the anomaly, the exception who breaks the scale of ranks itself. The tone is that of a greatness which disrupts order: not merely first among equals, but outside the system of equality — like the perfect carving among the many, peerless is the word for the thing that ends all argument about comparison. The word's senses and registers fan out in a graded series:
  • Of persons — without an equal in excellence, skill, or worth: a peerless craftsman, a peerless beauty; the word of courtly praise from the age of chivalry.
  • Of things and qualities — unrivalled, supremely good: peerless craftsmanship, a peerless collection; the standard superlative of rhetoric for anything without rival.
  • Register — formal, literary, and now faintly archaic or elevated; in modern commercial language it survives as a heightened, almost hyperbolic superlative in marketing and praise.
  • The word's double anchor — peer means both "an equal" and "a noble of equal rank," so that peerless inherits an aristocratic undertone: to call a thing peerless is to say that no other stands on its level, as no commoner stands beside a peer.
Its synonyms should be finely graded. Matchless and unmatched carry the image of a contest in which no opponent appears. Unrivalled and unparalleled suggest a competitor who never draws level. Incomparable is the logical extreme — too different in kind to be compared at all — and so is easily weakened into polite hyperbole. Unequalled is factual, measuring what has not been achieved; unsurpassed concedes that others have come close. Unique means one of a kind, not necessarily superior. And peerless alone speaks the language of rank: it was formed in the world of peers, nobles and equals, so that to call a thing peerless is to say that no other stands on its level — the word of the aristocrat applied, in Peake's world, to the carver who breaks the scale of ranks. Two further nuances distinguish the word. First, the rhetorical force of the privative suffix -less is absolute: where words like unsurpassed or unrivalled concede that others have approached the summit, peerless simply removes the category of comparison — there is no peer to be approached, so the question of nearness never arises. Second, the word's anchoring in the vocabulary of rank means that it is almost always used with conscious elevation: it is rare in plain speech and natural in praise, eulogy, and formal judgement, exactly the registers in which a castle's Earl pronounces on the year's carvings. (A caution for the careful reader: the unrelated verb "to peer," "to look closely," descends from a different Latin root — parere, "to appear" — and has nothing to do with peerless, despite the identical spelling.)
