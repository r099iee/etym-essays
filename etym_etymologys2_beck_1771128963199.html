Linear derivation chain for BECK (the noun 'a gesture of summons'), a back-formation from the verb 'to beck/beckon': • Proto-Indo-European *bʰeh₂- (conventionally *bhā-) — 'to shine, to gleam, to be manifest' — the root of light and visible display. (Reconstruction; note the debate below.) • Proto-Germanic *baukną (also *bauknam) — 'beacon, sign, signal; that which makes itself visible' — the visible token, especially a signal by light. (1st millennium BCE; reconstruction.) • Old English bēacen — 'sign, portent, beacon, banner, standard' — attested from the 8th century; the direct ancestor of modern English beacon. • Old English bēacnian (West Saxon) / bīecnan (Anglian) — 'to make a sign to, to nod, to summon' — the gesture-verb built on the sign-noun; attested in late Old English. • Middle English bekenen, beknen — 'to make a sign, to beckon' — 13th century; alongside the short form bekken 'to make a sign.' • English beckon — the modern verb 'to summon by gesture' — 16th century onward. • English beck (verb) — a shortened or aphetic form of beckon/ME bekken — 'to make a sign, to nod' — attested from the Middle English period onward. • English BECK (noun) — 'a nod or gesture of summons; a signal' — a noun back-formed from the verb, attested from the 14th century onward. Note on the debate: the connection of the beacon/beckon family to PIE *bʰeh₂- 'to shine' is the standard account (Pokorny's *bhā-), and the semantic line 'that which shines → that which makes visible → a sign' is well supported by the cognates. Some scholars, however, treat the Germanic sign-words as an independent formation on the same root, or emphasise a sense 'to show, point out' rather than light; none of these alternatives changes the attested English descent from Old English bēacen/bēacnian. The back-formation of the noun beck from the verb is likewise not in doubt, though dictionaries differ on whether the noun is first clearly attested in the 14th or 15th century.
