Bobbing is the present participle of a small, quick, intensely physical verb whose origins are, frankly, obscure — and the honest etymology must say so. Unlike the grand Latinate vocabulary of English, bob is a word of the body and the marketplace: short, blunt, and probably coined on the spot, over and over, wherever people described light things dipping and rising. 1. Middle English bobben: strike, jerk, and mock The first attested forms appear in the 14th century. Middle English bobben meant to strike, buffet, or pound — a light, repeated blow — and from there it spread into a cluster of related uses: to move jerkily, to shake, and, in another branch, to mock or deceive. Beside the verb stood the noun bob, meaning a blow or a small rounded mass or hanging bunch. Verb and noun almost certainly reinforced each other, the physical image of a compact thing being struck or bouncing being the natural raw material for all the later senses. The mocking and deceiving senses are sometimes attributed to Old French bobe/bobance, and a Celtic origin has been floated for the "bunch, tassel" noun, but no single source is proven for all uses. This is characteristic of expressive short verbs: they are coined repeatedly, in several dialects at once, and their histories tangle rather than tracing a clean line. 2. From striking to floating: the motion sense The semantic step that matters for this card is the development of the motion sense: from "strike lightly" and "move jerkily," English comes to use bob for anything that moves with short, repeated, up-and-down or to-and-fro motion — a cork floating, a boat riding a swell, a head dipping, a feather in the air. The image is one of lightness and buoyancy: what bobs is not heavy or dignified but quick and buoyant, and it keeps bobbing as long as the water, the wind, or the nervous energy continues. The present participle bobbing is the ordinary modern form for that ongoing motion, and it is the exact form the language produces when the motion is continuous rather than completed — a grammatical fact that Gloin's sentence in The Hobbit exploits directly. 3. The branching of the bob family Over the centuries bob sprouted a remarkable number of shoots, all sharing the notions of shortness, quickness, or a compact hanging object:
  • Bobbed hair — a short women's haircut, from the verb sense "to cut short," established by the early 20th century as the emblematic style of the modern woman.
  • Plumb bob — the weight on a line for testing verticality, keeping the "hanging, swinging" image.
  • A bob or bobbed curtsey — a quick, shallow bow of respect, still literally a short dipping motion.
  • Bobtail — a short, docked tail, and the animal so marked; bobcat keeps the same "short" idea in the tail.
  • Bob (slang) — a shilling, a coin-nickname of different and uncertain origin, a caution that not every "bob" belongs to the motion family.
  • Bobbin — a reel of thread, probably related to the "rounded lump" noun but partly a separate borrowing from French bobine; it must not be force-linked to the motion verb.
  • Bob and weave — the boxer's dip-and-dodge, a modern idiomatic compound built on the participle.
4. The participle and the Tolkien moment In Gloin's mouth, "the little fellow bobbing and puffing on the mat" uses the participle to shrink Bilbo socially. He is not standing like a warrior or even like a confident host; he is jigging like a cork, up and down with short breath, all nervous energy and no heroic poise. The -ing form is essential to the effect: it reports continuous, ongoing motion — a first impression that is still in progress — rather than a single completed bob. Every layer of the word's history, from medieval buffets to floating corks to nervous heads in a crowd, is compressed into that one participle, and the result is the most un-heroic word a dwarf could choose for a prospective burglar.
