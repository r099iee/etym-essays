egrets — In the line "egrets in their rookeries, white as candles among the moss," the plural "egrets" is the marsh's one flash of light: the birds stand in the gloom the way tapers burn in a dark church. The word in this context is less a piece of ornithology than a unit of pure illumination — long-legged, white-plumed waders picked out against moss and shadow, each one a still, upright flame. The literary tone is hushed and almost liturgical: "white as candles" turns the feeding birds into votive objects, and "egrets" supplies the whiteness that the rest of the sentence is conspicuously missing. It is a word of clean light, silence, and poised elegance, chosen precisely because the rookery that follows is anything but quiet.

Primary sense (ornithology). An egret is a long-legged wading bird of the family Ardeidae (the herons), distinguished within that family by its usually white or buff plumage and, in breeding season, by long, fine, ornamental plumes called aigrettes. English names several species: the Great Egret (Ardea alba), the Snowy Egret (Egretta thula), the Little Egret (Egretta garzetta), and the cattle egret (Bubulcus ibis), which has abandoned marsh and reeds to follow grazing livestock. The word "egret" thus names both a genus-level grouping and a general white-heron type, and in ordinary usage it contrasts with "heron" (the general family name, often grey, brown, or blue) precisely on the axis of whiteness and plume.

Secondary sense (the plume trade and fashion). Because the egret's breeding plumes — the "aigrettes" — were prized as hat ornaments in the millinery trade of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, "egret" and "aigrette" became words of fashion. The plume trade nearly exterminated egrets in North America: tens of thousands of birds were shot for their feathers to decorate women's hats, provoking the conservation movement that led to protective legislation and the Audubon campaigns. "Egret" therefore carries a charged conservation history: a word that moved from hunting-ground to hat-shop to protective law.

Tertiary and extended senses. Adjectivally and figuratively, "egret-white" or "egret" can stand for a shade of snowy whiteness ("an egret of a dress"), and the poet's image of "white as candles" exploits exactly this colour-essence. In heraldry the egret appears as a heraldic charge; in place-names and lodge names it connotes marshland elegance. The plural "egrets" further lets the word operate as a collective image of whiteness scattered through a landscape — a usage that feeds directly back into this line.

rookeries — The word "rookeries" (plural of "rookery") is the second light of the sentence, but its light is social and noisy. Its root sense is a breeding colony of rooks — the large, gregarious, black crows of the species Corvus frugilegus that nest communally in the tops of tall trees, often in a loud, disorderly cluster of nests.

  • Primary sense: a rook's colony, a nesting site where many rooks build their stick-nests close together and fill the trees with cawing and quarrelling. In the passage "in their rookeries," the birds stand in the very structures of their collective life — a noisy nest-village, exactly opposed to the silent, candle-white stillness of the egrets.
  • Secondary sense (ornithological extension): by the eighteenth century "rookery" was extended to the nesting colonies of any gregarious bird — penguins, gulls, herons (a "heronry"), albatrosses — a noisy, crowded breeding ground. A "rookery" is therefore any dense, communal nesting site, and the plural "rookeries" keeps that communal meaning.
  • Tertiary sense (human slums): in the nineteenth century the word made a striking semantic leap to crowded human tenements: the rookery slums of Victorian London — overcrowded, labyrinthine courts of lodging-houses, dens, and thieves' kitchens, packed "like nests" — were called rookeries. This sense took the idea of crowding, darkness, and close-packed disorder and applied it to human habitation, so that "rookery" could name a warren of slum dwellings. It is this range — from tree-top bird colony to packed human warren — that makes "rookeries" so rich in the line: the marsh birds stand in nests that share their name with the dens of the poor.
  • Figurative and derivative uses: "rookery" has also named a crowd or cluster of anything crowded (a rookery of ships in a harbour, a rookery of brokers on a street), and in animal lore a "rookery" of seals is a breeding haul-out. The word is a textbook case of semantic extension moving from a specific bird to a general principle of crowding.

Summary of polysemy. EGRETS: (1) white wading heron of the Ardeidae; (2) the plume-carrying hat-feather of the millinery trade (cf. aigrette); (3) a shade/emblem of snowy whiteness; (4) a conservation cause célèbre. ROOKERIES: (1) a rook's breeding colony; (2) any gregarious bird's nesting colony (heronry, penguin rookery); (3) a crowded slum warren (Victorian tenement); (4) any crowded cluster. The historical bridge in each case runs through the bird's defining behaviour — whiteness of plumage for the egret, communal noise and density for the rookery.
