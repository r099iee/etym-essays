English STINGY (adjective, formed in the 17th c. as sting + adjectival suffix -y; first attestations c. 1660s in the sense "sharp, biting, ill-tempered," with the modern "mean, niggardly" sense developing by the late 17th–early 18th c.) — from Middle English stingen "to sting, prick, pierce" (Old English stingan) — from Proto-Germanic *stinganą "to stick, prick, sting" — from the Indo-European root *steng- / *ste(n)g- "to prick, stick, pierce" (nasal-infix variant of *steg- "sharp, pointed"). Stage chain (single linear derivation): 1) PIE *steg- / *steng- "to be sharp, prick, stick, pierce" (reconstructed; a pointed/piercing root with a nasal-infix grade *ste(n)g-) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; the family also underlies Latin stimulus "goad, sting" (with variant *steig-) and Greek stízō "to prick, tattoo" (though the exact sub-branches are debated). 2) Proto-Germanic *stinganą "to stick, pierce, sting" (strong verb, reconstructed; class 3 strong verb with preterite *stang-/stung-) — projected c. 500 BCE–200 CE. 3) Old English stingan "to sting, pierce, thrust" (strong verb, attested from the 8th c.) — e.g. in glosses and prose; the past forms stang, stungen show the Germanic ablaut. 4) Middle English stingen "to sting, prick, wound with a point" (verb, attested 12th–15th c.), and the noun sting "a sharp pointed organ or weapon; the act or effect of stinging." 5) Modern English sting (verb and noun) "to pierce with a sharp point; to cause sharp pain; the wound or smarting caused by it" — the base on which the adjective is formed. 6) English stingy "mean, niggardly, unwilling to spend or share" (adjective, sting + -y "characterized by"; earliest attested senses "sharp, biting" and "ill-tempered, mean-spirited," c. 1660s–1700s; the financial sense is established by the early 18th c.) — the target root word, STINGY. Note on debate: The etymology of stingy is slightly contested. The traditional and majority view derives it from sting + -y, with the semantic path "sharp, biting" → "ill-tempered" → "mean-spirited in giving." Some dictionaries instead propose a separate derivation from dialectal stinge "a sting, a stingy person" or an independent regional formation; the OED connects it to sting while noting uncertainty about the exact route from "sharp" to "ungenerous." The PIE root is variously reconstructed as *steg-, *steig-, or *steng- (with a nasal); the Germanic line *stinganą → OE stingan → ME stingen → ModE sting is secure. The date of the first financial attestation is approximately 1670s–1700s; sources differ by a decade, so the range is given rather than a single year.
