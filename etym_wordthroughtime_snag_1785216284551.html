- [c. 1570: a northern nautical word] - Borrowed as a timber and seafaring term: a tree-stump or branch sticking up out of the water, a hidden hazard waiting to hole passing boats.
- [17th century: generalised sharp point] - The sense widens from water to land: any sharp, jagged projecting point or obstruction on which things catch, tear, or stumble.
- [1807, American frontier: the steamboat age] - 'To snag' means to run aground on or be holed by a submerged log; snags are the great destroyers of Mississippi river steamboats, and the word enters the national idiom.
- [19th century: the abstract hitch] - 'Hit a snag' passes into everyday use for meeting an unexpected difficulty that halts a plan; 'snagged' describes cloth or stockings torn on a sharp point.
- [1890s, American slang: the catcher caught] - A new verb sense flips the direction: to snag is now to seize, grab, or obtain something quickly — snag a bargain, snag a seat — turning the hidden hazard into the quick catch.
- [Contemporary sense] - Keeps both faces: a literal sharp remnant lying in wait to catch passers-by, and the idiomatic unexpected obstacle that derails progress.
