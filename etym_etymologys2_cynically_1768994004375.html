CYNICALLY — linear derivation chain: - Proto-Indo-European *ḱwōn / *ḱun- (oblique stem *ḱun-), "dog" (reconstructed, ca. 4500–3500 BCE) — a secure PIE word with cognates across the family: Sanskrit śván, Greek kyōn, Latin canis, Germanic *hundaz (ancestor of hound), Lithuanian šuõ, Old Irish cú, Armenian šun. - Greek kyōn (κύων), genitive kynós, "dog" (attested Homeric, 8th c. BCE) — the Hellenic reflex of *ḱwōn. - Greek kynikós (κυνικός), "dog-like, canine; churlish; Cynic" (4th c. BCE) — the adjective of the school of Antisthenes and Diogenes, so called either from the gymnasium of Kynosarges ("White Dog") where they met or, by popular etymology, from their "dog-like" contempt for convention and shamelessness. The semantic leap from "dog" to "philosopher of contempt for convention" happens here. - Latin cynicus, "a Cynic philosopher; dog-like" (borrowed from Greek, 1st c. BCE) — the word passes from Greek to Latin with the philosophical meaning attached. - English cynic, noun "one who believes human conduct is motivated wholly by self-interest" (16th c., 1540s) — at first still the Cynic philosopher, later generalised to the distrustful type of man. - English cynical, adjective "disbelieving in human sincerity; contemptuously distrustful" (17th c., c. 1580–1600), formed as cynic + -al. - English cynically, adverb "in a cynical manner", formed as cynical + -ly, where -ly continues Old English -līce (17th c.) — the final node, CYNICALLY.
