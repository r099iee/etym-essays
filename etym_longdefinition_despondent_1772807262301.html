When Ying-lian realizes that her long-awaited release is being delayed yet again, she begins to look worried and despondent. In this literary moment the adjective does not name a brief sulk or a passing disappointment; it marks a heavier psychological collapse in which hope for a better future seems to have been quietly surrendered. The word captures the weary, inward sag that follows repeated trauma: after years of suffering, the fragile happiness she had begun to trust now feels illusory, and the spirit starts to give way under the weight of renewed frustration. The tone is quiet rather than theatrical — crushing, resigned, almost hollow — signalling that her courage and expectation have both begun to drain away. The core meaning of despondent is 'in low spirits from loss of hope or courage'; the person has given up expecting a good outcome and has stopped fighting. The word belongs to a small family: despond (verb, now rare or formal), despondency and despondence (nouns), despondently (adverb), and the participial desponding found in older literature. Every member of the family carries the same semantic core — hope, not merely good humour, has been given away. The usage is broader than a single pattern, and the gradation is what makes the word precise:
  • Person sense: 'she grew despondent after her third job application was rejected' — a person whose hope has drained away and who has stopped expecting success.
  • Mood / expression sense: 'a despondent expression', 'a despondent tone', 'a despondent look' — applied to the outward signs of the inward collapse, not only to the person.
  • Situational / topical sense: 'despondent about the future', 'despondent at the news' — about a prospect or event that triggers the loss of hope.
  • Graded intensity: despondent sits between everyday discouraged and absolute hopeless; it is less clinical than depressed and less theatrical than in despair. One can be sad and full of hope, but one cannot be despondent and hopeful at the same time.
  • Historical register: in 18th- and 19th-century 'melancholy' psychology, despondency named a recognised spiritual and emotional condition — the quiet, inward collapse of spirit after repeated disappointment, distinct from the dramatic agonies of despair because it was resigned rather than raging.
Etymologically the word is a portrait of hope as a possession that can be pledged away. The Latin verb despondere meant to promise something away — to give up a claim, to betroth a daughter, to resign a right — and a striking figurative use, animam despondere, 'to promise away one's spirit,' crystallised the meaning 'to lose heart.' The English adjective despondent (late 17th century) preserves exactly that image: despair as the act of handing over one's hope. That is why the word is so apt in the Red Chamber sentence: Ying-lian's despondency is not a passing low mood but the quiet surrender of expectation after repeated betrayal, the moment when the spirit is pledged away. Register is standard literary and educated prose — common in novels, biography, and careful journalism when a dignified word for hope-starved low spirits is wanted — rather than slang or clinical jargon. Collocations: grow/feel/become despondent, despondent about/at, a despondent look/mood/tone, in a despondent state, despondent spirits. The nuance that separates despondent from mere sadness is precisely its focus on hope and courage rather than on feeling low: the word describes a person who has stopped believing that things can improve, and it is that surrendered expectation — not the depth of the emotion — that defines it.
