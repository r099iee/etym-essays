twings is a rare, dialectal plural meaning thin flexible twigs or shoots — in the source sentence, the material of a sweeping broom. John Ruskin in Praeterita writes that his stern grandfather sent the servant out forthwith to buy an entire bundle of new broom twings to whip her with: that is, a whole bundle of fresh birch or heather sprays of the kind bound into a besom (a traditional twig broom), bought new so as to sting. The word is effectively twigs in a northern or older spelling with an intrusive nasal — twing for twig — preserved in Ruskin's family anecdote and in a handful of 19th-century texts. Modern dictionaries do not list it as a headword; readers should understand it as broom-twigs.

In ordinary language the object is utterly familiar: the besom, the yard broom, the witch's broomstick of illustration, all consist of a handle (the broom-stick) with a head of bound twings — slender, springy, slightly leafy switches that flick dust and leaves without breaking. Fresh ones are supple and cruel as a whip; old ones grow brittle and shed. Gardeners still buy birch brooms and heather besoms for sweeping leaves from lawns, and thatchers use similar faggots; every such broom is a bundle of twings by another name. The source phrase new broom twings therefore specifies youth and flexibility: not worn-out sweepings but cut-green switches fit both to sweep a floor and, in the grandfather's discipline, to sting a culprit.

  • Core material sense: broom-twigs, besom switches: slender cut shoots bound as a broom head; e.g. a bundle of broom twings, new broom twings, birch twings. Countable, almost always plural, almost always with broom, birch, or besom. This is the source sense and the only one with textual support.
  • Extended botanical sense: any thin shoot or withe: by analogy a twing can be read as any pliant twig, osier, or wicker shoot used for binding, basketry, or kindling; e.g. hazel twings for tying faggots. The bridge is form and function: thinness plus bendability. This use is conjectural and dialectal, inferred from the twig kinship rather than separately attested.
  • Homograph warning — verb twing (to pluck, tweak, throb): English also has a verb twinge / twing meaning to pinch, pull sharply, or throb with pain (a twinging tooth, conscience twinges), from a different Germanic base. It must not be confused with the noun twing = twig, though Ruskin's scene puns on both at the reader's expense: the twings are precisely what will twinge.
  • Register and rarity: twings is rustic, northern, and archaic — the kind of word a Cumbrian servant or a Scottish nurse would use while a London ironmonger would write twigs on the bill. Its appearance in Praeterita is a piece of reported speech fossilised in print: the grandfather or the servant said twings, and Ruskin faithfully kept the oddity. Learners should recognise it passively and say twigs actively.
  • Collocations and grammar: plural twings with collective nouns — bundle, bunch, faggot, besom of twings; adjectives of freshness — new, green, supple, birchen. No singular idioms survive; no figurative extensions (twings of hope) are recorded. The phrase to whip with twings belongs to historical corporal punishment, like to birch, to switch, to rod.
  • What it is not: it is not twine (string), not twins (double children), not twinges (pains), despite the one-letter differences that make it a favourite trap in transcription. Nor is it a distinct botanical species: broom twings are not a plant called twing but twigs destined for a broom.

Because the word is so thinly attested, the honest lexicographic treatment is brief on authority and full on context: twings = twigs (especially broom-twigs), a dialectal spelling kept alive by one famous family story. Its value for the Anki learner is not frequency but texture — the springy, green, whistling bundle in the servant's hand as he hurries back forthwith, and the whole vanished world of besoms, birchings, and Victorian patriarchs that comes with it.
