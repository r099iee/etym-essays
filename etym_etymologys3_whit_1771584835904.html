The root: a noun of being The biography of whit begins with a Proto-Indo-European noun, *wekti-, meaning "thing, creature, being", a formation whose wider family is debated: some scholars connect it with the root *weg-, "to be vigorous, awake", which also gives Latin vigil, "awake" — the creature conceived as "the living, stirring thing". Whatever its ultimate links, in the Germanic branch it settled into Proto-Germanic *wihtiz, "thing, creature, being", and from that single noun every major Germanic language built some of its most basic words. The extraordinary fate of "thing" The word's extraordinary fate is that the ordinary noun "thing" became, in several languages, the very word for "not" and "nothing". Old English combined nā ("no") with wiht ("thing") to form nāwiht, "nothing", which passed through Middle English nought and not into modern English not — the universal negator — beside the numerals naught and nought. High German did exactly the same: niowiht ("not a thing") contracted to niht and then nicht, "not", while the plain noun Wicht ("creature, small wretch") survived separately. Dutch built niet ("not") from the same construction, and the Dutch noun wicht still means "child". Old Norse vættr, "creature", survives in Icelandic as a name for a "wight" or supernatural being — the word that modern fantasy literature has revived as wight. What began as a colourless noun of being thus became, independently and identically, in four Germanic languages, the machinery of negation itself. Sound and form
  • Old English wiht (also spelled wyht) meant "a creature, a being, a person, a thing" — one of the most common words of the language, used in the poetic epithets of warriors and monsters alike.
  • In Middle English the form wight became the familiar word for "a person", so that Chaucer can call a knight "a worthy wight".
  • Beside that full form there grew a weakened, reduced form whit (with loss of the medial vowel), used for "a very small thing, the least particle". It is this reduced form that English has kept, frozen almost entirely inside the idiom not a whit.
Semantic drift: from creature to nothing The semantic drift is elegant: "thing" → "little thing" → "least particle" → "a quantity of nothing". To say "I care not a whit" is to say "I do not care a thing" — and then "not even the smallest thing". The grammatical consequence of this drift was severe: a noun that had once named every creature of the earth was now so thoroughly locked into negative contexts that it could no longer stand alone. You cannot say "a whit of bread"; the word survives only where it is negated. Because the full noun wiht was replaced during the late Middle Ages by thing (from Old Norse þing, "assembly, matter"), whit lost its ordinary employment as a free-standing noun and survived only where it had fused with negation — hence not a whit, no whit, never a whit. Cultural and literary life The word's history explains its curious narrowness in modern usage and its enduring literary power. In the couplet Hawkes translates for Bao-yu — "Yet not a whit cared he how folk might jeer!" — the phrase retains all of its ancient force: the boy's indifference is not partial but total, because his care is literally "not a thing" — zero. A root that once named every creature of the earth has been whittled down to the measure of absolute indifference, and yet the little word still carries, in its very etymology, the whole history of "thing" turning into "nothing". It is one of the quietest words in English, and one of the deepest.
