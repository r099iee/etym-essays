- [Antiquity–c. 1300] - Ancient Greek *asphódelos* and Medieval Latin *affodillus* refer to the white-flowered asphodel of Mediterranean folklore and Elysian Underworld mythology - [connotation: sacred botanical medicine, ghostly remembrance, and immortal rest]
- [c. 1400–1500] - Middle English borrows Old French *affodille* as *affodill*, applied loosely to both asphodel and yellow wild narcissus plants - [connotation: medicinal herbal remedy and emerging spring flora]
- [1500–1650] - Early Modern English adds the playful prosthetic initial *d-* (from French *d'affodille* or Dutch *de affodil*), creating *daffodill* and *daffadowndilly*; Shakespeare immortalizes them as taking the winds of March with beauty - [connotation: joyous spring awakening, poetic pastoral charm, and lively rustic music]
- [1800–1900] - William Wordsworth's celebrated poem *I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud* elevates the golden daffodil to the supreme icon of Romantic nature worship, memory, and inward joy; Oscar Wilde features it in fairy tales - [connotation: transcendental floral bliss, meadow radiance, and nostalgic beauty]
- [20th c.–Present] - Established as the proud national flower of Wales and the global charitable emblem of hope on Daffodil Day for cancer research, alongside ubiquitous garden admiration - [connotation: resilience, spring renewal, national pride, and hopeful solidarity]
