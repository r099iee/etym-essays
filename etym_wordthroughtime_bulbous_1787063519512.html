- late 1500s–1600s: Borrowed from Late Latin *bulbosus* directly into botanical and medical texts to classify plants growing from true bulbs (such as onions, lilies, and tulips).
- 1700s–1800s: Expanded into anatomical, zoological, and architectural descriptions to denote naturally rounded or swelling structures (such as bulbous roots, bulbous domes, or bulbous arteries).
- late 1800s–1900s: Gained strong literary connotations in horror, gothic fiction, and fantasy (notably Lovecraft and Tolkien) to evoke grotesque, protruding, unblinking eyes or misshapen anatomy.
- 1950s–Present: Established in naval architecture (the *bulbous bow*) while remaining the definitive adjective for swollen noses, protuberant ocular organs, and organic rounded industrial designs.
