In the passage from which this card is drawn — "The cast of an eye, the excess of an appetite, the perversity of tastes, the beauty or deformity of stature—he witnessed all these with delight" — the word "stature" denotes the physical height and bodily build of the human beings Gibbon observed. It closes a catalogue of human particulars: the cast of an eye, the excess of an appetite, the perversity of tastes, and finally the beauty or deformity of stature. Its position in the list is telling, for "stature" is the last and most bodily item in a sequence that runs from the glance of an eye to the standing frame of a whole person. Gibbon is presented as an insatiable observer of the body as well as of the mind — one who takes in the entire physical presence of another human being, from the flicker of a look to the vertical measure of the skeleton. The tone is deliberately neutral and objective: "stature" here is a measurable feature, capable of being judged "beautiful" or "deformed," but it is recorded with the dispassionate curiosity of a naturalist rather than the censure of a moralist. The delight is that of a connoisseur of human variety who finds the whole spectrum of bodily form endlessly interesting. In its primary and oldest sense, "stature" is a purely physical word: it names a person's natural height, the vertical extent of the body when standing upright, together with the general build and bodily proportion that accompany that height. A person is said to be "of tall stature," "of medium stature," "of imposing stature"; one can speak of "stature" in adults, in children, even of trees or statues, always with the underlying idea of how high a thing stands and how it is proportioned. This physical sense is the root from which every later meaning grows, and it is worth stressing how precise it is: "stature" is not merely "height as a number" but height as it is embodied — the way a frame carries itself, the impression of bigness or smallness that a person projects. It is, etymologically, the quality of "standing," a word built on the Latin verb stare, "to stand," so that at bottom stature means "the manner in which a thing stands" — its standing-up-ness, its vertical presence. The great metaphorical shift, recorded from the seventeenth century onward, carries this physical image upward into the world of reputation and worth. A person of "stature" in this transferred sense is a person of importance, weight, and standing in the estimation of others — someone who "stands tall" in the world's judgment. One speaks of "a man of great stature in the scientific community," of "moral stature," "political stature," "international stature," "literary stature." The semantic bridge is the body: just as a tall person physically rises above the crowd and commands the field of vision, so a person of great reputation rises above the ordinary run of humanity and commands attention. The historical bridge is thus an image — height as eminence, standing as status. The physical and the moral senses are not merely related but continuous: a person of great stature in reputation is imagined as standing tall in a human landscape, looked up to in the most literal way. This dual life is the defining feature of the word in modern English, and it is the reason "stature" can be applied equally to an athlete's six-foot-five frame and to a leader's historical importance. Beyond these two great senses, "stature" has accumulated a web of secondary and technical uses that repay attention:
  • Measured height in formal registers: in medicine, physical education, and the military, "stature" is the standard term for a person's standing height taken as a measurement, e.g. "stature is recorded at the initial examination," "below-average stature for age," "stature percentile." Here the word is a synonym of "height" but carries a slightly clinical or documentary tone.
  • Animal and plant stature: zoology and botany use "stature" for the height or general size of a fully grown organism — "a bird of imposing stature," "trees of considerable stature" — always retaining the idea of standing upright.
  • Figurative stature of institutions and works: the word extends from persons to institutions, nations, movements, and achievements — "the stature of the Roman Empire," "a novel of international stature," "the growing stature of the university" — where it means importance, scale, or eminence without any literal body at all.
  • Stature as personal presence or gravity: in semi-figurative use the word shades into "dignity" or "weight of character" — "he carried himself with a certain stature" — describing not mere height but an impression of gravity and self-possession projected by the body.
  • Formal idioms: "to grow in stature" (to become more important or more respected), "to add to one's stature," "to diminish someone's stature," "of equal stature" (of equal importance or height), all of which trade on the literal/metaphorical ambiguity of the word.
The near-synonyms of "stature" map its boundaries precisely. "Height" is the pure measurement — a single number, entirely neutral, without any sense of proportion, presence, or reputation; one may have a height but not a "height in the community." "Build" refers to a person's muscular and skeletal shape — sturdiness, slenderness, compactness — and says nothing about how tall the person stands. "Size" is the broadest and crudest word, covering any dimension of any object, and loses the specifically human, upright meaning. "Rank," "standing," and "status" carry the metaphorical sense of "stature" but have no physical meaning at all — they are pure social measurements. "Eminence" and "prestige" denote high reputation but lack the bodily image of standing tall. "Gravity" and "weight" capture the sense of dignity and seriousness but not the vertical image. The genius of "stature" is that it alone holds both registers together — the tape-measure and the reputation — and the whole history of the word is the history of that union: a physical word that learned to describe moral and intellectual elevation, so that when we say a person "has stature," we mean they stand tall in the only way that finally matters.
