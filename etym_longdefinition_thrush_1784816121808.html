THRUSH — noun. Core meaning: a medium-sized songbird of the family Turdidae, typically brownish above with a spotted or speckled breast, famous for its clear, musical song and, in the case of the song thrush, for the habit of smashing snail shells on a chosen stone "anvil." The word also names, in an entirely different and unrelated sense, a fungal infection of the mouth and throat (candidiasis).

In the famous moon-letter prophecy of The Hobbit — "Stand by the grey stone when the thrush knocks" — the word names the song thrush (Turdus philomelos), here cast as a herald of hidden knowledge. The reference is exquisitely precise: the song thrush has the celebrated habit of selecting a stone as an anvil, then smashing snail-shells upon it to reach the flesh, producing a clear, repeated knocking. It is this small, ordinary, yet distinctive behaviour that Tolkien turns into the key to the secret door of Erebor: when the thrush knocks at the grey stone on Durin's Day, the last light of the setting sun will reveal the keyhole. The nuance of the word here is therefore double — on the surface a plain bird-name of the English countryside, beneath it an omen-bearing messenger whose natural action unlocks a dwarven mystery. The tone is mythic yet homespun: the great and the small meet, and an unremarkable bird carries the weight of an ancient prophecy.

Full polysemy.

  • The song thrush (primary ornithological sense): Turdus philomelos, the brown, softly spotted thrush of gardens and woodland, famed for the snail-anvil habit and for its loud, repetitive, flute-like song delivered from an exposed perch.
  • The family sense: thrushes collectively, family Turdidae, including the blackbird (Turdus merula), mistle thrush (Turdus viscivorus), fieldfare, redwing, and the American robin.
  • The unrelated medical homograph (candidiasis): a fungal infection caused by Candida albicans, most often appearing as white patches in the mouth or throat (oral thrush) or in the nappy area of infants. This word descends from a Scandinavian source (Old Norse / Danish troske, throske), not from the bird-name, and is a classic example of a false friend in the same language.
  • Poetic and dialectal names: throstle (native English doublet) and mavis (borrowed from French mauvis) are not different birds but older, poetic or dialectal names for the very same song thrush.
  • The proverbial / folk-lore thrush: in old bird-lore the thrush's song marks the return of spring and liveliness of the countryside; the mistle thrush is the "stormcock," singing even in rough weather.
  • The Tolkienian / mythic thrush: in The Hobbit the Thrush of Erebor is a particular, wise bird that understands the Dwarves' language; its knocking at the grey stone fulfils the prophecy. The word thus carries a literary-mythic charge unique to its use in Middle-earth.
  • Ornithological jargon: "thrush anvil" names the stone on which a song thrush breaks shells — a recognised term in ethology and birdwatching.

Semantic bridges. The core bird-sense is ancient, descending from a Proto-Indo-European bird-name spread across nearly every branch of the family. The medical homograph is entirely separate in origin, from a Scandinavian word; the two have simply converged on the same sound, so that a speaker of English today must disambiguate by context. The poetic names throstle and mavis sit beside thrush as synonyms of different register, and the word's proverbial and literary associations (the stormcock, the dawn chorus, the snail-anvil) have given it a rich figurative afterlife, culminating in Tolkien's transformation of a garden bird into the hinge of a prophecy.

Register and usage notes.

  • Plain bird-name: standard, unsentimental, familiar in every register from field guide to nursery rhyme.
  • Literary/mythic: in Tolkien and in Romantic and Victorian nature poetry the thrush carries emblematic weight.
  • Medical: oral thrush, thrush infection — a strictly separate sense, often confusing to learners.
  • Dialectal/poetic: throstle, mavis — elevated, archaic, or regional synonyms.
