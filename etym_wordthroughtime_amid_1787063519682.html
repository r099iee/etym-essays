- Old English - The phrase on middan (“in the middle”) begins fusing; the adjective midd supplies the core meaning of being spatially centred.
- 12th-13th c. - Amidde appears as a fused preposition meaning “in the middle of, among”; alongside it amiddes / amidst grows the excrescent -st that still flavours the longer form.
- 14th-16th c. - Amid becomes standard in literary prose and verse for spatial enclosure and membership of a crowd; both amid and amidst are equally respectable.
- 17th-19th c. - The word extends from space to time and circumstance: things happen “amid the battle”, “amid the merriment”; grammarians begin to note a stylistic gap between amid and amidst.
- 20th-21st c. - Amid becomes the journalist’s default: “amid reports of”, “amid growing concern”, “amid the chaos” — the shortest preposition for placing an event inside an atmosphere; amidst drifts toward poetry and archaism.
