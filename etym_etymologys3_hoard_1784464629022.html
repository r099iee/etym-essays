1. PIE *ḱeudh- and the Germanic hoard

The story of hoard begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ḱeudh-, "to hide, conceal, cover." The root's reflexes across the family cluster around hiding and covering: Greek keuthein, "to hide" (Homer's keuthmós, "a hiding-place"), and the Germanic forms. Under Germanic sound changes, *ḱeudh- produced Proto-Germanic *huzdą — and the striking fact is that Germanic did not merely keep the verb's sense of concealment: it specialized the nominal form to mean "hidden treasure," the valuables a person or dragon has hidden away. Gothic huzd, Old High German hort, Old Norse hodd, and Old English hord all mean "treasure, hoard, secret store." The Germanic word is native treasure-vocabulary, built directly on the idea of hiding.

2. Old English hord: the Anglo-Saxon gold-hoard and its world

In Old English, hord is a central word of the heroic world. Beowulf, the greatest of Old English poems, is built around hoards: the dragon guards a goldhord ("gold-hoard") in its barrow, the treasure of a vanished people, and the whole poem turns on the ethics of hoarded gold — the lord who gives treasure binds his retainers, while the dragon that keeps it brings ruin. The hord was, in Anglo-Saxon society, the very stuff of kingship: a king was a ring-giver who distributed the hoard in gifts, and the hoard that was not shared was a sign of greed and doom.

  • hord, noun: treasure, a hidden store, a hoard — the guarded wealth of king, lord, or dragon.
  • Word-formation: hord built a rich family — goldhord, maþmhord, hordcofa ("treasure-chamber"), hordwyrd ("guardian of a hoard").
  • The moral weight: shared gold binds society; hoarded gold corrupts and destroys. This ancient ethic is the seed of the modern pejorative verb to hoard.

3. Middle and early modern English: from hord to hoard

Middle English continued the word as hord, hoord, hoard, with the same concrete sense of hidden treasure. By early modern English the spelling had settled as hoard, and the noun's "hidden store" sense broadened from gold to any carefully kept accumulation — money, goods, food. The verb to hoard, "to amass and keep in secrecy or greed," is attested by the sixteenth century and carries, from the start, moral disapproval: to hoard is to keep beyond need and refuse to share. The noun and the verb are the same word seen from two ethics — the thrift of kings versus the greed of misers and dragons.

4. The double life of hoard in the modern world

The modern word keeps both faces of its history alive at once:

  • The epic face: dragon-hoards, gleaming golden treasure, the guarded wealth of legend and fantasy — a direct continuation of the Old English hord.
  • The clinical face: hoarding disorder (DSM-5), the compulsive acquisition of possessions and failure to discard them; data hoarding; the wartime economics of hoarding and profiteering — the old keep-and-don't-share now pathologized.
  • Archaeology: a hoard is a standard term for a buried deposit of valuables — coin hoards, gold hoards, votive hoards — one of the richest categories of archaeological discovery.
  • Ecology: animal hoarding (scatter-hoarding by squirrels and jays, larder-hoarding by hamsters and nuthatches) uses the same word for the storage of food.
  • Technology and economics: data hoarding, hoarding cash, hoarding supplies — the word moves easily from gold to bytes and food.

The historical bridge is the root itself: *ḱeudh- "to hide." Whether the hidden thing is a dragon's gold, a squirrel's nuts, a coin-hoard in a field, or a hard drive of files, hoard always names what is hidden, guarded, and kept beyond immediate use.

5. The horde confusion

Hoard must not be confused with horde, "a swarming crowd or multitude." Horde entered English from Turkish (ordu, "camp, army") through Eastern European languages (Polish horda, Russian ordá) in the seventeenth century, via the historical Golden Horde and Tatar hordes. It is a completely different word with a completely different origin — no connection to the Germanic *huzdą — and the near-identical spelling is a coincidence of history. Careful usage keeps the treasure and the crowd apart.

6. In the dwarves' song

In Tolkien's stanza — "There many a gleaming golden hoard / They shaped and wrought" — the hoard is exactly the Germanic treasure-complex the word has always named: worked gold, hidden and guarded, the moral fuel of the dragon-quest that follows. "They shaped and wrought" insists that the hoard is made, not merely found — dwarven craft turns raw gold into gleaming, gem-set wealth — and the word's ancient meaning of hidden treasure foreshadows the whole plot of guarded gold beneath the Mountain. The card form is the singular noun of the song formula, but its weight is the whole history of Anglo-Saxon and Norse treasure.
