The biography of arrear begins with a Roman sense of direction. Latin ad meant to or toward, and retro meant backward or behind — the word soldiers heard when the line stepped back and sailors when the oars reversed. Spoken Latin fused the pair into *ad retro, to the rear, a phrase for anything trailing, lagging, or coming late. It was a small honest spatial picture: the thing that has fallen behind the main body, whether a straggler on the march or a payment lagging after its due date.

Old French inherited the fusion as arere and ariere, still meaning behind or backward in the eleventh and twelfth centuries — the ancestor both of English arrear and of rear itself, the back of the army. Feudal accountancy gave the word its second life: money that stayed arere, behind its appointed day, was money in arrear, and the clerks of manors and Exchequers began writing arrere in their rolls around 1300 for rents, fines, and taxes outstanding. Chaucer’s England already knew the phrase, and the law took it up with enthusiasm, because a legal system built on quarterly payments and annual reckonings needed a name for the gap between what was due and what had arrived.

The road from backward to bankrupt:

  • Rome — direction: retro orders the backward step; lateness in space is the whole meaning, with no money in sight.
  • Gaul — fusion: Vulgar Latin *ad retro compresses toward plus backward into a single adverb of trailing behind, later worn down to French arere.
  • Norman England — accountancy: clerks apply behind to cash, and in arrear becomes the set formula of the tally stick, the manor roll, and the statute book.
  • Early modern law — hardening: leases, bonds, and Blackstone fix arrears of rent as the technical term, and the plural begins its long takeover of everyday speech.
  • Cobbett’s age — politics: radicals like Cobbett seize the ledger word as a moral weapon, and a large arrear to the public turns a collector’s balance into an accusation of plunder.
  • Today — the bank letter: in arrears survives in mortgage statements, tenancy agreements, and court orders, the Roman backward step still marching through every dunning letter.

Phonetically the word’s journey is a story of wearing down: the crisp two-part Latin ad retro rubbed itself smooth through centuries of quick speech into French arere, and English, borrowing it with the Norman clerks, levelled the vowels to the flat modern arrear, rhyming with rear, its twin born of the same root. Semantically the drift ran the other way, from thin to thick: a word that once meant merely behind accumulated the whole machinery of debt — reminders, interest, distraint, eviction — until to be in arrears means not just to be late but to be on the books, in the files, and one step from the court. That is why Cobbett’s sentence still stings two centuries on: an arrear to the public is lateness that the whole nation is waiting for, and patience, even the Exchequer’s, has its limits.
