- Latin dēsōlātiō (1st c. BCE – 4th c. CE): "a leaving alone, abandonment, laying waste" — a factual administrative and rhetorical word for empty, forsaken places. Connotation: neutral, bureaucratic.
- Vulgate Bible (4th c. CE): dēsōlātiō becomes the prophetic word for cities God has abandoned to ruin — Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel ("abominatio desolationis"). Connotation: apocalyptic dread.
- Middle English desolacioun (c. 1300, via Old French desolacion): "devastation, ruin, barrenness" of lands and kingdoms; Chaucer and Wycliffe use it of biblical ruin. Connotation: calamity, divine punishment.
- 16th–17th c.: the King James Bible (1611) gives "the abomination of desolation" (Matthew 24:15); at the same time the inner sense appears — "desolation of spirit", grief so complete the soul feels ruined and empty. Connotation: cosmic ruin and private despair together.
- 18th–19th c.: Romanticism makes desolation sublime — ruined abbeys, empty moors, Arctic wastes (explorers, Shelley, Byron); the word gains aesthetic grandeur. Connotation: awe tinged with sorrow.
- Modern: two living senses — physical wasteland (war reporting, Tolkien's "Desolation of Smaug") and psychological desolation (crushing loneliness in literature and clinical prose). Connotation: dramatic, media-ready, still biblically resonant.
