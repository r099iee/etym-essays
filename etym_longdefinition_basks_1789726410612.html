BASKS is the third-person singular present of bask, meaning to lie, sit, or rest exposed to pleasant warmth, especially sunshine, and by extension to rest in any benevolent glow. Seals bask on rocks, cats bask on windowsills, lizards bask on walls, tourists bask on beaches: the subject is typically a body relaxed, still, and open to heat, absorbing it with pleasure rather than labour. The verb profiles duration and passivity — basking takes time, requires no effort, and suggests contentment. Intransitive syntax dominates, usually with in or on: bask in the sun, bask on warm stone. The register is warm, idle, and benign, the opposite of toil.

In the immediate context, the form is relative-clausal and landscape-scale: murmuring streams that ______ in its waning summer gold. Here it is not an animal but running water and, by metonymy, the whole little-field country that basks — lying exposed to low, golden, late-summer light. The streams do not flow coldly; they loiter, glitter, and warm themselves in the season's last heat. The image consoles an exile: this basking England will still be there when you return, because basking implies permanence, peace, and being cherished. Beauty itself becomes the sunshine in which the land basks, and the reader basks in the sentence.

  • Primary sense: to expose oneself to warm sunshine or heat for pleasure. To bask in the sun, to bask on the sand, basking sharks cruising warm surface waters. The body orients to maximise warmth, often sprawling. Semantic bridge to the stream sentence is personification plus light-as-warmth: water flashing in low sun looks exactly like a body stretched in heat, glitter standing for bliss.
  • Secondary sense: to take pleasure in approval, favour, or reflected glory (bask in admiration, bask in success). Athletes bask in applause, authors bask in reviews, courtiers bask in royal favour. Bridge: mapping of physical warmth onto social warmth — praise is felt as heat on skin. The verb keeps passivity and duration: one does not seize applause, one lies in it. This explains why landscapes can bask in gold: nature receives light as a creature receives praise.
  • Tertiary and technical senses: wallowing, soaking, and zoological basking. The oldest English sense (late 14th c. basken) was to wallow, especially in warm water or blood — wounded knights basking in gore, deer basking in pools. Modern zoology retains basking for ectotherms thermoregulating (snakes, turtles, crocodiles basking) and ornithology for birds sunning to maintain plumage. Bridge: from wallowing in liquid to soaking in light, preserving immersion and saturation while changing medium from water/blood to sunshine. Shakespeare's 1600 sunshine use in As You Like It fixed the warm-light sense permanently.
  • Metaphoric landscape sense: places basking in weather, seasons, and history. Valleys bask in afternoon light, villages bask in prosperity, memories bask in nostalgia. The subject need not be animate; any hollow that holds warmth qualifies. Bridge: container metaphor — a valley is a basin that holds sun as a bath holds water, uniting the wallow and sunshine senses. The present streams basking in waning gold are textbook: water, light, hollow fields, and golden hour converge.

Grammar and collocation repay attention. BASKS as third-person singular suits habitual, timeless truths (the stream basks, England basks) rather than single events, reinforcing the promise it will still be there. Typical adverbials are luxuriously, lazily, contentedly; typical subjects are animals, bodies, crowds, and landscapes. Negative polarity is rare — things seldom bask in cold or gloom unless ironic. The -s ending also signals the relative pronoun agreement (streams that basks → streams that bask in standard grammar, but the card form preserves basks as cited), reminding learners that bask is regular: bask, basks, basked, basking.

For retention, contrast BASKS with bathes (active washing), sunbathes (deliberate tanning), wallows (muddy, debased immersion), and revels (active celebration). Bask alone combines animal stillness, solar pleasure, and innocent self-exposure. In this nostalgic wartime or exile sentence, to bask is to be worthy of lingering: the land is beautiful because it can lie still and be warmed, and the reader is invited to lie with it.
