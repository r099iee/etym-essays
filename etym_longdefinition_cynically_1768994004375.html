cynically — in a distrustful way that believes people are motivated purely by self-interest. In the sentence "'But I doubt if we'll need them — as long as we don't meet rival gold-hunters from the Earth,' he added cynically", the adverb performs the scene's moral work. The party has been arming itself against Martian monsters — the "them" of the sentence — and Bellman undercuts that whole fear with a sour, knowing amendment: the real danger, he implies, is not the alien but the competitor, not the desert but the greed of fellow men. "Cynically" tells us exactly how the line is delivered: with a wry, disillusioned smile, a shrug, a tone that assumes the worst of everyone, including, implicitly, himself. The tone is bitter-sardonic rather than openly hostile; it is the weariness of a man who has seen too much of human nature to believe in its altruism. The adverb thus does more than describe a manner of speaking; it characterises a worldview and colours the reader's entire judgement of Bellman as shrewd, guarded, and faintly jaded.

The core sense is precise: to act or speak cynically is to interpret noble or benevolent conduct as a mask for selfish calculation. Unlike scepticism (which doubts the truth of a claim and demands evidence) or pessimism (which expects bad outcomes), cynicism expects bad motives — the settled assumption that virtue is a performance and altruism a myth. The word's force is diagnostic: it strips away the surface and reads the self-interest underneath.

  • Adverbial sense (this card): describing the manner of an action or utterance — "he added cynically", "she cynically dismissed the selfless act as a PR stunt" — connoting wryness, disillusionment, and moral weariness.
  • Philosophical sense (historical origin): the Cynics (Greek kynikoí, "dog-like ones"), followers of Antisthenes and Diogenes in 4th-c. BCE Athens, who flouted convention, wealth, and rank. In this strict sense, "cynically" describes behaviour in the spirit of that school — austere, shamelessly blunt, contemptuous of luxury and pretence. The modern sense grew out of, and generalised, this original school-of-philosophy meaning.
  • Journalistic / political jargon: in modern media and politics, "cynically" judges an action as staged for appearance or advantage — "cynically exploiting the crisis", "a cynically calculated move" — a technical-sounding marker of manipulation.
  • Adjective family: the adverb is the -ly form of cynical (cynic + -al); the noun cynic names the person; cynicism the doctrine or attitude. The derivative chain shows the word hardening from a strange ancient philosophy into the everyday, weary suspicion of motive.

Register and boundary: "cynically" is standard, slightly formal, and carries moral judgement. Its near-synonyms divide the semantic field finely — sceptically (doubts truth, demands evidence), sardonically (adds mocking, bitter humour), pessimistically (expects bad outcomes), suspiciously (targeted distrust of a specific person), jaundicedly (habitual, bitter discoloration of judgement), disbelievingly (refusal to credit) — and each differs from "cynically" in whether the doubt concerns truth, outcome, or motive. Only "cynically" names the doctrine that all human conduct is self-interested, which is why it is the precise word for Bellman's wry warning about rival gold-hunters.
