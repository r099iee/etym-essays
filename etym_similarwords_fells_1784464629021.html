• Moor: Similar because both name open, uncultivated upland; differs because a moor stresses wet heath and bog, while a fell is the high barren hill or rocky height of northern diction (the two can overlap).
  • Mountain: Similar because both are high ground; differs because a mountain is the general high peak mass, while fells are lower, broader uplands and ridges.
  • Hill: Similar because both are raised land; differs because hill is lower and broader in ordinary use, without the fell's specific bleak, northern barrenness and treelessness.
  • Ridge: Similar because both are elevated land; differs because a ridge is a linear crest, while fells name whole expanses of high barren country.
  • Peak / Summit: Similar because both belong to high ground; differs because they name the topmost point of a height, not the fell itself.
  • Upland: Similar because both denote elevated terrain; differs because upland is a broad, neutral geographical term, while fell is specific to northern British bare heights.
  • Tundra: Similar because both can be treeless, exposed, cold-region terrain; differs because tundra is a climatic zone of the far north, not a British topographic term.
  • Down / Wold: Similar because both are English terms for open high ground; differs because downs and wolds are southern, rolling, and often grassed — softer and greener than the rocky northern fells.
  • Crag: Similar because both belong to rocky heights; differs because a crag is a steep rocky outcrop or cliff face, one feature of a fell rather than the whole upland.
  • Fell-field: Similar because both are high barren terrain; differs because fell-field is a technical term for frost-shattered rocky upland, while fells is the common plural for the hills themselves.
