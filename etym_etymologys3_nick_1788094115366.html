Introduction and the problem of origin. The English word nick is one of those short, stubbornly opaque monosyllables whose history resists tidy reconstruction. The OED classes its origin as "uncertain", and competing theories divide scholars. The dominant hypothesis connects it with the French word niche "a recess, a hollow", which descends through Vulgar Latin from Latin nīdus "a nest" — itself from the PIE compound *nisdós "a down-sitting place" (*ni- "down" + *sed- "to sit"), the same root that gives English nest, sit, and sedentary. On this view, a "niche" — a small recess or hollow — developed the sense of a small cut or notch when the word was borrowed into English in the 15th century: a notch in a surface is, visually, a small hollow scooped out of it. The alternative theory, preferred by some historical dictionaries, derives nick from Middle Dutch nik or Middle Low German nicke "notch, groove, crack", a word of obscure further origin; this would make nick a North Sea Germanic loanword rather than a Romance one. A third, folk-etymological strand attaches the word to the personal name Nicholas (as in Old Nick, the Devil), but the evidence points the other way: the name application came later and was itself drawn to the existing word. The honest lexicographic verdict is that nick "notch" emerges in Middle English around 1480 with the sense "a small cut or slit", that its Romance connection (via niche) is probable but unproven, and that its Germanic connection remains a live alternative. This is a case where the record forces us to state uncertainty rather than fabricate a chain.

Fifteenth to sixteenth centuries: the physical core. What is certain is the semantic core: from its first appearances, nick denotes a small cut, notch, or slit — the kind of mark made by a blade catching the edge of a surface. The word thrived in practical contexts: carpenters nicked timber to mark measurements, cutlers complained of nicks in blades, and tally-keepers recorded debts and the passage of time by cutting nicks into a stick. This tallying practice is almost certainly the cradle of the word's most famous idiom. A tally stick was a notched wooden record; the "nick" was the exact notch marking a particular sum or moment. From "the exact notch on the tally" to "the exact moment" is a short and natural step, and by the 1580s English had in the nick, "at the exact moment"; the fuller phrase in the nick of time is securely attested by the early 17th century and flourished in Elizabethan and Jacobean drama. The idiom's persistence across four centuries — from Elizabethan drama to Tolkien's Eagles arriving "down the gale in the nick of time" — testifies to the force of the underlying image: time, like a tally stick, has one precise notch at which action must fall.

Seventeenth century: the devil, the cheat, and the cut. The seventeenth century added two striking developments. First, the noun nick attached itself to the Devil: Old Nick, attested from the mid-17th century, is a euphemistic personification that plays on the personal name Nicholas while capitalizing on the word's existing overtones of cutting and sharp practice. The association of the Devil with thieving and sharp dealing made the fit natural; the phrase survives today as a mild, almost affectionate archaism. Second, the verb to nick broadened from "to make a nick in" to "to cut off, to snatch, to cheat": a sharper who "nicked" a coin or a purse was cutting it away, and by extension tricking its owner. This criminal coloration, already present in the seventeenth century, flowered fully in the nineteenth.

Nineteenth to twentieth centuries: the underworld and the idiom. In the nineteenth century British thieves' cant made nick a verb of theft — "to steal" — and by the early twentieth century the noun the nick had come to mean the police station or prison, so that "to nick" also acquired "to arrest". The semantic bridge is transparent: the criminal who nicks (cuts away, snatches) gets nicked (caught, arrested) and ends up in the nick (the lock-up). Meanwhile the respectable senses kept pace: cricket adopted nick for the fine deflection of the ball off the edge of the bat (the ball "nicking" the bat's edge), and British and Australian English developed in good nick, "in good condition", where the image is of a well-trimmed, well-kept state — a thing whose surface has been neatly cut and maintained. The twentieth century also cemented the nick of time as a fully idiomatic phrase of narration, used by writers from Tolkien to journalists whenever a rescue, a save, or a decision arrives at the last possible instant.

The homophone shadow: nickname and Nicholas. No account of nick is complete without its great phonetic accident. English nickname has nothing etymologically to do with cutting: it is a rebracketing of an eke-name, "an additional name" (eke from Old English ēac "also, in addition", cognate with eke out and Latin augere "to increase"). The sequence "an eke-name" was misheard and redistributed as "a neke-name" and then "a nick-name" by the fifteenth century, so that nick in nickname is pure sound-change, not meaning. The same accidental convergence attaches to the personal name Nick (Nicholas), which has colored the word's perception — from Old Nick to the jolly Santa Nick — without contributing to its etymology. Today nick lives on several parallel tracks: the everyday physical cut, the fixed idiom of timing, the British slang of theft and arrest, the sporting edge, and the invisible presence inside nickname — a small word with a genuinely uncertain but fascinating past.
