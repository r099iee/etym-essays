The name Saint Giles’s braids two long histories — a saint and a slum — into one possessive. The saint comes first. Giles (Latin Aegidius, French Gilles) was, by the medieval legend, an Athenian hermit who settled in the woods near Nîmes around 670, living on the milk of a tame hind. When the Visigothic king Wamba hunted that hind, his arrow missed the beast and pierced the hermit’s knee, crippling him for life; the king founded the abbey of Saint-Gilles-du-Gard in atonement, and Giles became, fittingly, the patron of cripples, beggars, and all the disabled poor. His name is traditionally read as a diminutive of Greek aix, a goat — the kid among the goats of Provence — though scholars admit the name could be native Gallo-Roman rather than Greek at all.

The cult swept medieval Europe: hundreds of churches, hospitals, and leper-houses took his dedication, because a patron of outcasts was exactly the guardian the outcast sick required. Outside London’s wall, beside the road to Oxford, such a hospital rose around 1117, and its chapel of St Giles-in-the-Fields seeded the parish. For centuries the fields kept their promise — the church stood among gardens and lazar-grounds — until seventeenth-century London burst its walls. Speculative builders threw up courts and alleys for the labouring poor, the Seven Dials pillar (1694) staked a bankrupt development, and by Queen Anne’s reign the parish was England’s most crowded acre: forty thousand souls, gin-shops on every corner, and hiding-places no constable would enter.

The eighteenth century made the name immortal and infamous. Hogarth’s Gin Lane (1751) — the starved mother, the hanged man, the pawnbroker thriving — was drawn from its streets and sold in support of the Gin Act; John Gay’s Trivia warned walkers against its alleys; Fielding’s magistrate writings and Colquhoun’s police surveys cited it as the head-quarters of mendicity. St Giles’s passed into the language as a common noun of degradation, and Cobbett, riding past Middlesex hovels in the 1820s, reached for it as the lowest mark on his moral scale: his villagers were about half there — dirty, ginny, and sliding.

  • The hermit’s legend: Athens to Provence, the hind, the arrow, the abbey — hagiography that made Giles the beggars’ patron across Europe.
  • The name’s cradle: Greek aix to Latin Aegidius to French Gilles to English Giles, with the goat-etymology traditional but debated.
  • Hospital to parish: the 12th-c. lazar-house outside the walls grows a chapel, a parish, and then a city quarter.
  • Rookery prime: speculation, overcrowding, and gin forge the Seven Dials maze that terrified and fascinated Georgian London.
  • Print immortality: Hogarth, Gay, Fielding, Dickens, and Mayhew fix St Giles’s as the national emblem of slum horror.
  • Clearance: New Oxford Street (1847), workhouses, and model dwellings erase the courts; the 20th c. keeps the name for offices and a circus.

Cobbett’s Saint with its capital and his Giles’s with its apostrophe preserve the form exactly as a church dedication mutates into social criticism: the grammar of the prayer-book (the parish of Saint Giles) compressed into the slang of disgust. Few English place-names carry so complete a moral arc — from a crippled hermit’s charity to a byword for degradation to a West End address — and none lets a reader watch, in two words, holiness curdle into horror and then evaporate into real estate.
