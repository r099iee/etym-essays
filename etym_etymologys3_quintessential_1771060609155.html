The history of quintessential is one of the strangest journeys in English etymology: a word born in ancient cosmology, carried through medieval alchemy, and finally settled into everyday praise for perfect examples. Its roots are two of the most fundamental in all Indo-European speech — the numeral "five" and the verb "to be" — and its compound meaning was created by philosophers trying to explain the heavens. The numeral root *pénkʷe "five" is one of the most secure reconstructions in the field, surviving in Sanskrit páñca, Greek pénte, Germanic five, and Latin quīnque; the verb root *h₁es- "to be" underlies the very word is. For millennia these two roots led separate lives. Their union was achieved in the Latin translation workshop of the Roman philosophical tradition, when Latin essentia was coined from esse "to be" to render the Greek philosophical term ousía, "being, substance, essence."

The decisive step came with Greek natural philosophy. The pre-Socratics and Aristotle had reasoned that the changing terrestrial world is composed of four elements — earth, water, air, and fire — which mix, separate, and corrupt. But the heavens, with their eternal circular motion and unchanging light, could not be made of such perishable stuff. Aristotle therefore posited a fifth, superior substance, the aither, the body of the stars, eternally uniform and incapable of change. Later Greek commentators called it the pémptē ousía, the "fifth substance" or "fifth being." When medieval scholars translated these ideas into Latin, they rendered the phrase as quinta essentia — literally "the fifth essence" — and the compound was born in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. In the cosmology of the age, the quintessence was the rarest and most perfect substance in the universe, the very stuff of which the changeless heavens were made.

It was the alchemists who transformed the phrase from cosmic physics into laboratory practice. For alchemy, the quintessence came to mean the purest extract that could be drawn out of any substance — the concentrated vital principle of a body, obtained by distillation and refinement, freed from all impurity. This is the sense in which quintessence entered Middle French in the thirteenth century and English by about 1400: Chaucer's prose and the alchemical texts of the fifteenth century used the word for the fifth element and for the pure extracted principle of things. At this stage the word carried an unmistakable flavour of the esoteric and the marvellous — the quintessence was something rare, hidden, and almost magical, obtainable only by those who understood the secrets of nature.

The extension from substance to symbol occurred in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. If a thing's quintessence was its purest, most concentrated core, then by metaphor the "quintessence of a quality" was its most perfect embodiment — the "quintessence of wit," of elegance, of villainy. Shakespeare's Falstaff, in Henry IV, calls drink "a good familiar creature" and, in a famous line, praises sherry as "the quintessence of ... valour." The adjective quintessential was formed from the noun in the early seventeenth century, giving English a word that could name the perfect specimen of anything. The semantic bridge is the idea of distillation and purity: what is quintessential has been reduced to its purest form, exactly as the alchemist reduced base matter to its quintessence.

In modern English the word has become a staple of description, criticism, and travel writing: the quintessential English village, the quintessential thriller, the quintessential New York bagel. The register has relaxed from the mystical to the admiring — but the ancient payload remains. To call something quintessential is to claim that it stands to its class as the fifth essence stood to the four corruptible elements: the eternal, pure, and perfect member of the group, the one that defines the standard for all the others. The word preserves, in miniature, the whole medieval cosmos — four mutable elements on earth, and a fifth, changeless essence above them. When a reviewer calls a film "the quintessential spy movie," she is, without knowing it, echoing Aristotle's astronomy and the alchemist's still, asserting that this particular specimen is the incorruptible, distilled embodiment of its kind. Few adjectives carry so much intellectual history in so small a compass, and fewer still have moved so effortlessly from the heavens to the everyday.
