The biography of keener begins, surprisingly, not with sharpness but with knowing. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵneh₃-, 'to know, to be able', one of the most widespread verbal roots of the family. In its nasal-present form *ǵn̥-néh₃-ti, 'he comes to know', it produced Sanskrit jā́nāti (जानाति), whose descendants jānnā (जानना) in Hindi and jāṇibā (ଜାଣିବା) in Odia still mean 'to know' today; its Iranian reflex survives in Avestan zainti- 'knowledge', and an Anatolian cognate, Hittite ganešš- 'to recognize', testifies to how early the root was fixed in the family's vocabulary. Beside this present stands the inchoative form *ǵn̥h₃-sḱé-, 'to come to know, to perceive', which lies behind Greek gignṓskein (γιγνώσκειν), whence the learned words gnosis, diagnosis, and prognosis, and Latin (g)nōscere, the source of cognition, recognize, cognizance, and ignorant, and of the whole Romance family of connaître, conoscere, and conocer. A further nominal branch, *ǵn̥h₃-ti- 'knowing, knowledge', gave Sanskrit jñāna- (ज्ञान) and the Slavic verb *znati, whence Russian znat', Polish znać, and Czech znát, with Baltic siblings žinoti (Lithuanian) and zināt (Latvian). Across the whole family the root's core meaning is remarkably stable: to know is to have grasped, to be familiar with, to be able.

In the Germanic branch the root took a decisive turn toward 'ability' and 'boldness'. Its zero-grade yielded the adjective *kunniz or *kōniz, 'knowing, able, capable', a formation that already leaned from knowledge toward competence — the same semantic slide that produced English can and cunning (Old English cunnan 'to know how'). Old English inherited this as cēne, 'bold, brave, fierce', a word of the heroic code: it appears in Old English poetry to praise warriors, kings, and champions, where a man's courage and his skill together made him cēne. The cognates confirm the earlier meaning: Old Norse kœnn means 'wise, skillful, learned', Old High German kuoni and Old Saxon kōni mean 'bold', and German kühn today still means 'bold, daring'. The English word alone travelled furthest from the root's 'knowing' sense, and it is precisely that Germanic inheritance — knowing → able → bold → fierce — that feeds the modern keen.

The great semantic revolution came in Middle English, when cēne / kēne moved from moral courage to physical and sensory sharpness. The bridge is easy to see: a bold warrior is one who cuts through, and the adjective migrated from the warrior to his blade, so that by the 13th century a knife or sword could be kene. From the blade the word spread to everything that cuts or pierces — a keen wind, keen cold, keen pain — and from piercing objects to piercing senses, so that eyes, ears, and noses became keen; by the time of Chaucer, a kene edge could be balanced against a kene glance. The final step carried sharpness into appetite: a sharp desire is a keen interest, a sharp hunger a keen appetite, and by the early modern period keen had settled into its two modern poles — acute perception on one side, eager desire on the other.

Key stages and attestations:

  • Old English (pre-1000) — cēne 'bold, brave, fierce', a heroic epithet in Anglo-Saxon poetry; cognates show the original 'knowing, able' meaning preserved in Norse.
  • Middle English (c. 1200-1400) — kēne gains 'sharp, cutting' of blades, winds, and pains, then 'sharp-eyed, sharp-eared', then 'eager, zealous'; Chaucer uses the word of both edges and appetites.
  • Early Modern English (16th-17th c.) — the two poles of modern sense are fixed; Shakespeare's keen ranges from keen weapons to keen sorrow and keen hunger.
  • 18th-19th c. — keen enters the language of commerce and competition ('keen prices', 'keen buyers') and of natural history (the keen senses of animals).
  • 20th c. — the noun keener emerges, especially in Canadian and school slang, for an over-eager person; the comparative stays rare and therefore strong, almost technical when grading an animal faculty.

Socio-culturally, the word's register has drifted from heroic to homely. What began as a warrior's epithet became, by the Victorian age, the language of the naturalist and the sportsman, and in modern speech keen survives most vividly in eagerness ('keen to go', 'a keen gardener') and in the folk-tale register of sensory danger that Tolkien exploits. The comparative keener, by keeping the sensory meaning alive, sounds almost archaic in everyday talk — which is precisely what makes it perfect for the storybook menace of the Wargs' sense of smell. The chain from PIE 'to know' to a wolf's nose is long, but every step — knowing, able, bold, sharp, acute, eager — is attested and each is the natural outgrowth of the last.
