whirligigs (plural noun; RP /ˈwɜː.lɪ.ɡɪɡz/) — toys or devices that spin, whirl, or revolve: a whipping top, a pinwheel, a merry-go-round, or any gyrating contrivance; by extension, anything that moves in a circle or is giddily inconstant. The word is a compound of whirl ("to spin rapidly round") and gig (an old word for a whipping top), and in this text it appears inside the mock-exclamation "Whistles and whirligigs!" 1. The word in this literary context. The line is spoken by the Dwarf Trumpkin in C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian: "Whistles and whirligigs! ... You don't mean you want to give the country to Humans?" Trumpkin is thunderstruck, and he reaches for the most absurd, domestic, spinning objects he can name to voice his outrage. The word is a token of an old-fashioned, low-grade oath — the flustered substitute for profanity that a respectable speaker produces when genuinely shaken (Gadzooks!, Zounds!, By golly!). Whirligigs are things that go round meaninglessly; to invoke them mid-exclamation is to say, half-fondly, "this idea is giddily, spin-headed nonsense." The tone is comic, exasperated, and utterly un-menacing — a dwarf's swearword made of toys. The plural with its three giddy syllables (whir-li-gigs) literally spins in the mouth, enacting the mental whirling it names. 2. Polysemy — the many things a whirligig can be. The Oxford English Dictionary records a rich spread of senses across five centuries:
  • A spinning toy (primary sense). The whipping top, the wind-spun pinwheel, and any hand- or wind-driven gyrating toy; the word's central home since the 15th century. In this sense it is a synonym of spinning top, teetotum, and windmill toy.
  • A spinning water-beetle. The whirligig beetle (Gyrinus), which gyrates rapidly on the surface of ponds — the word's most surprising specialist application, recorded from the 17th century.
  • A fairground or garden spinner. A merry-go-round, a roundabout, an ornamental wind-spinner for the garden — anything that revolves on a fixed course.
  • A fickle or inconstant person. By metaphorical extension, a person who "goes round" without fixed principle — the sense Shakespeare plays on when he writes "the whirligig of time brings in his revenges" (Twelfth Night, Act 5, Scene 1), making the spinning toy the emblem of fortune's giddy reversals.
  • A whirling contrivance of any kind. In dialect and mechanical English, any device that whirs or revolves, from a spindle to a fan.
3. Synonym boundaries. A whirligig differs from a pinwheel (a flat paper wheel spun on a stick by the wind) and from a spinning top or teetotum (a balanced toy spun on its point) in being a broad generic term for anything that whirls round. A merry-go-round or carousel is a fairground machine on a fixed circular course, not a free-spinning handheld toy; a British roundabout is a road junction, by figurative extension a whirl of traffic. The modern fidget spinner is its lineal descendant in the hand — a handheld spinning gadget — but without the archaic-whimsical register. 4. Register and the semantic bridge. The word has always been colloquial and slightly silly — the "-gig" suffix keeps a buzzing, giggling, jigging sound. That very silliness is what makes it a perfect oath-word for a comic dwarf: like all mild curses ("By gum!", "Dash it!"), it substitutes an absurd domestic object for the profanity the speaker is too respectable to use. The bridge from toy to metaphor runs through the idea of pointless, giddy rotation: a thing that whirls is a thing that never settles, so the whirligig naturally became the figure for fickleness, caprice, and the reversals of fortune — a figure alive from Shakespeare to the modern idiom of "the whirl of events."
