- 1570s–1650s — Theological battleground & the Ubiquitarian controversy: entered Western intellectual discourse as a Latin/Neo-Latin theological technicality (ubiquitas) during intense Reformation disputes over the Eucharistic presence of Christ's physical body everywhere (the Lutheran doctrine of ubiquity vs. Calvinist critiques).
- 17th c. — Scholastic philosophy & natural law: the noun ubiquity crossed into English philosophy and legal theory to describe the hypothetical capacity of monarchical power (the king's legal ubiquity in court) or natural forces (such as light and gravity) to be effective everywhere simultaneously.
- Early 19th c. — Coining & literary adoption of the adjective: the anglicized adjective ubiquitous emerged in British literary, satirical, and journalistic prose (c. 1800–1830), rapidly secularized as a witty, hyperbolic description of omnipresent busybodies, fashionable socialites, or standard commodities.
- Late 19th–20th c. — Industrialization, mass media & urban consumerism: shifted from polite satire to standard descriptive prose characterizing mass-produced industrial goods, telegraph networks, corporate logos, and consumer products that saturated modern civilization.
- 1988–Present — Digital technology & computing revolution: gained profound technological codification with Mark Weiser’s coinage of 'ubiquitous computing' (ubicomp), describing the seamless integration of microprocessors, sensors, and Internet connectivity into every facet of the physical environment.
