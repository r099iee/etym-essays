Chain for TWANGED (imitative / onomatopoeic coinage — NO PIE ancestry):
1. Onomatopoeic / sound-symbolic coinage in early modern English (c. 1530–1550) — the word is formed to imitate the sharp, ringing, nasal vibration of a plucked string; OED labels it “imitative.” No Proto-Indo-European or Proto-Germanic ancestor is attested, and no genuine cognates exist in Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Slavic, or Celtic.
2. English noun twang (attested c. 1550) — “a sharp, ringing, vibrating sound,” the bare echoic name of the noise itself, /twæŋ/.
3. English verb to twang (attested 1553) — “to make or emit such a sound,” a denominal formation from the noun by direct conversion.
4. English past tense and past participle twanged (16th c.) — /twæŋd/, the regular weak past of the verb; a single acoustic event locked in the past ── OUR WORD.
Note on nuance: the imitative analysis is not seriously disputed, but scholars note that the coinage was likely influenced by an older English echoic cluster (tang, ting, ding, clang, jangle); the word shares its formation-pattern with those forms even though it is not historically derived from any of them.
