murk — The biography of this word is a biography of dying light. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *mer-, whose oldest recorded sense seems to be "to flicker, to glimmer" — a root that could go either way: toward the sparkle (Greek amaryssein, "to sparkle, to twinkle") or toward the extinguishing (Greek marainein, "to wither, to quench," whence marasmos, the wasting away that gives English the medical word marasmus). Light that flickers is light that is dying, and the ancient mind ran the two together: the root also became the Indo-European word for death itself. In the Italic branch Latin morior, "to die," with its noun mors, mortis, "death," produced mortal, immortal, mortuary, and — through the Old French "dead pledge" — mortgage; in the Germanic branch the same root, extended with a suffix, produced Proto-Germanic *murthraz, "violent death," Old English morþor, and modern English murder. In Indo-Iranian it gave Sanskrit mṛ-, "to die," with mṛtyu, "death," which survives in Hindi as maut and marna and in Odia as maribā. The darkness branch of the family, the one that concerns us, was built by adding a *-k- suffix to the same root: PIE *merkw-, "to become dark," gave Proto-Germanic *merkwaz, "dark, gloomy." Its daughters ring the Germanic world: Old English mirce, "dark, murky," which survived through Middle English mirke and myrke into the dialectal adjective mirk (which Tolkien revived in the name Mirkwood); and Old Norse myrkr, "darkness, gloom," which lies behind the Scandinavian words for dark (Danish mørk, Swedish mörk) and which was borrowed into English in the sixteenth century to supply the noun murk, "darkness." The older English adjective and the Norse noun then merged and cross-fed, producing the modern adjective murky (fourteenth century) and murkiness. The semantic logic of the whole family is a single ancient equation: to the early Indo-Europeans, a flickering fire that fails is a fire that dies, and the darkening sky was the daily death of the day — so that murk, murder, mortal, and maut are all, at bottom, the same root looking at the same thing from different angles. The word's literary career tracks the history of gloom in English letters. For centuries the adjective was the everyday word for a dark, cloudy day, while the noun remained a northern and Scottish word; it entered standard English through the sixteenth-century borrowings and took firm hold with the Romantics and the Gothic revival, whose writers wanted a darkness thick enough to have substance — the murk of dungeons, of haunted moors, of the pre-dawn world of the sublime. In the twentieth century Tolkien's Mirkwood restored the older spelling mirk to public view, and modern fantasy and horror prose continue to use murk for the darkness of enchanted and accursed places. Meanwhile the street and gaming slang "murked" (killed, utterly defeated) has given the ancient death-root a modern, violent second life. That ancient fear is exactly what the present passage redeploys on a cosmic scale: the speaker sees a black curtain rising "high into the murk," encircling the land — darkness not as an absence but as an advancing, swelling substance, the dying of the light become a curtain and the curtain become a world. Key developments:
  • Sound and form: PIE *merkw- → Proto-Germanic *merkwaz → Old English mirce → Middle English mirke/myrke; the noun was reinforced by Old Norse myrkr; the modern spelling murk (with u) reflects the dialectal and Norse pronunciation.
  • Semantic drift: "flickering/dying light" → "dark, gloomy" → "dense darkness, gloom" → (noun) "thick obscuring darkness"; then figuratively "moral or emotional gloom, obscurity."
  • Double source: the modern noun blends the native Old English adjective line with the Old Norse noun line — a genuine historical nuance, flagged by most authorities.
  • The death connection: the same PIE root *mer- produced murk, murder, mortal, marasmus, and Hindi maut/maut, so the darkness of murk and the darkness of death are etymological kin.
  • Literary attestations: from the Exeter Book through Gothic and Romantic poetry to Tolkien's Mirkwood and modern fantasy, murk is the word for darkness that has become a substance.
