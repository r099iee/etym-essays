Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500 BCE)
*wed- / *wod- / *ud-
"water, wet"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*undā
"wave, surging water"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
unda
"wave, billow, surge of water"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
undāre
"to rise in waves, surge, billow"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
abundāre (ab- "from, away" + undāre)
"to overflow banks, surge forth, be copious"
↓
Late Latin / Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–8th c. CE)
abundāre / habundāre
"to overflow, be wealthy, teem"
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
abonder / habonder
"to overflow, be plentiful, teem"
↓
Middle English (c. 1300)
abounden / habounden
"to overflow, be plentiful, exist copiously"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.)
abound
"to teem, exist in great profusion"
↓
Modern English (present)
abound
"to exist in great numbers;
to be richly supplied or teeming"

Note: In Late Latin and Old French, the spelling was frequently written with an unetymological initial 'h-' ('habundāre', 'habonder'), reflecting a false folk-etymological association with Latin 'habēre' ('to have, possess'). English scholars during the Renaissance restored the classical Latin etymological spelling 'abound' (and 'abundance'), reflecting its genuine origin from 'ab-' + 'unda' ('wave').
