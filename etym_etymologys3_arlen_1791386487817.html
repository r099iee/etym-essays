Arlen is unusual among the words in this collection because its most famous bearer invented it. The name is not the product of centuries of sound change but of a single decision made in London around 1919. Its history therefore combines ancient ethnonym, modern onomastics and the sociology of immigration.

From Armina to Armenia. The country name entered the written record in the sixth century BCE as Old Persian Armina, in the trilingual inscription of Darius I at Behistun. Greek writers rendered it Ἀρμενία, and Latin took it as Armenia. The further etymology is disputed: some scholars connect it with the Urartian kingdom of the Armenian plateau, others with an older Anatolian or Indo-Iranian formation, and no reconstruction commands consensus. What matters for Arlen is that the country name was well established in English by the fourteenth century and carried powerful associations of antiquity, Christianity and diaspora.

The Armenian background. Dikran Sarkis Kouyoumdjian was born in Ruse, Bulgaria, in 1895, the youngest of five children of an Armenian merchant family that had fled Ottoman persecutions. The family moved to Southport, Lancashire, in 1901. He was educated at Malvern College and briefly at Edinburgh University before leaving for London in 1913 to make his living as a writer. His birth surname, Kouyoumdjian, was a constant social obstacle: it was long, unfamiliar and difficult for English readers. In wartime Britain, as a Bulgarian national of Armenian origin, he was also politically suspect and could not easily naturalise.

The coinage. Arlen's own accounts are consistent in outline. He wanted a name that was easy to say and looked English, but not ordinary. According to The New Yorker's 1949 profile, he took "the first two syllables of Armenia, his parents' country, using an 'l' instead of an 'm'". Other accounts say he checked the London telephone directories to make sure no other Arlens existed, and that he paid five guineas to adopt the name officially by deed poll. The result, Arlen, is phonologically plausible in English, faintly Celtic in flavour, and semantically opaque — which may have been part of its appeal.

Literary career and the making of a name. Arlen's first book, The London Venture (1920), was a collection of semi-autobiographical sketches; its preface explained the name change. These Charming People (1923) and The Green Hat (1924) made him a celebrity. The Green Hat sold enormously, was dramatised, and was filmed several times; its heroine, Iris March, became a type of the modern, independent, doomed society woman. Arlen's prose — vivid, colloquial, fond of unusual inversions and a "heightened exotic pitch" — was labelled Arlenesque, a rare case of a surname generating a critical adjective.

Social and cultural context. The 1920s were a period of intense self-fashioning in Britain and America. Advertising, cinema, mass-circulation newspapers and the cult of celebrity made names into brands. Arlen's change of name fitted this world perfectly: it was a piece of personal branding before the term existed. At the same time, his success exposed the fragility of the immigrant's position. He became a naturalised Briton in 1922, married into the aristocracy (Atalanta Mercati, daughter of a Greek count), and moved between London, Cannes and Hollywood. The name opened doors that Kouyoumdjian could not.

Later history and reputation. Arlen wrote prolifically in the 1920s and 1930s, then turned increasingly to political journalism and spent time in Hollywood. He served in civil defence during the Second World War and was in Coventry during the Blitz. He died in 1956. His novels went out of fashion, and for decades "Michael Arlen" was remembered mainly as a period curiosity. Recent biographies and Armenian diaspora studies have begun to restore him, emphasising his role as a mediator between Armenian experience and English popular culture.

Other Arlens. The surname also has independent life. Irish genealogists derive some Arlen forms from Gaelic Ó hArlain or Ó hAirleáin, and English habitational names such as Harland ("hare land") may lie behind others. The American composer Harold Arlen (born Hyman Arluck) made the surname famous in music, but his name has a different origin. Arlen also became a twentieth-century given name, especially in the United States. These homonyms mean that the word's modern reference is ambiguous: context must tell us whether we mean the novelist, the composer, the Irish clan or the given name.

Semantic legacy. The most durable linguistic legacy of Michael Arlen is the adjective Arlenesque, used for a style that is at once sophisticated, sentimental and slightly artificial. The surname itself has become a small emblem of the Jazz Age and of the immigrant writer who reinvented himself. Its history shows that names are not only inherited but can be designed — and that a designed name can acquire a cultural life of its own.
