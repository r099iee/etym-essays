Cavalry is, in its founding sense, the arm of an army that fights on horseback: troopers mounted, sabred, and drilled to the charge — the hussars, dragoons, lancers, and cuirassiers whose thundering lines decided battles from Blenheim to Balaclava. In the source sentence Cobbett pairs it with its volunteer cousin, the yeomanry cavalry raised to keep down the Jacobins and Levellers: county horsemen officered by squires, the landed interest mounted and armed against revolution at home. Whether regular or volunteer, the word carries the same picture — speed, shock, and hooves — and the same political charge in Cobbett’s mouth: force that moves fast and strikes from above.

Core meaning in detail: Classical cavalry fought in two tempers: the heavy shock of armoured horse, breastplate to breastplate, meant to shatter infantry by weight; and the light screen of hussars and lancers, meant to scout, pursue, raid, and harry. Gunpowder, barbed wire, and the machine gun killed the horse in the role between 1914 and 1942, but the word refused to dismount: armies transferred it to tanks (armoured cavalry), to trucks (motorised cavalry), and to helicopters (air cavalry, air assault), because what the cavalryman truly sold was never the horse but mobility — arriving fast, hitting hard, and vanishing before the enemy could set himself.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Horse soldiers as a military arm (primary historical sense, from the 16th c.): heavy cavalry, light cavalry, a regiment of cavalry. The bridge is the root itself: Italian cavallo, horse, plus the collective -eria — horsemen as a body, the mounted counterpart of infantry.
  • Yeomanry and volunteer horse (Cobbett’s sense, 1794–1908): yeomanry cavalry, the county volunteers. The bridge is imitation: amateur horsemen drilled in the regulars’ image, mounted on hunters instead of chargers, to do at home what the heavies did abroad — overawe.
  • Armoured and mechanised cavalry (modern military, from the 1930s): cavalry regiments in tanks and reconnaissance vehicles. The bridge is function preserved through form destroyed: when the horse died, the speed, shock, and scouting lived on in steel, and the old titles — the Queen’s Own Hussars, the Sherwood Rangers — rode armour.
  • Air cavalry — helicopters (from the 1960s): the US 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile) in Vietnam, bugles and Stetsons over the jungle. The bridge carries mobility skyward: the helicopter is the horse that flies, and the cavalry’s trumpet still sounds before the rotors.
  • The rescue metaphor (figurative, 20th c.): send in the cavalry, the cavalry’s arrived — any deliverance arriving in the nick of time. The bridge is Hollywood: a century of Westerns taught every audience that hoofbeats on the soundtrack mean salvation, and the metaphor rode out of the cinema into boardrooms, hospitals, and IT departments.
  • Chivalry’s twin (literary fringe): cavalry and chivalry share the horse-root, and poets sometimes lean on the kinship. The bridge is etymological romance: the same caballus that bore troopers into battle bore knights into courtesy.

Register and connotation: cavalry is dashing, romantic, faintly antiquarian — sabres, bugles, and thundering hooves — yet it never retired. Generals still name their swiftest formations for it, journalists still cry send for the cavalry when a crisis needs rescuing, and Cobbett’s fury proves its political temperature two centuries cold: mounted men mean power that can reach you. To invoke the cavalry is to promise speed and decision; to denounce it, as Cobbett does, is to accuse the powerful of keeping a fast horse saddled against the poor.
