- Late 14th c. — French loan and classical wonder: borrowed from Old French admirer, meaning primarily to be amazed, to marvel at, or to stand astonished before an unprecedented spectacle.
- 15th – 16th c. — Theological and philosophical adoption: the third-person form admireth becomes standard in English devotional prose and translations of Latin church fathers, denoting contemplation of divine wonders.
- Late 16th – Early 17th c. — Semantic dualism in Elizabethan prose: Hooker, Shakespeare, and Bacon use admireth both for sheer astonishment (‘marvels at’) and the emerging modern sense of high moral regard (‘esteems and honors’).
- Mid-to-late 17th c. — Phonetic and dialectal displacement: the northern inflectional ending -s (admires) conquers spoken English and secular literature, leaving admireth as a specialized liturgical, scriptural, and formal rhetorical marker.
- 18th c. — Johnsonian citation and conscious archaism: Samuel Johnson cites Hooker's admireth in The Rambler to evoke the moral gravity and balanced rhythm of Renaissance prose.
- 19th c. – Present — Literary and historical fossil: preserved in historical anthologies, biblical texts, and deliberate stylistic pastiches, serving as an emblem of Early Modern English grandeur and solemn philosophical cadence.
