- c. 1300 - bisegen appears in Middle English chronicles: strictly military — castles and towns invested by armies; perception: grim, factual, martial.
- 14th–15th c. - the participle besieged is standard in the chronicles, and homilies extend it to the soul besieged by devils; the word begins its double life, literal and moral.
- 16th–17th c. - the figurative leap: people besieged with requests, suits and affections; meanwhile siege warfare dominates European wars, keeping the literal sense headline-fresh.
- 18th–19th c. - 'besieged with petitions' becomes a stock phrase of public life; the literal sense continues through the great sieges of the era; perception: formal, dramatic.
- 20th–21st c. - journalism makes the figurative sense dominant (a besieged government, besieged by reporters), while literal sieges (Leningrad, Sarajevo) keep the word in the news; perception: inescapable pressure from all sides.
