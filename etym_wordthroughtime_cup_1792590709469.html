- [Late Old English (c. 10th c.)] - entered the language as cuppe, borrowed from Late Latin cuppa, primarily denoting a small wooden, ceramic, or metal vessel for drinking at banquets or church altars - [perception: regarded as a functional domestic container and solemn sacramental vessel]
- [Middle English (12th–15th c.)] - broadened through chivalric romance and religious literature (the Holy Grail legend and Chaucerian feasts) into a central symbol of aristocratic hospitality, fellowship, and festive revelry - [perception: celebrated as an emblem of communal warmth, courtly luxury, and spiritual mystery]
- [Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)] - immortalized in biblical idiom (King James Bible: "my cup runneth over") while expanding into natural philosophy to describe the woody protective base of the acorn (acorn cup) - [perception: revered as a profound literary metaphor for divine providence, mortal fate, and natural anatomy]
- [18th–19th c. Salon Culture & Athletic Era] - transformed by the global spread of tea and coffee into the quintessential symbol of refined bourgeois domesticity, while establishing itself as the premier trophy of organized international sporting championships ("World Cup", "America's Cup") - [perception: associated with polite social manners on one hand and supreme competitive athletic glory on the other]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] - formalized into standardized culinary recipes (8 oz volumetric measurement), athletic protective gear (groin guards), orthopedic surgical implants (acetabular cup), and everyday colloquial idioms ("not my cup of tea") - [perception: universally integrated into everyday household utility, sports culture, and medical science]
