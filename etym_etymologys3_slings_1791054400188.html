Sling descends from a Germanic verb for twisting and winding, and its story shows how a hand motion names first a weapon, then a support, then a throwing action. The root image is always a strap twisted around something and released with force.

The reconstructed PIE base *(s)lenk- means to twist, turn, or coil, with s-mobile explaining forms with and without initial s. It yields words for nooses, coils, and creeping motion across Indo-European. In Proto-Germanic this becomes *slingwan, to wind or swing in a circle and let fly, preserving the twist-to-throw logic. Old Norse slyngva and slynga, Old English slingan, and Middle High German slingen all attest the verb for hurling stones with a strap. The noun sling follows from the instrument: the strap itself.

  • Germanic verb stage: to wind and hurl. Early slingan already covers throwing with force. The whirling motion distinguishes slinging from simple throwing.
  • Old Norse influence c.1300: Middle English slingen is reshaped by Norse slyngva, standardising the -ing form. Early attestations describe David and Goliath and classical Balearic slingers.
  • Weapon noun 14th c.: a sling as two-corded pouch for stones and lead glands. Shepherds, armies, and hunters use it; Balearic, Rhodian, and English slingers are famed mercenaries. OED records sling for the weapon by 1330.
  • Support extension 16th-18th c.: from weapon strap to any supporting loop: arm sling for a broken limb, rifle sling for carrying, sling for hoisting cargo. The shared feature is a loop bearing weight.
  • Verb generalisation: to sling now means to throw carelessly, to hang, or to serve hastily, as in sling hash or sling mud. The violent throw bleeds into slang.
  • Modern plural slings: museum cases of ancient weapon slings, hospital slings for infants and fractures, climbing slings, and sling chairs. Context disambiguates.

In the source sentence slings close the catalogue of primitive arms, evoking David, Balearic islanders, and shepherd boys whose cheap strap levelled armoured giants. Museums display forked sticks, leather pouches, and piles of smooth sling-stones, and placards explain range over two hundred metres and the distinctive buzzing sound. That heroic underdog memory keeps the weapon sense alive even as hospitals use the same word for gentle support.

Phonetically the development is regular: Germanic slingw with nasal plus voiced velar simplifies to modern /sling/. RP plural /slingz/ voices the sibilant after the voiced velar nasal. Spelling sling is stable since Middle English after variants slynge and slyng. No Latin rival competes, and the twist-root explains cognates like slink and German Schlinge for noose, all involving winding loops.

Culturally the sling moves from battlefield to playground to clinic: Balearic mothers reportedly hung bread as targets so children learned accuracy, shepherds kept wolves at bay, Roman legions feared sling bullets inscribed with taunts, then firearms displaced the weapon and the word softened into care and carrying. Each phase keeps the loop-and-tension schema, whether hurling death or cradling an arm.
