The history of Loti as Pierre Loti is the history of a self-invented authorial brand, not a deep etymological lineage. Born Louis Marie-Julien Viaud in Rochefort (1850), he entered the French navy and in 1872, aged 22, spent two months in Tahiti. There, according to his own account and brother's letters, Tahitians nicknamed him Loti — usually explained as the Tahitian name for rose, itself a loan of English rose, or for a small Indian flower (lotus). He adopted it as pen name for his Tahitian novel, signing Rarahu / Le Mariage de Loti (1880) as Pierre Loti, Pierre allegedly from a boyfriend or Tahitian friend.

Stages:

  • 1872: Nickname in Tahiti: loti as Tahitian common noun (rose/flower, loanword). Viaud called Loti by islanders. Meaning: flower, delicate newcomer. Oral, not yet literary.
  • 1880–1887: Pseudonym adoption: Pierre Loti appears on title pages (Le Mariage de Loti 1880, Aziyadé 1879 anonymously then as Loti). Meaning shifts from nickname to author-function: naval officer who writes exotic idylls. French press fixes pronunciation /lɔ.ti/ and identity.
  • 1887–1914: Fame: Madame Chrysanthème (1887, Japan), Pêcheur d'Islande (1886, Brittany) make Loti a bestseller and academician (1891). Loti now connotes best-selling exotic melancholy, read across Europe; translated as Loti in English, German, etc. with no change of form. Streets named, Pierre Loti Hill in Istanbul from Aziyadé.
  • 1914–1950: Decline: After death (1923), modernists dismiss Loti as sentimental colonial kitsch; Gide, Mauriac cool toward him. Loti survives in school excerpts and travel nostalgia. Meaning: dated exoticist.
  • 1950–present: Reassessment: Postcolonial scholars, Roland Barthes on Aziyadé, and Pacific studies reread Loti as valuable witness (Tahitian language scraps, early photographs) yet textbook orientalist. Simultaneously, Sesotho loti currency (1980) and Indian loti vessel create unrelated homographs requiring disambiguation in dictionaries. Author-Loti remains the literary sense.

Formally there is no PIE root for the author-name; it is a Tahitian loan (loti < English rose / lotus confusion) repurposed as French pseudonym, then borrowed unchanged into English. If one traces the floral source, loti-rose < English rose < Old English rose < Latin rosa (of uncertain Mediterranean origin) and lotus < Latin lotus < Greek lōtos. But these are the etymology of the flower word, not of the author's choice; the authorial act is 1872–1880. This nuance is debated: some biographers derive Loti from Indian loti flower Viaud knew in childhood in Rochefort's conservatory, others from Tahitian; both agree it means a small exotic blossom and was chosen for euphony and strangeness. The debate is noted rather than resolved.

Literary attestations are the chain itself: naval carnets, Le Mariage de Loti (the exact milieu of the source cannibal anecdote), Loti's journals, obituaries, Académie discourse, and modern re-editions. Perception moved from beloved traveller (Belle Époque) to embarrassing colonial (mid-century) to useful-but-problematic archive (today). Knowing this trajectory lets a reader place the source sentence — a chief joking to Loti about roast white man — as typical Loti-effect: half reportage, half self-mythologising exotic frisson.
