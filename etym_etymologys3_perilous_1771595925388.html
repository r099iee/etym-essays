Perilous — the biography of the test. The adjective stands at the end of one of the most expansive roots in all of Indo-European, a root whose other children include fare, ferry, ford, fjord, experience, experiment, expert, pirate, and even the prefix per- of percent and per se. To follow perilous is to watch a verb of movement become a noun of trial and then a noun of danger — a small history of how experience itself came to be understood as risk. The spatial root. Proto-Indo-European *per- meant "to pass through, to lead across, to go forward," a verb of movement and crossing, and from that spatial sense alone came an extraordinary family. Greek peri, "around," gave perimeter, period, and peripatetic; Sanskrit pari, "around," gave a vast prefixal verb-system; Latin per, "through," gave per se, percent, and peregrinus, "one who passes through foreign lands," the ancestor of English pilgrim. In the Germanic branch, *faran, "to go, travel," produced English fare and wayfarer, ferry (the vessel that carries across), ford — literally "a place for crossing" — and fjord, the inlet across which one fares. But the root also developed a second, experiential branch: to "go through" something came to mean to undergo a trial, to put to the test. This is the semantic germ of perilous. The Greek experiment. The Greek branch exploited this extension richly. Peira meant "a trial, an attempt"; peiran meant "to attempt, to assay, to try." From peira came empeiria, "experience" — knowledge won by going through tests — which gave English empiric and empirical, the school of ancient medicine that trusted observation over theory. And from peira came peiratēs, "one who attempts," which took a sinister turn: the "attemptor" became the brigand, the freebooter, the man who tries his hand against travellers — our pirate, whose crime is by etymology nothing but bold attempting. The same root is at work in the pirate and the empiricist, two figures who both "try things out," one with a cutlass and one with a scalpel. The Latin test that became danger. Latin built the noun periculum on the same stem, with the meaning "a trial, a test." Because a trial is dangerous — it can be failed, it can destroy the one who undergoes it — the meaning slid: periculum came to mean "risk, danger" as well as "test." The slide is a textbook case of metonymy, the procedure standing for its outcome, and it was complete by the age of Plautus in the third and second centuries BCE, when periculum is already common in the sense of "danger, peril." Latin also used the stem for experiri, "to try out" (ex-, "out of," plus the root), which generated experience, experiment, and, through peritus, "skilled, expert" — words that all name knowledge won by going through tests. The Romans thus kept both halves of the root alive: the trial that gives knowledge (experience) and the trial that threatens destruction (periculum). Into French and English. From periculum, Latin formed the adjective periculosus, "dangerous, full of peril," and this travelled into Old French as perilleus, perillous — the language of the chansons de geste and the romances, where knights undertake "perilous passages" and "perilous adventures." Middle English borrowed the adjective in the thirteenth century (about 1250), together with the noun peril directly from Old French. The word entered English with the colouring of high romance already attached: the "perilous seas" and the "perilous pass" were the hazards of chivalric story, dangerous in a way that felt like fate rather than mere probability. Middle English also produced the corrupt variant parlous, used from the fourteenth to the sixteenth century and surviving today only as a jocular or dialectal word — "a parlous state of affairs." The moral turn. From the sixteenth century the word began to work metaphorically, applying the gravity of physical danger to moral and intellectual threats: a perilous doctrine, a perilous example, a perilous tendency. This is the sense in which Ruskin uses the word when he writes of the false critics' "perilous tendency as guides of public feeling": he borrows the weight of the romance — the shipwreck, the treacherous pass — and applies it to the corruption of taste, as if bad criticism could shipwreck a nation's judgment. The word's earnest, prophetic tone is part of its machinery: it turns the critic into a guide warning travellers away from a treacherous road. Today. Modern English has largely retired perilous from everyday speech, preferring the plain dangerous and the clinical hazardous and risky. But the word survives — and thrives — in the registers that still want a danger with moral or heroic weight: the sermon, the editorial, the adventure story, the epic translation, and the prose of a critic like Ruskin, who needs a word that makes bad taste sound like a shipwreck. Perilous remains the most literary member of the danger family, and it carries its whole biography inside it: the PIE verb of crossing, the Greek trial, the Latin test that became a threat, and the English adjective that still sounds like the warning of a prophet.
