Jovial is one of the few English words whose modern meaning is the fossilized remnant of an astrological doctrine. Its ancestry is among the oldest and most dignified in Indo-European: a root reconstructed as *dyeu-, meaning 'to shine, be bright', which came to denote the bright sky, the day, and the sky-god himself. From this single root, Indo-European languages built their great sky-father names: Sanskrit dyāus, Greek Zeus (genitive Dios), and Latin Iuppiter, 'Jupiter', a compound literally meaning 'sky-father' (from *dyeu- plus pater, 'father'). The root also underlies the Indo-European word for 'day' and other divine names scattered across the family tree.

In Latin the god's name, with its stem Iov- (as in the genitive Iovis, 'of Jove'), produced the adjective Ioviālis, 'of Jove, pertaining to Jupiter', formed with the common suffix -ālis (the same suffix in naturalis, mortalis). At this stage the word was purely descriptive — things of Jupiter were Ioviālis — and the astronomically important step was that Jupiter was also a planet. In the astrological system inherited from Babylonian and Greek astronomy and systematized by Ptolemy, each planet was assigned a character; Jupiter, the largest of the wanderers and second in brightness only to the Moon, was classed as the 'greater benefic', the most fortunate of the planets, linked in the humoral medicine of Galen to the warm, moist sanguine humor. The person born under a beneficent Jupiter was therefore held to be sanguine by constitution: healthy, prosperous, generous, and naturally of high spirits. This doctrine travelled through the Latin of the Middle Ages into Renaissance Europe, and in the sixteenth century Italian and French formed their adjectives — gioviale, jovial — from Latin Ioviālis, both meaning first 'of Jupiter, born under Jupiter' and then, by astrological logic, 'merry, cheerful'.

English took the word in the 1580s, and the earliest recorded sense is precisely the astrological one: a 'jovial' person was one born under the planet Jupiter, hence disposed to joy. Within a generation the specialized astronomy faded and the general sense flowered: from the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries onward jovial simply meant full of good humor, and Elizabethan and Jacobean writers used it of merry, warm, sociable characters. The sound changes are slight — Latin Iov- /jow-/ softened to the Italian and French /dʒo-/ and then to the English /dʒəʊ-/, with the stress on the first syllable — and the spelling settled early, but the semantics did the real travelling: from god to planet to temperament to temperament-word.

The word's history is thus a small museum of pre-modern cosmology. The four humors, the planetary benefics, and the doctrine of celestial influence — all abandoned sciences — survive inside a single everyday adjective meaning 'cheerful'. When we call a friend jovial we are, unconsciously, describing his horoscope as the Renaissance would have cast it. The divine family around the word also left further traces: French jeudi, 'Thursday', is literally 'Jove's day' (from Iovis diēs); the adjective Jovian was later coined as the strictly astronomical term for the planet, so that modern astronomy's Jovian moons and a friendly uncle's jovial laugh now use two descendants of the same god for two different jobs. Meanwhile the noun joviality and adverb jovially extend the word through the language, and the medieval image persists — the warm, generous, laughter-filled man under the sky-father's shining — now shorn of its stars but still glowing with the 'greater benefic' that gave it life.
