- Classical Latin (furia / furere, c. 1st c. BCE) - Referred to madness, raging frenzy, and the mythological vengeful deities known as the Furies (Furiae).
- Late Latin / Renaissance Latin (infuriare, c. 15th c. CE) - Coined as a prefix compound (in- + furia) meaning to drive into violent fury or frenzy.
- Early Modern English (17th Century, c. 1660) - Adopted into English during the Restoration, initially as a participial adjective ('infuriated') in epic poetry such as Milton's Paradise Lost.
- 18th to 19th Century - Transitioned into a fully conjugated transitive verb ('to infuriate') across political satire, Gothic novels, and historical narratives.
- 20th to 21st Century - Widely utilized in standard English for high-stakes psychological provocation, everyday intense frustration, and dramatic narrative conflicts.
