THE INDO-EUROPEAN ROOT: *SKEUBʰ-

Shove descends from a Proto-Indo-European root *skeubʰ- "to push, thrust, shove", itself an extension (with the -bʰ- suffix) of the root *skeu- "to bend, curve, press". The root appears across the family: Sanskrit kṣubhyati "to shake, be agitated", possibly Greek skýptō "to stoop, bend" (a debated connection), and the Germanic family that concerns us. In Germanic, *skeubʰ- produced Proto-Germanic *skeubaną "to push, shove", a strong verb of class II (ablaut eu/au/u), attested in Gothic skiuban "to push", Old Norse skúfa "to shove, push", Old Saxon skūvan, Old High German scioban (Modern German schieben "to push, shove"), and Dutch schuiven "to shove, slide". The family is coherent and well attested; the Germanic stage is not in doubt.

OLD ENGLISH SCŪFAN: THE STRONG VERB

Old English inherited the verb as scūfan (past scēaf, plural scufon, participle scofen), "to push, thrust, shove". It was a fully strong verb, used of pushing boats, of shoving men and loads, and figuratively of driving forces; the Psalms and homilies use it of God thrusting the wicked and of the shoving of crowds and armies. The strong paradigm did not survive: in Middle English, the strong past scēaf ("shof") and the participle scofen ("shoven") began to give way to weak forms in the 14th–15th centuries, a regularisation typical of common high-frequency verbs. By the 16th century the weak past shoved was standard, and only the participle "shoven" lingered in dialects. The important point for this card: the strong-to-weak transition happened early and completely, leaving no fossil forms like hove — the sibling verb heaved and shoved in the source text is entirely regular.

MIDDLE ENGLISH SHOVEN: THE SOUND CHANGES

The Middle English form shoven shows the regular Germanic sound changes. Proto-Germanic *sk- became Old English sc- (pronounced /ʃ/), which Middle English spelled sh- — the same change that gives ship from *skipą, shall from *skalą, and shape from *skapōną. The vowel has a subtler story. Old English scūfan had long ū. In most southern English words long ū became /aʊ/ in the Great Vowel Shift (house, mouse), but in a set of common words before labial consonants the vowel was shortened instead: love, above, glove, shove all show the same pre-/v/ shortening, giving modern /ʃʌv/. So shove rhymes with love and above — all three are products of the same regular sound change, and the modern spelling -ove with the /ʌ/ vowel is exactly parallel to love and glove. This is one of the cleanest demonstrations of a conditioned sound change in English.

SEMANTIC HISTORY: FROM PHYSICAL PUSH TO SOCIAL DISMISSAL

  • Old English: purely physical — pushing boats, men, and loads; also the psalmist's imagery of God thrusting the wicked.
  • Middle English: the physical sense continues; "shove off" appears in nautical contexts by the 15th century (pushing a boat from the bank).
  • 16th–17th c.: "shove off" transfers to people — first "to depart" ("we shoved off at dawn"), then the imperative dismissal "shove off!" = "get lost!" — the phrase crosses from water to land and from instruction to insult.
  • 18th–19th c.: "push and shove" names crowd behaviour; "shove-ha'penny" (19th c.) gives the word a pub-game sense; American English produces "shove it" as a vulgar dismissal.
  • 20th c.: "if push comes to shove" — a US idiom (1930s) that uses the step from pushing to shoving as the type of escalation; "shove up/over" = make room.
  • 21st c.: shove remains the bluntest member of the push family, standard in speech and sport, rare in formal writing.

SOCIOCULTURAL CONTEXT AND LITERARY ATTESTATIONS

  • The nautical shove-off is the word's turning point: every later sense of rude departure carries the salt of the boatman.
  • Tolkien's The Hobbit (the source of this card) uses "Shove off!" as the rowers' cry — dwarves and elves pushing barrel-boats into the river — the oldest sense in action.
  • Literary uses: Chaucer (physical shoving), Shakespeare (shove in the histories and comedies), and the modern idiom in journalism ("push and shove at the polls").
  • The strong/weak verb transition is complete: no modern speaker says "shave/shoven"; the regular past shoved is the product of 15th-century regularisation, and the imperative Shove on this card is the bare stem, unchanged in form since Middle English.
