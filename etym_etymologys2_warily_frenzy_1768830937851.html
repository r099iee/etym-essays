Chain for WARILY: - PIE *wer- 'to perceive, watch, take heed, guard' — the root also of aware, beware, ward, ward off, guard, warden, warn, warrant, and warehouse 'a guarded place.' - Proto-Germanic *waraz 'aware, wary, careful, on one's guard.' - Old English wær 'aware, wary, cautious, prudent' (attested from the Old English period) — the adjective from which 'wary' and 'ware' descend. - Old English wærlīce 'warily, carefully, prudently' — the adverb formed with the adverbial suffix -līce (the ancestor of modern -ly). - Middle English warliche / warli 'warily, carefully' (13th-14th c.). - Modern English warily — the adverb in its current form; the spelling settles by the 16th c. Note: 'wary' itself develops its modern form in Middle English from Old English wær, and the adverb's meaning has always been 'in a watchful, cautious manner.' The change over time is a register shift, not a chain change: the word moves from plain moral prudence to the heightened vigilance of narrative prose. Chain for FRENZY: - Greek phrēn (φρήνη) 'midriff, diaphragm; heart, mind' — the Greeks located the emotions in the midriff, and the word came to mean the seat of thought and feeling. (Origin debated — see note.) - Greek phrenēsis (φρήνεσις) 'delirium, madness, frenzy' — from phrēn with an abstract suffix; alongside the medical term phrenitis (φρενῖτις), 'inflammation of the brain, delirium.' - Latin phrenēsis 'frenzy, delirium, madness' — the medical loanword used by Latin physicians (1st c. CE). - Old French frenesie / frenesi 'madness, fury, frenzy' (12th c.; later re-spelled phrenesie under learned Latin influence). - Middle English frenesie / frenesi 'frenzy, madness, mania' (14th c., first in medical contexts). - Modern English frenzy — the spelling with -zy establishes itself in the 16th c. Note on debate: The Greek phrēn is the crux. Many older dictionaries connected it to a PIE root for 'to think' (*gʷʰren-), supported by some Balto-Slavic and Celtic cognates; modern scholarship (e.g. Beekes) treats the etymology as uncertain, possibly a Pre-Greek substrate word. The English word arrives by the double route of Latin and Old French, and the medical sense ('delirium, brain-fever') precedes the generalized sense of wild excitement by roughly two centuries.
