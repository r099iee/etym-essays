Enact is a verb with three large centres of meaning: legislation, performance, and implementation. To enact a law is to give it formal legal existence. To enact a role is to perform it on stage or in life. To enact a policy or reform is to put it into practice. The common thread is the transformation of something proposed, written, or imagined into action. The word therefore sits at the junction of law, theatre, and administration, and it is one of the clearest examples of how English uses a Latin root to talk about making something real.

The legal sense: making law

The most prominent modern sense is legislative. A parliament, congress, council, or other authority enacts a statute, act, bill, regulation, or measure. The word names the moment at which a proposal acquires legal force: a bill is debated and passed, but it is enacted when it becomes law. “Enact legislation” is a standard collocation in political and legal writing. The resulting noun is enactment, which can mean either the process of making law or the law itself. In constitutional language, an “enacted law” is one that has completed the required legislative procedure. The legal sense often implies formality, official authority, and public record.

  • Enact a statute: to give a statute legal effect.
  • Enact legislation: to pass and formally establish a body of law.
  • Enact a bill: to convert a bill into an act.
  • Enactment: the act of legislating, or the statute produced.
  • Re-enact: to enact again, especially a law that has lapsed or been amended.

The theatrical sense: making a role real

In theatre and performance, enact means to perform a role, scene, story, or event. It is close to act, perform, portray, and represent, but it often carries a stronger sense of embodying a whole scenario rather than merely playing a part. An actor can enact Hamlet; a company can enact a historical episode; a group can enact a ritual. The word is also used for historical re-enactment, in which participants recreate a past event. In the Wodehouse sentence on this card, Bertie Wooster is “forced to enact the role of King Edward III saying goodbye to that girl of his, Fair Rosamund” at a village pageant. The verb is precisely theatrical: he is not merely pretending in private but performing a scripted role in public.

The implementation sense: making policy real

In administration, business, and public policy, enact means to put a plan, policy, reform, or procedure into effect. “The company enacted a new safety policy” means it did not merely announce the policy but began to operate it. “The government enacted sweeping reforms” means the reforms were carried through. This sense is closely related to implement, execute, carry out, and realise. It extends the legal idea of giving formal force to something into the broader idea of making a decision operational.

The social-scientific and philosophical sense

In sociology, anthropology, and performance studies, people enact identities, roles, norms, rituals, and social scripts. The word captures the idea that social life is not simply given but performed and reproduced through action. A judge enacts the authority of the court; a teacher enacts a professional role; a community enacts a tradition. In cognitive science, enactivism and the adjective enactive describe theories in which cognition is understood as an active, embodied engagement with the environment rather than a passive reception of information. This technical use is a late twentieth-century development, but it grows naturally from the older sense of “putting something into action.”

Historical ordering and semantic bridges

The English word is first recorded in the early fifteenth century. The earliest sense was theatrical: “to act the part of, represent in performance.” The legal sense, “to decree or establish into law,” appears by the mid-fifteenth century. The implementation sense develops later, and the social-scientific sense is modern. The historical order is a useful reminder that enact did not begin as a legal term and then borrow a theatrical metaphor; the theatrical sense is among the oldest. The bridge between the senses is the idea of embodying a script: a law is a script for conduct, a role is a script for performance, and a policy is a script for action.

Grammar, derivatives, and collocations

Enact is a regular transitive verb: enact, enacts, enacted, enacting. It normally takes an object: one enacts a law, a role, a policy, a scene, a ritual, or a reform. The agent is an enactor; the process or result is an enactment; something that has been enacted is enacted. The adjective enactive belongs mainly to cognitive science, while enactable is rare but understandable. Common collocations include enact legislation, enact a law, enact a policy, enact reforms, enact a role, enact a scene, enact change, and re-enact a battle.

Nuances and common confusions

  • Enact vs act: act is broader and can be intransitive (“she acted quickly”); enact is transitive and implies bringing something into being or performance.
  • Enact vs pass: a legislature passes a bill through its stages; it enacts the resulting law. The words overlap but are not identical.
  • Enact vs implement: implement stresses practical machinery; enact stresses formal or performative realisation.
  • Enact vs perform: perform is the general theatrical verb; enact often suggests the realisation of a whole narrative or role.
  • Enact vs execute: execute can mean to carry out a plan or to put someone to death; enact lacks the lethal sense and is more formal.

Domain usages today

In law and politics, enact is the standard verb for legislation. In theatre and film, it describes performance and re-enactment. In public administration and business, it describes the operationalisation of policy. In sociology and anthropology, it describes the performance of social roles and rituals. In cognitive science, enactive describes embodied theories of mind. In ordinary speech, it can be used more loosely for any putting-into-action: “they enacted their plan,” “the scene was enacted before a crowd.” The word’s unity lies in the idea of turning a script, rule, or intention into a realised event.
