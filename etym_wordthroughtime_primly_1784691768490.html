- 14th century - "primly" descends from Latin "primus" ("first, foremost, finest") through Old French "prim/prims" meaning "fine, delicate, slender, neat" — a drift from priority in time to excellence and fineness of quality.
- 16th-17th century - English borrowed the word when "prim" still meant "excellent" or "fine", but it then narrowed from fineness of quality to fineness of behaviour: stiffly proper, formally neat, demurely exact, with a growing suggestion of prudery and self-conscious correctness.
- Modern English - the adverb "primly" keeps that blend of neatness and constraint with a faint hint of comedy: to do something primly is to be exaggeratedly proper, as though conscious of being observed — the deportment of someone trying to be proper and knowing it.
