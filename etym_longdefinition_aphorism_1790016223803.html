An aphorism is a terse, pointed, self-contained sentence that condenses a general observation about life, conduct, or the world into memorable form. Classic examples — Hippocrates Life is short, art is long; La Rochefoucauld Our virtues are most frequently no more than our vices in disguise; Nietzsche What does not kill me makes me stronger — share brevity, balance, wit, and a claim to universal application. Unlike a rambling explanation, an aphorism presents itself as finished: it does not argue, it pronounces. Its authority comes from form — rhythm, antithesis, paradox — as much as from content, which is why aphorisms are quoted, misquoted, and stitched into speeches long after their authors are forgotten.

The power of the aphorism lies in productive tension: it is at once modest (only one sentence) and immodest (claims a law of life). Readers enjoy testing it against experience, finding it true in one light and false in another. That testability is central to the genre. A successful aphorism is not a slogan that demands obedience; it is a whetstone that sharpens thought. This explains why philosophers, physicians, moralists, and novelists have all cultivated the form, and why your fragment — A moment ago you made use of the aphorism — treats the aphorism as a recognisable move in conversation, a tool picked up and deployed.

  • 1. Core literary sense: concise moral or philosophical maxim. A pithy sentence of general truth, often paradoxical or antithetical. Example: Power tends to corrupt. Bridge: direct descendant of Greek aphorismos definition, pithy medical precept; generalised from medicine to ethics. This is the dominant modern dictionary sense.
  • 2. Hippocratic / medical-historical sense: In the plural, the Aphorisms denotes the famous collection of some 400 Greek medical precepts attributed to Hippocrates (c.400 BCE), beginning Life is short. In medical history aphorism still means a compact clinical rule. Bridge: original technical usage before literary broadening; preserves the idea of definition that distinguishes and delimits.
  • 3. Philosophical fragment: Especially after Nietzsche, Kafka, Wittgenstein, and Cioran, aphorism means a discontinuous mode of philosophising — thinking in bursts rather than systems. Example: Nietzsche's Human, All Too Human as strings of numbered aphorisms. Bridge: Romantic and modernist distrust of system makes the fragment itself a philosophical statement.
  • 4. Distinguished from neighbours: An aphorism differs from a proverb (anonymous folk wisdom: A stitch in time), an adage / saw (aged, homely proverb), a maxim (rule of conduct, often didactic), a motto (adopted guiding principle), an epigram (witty, often satirical verse or turn, not necessarily true), an apophthegm (memorable saying of a named great person), and a dictum (authoritative pronouncement). Aphorism overlaps all but stresses terseness + general truth + literary polish + original authorship.
  • 5. Modern debased / digital sense: quotable take, one-liner. In journalism, self-help, and social media, any shareable sentence — often pseudo-profound — is marketed as an aphorism. Example: collection titles like 100 Aphorisms for Success. Bridge: the form's memorability makes it portable; portability invites commodification. Purists lament the dilution from tested wisdom to mere slogan, but the mechanism is ancient: commonplace books always harvested aphorisms.
  • 6. Rhetorical function in discourse: To use an aphorism, as in your context, is to borrow authority, to close an argument with a flourish, or to shift from particular to universal. It can enlighten, but it can also evade: answering a hard question with an aphorism sounds wise while committing to little. Skilled speakers know both edges.

Stylistic marks: brevity (usually under 15 words), balance (parallel clauses, chiasmus, antithesis), present tense universality, absence of qualification. The best aphorisms contain a twist — they begin conventionally and end unexpectedly — forcing re-reading. They are meant to be memorised, which is why rhythm and sound matter as much as logic.

For learners: if a sentence is anonymous and folksy it is probably a proverb; if it is witty and attributed and claims wisdom it is probably an aphorism; if it is witty and aims mainly to sting it is probably an epigram. Aphorism sits at the intersection of truth-claim and art.
