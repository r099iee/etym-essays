- [Dialect verb flurr (mid-16th c.)] - "To scatter, sprinkle, fly up" — used of grain being winnowed, of snow, of feathers, and of a startled flock bursting into the air; a quick, spattering motion with no human meaning at all.
- [Meteorological noun (1680s)] - The first recorded sense of flurry is "a gust of wind" — the brief eddying of air that whips up and passes, before or after a storm.
- [Fine weather extension (early 19th c.)] - The word stretches from wind to what wind carries: a brief, swirling shower of fine dancing snow, then rain, leaves, and sparks.
- [The leap to people (mid-19th c.)] - Flurry crosses from weather to commotion: a flurry of activity, of bustle, of panic — suddenness joined to agitation, motion that exhausts itself almost at once.
- [Modern register] - Energetic, light, and transient: a flurry of snow, of wind, of work, of excitement — never lasting, never deep, never quite under control; the storm's brief, harmless miniature, a puff rather than a blaze.
