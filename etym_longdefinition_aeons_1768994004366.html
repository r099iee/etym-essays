aeons (singular aeon; also spelled eon) — the word in this passage and in the language. In the source sentence — "No doubt it had been in earlier aeons the channel of an underground offshoot from the river" — the plural noun performs its most characteristic modern function: the notation of deep time. An aeon is an immeasurably long period of time, an age or an eternity, and "aeons" here marks a scale of duration that dwarfs all human history. The sentence is speculative geology — the reconstruction of a Martian cave-channel — and "earlier aeons" is the sign that this reconstruction operates on a planetary, even cosmic, timescale: aeons, not years, are the relevant unit. The word lends the scene vast antiquity and the quiet awe of forces — rivers, erosion, underground water — working over spans beyond the reach of any memory. The channel's former life as a river offshoot is so remote that only the largest unit of time can contain it.

1. General sense. A very long or immeasurably long period of time; an age; loosely, eternity. The plural "aeons" is the standard literary form: "aeons ago," "for aeons," "aeons of erosion." In this loose use the word is a rhetorical intensifier for "an unimaginably long time," and it is deliberately vague — aeons need not be countable. It is this sense, magnified by a Martian setting, that the source passage exploits.

2. Philosophical and cosmological sense. In ancient Greek thought an aiōn was an "age," a "generation," or a great cycle of world-time. In Stoic and Platonist cosmology the aeons are the vast periods of cosmic history, the great cycles of the world; the word is the technical term for a whole epoch of the universe's life. This sense is the historical bridge between the personal "lifetime" of the root and the cosmic "aeon" of geology and of the plural literary use.

3. Theological and Gnostic sense. In Gnosticism the "Aeons" (capitalized) are the eternal emanations, the personified ages or attributes that fill the divine Pleroma — often numbered in sets of thirty and paired as syzygies. In the New Testament the adjective aiōnios, "eternal" (of life, of punishment), gives English "aeonian" and "aeonic," and the underlying idea of "the age to come" (the world to come, ho aiōn ho mellōn) shaped the Christian vocabulary of eternity. In late antiquity Aion was even worshipped as a god — the personification of eternal, cyclic time, often depicted as a lion-headed, serpent-wound figure.

4. Geological sense (spelled eon). In the formal geologic time scale, an eon is the largest division of time, subdivided into eras, which are subdivided into periods and epochs. The Phanerozoic Eon (visible life, roughly the last 538 million years) and the Precambrian eons (Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic) are the current names. In this technical sense the word is countable and precise — the exact opposite of the loose, uncountable literary sense, and a striking illustration of how the same word serves both the vaguest and the most rigorous of time-measures.

5. Register and spelling. "Aeon" is the classical, British-flavoured spelling; "eon" is the common American spelling and the standard spelling in geology. Both are pronounced /ˈiːɒn/. The singular "aeon" is formal and often technical; the plural "aeons" is literary and slightly grand, prized by writers for its classical, period-sounding gravity — exactly why the passage uses it of a river-channel that once ran beneath the surface of Mars.

6. The synonym field — a study in the grading of time.
  • Ages: the loose, all-purpose term; "an age" can mean a human lifetime or a geological era, and its imprecision makes it weaker in just the way the passage needs precision of scale.
  • Eras and epochs: technical geological units — an era is a subdivision of an eon, an epoch a subdivision of an era; in strict scientific use they are smaller, ordered categories rather than interchangeable synonyms for "a very long time."
  • Millennia: exact and countable (thousands of years), too human-scaled for cosmic antiquity.
  • Centuries: shorter still, ordinary and countable, far too small for deep time.
  • Eternity: time without end, infinite duration, heavily theological — an aeon is enormously long but finite, and usable in geological and planetary contexts.
  • Forever and infinity: strictly non-finite notions from ordinary and mathematical speech, not units of time at all.
7. The semantic bridge, stated plainly. "Vital force of a living being" → "lifetime" → "an age, a generation" → "a great cycle of world-time" → "eternity" → (modern) "the largest unit of the geologic time scale." The word has travelled from the biology of the individual creature to the geology of the planet, keeping throughout its core claim: a span too large for ordinary counting — which is precisely the scale the passage needs for the former channel of a sub-Martian river.
