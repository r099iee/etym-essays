Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*ǵʰeh₂- / *ǵʰan-
"to yawn, gape, open the mouth wide"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*gapōną / *gepaną
"to open the mouth wide, stare open-mouthed, yawn"
↓
Old Norse (c. 8th–11th c.)
gapa
"to gape, open the mouth wide, look astonished"
↓
Middle English (Danelaw borrowing) (c. 1200–1400)
gapen
"to open mouth wide, stare foolishly/wonderingly, split open"
↓
Early Modern English to Modern English (c. 16th c.–present)
gape
"to stare open-mouthed in wonder; open wide as a chasm"

Note on Scandinavian influence: While Old English possessed native cognates like ġinian / gānian ("to yawn, gape") from the same PIE root *ǵʰeh₂-, the modern verb gape (along with its noun derivative gap, "breach, opening") was adopted directly into Middle English from Old Norse gapa during the period of intensive Anglo-Scandinavian bilingualism in the Danelaw.
