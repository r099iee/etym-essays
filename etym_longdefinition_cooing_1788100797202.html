Primary sense. “Cooing” is the present participle of the verb coo: the act of producing the dove’s or pigeon’s characteristic sound — a soft, low, breathy, closed-mouth murmur, usually two-syllabled (“coo-roo-coo”), made on the back vowel /uː/. The word is onomatopoeic (echoic): English coined it straight from the sound itself. Though doves provide the model, it is used of any similar soft murmuring — of turtle doves, collared doves and strutting roof pigeons, and, loosely, of soft rhythmic sounds that resemble the bird’s call, such as a mother’s gentle crooning patter over a cradle. As a participle it works both as verb (“the pigeons were cooing”) and as adjective (“a cooing voice”, “a cooing tone”); the gerund noun (“the cooing of doves at dawn”) is a stock item of nature writing.

  • Of human speech — tenderness. Since the later 18th century “to coo” has meant to speak in a soft, loving, endearing tone: lovers coo to each other, mothers coo over babies, guests coo over a new puppy. The semantic bridge is sensory — the dove’s soft murmur is the acoustic image of gentleness, so tender human speech is described “as doves speak”. The idiom “to bill and coo” (billing = doves touching bills, cooing = murmuring) was a Victorian favourite for lovers’ endearments and is still current in playful prose.
  • Of praise — gushing admirably. In modern journalism and gossip writing “cooed” is a speech-verb for extravagant, breathless admiration (“the crowd cooed over the royal baby”, “she cooed about the dress”). It is the hushed, fond register of gushing, and in this use usually carries a faint irony: a little too much soft praise sounds insincere or mawkish — “everyone cooed” is rarely a compliment to the depth of the conversation.
  • Noun and exclamation. “A coo” is a single such sound (“not a coo was heard”). The British interjection “coo!” — a mild exclamation of surprise — is a 20th-century shortening of “coo-ee” (the Aboriginal-Australian-derived bush call), and though it shares the spelling it belongs to a different branch of the sound-family: a loud call rather than a murmur.
  • Grammar and register. “Cooing” is the -ing form of the weak, completely regular verb coo (coo – cooed – cooed – cooing); it has no irregularities. Its register is essentially gentle and sentimental: ornithology uses it plainly, and of people it is cosy, tender, sometimes gently mocking (“stop cooing over him”). In Wilde’s story the pink-and-white doves of Baalbec are “cooing to each other” — the word supplies the entire mood of soft, idle affection against which the Prince’s sacrifice is framed.

One sound, four careers. Bird-sound (1660s) → lovers’ soft talk (late 18th century) → sentimental endearment over babes and pets → journalistic verb of gushing admiration: the whole career of “cooing” is the widening of one soft vowel from the dovecot to the drawing-room to the news page, with a dash of baby-talk (“coochie-coo”) and a British cry of surprise along the way.
