- Choking dust and smoke — 'pother' first appears in print around 1590 as a choking cloud of dust or smoke, a puff of kicked-up stuff — a vivid, physical word for being smothered in dry debris.

- Commotion and fuss — Within a generation (early 1600s) the dust-cloud turns mental: 'pother' means a commotion, bustle, or fuss, especially one out of proportion to its cause — people say 'to be in a pother' and 'what a pother!' with a distinctly dismissive snap.

- An everyday word of the 1600s–1700s — The verb 'to pother' flourishes alongside the noun ('to harass, perplex,' and 'to bustle about'), and the word is common currency in the lively prose of Dryden and Swift — ordinary, unbookish, energetic.

- The literary relic — Over the 1800s–1900s 'pother' fades from daily speech, surviving in print as an old-fashioned, faintly bookish word; its dismissive flavour (a fuss that exceeds its cause) survives intact — which is precisely why C. S. Lewis reaches for it to give his Narnian castle scene its archaic fairy-tale remoteness.
