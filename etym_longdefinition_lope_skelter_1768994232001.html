lope — In "the wind has a raw edge to it and leaves lope by the roadside," the verb lends the leaves the long, easy, swinging stride of a large animal. A lope is not a hurry: it is the unhurried, rhythmic, ground-covering gait of a wolf or a horse at an easy pace, and applied to leaves it turns the wind's work into a kind of kinetic grace — the dead litter moves as if with a body, swinging forward in long strides along the road. The tone is the poem's own: a raw wind, a night landscape, yet the leaves "lope" rather than "scurry," and that word choice gives the scene a large, wild, almost ceremonial ease. It is the animal energy inside the weather.

Primary sense (the gait). As a verb, "to lope" is to move with a long, bounding, easy stride that covers distance without strain — the characteristic gait of a wolf, a fox, a deer, or a horse moving at an easy, extended pace, between a trot and a gallop. As a noun, "a lope" is that stride or pace itself ("an easy lope," "a steady lope"). The word implies power held in reserve: the animal is moving fast enough to eat up ground but is not straining, not frantic, not breaking a sweat. It is the difference between a courier crossing a plain and a runner sprinting to a finish line.

Secondary senses and extensions. Because the gait is smooth and long, "lope" extends naturally to anything that covers distance with a long, unhurried motion: "to lope along the ridge," "to lope across the field," "the river loped between the banks," "the months lope past." Figuratively it names a relaxed, effortless, sometimes lazy progress — a career that "lopes" from success to success. As a noun it also names the action itself ("at a lope") and, in ranch and equestrian usage, the easy canter-like pace of a working horse. The word's register is literary and naturalist: it belongs to open country, ranch prose, and poetry rather than the city; it is mildly archaic and always faintly wild.

skelter — The second movement is the dispersal. "...and skelter on in the night fields" takes the second half of the old jingle "helter-skelter" and makes it a verb of its own: to skelter is to move in a hurried, confused, disorderly, headlong way — the scatter of many feet, the stampede of panic, the rush of things fleeing in all directions. Where "lope" is poise, "skelter" is panic.

Primary sense. As a verb (chiefly British, dialectal or informal), "to skelter" means to scurry, scamper, or hurry away in a confused rush — "to run hastily," "to scuttle off." The image is of legs moving fast and chaotically, things scattering: leaves skeltering across a yard, mice skeltering behind a wainscot, children skeltering to school at the last bell.

Secondary senses and the jingle. The word lives most famously inside the reduplicated compound "helter-skelter" (from the 1590s), which means "in headlong, confused, disorderly haste; pell-mell" and, as an adverb, describes frantic, jumbled motion ("the crowd fled helter-skelter"). In British usage "a helter-skelter" is also a fairground spiral slide, whose passengers come down in dizzying disorder — a 20th-century coinage that kept the word's sense of losing control. Back-formed out of the compound, "skelter" works as a verb ("to skelter"), and by analogy with hurry-scurry, hubble-bubble, higgledy-piggledy, it belongs to the class of expressive English jingle-words in which the sound itself conveys the confusion.

  • Semantic core: fast, disordered, headlong movement, usually with a scatter of separate agents (feet, leaves, creatures) rather than a single disciplined motion.
  • Contrast with lope: lope is single, long, smooth, and in control; skelter is multiple, short, irregular, and out of control. The poem sets the two against each other deliberately: the leaves lope along the road with animal grace, then skelter on into the night fields in panic.
  • Register: skelter is informal, expressive, and chiefly British; it is rare outside the compound, and when used alone it deliberately draws on the comic-disorder energy of "helter-skelter."

Summary of polysemy. LOPE: (1) verb — move with a long, easy, bounding stride; (2) noun — that gait or pace; (3) figurative — effortless, relaxed, long-covering progress. SKELTER: (1) verb — scurry or hurry in a confused rush; (2) member of the jingle "helter-skelter," headlong confusion; (3) noun/adjective in the fairground "helter-skelter," a spiral slide. The bridge between senses is always motion under different control: lope keeps its power in reserve; skelter spends its force in disorder.
