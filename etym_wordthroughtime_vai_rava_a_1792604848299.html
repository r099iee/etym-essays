- [Late Vedic Era (c. 1000–600 BCE)] — First emergence in the Atharvaveda and Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa as a shadowy chieftain of chthonic spirits, spirits of the earth, and king of the raksas — [perception: mysterious and fearsome lord of underground wealth and subterranean entities]
- [Classical Epic Period (Rāmāyaṇa & Mahābhārata, c. 500 BCE–400 CE)] — Fully elevated into the supreme Lokapāla of the North, undisputed master of the nine nidhis, and the literary benchmark for fabulous material wealth and royal architecture — [perception: glorious cosmic world-guardian and patron of prosperous kings]
- [Classical Kāvyā Era & Kālidāsa (c. 4th–5th c. CE)] — Celebrated in Kālidāsa's Meghadūta as the benevolent sovereign of Alakā, presiding over an enchanted Himalayan city of eternal youth, love, and artistic bliss — [perception: cultured divine patron of romance, luxury, and poetic beauty]
- [Early Buddhist & Pan-Asian Transmissions (c. 3rd c. BCE–8th c. CE)] — Re-conceptualized as Vessavaṇa in the Pali Canon and translated into Chinese as Duōwéntiān and Japanese as Bishamonten, becoming an armored warrior-guardian of the Buddhist Dharma — [perception: formidable protector of monasteries, vanquisher of demons, and dispenser of righteous victory]
- [Medieval to Pre-Modern Asia (c. 9th–18th c. CE)] — Worshipped across Japan as one of the Seven Lucky Gods (Shichifukujin) and in Indian merchant communities as a domestic deity of accounting, balance sheets, and gold — [perception: popular household harbinger of merchant fortune, financial security, and luck]
- [Modern Indology & Art History (19th–21st c.)] — Analyzed by scholars of Asian iconography as one of the most successful pan-Asian transcultural deities, uniting Vedic, Buddhist, Tibetan, and East Asian traditions — [perception: magnificent icon of ancient trans-Eurasian religious and commercial connectivity]
