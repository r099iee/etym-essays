- [13th–14th c.] - Middle English borrows the verb hasten from Old French haster, meaning to hurry or urge forward - [connotation: urgent physical exertion and spurring horses or companions forward]
- [16th c.] - Affixed with the inchoative/causative suffix -en to form Early Modern English hasten / hastened - [connotation: standard verb for both physical hurrying and accelerating events]
- [17th–18th c.] - Flourishes in philosophical, theological, and literary prose to describe divine providence or human frailty hastening natural ends - [connotation: dignified, solemn, and reflective literary term]
- [19th c. – Victorian Era] - Becomes a narrative cornerstone in Victorian novels (Dickens, Austen, Eliot) and fairy tales (Wilde) for purposeful character movement and conversational qualification ("hastened to add") - [connotation: polished narrative refinement, emotional earnestness, and courtly etiquette]
- [20th–21st c.] - Yields to hurried and rushed in casual spoken English, while enduring in formal literature, journalism, and historical prose - [connotation: elegant, slightly archaic, evocative, and dignified]
