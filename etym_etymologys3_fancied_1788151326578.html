1. Greek light: from phaínō to phantasía. The line begins with the Indo-European root *bʰeh₂-, "to shine, to appear", source of Greek phōs "light" (as in photography and phosphorus). Homer's phaínō, "to bring to light, show", developed a middle voice meaning "appear, seem", and from this verb Greek built phántasma, "appearance, ghost, image", and phantasía, the noun for the faculty of forming images - for Plato and Aristotle the capacity that produces mental pictures from perception. Aristotle's De Anima makes phantasía the hinge between sense and thought, and the word's cognitive gravity shows: it is a term of exact psychology long before it becomes a light entertainment.

2. From Greek into Latin and Romance. Latin naturalized phantasia in the first century BCE as a term of rhetoric and psychology - the mental image, the fancy - and Cicero and Quintilian use it freely. The Vulgate uses phantasma for "ghost". The word travelled on as fantaisie into Old French by the twelfth century with the whole Greek spread of meanings: appearance, imagination, whim, and "fantasy" proper. Middle English took fantasie in the 13th-14th centuries - Chaucer uses it for the imagination and for capricious desire alike - and then fourteenth-century speakers began clipping: fantasy shortened to fancy, a colloquial reduction recorded by the mid-fifteenth century. The ancient word for "making visible" had become a two-syllable English everyday noun.

3. The 16th century: fancy becomes a faculty. The shortened fancy rapidly absorbed the old word's whole semantic field. In Renaissance psychology fancy names the image-making faculty - the Latin phantasia inherited through memory treatises - and it is a technical word of Elizabethan science: the "fancy" is where sense-images assemble into dreams, inventions, and desires. Shakespeare's uses flow from this: in The Merchant of Venice, "Tell me where is fancy bred, / Or in the heart or in the head?" - fancy is the imagination in love. By 1600 "to fancy" already meant both "to imagine" and "to love, to like", because desire was understood as the mind's picture of a wanted object; the participle fancied is attested from the 16th century in exactly the double sense this card preserves; his "fancy" passages make the point without further citation.

4. The 18th century: fancy and imagination split. Enlightenment aesthetics grouped fancy with imagination, wit, and judgement - Addison's Spectator papers on the "pleasures of the imagination" treat the pair as twins, and Johnson's Dictionary defines fancy as "imagination; the power by which the mind forms to itself images of things". But Romantic criticism pried them apart: for Coleridge, imagination is the unifying, creative power, while fancy is only the associative "mode of memory", a lower faculty that rearranges received images. The consequence for fancied was a subtle devaluation: what is merely fancied is invented, not truly imagined; "fanciful" and "fancied" drifted toward the insubstantial and the merely decorative. Wordsworth, whose Prelude provides this card's sentence, participates in the same distinction: the "fancied scene" is the mind's own picture set against the real one with deliberate pathos. His line - "while upon the fancied scene / I gazed with growing love" - shows fancy doing its Romantic work: the imagined landscape is loved precisely because it is imagined, more loved, in that moment, than any actual view could be.

5. The 19th century: betting, desire, and the transatlantic split. Nineteenth-century English added two modern senses. From the racecourse came the sporting idiom: to "fancy" a horse is to back it with trust and wishing, and the "fancied" horse is the heavily expected winner - a sense that migrated from horses to teams, boxers, and candidates in the twentieth century (a "fancied favourite"). Separately, in British English the verb "to fancy" intensified toward plain wanting: "fancy a drink?", "I fancy her" - so fancied became the participle of desire ("the most fancied man in the office"). American English chose a different path: there the verb faded, leaving "fancy" chiefly as an adjective meaning ornate or high-quality ("fancy dress") and "fancied" meaning mainly "imagined, supposed". The Atlantic split is sharp and persistent: a London headline about a "fancied candidate" means a candidate expected to win; a New York headline would be about the candidate's imagination.

6. Sound and morphology. The phonological drift is compact but instructive: Greek ph- became Latin ph-, rendered f- in Romance and English; the cluster of phantasia softened through Old French to fantasy and then dissolved in the aphetic fancy; the final -y (from -ia, -ie) takes the regular past-participle suffix to give fancied, /ˈfænsɪd/, two syllables with a schwa-like -ied. The same family yields fancies, fanciful, fancying, and, at one remove, phantom, phenomenon, and photograph - all descendants of the root that meant "shine, show, appear". To trace fancied is to watch a Greek word for light become an English word for wanting something you have pictured.

7. Fancied today. Contemporary English exploits all three senses at once. Sports pages name the heavily fancied favourite; British idiom uses the reflexive "he fancies himself a bit of a poet" to flag self-regard; literary prose speaks of fancied insults and fancied wrongs, imagined injuries of the paranoid register; and Romantic allusion keeps the fancied scene current in poetry about imagination. Because the word carries "imagined" and "desired" inside one form, writers can imply that our wishes are our fantasies - a psychological insight compressed into a participle. Its history, from Greek light to British flirtatiousness and the betting slip, is a two-thousand-year lesson in how a word for "showing" became a word for "making up" - and then for "wanting".
