GRUNT is an imitative (onomatopoeic) word: its 'root' is the sound it names, and no single PIE lexical etymon can be reconstructed with confidence. The chain below follows the attested Germanic forms and notes the parallel expressive formations elsewhere. Expressive base grun- (sound-symbolic root shared across Indo-European, cf. Latin grunnīre 'to grunt,' Greek grýzein 'to grumble') → Proto-Germanic *grunōną 'to grunt, murmur' (with the frequentative *grunatjaną, giving Old English grunnettan) → Old English grunian 'to grunt, murmur' and grunnettan 'to grunt' (8th–11th c.) → Middle English grunten/gronten 'to grunt' (12th–15th c.) → Modern English GRUNT (noun and verb). Note on the debate: older etymological literature posited a PIE root *grun- 'to grunt,' but modern scholarship treats the family as imitative and expressive rather than as a regular inherited lexeme with a single PIE ancestor; the Latin and Greek parallels are independent sound-symbolic creations, not evidence of a common 'word.' Proper Germanic cognates include Old High German grunizōn and Middle Dutch grunsen.
