- 63 BCE — Senatorial Oratory of Republican Rome: Marcus Tullius Cicero coins the iconic exclamation in his First Catilinarian Oration before the Roman Senate to denounce Catiline's conspiracy and senatorial passivity.
- 1st to 2nd c. CE — Imperial Roman Satire & Meme: taken up by satirists Martial and Juvenal as an instantly recognizable cultural catchphrase to mock hypocritical moralists and lament the perceived vulgarity of imperial Roman society.
- Middle Ages (6th–14th c.) — Christian Monastic Lamentation: adopted by Christian scholars and monks in theological treatises to lament the vanity of the fallen secular world and the sinfulness of human flesh prior to the Last Judgment.
- Renaissance & Enlightenment (16th–18th c.) — Humanist Literary Staple: cited constantly across Europe by Erasmus, Montaigne, Shakespeare, Molière, and Voltaire; in England, Cicero became affectionately known as 'Tully', and his phrase was a fixture of educated political discourse.
- 19th c. — Victorian Parliamentary Debating Flourish: utilized in the British House of Commons, editorial cartoons, and satirical journals like Punch as the standard oratorical trope for mocking societal changes and governmental scandals.
- 20th to 21st c. — Ironic Meme & Intellectual Trope: continues to thrive in academic essays, legal opinions, and cultural commentary, functioning both as a sincere cry against political degradation and as a self-aware, mock-heroic caricature of pedantic traditionalism.
