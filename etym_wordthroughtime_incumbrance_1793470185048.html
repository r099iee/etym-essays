- c. 1300 — Medieval Military Barricades: Enters Middle English (chiefly as encombraunce) from Old French, describing military obstructions, felled timber blocking roads, and bodily peril.
- 14th–15th c. — Moral and Spiritual Entanglement: Extensively utilized in devotional treatises (e.g. Chaucer, Wycliffite sermons) for the worldly cares, riches, and sins that encumber the pilgrim's soul on its ascent to God.
- 16th–17th c. — Legal Codification and Orthographic Latinization: Solidifies as a formal term of art in English land tenure and property conveyancing; the spelling incumbrance (assimilated to Latin in-) gains prominent traction among jurists.
- 18th c. — Neoclassical Canon and Intellectual Restraint: Reaches peak frequency in high literary prose (Reynolds, Johnson, Blackstone, Burke), denoting both legal liens on real estate and intellectual/artistic rules that restrain rash audacity.
- 19th c. — Victorian Domestic and Social Vernacular: Frequently employed in social commentary and classified advertisements to designate domestic dependents or young children who hinder employment or ease of travel.
- 20th–21st c. — Specialized Legal Survival: While modern general English consolidated around the spelling encumbrance, the form incumbrance persists in statutory property codes, colonial deeds, and historical literary scholarship.
