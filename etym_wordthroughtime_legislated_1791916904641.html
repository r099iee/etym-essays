- 1719 — Legislate is coined, most likely as a back-formation from legislation; the new verb names the activity of law-making in the abstract, a word for political theorists and parliamentary reporters rather than ordinary speakers.
- 18th–19th c. — The verb settles into parliamentary and constitutional prose. "To legislate for" a colony or a class becomes a standard phrase of imperial and reform politics, often carrying a faintly paternalistic tone.
- Mid-20th c. — The aphorism "you cannot legislate morality" and the phrase "legislating from the bench" give the word a polemical edge: it can now imply overreach, whether by reformers or by judges.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Legislated flourishes as a participial adjective in policy language — legislated rates, legislated targets, legislated mandates — describing outcomes fixed by statute rather than by market or custom.
- Contemporary — The word remains formal and institutional, but its participle is common in news copy about minimum wages, emissions caps and constitutional rights, where "legislated" signals that a rule is imposed by law and may be repealed by law.
