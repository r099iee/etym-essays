From rooster to readiness

Cocked is the past-participial adjective and verb form of cock, and its whole history is the story of a single enduring metaphor: the alert, strutting, crest-raising rooster. English cock (the male fowl) is recorded in Old English as cocc (attested by the late first millennium in glosses and compounds). Its origin is onomatopoeic or expressive: the word imitates or evokes the bird's sharp call, and near-identical forms appear across the Germanic and Romance worlds — Old Norse kokkr, Middle Dutch cocke, Old French coc, with medieval Latin cocus-type forms alongside. The widespread, similar shapes point to a freely borrowed expressive word rather than a regular Indo-European inheritance; no PIE etymon can be securely reconstructed, and the word belongs with imitative bird names such as cuckoo and peewit.

The verb is born

From the noun came the verb cock, first meaning to raise, set upright or erect, modelled on the rooster's comb and its alert, jerking head. Early records of the 16th–17th centuries show it applied to turning things up jauntily: to cock the hat was to turn up its brim, so that a cocked hat — later the famous tricorne and bicorne — was one whose brims stood up like a crest. From headgear the sense spread to the firearm: the hammer of an early gun, when drawn back to the firing position, rose like a cocked comb, and so a cocked gun was one drawn, tensioned, and ready to fire. From weapons it moved into everyday motion language: ears cocked to listen, a head cocked on one side, a wrist cocked back for a blow — all borrowing the same image of something raised into a poised, ready position.

Into idioms

Firearms gave English its most durable idiom. A gun whose hammer was only half drawn back could discharge accidentally, or prematurely — and so a person who acts before all is ready goes off half-cocked (recorded from the 17th century, still fully current). The same imagery produced cocked and locked in modern tactical speech: a weapon drawn to the ready position with its safety still engaged. A slang offshoot of the 18th–19th centuries used cocked for being drunk — one half-cocked being unsteadily, prematurely intoxicated — though that sense has faded almost entirely from modern use.

Modern profile

Today cocked is most alive in three places: the neutral descriptive phrase head cocked on one side (curiosity, listening, suspicion — exactly the thrush's pose in the source text), ears cocked for alert attention, and the technical cocked gun and hammer with its idioms. The strutting bird that started it all has disappeared from the associations of the word — no modern speaker feels a rooster behind cocked head — but the erect, ready, alert posture of the fowl remains precisely the image the word still paints in every one of its senses. The past participle cements this: it names the completed pose of readiness, whether the head of a listening bird, the ear of a hound, or the hammer of a loaded rifle.
