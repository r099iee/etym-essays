Cemented is the past participle of the verb to cement, which means to bind, fix, or make firm as if with cement — the powdery mineral binder that, when mixed with water, sand, and aggregate, sets into hard mortar or concrete. In the literal, building-trade sense, a structure is cemented when its bricks, stones, or masonry units have been joined by mortar that has fully cured: the process is slow, chemical, and irreversible, producing a mass stronger than the sum of its parts. The participial form emphasizes the completed state of a process rather than the momentary act — not 'in the act of binding' but 'having been bound and now fixed permanently.' This is the crucial nuance of the -ed ending: cemented reports a finished outcome, a bond that has set, dried, hardened, and can no longer be undone without violence. Etymologically the word carries a residue of its surprising origin in Latin caedō 'I cut, hew,' because the earliest 'cement' was literally the quarry chips and rubble cut from rock faces and mixed with lime to make Roman mortar; the material that binds is thus, at its root, the waste product of cutting — an elegant inversion from fracture to cohesion. In Gibbon's sentence — 'The gentle, but powerful, influence of laws and manners had gradually cemented the union of the provinces' — the participle performs a full metaphor of masonry applied to imperial statecraft. Roman law and shared customs are imagined not as chains of conquest but as slow-setting mortar that binds heterogeneous provinces into a single political fabric. The adverb 'gradually' is essential: cementing is not a single decisive event but a long chemical hardening of social glue; the empire's cohesion is described as something that cured over time, quietly and pervasively, like lime mortar reaching full strength. The tone is constructive, almost admiring: empire as architecture, provinces as stones set into one wall, cohesion as a cured bond. No synonym captures this exact image: 'united' states the result without the bonding agent; 'fused' or 'welded' implies heat and metalwork too violent for 'gentle… influence'; 'consolidated' is administrative and fiscal; 'bound' or 'tied' suggests rope rather than mineral set; and 'glued' is too colloquial and weak. Cemented uniquely imports the Roman builder's craft into constitutional history. Polysemy and modern usage:
  • Construction and civil engineering: cement, cementitious binder, cemented masonry and aggregate — the concrete industry built on Portland cement (patented by Joseph Aspdin in 1824 and named for Portland stone); a structure is 'cemented' when its mortar or concrete has set.
  • Dentistry: dental cement is used to lute crowns, bridges, and inlays onto teeth; a crown or prosthesis is said to be 'cemented' into place, and cements also serve as fillings and bases.
  • Orthopaedic surgery: bone cement (typically PMMA, polymethyl methacrylate) fixes artificial joints and prostheses into the skeleton; a hip replacement is described as 'cemented' or 'uncemented.'
  • Geology and sedimentology: cementation is the diagenetic process by which dissolved minerals precipitate between grains and bind sediments into solid sedimentary rock — sandstone is 'cemented' by silica or calcite.
  • Chemistry and materials science: hydraulic cement, which sets and hardens under water, and the hydration chemistry (calcium silicate hydrates) behind modern concrete.
  • Figurative social and political use: to cement an alliance, friendship, union, relationship, or reputation — to make a bond permanent and reliable; a marriage or treaty 'cemented by common interest.'
  • Business and law: to cement a deal, a position, market share, or a legacy; a ruling or contract 'cements' rights and obligations.
  • Sport and competition: a team 'cements its lead'; an athlete 'cements their place in history.'
  • Personal and psychological: an idea, habit, or belief is 'cemented' in someone's mind when it becomes fixed and resistant to change.
  • Idiomatic and stylistic: 'cemented in place,' 'cemented together,' 'cemented by time and use' — usually implying something that cannot easily be moved or undone.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the core image is never glue but mineral setting. Latin caementum (from caedō 'to cut') named quarry chips and rough stone used in Roman rubble-work and mortar; as this material passed through Old French ciment into Middle English cement, the sense shifted from the chips themselves to the binding paste made with them, and eventually to any adhesive construction material. In modern chemistry the word extends to hydraulic and Portland cement, and the verb to cement was figurative almost from the beginning of its English life, applied from the 17th century to friendships, treaties, and unions made firm by shared interest. Register and collocations: cemented is standard in formal literary, historical, journalistic, and scientific prose — neither slang nor archaic. Chief collocations: 'cemented the alliance/friendship/union,' 'cement relations,' 'cemented by marriage/common interest,' 'cemented the team's lead,' 'cemented in place,' 'cemented his reputation.' The participle typically marks completed social or material solidification after a process.
