- c. 1380–1450 (Middle English forerenner) - Literal human foot-messengers, couriers, or scouts sent ahead of royal retinues, nobility, and military columns to secure provisions, prepare lodging, and announce impending arrivals; perceived as an honorable, physically demanding courtly and martial office.
- 1520s–1650s (Early Modern English) - Religious and theological elevation; applied specifically in biblical translations to John the Baptist as the prophetic forerunner of Jesus Christ (calquing the Greek prodromos and Latin praecursor), acquiring sacred solemnity and spiritual gravitas.
- 1680s–1800s (Enlightenment & Natural Philosophy) - Broadened to natural phenomena and intellectual history; used by early scientists and essayists to designate early warning signs (such as barometric shifts preceding storms) and early philosophical thinkers whose hypotheses anticipated empirical science.
- 1850s–1920s (Industrial & Evolutionary Era) - Applied extensively to technological prototypes (e.g., early atmospheric engines as forerunners of steam locomotives) and biological paleontology (archaic fossil organisms viewed as evolutionary ancestors).
- Contemporary Modern English - Established across academic, cultural, and everyday registers to describe aesthetic, literary, ideological, and mechanical pioneers that foreshadowed later modern breakthroughs.
