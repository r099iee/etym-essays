Fuss (noun) is excessive, excited, or anxious activity, attention, or concern over something — usually something that does not deserve it. A "great fuss" is a disproportionate display of commotion: much running about, talking, worrying, or pampering around a small matter. In Tolkien's The Hobbit, the crowd "made a great fuss of him" — that is, they surrounded Bilbo with lavish, flattering attention, patting him on the back and drinking his health, the friendly buzz of celebrity surrounding the burglar of the Lonely Mountain. The word is famously hard to translate because it packs together three ideas: noise, bustle, and disproportion.

Primary meaning. At its core, fuss names an excessive reaction. It is used in a family of closely related frames:

  • "Make a fuss" (complain / protest). "Don't make such a fuss" = don't make a disproportionate complaint about a small problem; "I don't want to make a fuss" = I don't want to cause trouble by objecting. Here the emphasis is on needless objection.
  • "Make a fuss of / over someone" (pamper / flatter). To make a fuss of a person is to treat them with doting, flattering attention — compliments, fussing, cosseting. This is the sense in the Tolkien sentence: the crowd makes a great fuss of Bilbo.
  • "Make a fuss over / about something" (fret). To fuss over a detail is to worry about it disproportionately or to work on it with anxious care — "she fussed over the seating plan".
  • As a mass noun. "All this fuss" = all this needless bother; "no fuss" = without ceremony or complications ("a quick, no-fuss dinner").
  • As a verb. To fuss is to behave in this way: to bustle anxiously, to worry needlessly, to fidget ("stop fussing"), or to cosset ("he fussed over his guests").

Derived forms. The adjective fussy is the most useful derivative: it means (1) hard to please, over-particular ("a fussy eater", "fussy about her clothes"); (2) needlessly elaborate, busy (of design: "a fussy pattern"); (3) fretful, restless (of a baby). Fussily is the adverb, fussiness the noun. The informal noun fussbudget (also fuss-pot in British English) names a person who makes a fuss about trivial matters. The compound fuss-free (marketing, informal) promises the absence of complication.

Secondary meanings and semantic bridges. All modern uses descend from the single original sense of "needless bustle" recorded in 1701, and the bridges are easy to see. From "bustling activity" came the complaint sense (activity you do not want → protest against bother), the pampering sense (activity lavished on a person → doting attention), and the worry sense (anxious activity → needless fretting). The adjective fussy shifts the spotlight from the activity to the personality: the person who is always bustling over details is "hard to please" — and from personality it slides to objects ("a fussy design" is one too busy with detail). Register is consistently informal-to-colloquial: fuss is a chatty, mildly impatient word, and even in its affectionate use ("making a fuss of the baby") it carries a hint that the attention is perhaps a little excessive.

Grammar and usage. Fuss is normally a mass (uncountable) noun, but in the phrase "a fuss" / "a great fuss" it is used countably for a single episode of commotion. The verb is regular (fuss, fussed, fussing) and can be intransitive ("stop fussing") or transitive with over/about ("fuss over the details", "fuss over a visitor"). Prepositional preferences differ: make a fuss of someone (pamper), make a fuss about/over something (complain or fret). In British English the two constructions are cleanly distinguished; American English tends to blur them, using "make a fuss over" for both.

In the reading context. "They made a great fuss of him" — the crowd's attention is warm but comic: the narrator adds that all this fussing "was just as well, for he was not feeling particularly cheerful." The word signals that the celebration is noisier and more flattering than Bilbo's mood requires, the perfect lightly ironic note for a hobbit who has been through a battle with a dragon.
