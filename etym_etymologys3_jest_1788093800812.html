1. The Latin base: gerere and gesta. English JEST descends, through French, from Latin gero, gerere 'to carry, bear; to conduct, do, perform' — a verb attested from the third century BCE (it is common in Plautus) whose own Indo-European ancestry is unknown: dictionaries of Latin etymology mark the origin of gerere as obscure or uncertain, with no agreed PIE root, so the honest chain begins at attested Latin. The past participle gestus 'carried, performed' generated, in the neuter plural, gesta 'things done, deeds, exploits' — a word of the highest literary pedigree. Roman historiography had already made res gestae ('achievements; history of achievements') a technical formula, most famously in Augustus's autobiographical monument, the Res Gestae Divi Augusti; and medieval Latin compiled the enormously popular story-collection Gesta Romanorum, 'Deeds of the Romans.' Every one of these uses carries the root meaning 'things performed.'

2. Old French geste: the heroic word. Old French took gesta as geste, with both meanings already present in the twelfth century: 'deed, exploit, achievement' and 'story or history of deeds' — the second visible in the epic genre chanson de geste ('song of deeds'), the tradition of the Song of Roland. French, like English, later let the word slip: modern French geste survives mainly in gestes for a chronicle and in learned uses, while the everyday word for joke is plaisanterie. The essential fact for the English word is that it entered the language on the heroic side of the street: a 'gest' was something knights did and minstrels sang.

3. Middle English: the gests of knights. Middle English borrowed geste (spelt geste, ieste, jeste) in the fourteenth century for 'deed, exploit' and 'tale of adventures, romance' — the 'gests' of Arthur and Alexander circulated in the same manuscripts as the romances, and chronicles record the 'gestes' of living lords. But even in Middle English the word was already leaking downward: beside martial deeds it could mean 'an idle tale, a trifling story,' and the moralizing literature of the fifteenth century lists 'jestes' among the vanities of the tongue. The semantic devaluation is visible in the collocations — merry geste, wanton words and jestes — where 'merry' begins to steer the word toward comedy.

4. The sixteenth-century inversion. In Early Modern English the joke triumphed. The heroic sense faded to memory while 'jest' hardened into its modern core: 'a sportive or witty saying; a joke; a trick played for laughter.' Several engines drove the change:

  • Court jesters institutionalized licensed comedy. The professional fool made jesting a courtly art; the word 'jester' (sixteenth century) names the role, and Shakespeare's fools — Feste in Twelfth Night, whose very name means 'feast, festivity,' Touchstone, Lear's Fool — gave it immortality on the public stage.
  • The jest-book industry commercialized wit. A C. Mery Talys (A Hundred Merry Tales, c. 1526, printed by John Rastell) and its successors made 'jest' a print commodity — short comic stories that anyone could retail, ancestors of the joke book and the sitcom premise.
  • The Reformation moralized against it. The King James Version's 'jesting' (Ephesians 5:4, translating Greek eutrapelia, 'wittiness') gave Puritan and Anglican moralists their proof-text: 'jesting' became a sin of the tongue, idleness dressed as wit. The proverb 'to jest with edge-tools' warned against dangerous trifling, and Elizabethan moralists from pulpit and press grouped jesting with the vices that corrupted the young.
  • Shakespeare canonized the word's wit in hundreds of uses, crowned by the famous anatomy of laughter in Love's Labour's Lost V.ii: 'A jest's prosperity lies in the ear / Of him that hears it, never in the tongue / Of him that makes it.'

5. Sound and form. The spelling chain geste → ieste → jeste → jest records the medieval French initial /dʒ/ (g before e) settling into the native letter j, and the loss of the final -e; Elizabethan rhymes ('jest' rhyming with 'rest,' 'best') confirm the single syllable. The same Latin participle produced English 'gesture' (via Latin gestus, 'bearing, carriage') and the rare literary doublet gest ('exploit, tale'), revived in the nineteenth century: English therefore owns both the joke (jest) and the bodily expression (gesture) from the one verb of performing — a neat illustration of a word about 'carrying on' splitting into performance and comedy.

6. Modern life. Since the eighteenth century 'jest' has retreated. 'Joke' owns the spoken language; 'jest' survives in 'in jest,' 'jesting aside,' in the fossil 'jesting Pilate' (Bacon, 1625 — 'What is truth? said jesting Pilate, and would not stay for an answer'), in the historical word 'jester,' and in any writer who wants an old-fashioned, world-weary touch of wit. The word's career — from Roman deeds, to medieval romances, to Elizabethan japes, to modern archaism — is one of the most complete social descents (and partial re-elevations) in the English vocabulary.
