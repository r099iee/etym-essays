- [16th century (1530s)] - Borrowed from Latin meticulosus meaning "fearful, faint-hearted, timid"; a purely negative word, used in the era of Shakespeare and the King James Bible.
- [17th–18th century] - Still means "fearful"; but the anxious person is increasingly pictured as one who cannot relax his attention, watching every detail lest danger arrive.
- [19th century (c. 1850s)] - A famous semantic inversion: "excessive care arising from fear of error" shades into "excessive care" and then simply "very great care".
- [1860s onward] - The noun meticulousness enters as a virtue of scholars and editors — exacting, detail-loving thoroughness; still faintly suggesting obsessive trembling vigilance.
