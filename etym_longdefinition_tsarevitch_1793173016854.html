Tsarevitch is, in its essential sense, the son of a tsar: a male prince of the Russian imperial house, the boy born in purple whose cradle stands nearest the throne. The feminine counterpart is tsarevna, a tsar's daughter, and in the source sentence the young Tsarevitch Alexei appears beside his three sisters, the Tsarevnas Anna, Olga, and Helena, exactly as a Russian wonder-tale arranges its royal nursery.

In the source sentence the word wears its full fairytale dress: beyond the blue sea-ocean and the void places, in a city set amid pleasant meads, there lived a Tsarevitch whose name was Alexei. Here the title is half rank and half character type: the questing youngest prince of Slavic folklore, the Ivan or Alexei who rides out to win the firebird, rescue Maria Morevna, or outwit the Baba Yaga. The capital T marks the storybook prince as much as the courtier's heir.

Core meaning in detail: Strictly, tsarevitch joins tsar with the patronymic suffix -evich, meaning son of, so the word literally declares its Bearer [REDACTED] father: the tsar's son. Russian usage distinguished the tsarevich, any tsar's son, from the cesarevich or naslednik, the formally designated heir apparent, especially after Emperor Paul's succession laws of 1797; English writers have often blurred the two. The last and most famous Bearer [REDACTED] the title was Alexei Nikolaevich, the haemophiliac heir of Nicholas II, whose illness and murder in 1918 closed the word's living history.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A tsar's son, an imperial prince (primary historical sense): the boys of the Kremlin and the Winter Palace, addressed and chronicled as tsarevitch from Muscovy to 1917. The bridge is patronymic grammar: tsar plus -evich equals the son of the tsar.
  • The fairytale prince (folkloric sense, as in the source): Tsarevitch Alexei or Ivan Tsarevich, the wonder-tale hero who quests beyond the thrice-nine lands. The bridge is typecasting: real court titles settle onto the stock hero of the skazki, so every youngest son rides out as a tsarevitch.
  • The designated heir, loosely (extended English usage): newspapers once called any Russian crown prince the tsarevitch, conflating tsarevich with cesarevich, the formal heir's style. The bridge is foreign imprecision: English reached for one romantic word where Russian kept two careful ones.
  • A figure of doomed innocence (modern cultural sense): since 1918 the word summons Alexei Nikolaevich, the sick boy in the sailor suit, and with him the whole murdered household at Ekaterinburg. The bridge is tragedy: history's last tsarevitch recolours all earlier ones with elegy.
  • Figurative pampered heir (journalistic metaphor): business or political dynasties are mocked as tsarevitches, princelings raised to inherit power they never earned. The bridge carries autocracy into the boardroom: any sheltered successor is a little tsar's son.
  • Spelling-variant echo of caesar (etymological shadow sense): every tsarevitch carries Julius Caesar in his name at five removes, through Gothic and Slavonic. The bridge is the long chain itself: the Roman dictator's family name became the Slavonic word for emperor, then for emperor's son.

Register and connotation: Tsarevitch with its capital is romance and elegy together: onion domes, wolf hunts, Fabergé eggs, and the shadow of the cellar at Ekaterinburg. Historians prefer the transliteration tsarevich, while storybooks keep tsarevitch with its French-looking -tch, the spelling that smells of illustrated gift-books. Lowercased it can tease; capitalised it mourns or marvels, and it never sounds merely administrative the way heir apparent does.
