GHETTO (capitalised in the source sentence from Oscar Wilde's The Happy Prince — "He passed over the Ghetto, and saw the old Jews bargaining with each other, and weighing out money in copper scales" — because Wilde means the historic Jewish quarter of the city) is a noun with a precise, dark, and much-debated history. In its primary historical sense it names the quarter of a city to which Jews were legally confined, beginning with the Venetian Ghetto of 1516; in its extended modern senses it names any district to which a minority group is segregated, and then, loosely and often offensively, any poor, rundown, or ethnically distinct urban neighbourhood.


Primary senses.



  • The Venetian institution (1516): On 29 March 1516 the Venetian Senate ordered the Jews of the city to dwell together on the island of the Ghetto Nuovo — so called because the island had been the site of the city's copper foundry (ghèto in Venetian dialect, "foundry; casting house"). The quarter was walled, its gates locked at night, and its residents had to pay the Christian owners of the surrounding houses for the privilege of being shut in. Within a few years of Venetian usage the place-name became a common noun: "ghetto" meant "the Jewish quarter" in Italian, and from Italian it travelled into every major European language.

  • The European Jewish quarter (16th–19th centuries): Papal Rome (1555, in the Sant'Angelo quarter beside the Tiber), Frankfurt (the Judengasse), Prague (the Jewish Town), and dozens of other cities copied the Venetian model — walled or gated Jewish quarters that survived into the nineteenth century and, in Rome, until 1870. In this sense the ghetto was simultaneously an instrument of religious segregation and a space in which Jewish communal life — synagogues, study, commerce, festivals — flourished and defined itself for centuries.

  • The Nazi ghettos (1939–1944): The German occupation of Poland and eastern Europe revived the word with genocidal force: the Warsaw Ghetto of October 1940, the largest, holding more than 400,000 people behind eight-foot walls, and those of Łódź, Kraków, Lublin, and hundreds of other towns were starvation-enforced holding pens on the road to extermination. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943 gave the word its fiercest heroic association.


Twentieth-century American extension.



  • From the 1920s onward, American sociologists and journalists applied "ghetto" to the segregated urban Black districts of the North — Chicago's Black Belt, Harlem, Detroit's Paradise Valley. Louis Wirth's The Ghetto (1928), St. Clair Drake and Horace Cayton's Black Metropolis (1945), and Kenneth B. Clark's Dark Ghetto (1965) made the term a standard analytical category for imposed racial segregation and for the structural racism that sustains it.

  • In contemporary usage "the ghetto" (or "the inner city") denotes high-poverty, minority-concentrated neighbourhoods shaped by redlining, disinvestment, and mass incarceration; the word is used analytically by historians and planners, and controversially by everyone else.


Slang, reclamation, and today's sensitivity. In African-American vernacular and hip-hop culture the word has a second life of defiant self-identification — "ghetto fabulous", "ghetto blaster", "ghetto chic" — negotiating pride and critique at once; simultaneously, the white slang sense, "ghetto" meaning "cheap, broken, improvised, low-class" (as in "that's so ghetto"), has been widely condemned as racist and offensive and is now avoided in careful speech. The semantic bridge running through every sense is that a ghetto is a place whose residents did not choose: confinement by others, whether by founding decree, papal bull, Nazi order, or redlining map. Every later sense carries that ingredient of imposed enclosure, which is what separates the word from mere "quarter" or "slum".
