I. The Indo-European root: projecting and threatening

The verb menace and the noun of the same form descend, through Latin and French, from the Proto-Indo-European root *men- ("to project, to protrude, to stand out"), the same root that gave Latin mons, montis ("mountain") - a mountain being literally something that juts upward. From this idea of "protrusion" Latin built a remarkable family of words around the notion of something that sticks out toward you, and therefore threatens you: minae ("threats"; also "projecting points, jutting rocks, spear-tips"), mināx ("threatening, overhanging"), minārī ("to threaten; to jut out, to overhang"), and the derived abstract mināciae ("threats").

II. From Latin to French

Classical Latin mināciae (plural) "threats" was re-analysed as a singular feminine noun in Vulgar Latin - *minācia "threat" - and passed into Old French as menace (with the verb menacer). Old French carried both the concrete idea of a spoken threat and the more physical idea of something looming or overhanging. The word crossed the Channel with the Norman Conquest: Middle English menace is attested from the early 13th century (in the South English Legendary and other texts), used both as noun ("a threat") and verb ("to threaten"). Chaucer uses the verb, and the word is common in Middle English religious and legal prose, where the menaces of God and of the law threaten sinners and criminals.

III. Early Modern English

In the 16th and 17th centuries "menace" is a formal, strong word. Shakespeare uses it sparingly but memorably: "menace" appears in the history plays with legal and martial weight. The noun could still mean a spoken threat or a looming danger. The King James Bible (1611) uses "menace" as a verb in the prophetic books, speaking of the menaces of enemies. Throughout this period the word is gravid, formal, slightly archaic-sounding even then - its French clothing and Latin bones made it a "learned" word beside the native "threat".

IV. The 19th century: the public menace

The decisive modern shift comes in the 19th century, when journalism and the new urban press seize on the word to name social dangers: the "public menace", the "menace of cholera", the "menace of immigration", the "menace to society". The noun moves from "a threat uttered" to "a threat embodied" - a person, disease, or institution that threatens the community. This is the sense most alive today: a "menace" need never have said a word; it merely needs to be dangerous. The same century gave the verb its cinematic habit of attaching to landscapes and weather ("the cliffs menace the channel"), a sense already latent in the Latin idea of overhanging rocks.

V. The 20th-21st centuries: menace in culture

Modern culture has made "menace" almost a genre word. In film and fiction the villain is "menacing"; the sky before a storm is "menacing"; the score of a thriller is "menacing". The word's emotional range splits in two directions: (a) serious and grand - political menace, nuclear menace, the menace of climate change; (b) playful and domestic - "he's a little menace" of a mischievous child, the "menace" of weeds in a garden. The 1993 film "Menace II Society" fixed the word in the lexicon of American street cinema. Meanwhile the verb has become increasingly literary and formal, outliving its colloquial competitors: in everyday speech one is more likely to say "threaten" than "menace", but the noun "menace" is fully alive, and the adjective "menacing" is a mainstay of narrative prose in English worldwide.

VI. Summary of drift

  • Threat uttered (Middle English) - speech act, formal register.
  • Threat embodied / looming danger (19th c.) - person, disease, storm; gravitas increases.
  • Dangerous or pestilent thing (19th-20th c.) - "public menace", "menace to society"; moral-panic colouring.
  • Atmospheric menace (20th c.) - menacing mood, menacing villain; cinematic, literary.
  • Playful nuisance (20th c.) - "little menace"; comic, affectionate.

Tolkien's "a dire menace even in his sleep" sits exactly in the middle of this drift: the dragon is a living threat embodied - the menace needs no words, only its sleeping, dangerous presence.
