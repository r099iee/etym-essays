Aristotle is the English name of the Greek philosopher Ἀριστοτέλης (Aristotelēs, 384–322 BCE), a pupil of Plato, tutor of Alexander the Great and founder of the Lyceum. In the source, Donald Bryant places him with Quintilian after Cicero among the sources of the rhetorical tradition that shaped the Renaissance and the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The name denotes a thinker of encyclopaedic range — logic, physics, metaphysics, biology, ethics, politics, poetics and rhetoric — and, in this reading, specifically the author of the Rhetoric, the first systematic analysis of persuasion.

Life. Aristotle was born at Stagira in Chalcidice, the son of Nicomachus, court physician to Amyntas III of Macedon. He entered Plato’s Academy at Athens around 367 BCE and remained for some twenty years, until Plato’s death in 347. He then travelled to Assos in Asia Minor, to the court of Hermias, whose niece and adopted daughter Pythias he married, and afterwards to Lesbos, where he pursued the biological researches that remained a lifelong passion. In 343 he was summoned to Macedon to tutor the young Alexander. He returned to Athens in 335 and founded his own school in the Lyceum, a gymnasium with a covered walk, peripatos, from which his followers took the name Peripatetics. After Alexander’s death in 323, anti-Macedonian feeling drove him from Athens; he died at Chalcis in Euboea in 322.

The corpus. What survives is largely the internal material of the school — lecture notes and treatises — rather than the polished dialogues that made his reputation in antiquity.

  • Logic: the Organon, including the Categories, De Interpretatione, Prior and Posterior Analytics, Topics and Sophistical Refutations, which gave Europe the theory of the syllogism.
  • Natural philosophy: the Physics, De Caelo, De Generatione et Corruptione, the meteorological and biological works, with the four causes and the distinction between potentiality and actuality.
  • Metaphysics: the inquiry into being as being, substance, form and matter, and the unmoved mover.
  • Ethics and politics: the Nicomachean Ethics with its account of eudaimonia, virtue as a mean and practical wisdom; the Politics with its claim that man is by nature a political animal.
  • Rhetoric and poetics: the Rhetoric in three books and the Poetics, the foundation of Western criticism.

Aristotle on rhetoric. The Rhetoric defines the art as the faculty of observing, in each case, the available means of persuasion. It analyses the three artistic proofs — ethos (the speaker’s character), pathos (the audience’s emotion) and logos (the argument itself) — and the three genres of speech: deliberative, judicial and epideictic. Its central instrument is the enthymeme, the rhetorical syllogism whose premises the audience supplies. Where Cicero and Quintilian teach the orator how to be trained and how to speak, Aristotle asks what persuasion is and why it works; that is why the Renaissance could treat him as the philosophical foundation beneath the Roman curriculum.

The name. Ἀριστοτέλης is a two-element Greek compound. The first element is ἄριστος (aristos), “best”, the superlative of ἀγαθός (agathos), “good”; the second is τέλος (telos), “end, purpose, completion”. The name is usually glossed “best end” or “best purpose”, but the sense of the compound is not certain, and the ancestry of both elements is debated. It was a normal aristocratic Greek name, borne by other men, not a philosophical coinage. Latin took it as Aristoteles (genitive Aristotelis), French as Aristote, and English as Aristotle in the Middle Ages.

Historical significance. Aristotle’s authority in the Middle Ages was so complete that schoolmen called him simply “the Philosopher”; Dante, meeting the ancient sages in Limbo, calls him “the master of those who know” (Inferno 4.131). Thomas Aquinas reconciled him with Christian doctrine in the thirteenth century, and the universities of Europe organised their arts curriculum around his logic, physics and ethics. The humanists of the Renaissance recovered his Greek text, retranslated him, and quarrelled with the scholastic Aristotle while absorbing his Rhetoric and Poetics into the theory of literature.

Why he matters in this reading. Bryant’s triad — Cicero, Quintilian, Aristotle — maps the three faces of the tradition: the model orator, the teacher of the art, and the philosopher who explained it. Renaissance writers on rhetoric and poetry read Aristotle’s Rhetoric in George of Trebizond’s Latin version and his Poetics in Lorenzo Valla’s, and built on them the doctrines of decorum, probability, catharsis and the unity of plot. When Nizzoli and his contemporaries argued about pure Ciceronian diction, the deeper question — what makes speech persuasive — was Aristotle’s.
