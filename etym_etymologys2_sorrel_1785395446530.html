SORREL enters English from French, but its story begins deep in Indo-European as a taste-word, and it is a doublet of the native English adjective sour — two English words, sour and sorrel, descending independently from the same Proto-Indo-European root. CHAIN (French route, the ancestry of SORREL): PIE *sūro- "sour, bitter, salty" (c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; the root is attested in many branches — see note) → Proto-Germanic *sūraz "sour, acidic, tart" (c. 1st c. CE; native English sour and German sauer descend from this) → Frankish *sūr "sour" (5th–7th c. CE; a West Germanic form carried by the Franks into Gaul) → (loan into Gallo-Romance) Old French sur / sor "sour, sharp-tasting, acidic" (9th–12th c. CE) → Old French surele / sorele "the little sour one" (sur + diminutive suffix -elle; 12th–13th c. CE) — coined as the plant-name for the acid-leaved herb → Anglo-French sorel → Middle English sorel (also sorell, sourel; attested c. 1400) "the sour dock-leaved herb" → English SORREL (modern form, stable since the 16th c.) NOTE ON DEBATE AND SISTER FORMS: The PIE root *sūro- is reconstructed with some confidence but with a wider meaning range than English "sour": sister reflexes include Lithuanian sūrus "salty", Latvian sūrs "bitter, salty", and Old Church Slavonic syrъ "raw, damp, uncooked" (Russian сырой syrój "raw"), showing that the root's core sense was a sharp or raw quality of taste, which each branch then pinned down differently. Within Germanic, the simplex *sūraz produced the native English doublet sour (Old English sūr) — so sorrel and sour are ultimately the same word twice over. The plant-name itself is a Romance coinage: Roman botanical Latin had used lapathum (a Greek loan) for the dock, but French speakers renamed the plant by its taste, making a diminutive of sur; the modern French name oseille, however, is unrelated, coming from a Celtic *os- "dock". All stages of the chain above are attested except the reconstructed PIE and Proto-Germanic forms.
