Cennino (/tʃɛˈniː.nəʊ/, Italian: [tʃenˈniːno]) is an Italian historical proper noun and art-historical archetype referring specifically to Cennino d'Andrea Cennini (c. 1360 – before 1427), the late medieval Florentine painter and author of Il Libro dell'Arte (The Craftsman's Handbook). Within the discipline of art history, the name stands as the supreme historical metonym for the living material culture, artisanal studio recipes, and apprentice traditions that bridged the late Gothic Trecento with the dawn of the Italian Renaissance.

The significance of Cennino extends across several crucial dimensions of historical and theoretical inquiry:

  • The Direct Lineage of Giotto: Cennino represents the vital, unbroken technical link to Giotto di Bondone, the founder of Western naturalistic painting. In the opening chapters of his treatise, Cennino proudly traces his artistic pedigree: he trained for twelve years under Agnolo Gaddi, who had studied under his father Taddeo Gaddi, Giotto's godson and direct workshop assistant of twenty-four years. Cennino thus transcribed the actual studio practices, chemical recipes, and drawing methods developed in Giotto's revolutionary Florentine workshop.
  • Author of Il Libro dell'Arte: Composed in Padua around 1390–1400, Cennino's treatise is the earliest surviving vernacular craft manual dedicated systematically to painting. Unlike earlier monastic recipe compendia (such as the Schedula diversarum artium of Theophilus), Cennino's manual was written in Tuscan vernacular for secular workshop practitioners. It covers every stage of the artist's training: from sharpening quill pens and making charcoal to grinding pigments on porphyry slabs, preparing gesso panels, laying gold leaf with Armenian bole, and executing true fresco (*buon fresco*) upon wet lime plaster.
  • Philosophical Elevation of the Artist: Cennino provided the philosophical catalyst that helped elevate painting from a mechanical trade (*ars mechanica*) to a liberal art (*ars liberalis*). He famously asserted that painting occupies a throne second only to poetry because both disciplines demand *fantasia* (creative imagination) and *operazione di mano* (manual execution) to give physical form to things not found directly in nature.
  • Benchmark for Conservation Science: In modern heritage science and museum conservation, the name Cennino functions as an indispensable technical standard. Conservators investigating the stratigraphy of Renaissance tempera paintings, egg-yolk emulsions, azurite underpaintings, or vermilion adulteration continually reference Cennino's specific instructions to authenticate and preserve early Italian masterpieces.
  • Metonym for Artisanal Integrity: Beyond the historical individual, *Cennino* is frequently invoked in cultural criticism, the Arts and Crafts philosophy of John Ruskin, and contemporary fine arts pedagogy as the embodiment of patient, disciplined craftsmanship, honoring the physical materials of nature against industrialized mass reproduction.

The semantic and historiographical bridge connecting Cennino's late fourteenth-century manual to modern art history lies in the nineteenth-century rediscovery of early Italian art. After remaining in manuscript obscurity for centuries, the text was rediscovered by Giuseppe Tambroni in the Vatican library in 1821. Translated into English by Mary Merrifield in 1844 and later by Daniel V. Thompson in 1933, Cennino became the philosophical touchstone for the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, who sought to purge academic painting of High Renaissance artifice by returning to the crisp clarity, devotional sincerity, and pure pigment mastery preserved in Cennino's pages.
