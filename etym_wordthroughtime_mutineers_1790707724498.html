- Late 16th Century - Entered Early Modern English (from French *mutin*) during the Elizabethan era, originally used as both an adjective ('mutine') and a noun for rebellious soldiers or sailors.
- 17th Century - Morphologically stabilized with the agent suffix *-eer* (forming *mutineer*); codified in maritime and military law (such as the Articles of War and the British Mutiny Act of 1689) as the most heinous capital military crime.
- 18th–19th Century - Gained iconic status in popular sea literature, maritime history, and drama (immortalized by Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe*, the historical *Mutiny on the Bounty* in 1789, and Robert Louis Stevenson's *Treasure Island*), embodying lawless nautical treason.
- 20th–21st Century - Retained its strict military/naval legal definition while broadening into a common corporate and political metaphor for rebellious team members, dissenting factional politicians, or boardroom conspirators attempting to oust a leader.
