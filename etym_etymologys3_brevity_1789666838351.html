The evolution of brevity spans three millennia from a Proto-Indo-European adjective for physical shortness to a modern literary ideal. Its ultimate ancestor is PIE *mregh-u-, also written *mreghu-, *mregh-ew-, meaning short. Pokorny (s.v. mreghu-) reconstructs it on the basis of a strikingly consistent set: Latin brevis short, Greek brakhys short, broad? (with metathesis of *mr- → br-), Old Irish berr short? (debated), Old High German murg? brief? (obsolete, short in time), Avestan mərəzu- short? The root is one of the few where Latin initial br- regularly reflects PIE *mr-, via Proto-Italic *mregwh-u- with labialization and r-metathesis. De Vaan (s.v. brevis) accepts this derivation: Proto-Italic *mregu-i-? from PIE *mregh-u- short, noting Greek brakhys and the characteristic Latin development *mr- to br-. Beekes (s.v. brakhys) concurs for the Greek side, deriving brakhys from *mrghu- with vocalic r. The reconstruction is therefore unusually secure, though details of the u-stem inflection and the exact laryngeal or aspirate (*gh vs *gwh) are debated in Indo-Europeanist literature — a nuance noted here rather than smoothed over.

In Latin, brevis (adjective, third declension: brevis, breve, gen. brevis) meant short, little, low, shallow, brief in space, time, and speech. It opposed longus. From it the Romans derived a rich family: brevitas, -atis (noun) shortness, brevity; breviare to shorten, abbreviate (source of English abbreviate, abridge); breviarium summary, compendium (source of breviary); breviter briefly; breviculus rather short. Brevitas itself is classical, attested in Cicero (brevitas dicendi brevity of speech), Quintilian's rhetorical theory (brevitas as a virtue of narration alongside clarity and probability), and Seneca. Already in antiquity brevity is both a physical fact and a stylistic norm.

  • Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Italic (c. 3000–1000 BCE): PIE *mregh-u- short → Proto-Italic *mregwh-u-? short. Sound shifts: syllabic *r vocalizes, labiovelar conditions survive, initial *mr- preserved. Cognates diverge: Greek *mrakhu- → brakhys via metathesis; Germanic *murgja-? survives marginally; Italic branch heads toward brevis.
  • Latin (c. 200 BCE–400 CE): Proto-Italic *mregw- → Old Latin *bregw- → Classical brevis via *mr- → br- and vowel weakening. The change *mr- to br- is regular in Latin (cf. *mrots? but brevis is the textbook example). Brevitas formed with abstract suffix -tas, -tatis (as gravitas from gravis). Pronounced bre-wi-tas with /w/. Meaning spans space, time, discourse. Quintilian institutionalizes brevitas as rhetoric.
  • Late and Medieval Latin to Old French (c. 400–1300): brevis → Old French brief, bref (masc.), breve/brieve (fem.), with loss of final -s voicing and diphthongization; brevitas, accusative brevitatem → Old French brieveté, breveté shortness. Anglo-Norman breveté carries the learned abstract into England. Phonetics: Latin /breːwitas/ → Gallo-Romance /brevitet/ → OF /briefté/ with stress shift to final syllable.
  • Middle English (c. 1400): brevity borrowed c. 1400 as brevite, brevyte shortness, briefness, from Anglo-French breveté and directly from Latin brevitatem (double etymology typical of Middle English learned words). Earliest uses denote both textual shortness and temporal briefness. Brief (adj.) enters slightly earlier from OF brief, establishing the adjective-noun pair. Competing forms brevitude and briefness coexist briefly; brevity wins as the prestige Latinate form.
  • Early Modern to Modern (1500–present): Shakespeare fixes the proverb (Polonius, Hamlet II.ii: brevity is the soul of wit, 1603), making brevity a household stylistic ideal. The 18th century cultivates it (Swift, Pope, Johnson's spirited brevity). Legal, military, and journalistic brevity codes of the 19th–20th centuries technicize it. Character-limited media (telegrams: brevity is economy; Twitter: brevity is form) complete its migration from virtue to infrastructure. Pronunciation stabilizes as /ˈbrevɪti/ with TRAP-to-DRESS? Actually DRESS vowel in first syllable, short i in second, in RP.

Semantic drift is conservative: the core shortness never wavers, but its prestige oscillates. Rome praised brevitas against Asiatic copiousness; the Middle Ages sometimes distrusted it as obscurity; Humanism restored it; plain-style science (Royal Society, 1660s: native easiness, brevity) mandated it; Romanticism rebelled toward sublimity; Modernism (Hemingway, Baker) re-embraced it as honesty. Baker's unafraid brevity thus stands in a 2,000-year debate in which brevity is alternately clarity and poverty — and Baker votes, with J. A. Baker of The Peregrine, for clarity as courage.

A final philological note: the doublet brief/breve/brevity/abbreviate/breviary all descend from the same brevis, illustrating English's layered borrowing: brief via French (popular), brevity via Anglo-French/Latin (learned), abbreviate via Latin past participle (Renaissance), breviary via ecclesiastical compendium. The family is a stratigraphy of English itself.
