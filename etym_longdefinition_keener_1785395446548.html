Keener is the comparative degree of the adjective keen, formed with the native English comparative suffix -er; it means more acute, more sharp, more highly developed, or more eager, and is used above all of a physical sense such as smell, sight, or hearing, and by extension of mental perception. In The Hobbit, Gandalf warns the company that the Wargs 'smell keener than goblins': the word names a predatory, almost super-canine acuity — not merely good, but heightened, instinctive, and threatening, a sensory edge that works even without sight, since the wolves 'do not need to see you to catch you.' The comparative suffix is doing precise grammatical work: it converts an impression of quality into a measurable, graded superiority, a power that outstrips even the goblins' notorious senses. The tone is folk-warning — cool, practical menace rather than open horror — perfectly matched to the storybook register of the scene, in which an experienced guide recites an ordinary fact of the wild and thereby makes the darkness far more frightening than any shouted alarm.

Primary senses (polysemy). The full semantic range of keener inherits the whole range of its base keen and can be organised into these strands:

  • Sensory acuity — sharper, more finely developed senses, especially smell, sight, and hearing: a keener nose, keener eyesight, keener hearing. This is the sense in the Tolkien context and the oldest recorded application of the base adjective to the senses in English.
  • Physical sharpness — a keener edge or point, a blade more finely honed: a keener knife. Here the word stays close to its literal 'cutting' meaning.
  • Mental acuity — a keener intellect, keener wits, a more penetrating mind; the metaphoric transfer from blade to mind, 'cutting' through a problem.
  • Eagerness and enthusiasm — more eager, more intensely interested, more zealous: keener to begin, a keener interest, keener on the idea. This is the modern sense of appetite and willingness, at home in everyday speech.
  • Sharp, biting cold or pain — a keener wind, a keener frost, a keener pang; the sense of piercing severity transferred to weather and sensation.
  • Intense competition or prices — keener competition, keener prices (i.e., more sharply cut, lower); commercial usage of the later modern period.
  • Noun keener (chiefly Canadian and school slang) — an over-enthusiastic person, a 'teacher's pet', an eager student who tries too hard; a 20th-century nominal use derived from the 'eager' sense.

Historical bridges. The oldest English sense of the base keen (Old English cēne) was 'bold, brave, fierce', a moral word for warriors; from 'bold, fierce' the adjective moved to 'sharp, cutting, severe' in Middle English, applied to blades, winds, and pains; from physical sharpness it moved to the sharpened senses and then to a sharpened or quickened mind; and from sharpened appetite it reached modern 'eager, enthusiastic'. The comparative keener simply grades each of these strands: it can sharpen a blade, a nose, a mind, or a desire, and in the Hobbit it sharpens the wolves' sense of smell into a graded, measurable superiority over the goblins themselves.
