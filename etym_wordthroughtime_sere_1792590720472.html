- [Old English (c. 8th–11th c.)] - recorded as sēar, describing dry timber, sapless woodland vegetation, and parched soil in heroic and natural descriptions - [perception: viewed as a physical, pragmatic description of lifeless, dry organic matter]
- [Middle English (12th–15th c.)] - evolved into seer / sere in forestry laws and scholastic texts, distinguishing dry gatherable firewood (sere-wood) from living royal timber - [perception: recognized both as an everyday technical term in rural forestry and an emblem of mortal decay]
- [Early Modern English (16th–17th c.)] - elevated to immortal poetic prominence by Shakespeare in Macbeth ("the sere, the yellow leaf") and Milton in Lycidas, transforming the drying of autumn leaves into the ultimate metaphor for human aging and emotional desolation - [perception: revered as a sublime literary trope for the autumn of life and the tragic loss of vitality]
- [19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] - championed by Byron, Shelley, Keats, and Edgar Allan Poe (Ulalume) as a quintessential gothic and romantic marker of melancholic landscapes, ruined abbeys, and exhausted passions - [perception: celebrated as an evocative, antique aesthetic term laden with bittersweet nostalgia and sorrow]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] - preserved almost exclusively in poetic registers, high literary prose, and ecological scholarship, recognized by educated readers as an exquisite archaic descriptor of dry autumn leaves and fading vitality - [perception: admired for its evocative sonic crispness and classical poetic resonance]
