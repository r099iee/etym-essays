Lures are objects, sounds, promises, or appearances used to attract, tempt, or deceive a person or animal into a desired place or action, originally and concretely a falconer's decoy of leather and meat swung on a thong, and in the source sentence a class of primitive hunting devices displayed in museums alongside bolas, traps, and slings.

The primary modern sense splits into concrete and abstract branches that share the logic of concealed purpose. A fishing lure is a spoon, plug, spinner, or soft plastic made to mimic prey; a shop-window lure is a discount or display; the lure of adventure is an imagined reward. In each case something desirable is staged to pull the target, and a hook, price, or risk is hidden behind it. The plural lures emphasises variety and repetition: many devices, many temptations.

  • Hunting and fishing sense: artificial bait mimicking fish, birds, or mammals. Angling distinguishes natural bait from artificial lures, the latter reusable and designed for movement, colour, and vibration. Duck decoys and falconry pads are historical lures.
  • Trap-associated sense in source: primitive lures as scent, sound, or visual decoys used with traps and nets to bring beasts within spear range. This preserves the oldest concrete meaning.
  • Figurative temptation sense: the lure of money, fame, the city, or exotic travel. Here the word names both the attractive quality and the act of enticing, as in the lure proved irresistible.
  • Marketing and tourism sense: loss-leaders, clickbait headlines, and resort brochures described as lures. The commercial register is mildly cynical, implying manipulation.
  • Sexual and social sense: charms, glances, or promises used to seduce. Literature speaks of amorous lures, keeping the hunting metaphor of prey.
  • Verb background: to lure is to entice with a lure, covering coaxing, decoying, and inveigling. Adjectives luring and alluring derive from it.

Grammatically lures is a regular count plural: one lure, many lures. It collocates with verbs set, lay, dangle, offer, resist, and fall for, and with adjectives irresistible, false, glittering, and siren. The siren collocation recalls Greek myth and shows how deeply the deception nuance runs.

Domain usages include angling catalogues with fine distinctions among topwater, diving, and jigging lures; conservation debates about lead lures poisoning loons; psychology of persuasion where lure names an incentive masking cost; and criminology where a lure draws victims, as in child-safety warnings. Literary nuance is quietly moralising: to call something a lure is already to warn that attraction may be a trap, unlike neutral synonyms such as attraction or invitation. That warning tone fits the source passage, where lures are instruments in a war of species rather than innocent toys.

Polysemy thus radiates from leather strap to decoy to temptation to marketing device. The historical bridge is falconry generalisation: once any bait that recalls a hawk could be called a lure, any bait for fish, then any enticement for humans, followed naturally. Each extension keeps the double structure of visible charm and hidden capture.
