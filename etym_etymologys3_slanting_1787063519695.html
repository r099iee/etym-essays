The history of slanting is the history of a small family of Germanic slip-and-slope verbs, and it is one of the few words of English whose origin is genuinely clouded. The earliest securely attested member of the family is the Middle English verb slenten to slope, to slip or glide sideways, first recorded around 1300. Slenten was a northern and Midlands dialect verb, and its existence in Middle English points to a Scandinavian source: the Old Norse sletta (recorded with meanings such as to throw carelessly, to cause to slip) and a reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *slintaną to slope, to slip are the most widely accepted ancestors. Some dictionaries connect these forms ultimately to a Proto-Indo-European root *slend- to slide, slip, which would place the word in the same great Indo-European family as slide, slip, sled, and slush. Yet the chain is nowhere fully certain: the PIE stage is reconstructed rather than attested, and alternative proposals derive the word from a base meaning to hang loosely or from a northern English sense of to stumble. Honest etymologies mark the origin as uncertain rather than pretending to certainty.

The crucial development comes in the early sixteenth century. Around 1520 the modern form slant appears, originally as a verb with the meaning to strike or fall obliquely — a development out of the older slip-sideways sense, in which the idea of a diagonal line of motion became dominant. In 1650 the noun slant a slope, an oblique direction is attested, and from the noun the participial adjective slanting leaning at an angle follows naturally. The adjective is thus a later derivative: while slenten was a verb of motion, slanting (like sloping and leaning) grammaticalises the participle to describe a static quality of orientation as well as an oblique trajectory.

Once established, slanting spread across descriptive registers. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries it was used of terrain, buildings, and natural features: a slanting hill, a slanting roof, slanting rays of sun. Its use for light is particularly old and persistent, since light arriving at a low angle is a conspicuous physical phenomenon of morning and evening. Landscape poets of the eighteenth century made slanting ray a fixed collocation, and the usage remained a staple of descriptive prose through the nineteenth century and into the twentieth — a lineage that reaches Tolkien's The Hobbit, where the Company rides slanting away from the River Running. In the nineteenth century the word also attached itself to penmanship, where a slanting hand described cursive sloping across the page.

The word's social history is more interesting in the noun. In the nineteenth century, slant developed an American sense of a chance, a try (to have a slant at something). In the twentieth century it acquired the crucial figurative sense of a point of view or bias, especially in journalism: to slant a news story is to present it from a prejudiced angle, and a slant is a particular take or interpretation. This rhetorical sense is a direct metaphorical extension of the physical diagonal: a straight report is even-handed and balanced, while a slanted one is tilted off the perpendicular of truth. The participial adjective has largely remained loyal to the physical sense, but it participates in the same figurative space — a slanting perspective is one that departs from a neutral straight-on view. Regrettably, the same shape-language produced an offensive use of slant as a slur for East Asian people, from the perceived angle of the eyes; that usage is abhorrent and obsolete in civilised speech.

Two further notes complete the picture. First, the adjective has always been more conservative than the noun: slanting has never acquired the strongly negative rhetorical charge that slanted now carries, and it remains the word a writer reaches for when the geometry of a lean or a diagonal line is the whole point. Second, the word's phonetic shape has been remarkably stable: the vowel reflects the Middle English and Scandinavian a before n plus consonant, and the present-day pronunciation has not shifted from its sixteenth-century form. In sum, slanting illustrates how a word can pass from a dialect verb of slipping motion, through a plain adjective of geometry, to the vocabulary of perception and bias — all while retaining a strong core of physical meaning that still governs its most common uses in prose.
