bitter — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *bʰeyd- “to split, cleave, divide” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — source of Sanskrit bhindáti “splits,” Latin findō “to split.” 2. Proto-Germanic *bītaną “to bite” (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE–100 CE) — the “cleave” root specialised to the cutting of the teeth; source of Old English bītan “to bite” (attested c. 725), Gothic beitan, Old Norse bíta. 3. Proto-Germanic *bitraz “biting, sharp, harsh” (reconstructed) — adjective formed from the verbal stem *bit- with the suffix *-ra-, literally “that which bites (the tongue).” 4. Old English biter, bittor “bitter, having a biting taste; harsh, cruel” (attested c. 725 in the Corpus and Epinal glossaries; the figurative sense “painful, cruel” is present from the earliest verse). 5. Middle English biter, bitter “bitter” (12th–14th c.). 6. English bitter (modern form settled 15th–16th c.). Note: the semantic path is unusually transparent — the taste was felt as a kind of biting by the tongue, and the “biting, cutting” sense of the root then fed the emotional extension to harsh, cruel, acrimonious feeling. No stage is genuinely in dispute; only the precise PIE ablaut grade behind *bitraz (from *bʰeyd- via *bʰid-) is a matter of routine reconstruction. rabid — linear derivation chain: 1. Origin of Latin rabere “to rave, be mad” (attested in classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) is uncertain. No secure PIE root is established; some scholars posit a base *rebh- “to be violent, impetuous, rage” (reconstructed, debated), but the connection is not universally accepted and the Latin verb is best treated as of unknown ultimate origin. 2. Latin rabidus “mad, raging, furious” (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. Lucretius and Virgil) — formed from rabere “to rave, be mad” with the adjective suffix -idus; the same family yields rabiēs “madness, rage, rabies” (attested 1st c. BCE) and rabiōsus “rabid.” 3. Latin rabidus → Old French rabide “rabid” (attested 14th–16th c.) → English rabid “furious, raging” (borrowed 17th c., first attested 1610s). 4. English rabid acquires the medical sense “affected with hydrophobia/rabies” (attested 17th–18th c.) and, from the late 19th c., the dominant modern sense “fanatical, wildly intense” (a rabid supporter). 5. English rabid (all senses current; modern form unchanged since the 17th-c. borrowing). Debate note: the Latin verb rabere has no generally accepted Indo-European etymology; the proposed root *rebh- rests on thin and disputed cognates, so the chain necessarily begins at Latin with the origin explicitly flagged as unknown. The English word, by contrast, has a short, well-attested history as a direct Renaissance borrowing.
