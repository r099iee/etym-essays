Core meaning. EVADING is the present participle and gerund of the verb EVADE: slipping away from something by a side-step rather than confronting, reforming, or abolishing it head-on. The image is spatial and athletic — a dodging motion, a quiet veer past an obstacle — and it applies equally to a physical pursuer, a question, a duty, a law, or a fact one does not wish to face. As a participle it works adjectivally, as part of the progressive construction ("he was evading the question"), or as a gerund acting like a noun ("evading the convention was impracticable").

In this passage. The sentence comes from a scholarly treatise on English metre in which the author admits that his hypothetical poet or reader has been "consistently masculine" throughout — not out of conviction, but because he has "found no practicable way of evading the established convention". The verb is chosen with forensic care. Rejecting, breaking, or reforming the convention would announce open opposition; ignoring it would be dishonest, since he plainly notices it; transgressing it would court censure. Evading describes instead a quiet, pragmatic detour: the convention is so deeply embedded that every alternative is impracticable, so he gently sidesteps the problem, acknowledges the artificiality of the masculine form, and proceeds. The tone is courteous and mildly rueful — the confession of a scholar who wished the convention away but could not afford to fight it. Notice how the participle softens the admission: evading is a process without finality, ongoing, provisional, and self-aware.

Polysemy: the senses of EVADE today. The modern verb carries a wide but tightly organized range of senses, all descended from the Latin idea of "walking out of" something:

  • Physical evasion. To escape or avoid capture, pursuit, or contact by movement: a prisoner evades his guards; a hunted animal evades the hounds. This is the oldest English sense, "to escape from", and it remains vivid in military and police prose.
  • Intellectual evasion. To avoid a question, issue, or argument rather than answer it: a politician evades a journalist's question; a witness evades a direct answer. This is the dominant modern sense and the one in this passage.
  • Legal evasion. To avoid the force or application of a law, rule, or obligation while technically staying within it: tax evasion, evading a draft, evading customs duties. Here the word acquires its strongest moral shadow — lawful on its face, unlawful in spirit.
  • Duty evasion. To shirk a responsibility or obligation: evading one's duties, evading responsibility for an error. Often, though not always, cowardly.
  • Technical and technological jargon. In engineering and military technology, evasion means a system or manoeuvre designed to escape detection or interception: radar evasion, electronic evasion, evasive manoeuvres, evasion of a missile lock. The nouns evasiveness and evasion and the adjective evasive ("an evasive answer") extend the family into everyday and bureaucratic language.
  • Extended and metaphorical uses. Abstract or quasi-physical uses: "evading the issue", "evading the difficulty", "a fact that evades memory", "the meaning evades the translator". In the last of these the subject and object reverse — the thing escapes the person, stressing the difficulty of the pursuit.

Semantic bridge. All these senses share a single underlying image: moving out and away from something that presses on you. Latin ēvādere meant "to go out" (ē-, from ex-, "out of, away from", + vādere, "to go, to wade"); from literal walking-out-of-a-trap the meaning stretched to escaping danger, then to escaping an obligation, then to dodging a question. The physical walk has become, over four centuries of English use, a mental side-step. The moral colouring is variable and context-dependent: evasion is heroic when a prisoner escapes tyranny, defensible when a bad question is sidestepped, and guilty when a tax is dodged.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Avoid is neutral and blameless, the mere staying clear of something. Dodge is sudden, physical, and often sly — one dodges a blow or a question with a quick movement. Elude implies successful flight from pursuit and stresses how hard the quarry is to catch. Circumvent means going around an obstacle by clever, often devious means. Shirk means avoiding a duty one is expected to perform, with overtones of cowardice. Sidestep and skirt are informal physical metaphors for slipping past the edge of a thing. Evade sits between them all: it suggests skill and a small moral ambiguity, but it can be perfectly legitimate — as here, where the evasion is a matter of stylistic convention rather than of principle or guilt.

Grammar, register, collocation. Evade is transitive and takes direct objects such as question, issue, difficulty, duty, responsibility, capture, detection, tax, draft, punishment, and the law. Common collocations: "evade a question", "evade capture", "tax evasion", "evasive answer", "evading the issue". Register: standard in formal and neutral prose, common in journalism, law, and official writing; "evading" as a participle keeps a faintly formal, precise tone that suits this scholarly sentence exactly.
