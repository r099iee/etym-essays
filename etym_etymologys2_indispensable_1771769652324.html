INDISPENSABLE is built on Latin dispendere/dispensare "to weigh out, to distribute," itself from the PIE root *spend- (with s-mobile, *spend-) "to pull, to stretch; to make an offering," though the immediate ancestor is the Latin verb. The chain runs from the PIE root of stretching/weighing through Latin administration to the English adjective. Chain: 1. PIE *(s)pend- "to pull, to stretch" (reconstructed; attested in Greek spandein "to pour a libation, to make an offering," Latin pendere "to weigh, to pay," Gothic *spinnan; the meaning "to pull taut" underlies the sense "to weigh by suspension"; per LIV, de Vaan) — the root of stretching and weighing. 2. Latin pendere "to hang, to weigh" (attested from early Latin, 3rd–2nd c. BCE; the verb "to weigh" is a specialization of "to hang," since early balances weighed by suspending) — the simple verb. 3. Latin dispensare "to weigh out, to distribute, to administer" (frequentative of dispendere "to weigh out," from dis- "apart" + pendere "to weigh"; attested classical, e.g. in administrative and household contexts, 1st c. BCE) — "to weigh out and hand around," hence "to administer, to manage, to dole out." 4. Late Latin dispensabilis "that can be weighed out, that may be dispensed, allowable" (adjective in -abilis formed on dispensare; attested in medieval Latin and canon law, 6th–12th c.) — "able to be done without, excusable." 5. Medieval Latin indispensabilis "not to be dispensed with, that cannot be excused or omitted" (in- privative + dispensabilis; attested in medieval scholastic and canon-law Latin, 12th–14th c.) — the negative formation that gives the English word its meaning. 6. English indispensable "that cannot be dispensed with, absolutely necessary" (borrowed from Medieval Latin indispensabilis or formed on dispensable; first attested 1530s–1540s, e.g. in religious and moral prose) — with the moral and practical senses developing together. 7. Modern English INDISPENSABLE (16th c. onward) — absolutely necessary, that cannot be done without; the modern practical sense dominant from the 18th–19th c. This is the final node of the chain. Note on debated stages: the reconstruction of PIE *(s)pend- is generally accepted (the Greek spandein "to pour a libation, to offer" and Latin pendere "to weigh" are standard cognates), though some scholars derive the "weighing" sense more directly from a separate root *pend- "to hang"; the distinction does not affect the attested Latin and English stages, which are secure. The formation indispensabilis in Medieval Latin is documented in the vocabulary of canon law and scholastic theology; no stage above is invented.
