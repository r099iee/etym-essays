- c. 1200–1400s: Borrowed into Middle English from Old French resne following the Norman Conquest, designating the literal leather steering straps of chivalric warhorses and hunting palfreys. - Highly associated with knightly combat, aristocratic dignity, and physical mastery.
- 1500s–1600s: Rapidly expanded into Renaissance political theory and moral philosophy as a universal metaphor for governance ('the reins of empire') and self-regulation over animal passions. - Perceived as the supreme emblem of rational leadership and ethical self-mastery.
- 1700s–1800s: Solidified in Victorian political journalism and equestrian carriage culture, alongside the emergence of classic idioms like 'free rein' and 'tight rein'. - Perceived as an everyday symbol of executive administration and vehicular control.
- Early 20th Century: Satirized in British comic literature (P.G. Wodehouse) by applying equestrian restraining vocabulary to human gluttony and domestic battles. - Appreciated for its humorous metaphorical incongruity.
- Late 20th–21st Century: Widely maintained in business, fiscal, and political discourse (to 'rein in inflation', to 'take the reins') while frequently falling victim to homophonic confusion with royal 'reign'. - Universally recognized as an indispensable metaphor for executive control and restraint.
