• roadside: Similar because both name the strip beside a travelled way; differs because roadside is modern and motoring, of verges and services, while wayside is older and more literary, of footpaths, pilgrims, and parables.
  • verge: Similar because the grass verge is exactly the physical wayside of a modern road; differs because verge is the technical term for the edging strip itself, while wayside includes its human uses: resting, begging, selling, hiding.
  • wayside inn: Similar because inns belong to the wayside world of travellers’ rest; differs because it names one building’s hospitality, while wayside names the whole margin that makes such shelter necessary.
  • pathside: Similar because both name the edge of a walked route; differs because pathside is rare and narrow, of footpaths only, while wayside covers roads, highways, and the moral metaphor built on them.
  • margin: Similar because both mean an edge beside the main channel, literal or figurative; differs because margin is abstract and general, of pages and societies, while wayside is concrete and dusty, always haunted by feet and wheels.
  • kerb: Similar because both bound the travelled surface from what lies beside it; differs because the kerb is the built stone edge of a street, while the wayside is the living strip beyond it where things grow and travellers sit.
  • lay-by: Similar because both are pauses carved out of the road’s edge; differs because a lay-by is an engineered parking pocket for vehicles, while the wayside is an unmade margin for feet, flowers, and fugitives.
  • hedgerow: Similar because in the source journey the wayside and the hedge are one tangled refuge; differs because hedgerow names the planted woody boundary itself, while wayside names the ground beside the road, hedged or open.
