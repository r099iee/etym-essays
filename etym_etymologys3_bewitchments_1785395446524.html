BEWITCHMENTS descends from one of the most debated word-families in English: the Old English nouns wicca (masculine, 'male wizard, sorcerer') and wicce (feminine, 'female witch'), attested from the ninth century in the laws and homilies of Anglo-Saxon England. Behind them linguists reconstruct a Proto-Germanic noun *wikkjaz, 'necromancer, one who works magic', whose relatives cluster in the west of the Germanic world — Old Frisian wicka 'wizard' and Middle Low German wicken 'to conjure, practise magic' — and whose ultimate Indo-European connection is disputed. The most popular theory, championed by many handbooks, derives the stem from the PIE root *weyk-, 'to set apart, consecrate', the same root that underlies the Germanic words for 'holy' — Gothic weihs, Old High German wīhen 'to consecrate', Old Norse vé 'shrine' — and, in Latin, victima, 'a sacrificial victim', the animal set apart for the gods. On this account the witch was originally one 'set apart', a person dealing in the consecrated and the forbidden, so that the same ancient root gave both 'holy' and 'witchcraft'. But honest lexicography must record the doubt: the Oxford English Dictionary marks the origin of wicca as uncertain, and the connection, while attractive, is a reconstruction rather than an attested fact. The homophonous roots *weyk- 'to bend, winnow' (Latin vices, English vetch) and *weyk- 'to overcome, be strong' (Latin vincere) are different roots entirely and must not be merged into the tree, and no Indo-Aryan cognates are attested for the witch-word — the family is western Indo-European only. The Old English pair wicca/wicce belonged to a legal and moral vocabulary: witches were prosecuted under the laws of Ælfred and his successors, and the homilist Ælfric condemned 'wiccan and wigleras' (witches and sorcerers). In Middle English the two forms collapsed into the gender-neutral wicche, 'witch, wizard, sorcerer', the direct ancestor of modern witch. English then built its verb and noun in two quick steps. In the mid-sixteenth century the intensive prefix be- (from Proto-Germanic *bi, ultimately PIE *h₁embʰi 'around, about', the same prefix as beset and bedazzle) was added to witch to form bewitch, 'to bind or hold completely by magic'; within a generation the verb had developed its famous figurative sense of irresistible attraction, so that beauty and delight could bewitch the beholder. A generation later still, the abstract noun bewitchment was formed by adding the suffix -ment (from Latin -mentum by way of Old French), which converts verbs into nouns of result or instrument: an act of bewitching, or the state of being bewitched. The plural BEWITCHMENTS, the form used here, is simply the count plural, denoting a collection or repertoire of such acts — exactly the 'special study' of Gandalf. The cultural life of the word tracks the history of European attitudes to magic:
  • Anglo-Saxon and medieval period — wicca/wicce are legal and homiletic categories; the 'craft' is feared, punished, and moralised against; the word is genuinely dangerous.
  • Early modern witch-hunts — bewitchment becomes the standard charge in trials (bewitching children, cattle, milk); 'witch' and 'bewitch' acquire their darkest register in the pamphlets and statutes of the 16th-17th centuries.
  • Renaissance figurative turn — poetry discovers that love and delight 'bewitch' the senses; Shakespeare's works are full of bewitched eyes and ears, and the word gains a pleasing, seductive half.
  • 18th-19th century folklore — the literal sense recedes into fairy tale, romance, and Gothic fiction; 'bewitching' survives mainly as praise, the pleasing sense largely crowding out the fearful one.
  • 20th century fantasy revival — Tolkien, a professional philologist, restores the older, stately register; 'bewitchments' in The Hobbit is chosen for its archaic dignity and its dry comedy, the greatest wizard of the age deadpanning his study of fireworks.
Sound and shape also tell a story. The initial /w/ and the back vowel of witch have remained stable for more than a thousand years, but the word has grown: from the one-syllable Germanic wicce to the four-syllable abstract plural BEWITCHMENTS, English has progressively elaborated the humble witch into a formal noun of art. In modern usage the word is chiefly literary — at home in fantasy, folklore, and period fiction — and it retains the full dyadic force that has defined it since Anglo-Saxon times: every bewitchment still needs a charmer and a charmed.
