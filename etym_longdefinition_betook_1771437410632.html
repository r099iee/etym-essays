Betook is the past tense of betake, "to go to, to repair to, to commit oneself to," and in this literary context it describes Yu-cun's deliberate progress to the gate of the powerful Rong mansion. The word is doing far more than the plain verb "went": to betake oneself is to take oneself — to gather up one's whole person and direct it toward a goal — and the construction is explicitly reflexive (betook himself), so the sentence presents Yu-cun as simultaneously the agent and the object of his own motion. The preceding detail, "dressed in his best," completes the picture: he has prepared himself, physically and socially, and the formal, slightly archaic verb matches the formality of the preparation. The tone is dignified and faintly ceremonious, the vocabulary of a narrator who wishes to show a man bracing himself for an important social encounter rather than merely strolling toward one. Because betake survives today chiefly in literary and deliberately old-fashioned English, its use here also adds a layer of historical distance and gravity, marking the passage as narration in an elevated register.

The word is polysemous across the two verbs it inherits, and each sense traces the compound's history from "hand over" to "commit oneself":

  • Sense 1 — went to, repaired to (the literary sense on the card). "He betook himself to his chambers," "they betook themselves to the hills"; the reflexive construction is idiomatic, and the past form betook seals the resolution as a completed deed.
  • Sense 2 — committed oneself to a course of action or activity. To betake oneself to study, to flight, to arms — the sense shades from physical motion into moral resolve: gathering one's energies and devoting them to a purpose.
  • Sense 3 — to commit, hand over, entrust (of things; 15th century, now archaic). One betakes a message, a task, or a person into the care of another; this is the earliest sense of the compound, formed from the intensifying prefix be- plus take, in the model of Middle English bitaken.
  • Sense 4 — grammatical range. Betake is a strong verb — betake, betook, betaken — built on take, took, taken, whose odd past form took reflects the old class-six vowel alternation of the Germanic strong verbs, preserved when English took the verb over from Old Norse.
  • Sense 5 — register and mock-heroic use. Formal, archaic, and literary, betake survives in narrative prose, oratory, and biblical English; in modern use "he betook himself to the pub" is deliberately jocular, the ceremonious verb applied to a trivial destination for effect.

The synonym boundaries are instructive. Went is the colourless, everyday word for motion, without any implication of purpose or resolve. Repaired to — another archaic construction — suggests resorting to a place that is one's proper or habitual station and carries a legal-flavoured formality. Resorted to emphasises turning to a place (or person, or expedient) as a resource in time of need. Adjourned to is lighter and more jocular, implying a break in a larger activity. What distinguishes betook oneself from all of these is its reflexive, self-committal force: the subject consciously dedicates his whole person to the journey, as though he were handing himself over to the destination. In collocation betake is used almost exclusively in the reflexive form betake oneself to, and its register is formal, archaic, and literary; a modern speaker would use it only for deliberate effect — and in this scene that effect is exactly right.
