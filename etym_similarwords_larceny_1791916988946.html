• theft: Similar because it is the general word for dishonestly taking another's property; differs because theft is the broad modern category, covering larceny, embezzlement, fraud and shoplifting, whereas larceny is the narrower common-law offence with specific elements.
  • robbery: Similar because it involves stealing from a person; differs because robbery requires force or the threat of force, while larceny can be committed silently and without any confrontation.
  • burglary: Similar because it is often committed by a thief; differs because burglary is defined by unlawful entry with intent to commit a crime, not by the taking, and can be complete even if nothing is stolen.
  • stealing: Similar because it is the plain-English equivalent; differs because stealing is informal and morally coloured, with no technical elements, while larceny is a legal term of art requiring trespassory taking, asportation and intent to deprive.
  • pilfering: Similar because it denotes small-scale theft; differs because pilfering emphasizes repeated, petty, opportunistic taking, usually by an employee or insider, and is never a formal charge.
  • shoplifting: Similar because it is a form of larceny from a shop; differs because shoplifting names the setting and the method, and many jurisdictions define it by statute separately from larceny.
  • embezzlement: Similar because it is a dishonest appropriation of another's property; differs because the embezzler lawfully possesses the property before converting it, so the taking is not trespassory and the offence is not larceny.
  • misappropriation: Similar because it means applying another's property to one's own use; differs because it is a civil and general term, often used of funds and trusts, and does not require the common-law elements of larceny.
  • fraud: Similar because both involve dishonest gain; differs because fraud turns on deception, not on the physical taking of property, and the victim usually consents to part with something because of the lie.
  • conversion: Similar because it involves treating another's property as one's own; differs because conversion is primarily a civil wrong (tort) and does not require intent to permanently deprive in the criminal sense.
  • plunder: Similar because it is violent or large-scale stealing; differs because plunder is literary and historical, evoking war, conquest and looting, and carries no precise legal definition.
