In the garden scene of Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), the wheelbarrow is transformed from a humble tool of labour into a childlike vantage point. Peter, searching for a way out of Mr. McGregor's garden, "climbed upon" the wheelbarrow and "peeped over" the wall. The reader is expected to know instantly what this machine is: a small handcart with a single wheel at the front, two handles at the back, and a shallow tray, tub, or box for the load, pushed by one person and easily tilted forward to dump its contents. The word's tone is resolutely plain and practical, a piece of everyday domestic vocabulary whose very homeliness makes the scene both comic and poignant: a creature of the wild must scale a gardener's tool simply to see over the wall that separates him from home. In the story's moral geography the wheelbarrow is an instrument of human labour, the very thing Mr. McGregor uses to carry off his harvest; by standing on it, Peter briefly borrows the human world's own height, turning a tool of the hunt into a lookout post. The word thus fuses the concrete (a specific, homely machine) with the narrative's need for elevation and hope.

Polysemy and secondary uses:

  • Core sense (the machine) — a one-wheeled cart with a wheel at the front, two handles and legs at the rear, and a body used to carry soil, rubble, garden waste, or building materials; pushed, never pulled, by one person.
  • Garden and building sense — the tool of the gardener, builder, and labourer; in older texts also spelled wheel-barrow, now usually the closed compound wheelbarrow.
  • Game sense — a wheelbarrow race, in which one child "walks" on his hands while a partner holds his ankles, imitating the machine; in gymnastics, the wheelbarrow is a balancing exercise or support position.
  • Verb use (informal, chiefly British) — to wheelbarrow something is to carry it in a wheelbarrow; in cricket slang the image of a runner "wheeling" the crease has produced phrases about scoring singles in a steady procession.
  • Metaphoric and journalistic use — the image of a wheelbarrow full of money (from German Schubkarre voller Geld) has become a universal figure for hyperinflation, especially of Weimar Germany, where banknotes were indeed carted in wheelbarrows; a wheelbarrow of currency is now shorthand for worthless, physically bulky money.
  • In horticulture and construction, tipping barrows, barrow ramps, and mortar barrows are named after it, and the compound handbarrow (a two-man carrying frame without a wheel) survives beside it.

The semantic bridges are visible in the compound's transparent structure: wheel + barrow. A barrow by itself was the older general word for any frame with handles used for carrying a load — carried by two people (a handbarrow) or dragged; the addition of a single front wheel made it a one-person machine and gave the compound its name. The wheel was the crucial innovation: it converted a carrying device into a rolling one, and "wheelbarrow" names exactly that invention — a barrow with a wheel.

Register and near-synonyms:

  • A bare barrow is the older general term for any frame with handles for carrying a load; it survives mainly in compounds (wheelbarrow, handbarrow) or in dialect, and by itself is vague about wheels.
  • A handbarrow is a barrow without a wheel, carried by two people at each end; the wheelbarrow's ancestor rather than its synonym.
  • A cart or handcart has two wheels (or more) and is pulled rather than pushed with a single forward wheel.
  • A trolley is a wheeled frame for moving loads on two or four wheels, usually on rails or smooth floors, without the tilted-dump action.
  • A dolly is a low flat platform on wheels for moving heavy objects, without sides or the tipping mechanism.
  • A tumbrel is an old two-wheeled cart that tips to discharge its load, a historical relative in the dumping function.

The wheelbarrow is thus the smallest, homeliest member of the family of one-person load carriers, and precisely because it is so small and homely, Potter can stand a rabbit upon it and let a child's story borrow the grown-up world's height for one hopeful moment.
