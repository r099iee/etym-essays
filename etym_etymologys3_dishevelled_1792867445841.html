The road into dishevelled runs through a head of hair, because the word's Latin grandparent names nothing but hair. Capillus in classical Latin means the hair of the human head collectively, the mane that barbers cut and mourners tore, and though antiquarians once split it neatly into caput, head, plus pilus, a single hair, modern etymologists leave its deeper formation marked as uncertain. What is secure is its French afterlife: popular Latin wore the word down to forms that surface in Old French as chevel and cheveux, a head of hair, the ancestor of the modern French cheveux that still sits on every Parisian head.

Medieval France then did something culturally revealing with this hair-word: it built a verb for undoing it. Descheveler, literally to de-hair or un-hair, meant to unbind the coif, to let the locks stream loose, and the occasions were ritual ones. Widows went dishevelled to funerals, penitents to shrines, prophetesses and madwomen in romances went about with streaming locks, because ordered hair was ordered society pinned up, and loosened hair announced that grief, frenzy, or haste had broken the pins. The past participle thus carried a whole social grammar: bound hair, mistress of herself; loose hair, mastered by feeling.

English borrowed the participle in the 1300s, first for mourners and the sorrowing bare-headed, and poets soon found it too good to confine to funerals. Chaucer's contemporaries and their Tudor heirs let winds dishevel oaks, let passions dishevel garments, and let battle dishevel whole hosts, each extension trading on the same visual rhyme between streaming locks and anything that straggles. By Shakespeare's day the word had settled into the literary register it still occupies: too elegant for mere mess, always hinting at the nobility of whatever has been unmade. A dishevelled king is still a king; a dishevelled cottage, like Bag End on Frodo's last evening, is still a beloved home.

The great modern fact about the word is orthographic rather than semantic: the parting of the Channel's l's. British English writes dishevelled with a doubled l, American English disheveled with one, following Noah Webster's campaign to lighten such consonants. Pronunciation never diverged, only the printed tail, so the same sigh of sympathy is spelled two ways across the Atlantic. Meanwhile the sense has crept steadily outward from heads to houses to abstractions: estate agents plead dishevelled cottages, leader-writers diagnose dishevelled finances, and stylists sell artfully dishevelled hair, disorder deliberately arranged at great expense.

Landmarks of the journey:

  • Roman barbershop: Latin capillus names the head's crowning hair, cut, curled, or torn in mourning.
  • French unbinding: Old French descheveler makes loose hair the ritual sign of grief and frenzy.
  • Chaucerian mourning: Middle English discheveled enters for widows and the sorrowing bare-headed.
  • Poetic weather: Tudor and Stuart verse lets wind and war dishevel trees, troops, and heroines alike.
  • Gothic pity: 18th- and 19th-century novels fix dishevelled as the tender word for noble ruin.
  • Atlantic split: British -ll- and American -l- divide the spelling while the sigh stays one.
  • Stylist's irony: the salon sells artfully dishevelled locks, neglect rehearsed to the minute.

When Tolkien calls Bag End dishevelled he plays the word's oldest string: the house with its pictures down and its curtains gone wears its emptiness like unbound hair, and the reader, schooled by six centuries of mourners, understands at once that walls can grieve.
