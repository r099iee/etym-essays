- Late 15th to Early 16th Century (Late Middle English / Early Tudor) - Coined as an English botanical compound (*lady* + *smock*), designating *Cardamine pratensis*; deeply tied to medieval Marian piety and the annual observation of Lady Day (March 25), when rural maidens bleached their white linen undergarments (smocks) upon green pasture grasses.
- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Era) - Immortalized in Renaissance pastoral poetry and drama, most famously in William Shakespeare's *Love's Labour's Lost* (1598) as "lady-smocks all silver-white"; recorded in John Gerard's landmark *Herball* (1597) as both *Ladies Smockes* and *Cuckowe flowers*.
- 17th to 18th Century (Augustan Era & Linnaean Botany) - Formally classified by Linnaeus (1753) as *Cardamine pratensis*; maintained in country herbalism as an antiscorbutic spring salad herb while retaining affectionate poetic status in rural British folklore.
- 19th Century (Victorian Romanticism) - Celebrated by romantic and pastoral essayists (such as John Clare and Oscar Wilde) as an archetype of unadorned English cottage-garden flora and undisturbed wet-meadow wilderness.
- 20th to 21st Century - Largely recedes from urban colloquial vernacular into the domains of specialized botanical conservation, wild-foraging gastronomy, and Shakespearean literary scholarship, standing as a precious ecological indicator of unimproved damp grasslands.
