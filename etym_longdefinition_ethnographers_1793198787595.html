Ethnographers, the plural of ethnographer, are in the primary modern sense the practitioners of ethnography: scholars who study peoples and cultures by going to live among them, watching, listening, asking, and recording, until the strange becomes intelligible from the inside. The ethnographer of a Siberian village, a City trading floor, or an online gaming guild alike produces the thick, first-hand description — kinship, ritual, gossip, work-rhythm — on which all comparison of cultures rests.

In the source sentence the word names the scholars who took up folk material: the Russian ethnographers adapted the mythological — gathering tales, rites, and beliefs in the field and resetting them within learned systems of myth and custom. Here the ethnographer is the bridge between the peasant hearth and the academy: the one who listens at the fireside and writes in the journal, turning living lore into documented culture.

Core meaning in detail: Classical ethnography, founded by figures such as Franz Boas and Bronisław Malinowski, demands prolonged participant observation — learning the language, sharing the routine, earning the trust that opens doors closed to questionnaires. Its product is the monograph: a whole way of life rendered in circumstantial detail, from yam gardens to initiation rites, with the native categories honoured before the analyst's theories are applied.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Practitioners of fieldwork anthropology (primary sense, from the 1840s): the scholar in the field with notebook and recorder. The bridge is literal composition: ethno-, people, plus -grapher, writer — one who writes peoples down, describing cultures as the geographer describes lands.
  • Folklorists and collectors of oral culture (19th-century sense, alive in the source sentence): the gatherers of fairy tales, byliny, charms, and rites. The bridge is the archive: before radio and schooling levelled peasant Europe, ethnographers salvaged its songs and stories as the raw matter of national mythologies.
  • Organisational and design ethnographers (late 20th c. extension): researchers embedded in hospitals, factories, and software teams to watch how work really happens. The bridge is method over subject: participant observation migrates from remote villages to office corridors, keeping the notebook but changing the tribe.
  • Digital and virtual ethnographers (21st c.): students of online communities, forums, and game worlds. The bridge is the field redefined: where Malinowski pitched a tent, the netnographer opens a laptop, but immersion, patience, and quoted voices remain the method.
  • Reflexive and native ethnographers (critical modern sense): insiders writing their own communities, and outsiders confessing their footprint in the text. The bridge is conscience: the science that once classified others now examines the classifier, and the ethnographer becomes a character in the ethnography.

Register and connotation: ethnographers is the sober professional plural of the social sciences — grant proposals, museum departments, university chairs — carrying patience, curiosity, and respect for difference. Against the armchair theorist it claims the moral high ground of having been there; against the journalist it claims slowness and depth. At its best it honours the people described; at its most criticised it recalls the colonial gaze that collected customs as empires collected territory — a tension every modern ethnographer writes with, and against.
