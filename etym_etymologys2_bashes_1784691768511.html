Origin of bash is DEBATED between two proposed sources; both are given, and the linear chain that follows keeps the attested record honest about the gap before 1640: Source (a): Old Norse *basca / *baska “to strike, thrash” (reconstructed; reflected in Swedish basa and Danish baske “to beat, thump”) — possibly carried into English by Viking settlers in northern dialects; unproven, since no Norse form is attested in English texts. Source (b): a native expressive blend of bang + mash (alongside dash, squash, crash) coined in the impact-family of English onomatopoeia; equally plausible, since the word's explosive b- and hissing -sh imitate blunt impact. Linear chain (first link debated, stated as such): 1. Old Norse *basca / *baska “to strike, thrash” (or native echoic blend of bang + mash; c. 8th–11th c. Viking age, reconstruction only) → Early Modern English bash (v., first recorded c. 1640s, “to strike violently, to smash in”; origin labelled “obscure” by the OED) 2. Early Modern English bash (v., 1640s) → English bash (n., 1818, “a heavy blow”; the noun formed from the verb) 3. English bash (n., 1818) → English bashes (plural, 19th century: “heavy blows; the bruises and swellings they leave on the flesh”) ◄ BASHES Debate note: the sudden appearance of bash in the 1640s with no Middle English ancestor, and its absence from the Germanic record before the early modern period, leave the Norse-borrowing and the native-onomatopoeic accounts open; the OED keeps both. The later senses — British “have a bash” (an attempt, mid-20th c.), American bash (a lively party, 1930s), and figurative “to bash” (to criticise savagely, 20th c.) — are semantic extensions of the noun and verb, not new etymological stages.
