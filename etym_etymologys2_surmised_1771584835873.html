PIE *meith- "to exchange, change, go in turns, alternate" (Pokorny's *meit(h)-; the traditional equation with Latin mittere, though de Vaan marks Latin mittere as "of uncertain etymology" — the equation is honoured, not proven) -> (Italic) Latin mittō, mittere "to send, let go" (attested from the earliest Latin records; the equation with *meith- is traditional but debated — see note) -> Latin supermittō, supermittere "to send or put over or upon" (super- "over, upon" + mittere "to send") -> Old French surmetre "to put over; to lay a charge upon, to accuse" (from Late Latin supermittere) -> (Anglo-Norman) sur-mise (noun, from the past participle of surmetre) "a placing on top; a charge, an accusation" (Anglo-Norman legal usage) -> Middle English surmise (noun) "a legal accusation, a charge, a formal allegation" (14th c.) -> English surmise (verb) "to suppose, conjecture without proof" (17th c.; the verb develops from the noun, shifting from legal accusation to intellectual guesswork) -> English SURMISED (past participle) Note on the root: the identification of Latin mittere with PIE *meith- is an old and honoured equation (Pokorny), supported by the semantic fit of "exchange" → "let go, send," but the evidence is thin; de Vaan lists mittere under "uncertain etymology," and no further Indo-European cognates are securely established. Vedic Sanskrit mith- "to alternate, pair, meet, contend" (methete "the two contend," Rigveda), mithuna "a pair" (the Gemini sign), and mithyā "false, turned the wrong way" (modern Hindi mithyā "false") are the root's clearest eastern fossils. The English semantic development is striking and exactly attested: a 14th-century legal "surmise" (an accusation laid upon someone) becomes, by the 17th century, a mental "surmise" (a supposition laid upon a scene). The prefix sur- is French, from Latin super- "over, upon." The modern past participle is first securely recorded in the 17th century.
