GROPED is the past tense and past participle of the verb grope, meaning to feel about blindly or uncertainly with the hands, fumbling for something that cannot be seen, or to search for one's way by touch when sight fails. The core image is tactile and helpless: outstretched hands, flat palms against cold surfaces, fingers searching for a wall, a door, a step that the eyes cannot find. The word implies slow, hesitant, undignified probing — the opposite of confident walking or striding — and it carries a built-in sense of disorientation, vulnerability, or fear. In the source context — Tolkien's The Hobbit, the bare sentence "Bilbo groped about in the dark" — the word carries precise physical and emotional weight: Bilbo, alone in the absolute blackness of the goblins' tunnels, must rely entirely on touch, and "groped about" suggests movement with no direction, circling and fumbling rather than proceeding purposefully. The word's Old English heritage gives it a plain, homely, physical concreteness that suits the narrator's quiet voice, and its choice over "felt," "fumbled," "pawed," or "searched" is deliberate: groping is blind, hesitant, and without method.

Full polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Blind tactile search (root sense): to feel about with the hands when sight fails; "grope for the light switch," "grope along the wall," "grope in the dark." The sense in the source sentence.
  • Figurative sense — groping after knowledge: to search blindly or tentatively for something intangible; "grope after truth," "grope for words," "grope toward an understanding." The mind's hand stretched into the unknown; the great metaphor of ignorance since the early modern period.
  • Modern dominant public sense — unwanted sexual touching: from the late twentieth century, to grope someone means to touch them sexually without consent; the noun a grope likewise. A narrowing of the old "touch, feel" sense into a specifically sexual, criminal, and political register.
  • Extended — tentative progress: to advance haltingly and experimentally; "groping toward a solution," "groping through uncertainty." The uncertainty of endeavour rather than of panic.
  • Rare older uses: in some dialects and older usage, "to grope" could mean to handle, to test the depth or nature of a thing, or to probe; largely obsolete.

Register and collocations: in its neutral literary use the verb is plain and physical, at home in narrative prose from Old English onward; modern formal and legal use is dominated by the sexual sense ("an allegation of groping," "groped a colleague"), a sense that has almost displaced the innocent one in public discourse. Fixed collocations include "grope for," "grope about," "grope along," "grope after," "grope in the dark," "grope one's way." The derived noun groper is rare; the continuous form "groping" serves as both verb and noun.

Semantic boundary: grope is distinguished from fumble (clumsy handling of objects already in hand), feel (neutral, calm touch), paw (grosser, clumsier contact), search (deliberate, methodical), and probe (systematic investigation) by its blindness and helplessness: groping is touch without sight, uncertain of what it seeks.
