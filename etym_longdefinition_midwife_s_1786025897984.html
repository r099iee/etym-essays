MIDWIFE'S is the possessive (genitive) form of the noun midwife, a practitioner—historically almost always a woman—who attends and assists mothers during pregnancy, labour, and childbirth. Grammatically, the apostrophe-s marks ownership, origin, or close association: a midwife's bag, a midwife's hands, a midwife's skill, a midwife's knowledge. At its most literal level, then, the form means "belonging to, produced by, or characteristic of a midwife," and it is in this sense a simple possessive of a very old profession—midwifery is one of humanity's oldest occupations, predating institutional medicine and serving as the principal pillar of community obstetrics in virtually every human culture before the rise of hospitals.

Yet the form is most famous in English not as a bare possessive but as the frozen half of an idiom: a midwife's tale, functionally interchangeable with an old wives' tale. In this phrase the possessive has been drawn out of the delivery room into the wider world of folklore. A "midwife's tale" is an unscientific folk belief, a popular superstition, or a traditional story passed down orally through the generations—typically lore about pregnancy, gender prediction, herbal remedies, birth omens, and domestic cures. The bridge between the two senses is the historical figure of the midwife herself: for centuries the midwife was the keeper of accumulated birth knowledge, the expert whose lore—some accurate, much of it folk wisdom or pure superstition—was handed on from mother to daughter and attendant to apprentice. "What the midwife teaches" therefore came to mean "traditional, unverified, woman-to-woman knowledge." As professional, university-trained obstetrics rose in the Enlightenment and the 18th century, male physicians dismissed this body of tradition as mere "old wives' tales," and the phrase took on a condescending, debunking colour that it retains to this day.

Primary and secondary senses

  • Literal possessive / attributive: belonging to or characteristic of a midwife ("a midwife's gentle touch", "a midwife's kit", "a midwife's observation"), used of the person, her tools, her methods, or her role.
  • Idiomatic / folkloric sense ("a midwife's tale"): an unverified folk belief, superstition, or traditional story—especially pregnancy and childbirth lore—passed down orally; the basis for debunking claims about health, pregnancy, or domestic life.
  • Metaphorical / catalytic sense: describing anyone who "brings forth" a new idea, work of art, institution, or movement, by analogy with the midwife's delivery of a child (cf. the Socratic maieutic method, "intellectual midwifery"); a politician might be called "the midwife of a new constitution."
  • Historical / professional register: the form appears in legal and institutional documents (licences, registers, oaths) to mark the practitioner's official capacity—"midwife's oath", "midwife's certificate".
  • Generic-genitive nuance: in phrases like "a midwife's duty" the possessive encodes not literal ownership but the role or office of the person, a use typical of agent nouns.

Historical semantic bridge

The move from person to superstition is a metonymy of profession. Because the midwife embodied traditional birth knowledge, anything "a midwife's"—her lore, her predictions, her cures—stood for the unlettered, empirical tradition of female healing. When medicine professionalised, the same phrase was re-imported as a slur against that tradition, and the idiom detached from the person: one need never meet a midwife to repeat a "midwife's tale." In modern fantasy and literary fiction, where the phrase often appears (as in the source context "A __________ tale"), it carries a double flavour—ancient maternal wisdom on one side, dubious superstition on the other.

Morphologically, MIDWIFE'S is midwife (a compound of mid "with" + wife "woman") plus the enclitic possessive -'s; the base noun forms its plural midwives, and the plural possessive midwives' is nearly identical in pronunciation. The apostrophe is the only written distinction between singular possessive, plural, and plural possessive. In careful speech the final cluster of the possessive form is /waɪfs/, with the voiceless f, identical to the plural. Semantically the word thus operates at three levels at once—literal ownership, folkloric idiom, and catalytic metaphor—and in any given sentence a reader must decide which is intended.
