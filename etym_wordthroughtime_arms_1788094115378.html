- Old English earm, plural earmas — the two upper limbs; already extended in OE to inlets (“arm of the sea”), the first “projecting part” sense.
- After 1066 — a second word arrives from Old French: armes, “weapons, armour”, from Latin arma; English suddenly has two identical-looking plurals meaning “limbs” and “weapons”.
- 14th century — “arms” gains the heraldic sense: the coat of arms, the knight's identifying device; “man-at-arms” enters the soldier's vocabulary.
- 16th–18th centuries — the extended senses multiply in literary English: arm of a chair, arm of a lever, arm of the law, arms of a mountain; “to bear arms”, “small arms”.
- 19th–20th centuries — “arms race” (20th c.), “arms control”, “nuclear arms”; the plural becomes almost synonymous with weaponry in political discourse, while “the arms of the Mountain” stays alive as a grand literary image.
- Modern — “arms” splits cleanly: anatomy (limbs), military and political discourse (weapons), heraldry (bearings), and metaphor (anything that reaches out); the mountain sense in the source text plays on the oldest personification of all.
