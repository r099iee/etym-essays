Derivation chain for TREMBLING (single root word, the present participle / gerund of tremble): 1. Proto-Indo-European *trem- "to tremble" (reconstructed root; twin apophonic form *tres- "to tremble, to flee in fear"; reflexes include Sanskrit trásati "he trembles, is afraid", Avestan tərəsaiti "he fears", Greek trémō "I tremble" beside treō "I flee in fear"), c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE. 2. Latin tremō, tremere "to tremble, shake" (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. Vergil uses tremere of the trembling earth and of fear). 3. Latin tremulus "trembling" (adjective formed from tremere; the base of later Romance verbs). 4. Vulgar Latin *tremulāre "to tremble" — a frequentative built on tremulus; the spoken-Latin base of the Romance verbs; late antique (4th–6th c. CE). 5. Old French trembler "to tremble" (from *tremulāre; attested 12th c.). 6. Middle English tremblen "to tremble" (borrowed from Old French; attested c. 1300). 7. Modern English tremble + the native present-participle / gerund suffix -ing, giving TREMBLING (attested from the 14th c., e.g. Middle English tremblende/tremblinge). Note: The twin roots *trem- and *tres- are generally treated as related ablaut/extension variants of one onomatopoeic base; some scholars group them under a single *trem- with an s-extension. The chain via Latin tremulus and Vulgar Latin *tremulāre is standard and is what links English tremble to the Italian tremare, French trembler, and Spanish temblar family.
