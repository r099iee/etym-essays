Tight-fisted describes a person who grips money tightly and is unwilling to spend or give it; stingy, miserly, niggardly. The image is literal: a fist clenched so tight that nothing can escape it — the antithesis of open-handed generosity.

Dickens's opening — "a tight-fisted hand at the grindstone" — doubles the hardness. Scrooge is a fist that will not open and a hand that grinds others down. Victorian readers heard both miserliness and ruthless labour discipline: he squeezes his own purse and squeezes his clerks.

  • Core sense — stingy with money: "A tight-fisted landlord raised the rent but cut repairs." "Tight-fisted donors gave pennies." It implies habitual unwillingness, not momentary caution. Near-synonyms range from mild (thrifty, frugal as virtues) to condemnatory (miserly, parsimonious). Tight-fisted sits in the middle: colloquial, disapproving, but comic rather than clinical.
  • Extended sense — stingy with anything: By metaphor, one can be tight-fisted with praise, time, affection, information: "tight-fisted with compliments." The fist now guards emotions, not coins. This extension is common in journalism and everyday speech.
  • Hand-at-the-grindstone nuance: The full Dickens phrase adds industrious oppression. A "hand at the grindstone" is a relentless worker who keeps others' noses to the stone. Scrooge is both grasping and grinding — he hoards and he drives. Modern use usually drops the grindstone, keeping only miserliness.
  • Degree and register: Informal, conversational, slightly old-fashioned but fully current. Stronger than careful, economical (neutral/positive) and weaker than miser, skinflint (character pathology). It is insulting but not obscene; usable in newspapers and classrooms. Hyphenated as attributive adjective ("a tight-fisted man"); predicative often open ("he is tight fisted") though hyphenated form dominates.
  • Distinction from thrift: Thrift saves wisely for good ends; tight-fistedness withholds meanly even when spending is right. Scrooge's cold office, meagre fire, and underpaid clerk illustrate the moral judgment: thrift serves life, tight-fistedness starves it.

Morphology: tight (adverb: tightly) + fist (noun) + -ed (adjectival "having"). Parallel compounds include close-fisted, hard-fisted, iron-fisted, open-handed, free-handed. Antonyms: generous, lavish, open-handed, free-spending, munificent. Pronunciation stresses the second element: /ˌtaɪtˈfɪs.tɪd/.

Polysemy is narrow — the word has one literal image and one moral — but its force lies in embodiment. Greed is not abstract; it is a hand you can see.

Extra note on tight-fisted stinginess in Victorian counting-houses and modern budgets, stressing habitual meanness and comic bodily image, moral judgment versus thrift, and enduring colloquial force in editorials and everyday talk. Very tight indeed.
