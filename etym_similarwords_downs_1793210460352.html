• Hills: Similar because both are raised rolling ground above the plain; differs because hills is the general word for any elevation, while downs names specifically smooth open chalk uplands with their sheep pasture and turf.
  • Uplands: Similar because both mean high open country set against valleys and lowlands; differs because uplands is a cool geographical term for any elevated region, while downs carries the chalk, the sheep, and the southern-English picture.
  • Wolds: Similar because the Yorkshire and Lincolnshire Wolds are the downs' northern twins — rolling limestone uplands of open arable and pasture; differs because wolds are oolitic limestone rather than chalk, and belong to the north and Midlands.
  • Moors: Similar because both are great open treeless grazings beloved of walkers; differs because moors are high, wet, peaty, and heathery ground of the north and west, while downs are dry, chalky, close-cropped turf of the south.
  • Fells: Similar because both are hill-pasture grazed by sheep under big skies; differs because fells are the rocky, craggy, rain-scoured heights of the Lake District and Pennines, while downs are smooth and gentle enough to gallop a horse along.
  • Heathland: Similar because both are ancient open grazings rich in wildlife; differs because heath is acid sand and gravel grown with heather and gorse, the botanical opposite of the lime-sweet downland turf.
  • Pasture: Similar because the source sentence's downs are valued precisely as sheep food; differs because pasture is any grazed grass anywhere, flat or steep, while downs is the particular rolling chalk country that grows it.
  • Prairie: Similar because both are vast open grasslands under open sky; differs because the prairie is the continental sea of grass on deep loess soils, while the downs are intimate island ridges on chalk — one for bison and wheat, one for sheep and larks.
