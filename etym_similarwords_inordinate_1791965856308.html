• excessive: Similar because it is the nearest plain synonym, naming something greater than a reasonable amount; differs because excessive measures against a norm and is relatively neutral in tone, while inordinate is bookish and more openly disapproving.
  • immoderate: Similar because it denies moderation and is often used of passions and appetites, exactly the source’s domain; differs because immoderate points to a lack of self-control in the person, whereas inordinate points to the disordered proportion of the thing itself.
  • disproportionate: Similar because both assert a failure of right proportion; differs because disproportionate is explicitly relational — too great compared with something else — while inordinate can stand alone as an absolute verdict.
  • exorbitant: Similar because both describe excess that ought not to be tolerated; differs because exorbitant is commercial and rhetorical, typically of prices, fees and demands, and carries the image of straying out of orbit.
  • undue: Similar because it means more than is warranted or proper, and appears in legal phrases such as “undue influence”; differs because undue is milder, more technical and less quantitative — it concerns entitlement rather than sheer amount.
  • intemperate: Similar because it names a failure of moderation, especially in desire, speech or drink; differs because intemperate describes a person’s habit or utterance, while inordinate can describe an impersonal quantity such as delay or risk.
  • unrestrained: Similar because it describes something not held within limits; differs because it is descriptive rather than judgmental, and it suggests the absence of an external check rather than a breach of due order.
  • extravagant: Similar because it implies excess beyond need; differs because it usually concerns spending, display or praise, and carries a note of waste or ostentation that inordinate does not.
  • unreasonable: Similar because both can condemn conduct that exceeds proper bounds; differs because unreasonable is broader and often means unfair or irrational, not simply too great in degree.
