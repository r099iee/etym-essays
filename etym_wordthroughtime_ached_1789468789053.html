- Old English (c. 9th–11th c.): Attested as strong verb acan (past ōc), designating continuous, gnawing physical pain, illness, or bodily suffering. - Somatic, direct, and visceral register.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.): Transitioned to aken while developing a split between verb ake (/eɪk/) and noun ache (/eɪtʃ/). Shifted from strong to weak inflection. - Everyday medical and physical register.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): Influenced by Renaissance Greek scholarship (associated with Greek achos), the spelling ache was applied to both noun and verb. Shakespearean drama established deep metaphorical use for romantic grief and spiritual yearning. - Dramatic, poetic, and emotionally intense register.
- 18th–19th c.: Dr. Samuel Johnson standardized the spelling ache with the pronunciation /eɪk/. Romantic poets (Keats's "My heart aches") immortalized the term as the definitive expression of bittersweet aesthetic and sentimental melancholy. - Melancholic, lyrical, and romantic register.
- 20th–21st c.: Contemporary usage balances everyday musculoskeletal soreness (post-workout stiffness, tension headaches) with rich literary evocations of existential longing and institutional strain. - Broad, versatile, and expressive register.
