- [14th century] - Borrowed from Old French pliant, "easily bent" (from plier, "to bend"); at first purely physical — pliant twigs, vines, and bows, things that yield under pressure without fracturing.
- [15th–16th century] - Extends from things to persons: a pliant nature or will is one easily bent, directed, or influenced; a cautious, faintly critical tone begins to gather around the moral sense — too ready to give way.
- [17th–18th century] - Both senses firm up: the physical (supple, flexible material) and the moral (accommodating, adaptable, submissive); pliant becomes the standard word for the willow that bends in the gale and the mind that yields before authority.
- [Modern register] - Literary and gently formal; the physical sense dominates natural description (the pliant reed, the pliant willow), while the moral sense has a skeptical edge in politics and character — a "pliant" official is one suspected of bending too readily before pressure.
