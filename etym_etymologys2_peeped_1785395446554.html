CHAIN for PEEPED (the "look" sense of peep, which is the sense in The Hobbit; the past-tense form): 1. Expressive imitative look-base *kīk- "to look, glance" — the earliest reconstructable form of the North Sea look-word family; no PIE etymon exists. The OED classes this family as of obscure, probably continental Germanic origin; the origin is not known and no guess is made. 2. Middle Low German kīken "to look, glance" (attested from the 13th c.); Middle Dutch kieken "to look" (13th c.) → Dutch kijken "to look, watch". 3. Middle English piken / peken "to look sharply, glance" (late 14th c.) → English peek "to glance furtively" (late 14th c.). 4. English peep (v.) "to look out quickly and furtively from a small opening" (c. 1470; an alteration of peek). 5. English peeped — regular weak past formation — ⟓ PEEPED "looked quickly and furtively". NOTE ON THE DEBATED STAGE: the vowel shift peek → peep is not phonologically regular; scholars treat peep as a remodelling of peek, probably reinforced by the rhyming chirp-homograph peep (below) and by Dutch piepen "to look slyly". SEPARATE, UNRELATED CHAIN — the homograph (the "chirp" sense). This is NOT the ancestor of the card's sense; English dictionaries keep the two words apart: 1. Expressive imitative base *pī- "small, high, repeated squeak" — no PIE etymon; the OED classes it as of obscure, probably imitative origin. 2. Middle English pepen "to cheep, squeak" (c. 1400). 3. English peep (v.) "to chirp, make a small shrill cry" → noun peep "the thin cry of a young bird"; figurative "the peep of day" (18th c.). NOTE: the same imitative base recurs independently across Europe — Latin pīpāre "to chirp" (of birds), French pépier, Dutch piepen, German piepen — each an autonomous formation, not a descendant.
