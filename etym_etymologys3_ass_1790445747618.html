The donkey-word ass has walked into English by the longest and dustiest of roads. Its ultimate ancestor is probably not Indo-European at all. Latin asinus, "donkey," sits oddly inside Latin phonology, and Michiel de Vaan's etymological dictionary judges it a borrowing; the great comparativists add that most Indo-European words for the ass are loans, pointing eastward to Asia Minor and to Sumerian ansu (also the likely source of Greek onos, whence English onager, "wild ass," and Armenian es). The donkey was domesticated in the Near East and Africa, and its name travelled with the animal along trade routes — a classic Wanderwort, or wandering word, carried by merchants rather than inherited by grammars.

  • Latin to Celtic to Germanic (1st millennium BCE – 1000 CE): from Latin asinus the word passed into Celtic as prehistoric Old Celtic *as(s)in (source of Irish and Gaelic asal, Welsh asyn), and from Celtic — the initial vowel of the English form betrays the Celtic detour — into Germanic. The direct Germanic borrowing kept the n as l (Gothic asilus, Old High German esil, modern German Esel, Dutch ezel — the source, by a furniture metaphor, of English easel), while the Celtic-mediated form gave Old English assa (Northumbrian assal, assald), "he-ass," recorded before the year 1000. Lithuanian asilas, Old Church Slavonic oslu, and Russian osel show how widely the Latin loan scattered.
  • Medieval moralizing (12th–16th c.): in bestiaries, fables, sermons, and proverbs the patient, braying, load-bearing beast became the living emblem of ignorance and obstinacy — the creature that "listeneth on a harp" without comprehension, the mount of fools. Hence asshead (late 15th century), "to make an ass of oneself" (1580s, crowned by Shakespeare's literal staging in Bottom's transformation), and the schoolroom's pons asinorum, the "asses' bridge" of Euclid's fifth proposition that fools cannot cross. Gower already pairs lion and ass as the poles of worth in 1393.
  • Polite retreat and vulgar collision (1785–present): around 1785 respectable speech adopted donkey (perhaps dun-coloured + -key as in monkey, perhaps from the pet-name Duncan), demoting ass to Scripture, zoology, Ireland, and affectionate insult — the exact register of Wodehouse. Simultaneously a wholly separate word moved in: Old English ears (arse), from Proto-Indo-European *ors-, "buttocks," cognate with Old Frisian ers, Old Norse ars, and Greek orrhos, began in some dialects to drop its r before s (compare bust/burst, cuss/curse, hoss/horse). Rhymes prove the merger was underway by the 1680s; nautical slang writes ass for the backside by 1860; American popular speech completes it after 1930, generating the whole taboo family — the body, the act (1942), the self (1958), the intensifier. Two millennia of Near Eastern trade and one lazy consonant thus produced a single spelling with a split soul.

Modern usage keeps the two souls apart by accent and company. In London arse still guards the anatomy while ass brays in the field; in New York one spelling does both jobs, and only context — barnyard versus bedroom — disambiguates. What unites them is the animal's ancient shadow: even the obscenity borrows something of the donkey's lowliness, stubbornness, and comic exposure.
