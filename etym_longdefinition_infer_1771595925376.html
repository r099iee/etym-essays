infer (verb): To deduce or conclude information from evidence and reasoning; to derive a statement or judgment that is not explicitly stated but is held to follow from given premises, facts, or probabilities; to carry a conclusion forward in the mind from what is given. In the Preface to Modern Painters (1843) John Ruskin writes: "I have said that the old masters did not give the truth of nature; if the reader chooses, thence, to infer that they were not masters at all, it is his conclusion, not mine." The word is the hinge of a precise rhetorical disclaimer: Ruskin commits himself to one limited claim and then disowns the larger conclusion that a hasty reader might build upon it. The verb here carries a faintly accusatory edge, for it attributes to the reader an act of construction — the building of a conclusion out of evidence that does not logically compel it — and the sentence ends in the dry, legalistic distinction "it is his conclusion, not mine." To infer is the act of a mind carrying meaning beyond the letter of what is said; Ruskin's whole sentence is a warning against carrying it too far. Polysemy and related senses:
  • Logical conclusion (primary modern sense): to derive a conclusion from evidence and reasoning, where the conclusion usually follows but is not always compelled — "from the smoke we infer fire."
  • Reading and conversational sense: to understand or gather from what is said or written without its being stated explicitly — the sense Ruskin exploits; often paired with the writer's or speaker's right to disown the conclusion ("that is his conclusion, not mine").
  • Statistical and scientific sense (technical jargon): in statistics and the sciences, to infer a population property from a sample — "statistical inference," "inferential statistics" — a rigorously defined technical sense built upon the logical one.
  • Historical / archaic sense: "to bring in, to adduce, to introduce" (especially of an argument or a text), from Latin inferre "to carry in"; now obsolete, it survives only in fossilised legal and theological phrases such as "we infer the writings of the Fathers."
  • Confused sense ("infer" for "imply"): in careless usage, infer is sometimes used where "imply" (the speaker's act of suggesting) is meant; the usage note that keeps the two distinct dates from the eighteenth century and is among the most famous in English.
The historical bridge between these senses is the metaphor of carrying. Latin inferre means "to carry in," and the logical sense developed from the physical one by a steady abstraction: what one carries into a place became what one carries into an argument, and then what one carries from premises into a conclusion. The word entered English in the fifteenth century with the concrete sense "to bring in, to import" (a meaning recorded for goods), and within a century the logical sense had hardened. The usage distinction between infer and imply is a later, prescriptive overlay: imply is the act of the writer who suggests, infer the act of the reader who concludes, and the two are complementary halves of the same communicative transaction. Register and usage notes:
  • Infer is the standard, unmarked English verb for drawing a conclusion; it sits between the strict deduce (logical necessity) and the loose guess or surmise (speculation).
  • It is used transitively with a that-clause, with an object noun, or with from: "to infer a conclusion from the evidence."
  • In technical writing it names the very heart of the empirical method — induction — as opposed to the locked-down deduction of pure logic.
  • The word's history preserves a beautiful archaic residue: the "carrying in" of the Latin root survives in formal phrases meaning "to import, to bring in," and the whole family (transfer, refer, confer, offer, prefer) still carries the physical image of carrying from place to place.
