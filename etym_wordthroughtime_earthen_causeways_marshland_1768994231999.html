earthen:
- Old English eorþen - the 'made of earth or clay' adjective, built from eorþe 'earth' with the -en suffix of material (wooden, golden, silken); a direct inheritance from the Germanic core of English
- Two thousand years of stability - while wooden and golden drifted into metaphor, earthen stayed literal, faithful to the humble and the handmade: the earthen pot, the earthen floor, the earthen rampart
- Contemporary - the deliberately archaic adjective of potters, ditch-diggers, and earthwork-builders - a word that insists on material fact, at home in Hardy, Tolkien, and biblical registers

causeways:
- The trodden road (Latin) - from calciata, 'a road trodden down', built on Latin calcare 'to tread, trample', via Old North French cauciee and Anglo-French causee
- The English raised way (14th c.) - 'causeway' enters English as a paved, raised highway, later narrowed to a road raised above waterlogged ground
- Modern extension - in North American usage any raised roadway across water or floodplain (the Lake Pontchartrain Causeway), and the monumental toponym Giant's Causeway shifts the word to geology

marshland:
- The sea-border (ancient) - from the Indo-European root *mori- 'sea', the marsh is the wetland at the sea's edge, the border where land and water mingle
- The compound (Middle English) - 'marsh' + 'land' names the wetland terrain class - the English Fens, the Dutch polders
- Contemporary - the neutral, ecological term for coastal and tidal wetlands, still layered with older folkloric associations of bogs, hidden pools, drowned roads, and ground that cannot be trusted
