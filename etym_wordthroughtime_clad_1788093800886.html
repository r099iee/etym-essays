- Old English: the verb clāþian "to clothe" (from clāþ "cloth, garment"); the participle is plain, everyday, working-class — the grammar of putting clothes on.
- Middle English, 14th century: the participle contracts — "clothede" is clipped to "cladde/clad" — a quick, natural spoken form of clothed.
- 16th–18th centuries: "clad" settles into the poetic and biblical partner of "clothed": chronicles, epic verse and elevated prose use it for dignity and resonance.
- 19th century, 1850s: the compound adjective explodes — "ironclad" warships, "steel-clad", "snow-clad" — perception turns heroic, rugged and metallic.
- 20th–21st centuries: building technology borrows the word back in "cladding"/"clad in panels"; today CLAD ranges from Tolkien's mail-clad dwarves to architects' facades, always precise about the material that fits the surface.
