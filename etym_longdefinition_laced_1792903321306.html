laced is the past participle of lace, and in the source sentence it describes branches laced and drooping to the ground, twined and interwoven into a living bower overhead. The image is of limbs crossing and threading through one another the way bootlaces cross over a shoe, so that separate growths become one woven roof, green, shady, and gently sagging under its own weight.

The word’s beauty is that one small crossing pattern explains a whole family of uses. A shoe is laced when its cord crosses eyelet to eyelet, a bodice is laced when its ribbons cinch the waist, a branch canopy is laced when boughs cross and knot, a drink is laced when one liquid threads through another, and speech is laced when irony threads through every sentence. In each case something thin and active — cord, ribbon, bough, rumour, flavour — runs through something plainer and transforms it.

Core meaning in detail: At bottom lace is a cord passed through openings or around things to fasten, tighten, or decorate. From that action come two great pictures that never quite separate. The first is fastening by crossing, as in laced boots, laced gloves, and fingers laced together. The second is decoration or mixture by threading through, as in a shawl laced with silver thread, tea laced with honey, or a lecture laced with jokes. Tolkien’s bower belongs to the first picture made botanical, branches doing without hands what fingers do when they interlace.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Interwoven or intertwined, crossing like cords (Tolkien’s sense, literary): branches laced into a bower, fingers laced in prayer. The bridge is visual analogy, nature’s lattice read through the familiar technology of shoelaces and netting.
  • Fastened or tightened with a lace or cord (oldest clothing sense, from 14th c.): laced boots, a tightly laced corset. The bridge is literal, the cord pulled through eyelets to close or shape a garment, once the everyday work of fingers before zips and elastic.
  • Trimmed or edged with ornamental lace fabric (textile sense): a handkerchief laced with white tatting, a gown laced at the collar. The bridge runs from the cord itself to the openwork fabric woven from fine cords, and then to anything edged with that fabric.
  • Spiked or dashed with a small strong addition, especially drink or food (19th c.): coffee laced with brandy, porridge laced with honey. The bridge is mixture imagined as threading, one flavour run through another so that every sip carries both.
  • Streaked or striped, especially with colour or light (descriptive): a sky laced with pink cloud, leaves laced with frost. The bridge is again visual, thin lines laid across a ground the way braid is laid on cloth.
  • Flavoured through with an abstract quality, especially speech and writing (figurative): an address laced with irony, memoirs laced with regret. The bridge carries the drink metaphor into language, so that tone threads through sentences the way rum threads through punch.
  • Struck or beaten, especially in sport slang (colloquial, chiefly past tense): he laced the ball into the top corner. The bridge is obscure but lively, probably from the crack of a lace or lash, transferring the snap of cord to the strike of boot on ball.

Register and connotation: laced is supple and slightly elegant, equally at home in a woodland idyll, a fashion plate, and a bar. Tolkien’s use is tender and sheltering, the bower as a woven cradle, while tightly laced can suggest primness or constraint and laced with rum suggests mischief. The constant is craft, thin things made strong by crossing.
