• Stage 1 — Uncertain, non-PIE origin: no Proto-Indo-European root can be reliably reconstructed for the stem. The Frankish form *gāhi "swift, sudden, impetuous" is reconstructed from later Germanic attestations (Old High German gāhi "sudden, quick", Middle Low German gā, Dutch gauw "quick, fast"). The ultimate origin is unknown and possibly imitative or expressive.
  • Stage 2 — Frankish *gāhi "swift, sudden, impetuous" (reconstructed; attested indirectly through Old High German gāhi and Dutch gauw).
  • Stage 3 — Old French gai "joyful, happy, pleasant, bright" (attested from the 12th c.).
  • Stage 4 — Middle English gay "joyful, light-hearted, bright, finely dressed" (attested late 14th c., e.g. Chaucer).
  • Stage 5 — Middle English gaily / gayly "cheerfully, brightly" (adverb formed in English, gay + the native suffix -ly, attested 14th–15th c.).
  • Stage 6 — Modern English gaily "in a gay manner: cheerfully; brightly, showily" (the modern spelling gaily fixed in the 16th–17th c.).
Note on the debate: The connection between Old French gai and Frankish *gāhi, though widely accepted, is not certain; some scholars derive Old French gai from Old Provençal gai or from Latin vagus "wandering, unsettled, light." The Germanic source is the usual view. The adverbial suffix -ly descends from Old English -līce, from the noun līc "body, form, likeness" (Proto-Germanic *-līka-, ultimately PIE *leig- "to be like, resemble"); the suffix chain is added for completeness, since the stem itself has no reconstructable PIE root.
