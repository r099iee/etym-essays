A preposition becomes a shelter Eaves descends through a single, unbroken Germanic line from the Proto-Indo-European root *upo, which meant "under, up from under, over". That small root was one of the most fertile in the family: it gave Sanskrit úpa (near, toward, under), Greek hupó (under), and Latin sub (under) with its lengthened form super (over). But the branch that concerns us is Germanic, where *upo yielded the adverb *ub, "under", and from it two lines grew. The first, *uber (over, above — from the lengthened PIE variant *uper), produced Old English ofer, modern English over, German über, and Dutch over, so that every English speaker already commands a cousin of eaves in the everyday word over. The second, built with a suffix, produced *ubaswō-, literally "the thing that lies under" — a porch or a projecting strip of roof beneath which one could stand. Here the decisive semantic shift is already visible: a general preposition of position ("under") has been turned into a concrete architectural noun ("that which shelters by being underneath"). The word was, in effect, the roof seen from below. Into the Germanic daughters
  • Old High German obasa / obisa, "portico, vestibule, eaves" — the German line, which survives today only in dialect forms and in the modern compound Dachvorsprung "roof projection".
  • Old Norse ups, "eaves" (rare, chiefly in compounds) — the Scandinavian line, showing a different treatment of the middle consonant.
  • Old English efes / yfes, "edge of a roof, that which overhangs" — the line that gives modern English its word.
Sound and form The Old English noun was singular and feminine; the familiar modern sentence "the eaves are leaking" hides the fossil of that older grammar. During the Middle English period the form settled as eves, ewes, or ifes; by about 1500 the final -s was reinterpreted as a plural ending, so that eaves came to behave like a plural while continuing to denote the single overhanging edge. This reanalysis is one of the most instructive accidents of English morphology: a genuine inflectional ending became indistinguishable from a plural marker, and modern speakers have even back-formed a new singular, eave ("the eave of the barn"), on the strength of the misreading. Figurative growth: eavesdrop The later career of the word is largely figurative. In the seventeenth century English coined the compound eavesdrop: to stand beneath the eaves, where the rainwater "dropped", in order to overhear what was said indoors, and by extension to listen secretly anywhere. The Elizabethans would have understood the image at once, since the eave was the natural hiding-place of the curious servant. The compound has outlived the architecture: eavesdrop and eavesdropper are everyday words even where nobody can say exactly what an eave is, and the verb has acquired its own legal shadow in "eavesdropping" statutes that regulate secret listening. Cultural and literary life In the nineteenth century the word acquired its familiar domestic poetry — swallows nesting under the eaves, icicles fringing them, and the eave as the homely threshold between the sheltered house and the weather. Cao Xueqin's Story of the Stone supplies the same imagery in translation: the verandah running "beneath the rear eaves of the hall" turns a structural detail into a figure of the mansion's sheltering presence, the roof's edge standing guard over those who pass beneath, and marking the boundary between the intimate apartments and the alley beyond. The PIE root *upo, which began as a preposition of humble position, has thus ended as a poetic noun for the roof's protective margin — a small history of shelter packed into a single word.
