Plaited is the past participle and simple past of the verb to plait, "to interweave three or more strands over and under one another into a single band, braid, or fold." In the context from which this card is drawn it describes the dressing of a young man's hair: his side hair is "dressed in a number of small braids plaited with red silk." The participle does exacting visual work. It tells the reader that the red silk is not merely tied or wrapped around the braids but woven through them strand by strand, so that hair and silk become a single composite textile. Every detail of the passage - the small braids, the red silk, the drawn-round queue, the four enormous pearls, the jewelled gold clasp - is arranged to present the young gentleman as a precious finished artefact, and plaited is the hinge of that presentation: it is the word that turns a hairstyle into a piece of craftsmanship. The tone is luxuriant and deliberately observational, as the narrator's gaze moves methodically from the side hair to the nape to the clasp, and the participle keeps the reader's attention fixed on the physical texture of the construction: tight, orderly, gleaming, and bound. In the social world of the novel such a hairstyle is a badge of status and of grooming labour; plaited quietly marks the young man as an object of elaborate domestic care, a figure whose very body has been worked upon like costly textile. Core senses.
  • Formed into a plait or plaits (hair). The central modern sense: hair interwoven in the characteristic over-under pattern of a braid, usually of three or more strands. "She wore her hair in two long, plaited braids."
  • Woven or interwoven (strands, straw, leather, cord). The same gesture applied to materials: plaited straw for baskets and hats, plaited leather for whips and reins, plaited cord for rope. Here the participle keeps its full etymological force - the material is literally interlaced, strand crossing strand.
  • Folded (cloth): the pleat connection. Historically the word's first sense in English was "folded" - a plait of cloth was a fold or pleat, and pleat is simply a later respelling of the same word. A garment is "plaited" or "pleated" when pressed into parallel folds, and this cloth sense survives in dressmaking terminology.
The noun plait names (1) a braid of hair, especially a single tail of hair woven from three or more strands; (2) a fold of cloth (the older sense, now usually written pleat); (3) a band or length formed by plaiting, as of rope, straw, or leather; and (4), by extension, the arrangement itself - the pattern of interlacing that makes the object. The verb to plait means "to braid; to fold; to interweave," and plaited is its past participle, functioning both as a verb form ("the hair she plaited") and as an adjective ("plaited hair"). Secondary, metaphoric, and technical usages.
  • Plaited rope and line (nautical and sporting). Rope formed by braiding or "plaiting" several strands rather than twisting them; plaited rope does not kink, is soft in the hand, and is standard for sheet halyards, dog leads, and belt lanyards - a precise technical use of the word in maritime and craft contexts.
  • Plaited straw work and corn dollies (folk craft). Straw and wheat plaiting, the old rural craft of braiding harvest straw into baskets, beehives, and corn-dolly figures, shows the word's long association with hand work and the countryside.
  • Plaited conductors (electrical engineering). A plaited conductor is a flexible electrical cable woven from many fine strands, so that the weaving image transfers cleanly into modern technical jargon.
  • Figurative "plaited" as intricate, ordered, worked-upon. Metaphorically the participle can describe anything elaborately interwoven or complexly arranged - a "plaited" design, a "plaited" narrative, an argument "plaited" together - though such transfers are literary and always lean on the physical picture of interlaced strands.
  • Heraldic and decorative plaiting. Plaited bands, plaited borders, and plaited guilloche ornament appear in architectural and textile description, connecting the word to the decorative arts of the Renaissance and beyond.
Why the word means what it means. The chain of senses is a chain of folds. Old French pleit meant "a fold," from Latin plicāre, "to fold." A fold of cloth became, by extension, a fold of hair - a plait of hair being hair doubled and interwoven - and the fold then became the braid, the orderly interweaving of strands. The spelling plait settled on the hair sense while pleat respelled the cloth sense, and the two words, born identical, now divide a wardrobe: a pleat is pressed cloth, a plait is braided hair. British English keeps plait as the ordinary word for a braid; American English, under the influence of braid, uses it far less, so that the word now carries a distinctly British and faintly old-fashioned flavour, evoking schoolgirls, period costume, and country craft. In all its senses, though, the core image is constant: strands crossing over and under, a texture that is orderly, secure, and deliberately made - the word of craftsmanship, whether the craftsman is a grandmother braiding a child's hair, a basket-maker, or a sailor splicing a line.
