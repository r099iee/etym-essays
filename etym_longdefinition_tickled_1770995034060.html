tickled: adjective (and past participle of the verb tickle). As an adjective it means delighted, pleased, or amused — especially in a light, spontaneous, often privately gratified way, as if the mind had been gently "touched" into laughter. The word is informal and warm, and it belongs to the register of everyday affection rather than grand emotion.

PRIMARY SENSE (adjective: delighted, pleased, amused). The adjective is the sense on this card. "He was tickled to discover..." means he was genuinely, often boyishly pleased — pleased in a way that shows itself in a smile one cannot suppress. In the card's source sentence ("Zi-xing for his part was tickled to claim acquaintanceship with a man of Yu-cun's great learning and culture"), the word captures a sudden light-hearted burst of social pride and self-congratulation: Zi-xing is not merely satisfied but privately delighted, as though his vanity had been gently prodded into a pleased smile. The adjective commonly appears in the fixed phrases tickled pink and tickled to death, both meaning "utterly delighted."

PRIMARY SENSE (verb: to touch lightly so as to excite). The underlying verb has two chief literal senses: (1) to touch or stroke a person or animal lightly so as to cause a tingling sensation, involuntary twitching, and often laughter or shivering; and (2) to excite, amuse, or stimulate agreeably — "tickle the palate" (to delight the sense of taste), "tickle the fancy" (to amuse or intrigue), "tickle the ivories" (to play the piano). The adjective's meaning is a direct product of this second, metaphorical use: the mind is "tickled" into amusement just as the skin is tickled into laughter.

SECONDARY SENSES AND EXTENSIONS.

  • tickled pink (idiom, 20th century) — extremely pleased or amused; the colour metaphor suggests a flushed, delighted glow.
  • tickled to death (idiom, 17th-18th century onward) — utterly delighted; originally hyperbole, now fixed and no longer felt as morbid.
  • tickle the fancy / tickle someone's interest — to amuse, intrigue, or gently excite curiosity.
  • ticklish (adjective) — (1) sensitive to tickling; (2) by extension, delicately difficult, requiring careful handling ("a ticklish problem," "a ticklish negotiation").
  • tickler (noun) — someone or something that tickles; in business jargon, a reminder or memorandum file.
  • titillate — the Latin-derived doublet of the same imitative family, meaning to excite or stimulate pleasantly (often sexually); see etymology.

GRAMMAR, COLLOCATION, AND REGISTER. As a participial adjective, tickled is used with the copula ("he was tickled," "she felt tickled"), often with a to-infinitive ("tickled to be invited," "tickled to hear") or with by/with ("tickled by the compliment," "tickled with the gift"). Collocations include tickled pink, tickled to death, quietly tickled, secretly tickled, rather tickled. The register is informal and colloquial; the word is warm, friendly, and faintly mischievous, and it rarely appears in formal or technical prose.

SEMANTIC BOUNDARIES. Pleased is calm, steady, and general; delighted is stronger and more formally expressive; amused is neutral about the source of entertainment; flattered involves an elevation of self-esteem through praise, with a hint of vanity; thrilled is intense and excited; charmed suggests winning grace. Tickled is lighter than all of these: spontaneous, physical, almost involuntary — the mind's equivalent of a giggle. Its historical bridge runs from the bodily reflex of laughter to the mental spark of delight, since the same word that names the finger's light touch on the skin names the pleasure that "touches" the ego.
