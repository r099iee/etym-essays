Single linear chain for the single root word aeons: 1. PIE *h₂eyu- "vital force, length of life, lifetime" (reconstructed root *h₂ey- "vital force," c. 4500–2500 BCE) — originally a biological word: the breath of life, the lifespan allotted to a living being. Attested across the family: Sanskrit āyus "life, vital force, age" (Vedic), Avestan āyū "life, age," Latin aevum "age, lifetime, eternity," Gothic aiws "time, eternity," Old English ǣ "eternity, law." 2. Hellenic *aiwōn — Greek aiṓn "lifetime, age, generation, a long period of time, eternity" (attested in Homer, 8th c. BCE). The Greek word already carried the full range from "a single lifetime" to "an age of the world" to "eternity." 3. Latin — aevum "age, a lifetime, an era, eternity" is the inherited Italic cognate of Greek aiṓn (both from *h₂eyu-); the Greek form itself was borrowed into Late Latin as aeon "an age, eternity" in Christian and philosophical writing (the ecclesiastical and Gnostic use). 4. English aeon (also spelled eon) "an age, an indefinitely long period of time" — borrowed from Greek aiṓn (via Late Latin aeon), attested in English from the 16th century; the plural aeons is the standard literary form. The later American and geological spelling "eon" is the same word. The chain is thus: PIE *h₂eyu- (vital force, lifetime) → Greek aiṓn (lifetime → age → eternity; Homeric) → Late Latin aeon (age, eternity; ecclesiastical) → English aeon / aeons (16th c. onward). The parallel inherited line — Latin aevum → Old French aage → English "age," and *h₂eyu- → Proto-Germanic *aiwaz → Old English ǣ/ā → "aye, ever" — runs on cognate tracks, not as part of the direct chain of the English loanword. No stage is guessed: the borrowing from Greek via Latin is the standard, well-attested account.
