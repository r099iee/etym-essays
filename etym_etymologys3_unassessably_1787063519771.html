A word assembled from three roots

Unassessably is a modern, dictionary-maker's assembly of three ingredients — un-, assess, and -ably — each of which carries an independent, well-documented history. The word itself is a rare, late (19th-century) formation that first achieved fame through J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), but every layer of its meaning was forged centuries earlier in the grammar of negation, the vocabulary of taxation, and the machinery of English adverbs.

Part I — un-: the ancient negative

The prefix un- is one of the oldest continuously used elements in the language, an unbroken inheritance from Proto-Indo-European *ne- ("not"), whose zero-grade form *n̥- became the Germanic prefix *un-. Old English used un- prodigiously to negate adjectives and participles — unmild, unclǣne, unforgifen — and the pattern has never stopped. Its meaning is simple and stable: "not, the opposite of, lacking." In unassessably, un- flips the whole complex "assessable(ly)" into its negation: not able to be assessed. Because the prefix is inseparable and native, English speakers feel unassessably as an ordinary, familiar structure even though the word itself is rare.

Part II — assess: from sitting beside to valuing

The heart of the word is the verb assess, whose history is the most interesting. Its root is the great Indo-European verb of sitting, *sed- "to sit," the same root that gives Latin sedēre, English sit, seat, session, settle, and sediment. Latin built from sedēre the compound assidēre (ad- "beside, at" + sedēre "to sit"), meaning "to sit beside someone" — and in particular, "to sit beside a magistrate as his assessor or aide." Roman administration made the fiscal leap: the judge's sitting assistant evolved into the assessor, the official who sat with the tax-farmer or magistrate to work out what a taxpayer owed. From the past participle assessus came a noun for the very thing fixed by such sittings — a tax, fee, or levy — and Medieval Latin assessāre "to fix a tax or charge."

Old French borrowed this as assesser, and English took it in the 15th century as assessen, meaning strictly "to impose or fix a tax, fine, or charge." The fiscal core stayed primary for centuries: an "assess" was an official levy; "assessments" were tax rolls. Then, in the 17th–19th centuries, the word widened along a natural bridge — since officials who set taxes must first judge the value of what is taxed, assess came to mean "to estimate the value of (property, income)," and from valuation the step to general judgement was short: today we assess situations, risks, damage, performance, and worth. The whole line can be summed in one movement: sit → sit beside (a judge) → fix a tax → estimate value → judge. It is a beautiful case of a word climbing from a body position to an intellectual operation.

Part III — -ably: the adverbial suffix

The ending -ably is a compound of two suffixes. The adjectival -able came from Latin -abilis (via Old French -able), meaning "able to be ...ed" — and, tellingly for English, it attaches with equal ease to native and borrowed stems (readable, cookable beside assessable, valuable). The adverbial -ly is the native Germanic suffix (Old English -līce) that turns adjectives into manner-adverbs. Stacked together, -ably means "in a manner that can be ...ed": assessably = "in a manner capable of being assessed"; the pair with un- gives "in a manner not capable of being assessed."

Part IV — Fusion and fame

The full word is therefore transparent even to a first-time reader: un- + assess + -ably = "in a way that cannot be valued, measured, or judged." The formation is 19th century and has always been rare — the OED records a thin trail of unassessable/unassessably uses, mostly in elevated or journalistic prose, and no heavy everyday usage ever developed.

Its cultural anchor is Tolkien. In The Hobbit (1937), Bilbo addresses the dragon: "O Smaug the unassessably wealthy." Tolkien, a philologist who knew the whole recorded history of the word's parts, chose it with full awareness of its fiscal genealogy: an "unassessable" fortune is precisely one that no assessor — no tax official, no valuer, no sedēre-seated judge of worth — could possibly price. The flattery is total, the irony delicious (the dwarf has just claimed the gold was an "afterthought"), and the word has never escaped its dragon.

Today unassessably remains a rare, dignified intensifier used for effect — in journalism about wealth, in rhetorical prose about scale, and wherever a writer wants "immeasurably" with a sharper, more official edge. Its three roots (an ancient negative, a Roman tax-office verb, and a pair of everyday suffixes) meet in one word to say, with perfect precision: beyond all possibility of valuation.
