muffled is the past tense and past participle of the verb to muffle, and as an adjective it means "wrapped up, enveloped, or covered so as to deaden sound and hide form; swathed in heavy cloth." The word yokes two senses into one image: the tactile (cloth wrapped around a thing or a person) and the auditory (the wrapped thing's noise reduced to a dull, indistinct murmur). In the passage from C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian that generated this card, Doctor Cornelius stands "muffled in a hooded robe," a figure swallowed by fabric in a moonlit room. The tone is secrecy and softness: to be muffled in a hooded robe is to be wrapped from head to foot so that one's shape, face, and even footfalls are rendered indistinct. Lewis's participle does double work — the robe conceals Cornelius's identity in the moonlit room, and the muffling principle, a layer of soft material that absorbs noise, extends to the "shod" feet that follow, so that "muffled" quietly forecasts the "almost no noise" of the escape. There is also a note of comfort: muffling is what one does against cold, and the hooded robe suggests a scholar bundled against a chill night. The word's own sound is part of the effect: the padded consonants and rounded vowels of muf-fled feel swaddled and hushed, and the word mimes the deadening of sound it names.

Principal senses (polysemy).

  • Wrapping / concealment (primary, oldest sense): "to wrap up, cover, or swaddle in cloth," especially about the face and body, for warmth or concealment. "Muffled up against the cold," "a muffled figure in the fog." This is the sense in the passage — a man swathed in a hooded robe.
  • Deafening of sound (the modern core sense): "to wrap or pad so as to deaden sound," hence "made hard to hear, dulled, indistinct." A muffled voice, a muffled cry, muffled footsteps, muffled bells, muffled drums. This sense developed naturally because wrapping a thing in cloth deadens the noise it makes; it is now the most frequent use of the word.
  • Technical / mechanical senses: a muffler first named a scarf wound round the neck (the wrapping sense) and then the device that deadens an engine's exhaust (the sound sense) — so "muffled" describes sound reduced by a silencer or suppressor, as in a muffled shot.
  • Figurative and transferred uses: "muffled" is used of colour or light reduced in intensity ("muffled light"), of emotions suppressed or kept from full expression ("a muffled anger"), and of communication that is vague or indirect. In all these the image is the same: a layer that absorbs and softens.
  • Sound-symbolic halo: the word's own phonetics — the soft labials and the closed, rounded vowel — imitate the hushed, indistinct quality it names, and it belongs to the expressive family that includes mumble, mum, and mutter, all built on m- for sounds made behind closed lips.

Near-synonyms and boundaries.

  • Muted is the closest modern cousin, but it is usually abstract, applied to sound or colour ("muted tones"), while muffled keeps the physical image of cloth wrapped around a thing or a voice.
  • Wrapped is the plain physical core but says nothing of concealment or of sound; one can be wrapped in a towel without being muffled.
  • Enveloped suggests being completely closed over, often by something vast (fog, a mystery), with a dramatic, almost abstract ring; it drops the cloth-and-sound image.
  • Swaddled implies tight, infant-like binding and a tender or helpless overtone.
  • Concealed and disguised name the purpose — hiding identity — but drop the fabric and the sound entirely.
  • Hushed describes quiet speech or sound but without the image of a wrapping layer; it is a state of silence, not of deadened noise.
  • Stifled, smothered, deadened, dampened, dulled all describe suppressed sound but lack the wrapping-cloth image; stifled and smothered add a sense of difficulty or suffocation, while deadened is mechanical and dampened belongs to the sound-engineering sense of damp.
  • Deafened is the false friend: it means made unable to hear, whereas muffled means made hard to hear.

Muffled is a common, register-neutral word in modern English, and its distinguishing feature is that it yokes the tactile (wrapped in cloth) to the auditory (deadened) in a single image — the perfect word for a figure hidden and silenced at once.
