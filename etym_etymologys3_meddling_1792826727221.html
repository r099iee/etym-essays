The evolution of meddling is a fall from the kitchen into the quarrel. Its deepest ancestor is the reconstructed PIE root *meik-, to mix, which Latin inherited as miscēre, the ordinary verb of blending wine, mingling crowds, and mixing elements. Nothing in the root blames; mixtures are the cook's virtue and the crowd's nature.

In Vulgar Latin the verb sprouted a frequentative form, *misculāre, to keep on mixing, and this is the stem Old French received as medler or mesler. French kept the full spread of mixing: to blend flour, to mingle at a feast, to embroil oneself in a dispute, to meddle troops in battle array. The slide from stirring soup to stirring strife needed no invention, only company: one who mixes himself into other men's business stirs their mixture, and mixtures resent uninvited spoons.

Middle English borrowed the verb around the fourteenth century as medlen, and early attestations still show the honourable pole. Chroniclers write of knights meddling in battle — mingling in the press, contending hand to hand — with no more blame than mingling at a dance. Lovers too could meddle, a soft euphemism for dalliance. Beside these stood the blaming pole: meddling in marriages, in lawsuits, in holy things, where the mingling was unwelcome and the mixture spoiled.

Over the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries the neutral and noble senses withered. Armour left the battlefield and the verb with it; euphemisms for love moved on to fresher veils; what remained was the intruder's verb, sharpened by proverbs about neighbours, servants, and priests who cannot leave well alone. The spelling doubled its consonant to meddle and the participle meddling became a moral noun, naming both the act and the habit: meddling and makyng in old complaints, meddling busybodies in Restoration comedy.

The modern age has scaled the parish busybody to the planet. Newspaper English now speaks of meddling in elections, markets, and straits, where covert money and messages replace the neighbour's spoon yet keep the old verb, because the moral picture is unchanged: a household — now a nation — whose mixture is stirred by hands it never invited. Saruman's complaint in the source sentence sits exactly on the older private line: deep knowledge grown proud until counsel itself tastes like trespass, the wizard guarding his mixture against every spoon but his own.

Stages and bridges at a glance:

  • Mixing to mingling: Latin blending of things becomes French mingling of persons, the body entering the mixture as an ingredient.
  • Mingling to embroiling: company curdles into quarrel, so that to mix oneself in is to stir trouble, the physical metaphor doing the moral work.
  • Honourable to blameworthy: battlefield meddling dies with chivalry and amorous meddling with euphemism, stranding the intrusive sense as the survivor.
  • Act to habit: the participle hardens into a character noun, the meddler, and the abstract meddling of proverbs and novels.
  • Parish to planet: the same verb scales from neighbours to nations, and election meddling becomes the headline sense without changing the ancient picture.
  • Knowledge to pride in Tolkien: the source sentence moralises the etymology, showing how mastery claims ownership of its mixture and renames every outside touch as trespass.

So meddling today carries three thousand years of stirring inside a single sneer: every spoon, every finger, every uninvited hand that ever troubled a mixture, from the cook's pot to the ballot box, condensed into two syllables of blame.
