Chain for TURGID
1. Latin turgēre, 'to be swollen, to swell' — 1st c. BCE — of obscure origin; NO secure Proto-Indo-European reconstruction is established (various speculative links — to a root for 'to twist,' to *teuh₂- 'to swell' — have been floated, but none commands general assent; see the debate note below)
2. Latin turgidus, 'swollen, distended' — 1st c. CE (adjective derived from turgēre)
3. English turgid, 'swollen, distended' — 1610s CE (borrowed from Latin turgidus; the figurative sense 'bombastic, inflated' of language develops later)
4. Modern English turgid — present day (the physical sense survives in botany and medicine, where turgor, from Latin turgor, 'a swelling,' names cell pressure; the dominant modern sense is of style: 'bombastic, inflated')

Debated / nuanced points: Latin turgēre has no accepted PIE etymology; the standard handbooks (de Vaan, Walde–Hofmann, the OED) mark its origin as unknown. Note also the near-twin family of tumēre, 'to swell,' from PIE *teuh₂- — giving tumid, tumor, tumescent, and English thumb (via the Germanic 'swollen finger,' *thūmōn). The two families rhyme in sense but are distinct in origin; the chain above deliberately does not force turgēre into the PIE *teuh₂- family.


Chain for CONTEMPT
1. Proto-Indo-European *temh₁- (also *tem-), 'to cut' — reconstructed; c. 4500–3000 BCE
2. Greek τέμνω (témnō), 'to cut' — 8th c. BCE (cognate branch; the root gives tome, atom ('uncuttable'), anatomy, epitome)
3. Latin temnere, 'to slight, to despise' — 1st c. BCE (origin debated; traditionally connected with the 'cut' root — to despise is to 'cut off' from consideration — though the semantic development is not fully explained; see the debate note below)
4. Latin contemnere, 'to scorn utterly, to despise' — 1st c. BCE (Cicero; the intensive prefix con- + temnere)
5. Latin contemptus, 'scorned, despised' — past participle of contemnere, also used as a noun 'scorn, contempt' — 1st c. BCE (Cicero)
6. Old French contempt, 'scorn' — 12th c. CE (borrowed from Latin contemptus)
7. English contempt, 'the feeling that something is worthless; scorn' — 14th c. CE
8. Modern English contempt — present day (also the legal phrase contempt of court, 16th c. onward)

Debated / nuanced points: The link between Latin temnere, 'to despise,' and the 'cut' root is traditional and often repeated, but the semantic bridge ('to cut off' → 'to despise') is not universally accepted; some scholars treat temnere as of uncertain origin. The chain above presents the traditional connection while flagging the uncertainty. The cognate Greek témnō 'to cut' is secure in itself (giving atom, anatomy, epitome, tome), even if its specific link to temnere is debated.
