- 14th to 15th Century: Middle English enditement / enditementes - Perceived strictly as a technical Anglo-French legal instrument; represented the formal written presentment of a criminal accusation by a sworn inquest or royal grand jury before itinerant justices of the peace.
- 16th to 17th Century: Early Modern English pedantic spelling reform - Re-spelled with an etymological Latin c (indictment) under Renaissance humanist influence while preserving the Norman French pronunciation /ɪnˈdaɪt/; retained its solemn judicial terror during state treason trials under the Tudor and Stuart monarchies.
- 18th to 19th Century: Constitutional codification and figurative expansion - Cemented in common-law constitutions (most notably the Fifth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution) as an indispensable bulwark of individual liberty against arbitrary royal prosecution; simultaneously adopted by Romantic essayists and Victorian social reformers (such as Thomas Carlyle and Charles Dickens) to denote sweeping moral critiques of industrial poverty and institutional hypocrisy.
- 20th Century to Present: High-stakes geopolitical, criminal, and sociopolitical resonance - Evoked in international tribunals (such as Nuremberg and the ICC at The Hague) for crimes against humanity; widely employed in modern journalism and sociology to denote inescapable manifestations or statistical proof of systemic social decay (e.g., educational collapse or environmental devastation).
