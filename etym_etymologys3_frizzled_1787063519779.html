The written history of frizzle is comparatively short, yet it hides a genuine etymological puzzle, for the word has two overlapping life-stories: a hair-curl story that enters English from French in the sixteenth century, and a cooking story that emerges fully only in the nineteenth century. Both strands braid together into the modern adjective frizzled used by Tolkien in 1937.

1. The French "curl" stream. The oldest English member of the family is the verb friz (later frizz), attested from about 1550 in the sense "to curl (hair) into a tight, crisp mass." It is borrowed from French friser, a sixteenth-century verb meaning "to curl, to frizzle, to dress with curls." The French word's own ancestry is uncertain: the most widely repeated proposal derives it from a reconstructed Frankish (West Germanic) *frisil- or *fris- meaning "curl" or "frizzy lock," which would make it one of the many hairstyle words the Germanic tribes bequeathed to Gallo-Romance. No direct descendant of that Frankish etymon is attested, however, and some scholars prefer to see French friser as formed from an earlier Romance root with the sense "to brush, to graze." The question is genuinely open.

2. The frequentative step. About forty years after friz appears, English speakers form frizzle (1590s) by adding the frequentative suffix -le, which gives verbs a repeated, diminutive, action-by-action feel (compare sparkle from spark, joggle from jog, tumble from tumb). The result is a verb meaning literally "to friz repeatedly," i.e. to make tight little repeated curls. The participial adjective frizzled is born here: hair so curled is frizzled.

3. The transfer to singeing. Because frizzing in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was often achieved with heated curling tongs or irons, the tight curls of the fashionable coiffure were literally produced by heat. From this coincidence of method, the word slips naturally from "curl with hot irons" to "scorch so that the surface curls and shrivels." By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries frizzle is fully at home describing hair singed, skin blistered, or any surface shrivelled and crispened by flame. This is the sense Tolkien activates — Bilbo's hair is singed and frizzled right down to the skin.

4. The cooking stream (1838). In 1838 the verb gains its now-familiar culinary sense: "to fry until crisp and curled," especially of bacon. The development is an internal English semantic extension — no new loan is involved. Cooks observed that thin bacon fried on a hot pan does exactly what scorched hair does: it shrivels, curls at the edges, and grows crisp. The word's onomatopoeic sizzle made it irresistible in the kitchen, and "frizzled bacon" became standard in American and British cookery writing.

5. The modern spread. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries frizzled continues to divide its labour: culinary (crisp fried food), personal care (heat-damaged or naturally tight-curled hair, with the adjective frizzy and noun frizz growing in the same family), and informal metaphor (sun-baked, dried-up, exhausted). Its register remains playful and vivid — a word with fizz built into its sound.

6. Tolkien's attestation (1937). In The Hobbit, Chapter 12, the word sits in a sentence that glints with humour after the terror of Smaug's attack. Tolkien, a philologist deeply alive to word history, uses frizzle in its older scorching sense, pairing it with singe; the choice is lexicographically exact and emotionally comic, a professional's nod to the word's long-standing meaning.

7. The sound-sense logic. Phonetically the word is almost an ideophone: the f-r-z-l sequence mimics the spit, hiss, and crinkle of heat on damp or oily surfaces. That sonic mimicry is the hidden engine of its semantic drift — from curling tongs, to singed skin, to sizzling bacon.
