- Classical Latin — the philosopher's tool: notio names the mind's grasp of a thing, the concept formed when something becomes known — technical, Stoic, serious.
- Medieval Latin — the schoolmen's counter: scholars debate notiones as the mind's currency, sorting divine and common notions with scholastic precision.
- 1500s — English adopts it: notion enters as a learned word for conceptions and opinions, at home in essays and sermons, still wearing its Latin gown.
- 1600s — philosophy crowns it: Locke's Essay makes notion and idea rival coins of epistemology; every theory of knowledge must settle what a notion is.
- 1700s–1800s — the descent to whimsy: everyday speech loosens the word toward sudden fancies — taking notions — while Yankee pedlars pun it into notions, small clever wares.
- Today — the light-footed idea: notion lives in conversation as the modest, disarming word for thinking aloud — I've a notion — philosophy's ex-heir working happily in kitchens and hobbit-holes.
