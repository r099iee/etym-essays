Core meaning. A whim is a sudden, capricious idea or desire — a notion that seizes the mind without reason or warning and is as quickly dropped. It is the mental event of arbitrariness: the impulse to turn left instead of right, to buy the hat, to change the plans, to pardon or to punish, for no better cause than momentary inclination. In the passage above, the "unintelligible whim of the elements" is Providence at play: nature's water and weather obey no logic a city can bargain with, and Nineveh or Babylon can be dried to dust at the whim of rain that never comes.

Character and register. Whims are short-lived, personal, and mostly harmless — the stuff of anecdotes — but the word scales up dangerously: "at the whim of a tyrant" names arbitrary power, and in that phrase whim carries all the fear of caprice. The noun sits between fancy (softer, more imaginative) and impulse (more motoric, less playful). It is commonly a mass noun in idiom ("acting on a whim", "at whim", "every whim") and a count noun in anecdote ("she has her whims"). The adjective whimsical (1670s) and the noun whimsy relax the word into charm — the eccentric, the playful, the self-delighting — and mark its strange double life: delightful when indulged in oneself, alarming when suffered at another's hands.

  • Sudden fancy: a passing desire for food, travel, purchase; the ordinary, nearly daily sense.
  • Arbitrary decision: a rule, pardon, or ban issued "on a whim" — power acting without stated reason.
  • The whim of fate or nature: unpredictable fortune; the anthropomorphic play of chance (the sense in our passage).
  • Psychological caprice: in older psychology, a show of waywardness or restlessness; whim as a near-synonym of caprice.
  • Artistic whimsy: the 19th-century aesthetic of the whimsical — nonsense verse, grotesque ornament, playful digression (Sterne, Lear, Carroll).

Origin and semantic depth. Though the modern word is seventeenth century, it is a clipping of whim-wham, a Tudor word for a fanciful trifle or ornament — the dangling bauble that catches the eye for no reason. A whim is therefore, etymologically, a bauble of the mind: something glittering, decorative, and purposeless that the attention reaches for. The word has kept that flavour. Whims are never urgent, never principled, and never heavy; they are the mind's jewellery — and, like jewellery, they can be worn by tyrants as well as by children, and the wearer's rank decides whether we call the whim charming or terrible.

Whims in idiom and sentence. The word lives largely in fixed phrases, and each idiom tunes the meaning. "On a whim" describes a single unpremeditated action, morally neutral and often endearing. "At the whim of" is the dangerous one: it names dependence on another's arbitrary pleasure, and its objects are usually institutions, weather, markets, or despots — "at the whim of the elements" in our passage is exactly this construction. "Every whim" and "indulge a whim" belong to the vocabulary of privilege, the amusement of those who can afford caprice; "a passing whim" reassures that the desire, however odd, will not last. The verb is rare but recorded: to whim, "to give way to whims", now archaic. This phrase-bound life is itself a clue to the word's character: English reserves its fixed grammatical slots for ideas that culture needs constantly — and every era, it seems, needs a name for the arbitrariness that escapes reason.
