- [Late Antiquity & Migration Period (c. 4th–8th c. CE)] — introduced into Germanic dialects (*kokur-) from Eurasian nomadic horse-archer technology (Scythian/Hunnic), denoting stiffened leather cases designed to protect arrows on horseback - [perception: practical, rugged martial technology of cavalry archers]
- [High Middle Ages & Anglo-Norman Era (c. 11th–13th c.)] — transformed in Old French into *quivre* / *coivre* and imported into England after 1066, identifying the standard arrow case of knights and archers - [perception: essential military hardware of feudal archery and woodland hunters]
- [Late Middle English (c. 14th–15th c.; Chaucer)] — naturalized as *quivere*, celebrated as the vital companion to the English longbowmen at Crécy and Agincourt; began phonetic convergence with the unrelated verb *quiver* ("to tremble") - [perception: iconic emblem of martial prowess, yeomanry, and woodland archery]
- [Early Modern & Renaissance English (c. 16th–17th c.)] — popularized in King James Bible (Psalm 127) and classical translations (Apollo's and Cupid's quivers), shifting into romantic, poetic, and allegorical realms - [perception: poetic instrument of love's arrows or divine vengeance]
- [Modern 19th & 20th Century English] — extended metaphorically into political, legal, and economic prose to denote an individual's or institution's complete toolkit of strategies ("an arrow in one's quiver") - [perception: widely recognized rhetorical metaphor for strategic reserves]
- [Contemporary 21st Century Usage] — continues in archery, bowhunting, and sports literature, while also denominating the demographic conservative Christian "Quiverfull" movement - [perception: duality between specialized sporting gear and sociological-theological terminology]
