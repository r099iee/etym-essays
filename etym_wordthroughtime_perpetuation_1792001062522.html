- Late 16th to Early 17th Century — Entered Early Modern English via Middle French from Late Latin perpetuatio; used primarily in royal charters, ecclesiastical tracts, and philosophical works to describe eternal endowments, hereditary royal succession, and the divine sustaining of creation.
- 17th to 18th Century — Deeply entrenched in Anglo-American common law through legal disputes surrounding property entails, leading directly to the establishment of the "Rule Against Perpetuities" to prevent dead-hand control; simultaneously utilized in natural theology to describe cosmological cycles.
- 19th Century — Expanded into biological, evolutionary, and anthropological treatises (Darwin, Spencer) to denote the reproductive survival and genetic continuity of living organisms ("the perpetuation of the species"); featured prominently in political oratory regarding constitutional endurance (e.g., Lincoln's Lyceum Address).
- Mid to Late 20th Century — Acquired an increasingly critical sociological and political register, frequently appearing in academic critiques of systemic inequality, institutional racism, and the cyclic reproduction of poverty and class privilege.
- 21st Century — Fully established across dual registers: celebrated positively in ecological conservation (perpetuation of biodiversity) and corporate memory management, while challenged critically in sociopolitical activism aimed at dismantling the self-perpetuating structures of discrimination and corruption.
