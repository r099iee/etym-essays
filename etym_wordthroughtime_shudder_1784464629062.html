“Shudder” has meant a sudden convulsive trembling of the body since its earliest English use — the involuntary shake of fear, horror, cold, or disgust.
“Shudder” through the centuries extended from people to things: machines shudder under strain, buildings shudder in a quake, the ground shudders — the violent trembling image applied to the inanimate world.
“Shudder” gave English the idiom “I shudder to think” for a wincing reluctance to imagine something, and “shuddering” became stock vocabulary of horror fiction.
“Shudder” today remains the short, whole-body convulsion of dread — a jolt of fear that passes through the frame in a single wave, the very motion of a company shuddering at a dark name.
