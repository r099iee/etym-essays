Greek βραχίων (brakhíōn) "the upper arm" (attested in Homer; deeper origin debated — sometimes connected to βραχύς brakhýs "short", but no secure Indo-European etymology; a pre-Greek origin cannot be excluded) → Latin bracchium / brachium "arm, especially the forearm" (borrowed from Greek, attested from the 2nd c. BCE, e.g. Plautus) → Latin plural bracchia "the two arms" (the paired-arms image that drives the shift) → Old French brace "the two arms; an embrace; a pair; a span" (11th–12th c.) → Middle English brace "a pair; a clasp; a fastening; a support or prop" (borrowed from Anglo-Norman / Old French, 13th–14th c.) → Modern English brace "support, prop, fastening; a pair" and plural braces — trouser shoulder-straps in British English (19th c.) → BRACES Note: English brace / braces is a Romance loan from the Latin arm-word via French; it is NOT from the Germanic root *bʰreg- "to break" despite the superficial resemblance in spelling.
