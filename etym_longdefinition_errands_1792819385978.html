Errands, the plural of errand, means in its central modern sense short purposeful journeys undertaken to perform small tasks or services — fetching groceries, posting letters, collecting a parcel, dropping off laundry, paying a bill — usually local, routine, and combinable, so that a morning of errands strings half a dozen little missions into one outing.

In the source sentence the word appears in a gnomic, almost proverbial line — Where many paths and errands meet — where it carries a slightly archaic, purposeful colour: not just shopping trips but the comings and goings of busy lives, every traveller on some small mission, all crossing at one meeting-place. The line treats errands as the very emblem of everyday human business.

Core meaning in detail: The prototypical errand has four features: it is short (an hour, not a voyage), practical (something must be fetched, delivered, or done), directed by another or by necessity (one runs errands for a household, an employer, or oneself under obligation), and completable (each errand ends with a ticked-off task). Children are sent on errands to learn responsibility; busy adults batch them into efficient rounds; offices once kept an errand boy for exactly this running about.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • A short trip to do a small task (primary modern sense): to run errands, to go on an errand — the Saturday round of shops, bank, and post office. The bridge is direct inheritance from the oldest sense below, shrunk from solemn missions to household chores.
  • The task or purpose itself (transferred sense): What is your errand here asks not how you travelled but what business brings you. The bridge is metonymy: the journey stands for the purpose that caused it, so the word slides from road to reason.
  • A solemn mission or message to deliver (archaic, earliest sense): in Old and Middle English an ærende was a message or mission entrusted by a lord, even a divine commission — angels and ambassadors bore errands. The bridge to the modern sense is democratisation: what kings once imposed on messengers, households now impose on whoever holds the shopping list.
  • A fool’s errand (idiom, from the 16th c.): a hopeless or absurd undertaking, like sending a novice for striped paint or a left-handed screwdriver. The bridge is mockery of the messenger sense: the messenger runs faithfully, but the mission itself is a joke, so the phrase now names any doomed quest.
  • An errand of mercy (fixed phrase): a compassionate mission — nursing the sick, comforting the bereaved, carrying aid. The bridge revives the old solemn sense deliberately: calling it an errand dignifies humble service with the gravity of a commission.
  • Errand boy or errand girl (social role, 19th–20th c.): a junior employee or youngster whose job is running such tasks. The bridge is occupational: the person is named for the journeys, and the term later broadened into an insult for anyone treated as a mere gofer.

Register and connotation: errands is warm, domestic, and unpretentious — the vocabulary of Saturdays, helpfulness, and list-ticking rather than of adventure. It suggests diligence without grandeur, and it almost always appears in the plural, because errands multiply: nobody runs just one. The singular survives mainly in fixed phrases and questions — on an errand, state your errand — where a faint old formality still clings to it.
