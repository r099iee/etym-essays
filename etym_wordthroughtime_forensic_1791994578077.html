- 17th Century (c. 1650s) — Borrowed directly into Early Modern English from Latin forensis; exclusively used to denote matters pertaining to courts of law, public judicial advocacy, and legal argumentation (e.g., "forensic rhetoric" or "forensic disputation").
- 18th to Early 19th Century — Solidified within academic, university, and parliamentary institutions as the formal designation for competitive debating societies and disputation clubs ("forensic societies"); simultaneously began qualifying legal-medical treatises as "forensic medicine."
- Mid to Late 19th Century — Underwent a major semantic pivot toward empirical science as pioneering criminologists (toxicologists, fingerprint analysts, autopsy surgeons) systematized the application of physical sciences to criminal jurisprudence.
- 20th Century — Witnessed explosive domain branching into forensic pathology, ballistics, forensic accounting, and DNA profiling; the pluralized noun "forensics" emerged as a popular collective term for both criminal investigative science and intercollegiate speech/debate competition.
- 21st Century — Pervaded global popular entertainment through crime-scene investigation television franchises (the "CSI effect"); expanded metaphorically into general corporate and political parlance to signify any ruthlessly detailed, microscopic factual audit.
