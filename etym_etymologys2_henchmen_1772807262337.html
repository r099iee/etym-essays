First element — hengest 'stallion':
Pre-Germanic stage unknown; the stallion-word is attested only in Germanic and has no secure Indo-European etymon (ultimate origin uncertain — see note) → Proto-Germanic *hangistaz 'stallion, gelding, male horse' (reconstructed) → Old English hengest 'stallion, gelding, horse' (attested in Beowulf and in glosses, 8th–11th c.; also the name of the legendary Jutish leader Hengest).

Second element — man 'person':
PIE *man- 'person, human being' (reconstructed; debated — some scholars derive it from a root meaning 'to think', cf. Sanskrit mánu- 'human being, man' and the 'mind' family; others treat it as isolated) → Proto-Germanic *mannaz 'person, human being' → Old English man(n) 'person, human being; servant, vassal' (attested from the 8th c.) → Middle English man 'person, servant' → Modern English man.

Compound path to the plural:
Old English hengest + man → hengest-man / hengesman 'groom, horse-servant' (compound formed in Old English, 10th–11th c.) → Middle English henxman / henchman 'attendant of rank, page of honour, personal follower' (14th c.; the horse-keeping sense receding) → Early Modern English henchman 'personal attendant, loyal supporter' (15th–18th c.) → Modern English henchman 'trusted follower, especially a criminal or unscrupulous underling' (pejorative sense established 19th c.) → plural henchmen.

Note on the debate and lineage: the ultimate source of *hangistaz is unknown; a connection to a PIE root for 'prickle, bristle, tail' has been suggested but is not generally accepted. There is no direct Romance loan in the compound: henchmen is purely a Germanic compound lineage.
