LINEAR CHAIN (single root word PORTENTS — the plural of PORTENT, a loanword from Latin; the Latin compound portendere itself joins the prefix por-/pro- with the verb tendere) Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ten- "to stretch, extend, pull taut, spread out" (c. 4000 BCE); a root of physical extension, attested in a vast family: Greek teinein "to stretch", Sanskrit tanoti "he stretches", Latin tenere "to hold" and tendere "to stretch". Stage 2 — Proto-Italic *tendō "to stretch" → Classical Latin tendere "to stretch, extend, aim, direct" (attested from the 3rd century BCE in Plautus and Ennius); the verb of spreading a cord, a sail, a hand, or a trap. Stage 3 — Latin portendere "to stretch forth, extend forward; to foretell, signify as an omen" (attested from the 1st century BCE, e.g. Cicero and Livy); formed from the old prefix por- (the archaic form of pro-, "before, forward, forth", from PIE *per-/*prō "before, forward, in front") + tendere "to stretch". The literal image is of the gods "stretching a sign forth" into the observer's path — hence "to portend, to betoken". Stage 4 — Latin portentum "a sign, token, omen, prodigy; a portentous or monstrous occurrence" (attested from the 1st century BCE); the neuter past participle of portendere, used as a noun: that which has been stretched forth as a warning. In Roman religion a portentum was a prodigy — an unnatural event (monstrous birth, eclipse, lightning strike) that the state consulted augurs and haruspices to expiate. Stage 5 — English portent "a sign or warning of a momentous event to come; an omen, a prodigy" (borrowed directly from Latin in the late 16th century; first recorded in English c. 1570 in the writing of Edmund Spenser). The plural portents follows the regular English plural formation. Final node — Modern English PORTENTS "signs or warnings that something momentum-altering is about to happen"; PORTENTS. NOTE ON THE TWO ELEMENTS: The single rootWord PORTENT is a Latin simplex loan, but the Latin word is internally a compound of two PIE-rooted elements. Element 1: PIE *per-/*prō "before, forward" → Latin pro-/por- "before, forth". Element 2: PIE *ten- "to stretch" → Latin tendere "to stretch". The Latin portendere then means literally "to stretch forward" — the root metaphor of a sign stretched forth along the path of the future. NOTE ON DEBATED ETYMOLOGY: The etymology is secure: Latin portentum < portendere < por- + tendere is firmly attested and uncontroversial. One nuance: the classical sense of portentum included the specific meaning "monstrous birth, prodigy to be expiated by ritual", which the English word preserved only weakly, so that modern English "portent" is somewhat narrower than its Latin ancestor.
