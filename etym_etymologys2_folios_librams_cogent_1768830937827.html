CHAIN 1 FOR FOLIOS: PIE *bʰel- (also reconstructed as *bʰleh₃- / *bʰelH-) — "to bloom, flourish, grow" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ PIE *bʰolyo-m — "leaf" (o-grade stem with -yo- suffix; the source of both Latin folium and Greek φύλλον phúllon) ↓ Latin folium — "leaf (of a plant); sheet of paper, leaf of a book" (attested Classical Latin, c. 1st c. BCE) ↓ Medieval Latin / book-trade folium, folio — "a leaf or sheet of paper; a book in which sheets are folded once" (attested c. 13th–15th c.) ↓ English folio — "a sheet of paper folded once; a large-format book made on such sheets" (attested 15th c.) ↓ Modern English folios — plural, "large-format volumes; sheets or leaves" (present day) CHAIN 2 FOR LIBRAMS: PIE *leubʰ- — "to peel, strip off" (reconstructed; the bark-peeling root; c. 4500–2500 BCE) [DEBATED — see note] ↓ Latin liber, libr- — "inner bark of a tree (used as writing material); a book" (attested Classical Latin, 2nd–1st c. BCE; the bark-sense is the older one, and the "book" sense grows from it because early Romans wrote on bark) ↓ English libram — "an ancient magical book" — a deliberate literary coinage by Jack Vance (1950, The Dying Earth / "Turjan of Miir"), shaped to evoke the Latin form librum (accusative of liber, "book") ↓ Modern English librams — plural in Vance's usage, "ancient magical books" (present day) CHAIN 3 FOR COGENT: PIE *h₂eǵ- — "to drive, lead, impel" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) ↓ Latin agere — "to drive, to set in motion, to do, to act" (attested 3rd–2nd c. BCE) ↓ Latin cōgere — "to drive together, collect, force, compel" (con- + agere; attested 2nd c. BCE, e.g. Cicero) ↓ Latin cōgēns, cōgent- — "compelling, forcing" (present participle of cōgere; attested Classical Latin) ↓ English cogent — "compelling, forcible, convincing" (borrowed c. 1650s, via French cogent or directly from the Latin participle) ↓ Modern English cogent — "forceful and convincing, especially of argument or evidence" (present day) NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) For FOLIOS, the exact PIE root form (whether *bʰel-, *bʰelH-, or *bʰleh₃-) is debated, and some scholars treat the agreement between Latin folium and Greek phúllon as evidence of a shared or borrowed leaf-word rather than straightforward core inheritance; the intermediate Latin and English steps are securely attested. (2) For LIBRAMS, the derivation of Latin liber from PIE *leubʰ- "to peel" is debated — alternative proposals derive it from other IE roots or treat it as a substrate loanword; and the English word libram is not a historically inherited word at all but a mid-20th-century authorial coinage, so the chain runs directly from the Latin source with no genuine intermediate historical stages. (3) For COGENT, the chain is well attested and uncontroversial.
