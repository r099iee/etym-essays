Remnant is a noun meaning "a small remaining part or number of something after the greater part has been removed, consumed, destroyed or scattered". It is a word built for loss: it presupposes a larger whole that is now gone or diminished, and it gathers up what is left — a handful of survivors, a scrap of cloth, a surviving trace of a former age. In the reading, Roäc the raven tells Thorin that "thirteen is small remnant of the great folk of Durin that once dwelt here" — the whole tragedy of the dwarves' dispersion, the fall of a great people to thirteen wanderers, compressed into a single noun.

Core senses:

  • A small surviving number of people (a survivor group): "a remnant of the army", "the last remnant of a tribe". Usually carries an elegiac undertone — the remnant is what escaped destruction or dispersal. This is the sense in the card context (the remnant of Durin's folk).
  • A leftover portion of a material, especially cloth: in the textile and drapery trade a "remnant" is the piece of fabric left on a roll after the rest has been cut and sold — large enough to be useful, cheap enough to be a bargain. "Remnant sales", "remnant stalls" and "remnant shops" have been fixtures of the clothing trade for centuries, and the word retains this commercial concreteness today ("a remnant of tweed for a cushion cover").
  • A surviving fragment or trace: "remnants of a meal", "remnants of a wall", "remnants of snow", "remnants of an empire". This generalizes the cloth sense: any material residue of an earlier state.
  • The theological sense — the Remnant: in Judeo-Christian scripture and preaching, "the remnant" is the faithful minority preserved by God through judgment and catastrophe — the saving residue of Israel. The prophets build a whole theology of the remnant, and Saint Paul applies it to the early Church (Romans 11:5). In religious register the word is capitalized and numinous: the remnant is not an accident but a promise.
  • Scientific and technical jargon: astronomy uses "supernova remnant" for the expanding shell of material ejected when a star explodes (the Crab Nebula is the archetype); cosmology and physics speak of "remanent" (or relic) radiation; ecology of "remnant populations" — small isolated groups of a species surviving in habitat fragments; medicine of "remnant cholesterol". Each use extends the core idea, "what is left after the event", into a precise technical domain.
  • A related learned doublet: the adjective remanent ("remaining, residual") — as in "remanent magnetism", the magnetization left in iron after the applied field is removed — is a 19th-century reborrowing from the same Latin ancestor, while remainder is an older doublet of the same descent via Old French.

Semantic bridge: all senses flow from the Latin verb remanēre, "to remain behind, to stay on after others have gone". Its participle remanēns ("remaining, that which is left") passes through Old French as the noun remanant, "the rest, the remainder", and enters Middle English as remenaunt. The single underlying image is the remainder of a subtraction: the whole minus the departed equals the remnant. The word's emotional temperature is set by context alone — it can be as flat as a shopkeeper's price tag or as grave as a funeral sermon.

Register and grammar: countable ("a remnant", "two remnants"); mildly formal — in casual speech one says "what's left", "leftovers", "the rest". "Remnant" tends to be chosen when the speaker wants to dignify or mourn what remains, which is why it appears so often in historical, biblical and elegiac prose; and precisely because of that gravity it is never wholly neutral.
