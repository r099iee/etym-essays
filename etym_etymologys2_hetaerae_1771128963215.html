Earliest stage. The ultimate root is the Proto-Indo-European reflexive/possessive base *swe- "self, one's own", which in Greek produced the family of words meaning "one's own people, one's own group, kin". This is a well-attested PIE root (Latin suus, Sanskrit sva-, Gothic swes "one's own").

Derivation chain:

  • Proto-Indo-European *swe- "self, one's own" (reconstructed; c. 4500–2500 BCE) — the reflexive base also underlying Latin suus and Sanskrit sva-.
  • Ancient Greek hetaîros, also hetâiros, "companion, comrade, mate, associate" (attested from Homer, c. 8th century BCE) — formed on the base *swe-t-ro- / *swe-tero- "belonging to one's own group", i.e. "one of one's own people". Some scholars trace it to het- + the suffix, but the connection to *swe- "own" is the standard view (cf. Latin socius via a related root).
  • Ancient Greek hetaíra, feminine of hetaîros, "female companion, courtesan" (attested from the 5th century BCE; used by Herodotus, Xenophon, Demosthenes, and in Attic comedy) — semantically narrowed from "companion" to "elite courtesan".
  • Latin hetaera, "courtesan" (learned borrowing from Greek in the Roman period; attested in Latin authors such as Nepos and in Roman comedy's Latin adaptations).
  • English hetaera, plural hetaerae, a scholarly loanword revived in the 18th–19th centuries in classical studies and historical writing (attested in English from c. 1800 onward, e.g. in works on ancient Greece).
  • HETAERAE (present-day English, learned plural).

Debate note: The internal derivation of Greek hetaîros is debated: the traditional connection to *swe- "one's own" is supported by the meaning "one's own people/companions", but some linguists prefer a link to het- (cf. hetás "comrade") of uncertain origin, or to the root of Latin socius "ally, companion" (PIE *sekʷ- "to follow"). Both routes yield the same Greek form and meaning; the reconstruction of the pre-Greek intermediate is the uncertain element.
