How is the modern English reflex of Old English hū, and its history is the history of the Indo-European interrogative pronoun in its instrumental case. The reconstructed root *kʷo- underlies the interrogative stems of most branches: Latin quis "who" and quod "what", Greek τίς (tís), Sanskrit ka-, Slavic kъto, Hittite kui-, and the Germanic family represented by Old English hwā "who" and hwæt "what". The instrumental case of that pronoun — "by what, with what" — became specialised as the manner adverb, and it is this form that survives as how.

The Germanic stage

  • Proto-Germanic had *hwaz "who" and an instrumental *hwō, glossed "how, by what means".
  • The cognates agree on the shape: Old Saxon hwō, Old Frisian hū, Old High German hwuo (modern German wie has been remodelled), Gothic hvaiwa.
  • Old English hū is attested from the earliest texts in both interrogative and conjunctive uses; the formula hū þæt ("how is it that") is a characteristic Old English construction.
  • One genuine philological difficulty: the Old English vowel ū is not the regular outcome of Proto-Germanic ō, which normally remains ō in Old English. Old Frisian shows the same ū, so the raising is at least Anglo-Frisian, but the handbooks differ: some posit a variant *hwū, others treat the form as an unexplained irregularity. The point is debated and should not be smoothed over.

Middle English

Middle English spellings — hou, hu, how — reflect the loss of the final vowel and the later Great Vowel Shift, which turned the long ū into the modern /aʊ/. The word's syntactic range widened considerably. It remained the ordinary interrogative of manner, but it also developed a conjunctive use meaning "that": "he told me how the king was dead" could report a fact rather than a method, and the fixed phrase how that made the subordination explicit. This is the period in which how begins to behave like a complementizer, a role it shares with that in older English and in some dialects still.

Early modern expansion

  • However and howsoever are formed in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, originally as phrases meaning "in whatever way", later as concessive and contrastive connectives.
  • Howbeit, from how be it, is a common fifteenth- and sixteenth-century conjunction meaning "although"; it survives in archaic prose and in the Authorized Version's style.
  • Exclamatory how becomes a staple of Renaissance poetry and preaching — "How art thou fallen from heaven" — where the word measures a fall or a wonder rather than asking a question.
  • How now? is a stock expostulation on the early modern stage, and how do you do? establishes itself as a polite formula in the seventeenth century.

Modern developments

  • The nineteenth century produces the colloquial and technical compounds: know-how (American, 1838), how come (by 1848), and how! as emphatic assent, and how-to as an attributive.
  • How's that? becomes a fixed appeal in cricket, addressed to the umpire, and survives as a catchphrase of the game.
  • The greeting How! in Western films derives from Dakota hau, not from English how; the resemblance is accidental, and the two words have no historical connection.
  • In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word dominates instructional registers — how-to books, explainers, tutorials — and acquires new discourse functions in speech ("how good is that?", "how about we leave").

The literary construction in the source

The fragment "in how altered circumstances" belongs to a conservative, rhetorically shaped English in which how can still modify an adjective inside a prepositional phrase. In ordinary modern syntax the word is extracted to the front of a clause and cannot be the complement of a preposition. The construction therefore marks the passage as deliberate, slightly old-fashioned prose — and it is precisely this archaic freedom that lets the writer turn a plain statement about changed times into an exclamation. The history of how is, in miniature, the history of English syntax: an old case-form of the interrogative pronoun, generalised into a degree word, a complementizer and a relative, and still productive after a thousand years.
