1. An imitative birth. Unlike most English vocabulary, chatter has no Indo-European pedigree: it is a native Germanic formation of the onomatopoeic type, created to imitate the rapid, repeated cries of birds. Middle English chateren is recorded from about 1200 (early 13th century) in exactly these senses — “to twitter, to make bird-like noises” and “to talk rapidly, jabber”. The word belongs to a large family of English frequentatives built with the Germanic suffix -er that denote repeated small actions and sounds — chitter, clatter, flicker, flutter, patter — and it is probably a twin of chitter (Middle English chiteren), with which it has exchanged sounds and senses for eight centuries. Old English already had the related imitative clatrian “to clatter”.

2. Sound symbolism. The phonetics of the word are the etymology: the initial affricate /tʃ/ is a noisy, “busy” sound; the reduplicated rhythm of cha-cha-cha imitates rapid repetition; and the frequentative -er suffix repeats the action, so the word itself is a miniature sound-picture of what it denotes. This is why parallel languages, with no genetic connection, built parallel words: Latin garrīre “to chatter” and garrulus “talkative, chattering” — Vergil calls the swallow garrula in the Georgics — are equally imitative, as are Greek and Slavic words of the same type. English chatter and Latin garrīre are thus a striking case of convergent sound symbolism, not of common descent.

3. From birds to people. The earliest attested sense is avian, but the human sense — rapid, idle talk — follows immediately (c. 1200). Throughout Middle English and into the early modern period, chatter and chattering describe gossip, nagging and empty talk with a dismissive tone; the word carried a social judgement from the start: chattering speech is sound without substance. This pejorative colouring intensifies in the 17th–18th centuries with compounds like chatterbox (recorded 1774) and chitter-chatter, and it persists in the modern phrase chattering classes (British journalism, 1980s), which dismisses liberal commentators as mere talkers — noise-makers in the public sphere.

4. Teeth: the body joins in. By the 17th–18th centuries the sound was transferred from voices to the body: “his teeth chattered” becomes a stock phrase for cold, fear and fever. The involuntary clicking of teeth in the shivering or terror response sounds exactly like rapid speech, so the teeth seem to “talk” — the semantic bridge is purely auditory. The image became a favourite of Gothic and Victorian fiction, where chattering teeth signal dread, and of medical writing, where chattering is a symptom of ague, malaria and exposure. The participial adjective chattering (“chattering teeth”) is the form that crystallised in this sense, and it is the form in this card's context: the Master, cornered by the crowd's shout for King Bard, grinds his chattering teeth in impotent rage.

5. Machines and the modern world. In the 20th century the word took its last major step, from sounds to objects: engineers applied chatter to the self-excited vibration of cutting tools and machine parts — the rapid rattling that ruins a finish on a lathe or mill. From machining the term spread to any unwanted rapid oscillation (relay chatter, brake chatter, valve chatter). In the same period, media and intelligence usage developed chatter for the low-level buzz of rumour, gossip and signal (“online chatter”, “radio chatter”), completing the word's drift from the bird's twitter to the noise of the whole modern information environment.

6. Register today. Modern chattering remains mildly pejorative for people, neutral-descriptive for animals and teeth, and purely technical for machines. Its eight-century career — from the medieval magpie to the chattering classes to the chattering lathe — is a single unbroken chain of imitative sound-extension: one of the clearest examples in English of a word whose history is written in its own phonetics.
