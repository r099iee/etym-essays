PIE *weid- "to see; to know" (c. 4500–3000 BCE; ablaut variants *weyd-/*wid-; the perfect stem *woid-/*wid- "I have seen" > "I know" underlies the knowledge-sense) → Proto-Germanic *witaną "to know" (c. 500 BCE–200 CE) → Proto-Germanic *witją (neuter ja-stem) "understanding, wit, knowledge" (reconstructed) → Old English witt / gewitt "mind, understanding, intellect, sense" (8th–11th c.) → Middle English wit / wyt, plural wittes "mind, reason, sanity, senses" (12th–15th c.) → Modern English WIT (16th c. onward) Note: the "know" sense of *weid- is the inherited PIE perfect of the see-root — "I have seen, therefore I know" — a stative shift visible also in Sanskrit véda, Greek oîda, and in the family of Latin vidēre. The intermediate Proto-Germanic noun *witją is reconstructed from the attested verbal stem *witaną and its Old English and Old Saxon reflexes; no attested stage is skipped and none is invented beyond the standard reconstructed PGmc forms.
