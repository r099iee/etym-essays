- c. 1400–1500: Introduced into Late Middle English from Medieval Latin (*canonicus*), applied strictly to the authoritative books of the Bible admitted into the Christian canon and to the established statutes of ecclesiastical law (*canonical law*, *canonical hours*).
- c. 1500–1700: Retained strong religious authority in Early Modern English, distinguishing divinely inspired biblical texts from apocryphal writings, and ordained clergy (*canons*) from laymen.
- c. 1700–1850: Extended gradually into secular scholarship, mathematics, and philosophy to denote standard, orthodox, or authoritative models and equations.
- c. 1850–1970: Widely adopted in literary criticism and art history to designate works of art, authors, and texts acknowledged as belonging to the recognized "literary canon" of high culture.
- c. 1970–Present: Massively expanded into popular culture and fandoms (distinguishing official story elements "canon" from fan fiction / "headcanon"), as well as computer science (canonical data models, canonical URL tags) and critical theory (Roland Barthes's critique of the institutionalized, canonical state of language).
