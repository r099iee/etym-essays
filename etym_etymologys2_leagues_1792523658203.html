Gaulish / Celtic substrate (c. 1st c. BCE)
*leuk- / *leuga
"a Gallic walking-mile, an hour's march"
(indigenous Celtic unit of distance)
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE; Ammianus Marcellinus)
leuga / leuca
"Gallic mile; measure of 1,500 paces"
↓
Old French (c. 11th c.; Chanson de Roland)
lieue
"league, travel distance of approx. an hour"
↓
Anglo-Norman / Middle English (c. 14th c.; Wycliffe, Chaucer)
leuge / lighe / leeg
"linear measure of approx. three miles"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 16th c.; Shakespeare, Hakluyt)
league
"distance of three miles; vast expanse"
↓
Modern English (plural form) (present)
leagues
"units of distance (approx. three miles each)"

Note on convergent homonymy: The English word league exhibits two completely distinct etymological roots that merged phonologically. The linear measure of distance shown above derives from Gaulish *leuga via Late Latin and Old French. By contrast, the homonym league meaning an alliance or confederation (as in the League of Nations or sports leagues) derives from PIE *leyǵ- ("to bind, tie") via Classical Latin ligāre, Old Italian lega, and French ligue, entering English in the mid-15th century.
