Councillors (British spelling; American councilors) is the plural of councillor, a noun meaning a member of a council — a deliberative, advisory, or administrative body of people gathered to discuss, decide, or recommend. In the source context from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the Master of Lake-town “spoke to the Master and his councillors” — that is, to the town's leading men who form his governing board. The word names people whose standing comes not from wealth or force alone but from office and deliberation.

Primary sense

  • A member of a council. The core meaning: someone appointed or elected to sit on a council. Councils are typically bodies for local government, deliberative assemblies, advisory boards, or corporate and ecclesiastical governing groups. Councillors take part in collective decision-making: they debate, vote, advise, and administer.
  • In the context of local government, especially in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, and other Commonwealth countries, “councillor” is the standard title for an elected member of a local authority (city, borough, county, or town council). A councillor is addressed as “Councillor Smith”.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Advisers to a ruler or institution. Historically, councillors were the trusted men and women who advised kings, princes, bishops, and guild masters — the “privy council”, “royal councillors”, “town councillors” of pre-modern Europe. This is the sense Tolkien uses: the Master's councillors are the chief men of Lake-town who help him govern.
  • Members of a deliberative or legislative body. In universities (senates and academic councils), churches (parish and diocesan councils), and professional bodies, councillors sit on governing committees. Here the word is formal and institutional.
  • Corporate and organizational councils. Some companies, unions, and associations have councils whose members are called councillors (e.g., a works council, a student council).
  • The spelling doublet “councillor” vs “counselor”. “Councillor” (with two l's and -il-) denotes a council member; “counselor” (with -se-) denotes one who gives counsel — a lawyer, a therapist, an academic adviser. The words are historical doublets from the same Latin source and are frequently confused; the semantic boundary is office (councillor) versus function (counselor).

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge

Both “council” and “councillor” descend from Latin consilium “deliberation, advice, counsel”. The bridge from “advice” to “adviser” to “office-holder” is straightforward: one who gives counsel to a ruler is institutionalised as an office, and once the office exists it can be elective and plural. As government democratised in the 19th century, the aristocratic “councillor at court” became the elected “town councillor”, and the word's centre of gravity moved from advising a monarch to representing a public. In all senses the common thread is collective, institutional deliberation: councillors exist because decisions are made by bodies, not by individuals alone.

Usage notes

  • British English spells the word councillor (double l); American English uses councilor (single l). The British spelling preserves the French-derived -ill-.
  • The singular is “councillor”; the plural “councillors” is the form in the source text.
  • “Councillor” is a title and is capitalised when used with a name: “Councillor Jones”.
  • The word is always used of a person who is a member of a council; it is never used of a council's staff or of someone merely advising it informally.
  • In many jurisdictions the title has legal force: only persons elected to the local authority may call themselves councillors.
