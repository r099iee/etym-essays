WARGS are the great evil wolves of Tolkien's Middle-earth — creatures far larger and more cunning than natural wolves, allied with the goblins, capable of coordinated hunting and, in places, of speech. In The Hobbit, the capitalized term is introduced with a deliberate act of naming: "even the wild wargs (for so the evil wolves over the Edge of the Wild were named) cannot climb trees." The word does double duty: it marks these wolves as a distinct, hostile nation of the wilderness — a proper-name species, not mere beasts — while the aside's matter-of-fact tone, a narrator glossing his own vocabulary as Tolkien's narrator so often does, gives the word an anthropological weight, as if naming an unknown tribe. The tone is at once scholarly and ominous: the parenthesis calmly files the Wargs under the natural history of the Wild, and their howling, gleaming-eyed presence looms all the larger for being catalogued. A warg in Tolkien is never simply a wolf: it is the wolf of Nordic legend, the vargr, outlaw and devourer, raised into fantasy's first true war-beast, and the capitalized plural makes it a species, a nation, an army.

The polysemy of warg in modern English is a layered stack of senses built on a single ancient word:
  • Sense 1 — Tolkien's Wargs (the sense of this card): the giant, malevolent, quasi-sentient wolves of The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings, allied with goblins, intelligent enough for coordinated hunting and sometimes speech; capitalized as a proper noun naming a race.
  • Sense 2 — the ancient Germanic word behind them: Old English wearg and Old Norse vargr meant "outlaw, felon, accursed one" — and, by a famous taboo-euphemism, "wolf"; Tolkien's Wargs deliberately revive this older semantic stratum, so every use of the modern word carries the ancient overtones of exile, curse, and the feared nameless beast.
  • Sense 3 — the generic fantasy warg: after Tolkien, "warg" became the standard genre term for the intelligent evil war-beast of epic fantasy, spread through role-playing games, wargaming, and later fantasy fiction; it names a category of creature, not just Tolkien's own.
  • Sense 4 — the gaming respelling worg: Dungeons & Dragons (mid-1970s) respelled the word "worg" for its own monster, and the spelling worg now names a distinct fictional species in tabletop and video-game settings.
  • Sense 5 — the verb "to warg": in A Song of Ice and Fire (from the 1990s) "to warg" means to enter and control an animal's mind, a skinchanger's art; the verb extends the noun's sense of mastery over beasts.
  • Sense 6 — metaphorical and derived uses: "warg-like", "warging", and similar coinages carry the word's aura of predatory intelligence; in Old Norse scholarship the word also still lives as a learned term for the outlaw sense, so modern warg carries both an ancient legal register and a modern genre register at once.

The historical bridge is a story of fear and naming. The Proto-Indo-European root behind the word meant "to press, oppress, choke" — from it Germanic formed a word for the man condemned to death by strangling, then the outlaw, the accursed outcast of human law. In the old North the wolf was so feared that men would not speak its true name and called it instead the vargr, the strangler, the outcast of the forest; so thoroughly did the taboo work that in modern Swedish the everyday word for wolf is still varg, the old euphemism having displaced the plain Norse úlfr. Tolkien, a professor of Anglo-Saxon steeped in Old Norse, reached for precisely this word when he needed a name for his great wolves, and the etymological weight — exile, curse, strangling, the wolf one dare not name — is woven into every appearance of the Wargs.

In register, warg is a proper noun and a genre term, capitalized in Tolkien, lowercase in generic use. It is not a natural-history word: no one uses "warg" for a real wolf. Its emotional colouring is uniformly ominous — evil, cunning, allied, hungry — and its plural form WARGS, as in the card, is the form under which the creatures enter the story as a hostile collective. In grammar it is a regular plural of a noun that behaves like a proper name: the Wargs, a warg, to warg, warging.
