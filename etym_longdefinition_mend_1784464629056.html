Mend is a verb meaning to repair something broken, torn, damaged, or defective and restore it to sound or usable condition, and by natural extension to improve, correct, cure, or heal — whether a physical object, a fault of character, a quarrel between persons, or the failing health of a sick body. The word descends as the aphetic (initial-syllable-losing) short form of amend, ultimately from Latin ēmendāre "to free from fault, correct," and its oldest and still central image is the removal of a defect: mend is the verb of making whole again what has gone wrong. In the narrator's aside from The Hobbit — "they can't make a thing for themselves, not even mend a little loose scale of their armour" — the bare infinitive after even sets the lowest imaginable bar of competence. The hobbits of the passage are market-wise consumers who know the current value of work but cannot perform it; they cannot even refasten a single loose scale of armour. The verb is deliberately unheroic and domestic, drawn from the world of sewing, net-mending, and farm repairs, and it stands in pointed contrast to the forging and smithcraft that the dwarves possess and the adventure will demand of Bilbo. Tone: dry social comedy with a sting of economic satire. Core senses.
  • Repair (transitive, concrete): to restore a broken, torn, worn, or defective thing to sound condition — to mend a coat, a net, a fence, a broken tool, a hinge; also to repair any damaged fabric, including the loose scale of armour in the text.
  • Restore, heal (of the body): to cure or make healthy again; intransitively, to be mending or to mend means to recover from illness, as in "she is mending nicely" or the idiom "on the mend."
  • Improve, correct (abstract): to set right a fault, error, or bad habit — to mend one's ways (to reform one's conduct), to mend one's manners; to make something better than it was.
  • Heal (of relationships): to restore harmony or repair a rift — to mend a quarrel, to mend fences (to repair strained relations, from the image of repairing boundary fences between neighbours).
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and idiomatic senses.
  • "On the mend": recovering from illness or difficulty — a fixed phrase from the health sense, common since the 19th century.
  • "Mend fences": to restore damaged relations — originally the rural image of repairing the boundary fence between properties, transferred to diplomacy and personal reconciliation.
  • "Mend one's ways": to improve one's behaviour — the moral sense of correction, attested since the 16th century.
  • Of cloth and handwork: darning, patching, and reweaving — "to mend a stocking," the homely trade of the seamstress, now extended to any small repair.
  • Figurative "make whole": in modern usage the word is used of healing wounds, repairing an economy, or restoring trust, always carrying the underlying image of making something whole again that was broken.
Historical and semantic bridges. The bridge between the concrete and abstract senses is the core idea of a defect or fault that must be removed. Latin menda and mendum mean "blemish, fault, defect"; ēmendāre "to free from fault" yields Old French amender and Middle English amenden, and the aphetic short form menden splits off to become modern mend. The same image of a fault explains why the word can repair a seam, a quarrel, a man's ways, or a body's health: each is a defect to be corrected. The domestic flavour of the modern word comes from centuries of use for mending clothes and tools; the moral and diplomatic uses are medieval, from the religious and chivalric language of "amending one's life." Differentiation. Repair is close but more formal and mechanical; fix is the general modern verb, wider in range and less craft-flavoured; patch names a partial covering of a hole rather than full restoration; restore implies a fuller overhaul back to an earlier or original state; amend is the fuller historical doublet, now used mainly of texts, laws, and behaviour rather than torn cloth; remedy is Latinate and focuses on curing a defect or disease.
