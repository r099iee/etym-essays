- [Old English (c. 700–1100)] - hægl / hagal named both the destructive frozen weather and the ninth letter of the Anglo-Saxon runic alphabet - [connotation: terror of agricultural devastation and winter ruin]
- [Middle English (c. 1150–1500)] - hayle / hail developed vivid military metaphors in chivalric romance (hail of arrows, hail of stones) - [connotation: lethal battlefield danger and overwhelming atmospheric fury]
- [Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700)] - hail appeared extensively in Shakespeare and Milton to evoke divine plagues, artillery barrages, and psychological torment - [connotation: cosmic retribution, violence, and dramatic noise]
- [19th c.] - Victorian literature and fairy tales (Oscar Wilde's The Selfish Giant) personified Hail as an eccentric, destructive seasonal figure - [connotation: atmospheric character, winter isolation, and physical energy]
- [20th–21st c.] - precise meteorological classification in atmospheric science alongside everyday figurative uses (a hail of questions, a hail of bullets) - [connotation: severe convective weather hazard or rapid figurative onslaught]
