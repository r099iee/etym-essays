• Chert: Similar because it is the same rock family (microcrystalline quartz); differs because chert is the general geological term for the rock, while flint is specifically the dark, nodular variety found in chalk.
  • Quartz: Similar because flint is a form of quartz; differs because quartz is the parent mineral species (often clear, crystalline), while flint is its opaque, microcrystalline, nodular variety.
  • Quartzite: Similar because it is a hard, quartz-based rock; differs because quartzite is metamorphic, formed from quartz sandstone under heat and pressure, and does not have flint's conchoidal fracture or knapping property.
  • Obsidian: Similar because it is a razor-sharp stone used for blades and tools; differs because obsidian is volcanic glass — glossy, black, and formed by rapid cooling of lava — while flint is sedimentary, cryptocrystalline quartz from chalk.
  • Silex: Similar because it is the ancient Latin and poetic name for flint and even shares the silica root; differs because silex is archaic/literary and now survives chiefly in the chemistry term silica, not in everyday tool or fire language.
  • Steel: Similar because it strikes sparks from flint in the traditional fire-making pair and in the flintlock; differs because steel is a manufactured carbon-iron alloy, not a stone, and cannot be knapped or used as a blade-stone.
  • Chalcedony: Similar because it is a related microcrystalline quartz family; differs because chalcedony includes waxy, translucent varieties used in jewelry, whereas flint is the dark, opaque, tool- and spark-making kind.
  • Granite: Similar because it is a hard stone; differs because granite is a coarse-grained igneous rock that does not fracture conchoidally and cannot produce a sharp edge or strike sparks like flint.
