The noun and adjective liege names the person and the relationship at the heart of medieval feudalism. As a noun, it denotes a feudal superior — a sovereign or paramount lord who is entitled to the absolute allegiance, homage, military service, and counsel of those bound to him; historically it could also denote the vassal bound to such a lord (a "liege-man" or "liege subject"). As an adjective it describes the quality of that bond — a "liege lord" is the lord to whom one owes one's highest duty, and "liege subjects" or "liege men" are those who owe that duty. In modern English the word survives mostly in the formal, archaic, or fantasy register, above all in the vocative phrase "my liege," the respectful address of a subject to a monarch.

Sense 1 — The liege lord (primary historical sense). In feudal law, the liege lord (Latin dominus ligius, Old French seigneur lige) was the lord whose claim to the vassal's service was supreme — it outranked every other feudal obligation. Because vassals often held land from several lords, feudal practice developed the idea of "liege homage": the one unqualified, absolute oath that took precedence over all other homages. The liege lord was thus not merely any lord but the paramount lord — typically the king, or the highest lord in a chain of tenure. In the Game of Thrones world of this card, a house's "liege lords" are the great lords to whom the lesser houses owe their first loyalty.

Sense 2 — The liege vassal or liege-man (reciprocal historical sense). The same word could be turned on the other side of the contract: a liege-man or liege subject was the vassal bound by liege homage, owing the lord his life, his counsel, his military service, and his loyalty "against all men." This reciprocity is essential to understanding the term: liege-ness describes a bond, not a rank, and both parties to the bond could be called liege. The ambiguity survives in the dictionary record and in phrases such as "his liege men."

Sense 3 — Vocative address, "my liege" (surviving ceremonial sense). In courtly and dramatic literature, "my liege" is the standard respectful address to a king or queen — a frozen vocative that outlived the end of feudalism and became a staple of Shakespearean and medievalist drama ("God save your majesty, my royal liege"). It connotes deference, ceremony, and the personal, oath-based relationship between a sovereign and a subject.

Sense 4 — Adjectival uses (liege lord / liege subjects / liege allegiance). Adjectivally, liege modifies the nouns of feudal loyalty: a liege lord, liege subjects, liege homage, liege allegiance. In these collocations the word does the work of "to whom supreme loyalty is owed" or "bound by supreme loyalty," and it is this adjectival use that keeps the word alive in historical and fantasy writing.

The historical semantic bridge — from "free" to "bound." The most striking thing about liege is the paradox of its origins. The medieval French word lige appears to descend from a Germanic adjective meaning "free, unencumbered" (compare Middle Dutch ledich, Old High German ledig, "free, idle, unencumbered"). Applied to a lord or to homage, "free" came to mean "absolute, unqualified, not shared with anyone else": a liege homage was the homage that was free of every other obligation, and a liege lord was the lord whose claim was free of all rivals. The word thus travelled from "free" to "bound absolutely," because the freedom in question was the freedom of the bond itself from competing claims — one of the most elegant paradoxes in the history of feudal vocabulary.

Register and modern use. Today liege is archaic, historical, ceremonial, or deliberately medievalizing. Outside fantasy literature, historical fiction, and courtly drama it is rare. It carries connotations of honor, oath, and reciprocal duty that plain words like lord or king lack. In the passage quoted on this card ("beneath the nose of their liege lords"), the word places the story squarely in a world of feudal obligation and dynastic loyalty.

Related vocabulary. fealty, homage, vassal, suzerain, liegeance (the status or obligation of a liege), allegiance, lord paramount, seigneur. The derived abstract noun liegeance / ligeance denotes the allegiance owed by a liege subject to his liege lord and is a technical term in English constitutional history.
