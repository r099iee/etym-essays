Superfluous means "more than is needed or wanted; unnecessary through being more than enough," and its literal Latin anatomy is "running over" (super- "over, above" + fluere "to flow"). The word keeps its hydraulic image inside it: a superfluous thing is a cup that has overflowed, a surplus beyond what the container of need can hold. In the literary context of this card the adjective performs a subtle rhetorical manoeuvre. The narrator has just described narrow side-tables holding tea things and vases of flowers, and then waves a hand at "other furnishings which it would be __________ to enumerate." The word does two things at once: it tells us that the inventory is impossible to complete, and it makes us feel the sheer superabundance of the room. Because superfluous keeps its image of a brimming vessel, the sentence implies that the household's possessions have overflowed the capacity of any list — there is simply too much for language to keep count. The tone is one of urbane, ironic modesty: the speaker declines to finish the catalogue, but the very decline is a boast, since it implies that the stock of beautiful objects is beyond compass. In the world of a great noble family, then, superfluous is not a word of waste but a word of conspicuous abundance: things are demoted from necessity to ornament, and the surplus itself becomes the evidence of power.

The senses of superfluous are broader than a single gloss suggests:

  • Primary sense: unnecessary through excess — more than is needed or wanted; so much as to be needless. "Superfluous words," "superfluous ornament," "superfluous precautions."
  • Rhetorical sense: redundant, pleonastic — in rhetoric and style, superfluous matter is padding that adds nothing to meaning or proof; a "superfluous" word or phrase repeats what is already conveyed. This is the sense that makes the narrator's "it would be superfluous to enumerate" a deliberate stylistic joke: the word itself calls attention to the rhetoric of omission.
  • Economic and material sense: surplus, excess — beyond what necessity requires; used of goods, wealth, or provisions in excess of need, e.g. "superfluous wealth."
  • Of persons and social roles (chiefly figurative, often ironic) — a person or class regarded as having no necessary function, surplus to requirement; the "superfluous man" is a type of the nineteenth-century novel, the extra, unoccupied gentleman.
  • Technical/scientific sense — in engineering and systems, "superfluous" can label a component or constraint that is redundant, that duplicates a function already performed; related to (and often confused with) "redundant" in its technical sense.
  • Historical sense: of bodily humours and fluids — in early modern medicine and physiology, "superfluous humours" were the body's excess fluids that needed to be expelled; the word was part of the technical vocabulary of humoral theory.
  • Derived forms — the noun superfluity (the state of being superfluous; an excess), the adverb superfluously, and the rarer abstract noun superfluousness.

The semantic history is a study in how a physical image becomes a moral and intellectual abstraction. The Latin compound superfluus was formed from super- "over" and fluere "to flow," and its earliest sense was literal: water that flows over, overflow. Because what overflows a vessel is more than the vessel can hold, the word took on the transferred sense of "more than enough, excess," and from "excess" it slid into "unnecessary" — for what is beyond need is, by definition, needless. The word entered English in the fifteenth century through French superflu and Latin, and by the sixteenth century it was established in its full range, from the moral vocabulary of excess ("superfluity of naughtiness" in the Book of Common Prayer) to the rhetoric of style. Modern usage still feels the overflow beneath the surface: superfluous is chosen over the plainer unnecessary precisely when the writer wants the suggestion of abundance — of more than enough — rather than the bare fact of non-requirement.

In contemporary English the word is formal and slightly literary. It appears in critical prose for needless repetition and ornament, in social and economic commentary for surplus wealth and surplus persons, and in ordinary usage when a speaker wants a more elegant, more ironic way of saying that something is not needed. The card's sentence shows the word at its most characteristic and most ironic: the narrator uses it to stop describing, and in doing so describes the very superabundance that makes further description pointless.
