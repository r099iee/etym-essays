• Condescending: Similar because both denote treating another person as inferior; differs because condescending emphasizes the act of stooping or lowering oneself to another's level, while patronising specifically evokes the patron who protects and supports but thereby asserts a one-sided authority.
  • Supercilious: Similar because both convey an assumed superiority over others; differs because supercilious stresses arrogant contempt expressed through expression and posture, while patronising works through an over-kind, explanatory, solicitous manner.
  • Paternalistic: Similar because both involve controlling or managing others from a position of superiority; differs because paternalistic describes systems, rulers, or institutions that restrict freedom "for your own good," whereas patronising is a personal tone or attitude between individuals.
  • Dismissive: Similar because both devalue the other person; differs because dismissive conveys outright rejection and brushing-aside, while a patronising person still engages with you — but from an assumed position of superiority.
  • Disdainful: Similar because both show a low opinion of the other; differs because disdainful is openly scornful and contemptuous, while patronising masks its contempt beneath kindness and solicitude.
  • Haughty: Similar because both assume a superior standing; differs because haughty describes proud, arrogant self-regard and aloofness, while patronising is a directed behaviour that belittles a specific person while appearing to help them.
  • Belittling: Similar because both make the other person seem small or inferior; differs because belittling is the general act of making someone seem less important, while patronising does it specifically through an indulgent, over-kind manner.
  • High-and-mighty: Similar because both convey a sense of superiority; differs because high-and-mighty is colloquial and describes the self-important air itself, while patronising names the act of treating others as inferior beneath that air.
  • Lordly: Similar because both suggest behaving as a superior over others; differs because lordly suggests imperious, aristocratic command, while patronising suggests the indulgence of a benefactor rather than the command of a master.
  • Smug: Similar because both carry an unearned sense of one's own superiority; differs because smug is the self-satisfied inner feeling, which may not be expressed toward others at all, while patronising is the outward behaviour directed at someone.
  • Indulgent: Similar because both tolerate or excuse another's failings from above; differs because indulgent is the permissive granting of licence and may be kindly, while patronising is the assumption of inferiority that makes the tolerance itself an insult.
  • Precious / condescendingly explanatory: Similar because both involve speaking to someone as if they were simple; differs because such explanatory over-kindness is only one tactic of patronising, which also includes smiles, pats, and patronising praise.
  • Disparaging: Similar because both lower the standing of the other; differs because disparaging is openly critical and belittling in speech, while patronising often accompanies apparent praise, which is why its condescension is harder to answer.
