Derivation of errant PIE *h₁ers- "to be in motion, to wander, to stray, to flow" (c. 4500–3000 BCE; the root of Sanskrit 'arṣati' 'he flows, moves rapidly', Greek 'érkhomai' 'I go', and Latin 'errare' — though the Greek and Latin assignments are debated; see note) → Proto-Italic *erzā- "to wander, to stray" (regular development of *h₁ers- in Italic, c. 1500–800 BCE) → Latin errāre "to wander, to stray, to roam, to go astray, to make a mistake" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; Plautus, Terence; the sense 'to make a mistake' is attested from the same early period) → Latin errāns, errantem "wandering, straying, erring" (present participle of errāre, attested 1st c. BCE; e.g. Cicero uses 'errantes' of the wandering planets) → Old French errant "travelling, wandering; straying" (present participle; the Old French verb 'errer' fused two Latin sources — errāre 'to wander/stray' and the rarer 'iterāre' 'to journey', from 'iter' 'journey' — a fusion that gives medieval 'errant' its dual sense 'roving' and 'straying'; 12th c.) → Middle English errant "wandering, travelling, straying, erring" (borrowed from Old French in the 14th c.; the phrase 'knight errant' appears by c. 1300 in the chivalric romances) → errant (Modern English; senses 'wandering from the proper course or standards', 'roving in search of adventure') Debated / nuanced points. The PIE root behind Latin 'errāre' is usually reconstructed as *h₁ers- 'to be in motion, to wander,' supported by Sanskrit 'arṣati' (he flows) and the Germanic 'irren' family (Old High German 'irri' 'wandering, confused', modern German 'irren' 'to err'), and by Greek 'érkhomai' (I go) in some analyses. However, the Greek assignment is disputed — some scholars instead connect Greek 'érkhomai' with a separate root *h₁erǵʰ- 'to set in motion' — and the exact laryngeal configuration of the wander-root is debated. More important for the word's shape: medieval 'errant' is a conflation. The French present participle 'errant' primarily continues Latin 'errāre' (wander/stray), but in chivalric contexts it was reinforced by Old French 'errer' (to travel, journey), from Latin 'iter' (a journey, a road), so that the 'knight-errant' is simultaneously a 'wanderer' and a 'traveller.' This dual etymology explains why 'errant' both admires (roving adventure) and condemns (straying from right) — the two Latin verbs had merged in the French participle long before English borrowed it.
