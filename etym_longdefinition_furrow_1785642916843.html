FURROW (noun and verb). A long, narrow, linear trench or groove cut into a surface — originally into soil by the blade of a plough — and, by extension, any such groove, most notably the deep parallel creases drawn into the skin of the forehead or brow by displeasure, concentration, anxiety, or stern reflection. As a verb, to furrow is to make such trenches or creases: 'to furrow the earth' is to plough it; 'to furrow one's brow' is to crease the forehead in thought or worry. The card's context — 'To furrow one's brow in displeasure, concentration, or worry' — places the word at its figurative extreme and asks the reader to watch a face being ploughed by feeling. The verb converts the forehead into a field and the emotion into the plough: displeasure and worry drive visible, deliberate channels across the brow, exactly as the share drives channels through earth. The tone is one of controlled intensity rather than violent passion. A furrowed brow is the facial signature of the person thinking hard, enduring something, or disapproving with clenched patience; it is quieter than a scowl and more legible than a frown of mere sadness. It registers mental pressure, the physical trace of a mind at work — and the agricultural metaphor quietly guarantees that the emotion is deep, straight, and hard to erase, like a cut made by iron through loam. Polysemy — the word's senses and their historical bridges:
  • Sense 1 — The agricultural trench: a long narrow cut made in the earth by a plough. The original sense, recorded in Old English before 1000: the furrow is the single straight channel left by the ploughshare as it slices the soil, the elementary line of arable farming. In pair with the ridge (the raised strip between furrows), the furrow organizes the whole geometry of the tilled field.
  • Sense 2 — Any long narrow groove or channel in a surface. By extension from the soil, furrow names long narrow depressions in many materials and surfaces: grooves in rock, channels in skin, ruts in a road or path. The bridge is simple shape-transfer: anything long, narrow, and cut into a surface is a furrow.
  • Sense 3 — The crease in the skin, especially of the forehead or brow. The figurative sense settled in the 16th–17th centuries: deep parallel creases in the skin of the forehead, marks of age, strain, or thought. The bridge is the plough-image — furrows in the face are lines cut into the surface of the flesh, just as plough-furrows are cut into the surface of the earth.
  • Sense 4 — The verb: to make furrows in the earth, to plough. 'To furrow the land' means to plough it, cutting parallel channels. This verb is attested from the 16th century, formed directly from the noun.
  • Sense 5 — The verb, figurative: to crease the brow. 'To furrow one's brow' — to draw one's forehead into lines of displeasure, concentration, or worry — is the fixed modern literary expression, and the everyday workhorse of narrative prose. The bridge: worry ploughs the face as the share ploughs the field.
  • Sense 6 — Technical and specialized uses. In agriculture and horticulture, 'furrowing' names planting or irrigating in channels; in geology and anatomy, a furrow is a long narrow groove on a surface (e.g., furrows on a mountain face or on the surface of the brain); in embryology the cleavage furrow is the groove along which a dividing cell constricts. Each extends the same 'long narrow cut' image into a technical register.
  • Sense 7 — Archaic: a measure of land. The old unit the furlong (from furh-lang, 'furrow-long') was the length of a ploughed furrow — originally the distance a team of oxen could plough without turning, standardized at 220 yards. The furrow is thus fossilized in a unit of measure still used in racing.
Synonym boundaries — the word against its nearest kin:
  • Wrinkle: similar in denoting a crease of the skin; differs because a wrinkle is a general, often passive crease, fine and irregular, produced by age, sun, or repeated motion, whereas a furrow is deeper, straighter, more deliberate, and carries the mechanical idea of something cut or ploughed — usually across the brow.
  • Crease: similar in denoting a fold in a surface; differs because a crease implies a sharp fold made by pressure or folding (trousers, paper), without the skin-specific, emotion-driven weight of furrow.
  • Groove: similar in denoting a long narrow channel; differs because groove is the neutral mechanical term for any long narrow cut (in wood, metal, records), lacking the agricultural origin and the facial use of furrow.
  • Trench: similar in denoting a long narrow excavation; differs because a trench is deep, wide, and often dug for defence or drainage, while a furrow is a shallow cut made by a blade for planting.
  • Channel: similar in denoting a long hollow passage; differs because a channel is typically a natural or constructed waterway, while a furrow is a dry cut in soil or skin.
  • Line: similar in denoting a long thin mark or crease; differs because line is utterly general (a line of ink, a line of thought), while furrow is specific to cut channels and facial creases.
  • Ridge: similar because it is the furrow's constant companion in the field; differs because the ridge is the raised strip between furrows — furrow and ridge are complementary opposites, not synonyms.
  • Frown: similar in denoting a facial expression of displeasure; differs because a frown is the whole expression (usually a drawing together of the brows), while a furrow is the specific crease line — one may furrow without frowning, and the furrow is the legible trace of the frown.
  • Plough: similar as the agricultural act that makes furrows; differs because plough is the verb of the whole operation of turning the soil, while furrow names the specific resulting line.
Both senses survive fully in modern English: the literal farming sense grows more remote and technical as mechanized agriculture replaces the plough, while the brow sense is the everyday workhorse of narrative prose — deep, straight, and hard to erase, like a cut made by iron through loam.
