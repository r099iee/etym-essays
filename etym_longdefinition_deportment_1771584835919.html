Deportment denotes the manner in which a person carries, holds, or conducts the body and self in public — bearing, carriage, and trained behaviour taken together. In the passage from The Story of the Stone, Dai-yu has been admitted to the most intimate part of the Jia household, and she studies the maids who serve the apartment: "their clothing, makeup, and deportment were quite different from those of the maids she had seen so far in other parts of the mansion." The word here does precise social work. Clothing and makeup are things a person puts on; deportment is something a person does — or rather, something the person has become, through long training. The difference the narrator observes in deportment is therefore the deepest sign of the maids' standing: they move differently because they have been drilled differently, and their trained carriage marks them as the elite attendants of an already elite inner world. The nuance is one of discipline made physical — the body as a record of the household's hierarchy, in which even the way a girl stands or offers a cup of tea announces which rank she serves and which training she has received.

The word's senses can be set out precisely:
  • Physical bearing and carriage: the way the body is held and moved — posture, walk, gesture — "a graceful deportment," "deportment and address."
  • Conduct and behaviour: the manner of behaving and conducting oneself in social life — "correct deportment," "the deportment of a gentleman."
  • Trained and schooled manners: deportment as a taught attainment — the "deportment classes" of Victorian finishing schools, judged on posture, walk, and courtesy; the word often implies cultivation and discipline.
  • Institutional and evaluative use: deportment as a graded quality of pupils, soldiers, or servants — "judged on deportment," "a deportment mark," "improper deportment" — conduct as something scored and corrected.
  • Figurative use: the "deportment" of things that carry themselves in a manner, e.g. the deportment of a vessel, a horse, or (loosely, in period writing) the demeanour of an institution or a party.

Deportment must be set against its near-synonyms. Bearing is the closest in physical meaning — the way the body is carried ("she has a dignified bearing") — but it is more static, a posture one has, while deportment is more active and behavioural, a manner one maintains through deliberate conduct. Demeanor is broader: it covers outward behaviour and attitude, including facial expression and mood ("a calm demeanor"), not just physical carriage. Comportment, from the Latin comportāre, "to carry together," is the closest formal synonym, but it leans toward the moral and behavioural regulation of oneself, appearing often in institutional or disciplinary contexts, whereas deportment is more conspicuously about the body. Posture is purely physical — the alignment of the spine and limbs — and lacks the social and moral overtones of deportment; mien is a literary word for the whole air or aspect of a person, more about countenance than carriage. The boundary is the blend: deportment uniquely fuses body, behaviour, and training, so that it can be taught, judged, and perfected — the very quality examined in the "deportment classes" of Victorian finishing schools.

Collocationally, deportment is a word of schools and societies: "poise and deportment," "grace and deportment," "a deportment class," "deportment lessons," "improper deportment," "judged on deportment," "ladylike deportment," "his deportment was irreproachable." Register-wise it is formal, slightly dated, and faintly Victorian; it survives in everyday use mostly in period description, conduct literature, and references to manners training, while "bearing" and "demeanor" have largely taken over the conversational space. Its strength, and its usefulness in the novel, is precisely this institutional weight: deportment is not how one happens to move but how one has been taught to move, the physical signature of upbringing and rank. In the Jia mansion, where the difference between the inner and outer maids is written on the body itself, the word names the very channel through which hierarchy is made visible — a girl's deportment tells the observer which apartment she belongs to before she says a word.
