The verb expostulated embodies an intricate semantic transformation from classical Roman legal grievance to refined Renaissance rhetoric, 18th-century moral fiction, and modern psychological discourse.

1. Proto-Indo-European Root and Latin Verb Formation:
The ultimate origin lies in the Proto-Indo-European root *preḱ-, signifying "to ask, entreat, pray, or request." This root generated significant cognate families across the Indo-European spectrum:

  • Latin: *precari* ("to pray, entreat", source of English *pray*, *precarious*, and *imprecation*) and the frequentative verb *poscere* (from Proto-Italic **posk-ō*), meaning "to demand or request."
  • Sanskrit: *praccháti* ("asks, questions").
  • Germanic: Proto-Germanic **fregnaną* (Old English *frignan*, modern German *fragen*, "to ask").
  • Slavic: Old Church Slavonic *prositi* ("to ask, beg").
In Latin, the frequentative base yielded the verb postulare ("to ask, demand, claim as a necessity", source of modern English postulate).

2. Prefixation and Classical Latin Meaning:
In classical Latin, *postulare* was compounded with the intensive prefix ex- ("out, thoroughly, vehemently"), creating expostulare:

  • Legal and Interpersonal Grievance: In the writings of Cicero, Livy, and Tacitus, *expostulare* meant to demand urgently, to insist upon satisfaction, or to lodge a formal complaint against an injury (*expostulare iniurias*).
  • Dialectical Debate: Because demanding redress in Roman society involved presenting explicit reasons and evidence, the word naturally began to describe vigorous dispute, verbal remonstrance, and mutual reasoning.

3. Renaissance Borrowing and Semantic Evolution in English:
The word entered the English language in the mid-16th century (attested from the 1540s) directly from the Latin past participial stem expostulātus:

  • Tudor and Stuart Usage: In 16th-century texts, *expostulate* was occasionally used in its older Latin sense of demanding a right or discussing a grievance. However, by the early 17th century (as seen in Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, where Polonius remarks: *"My liege, and madam, to expostulate / What majesty should be, what duty is"*), it had firmly settled into its modern sense of discoursing, analyzing, and reasoning earnestly.
  • The Shift to Dissuasion: In the 17th and 18th centuries, the word specialized further into the act of affectionate or earnest dissuasion—arguing against a friend's or relative's unwise decision.

4. Prominence in 18th-Century Moral Prose:
Daniel Defoe's 1719 passage in *Robinson Crusoe* provides one of the definitive literary illustrations of the term. When the gout-ridden father *expostulated very warmly* with Crusoe, Defoe captures the essence of the word: a serious, loving, and intellectually rigorous attempt by parental wisdom to avert catastrophe. In modern English, *expostulated* remains a vital, highly expressive literary verb representing principled, reasoned opposition.
