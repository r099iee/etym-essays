The history of elation is the story of a metaphor — being carried upward — that a language inherited and then narrowed from exaltation in general to joy in particular. The chain begins in the deep Indo-European past with the reconstructed root *bher-, "to carry, bear." This was one of the most productive verbal roots of the family. Vedic Sanskrit bharati "he carries" underlies the very name of the god Bhārata's land, Indian Bharata; Ancient Greek phérein "to carry" feeds the many phor- words of English (metaphor, phosphorus, euphoria); Proto-Germanic *beraną gives English bear, birth, and burden; and Latin ferre "to carry" stands behind English transfer, offer, infer, and fertile. The root's core was the human act of lifting and transporting, whether a load, a child, or a report.

Latin kept ferre as its basic "carry" verb, and from it the Romans compounded a whole arsenal of verbs by prefixing prepositions: conferre "to bring together", afferre "to bring to", auferre "to carry away". Among these, efferre — ex- "out, up" + ferre — meant "to carry out, carry away, carry up". From very early Latin, efferre was used of lifting things aloft, of burying the dead (carrying them out), and of raising someone's reputation: efferre aliquem laudibus, "to raise someone to the skies with praise". The verb therefore carried a double valence: it could elevate a person physically, socially, or morally — and, since Roman moralists were suspicious of anyone raised too high, it could also describe the overweening pride that comes with elevation.

A peculiarity of Latin made the next step irregular and famous. The past participle of efferre is not *effertus but ēlātus, "carried up, raised". This is because the verb ferre has a suppletive system: its perfect (tulī) and its participle (lātus) come from a different root, *telh-, "to lift, bear, endure", the same root that yields Latin tollō "I raise" and tolerāre "to endure" (English tolerate) and Greek talássein "to endure". The Romans themselves were unaware of the archaeology; they simply treated ēlātus as the participle of efferre, and from ēlāt- Latin derived the abstract noun ēlātiō, "a carrying up, elevation; exaltation of mind." Cicero uses ēlātiō for the "lifting up" of speech and the "elation" of the spirit; Quintilian discusses elatio as a figure of grandeur in oratory.

The noun crossed into English through the romance languages. Old French had elacion (twelfth century), and English recorded elation from the fourteenth century, meaning "a lifting up, an elevation". In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the moral double valence inherited from Latin was fully alive: the "elation" of the heart could be praised as noble exaltation or condemned as sinful pride and self-conceit. Biblical and moralistic writing of the period repeatedly warns against "elation of mind" and "vain elation", in the tradition of "pride goeth before a fall". Meanwhile the verb elate (sixteenth century) and the adjective elated carried the same two faces.

The decisive narrowing happened in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. As the language of feeling — the "sentimental" movement — came to dominate fiction and poetry, writers needed precise words for positive emotional states, and elation shed its blameworthy pride meaning. The word settled on its modern sense: exhilarating, soaring happiness, the inward lift that follows relief, triumph, or good fortune. The Romantic poets and the great novelists of the nineteenth century used it freely for moments when a character's spirits "rise" — the very metaphor of vertical ascent that the Latin participle had encoded. In the twentieth century the word entered clinical language, where psychiatry borrowed it for the pathologically elevated mood of mania, a use that keeps the ancient Latin idea of being raised beyond the ordinary, now re-read as a symptom.

In the source sentence — "The beauty of the moon seemed to increase their elation" — the word performs its modern sense exactly: the moonlight does not cause calm contentment but lifts the party's spirits upward, matching the exultation that follows Yu-cun's impromptu quatrain on the moon. The metaphor has come full circle from a Roman carrying his goods aloft to a moon that raises a stranger's heart.

Summary of the evolution:
  • Formal chain: PIE *bher- → Latin ferre → efferre → ēlātus (suppletive, from *telh-) → ēlātiō → Old French elacion → English elation.
  • Sound and morphology: the famous irregularity is the suppletive participle ēlātus, drawn from the "lift" root *telh-, not the "carry" root *bher-; English elat(e) + -ion normalises it as a simple derivative.
  • Semantic drift: "carrying up, elevation" → "exaltation of mind (praise or pride)" → "buoyant, exhilarating joy" → "pathologically elevated mood" in clinical usage.
  • Socio-cultural engines: Roman rhetoric and moralism; the Christian-sentimental culture of feeling in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Europe; nineteenth-century psychology and twentieth-century psychiatry.
  • Literary attestations: classical oratory (Cicero, Quintilian); Renaissance and seventeenth-century moral and religious writing; Romantic and Victorian fiction and poetry; modern psychological and psychiatric prose.
