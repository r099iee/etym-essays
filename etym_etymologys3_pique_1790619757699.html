The history of pique is the history of a point that migrated from the battlefield to the boudoir to the mind. The deepest reconstructible ancestor is PIE *peig- meaning to cut, carve or mark, which supplied a family of picking, pecking and pike words across Indo-European. In Germanic this became a cluster around *pikk- and *pik- denoting something small and sharp that pierces, the source of English pick, pike, peak and spike. Frankish speakers carried this sense into northern Gaul, where Old French absorbed it as pic for a sharp point, pickaxe, pike or lance-head, first attested in martial contexts of the 11th-12th centuries.

From this nominal base Old French formed the verb piquer, to prick, pierce, spur or sting, richly attested in 12th-13th century chivalric and agricultural texts: to piquer a horse was to spur it, to piquer cloth was to quilt or stitch it with points, to piquer an enemy was to pierce him. The verb already carried figurative extensions, for a sharp word could piquer like a spur. By Middle French of the 14th-15th centuries the reflexive se piquer had developed the sense to pride oneself, literally to spur oneself, and the noun pique had developed the senses of a quarrel arising from wounded honour and of a pikeman's weapon. The semantic logic was honour culture: gentlemen were imagined as exquisitely sensitive surfaces, and the slightest prick to reputation festered.

  • Martial to moral: pike-head to sharp reproach, preserving penetration as sudden pain.
  • Spur to self: to spur a horse to spur oneself with vanity, preserving self-propulsion.
  • Stitch to textile: pricked quilting to pique cloth and lace, preserving dotted surface.
  • Suit of cards: pique as spades, preserving spearhead shape as a pip.

English borrowed both noun and verb in the early 16th century through military contact, court fashion and Montaignean essay culture. Early attestations show pique as a quarrel or ill-will and to pique as to offend or to plume oneself, spellings picque and pike reflecting French pronunciation. The curiosity sense, to pique interest, emerges clearly in the 18th-century Enlightenment, when stimulation of inquiry became a positive value; what had been a wound to pride was revalued as a welcome wound to ignorance. By the 19th century novelists exploited the full polysemy: a heroine piqued by neglect is both intellectually alerted and emotionally nettled, a hero who piques himself on honour is both spurred and blinded.

Phonetically the word retained French /pik/ anglicised as /piːk/, resisting the Great Vowel Shift effects seen in native words and thereby signalling its foreign elegance. Orthographically the final -que, pronounced /k/, advertises Frenchness much like unique and antique. Socio-culturally the word travelled from soldiers to aristocrats to psychologists: duelling codes made pique a matter of life, salon culture made it a matter of wit, and modern popular psychology makes it a matter of curiosity management. The source sentence, where pranks seek to pique bourgeois conceptual reason, revives the oldest martial force: avant-garde provocation as intellectual pike-thrust against alphabetic rationality.
