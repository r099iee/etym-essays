Verbosity is the quality of using more words than a thought requires. It is a mass noun of style, almost always pejorative in ordinary prose: it names not merely length but a failure of discipline, the padding of a statement until its sense is buried under its own phrasing. In the source passage Cicero is “accused of being fascinated with his own verbosity, a stylistic show-off” — the charge is not simply that he wrote at length, but that he loved the sound of his own copiousness and displayed it for its own sake. That is the characteristic modern note of the word: verbosity is excess enjoyed by the person producing it and endured by everyone else.

1. The core sense: wordiness as a fault

In its central use verbosity denotes an over-abundance of words relative to the meaning they carry. The judgement is qualitative as well as quantitative: a long book need not be verbose, and a short memorandum can be. What makes prose verbose is redundancy, circumlocution, and self-regard — saying again what has been said, approaching a point by way of three subordinate clauses, preferring the impressive phrase to the plain one. The word therefore belongs with the classic vices of style rather than with neutral measures of length.

  • Core lexicographic wording: “the quality of being verbose; abundance of words; wordiness”; “excessive use of words, especially when it obscures meaning.”
  • Typical constructions: the verbosity of (a report, a speech, a style); “noted for his verbosity”; “cut through the verbosity”; “an excess of verbosity.”
  • Degree: strong enough to be a criticism, mild enough to be used in polite review; it does not carry the physical disgust of revulsion or the moral weight of deceit.
  • Register: formal and evaluative; common in literary criticism, editing, administration, and commentary on public speech.

2. The rhetorical tradition: verbositas against brevitas

Verbosity inherits a technical meaning from classical rhetoric, where the virtue of brevitas was opposed by a family of vices: verbositas, wordiness; perissologia, superfluous addition; macrologia, long-windedness; tautologia, needless repetition. Roman handbooks on oratory treat these as failures of control, and their recommendations shaped European prose education for centuries. When a critic calls a writer verbose, the judgement still carries this ancient pedigree: the writer has violated the economy of speech, and the violation is understood as a lapse of judgement rather than an accident of length.

  • Brevity as the norm: the plain style prized by Atticist critics made wordiness a marker of the debased or Asiatic manner — exactly the charge brought against Cicero in the source passage.
  • Copiousness as a virtue: the rival tradition of copia verborum prized abundance, so the same quantity of words could be praised as richness or blamed as verbosity depending on the school.
  • Modern survival: editorial style guides still speak of verbosity, padding, and redundancy as correctable faults, often with word-count targets.

3. The count use: a verbose passage

Less often, verbosity can be counted: a verbosity is a particular wordy utterance or passage, in the way that an obscurity can be a particular obscure remark. This use is literary and slightly old-fashioned, but it is useful when a critic wants to isolate an instance rather than condemn a whole style. The plural, verbosities, is rare and usually ironic.

  • Example shape: “the report is a compendium of verbosities” — a collection of wordy passages.
  • Distinction: the mass noun judges a habit; the count noun points to a specimen.

4. Technical and neutral senses: verbosity levels

Computing has given the word a new, morally neutral life. A program’s verbosity is the amount of detail it prints: a compiler may be run at a high verbosity, a logging system may have a verbosity setting, a command-line tool may accept repeated -v flags to increase verbosity. Here the word means “level of output detail,” with no implication of fault; a high verbosity may be exactly what a debugging engineer wants. This technical sense is now common enough that the word can appear in software documentation without irony, and it has begun to colour general usage, where “reduce the verbosity” can sound like a setting rather than a moral criticism.

  • Computing: verbosity level, verbosity flag, verbose mode, log verbosity.
  • Neutral value: in this register verbosity is a parameter, not a vice.
  • Contemporary extension: discussions of automated and machine-generated text often use verbosity for the tendency to pad answers with unnecessary phrasing.

5. The semantic bridge: word-fullness

The word’s structure explains its meaning almost transparently. Latin verbum is “word”; the suffix -osus means “full of, abounding in,” as in vinosus, “full of wine,” or periculosus, “full of danger”; and the abstract suffix -itas turns the adjective into a quality. Verbositas is therefore literally “word-fullness,” and English verbosity is the state of being stuffed with words. The pejorative charge comes not from the suffix but from the rhetorical culture that measured such fullness against the needs of sense: to be full of words is, by implication, to be empty of judgement. The metaphor is one of inflation — words as padding, stuffing, or filler around a small core of meaning.

6. Register, connotation, and company

  • Connotation: self-indulgence, pomposity, obscurity, lack of editorial discipline; often implies that the writer is more interested in performance than in communication.
  • Common collocates: tedious, turgid, bureaucratic, legalistic, empty, mere, sheer; verbs: cut, trim, reduce, admire, drown in, be buried in.
  • Related adjectives: verbose (the person or text), verbosely (the manner), verbosity (the quality).
  • What to avoid: verbosity is not the same as complexity; a technical text may be difficult without being verbose, and a simple text may be verbose through repetition.

7. In this reading

The source passage gathers the standard charges against Cicero — “an Asian, a wordmonger” — and the phrase “fascinated with his own verbosity” adds a psychological diagnosis to the stylistic one. Cicero is not accused of writing too much because he had too much to say; he is accused of loving the performance of saying it. The word’s later technical sense is a useful corrective to that Romantic view: verbosity can be measured, counted, and cut, and the passage itself is a reminder that every age invents a style it calls excessive.
