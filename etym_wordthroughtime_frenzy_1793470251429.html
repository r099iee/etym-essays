- 14th c. — Medieval Medical Pathology: Enters Middle English (as frenesie) from Old French, denoting an acute cerebral inflammation accompanied by high fever, raving delirium, and physical violence.
- 15th–16th c. — Poetic and Passionate Broadening: Expands from clinical diagnosis into dramatic literature; Shakespeare immortalizes the positive creative dimension in A Midsummer Night's Dream as the "poet's eye, in a fine frenzy rolling".
- 17th–18th c. — Neoclassical Political Indictment: Wielded by Enlightenment essayists (Swift, Burke, Reynolds) to condemn religious fanaticism, populist mobs, and factional political hysteria that threaten civil stability.
- 19th c. — Romantic Sublime vs. Industrial Anxiety: Romantics praise the ecstasy of artistic frenzy, while Victorian novelists and historians depict the terrifying frenzy of revolutionary crowds and industrial strikes.
- Mid-20th c. — Zoological Coining: Marine biologists observe sharks attacking prey in a blood-stimulated frenzy, giving birth to the iconic compound feeding frenzy.
- Late 20th–21st c. — Media and Market Metaphor: The phrase feeding frenzy is adopted by political journalism for scandal-hunting, while financial markets routinely describe speculative buying panics as trading frenzies.
