Derivation chain for ENMITY (single root word): 1. Latin amāre "to love" (etymology debated: possibly from PIE *h₂em- "to grasp, take," cf. Sanskrit ámiti "seizes, oppresses"; or an inherited nursery word; de Vaan marks it uncertain), attested 3rd c. BCE. 2. Latin amīcus "friend; friendly" (amāre + adjectival -cus, "one who loves / the beloved"), attested 2nd c. BCE. 3. Latin inimīcus "not-a-friend; hostile, enemy" (in- "not" + amīcus), attested 2nd c. BCE. 4. Latin inimīcitia "hostility, enmity, feud" (abstract noun in -itia from inimīcus; literally "not-friendship"), attested 1st c. BCE. 5. Old French enemisté, ennemistié "hostility, enmity" (12th c.), from Latin inimīcitia. 6. Middle English enmite, enemytie "enmity" (13th–14th c.), borrowed from Old French. 7. Modern English ENMITY "the state of being an enemy; active hostility" (standard form by the 16th c.). Note: The word enters English via Old French (enemisté) rather than directly from Latin; the sibling amity (from Latin amīcitia "friendship," via Old French amitié) is the exact antonym from the same root. The Latin word hostis, "public enemy of the state," is from an unrelated root (PIE *ǵʰostis "stranger") and is not part of this chain. The ultimate origin of amāre is debated (PIE *h₂em- "to grasp" vs. a nursery word); the chain from amāre onward is secure.
