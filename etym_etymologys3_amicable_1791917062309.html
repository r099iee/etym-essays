Amicable is one half of a doublet, and its history cannot be told without its twin. Both words descend from Latin amīcus, “friend”, a derivative of amāre, “to love”. The deeper origin of amāre is one of the standing puzzles of Latin etymology: it has no certain cognates outside Italic, and the suggestions that have been made — a nursery word for “mother, aunt”, or a root meaning “to take hold of” — are plausible rather than demonstrable. What is certain is that by the time of Plautus the verb was fully established, and that amīcus and the abstract amīcitia belonged to the central vocabulary of Roman social life.

Roman friendship as a public category

  • Amīcitia in Cicero's treatise De amicitia is not merely private affection; it is a bond of mutual obligation that can hold between citizens, families, and even states.
  • The political vocabulary of the late Republic used amīcus for a political ally and amīcitia for an alliance, which is why the word's descendants so often describe formal relations rather than intimate feeling.
  • This public, relational colouring is the seed of the modern English distinction: the Latin noun lent itself to treaties and settlements, not only to affection.

The Late Latin adjective

Late Latin formed amīcābilis from amīcus with the suffix -ābilis, which marks capacity, fitness, or worthiness — the same suffix that gives English -able in lovable, peaceable, and charitable. The adjective thus meant something like “capable of friendship, worthy of being loved, friendly”. It is recorded in Christian and legal texts of the fourth and fifth centuries, and it passed into the Romance languages in two directions: as French amiable, and as a learned Latinism in the chanceries.

Two borrowings, two destinies

  • Amiable entered English in the fourteenth century from Old French. French had shifted the sense toward “pleasant, kind, likeable”, and English followed: by the sixteenth century amiable was chiefly a word for a person's manner or temper.
  • Amicable entered English in the early fifteenth century, apparently straight from Latin amīcābilis rather than through French. It retained the older, more formal sense of friendliness between parties.
  • For a century or two the two words overlapped and were used interchangeably; then they specialised. The person took amiable; the relationship, treaty, and settlement took amicable.

Legal and diplomatic career

  • From the sixteenth century, amicable appears in treaty language and in the phrase “amicable composition”, an agreement to settle a dispute without litigation.
  • Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century diplomacy fixed expressions such as “amicable relations”, “amicable understanding”, and “amicable settlement”, which remain the word's most frequent habitats.
  • In civil-law arbitration, the amiable compositeur decides by equity rather than strict law; English writers who translate the phrase as “amicable compositeur” reveal how thoroughly amicable has become the English adjective of peaceful dispute resolution.
  • Modern family law gave the word a new market: “amicable divorce” and “amicable separation” promise a process without adversarial bitterness, and the adjective now carries a faint whiff of mediation and public relations.

The mathematical branch

A separate and surprising development is the term amicable numbers. Two integers are amicable when each is the sum of the other's proper divisors, the canonical pair being 220 and 284. The Pythagoreans invested such pairs with the symbolism of friendship, and the Latin phrase numeri amicabiles was used by later mathematicians; English adopted the calque in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Euler's systematic search in the eighteenth century and the ongoing computational hunt for new pairs have kept the term alive in number theory, where it now has nothing to do with human goodwill and everything to do with divisor sums.

Phonetic and orthographic note

The English word keeps the Latin spelling of the suffix: amic- plus -able, with no trace of the French reduction that produced amiable. Pronunciation is /ˈæm.ɪ.kə.bəl/, with a weak middle syllable; like its relative amiable, the word is stressed on the first syllable, though the two differ in vowel pattern (/ˈeɪ.mi.ə.bəl/). The divergence in sound is a useful reminder that these are two separate borrowings, not one word with two spellings.

Modern perception

Today amicable is a word of institutions and public statements. It promises that a conflict has been handled well, and it is therefore vulnerable to irony: an “amicable” split announced in a press release may be anything but. Its enduring usefulness lies in that very modesty — it claims no affection, only the absence of war.
