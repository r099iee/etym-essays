- [c. 1250–1350] — Borrowed from Old French hermitage / ermitage into Middle English, strictly describing the lonely rural dwelling or cave of a Christian religious solitary (hermit) living in the wilderness.
- [14th–16th c.] — Expanded to designate small dependent chapels, rural chantries, or outlying monastic houses located near forest boundaries, river crossings, or bridges where hermits maintained toll-crossings and prayed for travelers.
- [17th–18th c.] — Transformed during the Enlightenment and the Romantic landscape garden movement into an ornamental architectural folly built on aristocratic country estates for picturesque melancholy (often employing ornamental hermits).
- [Late 18th c.] — Applied by Catherine the Great to her private neoclassical art pavilion beside the Winter Palace in Saint Petersburg, laying the foundation for what became the world-renowned State Hermitage Museum.
- [19th–21st c.] — Adopted in orientalist and Indological literature as the standard English translation for the Sanskrit āśrama (forest hermitage of Vedic rishis), while maintaining general secular usage as a peaceful country retreat or writer's hideaway.
