The history of turning the corner is the history of two ordinary words becoming an extraordinary metaphor for hope. Turn begins in the workshop of antiquity. Greek tornos named the carpenter's compass and the lathe, instruments that produce perfect circles by rotation. Latin borrowed this as tornare, to work on a lathe, to round and shape wood or stone by turning. This technical, artisanal sense already contained rotation, transformation of raw material into finished form, and skilled control. Through Vulgar Latin and Gallo-Romance it broadened dramatically. Old French torner, tourner no longer meant only lathe-work but any rotation, diversion, or change of direction: to turn a wheel, to turn aside, to turn traitor, to turn one's mind. The phonetic path is regular: Latin /tornare/ loses final -e stress patterns, Old French nasalization and /o/ rounding shift toward /ur/, Middle English borrowing after 1066 adapts French ou to ur spelled turn, with the distinctive British /tɜːn/ and American /tɝn/ developing after the Great Vowel Shift and later rhotic splits.

Corner follows a different path from body to city. Latin cornu meant horn, then by analogy any hard pointed projection: horn of an animal, wing of an army, end of a sail yard. In Late Latin cornu plus suffix produced coronarius, corniere in Old French for an angle, a street intersection, a hidden nook for ambush. Middle English borrowed corner around 1300 for the meeting of walls or streets. Its phonetics show Norman French /kornjer/ simplified to /kɔːrnər/, with modern RP /kɔːnə/ losing rhoticity. Semantically it retained duality: a corner is both shelter and danger, a place to hide and a place where danger lurks unseen, where visibility fails.

  • Literal combination (pre-1800): To turn the corner meant simply to navigate a street bend, often with connotations of disappearance and reappearance, pursuit and escape in London crime reports and novels.
  • Medical bridge (early 1800s): Physicians describing fevers and consumptions began to say a patient had turned the corner when the fever broke, borrowing the spatial image of passing a critical bend in the disease course.
  • Commercial and moral extension (mid-late 1800s): Victorian newspapers applied it to banks, railways, and harvests recovering from panic, and to drunkards or bankrupts reforming, reflecting industrial boom-bust cycles and evangelical narratives of redemption.
  • Modern idiomatization (1900-present): The progressive turning or perfect turned became fixed, displacing competitors like doubling the cape from nautical language; sports commentary reinforced literal cornering while preserving figurative optimism.

Socio-cultural context explains its persistence. Nineteenth-century cities made corners ubiquitous experiential realities: gridded streets, shop corners, policemen on corners, children sent around corners on errands. At the same time, modern medicine made crisis theory popular: diseases were thought to have turning points where nature decided life or death. The idiom fused urban spatial experience with clinical drama. Literary attestations abound. Dickens uses corner-turning for street movement that foreshadows moral revelation; Trollope and Gaskell use it for financial recovery; by the 1920s P. G. Wodehouse can use it ironically, as in Jeeves stories questioning whether an estate or tipster was really recovering two years ago, showing the phrase had become cliché enough to mock. Later, Churchill, post-war economists, and AIDS and cancer memoirs cemented its association with collective resilience.

Phonetically the phrase illustrates English rhythm: weak-strong-weak stress in TURNing the CORner with linking /ŋðə/ and reduced the to /ðə/, allowing rapid colloquial delivery. Semantically it drifted from neutral motion to inherently positive evaluation, so that today turning the corner without qualification almost always means for the better, requiring modifiers like turning the corner toward disaster to reverse polarity. Debates remain only about first figurative attestation, variously dated 1810s-1840s, but the trajectory from lathe to street to sickbed to stock market is securely attested and exemplifies how concrete bodily-spatial language becomes abstract emotional infrastructure.
