The history of ANOINTED is the history of a single, humble human gesture — smearing oil on the skin — rising, over some five millennia, to become the defining ritual of sacred kingship and one of the founding metaphors of Western religion. The word carries within it the full arc: from the shepherd who greases a wound to the prophet who pours oil on a king's head to the party leader who "anoints" a successor.

The Indo-European and Roman foundation:

  • The deepest recoverable level is the Proto-Indo-European root *ongʷ-, "to salve, smear, anoint". In the climate of the early Indo-European world, oil and fat were not cosmetics but necessities: they protected skin from sun and wind, softened leather, healed wounds, and — greased into the hair or onto the athlete's body — conferred a glistening, vigorous appearance that was itself a mark of health and distinction. This practical root is securely attested in Latin (unguere, unguen "fat", unguentum "ointment") and has cognates in Greek and Hittite.
  • Roman daily life shows the root in its ordinary register. Latin unguere was used of the bather who oils himself after the baths, the host who perfumes a guest's hair at dinner, the physician who dresses a wound, and the family that prepares a corpse for burial. Anointing was the unremarkable bodily technology of the Mediterranean; the Roman moralists even mocked excessive unguent and perfume as luxury and effeminacy. From this neutral, bodily base the word was ready to be carried upward into the sacred.
  • The compound inunguere, with the prefix in- "on, upon", shifted the verb's focus from reflexive self-oiling to the deliberate application of oil to a person or object by another — exactly the structure of a consecration. Late Latin added inunctio, "anointing", the ancestor of English unction, with its double life as both the physical rite and the figurative "unction" of sanctimonious smoothness.

Anointing in the biblical and Near Eastern world:

  • The conceptual leap from oil as comfort to oil as consecration was made long before Christianity, in the ancient Near East. In Israelite religion the verb māshaḥ "to anoint" marked out priests, prophets, and above all kings: Samuel anoints Saul and David; Zadok anoints Solomon. The anointed king was mashiaḥ — "the Anointed One", the Lord's chosen — and his consecration made him sacred and inviolable ("the Lord's anointed" could not be harmed even in his sinfulness, as David repeatedly acknowledges of Saul).
  • Greek-speaking Judaism and the New Testament translated mashiaḥ by khristós, "the anointed one", a translation that became in English Christ and in Latin-derived languages Cristo/Cristo. The very title of Jesus is thus, etymologically, "The Anointed One", and the word anointed sits at the etymological heart of Christianity. The Old French verb enoindre that gave English anoint was itself the daily instrument of transmitting this biblical vocabulary into the vernacular — the fourteenth-century English religious texts in which anointen first appears are precisely the context of the "Lord's anointed".

Medieval coronation and the sacral king:

  • With the Christianisation of western Europe, the biblical rite was welded to the Germanic idea of kingship. The Frankish, Anglo-Saxon, and later English and French monarchs were anointed with holy oil at their coronations, transforming a war-leader or elected king into a sacerdotal figure whose authority was "by the grace of God". The English coronation rite of 973 for King Edgar at Bath included the anointing, and from the 14th century the French monarchy claimed the miraculous Sainte Ampoule, the flask of holy oil said to have been brought by a dove at the baptism of Clovis, for the anointing of its kings.
  • The word thus carried into English political thought the full weight of sacral kingship. "The Lord's anointed" was the standard justification for obedience to the crown, and the doctrine of the divine right of kings rested squarely on the unction: an anointed king could not be deposed by ordinary means, and regicide was, in the words of the era, an assault on God's own anointed.
  • Sound and form travelled from Latin to English through the French channel with typical changes. Latin inungere, through Vulgar Latin, gave Old French enoindre (the [gʷ]/[g] of unguere became [j] before the palatal glide, giving the [ɲ] sound written -ign- in enoindre). Borrowed into Middle English c. 1300 through Anglo-Norman, the initial vowel was lowered to a-, giving anointen, anoynt, and its past participle anointed. The modern pronunciation /əˈnɔɪntɪd/ reflects the French-derived spelling rather than the Latin one.

From coronation to the modern political metaphor:

  • After the Reformation and the revolutions of the 17th century, the political force of sacral anointing faded in Protestant England, though the rite itself survives in the coronation of the British monarch to this day. The word did not vanish from public life: it was secularised. To "anoint" someone in modern politics, business, or sport is to designate a handpicked successor before any open contest — the boss "anoints" the heir, the party "anoints" the nominee. The term is now frequently ironic or critical, hinting at unearned, pre-ordained privilege rather than divine sanction.
  • The humble senses never died. "Anointing" a wound with ointment, oiling the skin, and the sacramental anointing of the sick (extreme unction, the last anointing) keep the physical core of the word alive. Anointed is thus one of those rare English words that still carries, in a single sound, the whole ladder of meanings — from first-aid ointment to the title of Christ.
  • The semantic continuity is the root metaphor itself: oil is poured upon something to mark it, to set it apart, to make it shine, to heal it. Every historical sense of ANOINTED — the athlete, the king, the priest, the sick person, the political successor — is an application of that single act of marking with oil, and that is why the word can move with such ease between the altar and the newsroom.
