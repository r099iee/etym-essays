In The Tale of Peter Rabbit (1902), lippity is the sound-word Beatrix Potter gives to Peter's cautious hopping: "he began to wander about, going lippity—lippity—not very fast, and looking all around." The word is a pure imitation of a small rabbit's gait — a light, springy, slightly uneven rhythm — and it exists almost entirely in that single canonical sentence. Its phonetics are its meaning: soft consonants (/l/, /p/), a bright front vowel, and a bouncy dactylic beat (/ˈlɪpɪti/) that mimics the contact-and-release of hopping paws on garden soil. The dash-separated repetition (lippity—lippity) turns the word into a tiny refrain, slowing the narrative tempo to match Peter's wary, exploratory pace after his escape from Mr. McGregor.

Primary sense. As an imitative (onomatopoeic) word, lippity denotes the gentle, rhythmic sound or movement of hopping — specifically the soft, quick, repeated bounce of a small creature. It functions adverbially ("going lippity") and can also work adjectivally ("a lippity sound"). It is not a standard dictionary headword but a nonce or expressive coinage, which is precisely why its meaning must be inferred from its sound and its context in the tale.

Polysemy and related forms. Because lippity is a coinage rather than an inherited word, its "senses" are variations on one acoustic idea rather than distinct dictionary entries:

  • Lippity—lippity (reduplicated refrain): the paired form Potter uses, emphasizing continuous, rhythmic hopping; the repetition itself is the reduplication that signals repeated motion and gives the word its tiny musical shape.
  • Lippity-hop / lippety-hop: later children's variants that fuse lippity with hop, making the hopping meaning explicit and echoing the older, more famous hippity-hop.
  • Lippety (spelling variant): the -ety spelling, influenced by hippety-hop and clippety-clop, occasionally appears in imitative prose and in homage texts.
  • Adjectival use: "a lippity sound," describing the noise of light, rhythmic steps or bounces on a path or floor.
  • Extended playful use: in modern children's writing and whimsical prose, lippity can describe any light, springy, cheerful gait — a skipping child, a bouncing ball — though this is always a deliberate echo of its rabbit origin.

Jargon, register, and cultural status. Lippity has no technical or jargon sense. Its register is children's literature and playful descriptive prose; it is never used in formal adult nonfiction. Collocations are almost entirely reduplicative and literary: going lippity-lippity, lippity-lippity, occasionally extended by later authors in homage to Potter. Its cultural status is unusual: it is one of the most quoted made-up words in English children's literature, prized precisely for being an honest, un-dictionary imitation that does one job perfectly — making a rabbit's hop audible on the page.

Metaphoric and associative bridges. The semantic bridge is pure sound symbolism: light consonants plus a quick repeated rhythm evoke smallness, lightness, and repeated gentle motion. This is the same phonesthetic logic that produced hippity-hop, pitter-patter, and clippety-clop — a whole native workshop of reduplicative motion-mimics. Lippity is distinguished within that family by its caution and softness: it is not playful-hasty like hippity-hop nor rapid like scamper, but stealthy, exploratory, and "not very fast," exactly matching a frightened but curious animal's wary progress through a garden after a narrow escape.
