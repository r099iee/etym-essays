• possessive: Similar because both refer to grammatical forms indicating ownership, affiliation, or control; differs because possessive is primarily a semantic description focusing narrowly on ownership and is commonly used for English pronouns (e.g., his, theirs), whereas genitive is the broader morphological and syntactic case category embracing origin, partition, composition, and objective relations.
  • attributive: Similar because both involve a noun or modifier standing in close syntactic relationship to another noun without an intervening linking verb; differs because attributive describes syntactic position and modification directly preceding the head noun (e.g., stone wall, morning walk) regardless of case inflection, whereas genitive requires a specific morphological case form or marker.
  • relational: Similar because both establish a functional semantic connection between two nominal concepts; differs because relational is an abstract linguistic and philosophical descriptor encompassing any associative link (including relational adjectives like presidential), whereas genitive designates a concrete formal grammatical case or clitic.
  • adnominal: Similar because both describe elements syntactically dependent upon and modifying a nominal head; differs because adnominal is a purely functional syntactic classification denoting any constituent that modifies a noun (including relative clauses and appositives), while genitive is a specific morphological case realization.
  • ablative: Similar because both are oblique grammatical cases that can convey provenance, origin, or source; differs because the ablative fundamentally encodes movement away from, separation, instrument, or cause, whereas the genitive primarily binds nominal entities in adnominal association and inherent relationship.
  • dative: Similar because both represent oblique case inflections within Indo-European case paradigms; differs because the dative typically designates the indirect recipient, beneficiary, or target of an action, whereas the genitive primarily qualifies or quantifies another noun.
  • prepositional: Similar because analytical languages frequently substitute prepositional phrases (like of in English or de in French) for inflectional case endings; differs because prepositional refers to the syntactic structure governed by an adposition or a specific Slavic locative case, whereas genitive denotes the underlying case relation itself.
