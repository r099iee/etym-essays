- 14th century: “chiefest” enters Middle English as a regular superlative of “chief” (= first, principal) — used naturally in religious and courtly writing; no sense of redundancy; it simply means “most first”, “foremost”.
- 16th–17th century Renaissance: fully accepted in the highest literary register — Shakespeare and the King James Bible use it for solemn, superlative praise; perception is entirely positive and grand.
- 18th–19th century: prescriptive grammarians declare it illogical (chief is already absolute), and it is pushed out of ordinary prose — public perception shifts to “archaic” or “pedantic”; it survives in poetry, dialect, and deliberate pastiche.
- 20th–21st century: revived as a self-conscious literary archaism in fantasy and historical fiction (Tolkien's Smaug scene); modern perception is “grand, old-fashioned, emphatic” — a word used for rhetorical height rather than plain description.
