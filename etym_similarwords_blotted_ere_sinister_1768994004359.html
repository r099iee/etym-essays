• stained: Similar because both denote a mark or discoloration left on a surface; differs because "stained" need not involve a blot-shaped mark or the smear of ink or water — any lasting discoloration qualifies, with a more chemical sense.
  • smeared: Similar because both involve spreading a wet or sticky substance across a surface; differs because "smeared" emphasizes the smudge's spread and messiness rather than the specific ink-stain, and the mark may be light or heavy.
  • spotted: Similar because both name marks on a surface; differs because "spotted" suggests small discrete points, often in a pattern, whereas a blot is larger and irregular.
  • obscured: Similar because both can mean hidden from sight (blotted out = obscured); differs because "obscured" is neutral and general about the means, while "blotted" implies a staining, absorbing darkness.
  • effaced: Similar because both mean wiped away or rubbed out; differs because "effaced" suggests deliberate rubbing or smoothing over, "blotted" a messier absorbing erasure.
  • erased: Similar because both mean removed from view or record; differs because "erased" is precise and deliberate (as of a pencil line), while "blotted out" is a dark, absorbing obliteration.
  • obliterated: Similar because both express total removal from visibility; differs because "obliterated" is stronger and may imply physical destruction rather than a dark covering.
  • blacked out / covered: Similar because both mean hidden beneath darkness; differs because "blotted out" keeps the ink-and-stain imagery, while "blacked out" suggests lost consciousness or a power cut.
  • before: Similar because "ere" simply IS "before" in every grammatical function; differs only in register — "ere" is archaic and poetic, "before" the everyday word.
  • previously / earlier: Similar because both denote a point in time prior to another; differs because these are adverbs only, while "ere" can also serve as a preposition and a conjunction.
  • prior to: Similar because both mean "before (in time)"; differs because "prior to" is a formal prepositional phrase, "ere" a single archaic word.
  • afore / erewhile: Similar because these are fellow archaisms meaning "before"; differs in that they are even rarer, mostly dialectal or pseudo-archaic.
  • ominous: Similar because both suggest impending harm or threat; differs because "ominous" means foreboding of something bad to come, while "sinister" points to a quality of active evil or menace in the person or thing itself.
  • menacing / threatening: Similar because both convey danger or harm; differs because these describe a direct, active warning of attack, while "sinister" can be silent, subtle, and merely suggestive.
  • evil / malevolent: Similar because both denote wickedness; differs because "evil" is the bald moral noun or adjective, while "sinister" concerns appearance and impression — the look of evil rather than the fact of it.
  • baleful: Similar because both mean harmful or threatening, with a dark portent; differs because "baleful" stresses the destructive effect (full of bane), "sinister" the uneasy impression.
  • forbidding / foreboding: Similar because both suggest an unpleasant or dangerous future; differs because "forbidding" is about discouraging approach, "foreboding" about a feeling of coming evil, while "sinister" is the static quality of menace itself.
  • inauspicious / unlucky: Similar because both descend from the omens-and-left-side semantic field; differs because these denote bad fortune rather than intentional evil, while "sinister" implies a hostile or evil quality.
  • creepy / unnerving: Similar because both describe an uneasy, disturbing feeling; differs because these are informal and subjective, "sinister" formal and about perceived moral threat.
  • left-handed / sinistral: Similar because "sinister" literally meant "left" and still does in technical use; differs because these are purely positional, with no moral overtones.
