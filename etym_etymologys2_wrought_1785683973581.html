WROUGHT is the direct English descendant of the Old English strong past form of the verb "work," reaching back to one of the most productive Indo-European roots for human activity, *werg-. The chain is traced in a single linear line with its securely attested forms and dates. CHAIN 1 — the direct line to WROUGHT: 1) PIE *werg- "to do, make, work" (the root of purposeful activity and labour; source of Greek érgon "work" and English work; reconstructed as *wérǵ- in LIV) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Proto-Germanic *wurkijaną "to work, make" (also *wurką "work"; the class-I weak verb built on the full grade of the root) — projected c. 2500–500 BCE. 3) Old English wyrċan / wircan "to work, make" (attested; a strong verb of the class that took the past forms worhte, wrohte with the velar fricative *-h- < PGmc *-k-) — c. 7th–11th c. 4) Middle English worchen / werchen "to work" (attested; past tense and participle worhte, wrohte, wrouȝt(e), with the r metathesized after the vowel in some forms) — c. 12th–15th c. 5) Early Modern English wrought (past tense and past participle of work, "made, fashioned, effected"; the spelling with -ough- preserves the old *-x- sound that fell silent in the 17th century, exactly as in thought and brought) — c. 15th–17th c. 6) Modern English wrought — WROUGHT (now archaic as a working verb form, surviving in the craft sense "shaped by work," as in wrought iron, and in the figurative "wrought a change," "wrought havoc," "wrought up"; the ordinary past of work is now worked). NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The derivation of the root is secure: PIE *werg- is one of the best-attested roots of the family, with reflexes in Greek (érgon, energós, organon), Germanic (work, wright), Armenian (gorc "work"), Hittite (wark- "to till"), and, in the shape *werg-/*worg-, the Slavic and Indo-Iranian words for work (e.g., Avestan varəzi- "work, tillage"). The modern reconstruction *wérǵ- is standard. (2) The internal history of the English forms is clear in outline: Old English wyrċan had the past worhte (with -h- for a velar fricative), and by Middle English the r had shifted position (worhte → wrohte), producing the wrouȝt spelling; the fricative fell silent in the 16th–17th centuries, and the word lost its status as the ordinary past of work to the regular weak form worked by the 16th century, surviving thereafter in the archaic, craft, and figurative senses above.
