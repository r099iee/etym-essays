PIE *sek- 'to cut' (reconstructed) → extension *skei- 'to cut, split, separate' (reconstructed) → [semantic bridge: to cut = to separate = to distinguish = to know] → Latin scīre 'to know, understand' (attested from c. 3rd c. BCE) → Latin nescīre 'not to know' = ne- 'not' + scīre (attested classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) → Latin nescius 'ignorant, unaware' (literally 'not-knowing'; attested 1st c. BCE–CE) → Old French nice 'foolish, simple, silly, clumsy' (attested from c. 11th c.) → Middle English nice 'foolish, wanton; later shy, fastidious, delicate, precise' (attested from c. 1290) → Middle English nicetee / nycetee 'folly, foolishness; a fine point' (attested from c. 1375, e.g. Chaucer) → Early Modern English nicety 'delicacy of discrimination, exactness, a minute point' (attested from the 16th c.) → NICETY. Note on the debate: The traditional etymology connects Latin scīre to the PIE root *skei- 'to cut, split,' the idea being that to know is to separate and distinguish; this analysis is standard but debated, and some scholars propose a different source for scīre. The extension *skei- itself is a secondary formation built on *sek-. The negative particle ne- is added only at the Latin stage (nescīre), not at the PIE level.
