coveted — desired intensely and ardently, especially that which belongs to another, is forbidden, or is beyond one's rightful station; in its strongest, most traditional sense, to covet is to wish to possess what one has no right to possess. The word carries an ancient moral weight: "thou shalt not covet" is the tenth commandment, and covetousness is ranked among the capital sins, so that the very sound of the word in English still hums with the pulpit and the law. In the passage from Gormenghast, the carvers have won, by skill, the honour of walking the battlements at the full moon — an honour they so coveted — and the word describes the longing that preceded the winning: a fierce, simmering ambition for a privilege reserved for the few, a desire felt in a world of rigid hierarchy where such honour is granted only to the most consummate craftsmen. The nuance of the word here is that of earned longing: the coveting is not the sinful theft of another's possession but the burning aspiration of the excluded for the threshold itself, the moonlit walkway that marks their elevation above their own kind — a longing intense enough to be called by a word the Commandments use for sin. The word is polysemous in degree and moral colour:
  • The biblical and legal sense — to desire inordinately what belongs to another; the tenth commandment's verb, the sin of greed for another's goods.
  • The weakened modern sense — to long for eagerly and intensely, of any object, prize, role, or honour, with the moral judgement softened to pure ardour.
  • The adjective coveted — intensely sought-after: "the coveted award," "the coveted position," a staple of prizes, markets, and careers.
  • The character noun — covetousness, greed as a settled disposition, one of the seven capital sins in the moral-theology tradition.
Its synonyms must be measured. To desire is the neutral, general verb, without moral colouring. To crave is to want with a bodily, appetitive urgency, as one craves food or drink. To yearn is to long with emotional ache, often for something distant or lost. To envy is to resent another's possession and wish it from him — envy keeps its eye on the neighbour; coveting keeps its eye on the thing. To hanker is colloquial, restless wanting. Covet alone carries the full moralized charge: the desire judged, the longing weighed against law and station, the appetite that boils within the bounds of order — which is precisely the register in which Peake sets the carvers' ambition. battlements — the notched parapet or defensive wall-top of a castle, fortification, or tower, consisting of a low wall in which alternating solid sections called merlons are separated by open gaps called crenels (or embrasures), through which defenders could shoot while remaining protected. The word descends from the vocabulary of fighting: battlements are literally the wall for battle, the fortification built for combat. In the passage, the carvers' skill has won them the honour of walking the battlements at the full moon — the moonlit walkway along the castle wall, the liminal high place between the inner world of the Earl and the outer world of the carvers, the visible prize of the honour they covet. The word's modern force is almost wholly romantic and medieval: battlements signal "castle," the notched silhouette against the sky; by extension it names any notched or toothlike architectural feature, and the decorative crenellation reproduced on Victorian villas, railway stations, and civic buildings.
  • Battlement senses: (1) the crenellated parapet of a fortress; (2) by extension, any notched or embrasured parapet, decorative or defensive; (3) figurative, the embattled skyline of a stronghold.
