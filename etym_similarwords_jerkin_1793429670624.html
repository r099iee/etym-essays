• Doublet: Similar because both were close-fitting Renaissance men's torso garments; differs because the doublet was the primary inner garment (often with ornate sleeves and waist tabs), whereas the jerkin was an outer layer worn directly over the doublet for warmth or protection.
  • Vest: Similar because both are sleeveless upper-body garments worn over shirts; differs because a vest is modern, lighter, and typically formal or casual office wear, lacking the heavy leather protective construction and flared skirts characteristic of the historical jerkin.
  • Waistcoat: Similar because both fit snugly over the upper body without sleeves; differs because a waistcoat evolved in the late 17th century specifically as part of the formal three-piece suit, whereas the jerkin belongs to the earlier Tudor/Stuart era and rugged military workwear.
  • Gilet: Similar because both are sleeveless insulated outer vests; differs because a gilet is a contemporary sporting or cold-weather garment (often down-filled or fleece), while a jerkin is historically constructed from stiff hide or heavy wool.
  • Tunic: Similar because both cover the torso down to the hip; differs because a tunic is typically looser, often draped or belted, and worn as an all-in-one slipover robe rather than a structured, buttoned, or laced jacket.
  • Buff coat: Similar because both were constructed of heavy protective yellow oxhide leather; differs because a buff coat was specifically a heavier, longer, full-sleeved military coat worn by 17th-century cavalry troopers, whereas a jerkin was shorter, lighter, and predominantly sleeveless.
  • Gambeson: Similar because both provided non-metallic torso defense in warfare; differs because a gambeson was a quilted, padded linen garment stuffed with horsehair or wool worn under steel plate armor, whereas a jerkin was an unquilted leather or cloth garment worn over civilian clothes or light mail.
  • Cuirass: Similar because both protect the breast and back of a combatant; differs because a cuirass consists of rigid forged metal or boiled cuir-bouilli plates strapped together, whereas a jerkin is flexible tailoring made from dressed hide.
  • Tabard: Similar because both are sleeveless outer garments of historical origin; differs because a tabard is an open-sided heraldic or ceremonial drape bearing coats of arms, lacking the snug fit, buttons, and tailored construction of a jerkin.
