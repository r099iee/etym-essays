Crept is not a root in its own right but the modern strong past tense (and past participle) of the verb creep, so its true biography is the biography of creep seen through one particular inflectional shape. To tell the story honestly we must begin on the continental Germanic side, follow the verb across the North Sea into England, then watch it change its endings while its meanings crawl outward from body motion to the farthest reaches of modern metaphor. 1. The Germanic inheritance and the sound changes The secure starting point is Proto-Germanic *kreupaną, a class II strong verb meaning "to creep, crawl," reconstructed from a solid set of daughter forms: Old English crēopan, Old Frisian kriāpa, Middle Dutch crūpen, Old Norse krjúpa "to creep, crouch," and Old High German kriochan (modern German kriechen "to crawl"). The fact that nearly every Germanic branch has a member is the strongest evidence that the verb was already present in the shared ancestor spoken in the late 2nd and 1st millennia BCE, long before the Anglo-Saxons reached Britain. The phonological journey into Old English is regular and instructive. In class II strong verbs, Proto-Germanic *-eu- became Old English ēo, exactly as *beudaną "to offer" became bēodan and *dreupaną became drēopan. Thus *kreupaną yielded crēopan. The conjugation followed the class II pattern: present crēopan, singular past crēap, plural past crupon, past participle cropen. It is from this participial and plural material that the modern crept will eventually emerge, so the class II shape matters more than it might at first appear. 2. Old and Middle English: the inherited physical core Old English crēopan is attested from the later Anglo-Saxon period in prose and glosses, always with the concrete sense of slow, low, body-close movement: crawling creatures, creeping things, people edging furtively along. It belongs to the same semantic zone as crawlan "to crawl" and slīcan "to sneak," and it already carries a hint of lowliness or stealth that the Latinate vocabulary of polite motion lacks. The noun creopera (a creeper, one who creeps) grows directly from this Old English stem, as does the common modern creeper for a creeping plant or a crawling animal. In Middle English the verb survives as crepen, with past forms crop, creep and participles cropen, crope, ycrepe. It is in this period that the first clear figurative uses appear beside the physical ones: time and abstract conditions begin to "creep," and the verb begins to shade into servile flattery (to "creep to" someone, to cringe). The semantic bridge is transparent — both a crawling body and a crawling spirit stay low, quiet, and unobtrusive, and both advance on others without dignity. 3. The birth of crept in Early Modern English The modern strong preterite crept standardizes during the 16th and 17th centuries. Its origin is debated but well understood in outline: the modern form is built by attaching -t to the stem of the old participle and preterite material, exactly parallel to kept (from keep), left (from leave), and slept (from sleep), where a final -t replaced or fused onto an older ending. Older literary forms crope and cropen survive into the 16th century (Spenser, for example, still uses crope), before crept wins out as the standard literary past. The OED records that *creeped, formed by the weak pattern, appears occasionally from the 16th century onward but has never displaced crept in careful prose. The result is one of English's slightly anomalous-looking forms: a strong past that looks as if it ought to be weak, yet is fully standard. 4. Semantic drift and the growth of modern polysemy
  • Physical motion of persons and animals — the inherited core, unchanged for more than a thousand years.
  • Plants — creeping growth — attested by Middle English, fully standard in modern English ("ivy crept up the wall").
  • Time and abstraction — slow, inexorable advance: "the hours crept by," "a creeping sense of dread," present in literature since the 16th century and now everyday idiom.
  • Servility — "to creep to" someone, to behave fawningly: a derogatory social sense chiefly of the 16th–19th centuries.
  • Creep / creepy / the creeps — unease: the 19th century develops the noun "the creeps" ("it gives me the creeps") from the sensation of something crawling on the skin, and the 20th century extends "creep" to a repellent person. The low, skin-level, uninvited movement is now applied to human dread.
  • Feature / scope / mission creep — the 20th–21st-century technical metaphors: software, projects, and military missions "creep" beyond their boundaries precisely because creeping is slow, incremental, and easy to miss until it has already spread everywhere.
  • Engineering creep — slow deformation of metal under sustained stress: a 20th-century scientific jargon use of the identical gradual-advance image.
5. Tolkien and the literary moment In The Hobbit, the narrator's "he crept nervously to the door of the parlour" mobilizes this entire history in a single word. The verb is native Germanic, as old as the language itself; its strong past carries the dignified irregularity of centuries; and its whole semantic field — low, slow, stealthy, uneasy — is exactly what Bilbo's approach requires. A hobbit creeping is neither a hero striding nor a villain lurking; he is a modest householder edged on by his own anxiety. The choice of crept over creeped is not pedantry: the strong form is the literary standard, and it quietly signals to the reader that this is the language of tradition and tale-telling, the deep old stratum of English that Tolkien so prized.
