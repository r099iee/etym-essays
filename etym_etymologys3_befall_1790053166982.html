The evolution of befall is the evolution of a Germanic compounding idea — around-fall — from literal tumbling to fated happening, preserved almost unchanged in form for twelve centuries while its worldview shifted from pagan lot to Christian providence to literary archaism. It is be- + fall, and both halves tell a history.

  • Roots: around and to tumble. The prefix be-, Old English be-, bi- about, around, on all sides, continues Proto-Germanic *bi- by, near, around, from PIE *h₂embhi- around, about, on both sides, also seen in Latin ambi- around, Greek amphi- on both sides. The base fall, Old English feallan to fall, drop, fail, die, continues Proto-Germanic *fallaną to fall, tumble. Deeper PIE affiliation of *fallaną is debated: often referred to PIE *bhol-no- from *bhel- to blow, swell, bubble via sense of tumbling inflated motion, or to *(s)pel- to fall, with cognates uncertain; OED marks ultimate origin obscure beyond Germanic, and no secure non-Germanic cognate commands consensus. What is secure is Germanic: Old Saxon fallan, Old Norse falla, Gothic fallan, Old High German fallan all mean to fall. Compound befeallan in Old English thus meant to fall to, fall upon, fall around — leaves fall around a tree, a lot falls to a man, darkness falls upon travellers.
  • Old English befeallan: lot, arrival, deprivation. In Beowulf, the Chronicle, and laws, befeallan governs dative persons: him befeol — it fell to him, he received as share; hunger, night, death befeoll — fell upon. Senses include to fall to as inheritance, to come to pass, to be deprived of through falling away. Syntax is impersonal: me befallið what falls to me. Worldview is lot-casting: what befalls is what falls when dice, twigs, or shares are thrown — chance with divine supervision.
  • Middle English bifallen: chronicle and romance (12th–15th c.). Ormulum, Layamon, Chaucer use bifallen, befallen for happen, come to pass, arrive. Chaucer: as it bifel upon a day; sorrow bifel them. Past befell, befel, past participle bifallen stabilize. The literal to fall around fades; the eventive to happen to dominates. French hap chance, aventure begin to compete, but befall keeps native solemnity for evils and wonders. Pronunciation with be- unstressed /bə-/, fall with full vowel.
  • Early Modern: Bible fixes fatefulness (16th–17th c.). Tyndale, Geneva, and King James translators choose befall for Hebrew qarah to meet, happen and Greek symbainein to come together: lest any evil befall thee, no evil shall befall thee, all these things have befallen us. Liturgy and pulpit make befall the verb of providence. Shakespeare uses both neutral and dark: What hath befallen them?; Fierce wretchedness befell. Dictionaries define befall as to happen to, to light upon. Form settles to befall, befell, befallen; third singular befalls, befall-eth archaic.
  • Modern: retreat to literary doom (18th–21st c.). Everyday speech abandons befall to happen to, occur to, hit; befall survives in law's acts of God formulae, hymns, fantasy, and deliberate archaism. Tolkien, Lewis, and pulp voyagers — If such befall you — reach for it to make circumstance feel cosmic without invoking a god. Grammar retains dative experiencer: befall you, befell him, never befall to you. RP pronunciation is /bɪˈfɔːl/, with reduced be- /bɪ/, stress on fall, rhotic-free long awe. The verb is now strong-irregular by analogy with fall, fell, fallen, though befall historically showed mixed paradigms.

Debated nuance: the deeper PIE root of fall remains disputed and must not be overstated; Germanic *fallaną is secure, its beyond-Germanic link is not. All histories agree on be- from *h₂embhi- around and on the Old English compound sense to fall upon, and on the King James role in darkening and solemnizing the verb. To say today that tragedy befell a city is thus to speak Old English syntax with Jacobean gravity about a timeless image — misfortune as something that falls, surrounds, and cannot be dodged, even when we insist it came purely by circumstance.
