• Hurl: Similar because both descend from the same Middle English frequentative verb built on Old French hurter, and both involve violent motion; differs because to hurl is to throw — the subject is the thrower (a pitcher hurls a ball, an angry man hurls abuse) — while to hurtle is to be thrown, the thing itself moving at terrifying speed with no agent launching it.
  • Plummet: Similar because both describe rapid downward movement; differs because plummeting emphasizes a straight vertical fall under gravity and lacks the roaring, crashing volume and physical mass that hurtling insists on — a feather plummets, a boulder hurtles.
  • Plunge: Similar because both describe violent, uncontrolled motion; differs because plunging suggests immersion into water or darkness with a destination in view, whereas hurtling keeps the raw speed, weight, and imminent threat of impact.
  • Careen: Similar because both describe wild, rapid, poorly controlled motion; differs because careening implies swaying or lurching from side to side, whereas hurtling is a straight, mass-driven rush toward an impact.
  • Speed / race: Similar because both denote rapid motion; differs because speeding and racing imply deliberate, controlled velocity with an engine or a competitive purpose, and carry no menace, while hurtling is unstoppable, dangerous momentum.
  • Dash: Similar because both name a violent burst of motion; differs because a dash is directed and purposeful, launched by an agent, whereas hurtling is direction given by mass and gravity, not intention.
  • Rush: Similar because both involve fast movement; differs because rushing can be deliberate haste by a person or a crowd, whereas hurtling names the violent flight of a thing — usually heavy — with the constant threat of collision.
  • Career (verb): Similar because to career is also to move swiftly and uncontrollably; differs because career is less weighty and less menacing, often applied to a runaway vehicle or animal, while hurtling emphasizes mass, noise, and catastrophe.
