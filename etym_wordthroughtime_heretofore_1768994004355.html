- 14th c. - Middle English welds here + to + fore into one temporal adverb meaning 'up to this time'; the compound is a small architecture of pointing, converting three spatial deictics into a single time-pointer.
- 15th–17th c. - the word finds its permanent home in the law: contracts and statutes use it to seal a closed past (all obligations 'heretofore incurred'); its register hardens into the formal, bookish, legalistic.
- 18th–19th c. - it spreads through official and literary prose; Victorian adventure fiction (Burroughs) uses it as a raised curtain, holding a tableau of stillness before the storm breaks.
- Contemporary - rare in speech and confined to legal and formal registers, it still carries the finality of a closed ledger: it shuts the past whole, then opens the scene to what follows.
