- [c. 1300s–1400s: Abrasive domestic & military cleaning] - English speakers encounter scour(en) as hard rubbing of pots, armor, and gear; associated with sand, grit, and servant or camp labor rather than polite washing.
- [1500s–1600s: Sweeping the land] - Perception widens: to scour country is to ride or tramp through it clearing game, rebels, or threats; the word feels active, outdoor, and slightly martial.
- [1700s–1800s: Exhaustive hunt & inquiry] - Novels and reports use scoured for systematic search of moors, cities, and papers; readers hear thoroughness and urgency more than mere looking.
- [Late 1800s–1900s: Technical & journalistic hardening] - Engineering ‘scour’ (erosion of beds/piers) and press idioms (‘scoured the records’) fix the word as professional-grade completeness.
- [Contemporary dual life] - Household scouring (pads, powders) coexists with digital-age ‘scoured the internet’; public perception still pairs grit and relentlessness—no casual glance qualifies as scouring.
