- Late 15th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Middle French *banquet*; primarily denoted a light dessert course of sweetmeats, fruits, and wine served after the main meal.
- 16th to 17th Century (Tudor & Elizabethan Eras) - Maintained dual identity: strictly denoted the opulent sugar-and-spice dessert course consumed in specialized architectural "banqueting houses," while simultaneously expanding to describe any grand, celebratory feast of royal or aristocratic proportions.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Solidified in public consciousness as the standard term for a grand civic dinner honoring public figures, accompanied by toasts, formal orations, and patriotic declarations.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era & French Politics) - Acquired revolutionary political weight in France via the *Campagne des banquets* (1847–1848), which brought down the July Monarchy; concurrently adopted in French letters for intellectual circles such as Proust's magazine *Le Banquet* (1892), echoing Plato's *Symposium*.
- 20th Century - Formalized into commercial catering and hotel operations ("banquet hall", "banquet menu"); retained supreme prestige for diplomatic state dinners and international honors such as the Nobel Banquet.
- 21st Century - Continues as both the premier global designation for ceremonial milestone celebrations and a vibrant literary metaphor for intellectual and sensory richness.
