- [Early 18th century: a French import] - ogre enters English (c. 1705, via Perrault's fairy tales) naming the man-eating giant of the conte; at first it is a fashionable, exotic Gallicism confined to the nursery and the library of fairy tales.
- [18th–19th century: the fairy-tale staple] - the word hardens into the stock man-eating giant of folklore — the devourer of children who lurks in castles and dark places; Perrault and the oral tradition make it a byword of childhood terror, folkloric rather than supernatural.
- [19th century: metaphorical transfer to persons] - ogre extends to human cruelty: a cruel, devouring person or institution (an ogre of a boss, the ogre of hunger) keeps the appetite but drops the size.
- [20th century: genre vocabulary] - in modern fantasy (including The Hobbit, 1937) ogre is codified into the monster-menagerie — man-eating, humanoid, crude, distinct from giant, troll, and goblin — and becomes a fixture of games and children's fiction.
- [Contemporary sense] - ogres remain the archetypal man-eating folklore giants, nursery-tale terror without magic; in everyday speech the word is playful or figurative, always evoking the cautionary tale rather than real fear.
