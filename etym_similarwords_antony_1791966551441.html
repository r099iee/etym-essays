• Antonius: Similar because it is the Latin original of the English name and denotes the same Roman family; differs because Antonius is the formal nomen used in Latin sources and in scholarly writing, whereas Antony is the anglicised form and can also denote the triumvir.
  • Anthony: Similar because it is the same name and the common modern spelling in English; differs because the initial h is a sixteenth-century learned intrusion, and Anthony is now the default spelling for the saint and the given name, while Antony is retained for the Roman and in some British usage.
  • Mark Antony: Similar because he bears the identical Roman name and is the orator's direct descendant; differs because the triumvir belongs to the next generation, is famous for politics, war and Cleopatra rather than for eloquence, and is the Antony normally meant by the bare name.
  • Antonine: Similar because it derives from the related Latin name Antoninus and shares the same onomastic stock; differs because it is an adjective for the dynasty of Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, not a personal name, and refers to emperors of the second century CE.
  • Crassus: Similar because he is Antony's fellow consul and the other senior speaker in De Oratore; differs because Crassus represents the ideal of the philosophically educated orator, while Antony defends practical, experience-based technique.
  • Scaevola: Similar because the aged Quintus Mucius Scaevola is the third senior Roman authority in the dialogue's world; differs because he is a jurist and pontiff rather than an orator, and his role is to voice conservative scepticism about eloquence.
  • Anton: Similar because it is a continental given name from the same Latin Antonius; differs because it is the standard form in German, Russian and Scandinavian usage and has no connection to the Roman orator in English contexts.
  • Antonian: Similar because it is the adjective formed from Antony and can describe his style or his followers; differs because it is a descriptive grammatical derivative, not a name, and in classical usage the adjective can describe the legions or partisans of the triumvir.
