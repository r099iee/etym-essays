Section 1 — the whole career of “decrepit” English decrepit is a straight Latin loan whose force is still audible if one hears the creak inside it. Classical Latin crepāre meant to rattle, creak, crack, clatter, or burst with a sharp noise—the sound of something under strain or breaking: creaking doors, crackling fire, rattling wheels, a joint that snaps. Scholars generally treat the verb as imitative, echoing the noise itself, and no secure Proto-Indo-European root is reconstructed for it; the tree therefore begins in Latin, with no forced PIE ancestry. From the same noisy base Latin built a small medical and descriptive vocabulary: crepitus, “a rattle or crackle,” still used in English clinical language for the grating sound of a joint or the crackle of the lungs; crepitare, “to crackle,” giving English crepitate and crepitation; and crepāculum, the ancient Roman rattle used to call children—a charming proof of how everyday the “noise” idea was in the culture that coined the family. The adjective dēcrepitus adds the prefix dē-, “down, thoroughly, to the bottom,” to that cracking base. The resulting image is of something cracked all the way down: broken by use and time, creaking at every move. Latin usage already specialized dēcrepitus for extreme old age and infirmity—of people whose bodies creak and are spent, and by extension of things past their strength. The prefix’s force matters: dē- here is intensive as much as directional, turning “creak” into “creak out,” “crack to pieces.” A decrepit man in Latin is not simply an old man; he is one whose machinery has audibly run down, like a hinge that groans. The prefix, invisible in the modern English word, is what gives decrepit its peculiar finality: the wear is not incidental but thorough, down to the bottom. English borrowed decrepit in the late medieval to early modern period with the Latin sense intact, and in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries English formed the abstract noun decrepitude for the state of being so worn. The word has remained remarkably faithful to its Latin image for five centuries. Applied to people, decrepit means enfeebled and nearly spent by age—the passage’s Nannie Wang is “too old and decrepit to be of much real service,” a phrase that reads as practical household judgment because decrepit, then as now, is less a diagnosis than an assessment of usefulness gone. Applied to buildings, vehicles, and machines, the word extended (especially from the nineteenth century) to mean dilapidated, unsafe, on the point of collapse: a decrepit building is one whose fabric has cracked and creaked its way to ruin. The Romance family shows how the crack/break image slides toward total failure. Italian crepare means “to crack” and, colloquially, “to die”; French crever means “to burst” and, colloquially, “to die” (as in crever de faim, “to die of hunger”). English crack and creak are Germanic sound-imitative parallels of the same noise, not proven genetic relatives of Latin crepāre, and the words should not be merged in a single family tree. In English the adjective stays close to its Latin origin: decrepit is the word for age and decay that has audibly “creaked” a person or thing into near-uselessness—stronger, more pitying or contemptuous, and far more vivid than plain old. Section 2 — sense-by-sense evolution in detail - 15th century: decrepit arrives in English from Latin (via French intermediaries in some contexts) with the sense “worn out, broken down, enfeebled by age.” - 16th–18th centuries: applied chiefly to persons and animals; a decrepit old age, the broken-down frame of the aged; the noun decrepitude appears alongside. - 19th century: the range widens to buildings, vehicles, bridges, and machines fallen into ruin; decrepit becomes a standard word for structural dilapidation. - 20th–21st centuries: the figurative extension to institutions, systems, and arguments settles in; decrepit remains literary-to-standard, carrying more pity or contempt than plain old, and medical usage keeps crepitus for the actual sound. Section 3 — cultural and literary notes The word has always had a managerial edge, and the passage uses exactly that edge: Grandmother Jia is running a household, and decrepit is her accountant’s word for an asset past service. The Latin image of the cracking hinge is preserved in English across five centuries, which is why the word still works so vividly: we can hear the creak. Decrepit, then, is a word whose entire history is a single sound—crepāre, the rattle and crack of things breaking down—made into an adjective of age, ruin, and the moment when a person or thing passes the point of usefulness.
