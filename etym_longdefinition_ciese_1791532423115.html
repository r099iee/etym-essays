Form and core sense. Ciese is the Anglian Old English spelling of cēse, the word for cheese; the West Saxon form is cēse and the Anglian cīese shows a different vowel development. It is a very old loanword from Latin cāseus, "cheese," taken into West Germanic before the Anglo-Saxons migrated to Britain, and it survives as Modern English cheese.

The food and the word. Cheese is a preserved dairy food made by curdling milk, separating the curd from the whey, and pressing, salting and ripening the curd. The word names the product, the craft and, by extension, a whole family of dairy terms. In Old English it appears in glosses where Latin caseus is rendered cīese or cēse, and in everyday contexts such as food lists and monastic accounts, where cheese was a staple of the medieval diet.

The Latin loan in Germanic. The borrowing is one of a group of early Latin loans that entered the Germanic languages through trade, Roman military presence and the frontier economy. The same Latin word gives German Käse, Dutch kaas, Old Norse kæsir and Old English cēse, which shows that the loan predates the separation of the Anglo-Saxons from the continental Germanic peoples. The word therefore arrived in England already in the settlers' vocabulary, not as a later church or court borrowing.

Old English spelling variants.

  • Cēse is the West Saxon form, with a long close-mid vowel.
  • Cīese (often printed ciese) is the Anglian form, with a diphthongal or fronted vowel written ie.
  • Both continue the same West Germanic *kāsijaz, from Latin cāseus.
  • The initial c in Old English is pronounced /tʃ/ before a front vowel, which is why modern cheese begins with /tʃ/ rather than /k/.
  • The final -e was pronounced in Old English and was lost in Middle English, giving chese and then cheese.

The modern word and its extensions. Modern cheese has developed a remarkable range of secondary senses:

  • "Big cheese": an important person. This is probably not from the food at all but from Urdu or Persian chīz, "thing," via nineteenth-century slang; the homonymy is accidental.
  • "Cheesy": cheap, sentimental or in poor taste. The link is probably the idea of cheese as plain, common or slightly spoiled food, though the exact route is debated.
  • "Say cheese": the photographic instruction to smile, current since the early twentieth century, chosen for the shape of the mouth rather than for any meaning.
  • "Cheesed off": British slang for annoyed or fed up, recorded in the twentieth century, of uncertain origin.
  • "Cheese it!": old slang for "stop it" or "run away," recorded in the nineteenth century.
  • Sports and games: "cheese" can mean a soft or easily intercepted shot, a fastball, or a cheap tactic in video games.
  • Shape and texture: "head cheese" is a meat jelly, and "cheese" can name a pressed block of any substance, from tobacco to metal.

Technical and craft vocabulary. Cheesemaking has its own terms: curd, whey, rennet, starter culture, brine, rind, ageing, affinage. Cheeses are classified by moisture, milk, texture and origin — hard, semi-hard, soft, blue, washed-rind, fresh. The word also appears in food science (cheese analogues, processed cheese), in law (protected designations such as Parmigiano-Reggiano) and in commerce (cheese mongers, cheese boards).

Semantic bridges. The central sense is a food; the extensions are of three kinds. The first is metonymic and material: cheese as a shaped block gives "head cheese" and other pressed masses. The second is evaluative: cheese as ordinary, cheap or strong-smelling food gives "cheesy" and "cheesed off." The third is purely phonetic and gestural: "say cheese" is about the mouth, not the meaning. In each case the word's homely, concrete reference makes it available for metaphor and slang.

Register. Cheese is a basic, everyday word, but its slang derivatives carry strong evaluative colour: "cheesy" is mildly contemptuous, "big cheese" is jocular, "cheesed off" is informal and British. The Old English word had none of this; it was a plain item of diet. The modern richness is the product of centuries of urban slang and food culture.
