A glint is a brief, sharp flash of light, typically reflected back off a polished, metallic, or glossy surface at the exact instant it catches the eye. The essence of the word is brevity, suddenness, and hardness: a glint appears and vanishes in a moment, a bright point struck from a shadowed or dark background, never a steady or sustained illumination. It is the light of steel, glass, water, ice, and eyes — light that does not shine of itself but answers to a source, catching and throwing back a momentary spark.
  • Literal core sense: a small, brief flash of light caused by reflection from a shiny surface. The light source (moon, sun, firelight) stands behind the observer; the surface merely throws the light back for an instant, so a glint is always conditional and quick, dependent on angle and motion.
  • In the present literary context: the hobbit riding above the mountain sees the glint of the moon on goblin spears and helmets as long lines of the wicked folk creep down the hillsides. Here the word works as stealthy revelation: each glint is a tiny, hard spark of moonlight that betrays armed enemies in the dark, a menace accumulating glint by glint into a whole creeping host. The image is cool, metallic, and precise, the tone ominous rather than beautiful; the momentary nature of the light hints at furtiveness — the goblins would hide in the dark, and only the betraying flash of steel on helms and spears gives them away.
  • The figurative eye sense: a glint in one's eye or a glint of mischief/determination transfers the small bright flash to a human gaze, reading a sudden intention — mischief, cunning, ambition, or anger — from a flicker of light in the eyes. It implies that an inner purpose is betrayed by an involuntary spark, exactly as the steel betrays the goblin in the dark.
  • Metaphoric extensions: a glint of hope, of humour, of recognition treat the emotion as a brief, hard point of light in a dim situation — present but not lasting, enough to be seen but not to illuminate. The metaphor preserves suddenness and smallness: a glint of hope is a faint, momentary sign, not a settled confidence.
  • Verb senses (archaic and dialectal): the older verb to glint (and glent) meant both "to flash or gleam" and "to cast a quick sidelong glance", connecting light and sight: to glint at someone was to shoot them a darting look, and the eye sense is thus not merely modern metaphor but an ancient link in the word's own history.
  • Jargon and technical uses: in optics and astronomy, "glint" describes specular reflection off aircraft, satellites, or water that registers as a momentary bright point to an instrument; in military surveillance, "glint" names the radar return flashing off a fast-moving object's edges; in computer graphics, glints are the small specular highlights that make a surface read as wet, metallic, or polished.
The boundaries against neighbouring light-words repay attention. Gleam is soft, steady, subdued light, often gentle or benign; glow is warm, diffuse, emitted light without a hard edge; glitter is a series of many small bright flashes rather than a single point; glimmer is faint, wavering, unsteady light that may be only half seen; sparkle is lively, festive, fragmented light in motion; shimmer is a trembling, soft sheen as on heat-hazed air or silk; twinkle is small and rhythmic, often of stars or eyes; flash is larger and more sustained — a glint is, strictly, a flash of the smallest, hardest kind, one point of light for an instant, and its metallic connotation (steel, water, ice) is what distinguishes it from the warmer gleam and the festive sparkle. Grammatically, glint is both a countable noun (a glint, glints) and a verb (to glint, glinting, glinted); it typically appears in the singular with a of-phrase (a glint of steel) or in the plural where many separate points of light are meant (the glints of a distant fleet), and its register is cool, precise, and faintly literary. The word answers to a repeated pattern in myth and story: in the dark, the first sign of the enemy is not a shape but a light — the glint of a blade, the glint of an eye — and because it is momentary, it both warns and vanishes, which is why storytellers so often use it at moments of gathering danger. In the passage before us the word does exactly that work, and the plural-in-essence image of many weapons catching the moon turns a single small flash into the sign of an entire hidden army.
