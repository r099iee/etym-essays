Compound word with two root elements, each traced separately, plus the prefix dis-. ROOT 1: equi- (from equilibrium) — "equal, even, level." Linear derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eyḱ- (also cited *ayḱ-, older *aik-, *ayk-), "even, level, flat, equal; to make level" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Cognates are sparse but secure: the root is essentially confined to the Italic branch (Latin aequus) with probable distant connections debated. NOTE: the laryngeal/laryngeal-set spelling is debated among modern Indo-Europeanists, but the reconstruction "even/level" is standard (Pokorny *aik-; de Vaan *h₂eyḱ-). 2. Latin aequus, "level, even, flat, equal; just, fair, impartial" — attested from the 3rd century BCE (Plautus, Terence); from PIE *h₂eyḱ- with adjectival suffix, applied both to physical flatness and moral fairness. 3. Latin aequus + lībra (see ROOT 2) → Latin aequilibrium, "an even balance, equilibrium, a state of equal weight on both sides" — classical Latin, attested from the 1st century BCE (Vitruvius, Cicero), formed from aequus "equal" + lībra "scale, balance." 4. English equilibrium, "a state of rest or balance due to the equal action of opposing forces" — borrowed from Latin (via French équilibre) into English scientific and philosophical prose, attested from 1608. 5. English disequilibrium = Latin prefix dis- ("apart, asunder, reversal") + equilibrium, "the state of having lost equilibrium" — formed in English scientific writing, attested from the late 19th century (c. 1880s, economics and physics). TARGET: disequilibrium. ROOT 2: libra — "scales, balance, pound." Linear derivation chain: 1. Pre-Latin Mediterranean substrate word *lībra, "scales, balance, unit of weight (the Roman pound)" — of uncertain, probably non-Indo-European origin; possibly Etruscan, likely a loan into Latin from the Italic/substrate sphere. NOTE: the etymology is not settled; no convincing PIE root has been established, and most handbooks treat it as a substrate or loanword, possibly connected to Etruscan libr-. 2. Latin lībra, "a balance, a pair of scales; a Roman pound (by weight)" — attested from early Latin; the sign "£" for the British pound derives from lībra. 3. Latin aequilibrium, "an even balance" — compound of aequus + lībra (as in ROOT 1, stage 3). 4. English equilibrium (1608) → English disequilibrium (c. 1880s). TARGET: disequilibrium. PREFIX: dis- — from PIE *dwis- "in two, apart, asunder," via Latin dis- "apart, in different directions, reversal, negation"; productive in English learned compounds from the 16th century onward. Debated / nuanced note: the exact PIE reconstruction of aequus (whether *h₂eyḱ- or *ayḱ-) is a matter of scholarly debate, and the origin of lībra is unknown (substrate hypothesis); both are flagged rather than asserted.
