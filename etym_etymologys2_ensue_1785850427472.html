CHAIN: FROM PIE TO ENSUE (7 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *sekʷ- "to follow" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; one of the most widely attested IE roots — Sanskrit sácate "follows", Greek hépomai "I follow", Latin sequī).
  2. Latin sequī "to follow" (attested from the earliest Latin literature, 3rd–2nd centuries BCE; deponent verb, "to follow, pursue, come after").
  3. Latin insequī "to follow closely, pursue, attack" (classical, attested 1st century BCE; in- "on, upon, after" + sequī).
  4. Vulgar Latin *insequere "to follow, pursue" (reconstructed, c. 4th–6th centuries CE; the late re-forming of the deponent verb on a regular active model — the bridge by which the verb entered Romance).
  5. Old French ensuivre / ensuir "to follow after, ensue, result" (attested 11th–13th centuries; from *insequere; modern French retains the phrase il s'ensuit que, "it follows that").
  6. Anglo-French ensuer / enssuer "to follow, ensue" → Middle English ensuen "to follow, pursue, come after, result" (recorded from the 14th century; used by Chaucer in Troilus and Criseyde, c. 1374).
  7. English ENSUE "to happen afterward as a result or in immediate sequence" (modern form; the "pursue" sense became obsolete in the 17th century).

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The Vulgar Latin *insequere is reconstructed, not directly attested; the attested chain runs Latin insequī → Old French ensuivre, whose regular development already shows the re-formed stem.
  • The early English verb could still mean "to pursue" (following a person); this sense was lost in the 16th–17th centuries, leaving the impersonal "to follow in consequence".
