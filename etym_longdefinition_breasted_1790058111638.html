breasted is the past tense and past participle of the verb to breast, meaning to meet, push, or advance with the breast or chest leading, to confront head-on, or to climb over by chesting: he breasted the hill, she breasted the tape, they breasted the waves. In your sentence — Lanarck breasted the ship into the water beside the raft — it means he shoved, thrust, or eased the ship forward bodily, chest and shoulder to hull, forcing it into the water alongside the raft, as one breasts a crowd, a current, or a slope. The sense is physical, effortful, chest-first propulsion rather than towing or sailing: body as ram and lever.

The bridge from noun to verb is body-part metonymy universal in English (to head, to shoulder, to elbow, to breast): the prominent front of the torso that faces the world gives its name to the act of facing. Old English breost meant chest, bosom, heart-seat of feeling; Middle English to brest, to breast (14th-16th c.) came to mean to oppose with that chest, to meet waves, enemies, hills breast-to-breast, then to surmount them by that meeting. To breast a hill is both to face it and to conquer it; to breast the tape is both to touch it with chest and to win. Past breasted with regular -ed marks the completed push.

  • Physical shove with chest-shoulder (your sense): to push a boat, door, crowd, defender aside by leaning chest into it. He breasted the ship into the water, she breasted her way through the crowd, the prop breasted the tackler. Rugby, rescue, launching contexts. Implies close contact, short leverage, grunt effort, not mechanical haul.
  • Confrontational-face sense: to face boldly, to stand up to. To breast danger, to breast criticism, to breast the storm. Literary-heroic, slightly archaic: breast the blows of fortune. Bridge from bodily front to moral front — chest as seat of courage (breastplate, brave breast).
  • Climbing-surmounting sense (most common today): to ascend to the top of, to reach the crest of. To breast a hill, to breast the ridge, climbers breasted the summit at dawn. The chest leads uphill; the hill is met and overcome. Fell-walking and military reports favour it: the battalion breasted the slope under fire.
  • Sporting tape sense: to finish first by chesting the finishing tape or line. She breasted the tape in 11 seconds, he breasted the line. Athletics, horse-racing (horse's breast), swimming (to breast the wall). Photo-finish language; implies victory by inches of torso.
  • Nautical-technical nuance: to breast a ship can mean to bring broadside to, to secure by breast-ropes (breast lines, breast anchor), or to push off as here. Breast line, breast rope, breast oar all use breast as lateral-front. Your sci-fi launching blends everyday shove with this rope-work echo.
  • Participial-adjectival homograph: breasted also occurs as adjective in compounds — deep-breasted, broad-breasted, single-breasted, double-breasted (of coats, birds, ships, voices). Double-breasted suit overlaps breast front panels; deep-breasted song resonates from chest. Different formation (noun + -ed possessing) but same body imagery; context disambiguates from verbal past.
  • Noun background (for full polysemy): breast as noun means chest front, female bosom and mammary gland, meat of bird's breast (chicken breast), brisket of lamb, breast of a hill (slope), breast-wall, breastwork fortification. Verb senses inherit tenderness (bosom) and toughness (breastplate) together: to breast is both to nurture-forward and to armour-forward.

Formally breasted is regular weak past (breast-breasted-breasted, /ˈbrestɪd/ with syllabic -ed after t). Spelling breast with -ea- for /e/ is historic (Old English eo diphthong levelled). Collocations: breasted the hill, breasted the tape, breasted the waves, breasted his way, broad-breasted, double-breasted. Register is literary-muscular for the verb (fell-walking, sport, epic), everyday for food and clothing compounds. To say Lanarck breasted the ship dignifies a grunt of launching with epic chest-forward heroism.
