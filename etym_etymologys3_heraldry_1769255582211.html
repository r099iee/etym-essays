The biography of heraldry begins, remarkably, not with parchment and paint but with the camp-fire world of a migrating warrior band, and with two separate Proto-Indo-European roots that only met centuries later in the compound from which the word descends.

Root one: the war-band. The first root is PIE *koryo- (also written *koro-, *kórios), "war-band, host, army" — the levied mass of fighting men who accompanied a leader. In the Hellenic branch this root did not survive as the ordinary word for "army" (Greek used stratós, a different word); instead it left the leadership derivative koíranos "commander, lord", so that even in Greek the trace of the root points toward the man who heads the host rather than the host itself. In the Germanic branch, however, *koryo- flourished as the plain word for a body of armed men: Proto-Germanic *harjaz. Its reflexes — Gothic harjis, Old English here, Old Norse herr, German Heer — all still mean "army, host" today. It is from this Germanic stock, not the Greek, that the first syllable of our English word descends, and the deep military sense it carried would cling to the word's history for centuries.

Root two: the rule. The second, simultaneous current of the word's bloodline is the root *wal-, "to be strong, to rule." In Germanic this produced *waldaną "to rule, govern", whose reflexes give us the English verb wield (to handle with power), the noun wealth (the prosperity that flows from power and means), the Old Norse valda, and the German walten. In Italic the same root yielded Latin valēre "to be strong", the ancestor of valiant, valour, prevail, and valid.

The fusion in the Frankish camp. Now comes the crucial event in the whole history: somewhere in the West Germanic world — most probably in Frankish, the tongue of the Carolingian war-bands — the two roots were fused into the military command-compound *harja-wald-, literally "army-ruler." The same compound produced the personal name Harold (Old English Hereweald, Old Norse Haraldr, as in Harald Hardrada): a man named "army-ruler" was named for the very office from which the herald later took his title. The first element then eroded as the word crossed into Romance speech. In Old French the compound *hariwald became heraut, hiraut, heralt, first attested in the twelfth century — the heraut, the officer who announced war, cried a tournament, and served as the messenger and master of ceremonies of a prince. The original sense "ruler of the host" had faded into the specialized court office of the man who, on behalf of the ruler, proclaimed and announced.

From officer to science. The great semantic migration of the word happened in the later Middle Ages, when the institution the herald served grew into a science. Between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries, as closed helmets hid faces on the battlefield, the painted shield became the bearer's identity; the herald, who already attended tournaments and read the arms displayed there, became the expert who could identify a knight by his blazon, declare his lineage, and judge the propriety of his arms. English took the word in the fourteenth century as heraud, herald, and by the sixteenth century the officer's title had bred the noun for his entire discipline: heraldry, attested from around the 1560s–1570s, built from herald plus the suffix -ry (modelled on Old French -erie), meaning "the science, art, and legal record of armorial bearings."

The sixteenth century institutionalises the word. The age of the Tudors made heraldry an English legal discipline. The College of Arms was incorporated in 1484, and the great Tudor visitations sent heralds county by county to record the arms, pedigrees, and honours of the gentry — "heraldry" thus came to mean not only the art of blazonry but the whole apparatus of official record-keeping, genealogy, and the adjudication of who had the right to bear which arms. Blazon fixed an exact descriptive language (the "Azure, a lion rampant Or" grammar that survives unchanged today), and heraldry read as the written record of lineage, marriage alliances, and honours.

The modern drift. In the nineteenth century the Romantic-medievalist revival flooded the word with chivalric glamour: heraldry was re-read as the poetry of noble lineage and national pageantry rather than mere legal record. Today the word keeps three lives: the institutional science still administered by the College of Arms and the Court of the Lord Lyon; the ceremonial pageantry of royal and civic occasions; and a lively figurative sense — "the heraldry of a brand or cause" — in which any set of badges and emblems announcing an identity is called heraldry. Every one of these lives descends, through heraut and harjawald, from the two ancient roots of the war-band and of rule: the officer who once ruled the host is now the guardian of the emblems by which every host of men still declares who it is.
