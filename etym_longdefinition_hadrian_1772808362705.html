In Gibbon's catalogue of the happy period, Hadrian is the third of five names — "Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, and the two Antonines" — whose "virtue and abilities" conducted the public administration for more than fourscore years. The name is used synecdochically: Hadrian appears not as a biographical figure but as a unit of proof that competent personal rule can stabilize an empire. He is the consolidator after Trajan's expansion, the traveler-emperor and border-builder whose reign (AD 117-138) Gibbon folds into the rare stretch of prosperity before the decline narrative begins. The tone is panegyric yet analytical; the proper name functions as an item of historical periodization. Core reference. Hadrian (Publius Aelius Hadrianus) was Roman emperor from AD 117 to 138, successor and adopted heir of Trajan, and the builder of the frontier works — most famously the wall across northern Britain — and of the Pantheon's famous domed rebuilding and the villa at Tivoli. Polysemy and modern senses. "Hadrian" is a proper name, so its "senses" are its distinct referents and uses:
  • The emperor Publius Aelius Hadrianus (AD 117-138): the referent in Gibbon's list.
  • Hadrian's Wall: the Roman frontier fortification across northern England (begun c. AD 122), now a UNESCO World Heritage Site; the fixed archaeological monument that keeps the emperor's name in everyday geography.
  • Hadrian's Villa: the vast imperial villa complex at Tivoli (Villa Adriana), a World Heritage Site and key monument of imperial architecture.
  • Hadrianic (adjective): of the period, style, or policies of Hadrian — "Hadrianic architecture", "Hadrianic frontier policy".
  • The town Hadria (also Adria): the Italian place-name from which the family derived its name; the same town gives the Adriatic Sea its name.
  • Adriatic: the sea named from Hadria/Adria — a geographic sister derivation of the same toponym, not a separate root.
  • Papal and Christian usage: the name survives as "Adrian" or "Hadrian" among popes (e.g., Pope Adrian IV, the only English pope, 1154-1159) and as a given name in modern Europe.
  • Given-name forms: Adrian (English/German), Adriano (Italian/Spanish/Portuguese), Hadrien/Adrien (French) — vernacular continuations of the Latin cognomen.
  • Antinous and the classics: Hadrian's reign is associated with the cult of Antinous, with Greek cultural patronage, and with the rebuilding of the Pantheon in Rome — all standard referents in art history.
Historical bridge. The bridge from person to monument to period is straightforward toponymy plus reputation: the family name came from the town Hadria; the emperor's fame attached to the wall, the villa, and the age; the adjective "Hadrianic" and the phrase "Age of Hadrian" made a man into a period. The name itself never meant anything like "consolidator" — that content is historical, not etymological. Register and collocation. In scholarly English the name collocates with "Wall", "villa", "reign", "frontier", "Hadrianic" (adjective), and the full formal style "Publius Aelius Hadrianus". Register is formal-historical; the name is almost never a common noun. Modern popular history says "Hadrian" or "Adrian", while the full tria nomina is reserved for technical prose.
