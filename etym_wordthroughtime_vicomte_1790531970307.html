- Early Middle Ages (c. 6th–9th c.) - Evolved in Carolingian administration as a Latin compound title (vicecomes) denoting an administrative deputy or lieutenant acting on behalf of a territorial count (comes); perceived as a functional bureaucratic office rather than a hereditary peerage.
- High & Late Middle Ages (c. 11th–14th c.) - Transformed in feudal France and Normandy into a hereditary noble title (Old French visconte / viscomte) with territorial fiefdoms (viscounties), standing between a baron and a count.
- Early Modern Period & Ancien Régime (16th–18th c.) - Formalized within the rigid aristocratic hierarchy of the French court and peerage; designated scions of prestigious noble lineages, often younger sons of dukes and marquises or holders of ancient ancestral estates.
- French Revolution & Napoleonic Era (1789–1815) - Stripped of feudal privileges by the National Assembly in 1789; associated prominently with royalist émigrés who fled to London, Vienna, and St. Petersburg to preserve their noble pedigree and agitate for the Bourbon Restoration.
- 19th c.–Present - Preserved in English literature and historical scholarship in its distinct French orthography (vicomte) to evoke specifically French aristocratic culture, salon society (e.g., in Balzac, Dumas, and Tolstoy), and Ancien Régime heritage, distinguished from the Anglicized peerage title viscount.
