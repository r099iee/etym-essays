THIEVING = thieve + -ing (present participle / gerund). Modern thieve is a back-formation from thief; the older Old English verb and its participle run in parallel. Chain:
1. PIE *teup- "to crouch, press together, hide, sneak" (reconstructed) — c. 4000 BCE; the sense of crouching and hiding that underlies the thief. One rival reconstruction links the thief-word to *teub(h)- "to steal". (Debated — see note.)
2. Proto-Germanic *theubaz "thief, robber" (from *teup-) — 1st millennium BCE; reflected in Gothic þiufs, Old Norse þjófr, Old High German thiob.
3. Old English þēof "thief" (glosses Latin fur) — 7th–11th centuries; beside it the native weak verb þēofian "to thieve" and its noun þēofung "thieving, theft".
4. Middle English theef / thef "thief" — 12th–15th centuries (Chaucer); the participle thieving (theving/thefing) is recorded from the 14th century as both gerund ("the activity of stealing") and adjective ("inclined to steal").
5. Early Modern English thief (long -ee-) — 16th century onward.
6. Modern English thieve (v.) "to steal" — c. 1650s, a back-formation re-created directly from the noun thief after the Old English verb had died out.
7. thieving — present participle and gerund of thieve, Modern English, both as noun ("thieving is a crime") and adjective ("a thieving magpie").
8. thieving "stealing, filching; inclined to steal" — Modern English.
Note on debate: the PIE root behind Proto-Germanic *theubaz is disputed. Pokorny connects the word to *teup- "to crouch, duck, hide", making the thief etymologically "the skulker"; a rival view posits *teub(h)- "to steal" directly. The Germanic form itself is secure; only its Indo-European ancestry is uncertain.
