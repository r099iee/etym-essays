- [Mid-18th century: fresh French loanword] - English borrows the word directly from French (the past participle of rechercher, “to seek out”), keeping its spelling and nasal accent; it first denotes something deliberately sought out and therefore choice — a term of taste, not yet of censure.
- [18th–19th century: the connoisseur’s term] - In the vocabulary of refinement, fashion, and literary criticism it comes to mean “rare, exquisite, carefully chosen,” applied to subjects, allusions, dishes, and styles prized precisely because they stand out of the common way; its register is elegant and deliberately cultivated.
- [19th–20th century: the flavour turns ironic] - English speakers begin to taste affectation in it: “recherché” increasingly describes what is obscure and exotic for its own sake, and in criticism it is deployed with a mocking, satirical edge — learning paraded as ornament. The loanword’s very foreignness, still printed in italics, becomes the emblem of the pretentiousness it names.
- [Contemporary sense] - A high-register loanword of criticism and connoisseurship, praising a refined, out-of-the-way quality while hinting at exclusivity and pretension; it can admire genuine erudition and mock display in the same breath.
