In Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, from which this line is drawn, 'convulsions' is applied to the natural rather than the bodily world: the historian consoles himself with the reflection that mankind 'has much more to fear from the passions of his fellow-creatures than from the convulsions of the elements.' The word performs its effect through a deliberate and striking metaphor. A convulsion is properly a violent, involuntary, uncontrollable spasm of the muscles; Gibbon lends that medical image to earthquakes, storms, and volcanic tremors, depicting the earth itself as a living frame seized by a paroxysm it cannot command. The tone is one of high Augustan sublimity — grave, measured, Latinized. The sentence is built on a formal antithesis: human 'passions' (internal, moral, willed, and thus all the more culpable) are set against elemental 'convulsions' (external, physical, blind, and therefore blameless), and the paradox is that the smaller, man-made force is by far the more dangerous. The word thus carries a chilling irony: the same disorder that wracks a sick body can wrack a whole planet, yet humanity remains its own deadliest threat. CORE SENSE AND DEFINITION:
  • Primary sense (medical): a sudden, violent, involuntary, irregular contraction or spasm of the muscles, often repeated in a paroxysm — the classic symptoms of epilepsy, tetanus, and feverish seizure. Used chiefly in the plural: 'the patient suffered convulsions.'
  • Figurative sense (social/political): a violent upheaval or disturbance in a society, state, or institution — 'the convulsions of revolution,' 'political convulsions.'
  • Figurative sense (natural): a violent shaking or disturbance of the earth or elements — 'the convulsions of nature,' 'convulsions of the elements' (as in Gibbon: earthquakes, storms, volcanic tremors).
  • Figurative sense (emotional): uncontrollable spasms of laughter or weeping — 'convulsions of laughter,' 'convulsed with grief.'
  • Register: medical, then grandly literary; the plural form is far more common than the singular, because the phenomenon characteristically comes in bursts.
FULL POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Medical/clinical sense: violent muscular spasms of the body, whether generalized (as in an epileptic grand-mal fit) or localized. In this sense convulsion is strictly a bodily event, involuntary and pathological, and it belongs to the vocabulary of physicians from antiquity onward.
  • Natural/physical sense (18th century, the age of the sublime): convulsions of the earth, of the elements, of nature — the violent shaking of earthquakes, the eruption of volcanoes, the upheaval of storms. This is a poetic and scientific extension of the bodily image to the planet, and it flourished in the prose of Gibbon, in geological writing, and in the poetry of the sublime. The earth is treated as a body seized by an uncontrollable fit.
  • Social and political sense: convulsions of society, of empire, of the state — sudden, violent, uncontrollable upheavals in the political or social order, as revolutions, civil wars, and coups are described as 'convulsions.' This sense developed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and remains current in political writing.
  • Emotional sense: convulsions of laughter, of sobbing, of rage — violent, brief, uncontrollable bursts of feeling that seize the body. This is a controlled figurative use, usually comic or pathetic, and is common from the eighteenth century onward.
  • Economic/market sense (modern): convulsions in markets, currencies, and economies — sudden violent swings and turmoil. A late, twentieth-century extension of the political sense into finance.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE (how the senses connect): The whole family of senses radiates from the Latin verb convellere, 'to wrench, tear to pieces, shake violently,' and its participle convulsus, 'violently wrenched, torn, shaken.' The medical sense is the oldest: a convulsio is literally the body's parts 'wrenched together' in a cramp. From medicine the word was carried into the description of nature (the earth 'convulsed' as a body is convulsed) in the eighteenth century, and into the description of society (the state 'convulsed' as a body is convulsed) in the same period; the political and natural senses are parallel metaphors from the same bodily source. The emotional sense transfers the spasm to the involuntary movements of laughter and weeping. All senses share the core idea: a sudden, violent, involuntary, uncontrollable movement or disturbance, whether of muscle, earth, state, or feeling. CONTEXTUAL READING (Gibbon's sentence in full critical detail): Gibbon's sentence closes a reflection on the terrible earthquakes that shook the cities of the eastern Roman Empire. The historian 'may content himself with the observation... that man has much more to fear from the passions of his fellow-creatures than from the convulsions of the elements.' The antithesis is the whole point. The passions — ambition, envy, cruelty, fanaticism — are internal, moral, willed, and thus culpable; the convulsions of the elements — earthquakes, storms — are external, physical, blind, and therefore blameless. And yet the smaller, human force is by far the more dangerous: a thousand earthquakes could not destroy what a single war, a single persecution, a single tyrant can destroy. The word convulsions carries the metaphor of the earth as a living body seized by an uncontrollable fit — a sublime, shuddering image — precisely so that the contrast with the cold, deliberate, infinitely more destructive passions of men can register. The sentence is a masterpiece of Augustan irony: it grants the terrors of nature their full grandeur while dismissing them as trivial beside the man-made terrors of history. COLLOCATION AND SYNTACTIC BEHAVIOUR:
  • Overwhelmingly plural: 'convulsions' (the singular 'convulsion' exists but is rarer and more clinical).
  • Medical collocations: 'to suffer (from) convulsions,' 'convulsions of the muscles,' 'a fit of convulsions,' 'epileptic convulsions.'
  • Natural collocations: 'convulsions of the elements,' 'convulsions of nature,' 'convulsions of the earth.'
  • Political collocations: 'convulsions of the state/empire/society,' 'political convulsions,' 'convulsions of revolution.'
  • Emotional collocations: 'convulsions of laughter,' 'convulsions of sobbing/grief/rage,' 'convulsed with laughter.'
  • The related verb 'to convulse' (from Latin convellere via the participle) is transitive — 'an earthquake convulsed the city' — and the adjective 'convulsive' describes violent, jerky, uncontrollable movement.
RELATION TO THE REST OF THE WORD FAMILY: Convulsions comes from Latin convulsiō, 'a cramp, violent spasm,' the noun of action from convellere, 'to wrench, tear to pieces, shake violently' (con-, 'together, thoroughly, with force' + vellere, 'to pluck, pull, tear'). The same Latin verb family has given English avulsion ('a tearing away,' from āvellere), evulsion ('a plucking out,' from ēvellere), and revulsion ('a pulling back; a violent repugnance,' from revellere), as well as the adjective convulsive and the verb convulse. The ultimate PIE ancestor is generally taken to be *uelh₁-, 'to turn, wind, roll,' with the Latin base reconstructed as *uels-, but this deepest connection is debated among handbooks (see de Vaan; the identification of vellere's PIE ancestry is probable rather than proven). The Romance languages all continue the noun — Italian convulsione, Spanish convulsión, Portuguese convulsão — and the word entered English through Old French convulsion in the fourteenth century.
