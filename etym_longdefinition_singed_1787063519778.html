Singed is the past tense and past participle of the verb “singe”: to burn something superficially, scorching the surface, the tips, or the edges without consuming or destroying it. A singed object is damaged by heat but recognisably intact — the outer layer is burnt, blackened, or shrivelled while the interior survives. In the source context, the hair “had all been singed and frizzled right down to the skin”: the dragon's flame has burnt away the outer hair, curling and scorching it to the scalp.

Primary sense — a light surface burn. To singe is to burn the outer layer of something: the tips of hair, the edges of cloth, the feathers of a bird, the bristles of an animal. The word describes the least destructive kind of burning — enough to scorch, blacken, and shrivel, but not enough to set alight or destroy. A singed eyebrow, a singed hem, singed fur — each shows a quick, local, recoverable damage.

Practical and technical uses.

  • Preparing animals and birds for cooking: singeing removes the hair or pinfeathers from a carcass by passing a flame over it; the singed matter is then scraped away.
  • In tailoring and textiles: singeing fabric removes loose fibres from the surface, leaving a smooth finish; a flame is run over the cloth.
  • In candle- and wick-care (historical): trimming and singeing wicks to remove charred ends.
  • In medicine (historical): a light cauterising singe to seal a wound or treat growths.
  • In gardening and agriculture: plants “singed” by frost or scorching sun show burned, withered leaf tips and edges.

Figurative senses.

  • Singed by experience: to be superficially damaged or chastened by misfortune, criticism, or scandal — singed rather than burned, harmed but not destroyed.
  • The myth of Icarus: “singed wings” is a permanent idiom for over-ambition punished — the wings of wax were singed (melted) when Icarus flew too close to the sun.
  • Emotional and social heat: a reputation singed by gossip, pride singed by a snub — the connotation is of minor but painful scorching.
  • In descriptions of fire damage: singed hair and clothing recur in accident reports, fiction, and testimony as the classic signs of a near-miss with flame.

Degree and nuance. “Singed” sits at the mild end of the burn vocabulary. “Scorched” implies drier, more discoloured damage by dry heat or flame; “charred” implies blackened, carbonised destruction; “burned” is the general word for any degree; “seared” is a deliberate, brief application of intense heat (culinary or cauterising). “Singed” uniquely suggests the mere touching of flame — the quick hiss, the curled hair-end — and this is exactly the sense in the Tolkien passage, where the dwarves must “doctor his scorches” while his singed hair slowly grows back.

Register. The word is concrete, homely, and vivid, at home in cookery, craft, accident, and animal care. In figurative use it lends a physical, tactile sharpness to descriptions of minor hurt. As a participle, “singed” functions as an adjective (a singed lock of hair, singed edges).
