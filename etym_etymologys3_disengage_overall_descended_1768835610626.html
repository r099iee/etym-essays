disengage - the biography of the broken pledge. The PIE root *wadh- signified "to bind, to pledge" - an old juridical verb for the tying of a person's word to a promise, one of the family's deep legal roots. In Latin it produced vās, vādis, the surety who stands bail for another, the living bond. In the Germanic world the same root gave *wadjō, the pledge itself: Gothic wadi (earnest money), Old English wedd (pledge, covenant) - whence English wed, the marriage vow as the supreme pledge, the union contracted by word-binding. The Frankish form *wadja travelled into Gallo-Romance as gage, "pledge, security, pawn": the thing deposited as guarantee. From it Old French built engagier, "to bind by a pledge, to pawn" - to give oneself or one's property as security - which English took in the sixteenth century as engage: to bind by promise, to enter a contract, to join battle, to become betrothed. Each of those senses preserves the original drama of the pledge: the soldier "engaged" the enemy because battle was a mutual binding; the couple became "engaged" because marriage was a mutual pledge. The negation dis- ("apart, away") then reverses the whole drama: disengage is the un-binding, the withdrawal of the pledge - a word of retreat, of broken contact, of machinery thrown out of gear. The military sense (break off contact with an enemy) and the mechanical sense (throw a gear out of engagement) both descend from the same image: the disengaging of two things that were locked together by a commitment. In Peake's sentence the grandeur has shrunk to its minimum: Rottcodd disengages a jacket. But the ghost of ceremony remains - the ritual unbinding of the social self before the donning of the institutional overall, the pledge-economy of a whole civilization reduced to the unfastening of buttons. overall - the biography of totality, in two threads. The compound is built from two independent PIE roots. The first, *uper, "over, above, beyond", is one of the most widespread roots in Indo-European: Sanskrit upari, Greek hupér (our hyper-), Latin super, and in Germanic *uber, whence Old English ofer and modern over - the preposition of transcendence, of covering, of authority. The second, *al- or *h2el-, "beyond" (with the Germanic "all" words commonly, though not unanimously, traced to it), gave Proto-Germanic *allaz, "all, whole, entire", Old English eall, and modern all. The compound over all - "over everything, covering everything" - was natural English from the beginning: Old English ofer eall is attested in the sense "in every place, throughout". Through the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries overall served as adverb and adjective meaning "over the whole extent, taken as a whole" - an eye passing over a page, a total sum, a general impression. The seventeenth century consolidated the summarizing adverb; "overall length", "overall impression" are measurements and judgments of the whole. Then, in 1759, the noun overalls appears: heavy protective trousers with a bib and braces, the working man's garment - a thing worn "over all" the other clothes. The British singular "an overall" - the one-piece protective suit that covers the whole body - follows in the twentieth century, the garment of the workshop, the laboratory, the dustman's hall. The noun stays humble, utilitarian, protective; the adverb is abstracted into blanket summary ("overall, the day was quiet"). The wholeness of a garment has grown into the wholeness of a judgment - and Peake's "long grey overall", drawn over the head, sits at the meeting point: the envelope garment and the envelope summary, the whole of Rottcodd's life reduced to a shapeless covering. descended - the biography of climbing performed in reverse. The word reaches back to the PIE root *skend-, "to jump, leap, spring" - a verb of sudden upward motion, of the body springing from the ground. Its reflexes confirm the leap-oriented core: Sanskrit skándati, "he leaps, jumps"; Greek skándalon, "stumbling-block, snare" - literally the trap that makes one spring - the ancestor of English scandal, the leap of moral offense. Latin received the root as scandere, "to climb" - and here the etymology records a beautiful re-reading: the sudden spring of the athlete becomes the sustained climb of the mountaineer, the one-off leap re-imagined as a graduated ascent (the exact formation of scandere from the root is debated, but the semantic drift from "spring" to "climb" is transparent). From scandere Latin built a ladder of compounds: ascendere "to climb up", descendere "to climb down" (de- "down" + scandere), with the past participle descensus giving the noun "a going down". Old French descendre carried the verb into Romance, and Middle English descenden took it into English in the thirteenth century. The original meaning is preserved with remarkable fidelity: to descend is to climb in reverse, a step-by-step downward movement, not a fall. From that physical core the word radiated its later senses: a garment that "descended shapelessly to his ankles" moves down by the same graduated logic as a mountain path; a family "descended from" an ancestor is a line that has come down through generations, each step a generation; property, customs, and words are "descended" from their sources. The physical and the genealogical share the image of a graded ladder, and the word's dignity - even when applied to grey cloth - is the dignity of the stair, the controlled stepwise descent of a thing that never lets go of the climb it reverses.
