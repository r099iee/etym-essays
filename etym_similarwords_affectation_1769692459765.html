• Pretension: Similar because both assert an undeserved quality or status; differs because pretension is the claim or ambition itself (literary pretension = the aspiration), while affectation is the visible stylized behavior staged to support that claim.
  • Hypocrisy: Similar because both involve a gap between seeming and being; differs because hypocrisy deliberately conceals vice behind a claimed virtue and is a moral offense, while affectation may merely ornament behavior without claiming a virtue the person lacks.
  • Ostentation: Similar because both involve display designed to impress; differs because ostentation flaunts wealth, possessions, or magnificence openly, whereas affectation stages the self - voice, manners, and studied feelings.
  • Insincerity: Similar because both are the absence of genuine feeling; differs because insincerity is the broad term for dishonesty of expression (any lie of tone), while affectation specifically names the artificial mannerisms and speech that perform sincerity.
  • Artificiality: Similar because both mean constructed rather than natural; differs because artificiality is a neutral-to-negative descriptive quality of anything man-made or contrived, while affectation implies deliberate self-presentation designed to impress.
  • Posturing: Similar because both mean adopting an attitude for effect; differs because posturing is typically political or attitudinal (striking a stance) and often implies empty bombast, while affectation covers mannerism and speech in daily social life.
  • Posing: Similar because both mean assuming a false appearance; differs because posing is usually a temporary, single action (posing as an expert), while affectation is a habitual, woven-through style of being.
  • Pretense (pretence): Similar because both involve a false appearance; differs because pretense is the false appearance or claim itself (under pretense of friendship), while affectation is the manner in which the false appearance is enacted.
  • Mannerism: Similar because both concern studied behavior; differs because mannerism is a neutral stylistic habit (in art, a recognizable personal tic), while affectation always implies striving for impression.
  • Theatricality: Similar because both suggest performance before an audience; differs because theatricality is broader and can be positive (dramatic flair), while affectation is always artificial display viewed critically.
  • Airs: Similar because both name assumed superior manners (putting on airs); differs because airs are specifically about assumed superiority and social rank, while affectation ranges over taste, feeling, and speech.
