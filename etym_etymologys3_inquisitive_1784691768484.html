Inquisitive descends from the Latin verb inquirere — a compound of the preposition in- "into" and quaerere "to seek, ask, inquire" — and so literally means "to seek into". The deeper Indo-European background of quaerere is famously obscure: it has no certain Italic cognates, and the ancients themselves were unsure.

  • The debated deep root. The older scholarship connected quaerere with the pronominal root *kwo- "who? what?", the idea being that to quaerere is to ask "who?" or "what?" of the world; that stem has genuine and wide reflexes, among them Sanskrit kaḥ "who", Hindi kaun, Odia kie, Greek poû "where?", Latin quī, and our own who, what, and why. Alongside it, the related root *kwei- "to pay, value, seek" produced Greek poinē "blood-price, penalty" and, through it, Latin poena, the ancestor of English pain and penalty. Whatever the truth of the deep connection, the Latin verb itself is solid ground: no scholar doubts that inquirere means "to seek into", and no scholar doubts the line from inquirere to our modern adjective.
  • From Latin to English. From inquirere came the participle stem inquisit-, and from that, in Late Latin, the adjective inquisitivus "given to questioning". The word then travelled into English by the usual medieval route — Old French inquisitif into Middle English inquisityf, recorded from the late fourteenth century, already with the double charge the word still carries: eager learning on the one hand, prying curiosity on the other.
  • The double semantic charge. In the centuries that followed, the two senses flourished side by side: the positive use belongs to science and letters (an inquisitive mind), the censuring use to society (inquisitive neighbours), and both derive from the same root image of actively searching into things — a searching that is virtuous when the thing sought is knowledge and impertinent when it is other people's business.
  • The family of inquirere. The family grew large: Latin inquisitio "search, inquest" gave the Inquisition and the English inquisition, inquest, inquiry, and enquiry; the sibling compounds of quaerere produced exquisite (from exquirere, "to search out"), acquire (from acquirere), require (from requirere), and the nouns question and quest (from quaestio). The spelling split between inquire/inquiry (Latin route) and enquire/enquiry (Old French route) runs through the whole family and persists in modern British English as a stylistic choice.
  • Socio-cultural career. Inquisitive has always occupied the middle ground between praise and blame. The scientific revolution made an "inquisitive mind" an intellectual virtue — the curiosity that drives experiment and discovery; but the institutional memory of the Inquisition kept the word's darker edge alive, so that inquisitive eyes and inquisitive neighbours remain intrusions. The word's register is neutral enough for both: it is the adjective that can praise a child's curiosity and condemn a reporter's prying in the same breath.
  • Literary attestations. In the passage from "Roast Mutton", one of the dwarves warns the company: "the less inquisitive you are as you go along, the less trouble you are likely to find." The word here carries its practical, cautionary nuance: in lands that are "none too well known" and "too near the mountains", where travellers seldom come, the roads are unguarded, and the local king is unheard of, prying curiosity is not a virtue but a liability. Asking questions advertises that you are a stranger, invites suspicion, and stirs up trouble of exactly the kind a small, poorly armed party cannot afford. The warning belongs to the novel's folk-wisdom tone, and it gives the adjective an almost economic force: curiosity is a commodity that costs trouble.

Inquisitive, then, is a word whose Latin pedigree is certain, whose Indo-European ancestry is honestly uncertain, and whose meaning has been stable in its doubleness for over six centuries: the search into things, whether that search earns praise as learning or blame as prying, depends entirely on what is being searched and why.
