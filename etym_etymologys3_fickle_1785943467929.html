The history of FICKLE is one of the most instructive in English: a word that began as a harsh accusation of deliberate treachery has softened, over a thousand years, into a mild, almost affectionate complaint about changeability. To use the modern word about a lover, a crowd, or the weather is to carry, without knowing it, the ghost of an Old English word for a scheming traitor. The story shows how moral vocabulary wears down with use — how a culture's deepest fears about betrayal slowly dissolve into its everyday shrugs about human nature.

The Indo-European and Germanic foundation:

  • At the deepest recoverable level lies the Proto-Indo-European root *peig-/*peik-, "to mark, colour, paint, cut". This is the root of Latin pingere "to paint" (whence English picture and pigment), of Greek poikílos "variegated, painted" — and, crucially for our word, of Greek poikílos in its secondary sense "wily, crafty, cunning". The semantic link that scholars defend runs through the idea of variegation and disguise: something painted or spotted is no longer plain and trustworthy; it is covered, decorated, concealed. The painted thing and the deceiver share the quality of not being what they first appear to be.
  • It must be said at once that this Indo-European stage is a reconstruction and is debated. What is certain is the Germanic stage: Proto-Germanic *faik-/*fik-, "deceit, treachery, hostility", attested across the family in Old Norse feikn "peril, portent, evil omen", Old Saxon fēknea "deceit", Old High German feihan "to deceive", and — in English — Old English fācen "deceit, fraud, treachery". The Germanic world, like the Homeric, prized plain-dealing and feared the man whose word could not be trusted; the root is everywhere hostile in colouring.

Old English: the treacherous ficol:

  • Old English formed the adjective ficol "deceitful, cunning, crafty, treacherous" from the base *fik- with the suffix -ol, which marks an inherent disposition or propensity. Alongside it stood the noun ficolung "deceit, treachery" and the verb befician "to deceive, beguile". In Anglo-Saxon poetry, homiletic prose, and legal texts, ficol describes the false friend, the plotter, the man who speaks fair and stabs behind; the Exeter Book and the Blickling Homilies use the word and its family for the devil's arts and for human hypocrisy. The word in its oldest form is not a mild comment — it is a charge of active, scheming betrayal.
  • The sound of the word in Old English, [fikol], is very close to the modern [fɪkəl]; the form has changed remarkably little in a thousand years. What changed is not the word but its meaning.

Middle English: from treachery to inconstancy:

  • In Middle English the word appears as fikel from the early 13th century, first still meaning "deceitful, treacherous, false". But across the 13th and 14th centuries a decisive softening occurred. The moral vocabulary of medieval romance and satire began to use fikel less of the schemer who plots harm and more of the person whose promises, loyalties, or affections cannot be trusted to last — the friend who cools, the lover who moves on, the supporter who drifts away. The word slid from "actively deceiving" toward "unreliable, inconstant, changeable".
  • This drift is the classic case of semantic bleaching and moral softening. A society does not stop fearing betrayal, but it learns to name a lesser, more common failing with the old strong word; the accusation weakens in proportion to how ordinary the failing becomes. By the 14th century, English writers could use fikel of Fortune herself — the great classical and medieval figure of blind, arbitrary change — and once Fortune, the weather, and popular opinion were described as fickle, the word's centre of gravity had moved decisively from human vice to the general changeability of things.

Early Modern English and the golden age of "fickle Fortune":

  • The 16th century fixed the modern sense and the modern spelling (fickle, with the -le suffix). It also fixed the word's most famous association: the Renaissance personification of Fortune as fickle. Fickle Fortune, the goddess who spins her wheel, raises the low and dashes the high, and abandons her favourites without warning, became a stock figure of Elizabethan poetry, and the adjective rode with it.
  • Shakespeare uses the word with full modern force — most famously of the mob. In 2 Henry IV the crowd is "the fickle, the inconstant, the wavering multitude"; in other plays "fickle Fortune" and "fickle maid" play on the same instability. The word now named not treachery but the fundamental unreliability of human loyalty and worldly luck — a diagnosis rather than an accusation, though one still tinged with blame.

Socio-cultural factors and modern drift:

  • Since the 17th century the word has done what English moral words commonly do: it has expanded its range from persons to anything unstable. Fickle now applies to weather, fashion, markets, voters, consumers, and fans as readily as to lovers and friends — "fickle weather", "fickle public", "fickle consumers", "fickle electorate". In political science and marketing the word has been adopted as a descriptive term for swing voters and brand-infidel customers, drained almost entirely of moral force.
  • The archaic sense "deceitful, treacherous" survives only in dictionaries and in deliberate archaism; the trail from fācen "fraud" to fickle "inconstant" is still visible to the etymologist, and it explains the residual sharpness of the word even now — a fickle friend is still faintly accused of something worse than mere change of mind, because the word's DNA is treachery.
  • Sound change has been minimal: Old English ficol [ˈfikol] → Middle English fikel → Modern English fickle [ˈfɪkəl], with the unstressed final syllable reduced to a schwa. Form has been extraordinarily stable; meaning has travelled the whole width of the moral spectrum. FICKLE is therefore a small masterpiece of linguistic history: one syllable, a thousand years, and a journey from the traitor's den to the weather forecast.
