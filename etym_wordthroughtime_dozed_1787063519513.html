- 1640s–1680s: Emerged in Early Modern English (originally *dase* / *dose*) meaning to make stupid, dizzy, or dull with cold, grief, or drink (a frequentative offshoot of Middle English *dasen*, "to daze").
- 1700s–1780s: Underwent a semantic shift from "stupefied or dizzy" to "slumbering lightly or half-asleep," becoming common in Georgian literature for resting informally.
- 1800s–1900s: Firmly established in Victorian and modern prose as the primary verb for light, fitful, or unintended sleep (e.g., in armchairs, during lectures, or on journeys).
- Present Day: Retains universal usage for shallow micro-sleeps, sitting dozes, and resting in low-vigilance states without entering deep REM sleep.
