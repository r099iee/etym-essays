hauled:
- The sailor's verb (16th c.) - 'haul' enters English as the plain, muscular word of the ship: heaving ropes, nets, and sails with sustained effort
- Ashore with carters and drovers (17th-18th c.) - the word moves to land: hauling timber, coal, and freight over distance; still the verb of weight and strain
- The catch becomes a noun (17th-19th c.) - 'a haul' names what is dragged in at one effort - a catch of fish, a load of loot - and by the 19th c. a 'big haul' means the day's takings, honest or stolen
- Figurative endurance (19th-20th c.) - 'the long haul', 'to haul someone up', 'hauled over the coals': the word keeps its insistence that effort is real and costs something
- Contemporary - the verb of raw, body-committed effort: nothing about hauling is graceful or effortless - the full-length tug of one fighting momentum with nothing but leverage, sinew, and will

reins:
- The rider's paired straps (14th-15th c.) - the leather straps held in the hands to steer, stop, and restrain a horse; always plural, the matched pair treated as one instrument
- Control as the earliest metaphor (15th c.) - ruling is quickly conceived as 'holding the reins': the word transfers from horse to state almost at once
- Idiomatic consolidation (17th-19th c.) - take the reins, hand over the reins, a free rein, to rein in: the word becomes the standard figure for command by direct, hand-held restraint
- Contemporary - literal use is now the property of riding; in speech the word is chiefly the master metaphor for control, and every rein still means an act of holding
