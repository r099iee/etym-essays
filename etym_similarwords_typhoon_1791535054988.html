• Hurricane: Similar because both refer to the exact same intense meteorological phenomenon—a mature tropical cyclone with sustained winds exceeding 74 mph (64 knots) featuring an eye, eyewall, and spiral rainbands; differs because hurricane is the regional geographic designation for cyclones forming in the North Atlantic, central North Pacific, and eastern North Pacific (derived from the Taíno/Arawak storm deity *hurakán*), whereas typhoon is used exclusively in the northwestern Pacific Ocean basin.
  • Cyclone: Similar because both describe rotating atmospheric low-pressure systems characterized by inward spiraling winds; differs because cyclone is the broad overarching scientific classification for all such systems (and the specific regional term used in the South Pacific and Indian Ocean), while typhoon is a regional term for northwestern Pacific storms that have attained severe tropical storm intensity.
  • Tornado: Similar because both are violent, destructive, swirling vortexes of wind accompanied by intense storm conditions; differs because a tornado is a localized, small-scale atmospheric vortex formed beneath a convective storm cloud spanning a few hundred meters with a lifespan of minutes to hours, whereas a typhoon is a massive oceanic weather system spanning hundreds of kilometers that persists for days or weeks.
  • Gale: Similar because both describe severe, high-velocity winds that endanger maritime navigation and coastal infrastructure; differs because a gale is defined by the Beaufort scale as sustained winds between 34 to 47 knots without the circular cyclonic structure, central eye, or massive oceanic storm surge characteristic of a typhoon.
  • Tempest: Similar because both convey the image of a ferocious, turbulent storm threatening ships at sea; differs because tempest is an elevated, archaic or literary term describing any violent commotion of wind and rain, lacking the precise meteorological parameters and tropical genesis of a typhoon.
  • Squall: Similar because both involve sudden, dangerous bursts of violent maritime winds; differs because a squall is a brief, localized wind event lasting only a matter of minutes, usually associated with passing cold fronts or thunderstorms, lacking the immense barometric scale and multi-day duration of a typhoon.
  • Waterspout: Similar because both represent rotating oceanic columns of air and moisture; differs because a waterspout is a narrow vertical funnel cloud over water (often the marine equivalent of a weak tornado), while a typhoon is an expansive, planetary-scale storm system.
