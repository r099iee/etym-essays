Revelations is the plural of revelation: disclosures of something previously hidden, unknown, or secret, typically presented as surprising, illuminating, or momentous. Where the singular often denotes the act or fact of revealing in the abstract, the plural catalogues the items disclosed — the secrets told, the findings published, the truths brought to light one after another. Its core image, inherited from Latin, is that of a veil drawn back so that what was covered can now be seen.

In religious usage it retains its oldest gravity. Capitalised as Revelation or the Book of Revelation, it names the final, apocalyptic book of the New Testament; uncapitalised, it denotes divine disclosure generally — truths believed to be communicated by God rather than discovered by reason, such as, in Christian theology, the Incarnation or the content of prophecy. Theologians still distinguish revealed truth from natural truth, and mystics describe private revelations granted in vision or prayer.

In secular modern prose the word has broadened enormously while keeping its thrill of uncovering. Journalists promise revelations from leaked documents; biographers advertise revelations from diaries; scientists announce revelations from new data. Here the sense is evidential and narrative rather than supernatural: something concealed by secrecy, complexity, or inattention is exposed. Closely related is the personal, psychological sense — a sudden realisation that reorders one’s understanding, as in ‘her trip abroad was a revelation’, where the plural would enumerate several such turning points.

  • Divine disclosure (theology): truth communicated by God to humanity, e.g. scriptural revelation; opposite of discovery by unaided reason.
  • Apocalyptic vision: prophetic unveiling of ultimate things, especially allegorical visions of judgment and renewal after the biblical model.
  • Journalistic scoop: newly published secret information, e.g. ‘the files contained damaging revelations about the campaign’; the dominant contemporary usage, almost always plural.
  • Scientific or scholarly finding: a discovery that overturns prior assumptions, e.g. ‘the telescope yielded startling revelations about distant galaxies’.
  • Personal epiphany: a sudden clarifying insight into one’s life or art, e.g. ‘the exhibition was a revelation to her’; pluralised when several such moments accumulate.
  • Plot disclosure (narrative): in fiction, theatre, or memoir, the successive uncovering of hidden backstory — revelations that reframe everything the audience thought it knew.
  • Legal and official disclosure: facts emerging in testimony, inquiry reports, or declassified records; carries an overtone of accountability, of concealment overcome.

The bridge between sacred and secular senses is the veil metaphor itself. Whether God parts the heavens, a reporter opens sealed archives, or a reader of stylistics suddenly sees play and competition at work in prose, the structure is identical: ignorance figured as covering, knowledge as uncovering, and the emotional charge of astonishment preserved. That is why the plural in the source sentence — extraordinary revelations occurring when motive is viewed tripartitely — feels both scholarly and faintly awestruck: disciplined inquiry is promising the oldest pleasure of the word, the covered shown bare.
