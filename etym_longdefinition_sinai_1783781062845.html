SINAI (proper noun). The biblical mountain in the Sinai Peninsula where, according to Exodus 19–20, God descended in cloud and fire, proclaimed the Ten Commandments, and concluded the covenant with Israel; by extension the name of the surrounding wilderness and, in modern geography, of the entire peninsula between the Mediterranean, the Gulf of Suez, and the Gulf of Aqaba. As a proper noun its polysemy is a growth of reference — from peak to wilderness to peninsula — together with an unbroken train of theological, literary, and cultural associations. Senses and uses:
  • The mountain of the Law (primary biblical sense). Mount Sinai (Hebrew Har Sînay) is the mount of theophany: the fire and cloud, the trumpet, the voice of God, the Ten Commandments, the covenant, and the lawgiving to Moses (Exodus 19–20; Deuteronomy 5). It is the Old Covenant's mountain par excellence.
  • The wilderness of Sinai (geographical-biblical sense). The wilderness or desert of Sinai is the region through which Israel wandered after leaving Egypt (Exodus 16:1; Numbers 33), the scene of murmuring, manna, quails, and the giving of the Law; "the wilderness of Sin" (Exodus 16:1) lies nearby, near the modern name of the peninsula.
  • The Sinai Peninsula (modern geographical sense). Since the 19th century Sinai names the triangular peninsula of Egypt — the land bridge between Africa and Asia — famous for its granite peaks (Jebel Musa, the traditional mount), St. Catherine's Monastery, and its strategic position between the two gulfs.
  • Theological metonym: "the law of Sinai." "Sinai" stands for the Old Covenant, the Mosaic Law, and the dispensation of the commandments, contrasted in Christian theology with the grace of the New Covenant (Galatians 4:24–25, where Paul allegorizes Sinai as the covenant of bondage).
  • Literary symbol of prophetic and biblical inspiration. In Milton's Paradise Lost, "the secret top Of Oreb, or of Sinai" is where the Spirit inspired Moses the lawgiver — the biblical source of true song set against the pagan "Aonian Mount" of the Muses.
  • Etymological candidate senses of the name itself. The Hebrew name Sînay has been variously derived: (1) from the Semitic moon-god Sîn (Mesopotamian lunar deity of Ur and Harran), making Sinai "the mountain of Sîn"; (2) from a root meaning "clay, mud" (Arabic ṭīn); (3) from a root meaning "tooth" (šinn-), describing the serrated, notched granite peaks; (4) folk-etymologically from seneh, the burning bush of Exodus 3, or from Sinai's nearness to the "wilderness of Sin." None is certain; the true etymology is lost, possibly buried in a pre-Hebrew, perhaps Amorite or Egyptian, substratum.
  • Religious geography today. For Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike Sinai remains the mountain of the Law and of the theophany: the site of pilgrimage, monasticism (St. Catherine's), and interfaith reverence.
Historical bridges and reasoning:
  • The ladder of senses runs from sacred peak to wilderness to whole peninsula to symbol: the mountain where God gave the Law became the name of the surrounding desert, then of the entire land bridge, and then the standing figure for lawgiving, covenant, and biblical revelation.
  • Transmission: Hebrew Sînay → Greek Σινᾶ Sina (Septuagint) → Latin Sinai / Sina (Vulgate) → English Sinai (in English since c. 1300 via Vulgate and Anglo-Norman).
  • The pairing with Horeb (the alternative name used chiefly in Exodus 3, 17 and in Deuteronomy, from the Elohist source) makes Sinai and Horeb two names for one holy mountain; Milton's "Oreb, or of Sinai" registers the traditional equation.
Meaning today: Sinai names a mountain, a wilderness, a peninsula, and a symbol — the place of the Law, the covenant, and the dramatic theophany, as familiar in geography and geopolitics as in scripture and poetry.
