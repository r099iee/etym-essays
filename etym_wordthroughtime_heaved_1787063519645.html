- Old English hebban — to lift, raise, exalt — a neutral everyday verb for raising objects, crops, voices, and hands in prayer; the perception is purely physical and devotional.
- Middle English heven — broadens into "to raise the voice in outcry" (heven a cry) while remaining concrete and effort-focused.
- 15th–16th c. — the old strong past hove begins to lose ground to the new weak form heaved; heaved wins in ordinary speech while hove survives as a nautical fossil (hove to, hove in sight) — a register split between land and sea.
- 16th–18th c. — heave acquires "to throw or fling" (heaved a stone) and "to utter with effort" (heave a sigh, heave a groan); the effortful-lifting frame extends to breath and speech.
- 17th–19th c. — nautical jargon multiplies: heave ho!, heave the anchor, heave to — heave becomes the archetypal sailor's verb, and heaved carries the connotation of back-breaking maritime labour.
- 19th–20th c. — bodily and geological senses: the chest heaved with sobs; the ground heaved in the earthquake — heave now denotes involuntary, rhythmic, often distressful rising, losing some of its voluntary-effort colouring.
- 20th c. — slang: to heave = to vomit; to give someone the heave-ho = to dismiss them — the oldest "lift" sense drifts down to the most visceral bodily expulsion, completing the arc from raising up to throwing up.
- Today — heaved covers straining weightlifters, sighing lovers, and swaying ships alike; the shared thread is effort, strain, and mass in motion.
