- Late 14th c. (Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French and Latin to denote the sending of an ecclesiastical envoy or the official authority/commission of a Papal Legate (legatus a latere); perceived as a solemn religious and canonical delegation.
- 16th–17th c. (Early Modern Period) - Broadened to encompass secular diplomatic missions sent by monarchs and sovereign states to conduct treaty negotiations and maintain foreign representation; viewed as a formal state embassy or delegation.
- 18th–19th c. (Classic Diplomatic Era) - Codified in European international law (formalized at the Congress of Vienna, 1815) as a specific rank of diplomatic mission headed by a Minister Plenipotentiary or Envoy Extraordinary, ranking strictly below an Embassy (which was headed by an Ambassador representing the person of a monarch); also designated the territory governed by a papal legate in the Papal States (e.g., the Legations of Romagna).
- Mid-20th c. (Post-WWII / Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961) - Replaced almost universally by full Embassies as the principle of sovereign equality among all independent nation-states eliminated the hierarchical distinction between embassies and legations.
- Contemporary Era - Preserved in international relations for specific non-standard missions (e.g., the Holy See's apostolic delegations or specialized international bodies) and ubiquitously in historical scholarship, literature (e.g., Tolstoy, Henry James), and diplomatic history.
