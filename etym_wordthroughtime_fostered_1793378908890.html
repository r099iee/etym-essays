- 9th c. — native Germanic feeding: in Old English, fōstrian meant literally to provide food, sustenance, and physical provisions to infants, cattle, or dependants.
- 11th–13th c. — aristocratic social kinship: in Anglo-Saxon and Anglo-Norman society, fostering was a formal legal and political alliance where noble children were reared in allied households to cement peace.
- 14th–15th c. — metaphorical growth: Middle English writers began applying fostred to the cultivation of virtues, vices, arts, and spiritual habits (attested in Chaucer and Langland).
- 16th–17th c. — psychological interiority: Shakespeare and Elizabethan dramatists used fostered both for loving patronage and for nurturing internal passions, rebellions, and secret griefs.
- 18th–19th c. — civic and aesthetic cultivation: Enlightenment and Victorian social philosophers like John Ruskin celebrated institutions and masters that fostered public reverence, fine arts, and civic virtue.
- 20th c. — legal institutionalization: the rise of modern child welfare formalized foster care as a legal status distinct from adoption, while general prose preserved its abstract sense of nurturing innovation.
- Contemporary — corporate and cultural ubiquity: universally used in organizational and pedagogical language (fostering collaboration, fostering inclusion), retaining deep positive resonance.
