Bedewing is a small, old, and now faintly archaic word, but its ancestry is deep. It joins the Old English prefix be- to dew, a noun inherited from Proto-Germanic and ultimately from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning “to flow, run.” The history of the word is thus a history of two inherited elements and of a literary register that slowly narrowed.

The root *dheu- and the Germanic noun

Proto-Indo-European *dheu- is reconstructed with the sense “to flow, run,” and it underlies a wide range of descendants, including Sanskrit dhavate “flows, runs.” In Proto-Germanic the root produced *dawwaz, “dew,” which passed into Old English as dēaw. Cognates are widespread: Old Saxon dau, Old Frisian daw, Middle Dutch dau, Old High German tou, German Tau, and Old Norse dögg. The Germanic family thus preserves the noun with remarkable consistency.

  • *dheu- “to flow, run” — the reconstructed PIE root.
  • *dawwaz “dew” — the Proto-Germanic noun.
  • dēaw “dew” — the Old English reflex.
  • German Tau, Old Norse dögg — cognate evidence for the same noun.

The prefix be-

The prefix be- is one of the oldest productive prefixes in English, inherited from Germanic. It can mean “around, about” (as in besiege), can make an intransitive verb transitive (as in befall), or can add an intensive or completive force (as in bedazzle). In bedew it makes a transitive verb of the noun: to put dew on something, to cover it with dew. The same pattern gives besprinkle, besmear, and bedight.

Old English and Middle English

Old English had bedēawian, glossed “to bedew,” a formation exactly parallel to the modern verb. It belongs to the large Old English vocabulary of weather, agriculture, and devotional poetry. Middle English bedewen continues it, and the word appears in religious and love poetry of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, often in images of dawn, tears, and grace. The Wycliffite Bible and later devotional prose use it of spiritual refreshment.

  • Old English bedēawian: the earliest attested form.
  • Middle English bedewen: continued in poetry and devotion.
  • Semantic range: literal dew, tears, and figurative grace.

Early modern literary flourishing

The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were the verb’s heyday in elevated prose and verse. Poets bedewed brows, cheeks, altars, and flowers; preachers spoke of the soul bedewed by grace. The word suited the period’s taste for soft, liquid imagery and for Latinate or archaic diction. Shakespeare, Spenser, and the metaphysical poets use the broader vocabulary of dew and bedewing to figure freshness, grief, and transient beauty.

18th and 19th centuries: archaism and parody

By the eighteenth century bedew was already marked as poetic. It survives in hymns and in the deliberately archaic diction of Romantic verse, but prose writers increasingly preferred moisten, sprinkle, and wet. In the nineteenth century the word became available for parody: an author could use bedewing to sound quaintly elevated, especially about sweat or tears. This is precisely the effect in the comic passage, where “the persp., already bedewing my brow” mixes clinical abbreviation with romantic verb.

Phonology and morphology

The pronunciation is /bɪˈdjuː/ for the verb and /bɪˈdjuːɪŋ/ for the participle. The vowel of dew is /juː/ in Received Pronunciation, reflecting the Middle English diphthong that developed from Old English ēaw. The spelling bedewing drops no letters and follows the regular pattern of adding -ing to a verb ending in a consonant. There is no irregular sound change; the word’s interest is lexical and stylistic.

Modern survival

In the twenty-first century bedew is rare outside poetry, historical fiction, hymns, and humorous writing. Dictionaries label it literary or archaic. Its survival is a good example of how a word can persist not because it is needed — moisten does the job — but because it carries a distinctive tone that no synonym quite replaces.

  • Literary: “cheeks bedewed with tears.”
  • Humorous-pompous: “persp. bedewing my brow.”
  • Religious: “bedewed with grace.”
  • Botanical: “the bedewing mist.”
