LINEAR CHAIN (single root word FICKLE — a native Germanic adjective, Old English ficol) Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *peig-/*peik- "to mark, colour, paint, cut" (c. 4000 BCE); a root of marking and variegated colouring, securely attested in Latin pingere "to paint", Greek poikílos "variegated, painted, crafty", and Sanskrit piśá- "decorated, spotted". Some scholars also derive the "deceitful" sense from the semantic extension "spotted, variegated → disguised → deceptive". Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *faik-/*fik- "deceit, treachery; crafty, hostile" (roughly 500 BCE–200 CE); the Germanic branch narrowed the root to the sense of treachery and hostility. Attested cognates include Old Norse feikn "peril, portent, evil omen", Old Saxon fēknea "deceit", and Old High German feihan "to deceive". The noun Old English fācen "deceit, fraud, treachery" is the clearest Germanic reflex. Stage 3 — Old English ficol "deceitful, cunning, crafty, treacherous" (attested in Anglo-Saxon texts from the 9th–11th centuries); formed from the Germanic base *fik- with the adjectival suffix -ol, which marks a characteristic disposition or propensity (compare similar formations). Related Old English verbs include befician "to deceive" and the noun ficolung "deceit, treachery". Stage 4 — Middle English fikel "deceitful, treacherous" (recorded from the early 13th century), then increasingly "changeable, inconstant, unstable in loyalty or affection" (from the 14th century); the semantic softening from active treachery to mere unreliability took place within Middle English, documented in romance, homiletic, and satirical texts. Stage 5 — Early Modern English fickle "inconstant, changeable, not steadfast" (established by the 16th century, and made famous in the Renaissance personification of "fickle Fortune" and Shakespeare's "the fickle crowd" in 2 Henry IV and elsewhere); the modern spelling fickle stabilised with the loss of the Old English final -ol to -le. Final node — Modern English FICKLE "changing frequently, especially regarding loyalty or affection"; FICKLE. NOTE ON DEBATED ETYMOLOGY: The assignment of fickle to PIE *peig- "to mark, paint" is plausible and widely repeated but not universally accepted. Two problems attach to it. First, the semantic distance between "painted, variegated" and "treacherous" is real but indirect; it is defended by the parallel of Greek poikílos, which means both "variegated" and "crafty, wily, cunning". Second, the Germanic forms (Old English ficol, fācen, befician; Old Norse feikn; Old High German feihan) point securely to a Germanic root *faik-/*fik- "deceit", but whether that root is itself derived from the Indo-European painting root, or is an independent Germanic formation of unknown or non-PIE origin, remains debated. The Germanic chain (Stage 2 → Stage 3 → Stage 4) is certain; only the Indo-European stage (Stage 1) is a reconstruction and should be read as probable rather than proven. The connection with English fake is often suggested but is itself uncertain.
