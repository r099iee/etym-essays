Tinkle is a noun and verb for a light, clear, high-pitched ringing sound — the sound of a small bell, of thin glass being tapped, of small pieces of ice in a glass, or (as in the passage) of small gold or silver objects clicking together. It is a markedly onomatopoeic word: the very sound of the word imitates the sound it names.

Primary senses

  • Noun — a light ringing sound: "the tinkle of a cowbell", "the tinkle of piano keys in the next room", "the tinkle of glasses at a party".
  • Verb — to make such a sound: "little bells tinkled", "she tinkled a tune on the piano". Often used intransitively; used transitively it means to cause something to tinkle or to play something delicately.
  • In the source passage: As Bilbo stumbles on golden things in the dark, the dwarves "caught a glint and a tinkle" — the small, bright, metallic ring of gold striking gold, heard from afar in the cavern.

Secondary and extended senses

  • Urination (euphemism): Since about the early 20th century, "to tinkle" has been a childish or genteel euphemism for urinating, drawn from the light, tinkling sound of urine falling. It is informal, nursery-register usage ("I need a tinkle").
  • Delicate music: Of pianos, music boxes, harpsichords or celestas, "tinkle" suggests light, high, decorative notes rather than full chords — a tinny but pretty thinness.
  • Figurative light usage: "the tinkling of coins", "tinkling laughter" — small, bright, pleasant sounds; also the Bible's "sounding brass or a tinkling cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13), where a tinkling cymbal is a showy but hollow, empty noise.

Register and nuance

  • Smallness and lightness: A tinkle is always high, light and small. Big, deep, loud or harsh sounds are clangs, clunks, bangs or rings, never tinkles.
  • Pleasant or merely noticeable: The word is usually pleasant or delicate, but it can be mocking (as in the Biblical "tinkling cymbal" — bright but empty).
  • Form vs informality: As a sound word, tinkle is neutral-to-informal but widely accepted in literary prose; the urine sense is marked as childish/informal and is carefully avoided in serious contexts.
  • Auditory imagery: In writing, tinkle is a favourite for quick, specific sound-pictures because it is onomatopoeic and instantly recognisable — one word summons a bell, a glass of ice, a coin, a fountain or a music box without any further description.

Word family and structure

  • It is formed with the frequentative suffix -le, which builds verbs meaning "to do the base action repeatedly or with small motions": cf. sparkle, twinkle, wriggle, jiggle, dribble.
  • Related sound-words: clink, ting, jingle, ring, chink, tintinnabulation.
  • The pair "glint and tinkle" (light + sound) is a stock device of adventure prose for treasure: you see the glint, you hear the tinkle.
