- Old English (c. 10th–11th c.): “sēamestre” — a woman who sews; the suffix “-estre” is explicitly feminine, making this one of the oldest female job titles in English.
- 14th–16th c.: the suffix loses its feminine force — “sempster” can name a man or a woman (like spinster, webster, baxter, brewster, whose feminine marking weakened); gender clarity blurs.
- 15th–17th c.: “-ess” is added to restore the feminine — “sempstresse”, then “seamstress”, with the spelling re-anchored to the noun “seam”; the word becomes the standard name for female needleworkers.
- 18th–19th c.: the Victorian seamstress — emblem of exploited female labour; the “sweating system” of piecework, philanthropists’ reports, Hood’s “The Song of the Shirt” (1843), and the stock tearful seamstress of fiction; Wilde’s Happy Prince passage is a late, vivid specimen.
- 20th c.: mechanisation — “sewing machinist” and “garment worker” replace “seamstress” in industry; the word retreats to ateliers, bridal workrooms and historical fiction.
- Present: mildly dated and femininely coded; professional language prefers “sewer” (gender-neutral), yet “seamstress” survives for bespoke and bridal work — and its thousand-year-old morphology (seam + -ster + -ess) still displays the whole history of women’s work in one word.
