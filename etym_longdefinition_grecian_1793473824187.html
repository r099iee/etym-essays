Grecian is an adjective and historical noun primarily denoting that which pertains to ancient Greece—its civilization, mythology, philosophy, literature, architecture, and classical aesthetic ideals. While historically functioning as an exact synonym for Greek, modern usage has sharpened a clear stylistic and semantic boundary between the two words: Greek serves as the universal, neutral descriptor for the modern nation-state of Greece, its contemporary citizens, and the Greek language across all eras, whereas Grecian is deliberately reserved for antiquity, classical artistic canons, symmetrical physical beauty, and historical or literary retrospection.

In the source passage from David Hume's History of England, the author observes that early fabulous traditions substituting for genuine historical fact ought generally to be dismissed, conceding an exception only for the ‘ancient Grecian fictions’. Hume’s choice of Grecian rather than Greek reflects the characteristic eighteenth-century Enlightenment elevation of classical Hellenic mythology. To Hume and his contemporaries, the myths of Homer, Hesiod, and the Athenian dramatists were not merely crude superstitions or provincial folklore; they were monuments of supreme literary elegance, psychological insight, and harmonious invention. The term Grecian immediately summons this aura of elevated antiquity, distinguishing the immortal, universally admired allegories of classical Hellas from the comparatively obscure and barbarous legends of early Britain or northern Europe.

Morphology and Lexical Nuance: Etymologically formed from the toponym Greece (via Old French Grece and Latin Graecia) combined with the associative suffix -ian, Grecian historically mirrored the pattern of demonyms like Venetian, Persian, or Egyptian. In modern English, however, its grammatical distribution exhibits a notable split between its primary adjectival functions and several specialized nominal applications.

Exhaustive Polysemy, Modern Senses, and Domain Usages:

  • Relating to Ancient Greek Civilization and Culture (Primary Sense): Pertaining specifically to the history, polity, mythology, and cultural achievements of ancient Hellas prior to or during the classical and Hellenistic periods (e.g., ‘ancient Grecian philosophy’, ‘Grecian fables’, ‘Grecian temples’). The historical bridge for this sense arose during the Renaissance and Enlightenment, when humanists sought an elevated Latinate adjective to distinguish the venerated ancients from contemporary Ottoman-ruled Greeks.
  • Classical Architectural and Decorative Style: Denoting architecture, furniture, and ornamentation modeled directly upon ancient Greek principles of symmetry, post-and-lintel construction, fluted columns, and restrained decorative friezes (e.g., ‘the Grecian revival in British civic architecture’, ‘Grecian porticos’). The semantic bridge connects archaeological discoveries in Athens during the eighteenth century (such as Stuart and Revett's Antiquities of Athens) to a decorative movement distinct from Roman or Palladian classicism.
  • Physical Aesthetics and Facial Features: Describing human anatomical beauty characterized by balanced, statuesque regularity, most famously exemplified by the ‘Grecian nose’ or ‘Grecian profile’, wherein the bridge of the nose forms an unbroken, straight vertical line continuous with the slope of the forehead without an indentation at the nasal bridge. This bridge developed from the romanticized adoration of classical sculpture, projecting the idealized proportions of marble statues onto living human physiognomy.
  • Literary and Poetic Idealism: Functioning as an evocative poetic trope representing timeless artistic truth, idyllic pastoral beauty, and heroic myth, immortalized in John Keats's 1819 ode Ode on a Grecian Urn. Here, the word acts as a semantic conduit bridging mortal transience and the eternal, silent perfection of art (‘Thou still unravish'd bride of quietness, / Thou foster-child of silence and slow time, / Sylvan historian...’).
  • Historic Demonym and Scholarly Designation (Noun Usage): Historically, an inhabitant or native of Greece, particularly in biblical translations (e.g., the ‘Grecians’ in the King James Bible, translating Greek Hellēnistai, denoting Greek-speaking or Hellenized Jews). In British institutional history, a ‘Grecian’ denotes a senior student in the top academic form at Christ's Hospital school who has won an exhibition to Oxford or Cambridge to read classics, preserving an educational bridge to Hellenic scholarship.
  • Historical Fashion and Posture Idioms: Denoting historical fashion trends, such as the ‘Grecian bend’—an exaggerated, forward-leaning posture adopted by fashionable women in the late 1860s due to the introduction of high-heeled boots and the bustle, humorously likened to the stoop of classical statues—and the ‘Grecian knot’, a hairstyle where hair is pulled back into an elegant bun at the nape of the neck.

Register and Connotative Tone: The word carries a distinctly elevated, scholarly, and nostalgic register. In modern journalism or everyday discourse, using Grecian to describe a modern vessel, financial policy, or tourist destination would strike the ear as an unidiomatic error or an ironic affectation; Greek is the indispensable standard. Consequently, Grecian functions as a precious stylistic asset in English, allowing writers to evoke the marbles of Phidias, the verses of Pindar, and the temples of Paestum without the modern geopolitical connotations that inevitably cling to Greek.
