SLABS is the plural of slab: large, thick, flat pieces of solid material — chiefly stone, concrete, slate, or wood — cut with two broad, roughly parallel faces and a relatively small thickness. The word carries a distinctive weight: a slab is heavy, solid, and inert, the material of paving, tombstones, building-stones, kitchen counters, and the rough unworked mass from which a statue is carved. It names an unworked, plate-like mass rather than a shaped or precious object — the heavy raw material rather than the finished thing. PRIMARY SENSES
  • A thick flat piece of stone — paving slabs, stone slabs, a tombstone slab, the altar slab: the building and monumental sense, among the oldest in English.
  • A thick flat piece of timber — the rough outer slice of a log left when it is squared: the earliest recorded English sense (c. 1300), still used in the timber trade, where the slab is the waste side-piece of a squared log.
  • A thick flat piece of concrete — a concrete slab, the floor slab or foundation slab of modern construction.
  • A thick flat piece of any solid material — a slab of chocolate, a slab of butter, a slab of meat, a slab of metal: the general transferred sense of a big, flat, solid chunk.
SECONDARY AND EXTENDED SENSES
  • Mortuary and forensic sense — "the slab": the cold flat table on which a corpse is laid in a mortuary or morgue; common in crime and horror narrative and in forensic terminology.
  • Geological and tectonic jargon — a slab is a unit of crustal rock, especially the subducting lithospheric plate that descends into the mantle at a convergent margin; the word entered geology in the 20th century from the same flat-mass image.
  • Construction jargon — a slab as the horizontal structural plate of a building (a concrete slab, a slab foundation); and, as a verb in the trades, "to slab" means to cut or lay flat pieces.
  • Colloquial food use — "a slab" of cake, bread, cheese, or bacon: a generous, chunky, square-cut portion; hence slab cake, slab chocolate, slab bacon.
  • Dialectal and adjectival sense (16th century) — slab as "thick, viscous, gluey": the old "slimy" sense of the word's origin surviving in dialect (slab mud, slab clay), a fossil of the word's pre-history as a term for wet, spreading matter.
  • Sports slang — "the slab" as a cricket bat, and in some slang a surfboard: heavy flat things turned to play.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGES The earliest English sense of slab is the rough flat piece of timber, and behind even that lies an older sense of wet, slippery, muddy matter. The bridge between mud and flatness is the idea of a broad, flat, spreading mass: mud and slush spread out flat, a squared log leaves a broad flat side-piece, and a stone slab is the same shape in stone. From the timber sense the word transferred to flat building stone (14th century), then to any thick flat solid (19th century), and from flatness it acquired its colloquial generosity — a slab of cake is a big flat chunk. The mortuary sense and the tectonic sense both rest on the same core image: a heavy, flat, impersonal mass, one the body lies upon, one the earth itself swallows. THE WORD IN TOLKIEN In the landslide scene of The Hobbit, the plural slabs gives the disaster its scale. These are not pebbles, grit, or small split stones; they are great, plate-like fragments of rock, broken from the mountain and tumbling together. Cracking shows them breaking apart as the slope founders, and the mineral hardness of the word contrasts grimly with the soft turf and herbs of the slope a moment before: the mountain is shedding itself in huge flat pieces, and the company is caught among them. The tone is one of enormous, impersonal mass — stone so heavy that its mere presence is danger. DISTINCTIONS FROM NEAR-SYNONYMS
  • Stone — the general word for any piece of rock whatever its shape; a slab is always a stone, but a stone need not be a slab.
  • Boulder — a large rounded, detached rock, worn smooth by water or weather; massive but not flat.
  • Flag / flagstone — a flat stone of regular size, deliberately cut for paving; a slab may serve as a flag, but the flag is thinner, neater, and made for walking on.
  • Block — a solid piece of material with roughly square faces, thick in all dimensions, not flat.
  • Plate — a flat piece of metal or other material, but thin and manufactured, not thick and heavy.
  • Tile — a thin flat piece for covering surfaces, much thinner and more regular than a slab.
  • Pane — a flat sheet, especially of glass, thin and brittle.
GRAMMATICAL AND FORMAL NOTES Slab is a countable noun; the plural slabs distributes the image across many pieces, which is exactly how it works in the landslide passage. The word also serves as a modifier in compounds (slab-sided, slab timber, slab cake, slab foundation) and, in construction slang, as a transitive verb (to slab a floor — to lay a concrete slab). Its origin is obscure: no Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed, and the dictionaries keep the etymology open, pointing instead to a Germanic expressive base connected with wet, slippery matter.
