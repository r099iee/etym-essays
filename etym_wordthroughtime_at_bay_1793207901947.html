- 14th c. — the hunting field, literal: hounds bring the stag to bay, and bay is their deep barking; the phrase belongs to huntsmen and their manuals.
- 15th–16th c. — metaphor breaks cover: chroniclers and playwrights set armies, heroes, and martyrs at bay; the barking pack becomes any besetting enemy.
- 17th c. — cornered men: divines and pamphleteers hold error, sin, and sedition at bay; the phrase enters moral and political combat.
- 18th c. — Cobbett's England: yeomanry swords keep reformers at bay while tax-eaters plunder; the hunting image serves radical journalism.
- 19th c. — general currency: novels, newspapers, and empire hold everything at bay, from tigers to cholera to creditors; the literal hounds fade from mind.
- Today — the standoff phrase: floodwaters, hackers, and ageing all held at bay; everyone uses it, few picture the stag, but the suspense of the ring remains.
