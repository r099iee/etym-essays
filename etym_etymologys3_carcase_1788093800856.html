The recorded life of carcase spans roughly eight centuries, from the Anglo-French and Old French of the thirteenth century to the meat-packing plants of the present day. What makes the word so instructive for the historical linguist is precisely what makes it difficult: its pre-history is, by the consensus of modern dictionaries, unknown. The OED classifies the etymon as obscure, and no secure Indo-European reconstruction underlies it. The word therefore illustrates, almost in laboratory conditions, how a language can absorb, adapt, and multiply the senses of a loanword whose ultimate origin has slipped entirely away.

1. The Continental problem. The English word descends from Anglo-French carcois and Old French charcois, the ancestor of modern French carcasse “dead body, framework”. Beyond that point the record thins. Three families of explanation have been proposed, and none has won universal assent. First, derivation from or influence of Latin carō, carnis “flesh”, producing a Romance formation of the type *caronia (the same base that yielded English carrion); second, connection with Italian carcassa, the word for the ribbed hulk of a ship, which is itself of unclear origin; and third, an expressive or reduplicated base of the type kark-, of the sort Germanic and Romance both use for hard, rigid, creaking things. The existence of the Italian and Provençal forms already carrying the “framework” sense suggests that the transfer “dead body → ribcage → framework” had happened, or was at least latent, in Romance before English ever borrowed the word.

2. The medieval English record (c. 1300–1500). The first English attestations, around 1300, mean the dead body of a beast. Middle English spelling is fluid — carcois, carcas, carcase, carcasse all occur, as is typical of Anglo-French loans absorbed during the centuries when French was the language of law, hunting, and the kitchen. By the later fourteenth century the word is applied to human corpses, and it soon acquires the double charge it never loses: physical horror and moral contempt. The Authorized Version of 1611 gives the word classic status: in Judges 14:8 Samson finds “a swarm of bees and honey in the carcase of the lion”, embedding carcase in the most-read book in English for three centuries and guaranteeing that generation after generation met the word in its plainest animal sense.

3. The great widening (15th–17th centuries). Three extensions transform the word in the early modern period. First, the living body: from the sixteenth century, “my poor carcase” and its insults treat the human body as meat regardless of life — a reductive metonymy that spread from tavern jest to pulpit rhetoric, where sermons on mortality used the word to deflate human pride: this is what we are. Second, the structural sense: the carcase of a ship (its bare hull) and the carcase of a house (its shell after fire) transfer the skeleton image from anatomy to construction; the same image later gives ships their “ribs”. Third, the military sense: in seventeenth-century siege warfare a carcass was an incendiary shell — an iron sphere packed with combustibles, fired burning into besieged towns — a word that in its own age merged “body”, “corpse”, and “bomb” into one terrible object.

4. Fixation and the double spelling (18th–19th centuries). The eighteenth century fixes the word's status: Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) gives carcase as its headword form, and the -se spelling enjoys literary prestige in Britain even as French literary influence pushes the -ss spelling forward on the Continent and, increasingly, in commerce. The nineteenth century industrialises butchery: the dressed animal carcase becomes a weighable, gradeable unit of commerce, and the “carcase weight” of the trade enters statistical and legal language. The same century adopts the word into natural history as the standard term for a dead animal found in the field — a fully neutral, scientific use standing at the opposite pole from the pulpit's contempt.

5. The twentieth century to today. The spelling split hardens: carcass dominates globally, while carcase persists in British and Commonwealth trade practice (carcase weight, carcase classification) and in reference style. Ecology and forensic science adopt the word neutrally: a carcase is a keystone resource for the scavenger guild, and the state of a carcase helps date a death. Meanwhile the colloquial stream keeps the word as insult and gallows-humour — “get your carcase over here” — and editors balance the two spellings by convention rather than by rule.

Semantic drift in summary

  • Dead beast (c. 1300) → dead human (14th c.) → living human body as contempt or joke (16th c.).
  • Animal skeleton → ship's hull and building shell (16th c.) → any stripped framework.
  • Siege-warfare incendiary shell (17th c.), a grim pun on body and corpse.
  • Dressed slaughtered animal, weighed unit of meat commerce (19th–20th c.), where the word is fully neutral.
  • Spelling split carcase/carcass hardening: -se in British trade, -ss global.
