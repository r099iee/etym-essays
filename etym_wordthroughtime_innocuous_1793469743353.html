- Late 16th c. (c. 1590–1600) — Renaissance Latin borrowing: Adapted into English from Latin innocuus as a scholarly synonym for harmless, operating alongside the competing loanword innoxious.
- 17th c. — Natural philosophy and scientific inquiry: Sir Francis Bacon, Sir Thomas Browne, and early fellows of the Royal Society use innocuous to classify non-poisonous herbs, safe chemical distillations, and mild meteorological phenomena.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment social and literary critique: Samuel Johnson, Alexander Pope, and Augustan periodical essayists apply innocuous to social conversation, moral conduct, and literary reviews, frequently using it with ironic disdain to denote toothless, impotent writing.
- 19th c. — Victorian medicine and toxicology: With the rise of modern pathology and bacteriology, innocuous becomes a standard clinical term to distinguish benign tumors and non-pathogenic microbes from virulent strains.
- 20th c. — Diplomatic and sociological discourse: Widely adopted in diplomatic and political commentary to characterize gestures, treaties, or statements that are carefully designed to cause no diplomatic friction.
- Today — Digital technology and everyday idioms: Ubiquitous in modern prose, describing everything from pleasant social small-talk to deceptive digital threats (an innocuous-looking software link concealing malware).
