- Old English — segst, sægst: the second-person singular of secgan is everyday speech, as ordinary as you say is today, with no solemnity attached.
- Middle English — sayest, seyst: Chaucer's pilgrims use it in tavern talk and knightly romance alike; still the neutral thou-form, neither high nor low.
- 1500s–1600s — the golden age: Shakespeare gives it to kings and clowns, the 1611 Bible fixes it forever — what sayest thou — at the summit of solemnity.
- 1700s — the Quakers' witness: Friends keep thou and its verbs as plain speech while the world moves to you, so sayest acquires a sectarian, principled flavour.
- 1800s — the poets' costume: Romantics and hymn-writers don it for elevation, though no child now says it at home; archaism becomes decoration.
- Today — irony and liturgy: heard sincerely only in scripture readings and period drama, and everywhere else as a joke — the most solemn way to ask about pizza.
