In the courtroom scene of Honglou meng, when Yu-cun "put it down again and adjourned to his private chambers, dismissing everyone except the usher," the verb does not merely describe a man leaving a room. In legal and administrative English, to adjourn is to break off a formal sitting — of a court, an assembly, a parliament, or a meeting — with the express intention of resuming later. The magistrate is formally suspending public business: he dismisses the general attendance, retains only the usher, and moves the handling of the case from the open yamen hall into a closed chamber. The literary force is tactical. Public justice is paused; private negotiation, influence, and corruption can begin. The past form adjourned also carries the settled finality of an official act already taken — business is no longer in open session, and the move to the private chambers is itself framed as a formal proceeding. Core definition. Adjourn (verb) means: (1) to break off the proceedings of a court, legislature, committee, or other formal body with the intention of continuing them at another time or place; (2) more generally, to suspend or postpone an activity until a later time; (3) informally and with a preposition (adjourn to), to go to another place, especially after a meeting or a meal. The noun adjournment is the act or the period of suspension. The form adjourned is both the simple past and the past participle: "the court adjourned," "the meeting was adjourned," "adjourned to his chambers." Polysemy and senses:
  • Legal / procedural sense: a court, hearing, or trial adjourns or is adjourned — suspended until a future sitting. This is the core institutional sense and the earliest firmly established one in English.
  • Parliamentary / assembly sense: the House, the Senate, the committee adjourns — ends its sitting for the day or for a period; "adjourn sine die" (Latin, "without a day") means no date is set, i.e. an indefinite or final end of sitting.
  • Meeting / business sense: to put a meeting off, to break it up with the intention of continuing later ("let's adjourn until Tuesday").
  • Idiomatic "adjourn to": of a group, to withdraw or repair to another place, often socially, after formal business — "adjourned to the pub," "adjourned to the drawing-room." This is the sense in the novel, where the magistrate withdraws to his chambers.
  • Historical legal sense (older): to summon someone to appear on a fixed day; to fix a day for appearance. This is the sense of the Old French ancestor ajourner and survives in the early history of the English verb.
The historical bridge: the word literally means "to fix a day" (Old French a- + jour "day"), so its semantic core is calendar-setting — authority names the day on which business resumes. From "assign a day" it developed "put off to another day" and then the ritual suspension of a sitting; the "adjourn to a place" idiom extends the idea of formally moving the company somewhere. Synonym boundaries:
  • recess: similar in denoting a break in proceedings; differs because a recess is a scheduled, temporary pause within a continuing sitting, without necessarily reconvening at a newly fixed day or moving the body elsewhere.
  • postpone: similar in deferring; differs because postpone stresses delaying a matter to a later date without the ritual suspension of a body in session.
  • suspend: similar in halting temporarily; differs because suspend also covers disciplinary or constitutional interruption and need not imply reconvening by design.
  • defer: similar in putting off; differs because defer is lighter and applies to any delayed action, lacking the institutional assembly-procedure feel of adjourn.
  • prorogue: similar in ending a parliamentary session; differs because prorogue is the specific technical word for ending a session of parliament until the next session, not a day-to-day break.
  • retire: similar in withdrawing from a session; differs because retire focuses on persons withdrawing to deliberate (the jury retires), not on putting formal business to another day.
Collocations and register. Common collocations: adjourn the meeting, adjourn the court / hearing / trial, adjourn for lunch, adjourn until Tuesday / next week, adjourn sine die, the House adjourned, adjourn to the bar / drawing-room. Register is formal, legal, parliamentary, and administrative; in casual speech the word can sound stiff or mildly comic when applied to trivial movements ("shall we adjourn to the kitchen?"). In the novel, the choice of adjourned dignifies Yu-cun's withdrawal with procedural formality — an act of institutional power that switches the scene from public justice to private manipulation.
