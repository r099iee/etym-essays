DOCTORED (/ˈdɒktəd/) is the past-tense and past-participle form of the verb doctor, a word with two very different faces in modern English. On the one hand it means “treated medically, given medical care” (a person who has been “doctored” has been looked after by a physician); on the other hand — far more common today — it means “altered, falsified, adulterated, or tampered with” (doctored accounts, a doctored photograph, doctored wine). The two senses sit at opposite ends of a single historical drift: the word travelled from “to treat as a doctor does” to “to patch up”, and from “to patch up” to “to fix dishonestly”.

Sense 1 — to treat medically: The original verb sense (18th century) simply means “to act as a physician toward, to give medical treatment to”. This sense survives chiefly in regional, colloquial, or old-fashioned use: “the old woman doctored the children with herbs and broths”.

The double life of the participle: Because doctor is a regular verb, doctored works both as a simple past (“they doctored the horse”) and as a passive participle (“the horse was doctored”). It is in the passive use that the modern split is most visible: a “doctored” patient has been healed and cared for, while a “doctored” document has been corrupted — the same word, two moral worlds, separated only by the noun it attaches to.

  • Sense 2 — to repair, patch up, or fix: a 19th-century extension; to “doctor” a machine or a meal is to mend it or make it usable, often in a rough-and-ready way — “he doctored the leaky roof with tar”.
  • Sense 3 — to alter or adulterate (dishonest): the dominant modern sense; to “doctor” evidence, figures, photographs, or food is to change them for the worse — falsify, tamper, spike, or cut. “Doctored” images and “doctored” statistics are a defining problem of the age of digital media.
  • Sense 4 — to improve or give a flavour to (food and drink): a mildly positive or neutral cousin of Sense 3; to “doctor” a cocktail or a sauce is to add extra ingredients to improve it: “he doctored his coffee with cinnamon and cream”.
  • Sense 5 — to castrate (an animal): in rural and veterinary use, to “doctor” a dog or cat is to neuter it; “doctored” is the polite euphemism for “neutered”.
  • Sense 6 — to confer a doctorate upon: a rare and scholarly sense of the verb; a university “doctors” someone when it grants the degree.

Historical bridge: Doctor comes from Latin doctor, “teacher”, from docēre, “to teach”. In English the noun first meant “learned teacher”, and only in the 16th century did “doctor” come to mean “physician” (the qualified teacher of medicine). The verb to doctor (“to treat medically”) is a natural back-formation of the 18th century. Its later senses grew by metaphor: to “doctor” a thing is to handle it as one handles a patient — to patch it, correct it, or improve it — and the dishonest “tamper” sense crystallised in the 19th century when the verb began to be applied to food, drink, and documents. The semantic gulf between “healing” and “falsifying” is thus bridged by the single idea of a hand applied to set things right — or to set them wrong.
