The rich historical trajectory of interpolations illustrates how an ancient Roman craftsman's term for patching and scouring soiled cloth transformed into a technical instrument of Renaissance philology and ultimately into a cornerstone of modern digital computing and cognitive neuroscience.

The ultimate ancestor of the word is found in the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *pel- (or its extended variant *pol-), which carried connotations of 'to strike, thrust, drive,' and developed in various dialects into words associated with dressing, fulling, and smoothing skins and woven fabrics (cognate with Greek pallein 'to shake' and Latin pellis 'skin'). In Proto-Italic, this root yielded the verbal stem *poln-i-, which produced the Classical Latin verb polire, meaning 'to polish, smooth, clean, rub bright, furbish, or refine.'

In Republican Rome, craftsmen known as fullers (fullones) washed, bleached, scoured, and refurbished wool garments that had grown dingy or worn. When fullers patched old garments or cleaned cloth by inserting fresh threads and scouring powders between the fibers, Romans described this restorative patching with the compound verb interpolare, forged from the prepositional prefix inter ('between, among') and the base verb polire ('to smooth, polish, touch up'). In its original domestic and commercial setting, interpolare meant literally 'to refurbish, revamp, dress up between, or give a new face to something old by patching.'

However, Roman legal scholars and satirists quickly recognized that 'touching up' an old garment was perilously close to falsification. By the 1st century BCE, writers such as Cicero, Plautus, and Ulpian were using interpolare and its abstract noun interpolatio in a pejorative sense: to falsify, tamper with, corrupt, adulterate, or disguise an original document, will, or commodity by inserting fraudulent clauses between authentic lines. Ulpian famously discussed legal penalties for those who committed interpolatio upon public records.

Following the fall of Rome, the term remained preserved in Medieval Latin legal registers. During the Renaissance, humanists such as Lorenzo Valla, Desiderius Erasmus, and Joseph Justus Scaliger initiated the era of modern textual criticism. When Valla demonstrated in 1440 that the famed Donation of Constantine was an eighth-century forgery, and when Erasmus published his critical edition of the Greek New Testament in 1516, scholars needed a precise term to describe the unauthorized passages that medieval scribes had slipped into ancient manuscripts. They adopted interpolatio (entering French as interpolation and English in the 1610s), defining interpolations as foreign textual grafts corrupting classical and sacred texts.

In the late seventeenth century, mathematics reclaimed the term from its purely pejorative associations. Mathematician John Wallis, in his Arithmetica Infinitorum (1656), and Sir Isaac Newton, in his work on orbital mechanics, required a systematic term for estimating points on a curve falling between known observations. They adopted interpolation to describe calculating intermediate values within known tables. This mathematical formulation flourished through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries in the work of Lagrange and Gauss, becoming essential for naval navigation and astronomical ephemerides.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, the digital revolution and cognitive science expanded interpolations into the core of daily technological and psychological life. Today, computer graphics pipelines run polynomial interpolations billions of times per second to upscale video streams and render 3D animation, while cognitive psychologists recognize that human perception itself relies upon continuous neural interpolations. From an ancient fuller's patch on Roman wool, the word has ascended to describe the very algorithms that construct our mathematical, digital, and perceptual realities.
