Romanian is the English name of the Eastern Romance language spoken chiefly in Romania and Moldova, and, as a noun, of the people who speak it. It is the only major Romance language of eastern Europe, the official language of the Romanian state, and one of the official languages of the European Union. The word also serves as an adjective covering the country, its culture, its institutions and a number of specialised uses, from weightlifting to embroidery.

The language. Romanian (limba română) belongs to the Eastern Romance branch of the Indo-European family, descended from the Vulgar Latin carried into the Balkans and the lower Danube by Roman soldiers, colonists and administrators. Its closest relatives are the small Balkan Romance languages: Aromanian (spoken in Greece, Albania, North Macedonia and the diaspora), Megleno-Romanian and Istro-Romanian. The standard language is sometimes called Daco-Romanian to distinguish it from those cousins. It preserves a Latin core vocabulary, a two-case system, a neuter gender, and a definite article suffixed to the noun (lupul "the wolf"), features that make it in some respects the most conservative Romance language and in others the most innovative, since it shares a Balkan Sprachbund profile with Bulgarian, Greek and Albanian.

Classification and structure.

  • Phonology: the central vowel /ɨ/, written â or î, is a hallmark; Latin long ā became this vowel, and Latin l between vowels became r (Latin caelum "sky" gives cer, solem "sun" gives soare). Stressed e and o diphthongise in many contexts (ferrum gives fier, porta gives poartă).
  • Morphology: nouns retain a nominative-accusative versus genitive-dative distinction; the neuter is a surviving Latin category, with masculine forms in the singular and feminine in the plural.
  • Lexicon: a Latin inheritance, a Dacian/Thracian substrate of uncertain extent, Slavic superstrate and later borrowings, and a large nineteenth-century layer of French and Italian learned words.
  • Script: Cyrillic was used in the Romanian principalities into the 1860s; the Latin alphabet became official in 1866, and the orthography was reformed in 1953 and again in 1993.

History. Roman Dacia was conquered in 106 CE and abandoned by the army in the 270s, and the survival of a Latin-speaking population north of the Danube has been debated for centuries. The continuity theory holds that Latin-speaking Daco-Romans remained in the Carpathian basin; the migration theory holds that Romance speakers moved north from the Balkans later. Whatever the balance, a Romance speech community is documented in the region by the late Middle Ages, and the first surviving Romanian text, Neacșu's letter of 1521, already shows a fully formed language. The principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia used Church Slavonic for liturgy and chancery before Romanian gradually took over; Transylvania, under Hungarian and then Habsburg rule, produced the Greek-Catholic intellectual movement known as the Transylvanian School, which emphasised the Roman descent of the Romanians.

The people and the national name. The endonym român descends directly from Latin Romanus, "Roman", and the country's name România means "land of the Romans". Foreigners long called the people Vlachs (German Walachen, Slavic Vlasi, Hungarian oláh), a term derived from the Germanic word for a Celtic or Romance speaker. Modern national identity was constructed in the nineteenth century through language, history and the Latinist movement, and "Romanian" became the standard English designation, displacing older Rumanian and Roumanian spellings.

Adjectival and specialist senses.

  • Romanian deadlift: a stiff-legged weightlifting variant, named for the style attributed to Romanian lifters.
  • Romanian blouse (ie): a traditional embroidered garment, now a marker of folk dress and fashion.
  • Romanian Orthodox Church: the autocephalous Eastern Orthodox church of Romania.
  • Romanian Revolution: the 1989 uprising that ended the Ceaușescu regime.
  • Romanianization: the policy or process of making something Romanian in language, personnel or culture.
  • Romanian leu: the national currency, literally "lion".

Modern connotations. In contemporary English "Romanian" is mostly neutral and geographical, but it can be coloured by migration debates, by the memory of Ceaușescu's dictatorship, and by the country's post-2007 EU membership. In linguistics it is a precise classificatory term; in everyday use it may loosely cover Moldovan, though the status of "Moldovan" as a separate language is politically contested and most linguists treat it as a variety of Romanian.

Semantic bridges. The word's unity comes from a single audacious claim: that a province on the lower Danube preserved the name and speech of Rome. Citizenship, soldiering, church Slavonic, Cyrillic, French fashion and EU accession all left marks on the language, but the name itself kept pointing back to Romanus, and that is why "Romanian" is at once a linguistic classification, a national identity and a historical argument.
