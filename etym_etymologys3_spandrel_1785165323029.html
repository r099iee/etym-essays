Spandrel is a word of the builder's yard whose biography reaches back, through Norman French and classical Latin, to a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to spread," and whose modern fame rests on a paper about a Venetian church. The Oxford English Dictionary records the first English appearances of the word in the late fifteenth century, in forms such as spandreil, spaundrel, and spandrelle, and it assigns the word a probably Romance origin: from the Anglo-Norman and Old French verb espandre ("to spread out, pour, scatter"), itself from Latin expandere, "to spread out," a compound of ex- ("out") and pandere ("to spread, unfold, open"). Pandere in turn is assigned, in the standard handbooks, to the Proto-Indo-European root *pet(h2)- "to spread or open wide," a root whose descendants reach across the family: Greek petannymi ("to unfold") gave petalon, "a spread-out plate," whence our petal; Latin patere ("to lie open") lies behind English patent, the "open" letter conferring a public right; and the Germanic branch of the same root gave Old English faethm, "the spread of the arms," our fathom. If the connection holds, the spandrel is etymologically the "spread-out" part of the wall — the surface that spills outward beneath the arch's curve. It must be said plainly, however, that the word's exact route into English is disputed: the OED labels the derivation as probable rather than certain, and some scholars have looked instead toward dialect forms of the same spreading stock. The chain from pandere backward is the traditional account and is presented as such.

Whatever its precise birth, the word's meaning was fixed by the geometry of medieval building. Wherever a mason set a semicircular or pointed arch beneath a rectangular frame — and the whole of medieval and Renaissance architecture is full of such settings — a triangular space was left at the corner, between the arch's outer curve and the horizontal line above. This space the builders called the spandrel. Because the arch could not exist without it, the spandrel was not a choice but a condition of the arch; and precisely because it was unavoidable, it became an opportunity. In the vaulted ceilings of Gothic cathedrals the spandrels between the crowns of the ribs were filled with paneling, carving, and painted figures; on Renaissance facades the spandrels of the arches were covered with the winged victories, wreaths, and grotesques that turn a structural necessity into ornament; and in the timber roofs and staircases of the same centuries, spandrel steps and spandrel beams multiplied. The word thus enters English prose already laden with the paradox that would make it famous six centuries later: it names a space that nobody designed, and that everybody decorated.

The transfer of the word from masonry to biology was made by two paleontologists, Stephen Jay Gould and Richard C. Lewontin, in a celebrated essay of 1979, "The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm." Walking the great church of San Marco in Venice, with its dome raised on arches that spring from the crown of a lower arch, they took the cathedral's spandrels — the triangular panels of mosaic between the arches — as a symbol of an elementary point about evolution: many features of an organism exist not because they were selected for, but as structural consequences of features that were. The arches create the spandrels, whatever the mosaicist then puts on them; the selected trait creates the byproduct, whatever use a later generation of selection finds for it. The essay was aimed at the "adaptationist programme," the habit of telling a story about why every part exists for its own adaptive good, and it urged that the correct null expectation for many traits is that they are spandrels: necessary, incidental, and often subsequently co-opted. Gould returned to the word repeatedly in the 1990s — arguing, for instance, that the human chin is a spandrel of jaw shrinkage, that the human capacity for language may rest on spandrels of the enlarged brain, and that many aspects of mind are better read as structural byproducts than as Darwinian achievements.

The word's career since 1979 illustrates exactly the process it names. Born as a plain term of the mason's craft, it was co-opted by theoretical biology and there became a keyword of the anti-adaptationist turn; from biology it leaked into philosophy of science, cognitive science, and cultural commentary, where "spandrel" is now a general name for any feature that is a constraint-generated byproduct of a designed system — from the spaces between the buttons on a keyboard to, in some writers, the accidental affordances of software. Throughout, the two senses remain joined by a single intuition: what is forced on a structure by its own geometry is not the same as what is made for a purpose, yet the forced thing may, in time, be turned to a purpose. That is exactly the claim of the present context — an evolutionary "side effect," later so useful that humans "quickly turned it into a survival tool." No other word in the biological lexicon holds the architectural image of the inevitable gap, and it is the image that does the explanatory work: the spandrel is what the arch leaves behind, and the arch cannot help leaving it.
