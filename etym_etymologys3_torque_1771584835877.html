The word torque is a linguistic double agent: one spelling, two distinct careers, both rooted in the same ancient idea of twisting. The Celtic neck-ring and the engineer's rotational force share not only a form but a genealogy — both descend from the Latin verb torquēre, "to twist" — and the history of the word shows how a single root could produce a barbarian treasure and a unit of modern physics without ever losing its basic sense of the twist. Its appearance in the passage, as a "golden torque in the likeness of a dragon," binds both lives together: an object twisted from precious metal, worn as a ring of rank, that is at once jewellery and insignia.

1. The Indo-European root of twisting. Both senses reach back to the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *terkʷ-, "to twist." The root is among the more securely attested reconstructions of its semantic field: Greek átraktos "spindle" preserves the image of the twisted thread and the twisting tool, Sanskrit tarkú- "spindle" matches it closely, and Latin torquēre "to twist" gives the same root a large and well-documented family in the West. The spindles and distaffs of the Indo-European world were the model for all twisting — the making of thread is the oldest technology of the twist — and the root carried that mechanical image into dozens of derivatives. (The only debated corner of this early stage is whether some Germanic forms, such as the Old English word for "distaff," belong to the same root; the Latin side is not affected by the debate.)

2. Latin torquēre and the torque of the Gauls. Latin torquēre, attested from the third century BCE, meant "to twist, turn, wind, distort" — a common verb with a rich family: tortus "twisted" (source of English "tort," "torture," "tortuous," "torsion," "tortilla," "torment"), torqueō, and the deverbal noun torques (also torquis), "the twisted thing," which by the second century BCE had settled on a specific object: a twisted metal neck-ring. It is no accident that the word for "necklace made by twisting" named the most famous ornament of the ancient Celtic world. Caesar, in his Gallic War, and Livy describe the torques worn by Gallic warriors and chieftains — the great collars of gold and bronze that the classical world saw as the badge of the barbarian nobleman. Roman discipline absorbed the ornament: the Roman army awarded the torques as a decoration for valour (the "torc of honour"), so that a Gallic neck-ring became a Roman military medal, one of the clearest early examples of the empire borrowing the symbols of its own defeated enemies.

3. The French bridge and entry into English. Latin torques passed into Old French as torque or torce — "a twisted band, a collar" — and it was through French that the word reached English in the late sixteenth century. In Elizabethan and Jacobean England the torque was already an antiquarian object: writers of the period use the word for the collars of the ancient Britons described by Roman historians, and the revived interest in classical antiquity — and in the British past — kept the word alive as a learned, archaeological term. For three centuries it slept in dictionaries and histories as the name of an ancient ornament, exactly as it appears in the passage's portrait of an exotic, archaic, almost talismanic piece of jewellery. The torque was not a modern word in this period; it was the word of scholars and poets reaching back across two thousand years to the warriors of Gaul and Britain.

4. The nineteenth-century rebirth as physics. In the 1880s the word received its second life. James Clerk Maxwell, the Scottish physicist, needed a concise English name for the moment of a force about an axis — the twisting effort measured by a wrench or a crankshaft — and reached for the Latin torquēre, proposing "torque" for the turning moment. The coinage was an inspired piece of lexical archaeology: the ancient word for "twist" became the modern name for rotational force, and the same spelling that named the twisted neck-ring now named the twisting force itself. The word spread rapidly through mechanical and electrical engineering — engine torque, torque converters, torque wrenches — and by the twentieth century the technical sense was, for most speakers, the dominant one. The two senses now coexist without confusion: archaeology and engineering share the spelling but not the context.

5. The word in the passage. In the novel, the golden torque "in the likeness of a dragon" does its ancient work with undiminished force. The torque is stiff and massive, engineered to be worn as a statement of rank — unlike a necklace, which hangs and dangles, it encircles the throat as a closed ring of precious metal. Because it is wrought in the likeness of the dragon, the imperial emblem of China, it proclaims quasi-royal lineage; because it is a torque — a twisted band closed in a ring — it suggests the hereditary bond that circles the neck of the pampered heir, an emblem of honour that is at the same time a fine, gilded confinement. The word's twin etymologies supply a quiet pun: the torque of the passage is a thing twisted from metal, and it is worn as a ring of fate that twists around the wearer. Jewellery, insignia, and mechanics meet in a single word whose two-thousand-year history of twisting is still coiled inside its five letters.
