The English category narcotics descends from a Greek vocabulary of numbness. Ancient Greek narkē meant numbness, deadness, stupor, and also the crampfish or electric ray whose shock numbs the hand — a vivid metaphor where animal and symptom share a name. The verb narkoun meant to benumb or deaden; Hippocratic writers use it for limbs gone numb with cold or disease. The adjective narkōtikos, benumbing, appears in medical writers and prominently in Galen (2nd century CE) for drugs that deaden pain and induce sleep: opium, mandrake, henbane. This is the direct semantic ancestor: a medicine that makes tissue unfeeling.

Transmission to English passed through Latin and French medicine:

  • Medieval Latin narcoticus (14th century): Scholastic translations of Avicenna and Galen use narcoticus for soporific simples; Middle English recipes already speak of narcotykes.
  • Middle French narcotique: Paré and 16th-century surgeons distinguish narcotics (sleep-producers) from anodynes and hypnotics.
  • Early Modern English narcotic (c. 1600s): Philemon Holland's Pliny (1601) and later pharmacopoeias list opium as the chief narcotic; the noun and adjective are interchangeable.
  • Pluralisation to narcotics (18th–19th century): Materia medica speaks of the narcotics as a class — opium, morphine (isolated 1804), codeine, belladonna — opposed to stimulants, tonics, and emetics.

Phonetically the chain is borrowing, not regular sound change: Greek -ōtikos → Latin -oticus → French -otique → English -otic, with initial nark- → narc- under Latin c spelling. Stress settled on the second syllable in English: nar-COT-ic. The final -s in the source passage is the ordinary English plural marking a class of substances thrown into streams.

The great semantic drift occurred in the 19th and 20th centuries under law. Medically, narcotic properly means an opioid that induces stupor, relieves pain, and is addictive — morphine, heroin, codeine, fentanyl. Legally, especially in the United States after the Harrison Act (1914), the Controlled Substances Act (1970), and the War on Drugs, narcotics expanded to mean any illegal or controlled drug, including cocaine and cannabis which are pharmacologically stimulants, not narcotics. Police, press, and treaties speak of narcotics bureaus, narcotics trafficking, and narcotics dogs even for non-narcotic drugs. Physicians protest this misuse, but popular usage has fixed it. Hence dictionaries now list two definitions: strict (stupor-inducing opioid) and loose (any illicit drug).

Key attestations include:

  • Hippocrates and Aristotle (5th–4th c. BCE): narkē for numbness and torpedo-fish.
  • Galen (2nd c. CE): narkōtika pharmaka as a therapeutic class.
  • Chaucer and medieval herbals: dwale and morel as narcotics avant la lettre.
  • 1601 Holland's Pliny: first naturalised English narcotike.
  • De Quincey, Confessions of an English Opium-Eater (1821): literary glamour and horror of narcotics.
  • 20th-century treaties: Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs (1961) codifies the legal super-sense.

In the Tahitian fishing passage, narcotics is used in the older broad pharmacological sense: any plant substance that stupefies (fish-poisons, piscicides), not the modern opioid-specific or criminal sense. That usage preserves Galen's logic — that which benumbs — applied to fish rather than surgical patients, and predates the pejorative criminal aura the word acquired after 1900.
