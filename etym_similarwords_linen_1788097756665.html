• Flax: Similar because linen IS the cloth of flax, sharing its fibre and crop; differs because flax names the plant and fibre themselves, while linen names the finished textile (and, by extension, the household goods made from it).
  • Cloth: Similar as a generic woven material; differs because cloth is fully general (wool, cotton, synthetic), while all linen is a specific flax-derived fabric.
  • Fabric: Similar as manufactured textile material; differs in register — fabric is the neutral retail and sewing term, with no fibre claim at all.
  • Cotton: Similar as a plant-fibre textile with comparable uses; differs in fibre (cotton is seed hair, linen is bast fibre), in texture (cotton soft, linen crisp and cool), and in drying and wrinkling behaviour.
  • Cambric: Similar as a fine white cloth; differs because cambric names a specific fine weave, now usually woven in cotton; originally a linen.
  • Damask: Similar as a fine patterned household fabric; differs because damask names the figured reversible weave technique (once of silk as well as linen), not a fibre.
  • Muslin: Similar as a light fine cloth; differs because muslin is cotton, and its connotation is sheerness and cheapness rather than strength and cost.
  • Sheeting: Similar as plain-woven cloth for beds; differs because sheeting is a product category (cotton, polyester, or blends) rather than a fibre name.
  • Napery: Similar as table linen; differs because napery is a trade and archaic collective term restricted to tablecloths and napkins, never bed or body linen.
  • Lingerie: Similar as the historical descendant of the “body linen” sense (French linge, “linen”); differs because lingerie now means women's underwear exclusively, with no fibre connection to flax.
  • Byssus: Similar as the ancient fine linen of scripture and ritual; differs because byssus is a historical/technical term (also the silky fibre of a fan mussel), never an everyday word.
