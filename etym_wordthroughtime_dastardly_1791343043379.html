- 15th c. (noun dastard) "a dullard, a dazed or sluggish person" - derived from daze; referred to someone stupefied, inactive, or lacking wit and vigor.
- Early 16th c. "a base coward, an unchivalrous poltroon" - in knightly and military contexts, sluggish inaction in the face of battle was judged as shameful cowardice.
- 1560s–17th c. (adjective dastardly) "meanly cowardly, treacherous, base" - the adjective arose with the suffix -ly, applied to unchivalrous acts of betrayal, poison, and sneakiness.
- 18th–19th c. "villainous, sneakily malicious" - flourished in romantic literature and Victorian stage melodrama as the quintessential epithet for scheming villains.
- 1941 (FDR Pearl Harbor speech) "an unprovoked, deceitful sneak attack" - cemented in modern political and military rhetoric to denote surprise attacks against unsuspecting nations.
- Contemporary usage "contemptibly cowardly; mock-heroically unfair" - balances serious geopolitical condemnation of terrorism/sneak attacks with comedic, everyday accusations of petty betrayal among friends.
