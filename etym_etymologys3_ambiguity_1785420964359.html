AMBIGUITY is a Latin compound, and its evolution must be traced through its two Indo-European roots before they meet in the single word. The two essays below follow each root in isolation; a concluding section unites them in the Latin compound and follows the word into English. === ESSAY 1: AMBI- (THE "BOTH" PREFIX) === The prefix ambi- descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂embʰi-, "around, on both sides, both," one of the family's prepositions of encircling and doubling. The root is attested across the ancient branches with remarkable consistency. In Greek it appears as amphi-, "on both sides, around," the source of English amphibious ("living in both elements"), amphitheatre (a theatre with seating around), and amphora (the two-handled jar, whose name literally describes a vessel held from both sides). In Indic it appears as Sanskrit abhi-, "to, toward, over," attested from the Rigveda, where it forms compounds of approach and superiority such as abhibhu, "overcoming"; the Hindi prefix abhi- continues the same element in modern compounds. In Latin the root took the shape amb- before vowels and some consonants, ambi- elsewhere, with an older Italic variant am- preserved in words like amens, "out of one's mind," literally "beside oneself, driven away from one's mind," and the adjective ambo, "both." The Latin family built on amb- is instructive because it shows how a preposition of "around" could generate verbs of politics, appetite, and intellect. Ambire, "to go around," from amb- + ire "to go," produced the noun ambitio, literally "a going around" — the Roman canvassing practice of walking around the forum to solicit votes — and so English ambition, the desire for advancement, retains in its etymology the image of a candidate going round the voters. Ambitus, "a circuit," gives English ambit, the sphere or scope of something. Ambidexter, "two right hands," gives ambidextrous. And ambigere, "to drive around, to drive both ways," from amb- + agere "to drive," gave the verb of being in doubt, because to drive something this way and that, to wander around without settling, is to hesitate and waver. The prefix thus brought to the compound the idea of doubleness and encircling: a man who "drives both ways" cannot choose, and the image of motion around and between is the seed of every "both" word in the family. The sound history of the prefix is short but real. The PIE *h₂ embʰi- shows the laryngeal *h₂, which in Latin and Greek was lost in this position, leaving the initial vowel; the labial cluster *-mbʰ- developed into the Greek -mph- (amphi-) and the Latin -mb- (amb-), with the Italic loss of the aspiration that Greek retained. In English the prefix has never been native; it arrives only in learned borrowings from Latin (ambivalent, ambidextrous, ambiguous) and Greek (amphibious, amphitheatre), so the sound changes that shaped it belong to the classical languages, not to English. What English did contribute was the productive life of the prefix in modern scientific and psychological vocabulary: ambivalence, coined in the twentieth century from ambi- and valere, "to be strong," names the state of being pulled toward two values at once — a modern word built on the same ancient image of doubleness that produced ambiguity. The prefix's contribution to the compound is therefore the idea of "both sides," of being surrounded or doubled, the essential precondition for the double meaning the noun names. === ESSAY 2: AGERE (THE "DRIVE" VERB) === The base verb agere descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *h₂eǵ-, "to drive, lead, set in motion," one of the most productive verbal roots of the entire family. The root's fundamental image is the driving of animals or the leading of a procession: Sanskrit ajati, attested in the Rigveda, means "he drives, leads," said especially of driving cattle; Greek agein, from Homer onward, means "to drive, lead," and from it English has borrowed agony (the struggle or contest, the state of being driven hard), protagonist (the first actor, the one who acts and leads the drama), pedagogue (the child-leader, the slave who led boys to school), strategy (the general's art, from stratos "army" + agein "to lead"), and demagogue (the people-leader). In every branch the root means the same physical thing: to set something in motion and keep it moving in a direction. In Latin the root produced agere, attested from the third century BCE, and Latin turned it into the verb of action itself — "to drive, do, act, perform." The semantic development is striking: from the shepherd driving his flock, agere came to mean any deliberate doing, so that Latin could say agere vitam, "to live a life," agere gratias, "to give thanks," and agere negotium, "to conduct business." From agere and its participle actus come English agent (one who acts), act and action, agile (quick-moving), agenda (things to be done), agitate (to set in motion, to stir up), and, with prefixes, cogent (from co- + agere, "to drive together," hence convincing), exact (from ex- + agere, "to drive out," hence to demand), and prodigal (from prodigere, "to drive forth, squander"). The noun actor is literally "one who drives or does." In Latin itself the verb already spanned the whole range from herding cattle to performing on the stage and pleading in court, and the English borrowings have simply inherited that width. The morphology of agere is also worth recording, because it shaped the word's later life. Latin agere is built on the present stem ag-, and its participles and gerundives — actus "done, driven," agenda "things to be done," agentem "acting" — are the forms that actually entered English, since learned borrowing tends to take the participle stem rather than the bare infinitive. Thus English agent, act, actor, exact, and agenda all descend from the participial stem act-, while agile preserves the fuller present stem ag-. The same verbal system generated the frequentative agitare, "to set in motion repeatedly," the source of agitate and of the agitation of politics and crowds, and the iterative sense shows the root's core idea — driving, repeated driving — extending into the social and political sphere. The sound history of the root is the history of its laryngeal and its palatals. The PIE *h₂eǵ- shows the voiced palatal *ǵ, which in Greek became the voiced velar stop g (agein) and in Latin the voiced velar stop g (agere); the initial laryngeal *h₂ was lost in both languages in this environment, leaving the initial a. The root is thus one of the cleanest Indo-European equations: Sanskrit aj-, Greek ag-, Latin ag-, all "drive," with the palatal preserved as g in the western languages. When English borrowed agere's derivatives through Latin and French, it did so at several dates — agenda and agile through Renaissance Latin, actor and action through Old French and then Latin — so the English word family shows the whole chronological range of learned borrowing, from the medieval (act, through Old French acte) to the modern scientific (agentive, agitate, and, in the twentieth century, agenda as a noun of meetings). === CONCLUSION: THE COMPOUND AND ITS ENGLISH LIFE === The two roots meet in the Latin verb ambigere, literally "to drive both ways," attested in the second and first centuries BCE. The compound takes the "both/around" of amb- and the "drive" of agere and produces the image of something driven this way and that, unable to settle: hence ambigere "to hesitate, dispute, be in doubt," the adjective ambiguus "shifting, doubtful, double-faced," and the abstract noun ambiguitas "double meaning, uncertainty." The word travelled to English through French — Middle French ambiguite, attested in the fourteenth century, and Middle English ambiguitee in Chaucer's generation — and settled into the modern spelling ambiguity by the 1580s. For four centuries the word kept its negative rhetorical colouring: ambiguity was what a sophist exploited and a careful writer removed. William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) reversed that valuation, treating the double-driven word not as a flaw but as the engine of poetic meaning, so that the ancient image of the driven and undecided — the herd driven both ways at once — became, in the twentieth century, the celebrated source of literary richness. The word thus carries in its two Latin roots the whole story: the "both" of ambi- and the "drive" of agere, united to name the meaning that moves, like a driven herd, in two directions at the same time.
  • PIE *h₂embʰi- "around, both" → Greek amphi-, Sanskrit abhi-, Latin amb-/ambi- → the prefix of doubleness in the compound.
  • PIE *h₂eǵ- "drive" → Greek agein, Sanskrit ajati, Latin agere → the verb of action and motion in the compound.
  • Latin ambigere "to drive both ways, be in doubt" → ambiguus "of double meaning" → ambiguitas "double meaning" (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE).
  • Old French ambiguite (14th c.) → Middle English ambiguitee (Chaucer, late 14th c.) → English ambiguity (c. 1580s), revalued as a poetic virtue by Empson (1930).
