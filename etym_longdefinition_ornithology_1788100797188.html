Ornithology (from Greek ὄρνις, órnis, "bird", genitive ὄρνιθος, órnithos, + -λογία, -logía, "study, account") is the branch of zoology devoted to birds. A person who practises it is an ornithologist; the adjective is ornithological; the birds of a region, collectively, are its avifauna. The word entered English in the late 17th century as part of the great European explosion of "-ology" sciences, and it remains one of the most transparently built technical words in the language: "bird-account", the giving of a reasoned account of birds.

1. Core scope (primary sense). Ornithology today is not merely bird-watching (the amateur recreation, properly called "birding") but a full scientific discipline. Its principal branches include:

  • Systematics and taxonomy: naming, classifying, and reconstructing the evolutionary relationships of the roughly 11,000 living bird species, from ratites to the great passerine radiation of songbirds;
  • Avian biology and physiology: flight mechanics, feather structure and moult, the extraordinary avian respiratory system, endothermy, egg and nesting biology;
  • Behaviour and ethology: song and vocal communication, migration navigation, mate choice, lekking, tool use (as in crows), and social organisation;
  • Ecology and distribution: habitat requirements, feeding niches, population dynamics, and the biogeography of avian communities;
  • Conservation science: endangered-species recovery, disease monitoring (as with avian influenza), habitat restoration, and the use of birds as indicator species for ecosystem health;
  • Applied and modern methods: bird banding, radar and satellite tracking of migration, stable-isotope analysis of feathers, genomics, and vast citizen-science databases such as eBird.

2. The distinction from allied activities. Ornithology is the scientific study; birding/bird-watching is the recreational pursuit of observing birds; aviculture is the keeping and breeding of birds in captivity; falconry the sport of hunting with trained raptors; poultry science the applied study of domesticated birds. Professional ornithologists may do fieldwork, but they characteristically measure, test, publish, and theorise; the word carries institutional weight — university chairs, learned societies, and journals — and the "Professor of ornithology" of the source context is a recognisable type of the nineteenth-century learned scientist.

3. Lexical reach (secondary and tertiary senses). Because the two Greek roots are productive, and because the word itself carries prestige, "ornithology" and its neighbours develop satellite uses:

  • ornithomancy — divination by the flight and cries of birds, an ancient Greek and Roman practice (note that the ancient Greek word ὀρνιθολογία meant precisely this augury, not the modern science — the meaning of the Greek compound inverted when the science was born);
  • ornithopter — a flying machine with flapping wings, imagined from Leonardo da Vinci's designs onward;
  • ornithosis — a bird disease transmissible to humans;
  • figuratively, "a fine ornithological eye" denotes trained observational skill, applied by extension to any connoisseur-level noticing;
  • , humorously in student slang, "ornithology" sometimes puns on "bird course" — an easy elective, from the idiom "for the birds" (i.e., trivial) — a playful degradation of the word's learned dignity.

4. The semantic bridge. The modern meaning — the science of birds — is the result of a two-stage development. In ancient Greek, ὀρνιθολογία was the art of reading bird-signs: the Greeks and Romans governed countless private and public decisions by observing birds, and professional bird-readers interpreted omens in flight patterns and cries (the Latin auspex, "bird-watcher", gives us "auspices"). During the 16th–17th-century scientific revolution, European naturalists re-appropriated the Greek components with a new, empirical sense: "the systematic study of birds as natural objects". The word thus crossed a decisive bridge from divination to science — the same bridge travelled by astrology → astronomy and by physiology (from a Greek word for natural philosophy) — substituting observation for omen-reading while keeping the Greek clothing intact.

5. Institutional history. Coined in the 1670s (the adjective "ornithological" is attested 1656, the noun by 1673), ornithology grew through the encyclopaedic natural history of John Ray and Francis Willughby, the Linnaean system (1758), the great voyage collections of the 18th–19th centuries, Darwin's studies of finches, pigeons, and wing structure, the founding of the British Ornithologists' Union (1858) and the American Ornithologists' Union (1883), and the 20th century's turn to ecology, behaviour, and conservation. Today ornithology is a public-facing science: millions of citizen birders contribute data, and the discipline leads the world in demonstrating how a scientific vocabulary — "ornithology" — can pass from the augur's stool to the genomics laboratory while remaining, at its root, simply the study of the bird.
