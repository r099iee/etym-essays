Chain for CORONATION (single root):
1. Proto-Indo-European *(s)ker-, “to turn, bend, curve” (reconstructed; pre-3rd millennium BCE) — the root of curves, rings, and crowns; zero-grade *kor- underlies the extended stem *kor-ōn- “a curved thing.” Note: Beekes (EDG) allows that Greek korṓnē may be a Pre-Greek substrate word; the traditional PIE assignment (Pokorny, Etymonline) links it to *(s)ker-, and the recorded history from Greek onward is unaffected by the debate.
2. Greek κορώνη (korṓnē), “anything curved; a wreath, garland, crown; the hooded crow” (Classical period) — the curved thing par excellence, including the twisted circle of leaves set on a victor’s head, and the crow named for its curved beak.
3. Latin corōna, -ae, “garland, wreath, crown of victory” (1st c. BCE) — a borrowing from Greek korṓnē; the word of Roman military and civic crowns, later of the emperor’s crown.
4. Latin verb corōnāre, “to crown, to wreathe” (Roman period) — denominative verb from corōna.
5. Latin corōnātiō, -ōnis f., “a crowning” (Late/Medieval Latin) — the act-noun of crowning a sovereign; the form that reached the Romance languages.
6. Old French coronacion, “coronation” (13th c.) — the Gallo-Romance conduit into English.
7. English coronation (late 14th c.) — /ˌkɒrəˈneɪʃən/, “the ceremony of crowning a sovereign” ── OUR WORD.
Note: English took two parallel words from the same Latin stock — “crown” (12th c., from Anglo-Norman coroune) for the object, and “coronation” (late 14th c., from coronacion) for the ceremony; the “-ation” form kept the Latin spelling and its grand, learned ring.
