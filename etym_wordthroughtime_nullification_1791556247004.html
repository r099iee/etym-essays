- Late 16th to 17th Century: Scholastic and theological sense of treating something with utter contempt, reducing spiritual pride to nothingness, or viewing worldly pretensions as mere vanity - Perceived as a pious, moral, or philosophical act of humbling and debasing secular arrogance.
- Late 18th Century (1798): Emergence of constitutional theory in the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions, positing that sovereign states hold the inherent right to void unconstitutional federal mandates - Viewed by states' rights advocates as a vital safeguard of federalism and liberty, but by federalists as incipient treason and chaos.
- Early 19th Century (1832–1833): John C. Calhoun and the South Carolina Nullification Crisis over tariffs, bringing the young American republic to the brink of armed conflict - Acquired intensely fraught, explosive connotations of regional defiance, sectionalism, and existential peril for the Union.
- Mid to Late 19th Century: Post-Civil War constitutional consolidation; rejection of state nullification doctrines by the Supreme Court - Perceived politically as a discredited, unconstitutional relic of secessionism, while jury nullification gained prominence as a heroic or subversive weapon against oppressive statutes.
- 20th Century: Expansion into civil rights discourse, where the nullification of constitutional protections was seen as authoritarian state overreach; emergence of technical usage in contract law and computer science - Connotes profound institutional crisis, deliberate systemic erasure, or calculated refusal to submit to governing norms.
- 21st Century: Resurgence in political rhetoric surrounding state resistance to federal regulations, sanctuary jurisdictions, and digital data eradication - Evokes fierce polarized debate between decentralized autonomy and the rule of law.
