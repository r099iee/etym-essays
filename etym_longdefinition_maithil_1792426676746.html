Maithilī (Sanskrit: मैथिली, IAST: Maithilī; Maithili / Hindi pronunciation: [ˈmaɪtʰɪliː], Sanskrit pronunciation: [mɐɪtʰɪliː]) is an ancient and revered Sanskrit proper noun and relational patronymic meaning "she of Mithilā" or "the Princess of Mithilā", functioning across classical Indian civilization as one of the paramount, most emotionally charged epithets of Sītā, the divine heroine of the epic Rāmāyaṇa. In modern linguistics, culture, and geopolitics, it also identifies the ancient Eastern Indo-Aryan language spoken by over 35 million people across the Mithila region of northern Bihar (India) and the southeastern Tarai / Madhesh Province of Nepal, as well as the indigenous visual art tradition (*Madhubani* painting) and socio-cultural identity of that historic realm.

1. Primary Epic and Mythological Identity (Epithet of Sītā):

  • The Princess of King Janaka’s Realm: In Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa, Maithilī is an affectionate, royal, and sacred epithet for Sītā, celebrating her birth, childhood, and spiritual roots in the glorious kingdom of Mithilā (the capital of ancient Videha, modern Janakpur and surrounding regions). As the daughter of the philosopher-king Janaka (who uncovered her in a golden furrow while plowing the earth for a Vedic sacrifice), she embodies the sacred fusion of the earth’s primal fertility (*Ayonijā*) with the supreme spiritual wisdom of the Janaka royal lineage.
  • Symbol of Unbroken Resilience and Dignity: Throughout the tragic trials of the epic—from her blissful wedding in Mithilā to her arduous fourteen-year forest exile, her violent abduction by Rāvaṇa, her unyielding resistance against the demon king in the aśoka grove of Laṅkā, and her vindication in the ordeal of fire (*agniparīkṣā*)—she is repeatedly addressed as Maithilī by Rāma, Lakṣmaṇa, Hanumān, and the poet Vālmīki. The epithet serves as a persistent literary reminder of her royal nobility, philosophical fortitude, and unshakeable moral autonomy, showing that she is never a mere passive victim, but the proud daughter of Mithilā’s sovereign sages.
  • Devotional Adoration in Vernacular Traditions: In the vernacular devotional literature of North and East India (such as Tulsīdās’s Gītāvalī and Rāmcaritmānas, and the Bengali Kṛttivāsī Rāmāyaṇ), the name Maithilī evokes deep protective affection and tender beauty (*vātsalya* and *mādhūrya bhāva*). The women of Mithilā treat Rāma not merely as an awe-inspiring divine avatar, but playfully as their son-in-law (*pahuna*), viewing Maithilī as their beloved royal daughter whose honor is inextricably bound with the soil of Mithilā.

2. Polysemy, Linguistic Heritage, and Cultural Traditions:

  • The Maithili Language: In historical linguistics, Maithili is an Eastern Indo-Aryan language belonging to the Bihari sub-branch of the Indo-Iranian family, descended from the ancient Magadhi Prakrit and Magadhi Apabhraṃśa. It possesses an illustrious literary heritage dating back to the 14th century, immortalized by the sublime love lyrics and devotional songs (*Padāvalī*) of the master poet Vidyāpati (known as Maithil Kavi Kokil, "the cuckoo of Maithili poets"). Today, Maithili is an officially recognized language under the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution of India and the second most widely spoken language in the Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal.
  • Historical Scripts (Tirhuta and Kaithi): Historically, the Maithili language developed its own sophisticated calligraphic script known as Tirhuta (or Mithilākṣara), closely related to early Bengali and Assamese scripts, which was preserved for centuries by Maithil Brahmins and Kayasthas on palm-leaf manuscripts (*tāḍapatra*) for recording legal deeds, genealogies (*pañjī prabandha*), and philosophical treatises, alongside the cursive Kaithi script and modern Devanagari.
  • Mithila / Madhubani Art: The term Maithili also designates the world-renowned folk painting tradition cultivated for centuries by the women of the Mithila region. Traditionally painted onto mud walls of village courtyards during weddings, festivals (*Chhath*, *Madhushravani*), and religious ceremonies, Madhubani art features vibrant natural dyes, geometric stylization, prominent almond-shaped eyes, and mythological scenes depicting Maithilī (Sītā) and Rāma amidst blossoming sacred flora and fauna.
  • Socio-Cultural Identity: As a demographic noun, Maithilī (or Maithil) refers to any native inhabitant of the historic Mithila realm, celebrated for an enduring intellectual heritage in Navya-Nyāya (New Logic), Mīmāṃsā philosophy, and intricate culinary traditions.
