The story of REEK is the story of a neutral physical word turning negative — a word that once simply meant "to give off smoke or steam" and now means, almost always, to smell foully or to be saturated with something corrupt. It is a model case of semantic pejoration driven by association, and it has its roots in the oldest Germanic vocabulary of fire and vapour.
  • The Germanic inheritance: the word belongs to the common Germanic stock of verbs for smoking and steaming. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *reukaną "to smoke, to give off vapour" and the noun *raukaz "smoke, reek" are attested across the family — Old Norse rjūka, Old Saxon riokan, Middle Dutch roken, Old High German riohhan, and modern German rauchen "to smoke" — so English reek is not a loan but a native word with cognates in nearly every Germanic language. Notably, no secure PIE ancestor can be reconstructed; the family looks Germanic and possibly Balto-Slavic (Lithuanian rūkti "to smoke" has been compared), but the correspondences are not regular enough for handbooks to affirm a PIE root, and honest etymologizing stops at Proto-Germanic.
  • Old English rēocan and rēc: in Old English the verb rēocan (a class II strong verb) meant "to give off smoke, steam, or vapour", and the noun rēc meant "smoke, vapour". The word appears in the prose of glosses and homilies and in the verse of Beowulf, where smoke and reek rise from burning halls and where the mere's steaming water is described in the same vocabulary. The sense is entirely neutral: a fire reeks because it smokes, a hall reeks after burning, a pot reeks with steam — there is no moral or olfactory judgement in any of it. The word also had a technical, industrial life: to reek hides was to cure or dress leather by smoking, a routine craft process, which shows how everyday the word was.
  • Middle English rēken: the word continues unchanged in sense through Middle English. Rooms reek of heat, horses reek with sweat, cooking reeks with steam; the verb "to smoke" and "to steam" are synonyms of reek, and there is still no smell attached to the word. The great Middle English texts use reek freely of fires, chimneys, and bodily heat, and no reader would have found the word unpleasant. The modern spelling reek is fixed in this period by the regular development of the Old English vowel.
  • The seventeenth-century shift: the decisive change comes in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. From c. 1600 the verb begins to mean "to emit a strong smell, to smell strongly of something", and the noun gains the sense "a strong odour" alongside its smoke sense. The bridge is associative and physical: smoke and steam carry odours with them, so a thing that reeks (gives off vapour) comes to be perceived as reeking (giving off an odour) — and, because the odours carried by thick vapour are as often foul as not, the word begins to pick up a negative colour. The reek of a fire, of burning tallow, of a damp cellar, of sweat and smoke all share the same thick, enveloping character, and it is that character — density, saturation, unavoidability — that becomes the word's emotional core.
  • The nineteenth-century pejoration: by the nineteenth century the olfactory sense has become dominant in England, while the smoke-and-steam senses survive in Scottish and northern English usage and in the literary tradition that draws on them. Writers of the period use reek for the foul vapours of cities, slums, and drains; Dickens and the Victorian novelists make the word a sound effect of squalor — the reek of the London street, the reek of the prison, the reek of poverty itself. The word is by now almost wholly negative: no one says a thing "reeks" pleasantly.
  • The figurative explosion: once the word means "to smell foully", it is a short step to the modern figurative sense, "to be saturated with and betrayed by something bad". To "reek of hypocrisy", "of corruption", "of privilege", "of danger" is to be so obviously, overwhelmingly marked by the thing that nothing can conceal it — the moral odour is as unmistakable as the physical. This modern idiom dominates educated usage: it is a stock device of political and literary condemnation, and it keeps, in the moral register, the old physical idea of the reek that cannot be escaped or mistaken.
  • Tolkien and the literary smoke sense: in The Hobbit, Tolkien deliberately restores the older, physical, smoke-centred sense of the word. Bilbo's reek is the thick, blinding smoke of the goblins' fire — so dense it stings his eyes and veils the dancing goblins beyond it, "like people round a midsummer bonfire". The word is chosen for its archaic, saga-like register: the smoke sense is the northern, ballad sense, and Tolkien, a philologist, knew exactly that reek had once meant only smoke and steam. But the modern negative colour is not wasted: the reek of the goblin fire is both physically suffocating and morally foul, and the homely image of the midsummer bonfire applied to goblins sharpens the horror by twisting a familiar festivity into a threat. The word thus works on both of its historical layers at once — the old physical density and the modern foulness.
  • Socio-cultural valence and modern use: today reek is almost entirely negative. As a noun it means foul smoke or a foul smell; as a verb, "to reek of" means to be saturated by something bad, physically or morally. The neutral steam-and-smoke senses survive only in historical, dialectal, and deliberately archaic writing. The word is the standard example, in lexicography and in teaching, of pejoration — of a neutral word dragged downward by the company it keeps; and it shows how a single sensory fact (thick vapour carries strong smells) can, over three centuries of use, turn a craft term for curing hides into a word for moral corruption.
The career of reek thus runs from a neutral Germanic verb of smoking and steaming, through the everyday and industrial vocabulary of Old and Middle English, into the seventeenth-century association with strong smells, on to the nineteenth-century pejoration into foulness and squalor, and finally out into the modern figurative idiom of corruption — while retaining, beneath it all, the ancient image of the thick, blinding vapour that cannot be escaped, the image Tolkien reaches for when Bilbo's world fills with the goblins' fire.
