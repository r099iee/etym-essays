Latin talus 'ankle; anklebone (the astragalus); also the knucklebones used as dice in gambling — the 'tali'' (attested Classical Latin; no secure PIE etymon — see debate note below) → Late/Vulgar Latin *talō, *talōnem 'heel' (accusative of talo; a derived form built on talus, attested through its Romance descendants) → Old French talon 'heel' (of the foot, of a boot, of a stack of cards; attested from the 10th–12th c.) → Anglo-Norman/Middle English taloun, talon 'heel' (attested from c. 1300) → English talon, with the falconry transfer: the raptor's hindmost claw — the great hooked hallux, which sits where a heel would be — comes to be called the bird's 'heel' or talon (15th c., in hawking manuals) → English talon 'the hooked claw of a bird of prey' (generalized from the 16th c. to all the raptor's claws, then to mythic beasts and, figuratively, to grasping human hands) → plural talons → TALONS DEBATE / NUANCE: No secure Indo-European etymology for Latin talus exists; de Vaan records no convincing IE reconstruction, and a Mediterranean or other substrate origin is suspected. The word has no Greek cognate (Greek tálos means 'a young one, lad', unrelated), no Sanskrit cognate, and no Germanic cognate (English heel is a different root). The chain from Latin talus onward is well attested; only the ultimate origin of talus itself is genuinely unknown.
