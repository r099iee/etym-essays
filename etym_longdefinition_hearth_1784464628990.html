HEARTH (noun). Primary sense: the floor of a fireplace, made of stone, brick, or clay, upon which the fire is laid; by extension, the fireplace itself and the area immediately before it, where the household fire burns and meals are cooked. Symbolically — and this is the word's most important modern weight — the hearth is the home, the fireside, the domestic sanctuary: the warm centre of family life. Polysemy — all senses:
  • The physical fire-floor — the flat stone or brick surface at the bottom of a fireplace; also, by extension, the grate and surround of a modern fireplace. In houses without fireplaces the word may survive only for the decorative floor before the fire or as a historical term.
  • The fireplace and fireside — "to sit by the hearth", "the hearth and the chimney"; the whole domestic fire installation seen as one unit.
  • Metonymy: the home and household — from the fire-floor to the dwelling: "hearth and home", "the family hearth", "to forsake one's hearth". The hearth stands for the seat of the family, its warmth, its hospitality, and its property.
  • Symbol of nationhood and continuity — "the national hearth", "the ancestral hearth"; in poetry and oratory the hearth as the focus of belonging, lineage, and inherited identity.
  • Hearth tax (historical) — a tax levied on the number of hearths or fireplaces in a dwelling; in England the Hearth Tax was levied 1662–1689 ("hearth-money"). The surviving tax records are a standard demographic source.
  • Industrial metallurgy (open-hearth furnace) — since the 19th century the "hearth" is the floor of a reverberatory or smelting furnace on which the charge is placed and heated; the open-hearth furnace (Siemens–Martin process, 1860s) was the dominant steel-making method before the basic oxygen furnace.
  • Domestic architecture terms — hearthstone (the stone of the hearth; also the threshold of the home), hearthside, hearth rug, hearth fire.
Usage in current English: "hearth" appears chiefly in three places — (1) descriptions of houses and interiors ("the fire crackled on the hearth"), (2) the fixed phrase "hearth and home" with its poetic warmth, and (3) compounds like hearthstone, hearthside, and open-hearth in historical and industrial contexts. In Tolkien's sentence the word is used in its concrete sense: the coffee jug is set in the hearth to keep warm beside the fire, while the scene's whole cozy atmosphere carries the symbolic sense. Semantic reasoning and historical bridges: Old English heorð names the flat stone or clay floor of the household fire — the place where the fire literally lives. Because that fire was simultaneously the cooker, the heater, the light, and the social centre of the dwelling, the word took on the dwelling itself by the oldest of metonymies; "hearth" and "home" became interchangeable in the phrase "hearth and home". When industrial steel-making needed a word for the furnace floor on which its fire "lives", it reached for the same domestic term — the metallurgical sense is a technical extension of the primitive meaning "place of the fire", not a separate word. The modern split between fireplace floor and symbol of home is therefore two ends of one unbroken chain: fire-place → household → home → furnace floor.
