DOGMA, noun (plural dogmas, rarely dogmata). In the Gibbon passage the word appears in adjectival form, "dogmatic," and the context is a celebration of intellectual openness: Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, a work of unusual longevity, respectability, and popularity in the social sciences, is praised because it contains "so small a dogmatic or doctrinaire ingredient." The claim is that Gibbon's history does not propound sacred certainties or ask readers to accept unargued articles of faith; it argues, doubts, and reasons rather than decrees. A dogma, by contrast, is a principle or set of principles laid down by an authority as incontrovertibly true, closed to revision by evidence, and immune to debate. To be dogmatic is to refuse argument; to be doctrinaire is to apply a theory rigidly and unconditionally. Gibbon is credited with neither failing. The core sense of DOGMA:
  • An authoritative tenet. A principle or set of principles that an authority — a church, a school of thought, a political party, a ruler — declares to be beyond question. The essential feature is that dogma is not argued for but asserted; doubting it is treated as error or heresy.
  • Religious doctrine. In theology, a dogma is a doctrine revealed by God and formally defined and proclaimed by the magisterium or council of a church, requiring the assent of believers. Examples: the dogma of the Trinity, the dogma of the Immaculate Conception, the dogma of papal infallibility.
  • Institutional doctrine. The body of beliefs held as fixed by a secular institution or school of thought: party dogma, Marxist dogma, the dogma of the marketplace. Here the word often carries a note of disapproval, implying that belief is imposed rather than reasoned.
  • In science and philosophy. A once-unquestioned assumption or established theory treated as beyond challenge. The "central dogma" of molecular biology (Crick, 1958) states that genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein; the word was chosen partly in irony. In philosophy, dogma and dogmatism name the attitude of asserting principles without examination, opposed since the Enlightenment to the critical, sceptical temper.
Secondary and extended senses, with historical bridges:
  • Dogmatic (adjective). Of or pertaining to dogma; asserting opinions in an arrogant, unyielding manner. The Gibbon passage pairs it with doctrinaire: both mean stiff, opinionated, unopen to counter-argument, but dogmatic stresses refusal to question fixed truth while doctrinaire stresses rigid application of a doctrine to practical matters.
  • Dogmatism (noun). The tendency to lay down principles as incontrovertibly true without proper evidence; the habit of asserting rather than arguing.
  • Dogmatist (noun) / dogmatize (verb). One who asserts dogmas, and the act of asserting them. Dogmatize dates to the 17th century and is nearly always pejorative.
  • Dogmatics (noun). In theology, the systematic study and exposition of a religion's dogmas, especially in Protestant divinity schools (from German Dogmatik). A neutral, technical term.
The historical bridge that explains the polysemy: in Classical Greek, δόγμα (dogma) began as a philosophical term meaning "that which seems true to one; an opinion or belief" — the noun built on the verb δοκέω (dokéō), "to think, to seem, to suppose." A dogma was simply what one holds to be so. In Hellenistic Greek the word shifted toward official decree: the Septuagint and the New Testament use it for an edict or decree (Luke 2:1, a decree of Caesar Augustus; Acts 16:4, the decrees of the apostles). The early Church then took this official-sounding word for its defined doctrines, and so dogma travelled from "personal opinion" to "authoritative institutional decree." English borrowed the word in the 1540s directly from Latin dogma and Church Latin usage, already carrying the theological sense, and the later scientific and political uses extend the same structure: an assertion removed from debate. Usage and collocations:
  • Common collocations: religious dogma, party dogma, political dogma, central dogma, article of dogma, beyond dogma, dogma of the church.
  • Register: formal to semi-technical; in religious contexts neutral or positive, in political and scientific contexts usually critical.
  • Family words: dogmatic, dogmatism, dogmatist, dogmatize, dogmatics, doctrinaire (a sibling via Latin doctrina), doctrine (also from Latin doctrina).
  • Opposites: scepticism, critical thinking, empiricism, open-mindedness; the word dogmatic is routinely set against scientific and philosophical openness.
