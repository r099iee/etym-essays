In strict zoological terms, a whisker is a vibrissa — one of the long, stiff, deeply rooted tactile hairs that grow on the muzzle, cheeks, brows, and forelegs of most mammals. Unlike ordinary fur, each vibrissa sits in a dense follicle filled with blood sinuses and threaded with thousands of nerve endings, so that the merest deflection of the hair — by a draught, a blade of grass, or a wall edge — is instantly transmitted to the animal, furnishing a three-dimensional sense of “touch at a distance” that is especially keen in darkness. Cats, dogs, rats, and weasels use their whiskers to gauge the width of a gap, to read air currents, and to detect prey; in aquatic hunters such as seals and walruses the whole muzzle bristles with them. The scientific term vibrissa is Latin for a hair on the nostril.

Transferred to human beings, the word denotes the long hairs that grow about the mouth and cheeks — the moustache and the side-whiskers. And by a further figurative step it has come to mean the very smallest measurable degree of anything: to win “by a whisker” is to win by a hair’s breadth, and “not to stir a whisker” is to make no movement at all.

Polysemy and secondary senses.

  • Zoological sense (primary) — a vibrissa: a stiff, deeply rooted tactile hair on the muzzle and face of a mammal, used for sensing the environment; the plural “whiskers” is the usual form for the whole set.
  • Human facial hair — the moustache and the growth on the cheeks and chin; in older usage “whiskers” could mean the long side-hair (side-whiskers) and, loosely, a beard.
  • By a whisker — by the narrowest possible margin: “won by a whisker,” “passed by a whisker.” The image is of a hair’s breadth.
  • Not to stir a whisker — to make no movement or reaction at all; the idiom used by Tolkien in The Hobbit of the weasel’s complete stillness.
  • A whisker of a thing — colloquially, a tiny bit or a very small amount; the merest fraction.
  • Historical: a whisk-broom or one who whisks — the original, now rare, agent-noun sense from which the hair-sense developed in the 16th century.
  • In science and engineering — by extension, a fine hairlike crystal or filament (e.g., a metallic whisker growing on a surface), named from the animal hair; also the “cat’s whisker” of the early crystal radio, the thin wire that made contact with the crystal.

Historical bridge and formation. The word is built on the verb whisk — “to move with a quick sweeping stroke” — plus the agent suffix -er, and it originally meant “a thing that whisks,” a small whisk-broom. In the 1580s English speakers made the transfer that gives the word its modern life: the bristling hair of the upper lip looked like a tiny whisk-broom, and so the moustache — and later the side-whiskers of the Victorian gentry — was jocularly called a whisker. From human faces the name travelled back to the animal kingdom, where the long tactile hairs of cats, dogs, rats, and weasels became whiskers; the sensory hairs were named after the face-hair that was already called a whisker, not vice versa.

The word in context (The Hobbit). In J. R. R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit, Chapter 2 (‘Roast Mutton’), the narrator says of Bilbo’s prim, quiet approach that “I don’t suppose even a weasel would have stirred a whisker at it.” The idiom “to stir a whisker” means to make the faintest involuntary twitch — the single tiny movement that a vigilant creature cannot suppress when something is amiss. Tolkien chooses the weasel with care: no animal is more twitch-alert than a mustelid, whose whole life is guided by vibrissae that register the smallest movement in the grass. The narrator’s claim is comic hyperbole of praise: Bilbo’s creeping was so utterly unobtrusive that not even the finest sensory equipment in the woodland would have registered him.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. A moustache is specifically the hair above the lip; a beard is the hair of the chin and jaw; sideburns (side-boards) are the modern fashion term for the cheek-hair; bristles are short, stiff, coarse hairs (of a brush or a stubbled chin), whereas whiskers are long and fine. A vibrissa is the scientific term for the animal whisker. A filament or strand is the technical word for the hairlike object in engineering, of which the whisker is the vivid popular name.
