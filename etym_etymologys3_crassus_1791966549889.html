The name Crassus belongs to the oldest layer of Roman onomastics and to one of the most productive metaphors in the Latin lexicon: the transfer of physical thickness to mental dullness. Its history runs through the Roman naming system, the literary construction of the ideal orator, and the modern English adjective crass.

The Roman name system

  • By the middle Republic a male citizen of standing normally carried three names: a praenomen (Marcus, Lucius, Gaius), a nomen identifying the gens (Licinius, Antonius, Tullius) and a cognomen distinguishing the branch of the family (Crassus, Caesar, Cicero).
  • Cognomina began as descriptive bynames — hair colour, physique, a notable deed or defect — and then became hereditary, losing their descriptive force. A later Licinius Crassus need not have been stout at all.
  • The Licinii were a plebeian gens of great antiquity; by the first century BCE the Licinii Crassi were firmly senatorial and consular, and the cognomen advertised a specific, well-connected line.

The Latin adjective and its semantic field

  • Crassus is attested in early Latin and classical prose in the material sense "thick, solid, dense, fat": thick air or mist, thick blood, solid earth, coarse wool, fat meat.
  • Because density implies a lack of refinement, the adjective slides toward "coarse, gross, rude," and then toward "stupid, obtuse" — the same path taken by English thick and by dense in modern colloquial use.
  • The cognomen sits at the neutral, descriptive end of this spectrum. It named a physique, not a failing; the pejorative sense existed in the adjective but was not intended by the name.
  • The Latin noun crassitudo "thickness, density" and the medieval medical Latin crassamentum "clot" show the word's continued life in technical prose after the classical period.

Lucius Licinius Crassus and the making of a reputation

  • Born in 140 BCE, Crassus rose through the quaestorship and praetorship to the consulship of 95, which he held with Quintus Mucius Scaevola the Pontifex, the greatest jurist of the age.
  • As censor in 92 BCE with Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus he issued the edict closing the Latin schools of rhetoric, an episode reported by Suetonius and endlessly discussed by historians of Roman education.
  • Cicero, born in 106 BCE, was young enough to have heard him and old enough to remember; in the Brutus he presents Crassus and Marcus Antonius as the two consummate orators before his own rise, and in De Oratore he makes Crassus the champion of the union of eloquence and wisdom.
  • Crassus died in 91 BCE, in the year the dialogue is set, as the Social War was breaking out; his death and Antonius's murder four years later conveniently close the era that Cicero's dialogue commemorates.

The other Crassus and the split in the name's reference

  • Marcus Licinius Crassus (c. 115–53 BCE), the orator's nephew, became the richest man in Rome, crushed the slave army of Spartacus in 71 BCE, and formed the First Triumvirate with Caesar and Pompey in 60 BCE.
  • His disastrous Parthian campaign ended at Carrhae in 53 BCE, where he was killed and his legions destroyed — an event that haunted Roman memory and gave the name a second, darker association.
  • English literature and proverb know this Crassus: "as rich as Crassus" and the stock figure of the wealthy, overreaching plutocrat derive from the triumvir, not the orator.
  • Classical scholarship therefore disambiguates: "Crassus the Orator," "L. Licinius Crassus," or "Crassus the triumvir." The bare name is a trap for the unwary reader of Cicero.

English reception and the afterlife of crass

  • English acquired the proper name through Renaissance translations of Cicero and Plutarch and through histories of Rome; it remains a learned, bookish term with no popular currency.
  • The adjective crass entered English in the sixteenth century, probably through French crasse and learned Latin, meaning "gross, coarse, stupid, insensitive"; crassly, crassness and crassitude followed.
  • Modern usage of crass is strongly pejorative and typically moral: crass behaviour, crass materialism, a crass remark. The physical sense of thickness has vanished from English, leaving only the judgement.
  • The proper name and the adjective have thus drifted apart: Crassus is a neutral historical label, while crass is an insult — a split that mirrors the Latin word's own double life.

Why it matters in this reading: the quoted passage is a study in authorial voice. Cicero distributes his own opinions among Crassus, Antonius and the aged Scaevola, and the question of whether Crassus "may well be identified as the spokesman of Cicero" governs the interpretation of the whole dialogue. Knowing that this is Lucius Licinius Crassus the orator — censor, consul, defender of a philosophically grounded eloquence — and not the triumvir of Carrhae, is the first condition for reading the argument correctly.
