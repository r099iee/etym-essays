- 14th century - Middle English famen/afamen borrowed from Anglo-Norman afamer 'to starve' - the literal, grim sense: to die or kill by hunger
- 15th-17th centuries - famished as an adjective means 'starved, wasted by hunger, at the point of death from want'; used of the poor, prisoners, besieged cities, and starving animals
- 18th century - as famine recedes from ordinary English life the word begins to soften toward 'extremely hungry' while keeping its serious register for real starvation
- 19th century - literature exploits both registers: Dickens's starving poor and Kingsley's 'famished and savage look' are sincere; 'I am famished' for a missed dinner becomes homely hyperbole
- 20th-21st centuries - the modern double life is fixed: sincere in famine, war, and animal-welfare contexts; warm, emphatic exaggeration ('I'm famished!') in everyday speech for a hearty appetite
