- Late 17th Century (French Neoclassical Court) - Celebrated as the ultimate hallmark of aristocratic taste, courtly urbanity, and humanist refinement, distinguishing polished literature from dry scholastic pedantry.
- 18th Century (Scottish Enlightenment & British Academic Institutionalization) - Adopted into university professorships (notably by Adam Smith and Hugh Blair) as a foundational academic subject combining rhetoric, literary criticism, and the cultivation of refined taste.
- 19th Century (Romantic and Victorian Eras) - Narrowed from general humane learning to imaginative, non-utilitarian literature (poetry, drama, and discursive essays), standing as an aesthetic sanctuary against industrial utilitarianism.
- Early to Mid-20th Century (Modernism & New Criticism) - Suffered a sharp reputational decline among academic scholars, who castigated the term as emblematic of superficial, impressionistic, and gentlemanly dilettantism.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Preserved primarily in antiquarian bookselling, library classification systems, and historical retrospectives as a nostalgic, slightly archaic designation for elegant essays, literary memoirs, and stylistic miscellanies.
