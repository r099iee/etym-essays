Beech is the common English name for trees of the genus Fagus, above all the European beech (Fagus sylvatica), a large, stately deciduous tree of the temperate woodlands, recognisable at once by its smooth, silver-grey bark, its wavy-edged oval leaves, and its small triangular edible nuts — the beech-mast — which are borne in pairs within prickly husks and feed both woodland animals and, in the old husbandry, droves of pigs. The wood is close-grained, heavy, and hard, superb for furniture, tool handles, bowls, and turnery; and for the fire it is one of the finest of all fuels: seasoned beech burns with a clear, steady flame, throws out intense, long-lasting heat with a deep bed of glowing embers, and makes little smoke, which is why it has been prized for centuries for cooking fires, baking ovens, and the smoke-houses where herring and bacon were cured.

The word carries an enormous load of old culture. The Germanic peoples cut their runic inscriptions on smooth tablets of beech wood, and the very word for the tree became the word for “book” — Old English bōc “book,” German Buch, Old Norse bók. The connection is preserved in German Buchstabe, “letter,” literally “beech-stave.” English keeps the double destiny in its own vocabulary: beech (the tree) and book (the writing material and, by extension, the written work) are descendants of the same ancient word, and this is one of the most famous etymological pairings in the language.

Polysemy and secondary senses.

  • The tree — a large smooth-barked deciduous tree of the genus Fagus, especially F. sylvatica; also the American beech (F. grandifolia) of North America, and other species in Asia.
  • The wood / timber — the close-grained, hard, pale wood of the tree, used for furniture, flooring, tool handles, bowls, turned ware, and fuel; in the furniture trade “beech” is a standard, economical hardwood.
  • Beech-mast — the small triangular nuts of the beech, a major food for woodland animals and, in traditional pannage, for pigs driven into the woods to fatten.
  • Beechwood / beech-wood — a wood or grove of beeches; also the timber.
  • Beech-log — a cut trunk or log of beech, prized as firewood; the exact collocation used by Tolkien of the trolls’ fire.
  • Attributive and compound uses — beech-bark, beech-leaf, beech-nut, beech-copper (a copper beech, a purple-leaved cultivar), beech-fern (a plant), beech-drops (an American parasitic herb).
  • The “beech argument” in linguistics — the famous inference, drawn from the fact that the wild beech does not grow east of a line through Poland and western Ukraine, that the Indo-European homeland lay west of that line, if the reconstructed PIE word really named this tree; the argument is famous but debated.
  • In place-names — beech was a common tree of English lowland woodland, and its Old English name lies behind many place-names (Bexhill, Bexley, Beechwood, and similar) from the tree’s abundance.

The word in context (The Hobbit). In J. R. R. Tolkien’s The Hobbit, Chapter 2 (‘Roast Mutton’), the dwarves and Bilbo come upon “three very large persons sitting round a very large fire of beech-logs.” The detail is quietly functional: beech-logs give the even, ember-rich heat needed to toast a joint of mutton slowly without scorching it, and they throw off so little smoke that the meat is not tainted — the trolls, for all their brutishness, have chosen the connoisseur’s fuel. The description grounds a scene of high fantasy in homely English woodland craft, and the cosy, wholesome glow of the fire is set in deliberate contrast with the menace of the three enormous figures who sit round it.

Distinguish the near-synonyms. The birch (Betula) has a superficially similar smooth pale bark, but it is a lighter, faster-growing wood that crackles and burns away quickly with little lasting coal. The oak (Quercus) is the other great English hardwood and mast tree, but it is heavier and more durable, with a gnarled, fissured bark, and it served the shipwright and the builder where beech served the craftsman and the cook. The hornbeam (Carpinus) is a smooth-trunked tree of the same family (Fagales), but its wood is still harder and is chiefly used for mill-work and cogs. A beechwood is a grove or forest of beeches; “beech” alone names the single tree.
