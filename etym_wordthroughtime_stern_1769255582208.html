- Old English (styrne) — The word is already fully abstract at its earliest attestation: 'severe, strict, hard, cruel, inflexible,' applied to God, to laws, and to hard-hearted rulers; its permanent image is that of a thing that does not bend.
- Middle English (sterne) — A favourite of moral and religious writers, who use it of God's judgements and of unyielding authority; 'sternness' reads as moral and emotional rigidity, the human posture of a thing held stiff.
- Early modern broadening — The adjective spreads across the whole range of authority — faces, looks, teachers, captains, laws — keeping its cold, unsmiling temperature; meanwhile an unrelated homograph, the noun 'stern' ('back of a ship,' from Old Norse), settles beside it, sharing the form but never the meaning.
- Contemporary sense — 'Stern' remains in firm use for persons, voices, and institutions that show no indulgence, but it now carries a formal, slightly old-fashioned dignity; everyday speakers reach instead for the cooler, more neutral 'strict,' reserving 'stern' for the grim and unrelenting.
