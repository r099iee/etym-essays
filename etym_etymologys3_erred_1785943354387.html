A root of motion: the Indo-European inheritance. The history of "erred" begins not with mistakes but with motion. The reconstructed root *ers-, "to be in motion, to wander," is one of those ancient verbal bases that set the body and the mind in movement at once, and its descendants scattered across the Indo-European languages testify to the root's age and vitality. In Germanic it produced a family of words for agitation and anger — Old English eorre "wrath," Old Norse jǫrask "to grow angry" — because the agitated mind, set in motion, was conceived as straying or seething out of its proper calm. In Italic it produced the verb errāre, "to wander, to rove," attested in the earliest Roman comedy: Plautus and Terence use it of men wandering through streets, of the mind straying, of things gone astray. Already in Latin the double career of the word is complete — errāre means both "to wander physically" and "to be mistaken," because Roman thinkers, like every people, found the metaphor of the mind as a traveller natural: to think wrongly is to have left the true road. Rome and the wandering knight. From Latin errāre came a rich family. The noun error "a wandering; a mistake" gave English both "error" and "erroneous." The adjective errāticus "wandering" gave "erratic." And the verb itself passed into Old French as errer, "to wander, to travel, to roam," which the medieval imagination seized upon: the chevalier errant, the knight errant, is the knight who wanders the roads in search of adventure, and the whole medieval genre of romance — from Chrétien de Troyes to Malory — is populated by these wandering riders. It is a pleasant irony that the ancestor of our formal word for "making a mistake" was the daily verb of the adventurous knight: to err was first to ride out, and the ride out could go wrong. Into English: two senses at once. Middle English borrowed errer as erren in the 13th century, and from the beginning the English verb carried both halves of the Latin inheritance. In Middle English devotional and moral writing, "to err" meant to stray from the path of right conduct, to sin, to fall away from the true faith — the language of the sermon and the confessional. In legal and administrative writing, "to err" meant to make a mistake, to be wrong about a fact or a judgment. The two senses coexisted for centuries: "errant" remained a living word for "wandering" well into the modern period, and "erratic" still means "wandering in its course." Gradually, however, the physical sense withered. By the 18th century "to err" as "to wander" survived only in the fossilized phrase "knight errant" and in deliberately archaic poetry; the living verb was the intellectual one, "to be mistaken." The modern word "erred" is therefore the tip of an old iceberg: beneath the plain past tense — "I have erred, my lord" — lies the whole history of the wandering knight and the straying mind. The moral and religious register. The word never lost its moral colouring. Christianity made "erring" a central image: the straying sheep of the parable, the soul that errs from God's path, the "erring" that requires forgiveness. When Alexander Pope wrote "To err is human, to forgive divine" in 1711, he distilled a millennium of religious usage into an epigram, and the line so perfectly caught the word's double register — factual mistake and moral failure — that it became proverbial, permanently fixing "err" in the dignified vocabulary of fallibility. In the same register, the medieval and early modern confessional formula — "I have erred" — carried the weight of acknowledged sin as well as mistake. The card's sentence, "I have erred, my lord," stands squarely in this tradition: it is a confession that can be read as a simple admission of a mistake or as the humble acknowledgment of a moral straying, and the word keeps both readings alive. Modern sense and idiom. In modern English the centre of gravity has settled on the intellectual and legal senses. Courts "err" in their rulings, and appellate judges write "the court erred in its application of the law"; historians, economists, and scientists "err" in their calculations and theories. The word has produced a distinctive modern idiom: "to err on the side of caution" (or safety, mercy, generosity), meaning deliberately to choose the safer or kinder option when in doubt — an ironic use in which "err" no longer means "be wrong" but "incline," the danger of error being acknowledged and deliberately courted in the direction of safety. Phonologically the word has been stable: the Middle English erren became modern err, pronounced with the short vowel of "her" (RP /ɜː/ before r), and the weak past "erred" (/ɜːd/) is formed by the ordinary -ed suffix that English uses for the vast majority of its verbs. The word thus unites an archaic soul — the wandering knight, the straying soul, the Roman wanderer — with a perfectly regular modern grammar, and its dignity, its formality, and its deep image of the road left behind have kept it in honoured use whenever a mistake needs to be named with gravity.
