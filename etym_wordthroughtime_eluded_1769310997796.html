- 16th c.: borrowed from Latin "eludere" ("to parry, deceive, escape") — to slip away from capture by trickery or dexterity; a clever, nimble register.
- 17th–18th c.: extends from physical escape to intellectual escape — that which eludes comprehension, memory, or pursuit; gains an elegant, elusive tone.
- 19th c.: a favourite of novelists for graceful, mocking evasion — Austen's Mr. Bennet eludes his family's questioning as if it were a game of skill.
- Contemporary: mostly abstract — answers, memories, and suspects "elude" us; retains a formal, slightly literary elegance beside the plainer "escape" and "evade".
