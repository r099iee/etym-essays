- 17th Century - Enters Early Modern English theological discourse from Late Latin and French; exclusively characterizes the saving sacrifice of Jesus Christ in delivering fallen humanity from eternal damnation and original sin.
- 18th Century - Broadens into moral philosophy and Enlightenment literature; applied to heroic human acts of self-sacrifice, forgiveness, and moral restitution that reclaim individuals from public disgrace or ethical error.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Flourishes in Romantic poetry, Victorian melodrama, and social reform movements; denotes the purifying, moralizing power of romantic love, maternal devotion, and charitable works in elevating fallen individuals.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Radically transformed by modernist aesthetics and existentialism (Marcel Proust, Walter Benjamin, Theodor Adorno); designates the power of art, memory, and voluntary creation to rescue human consciousness from the destructive, entropic devastation of historical time.
- Late 20th Century - Pervades psychology, cinema studies, and popular culture; crystallizes into common critical tropes such as the "redemptive character arc" and the psychological concept of "redemptive personal narratives" in post-traumatic resilience.
- 21st Century - Ubiquitous across restorative justice discourse, cultural criticism, and everyday speech; denotes transformative systemic healing, ex-offender rehabilitation, or the singular saving grace within an otherwise flawed undertaking.
