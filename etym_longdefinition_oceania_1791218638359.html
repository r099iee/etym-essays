Oceania is a proper geographical name for the vast island region of the central and southern Pacific Ocean together with Australia, New Zealand and New Guinea — in short, the lands and seas between Asia and the Americas that are neither Asian mainland nor American mainland. In the source sentence it denotes the culture area whose natives made real houses of carefully cut boards, the culmination of wooden-dwelling evolution.

Unlike Europe or Asia, which are ancient, Oceania was deliberately coined around 1812 and adopted into English in the 1840s. It is therefore a learned, cartographic term before it is a lived identity; few Pacific Islanders historically called themselves Oceanian, though the label is now embraced in regional institutions, sport and education.

  • Core geographic sense: the UN geoscheme region comprising Australasia — Australia, New Zealand — plus Melanesia — Fiji, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia — Micronesia — Kiribati, Palau, Marshall Islands, Federated States of Micronesia, Nauru — and Polynesia — Samoa, Tonga, Tuvalu, Cook Islands, French Polynesia, Hawaii, Rapa Nui. Example: Fiji is in Oceania.
  • Culture-area sense: Oceania as an anthropological field — Oceanic art, Oceanic navigation, Oceanic houses — contrasting Lapita-derived Austronesian traditions with Papuan ones. The source uses it exactly so: the natives of Oceania as master woodworkers.
  • Sporting and institutional sense: Team Oceania, Oceania Football Confederation, Miss Oceania, Oceania Athletics. Here Oceania functions as a continental team bloc like Europe or Africa.
  • Literary-dystopian sense: Orwell's Oceania in Nineteen Eighty-Four — the superstate comprising the Americas, Britain, Australia and southern Africa, perpetually at war with Eurasia or Eastasia. Students must distinguish Orwell's Oceania from the real Pacific Oceania; context always disambiguates.
  • Extended commercial sense: Oceania Cruises, Oceania House — where the name evokes exotic seas, travel and leisure, trading on Pacific imagery.

Boundaries and competitors: Oceania overlaps but is not identical with Australasia — Australia, New Zealand and sometimes New Guinea — Pacific Islands — often excluding Australia — South Pacific — a vaguer tourist term — and Asia-Pacific — a business bloc adding East and Southeast Asia. Textbooks now prefer Pacific or Moana Pacific language in Indigenous-centred curricula, while retaining Oceania for statistics.

Polysemy bridge: from sea-name to sea-region to superstate, every sense keeps the idea of watery vastness. Greek Ōkeanos encircled the world; Malte-Brun's Océanie encircled the Pacific; Orwell's Oceania encircles power across seas. The suffix -ia — as in Australia, Polynesia, Utopia — turns water into place and place into polity, which is why one word can name atolls, a cruise line and a nightmare state.
