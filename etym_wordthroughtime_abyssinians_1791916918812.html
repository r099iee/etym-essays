- 1630s — English writers adopt Abyssinia from Modern Latin and Arabic sources; Abyssinian names the subjects of the Ethiopian emperor, a distant Christian kingdom of great antiquarian interest.
- 18th c. — Travel literature, above all James Bruce's Travels to Discover the Source of the Nile (1790), fixes the word in the English imagination as a land of ancient Christian monarchy and exotic custom.
- 19th c. — The British expedition to Magdala (1868) makes "Abyssinian" a newspaper word; it also hardens racial and ethnographic classifications that treat the highland people as a distinct "type".
- 1896–1930s — The victory at Adwa and Ethiopia's admission to the League of Nations keep "Abyssinia" in diplomatic use alongside "Ethiopia"; the Italo-Ethiopian crisis of 1935–36 makes it a global headline word.
- Mid-20th c. — Rastafari gives "Abyssinia" a sacred, diasporic resonance, while official and scholarly usage increasingly prefers "Ethiopia" and "Ethiopian".
- Contemporary — The term survives as a historical exonym, in the Abyssinian cat breed and the Abyssinian well, and in older literature; for the people themselves, Ethiopian or Habesha is now the normal choice.
