The adjective Omnipotent is an elevated Latinate compound whose etymological history unites two foundational elements of classical Latin morphology: the distributive adjective omnis ("all, every, the whole") and the participial adjective potens ("powerful, capable, mighty").

Morphological Analysis of the Constituent Roots:

  • The Second Element (*pótis $\rightarrow$ potens): The root of potens is the Proto-Indo-European noun/adjective *pótis, meaning "master, lord, husband, ruler", and by extension "possessing the capacity or power to act".
    • In Sanskrit, this root produced páti- ("master, lord, husband"); in Ancient Greek, $\pi\acute{o}\sigma\iota\varsigma$ (pósis, "husband") and $\delta\epsilon\sigma\pi\acute{o}\tau\eta\varsigma$ (despótēs, "master of the house", whence despot); in Lithuanian, pàts ("self, master").
    • In Proto-Italic, *potis combined with the copula *es- to form *pote-sum $\rightarrow$ Latin posse ("to be able"). The present active participle of this verb system formed potens (genitive potentis, "having power, mighty").
  • The First Element (omnis): Latin omnis (of debated PIE origin, possibly from *op-ni-, "abundant, working", from PIE *h₃ep-, "work, abundance", or an Italic substrate) served as the universal quantifier meaning "all, every".

Classical Latin Synthesis and the Imperial Epic Tradition:

  • In classical Latin, compounding omnis + potens produced the majestic compound adjective omnipotēns (genitive omnipotentis).
  • In pagan Latin epic poetry, omnipotens was utilized by Virgil in the Aeneid and Ovid in the Metamorphoses as a solemn, thunderous title for Jupiter ("Iuppiter omnipotens, precibus si flecteris ullis...") and Juno (Iuno omnipotens).

Christian Vulgate and Patristic Latin Integration:

When early Christian scholars and St. Jerome translated the Scriptures into Latin in the 4th century CE (the Vulgate), they selected omnipotens to translate two paramount biblical concepts:

  • The Hebrew divine title El Shaddai (אֵל שַׁדַּי, traditionally rendered "God Almighty").
  • The Hellenistic Greek title Pantokratōr ($\Pi\alpha\nu\tau\mathrm{o}\kappa\rho\acute{\alpha}\tau\omega\rho$, literally "All-Ruler" / "Ruler of the Universe") in the Septuagint and the New Testament Book of Revelation.
  • The opening lines of the Nicene and Apostles' Creeds firmly embedded the term into daily Christian liturgy: "Credo in Deum Patrem omnipotentem..." ("I believe in God the Father Almighty...").

Transmission through Old French to Middle English:

  • In Gallo-Romance, the word entered Old French as a learned theological borrowing: omnipotent (c. 12th century).
  • It was borrowed into Middle English in the early 14th century (c. 1325–1350), appearing in religious lyrics, Cursor Mundi, and the works of Geoffrey Chaucer (such as The Second Nun's Tale and The Man of Law's Tale).
  • Throughout the Middle English period, omnipotent functioned almost exclusively in religious discourse as the ultimate epithet for the Creator.

Early Modern English, Epic Poetry, and Enlightenment Philosophy:

In the 16th and 17th centuries, the word reached the pinnacle of poetic expression in English literature. In John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667), the word recurs with monumental force to describe the absolute sovereignty of God over rebel angels and cosmic chaos.

  • During the 18th-century Enlightenment, essayists like Joseph Addison in The Spectator employed omnipotent to demonstrate that true moral peace and courage flow from trust in an all-powerful, benevolent Deity who governs the universe with perfect justice.
  • Phonetically, the Latin stress on the antepenultimate syllable was preserved in the English Received Pronunciation /ɒmˈnɪp.ə.tənt/.
  • Today, omnipotent stands as the quintessential term for infinite, boundless, and cosmic power.
