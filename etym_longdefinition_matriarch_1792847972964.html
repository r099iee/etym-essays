A matriarch is the female head of a family, clan, tribe, or social group: the grandmother, dowager, or elder whose word is law, whose memory holds the family archive, and around whom generations gather. In the source sentence Sméagol's grandmother is guessed to have been a matriarch, a great person in her way: the formidable old ruling mother of her river-bank people, credited in family legend with power and treasure far beyond the plausible.

Core meaning in detail: the matriarch rules not by election but by age, kinship, and force of character. She keeps the family stories, settles its quarrels, guards its rituals, and distributes approval the way patriarchs distribute inheritance. The word carries warmth as well as weight: a matriarch is loved and obeyed at once, feared a little by the young, appealed to by all. Anthropology reserves the term for societies where descent and authority genuinely pass through the female line, but everyday English lends it freely to any commanding grandmother.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Female head of a family or clan (primary modern sense): the ruling grandmother or eldest woman, from Victorian dowagers to immigrant founding mothers. The bridge is direct composition: Latin mater, mother, raised onto the throne of Greek archein, to rule, in deliberate parallel with patriarch.
  • Female founder or ruling ancestress (historical): the biblical Sarah or Rebekah, the legendary queen-mothers of tribes. The bridge extends headship backward into origins: the matriarch is not only the current ruler but the root from which the whole line claims to grow.
  • Dominant breeding female of an animal group (zoology): the elephant matriarch who leads the herd to water she remembers from fifty years back, the orca grandmother who feeds her grandchildren. The bridge is behavioural: researchers saw leadership, memory, and care concentrated in one old female and borrowed the human title.
  • Venerable senior woman of an institution (figurative): the matriarch of a theatre company, of a fashion house, of a political movement. The bridge carries family authority into chosen families: long service plus commanding presence earns the crown without blood kinship.
  • Ecclesiastical and honorific uses (rare): in a few traditions the wife or widow of a patriarch, or a titled abbess, styled matriarch. The bridge is courtesy analogy: where a church knows patriarchs, their female counterparts take the matching name.
  • Feminist and theoretical — matriarchy (derived system noun): the hypothesised or advocated social order ruled by mothers. The bridge runs from the person to the polity: one commanding grandmother becomes the model for a whole imagined constitution, debated fiercely by anthropologists.

Register and connotation: matriarch is respectful and slightly grand, redolent of Sunday dinners, inherited jewellery, and unargued seating plans. It flatters where dowager might chill and where old woman would insult: to call someone a matriarch is to acknowledge a reign. The word can turn arch or ironic when applied to bossy hostesses and family tyrants, but even the irony confesses power. Gandalf's use is half-amused: greatness in her way concedes the river-bank kingdom while doubting the Elven-rings.
