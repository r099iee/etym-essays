SNAG is a noun meaning a sharp, jagged, or projecting point — a rough, protruding object, such as a broken branch, a stump, a root, or an irregular tooth, on which passing things catch, tear, or stumble. The word's core image is of an obstruction that is at once sharp and leftover: something that once belonged to a larger structure (a tree, a branch, the floor of a cave) and now stands as a hidden point waiting to catch whatever comes against it. The physical snag is a hazard of the wilderness and the river — a submerged log that holes a boat, a splinter that tears a stocking, a spur of rock that trips a runner in the dark.

In The Hobbit, the word appears at a moment of pure panic. Bilbo, terrified and trying to run faster through the goblin tunnels, "struck his toes on a snag in the floor, and fell flat with his little sword under him." The snag is a hard, unyielding projection of rock or root rising invisibly out of the cave floor — concealed in the darkness, sharp enough to hurt, cunningly placed to catch the unwary foot. The word's tone is exactly right for the moment: it is the language of concealed hazard, of a trap the darkness hides. Bilbo does not trip on an obstacle he could have seen; he trips on a snag — something that caught him precisely because it was hidden.

The word's senses, past and present:

  • Physical snag (primary): a sharp, jagged projection or point — a broken branch, a protruding root, a stump, a splinter, an irregular tooth. Something on which cloth tears, skin catches, or feet stumble.
  • Nautical/timber snag (historical core): a tree-trunk, stump, or large branch fixed in the bed of a river or lake, dangerous to boats; the great destroyer of Mississippi steamboats in the nineteenth century.
  • Idiomatic "hit a snag": to meet an unexpected difficulty, hitch, or obstruction that halts or delays progress — "the project hit a snag." Here the physical obstruction becomes an abstract obstacle in a plan.
  • Snag (verb), physical: to catch, tear, or damage on a sharp projection — "she snagged her stocking on a nail."
  • Snag (verb), nautical: to run aground on or be holed by a snag — "the boat snagged on a submerged log" (1807, American).
  • Snag (verb), slang (1890s, American): to seize, grab, or obtain quickly and opportunistically — "snag a bargain," "snag a seat." The catching is reversed: the hidden catcher becomes the quick grabber.
  • Project management and software: a "snag" is a minor unexpected difficulty, flaw, or bug; a "snag list" (or punch list) itemises small defects to be fixed before completion.
  • Snaggletooth / snaggle-toothed: of a tooth — irregular, projecting, broken-looking (from the 1600s), keeping the oldest physical sense alive.

The word has no demonstrated Indo-European ancestry. It belongs to the vocabulary of the North and West Germanic seas: the Proto-Germanic base *snag- "projecting point, knob" appears in Old Norse snagi, Norwegian dialect snag "sharp point," and Middle Low German snagge "branch, broken tooth." English took the word up around 1570, first as a timber and seafaring term, and the nineteenth-century American frontier — the age of the river steamboats — carried it into the national idiom. A snag is thus a word of the water carried onto land, and finally into the abstract reaches of bureaucracy and software: the sharp point that was once a sunken log now lies in wait in a schedule, a budget, or a line of code, still catching the unwary.
