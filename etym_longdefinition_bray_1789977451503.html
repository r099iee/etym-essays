bray in the source sentence — Suddenly he stretched his neck and began to bray — is the verb for the loud, harsh, two-toned cry of a donkey or mule, conventionally rendered hee-haw: a grating inhaled hee followed by an explosive exhaled haw, produced with outstretched neck, flattened ears, and bared teeth. It is one of the most imitative and comic animal words in English, instantly conjuring stubbornness, rural poverty, and pathos — exactly the dying donkey's last protest in Maupassant's poaching episode.

As both verb and noun, bray covers the animal cry and its human echoes. Donkeys bray from hunger, loneliness, fear, or dawn habit; mules bray more rarely and harshly. By transfer, people are said to bray when they laugh loudly and gratingly (a braying laugh), speak in a loud nasal upper-class voice (a braying Sloane), or when brass instruments blare coarsely (trumpets brayed). The noun names each instance: a loud bray, a mournful bray, the bray of the donkey at dawn.

  • 1. Donkey / mule cry (verb intransitive, core sense): The donkey brayed loudly; He stretched his neck and began to bray. Often with manner adverbs: to bray mournfully, piteously, loudly. The posture — neck stretched, ears flat — is part of the lexical image, as the text notes.
  • 2. Harsh human laugh or voice (verb, derogatory): He brayed with laughter; She brayed orders at the servants; to bray like a donkey. Implies loudness, nasality, lack of refinement, and unwanted dominance. British satire uses braying for upper-class loudness and for political heckling.
  • 3. Harsh sound of instrument (verb): Trumpets brayed; The saxophone brayed. Brass or amplified sound that is powerful but coarse, lacking sweetness. Biblical braying trumpets at Jericho echo here.
  • 4. The cry itself (countable noun): to give a bray; to utter a bray; a sudden bray broke the silence. Plural brays. Used for both animal and human/instrumental senses.
  • 5. Homonym — to pound or grind (verb transitive, technical, now rare): to bray roots in a mortar; brayed pigments. From a different Old French root meaning to crush. Modern readers meet it mainly in the King James Bible (Though thou shouldest bray a fool in a mortar, Proverbs 27:22) and in pharmacy history. It is etymologically distinct but spelled identically, and must not be confused with the donkey cry.

Phonetically bray /breɪ/ belongs with neigh, whinny, bleat, low, moo — the small set of specific animal-voice verbs. Unlike generic cry, call, sound, it encodes species, timbre, and comedy. Dictionaries render it as a harsh, grating cry; field guides stress volume (audible over a kilometre) and function (contact call, alarm, mating display). Knowing this helps the Maupassant scene: the donkey, exhausted, stiff-jointed, bleeding, still stretches its neck for the species-typical call — a last assertion of life.

Connotation is double-edged. Literally it evokes pity, rural labour, and stubborn endurance; figuratively applied to humans it is almost always mocking — to call someone's laugh a bray is to call them asinine. Biblical and literary uses exploit both: Balaam's ass, Bottom's ass-head in A Midsummer Night's Dream, Sterne's donkeys, and Orwell's Benjamin all bray to signal humble truth against human folly. The dying donkey's bray in the story therefore resonates beyond naturalism into fable.
