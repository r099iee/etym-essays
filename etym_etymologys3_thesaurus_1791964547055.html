Thesaurus is a Greek word that has travelled from the temple treasury to the word processor, gathering meanings at every stage without ever losing the metaphor of stored wealth.

The Greek treasury

Greek thēsauros is attested from the fifth century BCE in the sense “treasure, store” and also “treasury building”. At Delphi, Olympia and Delos the thēsauroi were small monumental chapels built by individual cities to house their votive offerings — the Athenian Treasury at Delphi, raised from the spoils of Marathon, is the most famous. The word therefore begins in religion and finance at once: a consecrated strong-room and the wealth inside it.

  • Herodotus and the historians use thēsauros for a royal or temple store of treasure.
  • Inscriptions on the treasury buildings preserve the architectural sense.
  • The underlying idea is always the same: value set aside, guarded, and available when needed.

A disputed etymology

The word’s origin is not settled. The common handbooks connect it with tithēmi, “to put, place”, and explain thēsauros as “a laying down, a deposit”; the sense is plausible, since a treasure is precisely what has been laid up. But the ending -sauros cannot be derived from that root, and the best current authority treats the word as Pre-Greek, a relic of the Aegean substrate. The honest position is that the first element may be Greek and the whole word may not be.

Latin and the Middle Ages

Latin borrowed thesaurus with the same double sense, “treasure” and “storehouse”. Plautus built a comedy, the Trinummus, on a buried hoard; Cicero and Livy use the word for both literal and figurative stores; the Vulgate makes it the ordinary word for treasure, including the treasures laid up in heaven. Roman law recognised thesaurus inventus, treasure trove, and debated its division between the finder and the owner of the land.

Medieval Latin extended the metaphor in a theological direction. The thesaurus ecclesiae, the treasury of the Church, and the thesaurus meritorum, the treasury of merits, named the superabundant grace and merit accumulated by Christ and the saints and dispensed through the sacraments. Here the word is at its most serious: not a book, not a hoard of coin, but the economy of salvation itself.

The Renaissance title

The decisive shift is typographic. When the great humanist philologists came to publish their dictionaries, they reached for a word that would claim completeness and value, and they chose thesaurus. Robert Estienne’s Thesaurus linguae Latinae (1531) established the convention; Henri Estienne’s Thesaurus Graecae linguae (1572) confirmed it; and a swarm of author-lexica followed, of which Nizzoli’s Thesaurus Ciceronianus is the type. These books were not synonym lists. They were total inventories of a language or an author, with citations, and they answered the humanist demand for copia — abundance of words and things.

  • The title promises that nothing worth having has been left out.
  • It identifies words with wealth, and the lexicographer with a treasurer.
  • It implies use: a treasury exists to be drawn on, which is exactly what an imitator of Cicero did.

Roget and the modern sense

In 1852 Peter Mark Roget, a retired physician and secretary of the Royal Society, published his Thesaurus of English Words and Phrases, Classified and Arranged so as to Facilitate the Expression of Ideas and Assist in Literary Composition. Roget’s innovation was to arrange words by ideas rather than alphabetically, so that the book became a treasury to be entered from the side of meaning. Its enormous success fixed the modern sense of the word: a dictionary of synonyms. The older sense of “treasure” survived in literary metaphor, but the book had taken over the noun.

The word in the twentieth century

Two further developments split the term. Classical scholarship produced the Thesaurus linguae Latinae, begun in 1894 and still unfinished, the most thorough dictionary ever attempted for any language. Information science, meanwhile, adopted thesaurus for a controlled vocabulary of indexing terms linked by broader, narrower and related relations — a use that has nothing to do with synonym-hunting and everything to do with retrieval. Medical Subject Headings and the UNESCO thesaurus are its best-known products.

Modern usage

Today the synonym sense dominates everyday English, reinforced by the thesaurus built into every word processor and the online thesauri that appear at the top of search results. The word retains its old dignity — a thesaurus is a grander thing than a dictionary of synonyms — and a mild modern suspicion attaches to it, since a synonym chosen for variety rather than precision is the mark of uncertain prose. In the source, none of that ambivalence is present: the Thesaurus Ciceronianus is a monument of humanist scholarship, and the capital letter marks it as a title rather than a common noun.
