Single root word: disdained (past form of disdain). Stage 1 — PIE *dek- "to take, to accept; to perceive; to honor": reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root (Pokorny *dek-), the root underlying a wide family of "taking, fitting, honoring" words across the family. Stage 2 — Latin dignus "worthy, deserving": formed from *dek- with the participial-adjective suffix *-no- (i.e., "acceptable, taking/taken as worthy"); attested from early Latin (Plautus, 3rd–2nd century BCE). Stage 3 — Latin dignari "to deem worthy": denominative verb built on dignus, attested in classical Latin (Cicero, 1st century BCE). Stage 4 — Latin dedignari "to deem unworthy; to refuse contemptuously": negative compound of dis- ("apart, un-") + dignari; attested in classical and silver Latin (e.g., Pliny the Younger, Tacitus). Stage 5 — Old French desdeignier "to refuse with contempt, to deem unworthy": inherited Romance verb (from *dedignare with regular sound change), attested from the 11th–12th century. Stage 6 — Middle English disdeinen "to refuse with contempt": borrowed from Old French via Anglo-Norman, attested from the 13th century (first recorded usage c. 1290). Stage 7 — English disdain "to regard as unworthy; to refuse haughtily" and the past form disdained: modern English, with the noun disdain, the adjectives disdainful/disdainful, recorded continuously from the 14th century onward. Note on nuance/debate: the derivation of Latin dignus from PIE *dek- (via *dek-no-, "taken as fitting, acceptable") is the standard reconstruction but is not without its modern challengers, some of whom prefer a connection to *deiḱ- ("to point out, show"); the *dek- derivation remains the majority view (de Vaan, Ernout–Meillet). All later stages — dignari, dedignari, Old French desdeignier, Middle English disdeinen — are secure and uncontroversial.
