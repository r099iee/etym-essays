Single root word: symptomatized (from the English verb symptomatize, formed on symptom). Linear derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *pet- (also reconstructed *peth₁-), "to fly, to rush, to fall" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. The root produced both flight and falling vocabulary across the family: Sanskrit pátati "he flies, falls," Greek píptein "to fall," Latin petere "to rush at, seek," Germanic *feþrō (English feather). 2. Ancient Greek píptein (πίπτειν), "to fall" — attested from Homer onward (8th century BCE); the zero-grade/present-reduplicated form of the root in Greek. 3. Ancient Greek ptōma (πτῶμα), "a fall; that which falls; a fallen body, a corpse" — attested in Classical Greek (5th–4th century BCE, e.g. Thucydides, Aristophanes); formed from the root ptō- (from *pet- with the o-grade) + nominal suffix. 4. Ancient Greek sýmptōma (σύμπτωμα), "that which falls together with; a coincidence, an occurrence; (by medical extension) a symptom" — attested from the 4th century BCE; formed from sym- "together" + ptōma, literally "a falling together." NOTE: the medical narrowing from "coincidence" to "symptom" took place within Hellenistic medicine (Hippocratic Corpus tradition). 5. Late Latin symptōma, symptōmatis, "a symptom" — loan from Greek, attested from the 1st century CE in Latin medical writing (Celsus, Scribonius Largus). 6. Middle French symptôme, "a symptom" — from Late Latin, attested in Old/Middle French from the 14th–15th centuries. 7. English symptom, "a sign or indication of a disease or condition" — borrowed from Middle French, attested from 1541. 8. English symptomatize, "to be a symptom of; to indicate or manifest through symptoms" — formed from symptom + suffix -ize (with connecting element -at-, from the Latin stem symptōmat-), attested from the 19th century in medical and analytical prose; the past participle symptomatized is used passively: "X is symptomatized by Y." TARGET: symptomatized. Debated / nuanced note: the precise Greek derivational base of ptōma (o-grade *p-tō-) and the exact timing of the medical sense of sýmptōma are matters of detail; the standard chain *pet- → Greek → Latin → French → English is secure, while the modern verb symptomatize is a late, bookish coinage of the 19th century, not inherited from antiquity.
