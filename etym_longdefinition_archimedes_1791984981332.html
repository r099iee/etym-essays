Archimedes (Greek Ἀρχιμήδης, Arkhimēdēs; c. 287 – 212 BCE) is the proper name of the Greek mathematician, physicist and engineer of Syracuse, and in English the standard designation for him and, by derivation, for the principles and devices that bear his name. In the source sentence — “...leading to the discovery of the tomb of Archimedes at Syracuse” — it denotes the man whose tomb Cicero claimed to have found in 75 BCE. The word is a personal name, not a common noun: it has no abstract dictionary sense, and what it “means” in modern English is a body of mathematics plus a cluster of eponyms.

The person

  • Origins: Born at Syracuse, the son of Phidias, an astronomer. Plutarch and Polybius describe him as a kinsman or close associate of King Hiero II, the ruler who gave Syracuse its long peace in the third century BCE.
  • Training: He was almost certainly educated at Alexandria — his surviving prefaces are addressed to the Alexandrian mathematicians Conon of Samos and Dositheus — though no ancient source says so directly, and the inference rests on those dedications and on the style of his mathematics.
  • Character: The ancient tradition presents a man absorbed to the point of eccentricity, who forgot to eat, drew diagrams in the dust, and had to be reminded to bathe. Plutarch uses him as an example of the mind that delights in theory for its own sake.

The work

  • Geometry: On the Sphere and Cylinder established that the surface of a sphere is four times that of its great circle and that its volume is two-thirds that of the circumscribing cylinder; Measurement of a Circle bounded π between 223/71 and 22/7; On Conoids and Spheroids, On Spirals and On the Equilibrium of Planes extended Greek quadrature and centre-of-gravity theory.
  • Number: The Sand Reckoner invents a notation for numbers far beyond the Greek myriad and uses it to estimate how many grains of sand would fill the universe — incidentally quoting the heliocentric hypothesis of Aristarchus.
  • Mechanics: On Floating Bodies states the principle that bears his name; the treatises on levers and pulleys produced the boast that, given a place to stand, he could move the earth.
  • Engineering: He is credited with the Archimedean screw for raising water, with compound pulleys used to launch the great ship Syracusia, and with the defensive machines — the claw and perhaps the burning mirrors — that delayed the Roman assault on Syracuse. The screw and the mirrors are traditional attributions rather than certain ones.

The death and the tomb

Syracuse fell to Marcellus in 212 BCE after a long siege, and Archimedes was killed in the sack by a soldier, despite orders that he be spared. Plutarch gives several versions of his last words, the most famous being a protest that the man was disturbing his circles — rendered in Renaissance Latin as Noli turbare circulos meos. He had reportedly asked that a sphere inscribed in a cylinder, with the ratio 3:2, be carved on his tomb. Cicero, quaestor in Sicily in 75 BCE, found the monument outside the Achradina gate, overgrown with brambles and identified only by that figure (Tusculan Disputations V.64–66).

Derived and eponymous uses

  • Archimedean: the adjective, used of his mathematics and, by extension, of any reasoning that starts from an external fixed point.
  • Archimedes' principle: the buoyancy law, and the standard illustration of scientific insight in schoolbooks.
  • Archimedean point: a philosophical term for a standpoint outside a system from which it can be judged, used from Descartes onwards and central to Hannah Arendt.
  • Technical and cultural namesakes: the Archimedes number in fluid dynamics, the bryozoan genus Archimedes, the paddle steamer SS Archimedes (1839), and the Acorn Archimedes computer (1987).

Why the name matters in this reading

The sentence is an anecdote about antiquarianism: a Roman official with literary tastes goes looking for a Greek mathematician's grave and finds it by remembering a theorem. Archimedes is the point where Greek theoretical genius and Roman commemorative piety meet, and the discovery says as much about Cicero's cultural ambitions as about the dead man's fame.
