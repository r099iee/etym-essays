BOLTED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to bolt, formed from the noun bolt. The chain runs from an uncertain PIE root through Germanic to Modern English.

CHAIN — BOLT:
Stage 1: Probable PIE root *bʰeld- (also written *bʰelː- / *bʰh₁el-), meaning “to knock, strike, beat, cut” — the base proposed for the Germanic “bolt / arrow” word (c. 4000–3000 BCE). [This connection is debated; see note below.]
Stage 2: Proto-Germanic *bultaz, meaning “a bolt; a short, stout arrow; a missile; a thick pin or bar” (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE).
Stage 3: Old English bolt, meaning “a short, stout arrow; a crossbow missile; a bolt (pin or bar)” (attested from c. 1000 CE, e.g. in Byrhtferth’s Enchiridion and other Old English texts).
Stage 4: Middle English bolt, meaning “a crossbow missile; a bolt or bar for fastening; a flash of lightning” (c. 1100–1500 CE).
Stage 5: Modern English noun bolt, “a missile; a sliding bar for a door; a screw-like metal fastener; a flash of lightning; a roll of cloth” (1500–present); and the derived verb to bolt, “to shoot forth, to spring away suddenly; to fasten with a bolt” (attested from c. 1200, fully developed in the 16th c.).
Stage 6: Past tense / participle: bolt + -ed → bolted, “having run away suddenly and uncontrollably; fastened with a bolt; swallowed hurriedly” (modern English).

Debated / nuanced points: The deepest stage is uncertain. Many authorities derive Germanic *bultaz from a PIE root meaning “to strike, beat” (whence also the verb bale, “to beat,” and ball, “the round thing struck”), but the precise root form is disputed and some handbooks mark bolt’s pre-Germanic ancestry as unknown or possibly from a non-Indo-European substrate. The word’s Germanic cognates are solid — German Bolzen, Dutch bout, Swedish bult, all meaning “bolt, pin” — but nothing between the PIE level and Proto-Germanic is attested, so the reconstructed root is flagged as probable rather than certain. The verb to bolt is securely attested from Middle English onward, and the two great senses — “to fly off suddenly” and “to fasten with a bolt” — developed side by side from the same missile-bar image.
