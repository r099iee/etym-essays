The lip begins with the tongue. Its deepest ancestor is PIE *leb-, to lick or lap — the dog's and the infant's first verb — which named the mouth's rim for what it does at the breast and the bowl. From this lapping root the family spreads in two directions at once: toward the kiss and toward the cup. Latin keeps both in labium and labrum, lip and basin-rim, proving that Romans already felt what English still feels — that the mouth and the vessel share a single curved geometry, and that one word can serve them both.

In Proto-Germanic the root settled into the noun *lepōn-, the lip itself, inherited by every daughter tongue: Old Norse leppr, Middle Dutch lippe, Old High German leffur, German Lippe. Old English lippa is already fully modern in its anatomy — the Leechdoms prescribe salves for sore lippan — and Middle English lippe widens the range exactly as bodies always widen words: Chaucer's lovers pledge with lippes, his Miller's wife kisses with hir lippes sweete, and armorers speak of the lippe of a wound where flesh folds back. The rim-of-things sense grows steadily through the craft centuries, as potters throw lips on jugs, carpenters plane lips on joints, and sailors learn the lip of every reef and harbour mouth.

Centuries of the rim:

  • Old English body (9th–11th centuries): Lippa belongs to medicine, prayer, and appetite — lips that suckle, sip the communion cup, and recite the psalms. The word is intimate and everyday, innocent of metaphor beyond the kiss.
  • Medieval craft (12th–15th centuries): The rim-sense hardens as trades multiply: vessels, wounds, and pits all acquire lips in wills, surgical texts, and building accounts. Love poetry simultaneously refines the body-sense — the beloved's lips become rubies, cherries, coral — stocking the metaphor shelf for centuries.
  • Reformation and stage (16th–17th centuries): Translators give England unclean lips in Isaiah and lying lips in the Psalms, while Shakespeare makes lips do everything: pray, swear, freeze in death, and part in music. The metonymy of speech — lips for words — becomes the period's favourite shorthand for testimony and treason.
  • Empire and slang (18th–19th centuries): Geologists mapping craters and engineers cutting canals make lip a technical term of strata and spillways, while across the Atlantic the young republic coins give me no lip. Tenderness and impudence now share the organ, and mothers police both with a look.
  • Modern ubiquity (20th century to today): Lipstick industrialises the mouth, lip-reading aids the deaf, lip-sync shames the miming star, and plastic surgery debates the perfect lip ratio. Meanwhile every hiker still walks the lip of the hollow, exactly as in the source sentence — the ancient body-word still edging the ancient land.

Sound shifts and kin: The short vowel of lippa survived the Great Vowel Shift untouched, sheltered by the closing double consonant, and the spelling normalised early, sparing lip the chaos that tangled fowl and heath. Its kin crowd the face and the kitchen: lap, to drink like a dog; slabber and slobber, to drool over the lip; Latin labium, surviving in anatomy as the labia; and labial itself, the consonant family — p, b, m — shaped by the very lips the root names. Few etymologies close their circle so neatly: the word for the organ is made by the organ, and every speaker re-enacts the root each time the lips meet on its final p.
