Lychee is a small tropical fruit with a rough, rosy-red shell and a translucent, pearly-white, extraordinarily juicy flesh; in this passage it is a simile for perfect skin. The first girl “of medium height and slightly plumpish” has “cheeks as white and firm as a fresh lychee.” The comparison works on several senses at once. A fresh lychee, split open, reveals flesh that is luminous, almost opalescent, and firm to the touch—neither soft nor pulpy but taut, with a delicate spring—and its whiteness is the prized whiteness of the novel’s beauty ideal, the “white and firm” complexion of the high-born. The simile also carries luxury: the lychee was a southern tribute fruit, rushed by relay horsemen to the imperial court, and comparing a girl’s cheeks to a “fresh lychee” borrows that prestige, marking her as exotic, desirable, and expensively fresh—beauty that will not keep, like the fruit itself, which spoils within days of leaving the branch. The word must be distinguished from its fruit-metaphor neighbours. Peach (“peachy cheeks”) suggests softness, blush, and youth; apple evokes firm, round, rosy freshness; milk (“milk-white”) emphasises uniform whiteness but not firmness; jade and porcelain stress cool, flawless hardness; while pearl (“pearly skin”) captures translucence but not moistness. The lychee is distinct because it is simultaneously white, firm, translucent, and juicy—a conjunction no European fruit quite matches, which is precisely why the simile feels exotic and southern, tasting of an empire’s subtropical margins. Its whiteness is not pallor but succulence; its firmness is not rigidity but spring. In register, lychee is a loanword noun of trade and gastronomy. It appears in menus, botany, and descriptive prose; it collocates with fresh, canned, sweet, pale, and white (“fresh lychee,” “lychee flesh”), and, increasingly, in cocktail and dessert vocabulary (“lychee martini,” “lychee sorbet”). In the novel its single appearance does enormous work: one exotic word imports a whole southern landscape of imperial tribute, perishability, and sensory luxury, and makes the girl’s beauty something to be tasted—fresh, precious, and briefly perfect, like the cheeks the narrator holds up for our admiration.
  • Primary sense (fruit): A small, rounded fruit with a thin, rough, knobbly red-brown shell; sweet, white, translucent, juicy pulp; and a single glossy dark seed.
  • Extended sense (tree and produce): The tropical evergreen tree (Litchi chinensis) that bears the fruit, and the fruit as traded and eaten, fresh or preserved.
  • Gastronomic sense: The flavour itself—used in lychee tea, lychee martinis, lychee sorbet, and canned or dried lychees.
  • Register: Trade and gastronomy, botanical description, and literary simile; delicate, exotic, and southern in connotation.
Polysemy & Secondary Modern Meanings: (1) The fruit of Litchi chinensis, a member of the soapberry family (Sapindaceae), native to southern China and now cultivated across the tropics; (2) the tree itself; (3) the flavour in modern cuisine and drinks; (4) in variant spellings—litchi (the older British botanical spelling), lichee, lichi, and lychee (the standard modern form)—the word appears throughout trade records, botanical Latin (Litchi chinensis), and menus; (5) figuratively, the fruit’s perishable perfection makes it a natural simile for brief, exquisite beauty, exactly its role in this passage. Semantic reasoning & historical bridge: the Chinese compound lìzhī (荔枝) is traditionally glossed as lízhī, “detached from the branch,” because the lychee spoils within days of being plucked; sixteenth-century Portuguese traders at Guangzhou recorded the Cantonese and Hokkien forms (lechia, líchia), and the word entered English botanical and culinary use from the eighteenth century. In the novel, the simile uses the fruit’s double character—luminous firmness and imminent perishability—to render a beauty that is both exquisite and doomed, a perfect emblem of the novel’s young women.
