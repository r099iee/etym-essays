Proto-Celtic (reconstructed, c. 500 BCE)
*dūno-
'fort, fortified height'
↓
Old Irish (attested cousin) (8th c.)
dún
'fort, stronghold'
↓
Old English (borrowed) (9th c.)
dūn
'hill, open high ground'
↓
Middle English (13th c.)
doun
'hill; upland pasture'
↓
Modern English (plural)
downs
'chalk uplands'

Note: the Celtic word is securely attested across Old Irish dún, Welsh din, and Gaulish place-names in -dunum, but its deeper Indo-European pedigree is debated, with no single agreed root; the chain therefore begins at the secure Proto-Celtic stage. The English sense shifted from 'fortified height' to open hill as Saxon settlers applied the British word to the unfortified chalk ridges they pastured. Not to be confused with feather down from Old Norse dúnn, an unrelated word.
