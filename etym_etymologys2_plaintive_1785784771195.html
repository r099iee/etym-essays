Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *pleh₂- 'to strike, beat' (reconstructed root; the extended forms *pleh₂-k- and *pleh₂-g- yield the Greek and Latin families respectively). Stage 2 — Latin plangō, plangere 'to strike, beat, especially the breast in mourning; to lament' (attested from the 2nd century BCE in Roman comedy; principal parts plangō, plangere, planxī, planctum). The bridge from 'strike' to 'lament' reflects the ancient Mediterranean gesture of beating the breast in grief. Stage 3 — Latin planctus, -ūs 'a beating of the breast, a lamentation', and the noun use of the feminine past participle plancta 'a lament' (classical Latin). Stage 4 — Old French plainte 'lamentation, complaint; a formal complaint at law' (from Latin plancta; attested from the 11th–12th centuries). Stage 5 — Old French plaintif 'lamenting, aggrieved, complaining' (adjective, from plainte + the suffix -if, from Latin -īvus; attested from the 12th–13th centuries). The same word used as a noun meant 'complainant, plaintiff' — the source of the English legal doublet. Stage 6 — Middle English plaintif / plaintive 'mournful, lamenting' (borrowed from Anglo-French in the 14th century; the adjective sense is attested alongside the legal noun sense 'plaintiff'). Stage 7 — Modern English PLAINTIVE 'expressing sorrow, melancholy, or mournful lamentation; mournfully expressive, especially of voice or music' (adjective stabilized from the 16th century onward; the spelling -ive, on the model of other Latin-derived adjectives, separated it from the legal plaintiff). Note on the doublet: plaintive and plaintiff are doublets from the same Old French plaintif; the courtroom noun split off early and retained the older -if / -tif spelling, while the mournful adjective was re-shaped as plaintive. The derivation is secure throughout; no stage is debated.
