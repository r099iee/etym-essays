The evolution of “amulet”: a word of unknown origin that never changed. Every etymology of amulet must begin with an admission: no one knows where the word comes from. The Latin noun amulētum is attested already in Pliny the Elder’s Natural History (first century AD), where the great encyclopedist recommends amuleta worn against fever and the evil eye, and the word has no known relatives elsewhere in the Indo-European family — it is, in the strict sense, an isolate within Latin, and it has no PIE ancestry that can be demonstrated.
  • The two competing theories. (1) Latin amoliri. The grammarians’ derivation derives amulētum from the verb amoliri, “to remove, to push away, to ward off” — itself a(b)-, “away from,” plus moliri, “to exert oneself, to heave,” from the noun moles, “a mass, a burden, an effort” — so that an amulet is literally “a thing that pushes danger away.” The supporting noun amolimentum, “a means of averting,” is genuinely attested, giving the derivation respectable internal footing; but the vowel-grade mismatch (amu- against amo-) has always troubled it. (2) Arabic ḥamāla(t). The popular 19th-century theory reaches outside Indo-European: Arabic ḥamala means “to carry, bear,” and ḥamāla(t) denotes a strap or pendant carried on the body, an object “worn” — the semantic match to the neck-cords, wrist-bands, and belt charms of the medieval and modern East is perfect. But the chronology is fatal: Pliny wrote in the first century AD, long before any substantial Arabic influence on Latin, and the theory is now generally abandoned, though it lingers in popular dictionaries. The honest verdict: amulētum is a Latin word of uncertain, possibly non-Indo-European (perhaps Etruscan) origin.
  • English entry (1603). English received the word late and directly: Philemon Holland’s celebrated translation of Pliny’s Natural History (1603) introduced amulet in the sense of a charm hung about the neck to prevent or cure disease — a bookish, quasi-medical word. The same Latin stock produced French amulette and Italian amuleto, and the word entered the vernacular of magic, medicine, and superstition across Europe.
  • Widening and specialisation (17th–19th centuries). From a learned remedy the word widened to any protective object worn on the person — pendant, ring, band, inscribed stone — recorded with wonder in travel literature and foreign religions. The 19th century brought archaeology and anthropology, which adopted amulet as the neutral, scientific term for protective objects across cultures, setting it apart from the more ceremonial talisman and the folk charm. It became a category of material culture: the scarab, the Egyptian eye, the Roman bulla, the medieval hag-stone.
  • Modern technical and fantasy life (20th–21st centuries). The word keeps its matter-of-fact register — an amulet is equipment, not ornament. In anthropology it remains the standard classificatory term; in fantasy literature and games it became the common noun for a wearable magical accessory with defined protective powers (“amulet of health,” “amulet of warding”), and in popular spirituality it survives in the market for protective crystals and inscribed pendants. Its classical gravity — Pliny’s fever-charm behind every game item — makes it the favoured word for the unsentimental magician.
The career of amulet is therefore the opposite of most etymologies: not a long drift of meaning, but a word of unknown origin that has kept one meaning with astonishing fidelity for two thousand years. From Roman fever-charm to Turjan’s wrist-bound rune to the gamer’s inventory, the amulet remains what it always was: a small object worn to keep harm away, and a word whose own history is as dark and unlocatable as the forces it was invented to repel.
