Plundering is a word of war with a deliberately obscure and modest beginning: a continental noun for the movable stuff of a household — clothes, bedding, baggage — which grew into the verb for taking that stuff by force, crossed the Channel in the middle of Europe's bloodiest religious war, and has been emptying houses, cities, economies, and forests of their movables ever since. Its story is a rare case where the honest etymology must begin not with an Indo-European root but with a humbly attested West Germanic noun of unknown deeper origin. 1. The obscure West Germanic origin The chain begins with Middle Low German plunder and Middle Dutch plunder, nouns meaning "household goods, clothing, baggage," with a secondary, dismissive colour of "lumber, trash, odds and ends." The deeper origin of this noun is unknown: no secure Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed, and responsible dictionaries decline to force a PIE pedigree. The dismissive "lumber" sense suggests the goods of an ordinary, unpretentious household — the movables of people who are not rich enough to have anything grander to lose. This is the crucial image: plunder begins as the taking of household movables, not as a vague synonym of "steal."
  • Form: Middle Low German plunder / Middle Dutch plunder, "household goods, clothes, baggage; lumber," 14th–15th c.
  • Origin: obscure; no secure PIE root; treated as a West Germanic noun of unknown deeper history.
  • Core image: the movable stuff of a home — clothes, bedding, furniture, kit — the everyday property of an ordinary household.
2. From noun to verb: plündern From this noun the continental dialects formed a denominal verb meaning "to strip a household of its goods." Middle High German plundern becomes modern German plündern "to plunder, to sack," with the umlaut, and the parallel Dutch plunderen and Low German plunderen confirm that the whole continental West Germanic area knew the verb. The semantic step is ordinary and transparent: if the noun names the goods, the verb names the act of taking them. In the German-speaking lands of the late medieval and early modern period the verb was already a standard word for the stripping of a town or region in war. 3. The Thirty Years' War and the English borrowing The word's prestige and spread were military. During the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) and the related seventeenth-century campaigns, the systematic stripping of towns by mercenary armies made plündern a word of the age, and English — which was in intense contact with the continental war vocabulary — borrowed it in the 1630s as the verb plunder, with the noun "plunder" (goods taken by force) following in the 1640s. The borrowing is a textbook case of war-words crossing borders: a generation that lived through the sack of towns needed exactly this word. Once in English, plunder immediately generalized from "take household contents in a sack" to any forceful looting — by armies, pirates, and bandits alike. 4. Expansion: piracy, empire, and politics In the centuries after the borrowing, plunder became the standard English word for the seizure of valuables by force, with three great fields of use:
  • Military sack: armies plundering captured cities — the word of the Thirty Years' War and of every war since.
  • Piracy: the vocabulary of the age of sail — "the pirates plundered the merchant ship" — fixed in adventure literature.
  • Empire and politics: the "plunder of empire," colonial looting, and political "plunder" as the corrupt enrichment of rulers at the expense of the ruled; in the law of armed conflict, "pillage and plunder" is a formal war crime.
5. Modern figurative and economic senses In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word broadened to any forcible or reckless taking and to figurative "stripping" of resources: plundering nature, plundering the earth, the "plunder" of corporate asset-stripping and economic predation. The image at every step remains the old one — taking everything movable by force — but the force has become greed, policy, and machinery as much as the sword. 6. Tolkien and the literary moment In Bilbo's fear, "plundering dragons settling on his quiet Hill" reapplies a Thirty Years' War word to mythic raiders. The dragons are imagined as agents defined by the act — creatures whose nature is to strip households and halls, emptying Bag End of everything movable before burning it. The hobbit's Hill becomes, in fantasy, a place that could be sacked, and the old continental drama of the household stripped of its goods is raised to dragon scale. The participle keeps the act ongoing, so the danger feels present rather than past. Every layer of the word's history — obscure household noun, wartime verb, pirate loot, economic plunder — is compressed into that one adjective of dread.
