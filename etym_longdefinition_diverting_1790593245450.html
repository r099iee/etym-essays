Diverting as an adjective meaning entertaining, amusing, agreeable in a light, distracting way describes something that turns attention pleasantly aside from dullness, worry, or routine. When the narrator says many find it diverting, he means many find the process amusing, entertaining, fun to watch or do, a pleasant diversion, even though he himself finds it silly. It is faint praise with a smile: not profound, moving, or important, but diverting, enough to pass the time agreeably.

The participial form retains the verbal sense of to divert, to turn aside. Literally to divert a river, traffic, funds, or attention is to redirect its course. Figuratively to divert a person is to entertain them by redirecting their thoughts away from cares. Diverting therefore sits at the intersection of distraction and delight: it diverts because it distracts, and it pleases because the distraction is welcome. A diverting book, companion, spectacle, or anecdote does not demand deep study; it lightly occupies, tickles, and refreshes.

Core modern senses of the adjective include:

  • Entertaining and amusing: a diverting story, a diverting fellow, a diverting evening. The dominant literary and conversational sense, implying light charm rather than weighty art. Example: a most diverting account of village life.
  • Distracting from seriousness: a diverting interlude, diverting chatter during a crisis. Here the emphasis is on relief, turning minds aside from anxiety, grief, or boredom, as diversions in war or hospital.
  • Digressive or tangential: rare, but critics may call a diverting episode in a novel one that turns aside from the main plot, a detour that is pleasant even if inessential.
  • Technical diverting as turning: in engineering or military language, diverting traffic, diverting funds, diverting aircraft, the present participle keeps its literal redirective force, distinct from the aesthetic evaluation, though both share turning-aside.

The polysemy bridge from turning-aside to amusing is historically transparent. Latin divertere meant to turn in different directions, to turn aside, to go to different houses. From turning aside comes the noun diversion: a turning aside of troops, then a turning aside of attention, then any pastime that turns attention aside agreeably. To divert troops is strategy; to divert children is kindness; both redirect. By the 17th century, divert had acquired the sense to entertain, and diverting as its adjective naturally meant affording diversion. The silliness noted by Wodehouse is therefore built in: diverting things are by definition not serious, they are side-turnings from the main road.

Connotation is urbane, slightly patronising, and gently ironical. To call something diverting is to praise with reserve, to signal cultivated amusement without enthusiasm. It suits comedy of manners, book reviews, travel writing, and polite conversation where ecstatic is too much and boring too rude. A diverting fellow is good company but not a genius; a diverting book passes a train journey but does not change a life; a diverting spectacle like aquaplaning is fun to watch but, to the detached observer, faintly absurd. Wodehouse exploits this perfectly: the narrator concedes others find aquaplaning diverting while maintaining his own superior detachment, achieving comic snobbery without malice.

Degree and register distinguish diverting from neighbours like funny, entertaining, fascinating, or delightful. Funny stresses laughter; diverting stresses pleasant distraction and may raise only a smile. Entertaining is broader and warmer; diverting is lighter and more incidental. Fascinating implies gripping interest; diverting implies passing interest. Delightful implies joy; diverting implies agreeable occupation. Mastering diverting is thus mastering the English art of measured, civilised understatement in aesthetic judgment.
