- 1570s — quagmire coined in English (quag + mire) for soft, shaking marshland that yields underfoot; primarily a concrete landscape term.
- 17th–18th centuries — standard in descriptions of English and colonial wetlands; appears in travelogues, agricultural complaint, and military reports of impassable ground; early figurative hints via general ‘mire’ metaphors but compound still chiefly literal.
- 19th century — Romantic and Victorian prose keep the literal scenic/hazard sense alive; novelists use quagmires as plot obstacles; figurative use grows slowly for sticky troubles.
- early–mid 20th century — figurative sense surges in political journalism: diplomatic and military ‘quagmires’ as situations of no clean withdrawal.
- 1960s–1970s — Vietnam War discourse hardens public perception: quagmire becomes a stock anti-escalation keyword in American English; plural and attributive uses proliferate.
- late 20th–21st century — generalized political and bureaucratic metaphor (‘regulatory quagmire,’ ‘legal quagmire’) coexists with continued literal use in nature writing; speakers hear the word first as ‘sticky mess you sink into,’ whether mud or policy.
