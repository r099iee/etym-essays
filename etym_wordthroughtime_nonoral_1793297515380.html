- Antiquity — the mouth is named: Latin os, oris gives anatomy its word for the mouth, and Late Latin oralis extends it to ‘of the mouth’.
- 16th c. — oral enters English: anatomists and divines write of oral traditions and oral confession, the spoken and the mouthed as one.
- 19th c. — speech versus script: philologists contrast oral epic with written literature, and educators debate oral versus written examination.
- Early 20th c. — the classifier non-: bureaucracy and science mass-produce non- adjectives, and nonoral appears for drug routes and for traditions carried otherwise than by speech.
- 1960s–80s — media theory elevates it: McLuhan and Ong make orality itself a world-historical force, and nonoral channels — manuscript, print, gesture — gain dignity as full carriers of mind.
- Today — the silent boom: texting, emoji, video without sound, and accessible design make nonoral communication the default of digital life, exceedingly rich exactly as the source sentence claims.
