INTRIGUES — The Twist at the Root The history of intrigues begins, as its etymology insists, with the act of twisting. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *terkʷ- meant "to twist, to turn, to wind" — the gesture of the spinner's hand and of everything coiled and tangled. Its fingerprints are scattered across the daughter languages: Sanskrit tarkú, "spindle, distaff," the instrument round which thread is wound; Greek átraktos, likewise the spindle; and on the Italic side Latin torqueō, "to twist, whirl, wrench," which generated the enormous English family of torque, torsion, contort, distort, retort, extort, torment, and torture — a whole vocabulary of twisting, both physical and agonizing. The image latent in all of them is of something wound tight, pulled out of shape, forced into knots. From the same root Latin produced the plural noun trīcae, "entanglements, perplexities, wiles, tricks, hindrances." The etymology of trīcae is itself genuinely debated — some scholars group it with the *terkʷ- twist family on the analogy of "twisted knots, tangles," while others declare it of unknown origin — but the semantic picture is consistent: trīcae are snarls, obstructions, knotted hindrances. Upon this noun Latin built intrīcāre, with the prefix in-, "into": literally "to tangle up into knots," hence "to entangle, embarrass, perplex." Even at this early stage the word already carried both senses that would later split apart in the modern languages — the physical tangling of ropes and affairs and the mental perplexing of the understanding. English drew twice from the same Latin verb: from the past participle intricātus it borrowed intricate (fifteenth century, "entangled, involved, perplexing"), and from its mirror compound extrīcāre ("ex-," "out of") it took extricate, "to disentangle" — the two words showing the root's perfect polarity, to tie and to untie. Our word, however, took a roundabout path through Italy and France. In Renaissance Italian, intrigare shifted the sense of the root from physical tangling toward social and political meddling: to intrigare was to embroil oneself, to meddle, to weave plots — and from the verb came the noun intrigo, "a plot, a tangle, a scheme." The step is a natural extension of the ancient image: a plot is itself a tangle of threads, and the plotter is a man entangled in his own web. From Italian the word crossed the Alps into French as intriguer, "to puzzle, to scheme, to plot," and the noun intrigue, and it was from French — the language of courts and their etiquette — that English took it in the early seventeenth century. The timing is significant: the word entered English in the age of the absolutist court, when palace politics made covert manoeuvring the very substance of power, and when the courtier's career depended on influence exercised through whisper and private audience. In English the word's first home was the theatre of the seventeenth century. The verb intrigue, attested from about 1609, meant "to trick, to perplex, to entangle in schemes," and by 1611 the noun named a secret plot. Restoration drama cultivated the "intrigue plot" — the intricately tangled scheme of disguise, mistaken identity, and concealment that drove comedies such as Wycherley's The Country Wife, where the word's theatrical and amorous senses fused. From the same dramatic ferment came the romantic sense: by the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries an "intrigue" was also a secret love affair, a liaison conducted with the flair of a French novel — the sense the French themselves called intrigue amoureuse. The most remarkable development, however, is modern. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the word has migrated from conspiracy to curiosity. The verb to intrigue now almost always means "to fascinate, to excite interest": a mystery intrigues us, a person is intriguing, a book is full of intrigue. The bridge is the ancient image once again. A tangle of secrets winds round the mind as a rope winds round a spindle; what "intrigues" us is what twists about our attention and will not let go. The old courtly plots survive in historical prose and political journalism — "palace intrigues," "the intrigues of the boardroom," "spy intrigue" — while the psychological sense, born in the twentieth century, has made the word at once more common and less sinister. From PIE *terkʷ- to "what fascinates me," the semantic thread has never once broken: always twisting, always entangling, always drawing something — a rope, a scheme, a heart — into its coils.
