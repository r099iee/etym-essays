THE EVOLUTION OF "GULLS'" The life of gulls' is really the biography of three things braided together: the bird, the spelling, and the little apostrophe that turns a flock into an owner. Each has its own history, and each can be told separately. THE BIRD-NAME: A CELTIC LOANWORD
  • No Indo-European root. Unlike most English bird-names, gull cannot be traced back to Proto-Indo-European. Its earliest reconstructible form is Proto-Celtic *weliannā, "seagull", a word whose deeper origin is unknown — it may have been coined in imitation of the bird's cry, but that is speculation, not fact. The word therefore enters English not as an inheritance but as a loan from the westernmost speakers of the island.
  • From Cornish and Welsh. The recorded Celtic forms — Old Welsh guilann, Old Cornish guilian, later Welsh gwylan and Cornish guylan — are the direct ancestors of the English word. Because gulls swarm the Atlantic coasts of Cornwall and Wales, and because the English form appears in the late 15th century with no native Germanic pedigree, the accepted explanation is a borrowing from the south-west. For hundreds of years earlier, English speakers had called the birds "mews"; the loan displaced the old word.
  • Sound and sense. The gull's unmelodious, mewing cry is one of the most recognisable sounds of the coast, and the bird's grey-and-white body, long wings and mastery of wind and wave made it a natural emblem of the sea itself. The borrowed name came in plain and uncoloured, but the sea's poetry soon attached to it.
THE HOMONYM: THE OTHER "GULL"
  • From the 16th century English also had a verb to gull, "to trick, cheat, or dupe", and a noun a gull, "a dupe". Its origin is separate and itself uncertain — possibly from an old word for an unfledged bird (something easily caught), a connection some authorities accept and others reject.
  • This verb gave the modern adjective gullible ("easily deceived"), and because the two gulls sound identical, the innocent seabird has carried a sly, "dupe-able" undertone in popular speech ever since. Any careful account of the bird-word must flag this homograph: they are not one word but two.
THE GRAMMAR: HOW ENGLISH LOST ITS CASES AND GAINED AN APOSTROPHE
  • The old genitive. Old English marked possession with case endings: the genitive singular ended in -es, the genitive plural in -a (a gull's egg would have been gullæs æg; "gulls' eggs", the genitive plural gulla). After the Norman Conquest the elaborate noun-case system collapsed.
  • The -'s clitic. As inflections fell away, English rebuilt possession around a single invariant ending descended from the old genitive singular -es, which was eventually written with an apostrophe: -'s. The spelling convention — apostrophe before the s for singular (gull's), apostrophe after the s for regular plurals (gulls') — was settled by printers in the 16th–18th centuries, following Latin-style punctuation fashion.
  • What the apostrophe hides. Gulls' is therefore a fossil: one punctuation mark encoding a thousand years of sound change, case loss and print convention. It is also a classic learner's trap, precisely because the rule (apostrophe after the -s for a plural) is so simple to state and so easy to misapply — one tiny mark before the s means "one bird owns it"; after the s, "the flock owns it".
THE WORD IN CONTEXT AND LITERATURE
  • Real food. Gulls' eggs were a genuine coastal harvest in Britain — gathered from ledges and shingle, boiled and eaten — and the novel's children treat them as a normal, slightly exotic meal. The word in this form is a small piece of real island life dropped into fantasy.
  • Register shift. In everyday speech the birds became "seagulls"; field naturalists insisted on plain "gull", noting that gulls live far inland too. The plain form kept the literary and technical high ground, and it is the form a writer like Lewis uses for a precise, unsentimental bird.
  • Modern symbol. From "gull's wing" in poetry to the gull as an emblem of the sea, the word has accumulated a century of symbolic weight — freedom, flight, the cry of the coast — even while its possessive plural remains the most ordinary possible grammar.
CONCLUSION Gulls' gathers four histories into three letters and an apostrophe: a Celtic bird-name of unknown antiquity, a sound-alike cheating verb that muddied its reputation, the collapse and rebuilding of the English case system, and a genuine coastal food culture. It is at once the hardest kind of apostrophe to place and one of the plainest windows onto how English is made — a wild bird, loaned across a sea, dressed in a fossil grammar, and set to work collecting eggs for hungry children.
