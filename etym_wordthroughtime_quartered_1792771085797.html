- Late Middle English (c. 1350–1400) — Derived from quarter (Latin quartus, "fourth"); originally designated dividing physically into four parts, particularly the terrifying medieval penal execution for high treason ("hanged, drawn, and quartered") — [perception: visceral manifestation of monarchical justice, terror, and absolute sovereign power].
- High Renaissance & Heraldry (15th–16th c.) — Codified in aristocratic heraldry to describe dividing a knight's shield into four quadrants to display married coats of arms and inherited titles — [perception: prestigious emblem of aristocratic lineage, chivalric honor, and dynastic legitimacy].
- Early Modern & Civil War Era (16th–17th c.) — Extended from military quarters (the assigned square barracks or campsite) to the verb meaning to forcibly lodge troops in civilian homes; heavily resented in England and colonial America, sparking the Third Amendment to the US Constitution — [perception: tyrannical, invasive military oppression and violation of domestic sanctuary].
- 18th to 19th Century Domestic Literature — Softened in peaceful domestic parlance to describe providing generous seasonal lodging and guest quarters for traveling craftsmen, harvest hands, cooks, and visiting relatives (as in Tolkien's Shire) — [perception: cozy, generous, and organized household hospitality].
- 20th to 21st Century — Retains specialized military logistics ("officers' quarters"), culinary precision (quartering citrus fruit), and canine hunting terminology ("quartering the field"), alongside nostalgic literary usage — [perception: formal, structured descriptor of residential billeting or geometric division].
