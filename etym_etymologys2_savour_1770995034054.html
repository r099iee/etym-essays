PIE *sep- "to taste, to perceive, to try" (reconstructed, c. 4500-3500 BCE) → Latin sapere "to taste of, have a taste; to be wise, to know" (attested 3rd-1st c. BCE) → Latin sapor "taste, flavour, relish" (attested 1st c. BCE) → Old French saveur "taste, flavour, smell" (10th-13th c.) → Anglo-Norman savour (12th-14th c.) → Middle English savour (noun, 13th c.; verb savouren borrowed from Old French savourer "to taste, appreciate," also 13th c.) → savour (modern English; US spelling savor) Note: the Germanic line (Old English sæp "sap, juice," from the same PIE root) is related but does not feed directly into savour, which is a Romance borrowing. The Sanskrit sapati "he serves, honours" is sometimes linked to the same root, but the equation is debated and many linguists treat "taste/perceive" and "serve/honour" as separate roots.
