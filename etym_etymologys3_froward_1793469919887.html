The historical biography of froward is an elegant demonstration of how concrete spatial orientation can transform into an enduring diagnosis of human moral stubbornness. The word is an intrinsic relative of one of the simplest grammatical pairings in the English language: toward and froward. In their earliest Germanic origins, these two words served as reciprocal directional indicators. Just as toward (from Old English tōweard) originally meant ‘oriented in the direction of, approaching,’ and later developed the figurative senses of ‘propitious, compliant, apt to learn,’ froward was engineered to express the diametric opposite: ‘oriented away from, turning one’s back, departing, averse.’

The root element derives from Proto-Indo-European *pro- (‘forward, forth, in front’), which in Proto-Germanic developed into the preposition and adverb *fram, signifying motion away or separation. While Old English possessed a native descendant in fromweard (meaning ‘averse, turned away, about to depart’), the specific phonetic shape of froward owes its existence to the intense Scandinavian settlement of the Danelaw in northern and eastern England during the ninth and tenth centuries. Old Norse frá (‘from, away’) entered Northern Middle English as the adverb and preposition fro (which survives in the common fossilized idiom to and fro). In the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, northern English speakers affixed the native Germanic suffix -ward (descended from Proto-Germanic *-wardaz and PIE *wert-, ‘to turn’) to fro, creating fraward or froward—literally signifying ‘turning away from.’

Key historical eras, phonetic shifts, and semantic milestones:

  • The spatial foundation (Early Middle English, c. 1175–1250): The compound first designated physical trajectory—facing or journeying away from an observer or destination. Almost immediately, however, the concept of turning away acquired deep moral connotations in early Christian homiletic literature, where to turn one’s back on God, baptismal vows, or maternal guidance was the prototypical act of sin.
  • The moralization of contrariness (14th century): In the poetry of Geoffrey Chaucer, John Gower, and the Wycliffite Bible, froward lost its purely spatial meaning and solidified into a psychological epithet. A person who was froward was refractory, unruly, and intentionally opposed to reason, law, and virtue. Chaucer repeatedly uses the word to describe rebellious wives, contrary lovers, and turbulent citizens who kick against legitimate governance.
  • The Tudor and Jacobean biblical canonization (16th–17th century): The golden age of froward arrived with William Tyndale’s 1526 translation of the New Testament and Miles Coverdale’s 1535 Great Bible, culminating in the 1611 King James Authorized Version. The translators selected froward as their definitive, sharp-edged weapon to render Hebrew terms like ‘iqqesh (‘twisted, perverted, crooked’) and Greek skolios (‘curved, tortuous, perverse’). Phrases such as ‘a froward heart’ and ‘a froward mouth’ became deeply embedded in the cultural consciousness of every English-speaking congregation.
  • Elizabethan drama and philosophy: Shakespeare seized upon the word’s vivid domestic and psychological utility. In The Taming of the Shrew, Katharina is repeatedly branded a ‘froward’ woman whom Petruchio vows to tame into a ‘toward’ (docile) wife. Richard Hooker, in his seminal Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, applied the term with philosophical brilliance to describe societal obstinacy, observing that the world is an unyielding, froward opposite to living wisdom.
  • The Great Vowel Shift and phonological stabilization: During the transition from Middle to Early Modern English, the northern open vowel /aː/ in fraward gave way in southern standard speech to the rounded long /ɔː/ and later diphthongized /oʊ/, while the unstressed suffix -ward underwent vowel reduction to /wəd/ or /əd/. The initial consonant cluster /fr-/ maintained its energetic, fricative punch, perfectly matching the harsh, dismissive nature of the concept.
  • Post-eighteenth-century archaization: As the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries advanced, colloquial English favored simpler, Latinate, or French-derived terms like stubborn, perverse, and obstinate. Consequently, froward withdrew into elevated, self-consciously archaic literary prose. Authors like Samuel Johnson, Sir Walter Scott, and Charlotte Brontë utilized it specifically when they wished to evoke the austere moral clarity and rhetorical weight of seventeenth-century discourse.

In modern lexicography, froward stands as a magnificent monument to the English language’s capacity to distill spatial dynamics into moral judgments. From a Viking preposition of departure fused with an ancient Indo-European root of turning, English forged a word that captures the tragic, perverse human impulse to turn one’s face away from truth and light, choosing instead the lonely obstinacy of contradiction.
