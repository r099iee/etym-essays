- [15th c. — “of the ordinary course”] Adjective use of the noun course: goods and fare “of course” are the common, everyday, unexceptional kind.
- [16th c. — falling in quality] The sense slides from “ordinary” to “of low or inferior quality”: coarse cloth, coarse food, coarse fare — base, rude, common; the spelling coarse separates from course.
- [17th–18th c. — texture and morals] Applied to touch — coarse sand, coarse hair, coarse hands — and hardened into moral discourse: coarse manners, coarse language, coarse jesting; the drawing-room word for vulgarity grows a class sting.
- [19th c. — science and sport] Technical senses branch off: coarse fishing for non-salmonid freshwater fish (Britain), coarse adjustment on microscopes, coarse-grained rocks and photographs, coarse thread and coarse aggregate.
- [20th–21st c. — the register split] “Coarse” stays neutral for texture, technical for fishing and science, and gently euphemistic for indecency; its old “ordinary” sense survives only in the fossil phrase “of course”.
