SPITTOONS are receptacles designed to receive saliva and phlegm — vessels into which one deliberately expectorates, most famously in the era of chewing tobacco. In the elegant dining scene of The Story of the Stone, the "maids holding fly-whisks, spittoons, and napkins" who range themselves on either side of the table signal a paradox the modern reader must adjust to: far from being coarse, the spittoon is here a mark of the highest refinement. In Qing aristocratic etiquette, expelling a mouthful of saliva or clearing the throat into an attendant-held vessel was a choreographed act of bodily management, as much a part of the ceremony of the meal as the distribution of dishes by the ladies of the house. The word's tone in this context is therefore fastidious and institutional — it testifies to a household so wealthy and so disciplined that even involuntary bodily functions are assigned to specially trained servants and properly appointed vessels. The presence of spittoons, like the fly-whisks beside them, converts a meal into a ritual of total control, in which the "lower" needs of the body are accounted for by the most expensive domestic infrastructure. Nothing in the scene is accidental; even a mouthful of saliva has its appointed place and its appointed holder.

Polysemy and the full range of senses:

  • The receptacle for expectoration — the primary sense: a metal, earthenware, or porcelain vessel into which one spits; the standard term from the nineteenth century, used in saloons, railway stations, courthouses, hotels, and aristocratic banquets alike.
  • The chewing-tobacco era sense — the spittoon as the signature fixture of American public life in the age of chewing tobacco and its institutionalised expectoration, in brass, sand-filled, and later porcelain form.
  • Figurative and dialectal uses — rarely, "spittoon" appears figuratively for anything receiving abuse or waste; in some dialects the word shifts toward "chamber pot" or basin, showing how the "spitting vessel" family blurs into other vessels of bodily waste.
  • Historical and period-flavoured register — since the decline of chewing tobacco, the word has become historical, comic, or exotic, evoking old saloons — or, in a Chinese aristocratic setting, the fastidious ceremony of a great household.

Boundary comparisons clarify the object's specific niche. Cuspidor is the elegant alternative name, from the Portuguese verb cuspir, "to spit," and in nineteenth-century America it designated the same object in its more ornamental, porcelain, hotel-lobby form — a word that sounds refined even when the thing it names is not. Spit-bucket is the crude colloquial alternative of saloons and barrooms: cheap, functional, and slangy. Spittoon, by contrast, is the general and now slightly old-fashioned standard term for the vessel wherever expectoration is institutionalized. A receptacle or basin is a completely neutral, general container of any kind, lacking the specific bodily, historical, and institutional identity of the spittoon; a chamber pot receives urine and faeces rather than saliva; and a bowl is simply a round open vessel, with no dedicated function. Only spittoon names the vessel whose sole purpose is to receive what the mouth rejects — a purpose so specific that its very presence in a room speaks of the elaborate bodily discipline of the society that used it.
