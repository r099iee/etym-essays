The noun ENCOMIUM entered English in the late sixteenth century as a direct borrowing from Latin encomium, which had itself borrowed the word from Ancient Greek enkōmion. The Greek noun means literally “something belonging to the revel,” a derivative of enkōmios (“of the kōmos”), and it designates the choral song sung in praise of a victor at the games as the celebrating procession wound its way home. The word is composed of the preposition en- (“in, within”) and the noun kōmos (“a festive procession, a band of revellers”), the same kōmos that appears in kōmōidia, “comedy,” the “song of the revel.”

Disputed prehistory. The deeper history of kōmos itself is disputed. Some linguists trace it to the Indo-European root *keim-, “to lie, to rest,” which would connect it with Greek keīmai (“to lie”) and kōmē (“village”), the revel being originally the village festival; others, following Beekes, treat the word as part of the Pre-Greek substrate, with no secure Indo-European ancestry at all. What is certain is the concrete picture the word preserves: an intoxicated, singing, dancing throng moving through a settlement, honouring a champion. In classical Greece the enkōmion was one of the fixed lyric genres — a formal ode of celebration, composed to measure, sung in honour of victors, rulers, or the dead, and distinct from the looser improvisations of the street revel from which it took its name.

From ode to rhetoric. Latin borrowed enkōmion as encomium and generalized it: the word no longer meant a Greek choral ode but any formal speech or writing in praise of a person or thing. It was in this generalized, rhetorical sense that the word passed into the learned languages of Europe and, in the sixteenth century, into English. English rhetoricians and critics adopted it as a precise term for the set-piece tribute, at home in dedications, commemorative verses, funeral orations, and (increasingly) in literary reviews written in a spirit of pure admiration.

The Victorian turn and Mill. By the nineteenth century an encomium was a polished set-piece of commendation, and it is in this sense that Mill's circle used it. In the Autobiography, the word labels the lavish tributes Mill paid to his wife — tributes that contemporaries found disproportionate and that the word's very formality makes faintly ironic. The connotation of surplus admiration, already latent in the classical genre of praise, hardened into the modern flavour of the word: an “encomium” today is an ornate, self-conscious, and perhaps excessive tribute, more museum-piece than live emotion.

  • Sound and form. The word has kept its Latin shape encomium unchanged; the Anglicized plural encomiums competes with the classical plural encomia.
  • Register drift. From a genre-name of Greek lyric poetry, the word became a rhetorical term for formal praise, and finally a slightly mocking word for over-lavish commendation.
  • Literary attestations. Beyond the Greek lyric poets and the rhetorical handbooks, the word appears in English from the Elizabethan translators of rhetoric, in the criticism of the eighteenth century, and in Victorian biography and review — culminating in the Mill context of this card.

The word thus carries a whole history of public praise within it: the drunken village revel of ancient Greece, the victory ode of the games, the panegyric of the Roman world, the dedication and the review of modern letters — and, in Mill, the slightly embarrassing surplus of a great man's love for his wife.
