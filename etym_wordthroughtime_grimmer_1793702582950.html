- **Old English (c. 800-1100)** - 'Grim' was primarily associated with fierceness, cruelty, savagery, and terror. It described warriors, beasts, or actions that were violent and formidable. The connotation was one of active menace and formidable power.
- **Middle English (c. 1100-1500)** - The meaning began to broaden, retaining its sense of fierceness and cruelty but also developing connotations of sternness, ugliness, and unpleasantness. It could describe a harsh appearance or a difficult situation. The societal perception started to shift from purely active aggression to also include a more passive, descriptive quality of severity.
- **Early Modern English (c. 1500-1700)** - The senses of sternness, forbidding appearance, and harshness became more prominent. It was frequently used to describe a severe countenance or a harsh reality. The word began to evoke a sense of unyielding resolve or a bleak outlook, moving away from its more purely savage origins.
- **18th-19th Centuries** - The meaning expanded further to include the sense of something dismal, depressing, or ghastly. It was increasingly applied to situations, prospects, or environments that were bleak and devoid of hope. The connotation became more about a general unpleasantness or a somber atmosphere, often used in literature to set a mood.
- **Modern/Contemporary Usage (20th Century - Present)** - 'Grim' and 'grimmer' encompass all these senses: sternness, cruelty, bleakness, and resolute determination. The word is versatile, used to describe everything from a person's expression to economic forecasts or a dark sense of humor. The comparative 'grimmer' intensifies whichever of these nuanced meanings is intended, indicating a greater degree of severity, unpleasantness, or resolve.
