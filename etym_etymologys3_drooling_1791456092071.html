1. Old English dreflian

The earliest securely attested member of the family is Old English dreflian, "to slaver, drivel, snivel". It belongs to the vocabulary of the body, alongside words for spittle, mucus and the running of the nose. The verb describes a liquid leaving the mouth or nose without control, and it carries no intellectual judgement whatever. Its deeper Germanic prehistory is uncertain, and no reliable Indo-European root can be reconstructed.

2. Middle English and the dialectal split

In Middle English the word survives in forms such as drulen and in the better-known drivel. The two branches diverged: drivel became the standard literary word for both slavering and foolish talk, while drool remained a dialectal or colloquial variant. This split explains why modern English has two overlapping words where many languages have one.

3. The eighteenth-century record

Drool enters printed English in 1802. The late date does not mean the word was new in speech; it means that a dialect form was finally written down. Eighteenth- and early nineteenth-century dialect lexicographers and novelists were beginning to record the speech of ordinary people, and words like drool came into literature through that channel.

4. From saliva to nonsense

By 1826 drool is recorded in the sense "to talk nonsense". The metaphor is almost too obvious to need explanation: speech that flows without rational control is like saliva that flows without muscular control. The word thus joins a large family of English terms that equate loose mouth with loose mind — drivel, slobber, babble, gibberish. The noun drool "saliva" appears in the 1860s, and the gerund drooling becomes a ready-made label for feeble utterance.

  • Physical base — saliva escaping the mouth.
  • Metaphorical extension — words escaping reason.
  • Evaluative force — the speaker is contemptuous of what is said.
  • Gerund — drooling names the activity and can stand as a noun.

5. Medical and clinical language

In modern medicine the literal sense is usually expressed by sialorrhea or by descriptive phrases such as "excessive salivation". Drooling nevertheless remains common in clinical notes, nursing records and patient accounts because it is immediately understood. It is associated with cerebral palsy, Parkinson's disease, stroke, motor neurone disease, swallowing difficulties and intellectual disability, and it is assessed with severity scales in paediatric and neurological care.

6. Desire and the internet

The twentieth century added the sense "to show excessive desire", usually with over: drooling over a sports car, drooling over a photograph. The image is again bodily: appetite so strong that it becomes visible at the mouth. In the twenty-first century the informal compound drool-worthy turns the word into a light compliment for food, design or attractive people, while drool cup and drooling idiot remain aggressive insults.

7. Phonetic history

The change from drulen to drool is a familiar dialect development: the short u of the Middle English form was lengthened and rounded to oo in the modern standard. The initial dr- cluster is stable throughout, and the word has always been monosyllabic in its base form. The spelling drool is first fixed in the nineteenth century.

8. Literary attestations and the present passage

The word's literary career is modest but telling. It appears in nineteenth-century fiction and dialect writing as a marker of bodily or mental weakness, and in twentieth-century prose as a contemptuous description of infatuation or incoherence. In the present passage, "these broken utterances would have appeared mere drooling" uses the gerund as a noun of judgement: the speaker fears that his fragmented words will be heard not as meaning but as bodily noise. The word's long history — from Old English slavering to modern insults — is compressed into that single dismissive phrase.
