Introduction. The word archway is a late-born compound fusing two mature streams of English vocabulary: arch, which entered through Norman French in the age of cathedral-building, and way, which is among the oldest core words of English itself, its form and meaning unchanged for over a thousand years. Their union in 1774 says something about the language’s habit of compounding native and borrowed stock to name new, concrete things. The two chains must therefore be traced separately, then joined.

Part One: the arch (Latin arcus → Old French arche → English arch)

The Latin base. Latin arcus (genitive arcūs) is a fourth-declension masculine noun meaning “a bow” (the weapon), “an arch, a curve, anything bent”, and — importantly for the later chain — “a rainbow”. It is attested throughout classical Latin, from Plautus and Caesar to Vitruvius, whose De architectura (c. 25 BCE) is our richest ancient source for the construction of arches and vaults. The further etymology of arcus is debated. The traditional view, followed by many handbooks and by Pokorny, connects it with the root *h₂erk-, “to hold, contain, enclose, protect”, the same base that gives Latin arceō (“to shut in, ward off”) and arca (“chest, strongbox”) — the arch, on this view, is “the enclosing shape”. More recent scholarship (e.g. de Vaan) is cautious: arcus may simply be an old word for “bow/curve” of uncertain Indo-European pedigree, possibly related to Greek arkys (“net”) but not demonstrably derived from *h₂erk-. Either way, the Roman achievement was engineering: the Romans did not invent the arch but perfected the voussoir arch and the semicircular form that carried their aqueducts, bridges, and vaults, and Latin arcus travelled with the technique.

The Romance stage. From Latin the word passed into the Romance vernaculars. Old French had arche (also arc), “arch of a bridge, vault, curve”, attested from the 12th century, e.g. in the chronicles describing the bridges of Paris. The precise Latin form behind French arche is disputed: the standard account derives it from the accusative arcum with the usual French treatment of final -m, but some authorities posit a Vulgar Latin feminine *arca (“arch”) created by analogy with the many feminine technical nouns in -a; the two explanations converge on the same Latin source, so the safest statement is that French arche continues Latin arcus. Norman French carried the word over the Channel: Middle English arche is on record from the early 13th century, first in church and castle contexts — the pointed arches of the new Gothic style — and soon in bridge, window, and vault descriptions.

English developments. In English, arch (older spelling arche, the final -e later lost) developed its full range: the curved structure; the upward-curved underside of a bridge or vault; and the curve itself as a geometric figure — the arch of the foot, the arch of the sky, the rainbow’s arch, a direct inheritance of the Latin sense “rainbow”. Seventeenth-century science and navigation speak of the arch of a trajectory. The word also spawned derivatives: arched passages, archer (from the bow-sense, via French archer), and — quite separately — the abstract prefix arch- (“chief, first”, as in archbishop), which is NOT from arcus at all but from Greek arkhē (“beginning, rule”): an etymological coincidence that has misled many learners, and a useful caution against assuming related forms share roots.

Part Two: the way (PIE *weǵʰ- → Proto-Germanic *wegaz → Old English weg → way)

Indo-European base. English way is a direct, unbroken descendant of a Proto-Indo-European root *weǵʰ-, “to travel, to convey, to go” — the same root that gives Latin vehere (“to carry”, hence vehicle), Sanskrit vahati (“he drives, carries”), Old Irish fen (“wagon”), and the English words wagon, wain, and weigh (through the idea of carrying on a balance). From *weǵʰ- the Germanic branch built the noun *wegaz, “a road, a path, a course or manner of travelling” — already in Proto-Germanic the word carried both the concrete sense (the track on the ground) and the abstract senses (the course of a journey, the manner of doing something, “in this way”).

Old and Middle English. Old English weg (masculine) is attested from the earliest documents and glosses with the full double nature intact: weg is the road one walks, and on weg (“away”) shows the blur of “path” into “departure”. The Beowulf poet uses it of sea-paths (hronrāde, “whale-road”) and battle-paths; the word is everywhere in Anglo-Saxon poetry and law. Middle English wey preserved both concrete and figurative senses: Chaucer’s pilgrims travel weyes, while his narrators speak of in this wey, “in this manner”.

The compound (1774). English had long expressed the idea periphrastically — “the way under the arch”, “the arched gate” — and French offered ready models such as passage voûté, but the single compound archway is first recorded in 1774, in the flavour of neoclassical architecture: the age of grand gateways, garden follies, college fronts, and rising interest in medieval remains made the arched passage a named, designed thing rather than an anonymous bit of a bridge. The word’s career through the 19th century was powered by engineering — railway viaducts and iron bridges produced archways by the mile — and in the 20th century by urban toponymy (London’s Archway, the 1813 tunnel). The compound’s elegance lies in its economy: the arch supplies the shape, the way supplies the function, and between them they name one of the oldest and most necessary passages in human building — the opening through which, as in Wilde’s story, even the cold and the desperate can shelter.
