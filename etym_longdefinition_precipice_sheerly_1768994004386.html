precipice (noun): A precipice is a very steep, almost vertical rock face or cliff — a place where the ground abruptly ends and falls away sheer, usually to a great depth. The word's core geological sense is a nearly perpendicular descent of rock, and its defining experience is the edge: to stand on the brink of a precipice is to stand at a point where the ground keeps its promise until it suddenly does not, where the floor becomes a wall and the wall falls into space. In the source passage — "Suddenly his torch revealed the verge of a precipice, where the olden channel ended ... and the shelves and walls pitched away on each side into incalculable space" — the word does not describe a landmark so much as a termination: the ancient riverbed stops being a floor and becomes an edge, and the accompanying note ("the vertical cliff that fell at his feet into darkness with no apparent bottom") completes the vertigo. The etymological skeleton of the word — Latin praeceps, literally "headfirst, headlong," from prae "before, forward" + caput "head" — is still felt in its usage: to be at a precipice is to feel the head leaning toward the abyss, and the passage's own posture, a man crawling to the edge and dipping his light down, enacts exactly that headlong magnetism.

Polysemy and modern extensions: (i) the primary, concrete sense — a sheer cliff or very steep rock face, as in "the precipice fell a thousand feet to the gorge"; (ii) by extension, any steep or dangerous descent, including a crumbling riverbank or the vertical drop at the edge of a plateau; (iii) the dominant figurative sense, developed from the 17th century onward — a crisis, a point of imminent and catastrophic ruin, the brink of disaster: "to be on the edge of a precipice" means to be on the verge of financial, moral, or political collapse, "to lead someone to the precipice" to bring them to catastrophe; (iv) in logic and rhetoric, the "precipice of a decision," the moment before a choice that cannot be unmade; (v) technical uses — in mountaineering and geology a precipice is any near-vertical rock face too steep for normal climbing, and the word is used in cartography and hazard assessment for a vertical drop-off; (vi) adjectival and derivative forms — precipiced, precipital, and the verb to precipitate (from the same Latin stem), "to cause to fall headlong; to hurl down; to hasten suddenly," and precipitance/precipitousness for rash, headlong haste, in which the "headfirst" image survives as "acting before thinking." The semantic bridge from cliff to crisis is the oldest metaphor in the word: a person on the edge of ruin is a person on the edge of a fall, and the headlong body of the original meaning haunts every figurative use.

sheerly (adverb): Sheerly means "in a complete, absolute, or total manner; entirely, purely, wholly" — the adverb of the adjective sheer in its intensifying sense ("utter, complete, absolute," as in "sheer nonsense," "sheer joy," "by sheer force"). In the passage the torch reveals "the olden channel ended sheerly," where the adverb carries the adjective's other great sense — "vertically, perpendicularly, straight down, without any slope or break" — and both senses are at work at once: the channel ends completely, and it ends vertically, dropping sheer. Sheerly is rarer than the adjectival sheer and than its near-doublets absolutely and entirely, but it is a legitimate, long-attested adverb built from sheer + -ly (Old English -līce, the standard Germanic adverbial suffix), and it is particularly useful where the writer wants the two meanings of sheer — totality and perpendicularity — to fuse. Polysemy: (i) absolutely, completely, utterly ("sheerly impossible"); (ii) purely, unmixedly, without admixture ("sheerly by chance"); (iii) vertically, perpendicularly, at a sheer angle ("the wall fell sheerly into the black water"); (iv) plainly, simply, without qualification. Register: literary and formal; in everyday speech the adverb is usually suppressed in favor of sheer + adjective ("sheer stupidity") or of absolutely/vertically, so that its appearance in prose is marked and deliberate. Historical note: the two semantic strands of sheer — "clean, cut free, unobstructed" and "bright, pure, clear" — arose together in Middle English from a single Old English word scīr, and "verticality" developed in the 16th century from the idea of a clean, unobstructed drop: a sheer cliff is one that falls away without anything to break it, a clean cut through the air.
