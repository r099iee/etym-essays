- [1860s–1878] - Emerges in Victorian Britain to describe ardent revivalist missioners working among the impoverished urban proletariat, crystallizing definitively in 1878 when William and Catherine Booth rename their East London Christian Mission the "Salvation Army" - [connotation: street-corner revivalism, militant evangelism, radical temperance, working-class piety]
- [1880s–1910s] - Becomes an internationally recognized noun and adjective for uniform-wearing Salvation Army officers known for brass bands, open-air preaching, and pioneering soup-and-shelter social welfare programs - [connotation: tireless charity, anti-slum activism, disciplined moral crusade, populist religion]
- [1920s–1950s] - Broadens into political, social, and literary discourse as a descriptor for any dogmatic, moralistic, or utopian movement that promises total temporal salvation through rigorous doctrinal discipline - [connotation: moralistic earnestness, ideological messianism, evangelical fervor]
- [Late 20th c.–Present] - Extended in literary, philosophical, and psychoanalytic criticism to characterize secular practices—such as Proustian memory retrieval or artistic creation—that operate as systematic exercises in reclaiming lost experience and redeeming life from mortality - [connotation: redemptive, restorative, secular salvation, psychological rescue]
