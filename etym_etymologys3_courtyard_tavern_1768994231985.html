The story of courtyard is the story of two different peoples discovering the same idea — the enclosure — and of two separate words that English later welded into one. The deepest surprise is that both halves of the compound descend from a single Proto-Indo-European root, *ǵʰer-, "to enclose, fence in, grasp," so that courtyard means, etymologically, "enclosure-enclosure." The root's oldest reconstructible image is of hands closing around something and of walls closing around a space: to grasp and to fence are the same gesture at different scales. That root scattered its descendants across the whole Indo-European family. In Greece it produced khórtos, "enclosed yard, feeding-ground," the fenced pasture of the Homeric farmstead. In Italy it produced hortus, "garden," the walled plot beside a Roman house, which later yielded horticulture and the hortus conclusus of devotional imagery. In the Baltic and Slavic lands it made *gordъ, "fortified town," the ancestor of Russian gorod and of the name of Novgorod, the "new-town." In India a contested reflex may lurk behind Sanskrit gṛhá and Hindi ghar, "house," though that link is debated and insecure. One root, one idea of enclosure, spoken in a dozen languages.

From the Italic branch came the element court. Latin hortus "garden" joined the prefix co(m)- "together" to form cohors, originally "an enclosed farmyard" — the bounded pen where a Roman household kept its animals — and from that concrete pen the word did something remarkable: it became the name of a military unit, the cohort, a tenth of a legion, because soldiers were counted as the men who shared an enclosed yard. The whole history of court is this slippage between space and people: a yard that holds a household becomes the household itself, then the lord's household, then the lord's assembly, then the king's palace and the place of law. Old French cort carried all these senses into England after the Norman Conquest, and Middle English court meant at once the king's residence, the law-court, and the courtyard. When English later needed a word for the humble enclosed open space of a house or inn, it combined court with its own native word yard.

The element yard came down a separate Germanic road from the same root. Proto-Germanic *gardaz "enclosure, yard, dwelling" was a robust word of settlement: Old Norse garðr named the farmyard and the town wall, and Garðaríki, the "land of towns," is still what the Norse called Russia; Gothic gards meant "house." Old English geard "enclosure, yard, fence, dwelling" lies behind modern yard, garden (via Frankish *gardo, borrowed into French and re-imported), girdle and girth (things that encircle), and garth (an enclosed piece of ground, alive in dialect and place-names). So the compound courtyard, first attested around 1550, spliced the French-derived court to the native yard — two descendants of the same enclosure-root reunited after a separation of millennia.

The later career of courtyard is that of a word growing steadily more domestic. In the 16th–17th centuries it named the enclosed open spaces of castles, great houses, inns, and colleges. In the industrial 19th century it became the airy "lung" of tenements and working-class courts — a hidden interior where washing, children, and gossip lived out of public sight. In the 20th–21st centuries it has been absorbed into architecture and real estate (courtyard house, courtyard hotel, courtyard garden) and has acquired a marketed value of calm and privacy — the very value the passage exploits when it hides the child's room behind a courtyard, an unroofed sanctuary that shields him from the tavern's violence.

  • c. 1550s: compound formed from court + yard; a plain architectural noun meaning an enclosed open space.
  • 16th–17th c.: spreads to modest houses and civic buildings; takes on a value of seclusion and defence.
  • 18th–19th c.: the tenement and inn courtyard — cramped but hidden sanctuary behind the street.
  • 20th–21st c.: migrates into architecture, hospitality, and real estate as a marketed amenity of privacy and calm.


Tavern descends from a different root entirely, PIE *treb-, "dwelling, building, timber house," and its history is the history of a plank-hut that became a public institution. The root *treb- named the most basic human architecture — the roofed dwelling — and it left heirs across Europe: Lithuanian troba "dwelling, house," and the Germanic *þurpą "village, farmstead," the ancestor of English thorp, the "village" of place-names like Scunthorpe. In Latin the root produced trabs, "beam, timber, plank," the raw material of building, and from trabs the Romans built taberna, traditionally explained as *traberna, "a building made of boards" — a rude hut, shed, or shop of timber. Some scholars prefer to derive taberna directly from *treb- "dwelling" and treat the trabs link as secondary; either route lands on the same picture of a small wooden building for trade.

In republican Rome the taberna was already the shop of the street: the taberna cauponaria was the victualler's booth, and the word could name a workshop, a storeroom, or a modest place selling food and drink. The famous Tabernae of Rome — rows of one-room shops, and later the great vaulted shop-arcades of Trajan's Market — show how thoroughly the word belonged to commerce. This is the first great semantic shift: a word that began as a roofed dwelling of boards became the standard Latin term for a place of retail trade, and drinking was only one of the trades conducted there.

Latin taberna passed into the Romance vernaculars as Old French taverne, and after the Norman Conquest English took it as taverne in the 13th century. For medieval England the tavern was a sharply defined legal and moral thing: a licensed public house selling wine, distinct from the alehouse that sold beer; the place where wine was drunk by the middling and upper orders, and the haunt of gamblers, minstrels, prostitutes, and the politically idle. The church and the law regarded it with suspicion — taverns bred idleness, vice, and sedition — and repeated statutes sought to regulate closing times, prices, and customers. Yet the tavern was also the indispensable social engine of the town: the meeting place of merchants, the inn of news and rumour, the stage for Shakespeare's Falstaff, the address where a man heard the gossip of the world.

The word's later career tracks the rise and fall of its institution. In early modern England taverns were distinguished from inns (which lodged travellers) and from alehouses (which were rougher and cheaper); by the 17th–18th centuries the tavern was a quasi-respectable urban clubhouse for writers, politicians, and men of business — the coffee-house's rival. In colonial and frontier America the tavern doubled as courthouse, town hall, and post office — the first public building of the town — before sliding toward the rough saloon of the 19th century. In the 20th century, with the rise of the pub and the bar, tavern retreated into the historical and literary registers: today it survives as an atmospheric, old-fashioned word, candlelit and smoky and faintly dangerous, the stage of the story's brawls, where the child "comes down at night like some fairybook beast to fight with the sailors."

  • 1st c. BCE: Latin taberna — a wooden shop or booth; taberna cauponaria a place selling food and drink.
  • 11th c.: Old French taverne "shed; tavern"; 13th c.: Middle English taverne, a licensed wine-selling public house.
  • 16th–18th c.: distinguished from inn and alehouse; becomes a quasi-respectable urban meeting place.
  • 18th–19th c. (America): frontier tavern doubles as courthouse, town hall, and post office.
  • 20th–21st c.: archaic-literary word of atmosphere — candlelit, smoky, faintly dangerous.
