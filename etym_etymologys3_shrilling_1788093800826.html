Shrilling, the present participle and gerund of shrill, belongs to the small class of English words whose very sound is their meaning. The word family is Germanic and imitative: it was created — and repeatedly re-created — to capture the acoustic quality of a high, thin, piercing sound, and its history is the story of how that sound-sensation spread from the trumpet field to every corner of human expression, from birdsong to political polemic.

1. The imitative origin (14th century)

  • The Middle English verb shrillen appears around 1300 meaning "to utter with a high, piercing sound; to sound shrilly". Its origin is uncertain but almost certainly onomatopoeic: no Old English ancestor is attested.
  • Parallel Germanic formations — Middle Low German schrellen, schrillen "to resound, sound sharply", Middle High German schrelen, modern German schrillen "to scream, shriek" — show the same imitative base *skrell-/*skril- independently at work; some scholars have also connected the English word with the rare Old English scralletan "to sound loudly", but the derivation is not secure.
  • The phonetics enact the meaning: the initial fricative /ʃ/ is itself a hissing, high-energy sound; the /r/ adds a vibrating roughness; the high front vowel /ɪ/ is acoustically one of the brightest, thinnest vowels in English; and the final /l/ prolongs the tone. The word is a compressed imitation of the noise it names.

2. The adjective emerges (late 14th century)

  • By the late 14th century the adjective shril(le) is attested meaning "high-pitched, piercing, sharp" — probably derived from the verb — and a rare noun use, "a shrill" (a shrill sound), follows.
  • Middle English religious and romance texts use the word of trumpets, horns and voices; the trumpet collocation that survives in Tolkien's "shrilling of the trumpets" is thus already five centuries old by the time The Hobbit was published in 1937.
  • In the 16th–17th centuries the word becomes a favourite of poets for winds, whistles, birds and battle-instruments; epic and pastoral alike exploit the contrast between the shrill (sharp, keen, piercing) and the deep or soft.

3. Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: natural history and the voice

  • With the rise of natural-history writing, "shrill" becomes a standard descriptor of bird calls (swifts, terns, waders, cicadas) and of the sounds of the natural world; the word acquires scientific respectability in ornithology and acoustics.
  • In social description, "a shrill voice" becomes a marker of class and gender in Victorian fiction: the shrill voice is the voice of the servant, the fishwife, the scold, the nervous woman — high pitch coded as lower status, loss of control or lack of breeding, while the deep voice is coded as authoritative.
  • The negative colouring of the word — shrill as unpleasant, grating, excessive — consolidates in this period, preparing the ground for its modern figurative career.

4. The figurative explosion (20th–21st centuries)

  • In the 20th century "shrill" extends from sound to discourse: shrill rhetoric, shrill criticism, shrill headlines mean strident, insistent, alarmist, exaggerated — language that forces itself on the listener and grates.
  • The bridge is the physics of attention: a shrill sound cannot be ignored; it pierces every other sound and demands response. A "shrill" argument likewise is one that makes its point by sheer vehemence and is therefore felt as excessive, unbalanced or emotional.
  • In media criticism of recent decades the word has been repeatedly analysed by linguists as a gendered weapon: women's voices in public debate — especially feminists and campaigners — are disproportionately described as "shrill", while the same intensity in men is more often praised as "passionate" or "forceful". The word thus carries a live sociolinguistic controversy.
  • "Shrilling" itself remains a vivid literary participle: it is used of trumpets, whistles, alarms, kettles, gales, birds and voices, and continues to do the acoustic work it has done since 1300.

5. Phonetic stability

  • Unlike most English words, "shrill" has changed almost no sound in seven centuries: Middle English /ʃril-/ is essentially modern /ʃrɪl-/. The word has been phonetically frozen because it is iconic — to change its sounds would be to damage its meaning.
  • The adverb "shrilly" and the occasional noun use complete a small, stable word-family, all built on the same imitative core.

6. Literary attestations

  • The source passage — the "shrilling of the trumpets" in Tolkien's The Hobbit — is a canonical 20th-century use, placing the word in the epic tradition of trumpet-calls that announce battle.
  • Earlier attestations run from 14th-century devotional and romance verse through Spenser's Faerie Queene (1590s), which repeatedly sounds "shrill" trumpets and horns, and the naturalists' bird books of the 18th–19th centuries, to the modern journalism where "shrill" now does its most controversial work.

In sum, shrilling is a word whose whole history is a single sound: a high, piercing note that has never stopped cutting through the noise around it — first in the trumpets of medieval battlefields, then in the birds of the Victorian countryside, and finally in the strident rhetoric of modern public life, where the word's cutting edge is felt more sharply than ever.
