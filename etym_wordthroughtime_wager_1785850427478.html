- Medieval Anglo-French / early ME — wager/gage perceived as pledge or security, including tokens of legal obligation and combat challenge; solemn, feudal, courtroom-and-lists atmosphere rather than casino slang.
- 14th–16th centuries — sense shifts and expands into staking money or valuables on games, matches, and contingent outcomes; still more formal than later “bet.” Public ear hears agreement + risk.
- 17th–18th centuries — philosophical elevation (Pascal’s Wager) and literary use for calculated risks; the word can mean a serious intellectual or spiritual stake, not only sport.
- 19th century — common in racing, gentlemen’s agreements, and novels of honor; dual life as polite synonym of bet and as legal-historical term in discussions of older “wager of law/battle” traditions.
- Modern usage — noun/verb for formal betting and for figurative confidence (“I’d wager…”). Perception: slightly elevated, old-fashioned-elegant alternative to bet; carries residual seriousness of pledge and challenge.
