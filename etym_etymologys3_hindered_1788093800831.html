The PIE root and the "behind" word. The story begins with the PIE root *kom- "beside, near, with", extended by the comparative suffix *-tero- to form *kom-tero-, which produced the Proto-Germanic adverb and preposition *hinder "behind, back, backward". The spatial meaning is comparative: literally "more on the near side", hence "behind". This Germanic word is richly attested in the older dialects: Gothic hindar, Old High German hintar, German hinter "behind", Old Norse hindri "hinder, latter". English preserves the bare adverb in behind (be + hind) and in the adjective hinder "rear" — hinder legs, hinder parts — now largely archaic except in fixed phrases.

Old English: hindrian. From the prepositional stem came the denominative verb: Proto-Germanic *hindarōjaną "to put behind, hold back", giving Old English hindrian (attested in the pre-Conquest period). Significantly, the Old English verb had two senses: "to injure, harm" and "to hinder, impede". The "harm" sense is thought to reflect the primitive image of striking from behind — a blow delivered unseen — while the "hold back" sense reflects the simple spatial idea of putting something behind, i.e., away from its goal. Cognates across the Germanic world show the same family: Old Norse hindra "to hinder", Dutch hinderen, German hindern "to hinder, impede".

Middle English: hindren. In Middle English (13th century) the verb settles as hindren, and the "injure, harm" sense fades away, leaving only obstruction and delay. The phonology is uneventful: Old English hindrian loses its weak-verb infinitive ending to give hindren, then modern hinder /ˈhɪndə/. The noun hindrance (with an intrusive -r- from the verb stem, later respelled hindrance after the verb) is recorded from the 15th century.

The Bible fixes the modern sense. The King James Version (1611) uses hinder in memorable passages that fixed the word's meaning for four centuries of English speakers: the Ethiopian eunuch's question "What doth hinder me to be baptized?" (Acts 8:36), Paul's "Who did hinder you that ye should not obey the truth?" (Galatians 5:7), and Christ's rebuke of the lawyers who "hindered them that were entering in" (Luke 11:52). In these verses hinder means exactly what it means today: to obstruct, delay or stand in the way of someone's progress toward a goal.

Modern career.

  • 17th–18th centuries: the word enters the language of law, commerce and science; hindrances to trade, navigation and knowledge are catalogued and argued over.
  • 19th century: industrial and administrative English adopts the passive participle "hindered by" as a workhorse of reports — hindered by weather, terrain, finance, disease.
  • 20th–21st centuries: the word becomes fully abstract and psychological — people are hindered by fear, prejudice, bureaucracy, lack of opportunity; it remains a staple of news, policy and self-help prose, naming obstacles without the drama of "blocked" or "stopped".

Summary of the drift. Hindered has travelled from "struck from behind" (Old English harm) through "pushed back, held back" (Middle English obstruction) to the modern sense of "slowed and obstructed in one's advance" — a semantic journey from physical injury to institutional and psychological inconvenience, always preserving the spatial root: the hindrance is what stands behind you, holding you back from what lies ahead.
