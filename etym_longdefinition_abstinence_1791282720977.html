Abstinence is the voluntary practice of deliberately refraining from or going without something, most typically food, alcoholic drink, drugs, or sexual activity, as a matter of moral discipline, health, religious observance or personal choice. Where mere lack or deprivation implies an involuntary absence, abstinence stresses conscious self-restraint: a person who practises abstinence could indulge but chooses not to. In its most familiar modern uses it denotes sobriety from alcohol or drugs, sexual continence outside marriage or for a period, and fasting or selective avoidance of foods, but it extends to any principled non-participation, such as abstinence from voting or from criticism.

The core idea is holding back an appetite. Classical and Christian ethics framed abstinence as mastery of the body by reason and will: the abstinent person demonstrates continence, temperance and purity. This moral colouring persists today. To describe someone's drug-free life as abstinence rather than just non-use ennobles it as an achievement of discipline. In medicine, abstinence is both a treatment goal (sustained abstinence from opioids) and a symptom (withdrawal upon abstinence), while in public health it appears in phrases like abstinence-only education and abstinence-based recovery programmes. In Catholic canon law abstinence has a technical sense: refraining from meat on prescribed days, distinct from fasting, which limits quantity.

  • Voluntary restraint from drink or drugs: total abstinence, teetotal abstinence, and abstinence-based treatment all refer to complete non-use of intoxicants. This is the dominant contemporary sense in clinical and everyday language, carrying connotations of recovery, willpower and moral seriousness.
  • Sexual continence: sexual abstinence means refraining from sexual intercourse, whether temporarily (before marriage, during illness, in preparation for athletic or religious commitments) or indefinitely (celibacy, vows of chastity). Abstinence-only sex education promotes this as the sole acceptable prevention for pregnancy and disease.
  • Fasting and dietary restraint: abstinence from meat, rich food or particular indulgences, especially in Lent, Ramadan parallels, or health regimes. Catholic Fridays of abstinence and monastic abstinence from wine exemplify this religious-dietary sense.
  • General non-participation or forbearance: by extension, abstinence from voting (choosing not to vote), abstinence from comment, or, comically as in Wodehouse, abstinence from indigestion — treating even involuntary ailments as things one might nobly refrain from. This ironic extension shows the word's flexibility as a mock-heroic term for going without.
  • Legal and institutional senses: abstention and abstinence overlap in parliamentary procedure, where abstaining means formally declining to vote; in ascetic theology abstinence is distinguished from continence and fasting by degree and object.

In the Wodehouse line the word is used for comic elevation: indigestion is presented as though it were wine or pleasure, something a gentleman might genteelly abstain from, when in fact it is a humiliating ailment. The humour depends on the reader knowing that abstinence normally implies virtuous self-denial, not a valet's euphemistic report of his master's stomach. Stylistically abstinence is formal and slightly literary; everyday speech prefers going without, giving up, staying off, or staying sober. Its register makes it ideal for medical leaflets, sermons, pledges and parody of all three.
