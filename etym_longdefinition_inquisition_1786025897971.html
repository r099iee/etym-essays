The noun inquisition has three distinct but historically connected lives: a harsh, intrusive, and relentless questioning; a formal official or judicial investigation; and, capitalized, one of the great historical institutions of the Roman Catholic Church, the Inquisition, established to seek out and suppress heresy. In modern speech the word is most often used as a rhetorical exaggeration — calling a session of questioning an "inquisition" implies that the questioner has become an aggressive, merciless judge determined to extract a confession or a fault, not a neutral seeker of information.

Sense 1 — Severe, intrusive interrogation (primary modern sense). In everyday language, an inquisition is a prolonged, aggressive, and uncomfortable interrogation. A parent's cross-questioning of a teenager returning home late, a hostile job interview, a journalist's grilling of a politician — each may be called "an inquisition." The word is chosen precisely for its dramatic overtones: it turns the listener's mind to the church tribunals of the past and to the image of a powerless person subjected to the relentless probing of an authority figure. This sense is always hyperbolic; nobody who says "I was subjected to an inquisition" expects the torture chamber, only the emotional experience of being harried.

Sense 2 — Official or judicial investigation (formal sense). More soberly, an inquisition is a formal investigation into wrongdoing, misconduct, or complex matters — the lineal descendant of the Latin legal meaning, "a judicial search for evidence." Governmental commissions of inquiry, parliamentary hearings, and internal investigations may all be described as inquisitions when they are searching, sustained, and adversarial. In Roman law and in the canon-law tradition, the inquisitio was a specific procedural form in which the magistrate or judge actively gathered evidence rather than waiting for a private accuser to bring a case — the origin of the modern "inquisitorial" legal system used in much of continental Europe, contrasted with the "accusatorial" or "adversarial" system of the common-law world.

Sense 3 — The historical institution (capitalized sense). The Inquisition refers to a series of ecclesiastical tribunals of the Roman Catholic Church, active mainly from the 12th to the 19th centuries, charged with detecting, trying, and punishing heresy. The medieval Inquisition of the 13th century was established to combat Catharism and Waldensianism; the Spanish Inquisition, founded in 1478, became notorious in the 16th–18th centuries for its severity, its secrecy, its use of torture and auto-da-fé burnings, and its persecution of converts from Judaism and Islam; the Roman Inquisition (the Holy Office), founded in 1542, policed orthodoxy in Italy and censored books. Because of these institutions, "inquisition" carries enormous historical baggage of fear, secret proceedings, torture, and religious persecution — and it is precisely this baggage that the modern hyperbolic sense taps into.

Sense 4 — Legal and procedural jargon. In comparative law, the inquisitorial system (from inquisitio) is the mode of legal procedure in which the court itself takes an active role in investigating the facts, in contrast to the adversarial model. The adjective inquisitorial and the noun inquisitor (the officer who conducts an inquisition) remain living technical terms.

Sense 5 — Figurative and extended uses. By metaphorical extension, an inquisition may be any sustained campaign to extract information, enforce conformity, or root out dissent — an "inquisition" into a colleague's political opinions, a "cultural inquisition." It also shades into the idea of a witch-hunt: a relentless, predetermined search for offenders driven by zeal rather than by evidence.

Historical semantic bridge. The word's whole career is a slow expansion from procedure to institution to metaphor. In classical Latin, inquisitio was simply "a searching into, an examination" — a neutral legal term. In the medieval church it became the name of a specific tribunal and, by the 16th century, of the terror associated with those tribunals. In the 19th and 20th centuries, as the historical Inquisitions faded, the lower-case word was re-exported into everyday speech as a vivid metaphor for any harsh, domineering questioning. Modern speakers thus use, without thinking, the emotional residue of the Spanish Inquisition every time they call an interrogation an inquisition.

Register and connotation. The word is dramatic, heavy, and almost always pejorative in modern use. It implies abuse of power: the questioner is cast as the judge, the questioned as the defendant, and the proceeding as one designed to produce condemnation. The phrase "to subject someone to an inquisition" (as on this card) is the canonical construction, and it always frames the questioning as an ordeal.
