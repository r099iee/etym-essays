The word that never left the body

Exude is a word that has never left the body. Its story begins in the Proto-Indo-European root *sweid-, 'to sweat,' one of the few roots to survive almost unchanged in meaning across three great branches of the family.

  • Indo-Iranian: the root produced Vedic Sanskrit svēdá (स्वेद), 'sweat, perspiration,' still the classical word for it, and the compound prasvēda, 'profuse sweat,' which became, through Prakrit and with cluster simplification, the ancestor of Hindi pasīnā (पसीना), the everyday modern word — so that a Hindi speaker saying pasīnā is, at a depth of several millennia, using the same root that English speakers sweat with. The same root in Iran gave Avestan xvaēδa-.
  • Hellenic: the root emerged as ancient Greek hidrṓs (ἱδρώς), 'sweat,' where the initial *sw- was remade as h-; it went on to give the medical vocabulary hidrosis, hyperhidrosis, and anhidrosis still used by dermatologists today.
  • Italic: Latin took *sweid- and made sūdāre, 'to sweat,' dropping the *w (so *swoid- → sūd-). From it flowed a whole Roman lexicon of perspiration: sūdor 'sweat,' sūdātiō 'a sweating,' sūdātōrium, the sweating-room of the Roman bath-house, and sūdārium, the handkerchief for wiping sweat — a word that later took on sacred fame as the Sudarium of St Veronica, the cloth said to bear the face of Christ.
Then, in a simple act of composition, the Romans added the prefix ex-, 'out,' to sūdāre and produced ex(s)ūdāre, 'to sweat out,' a verb for moisture forced through a surface. English borrowed exude in the 1570s, initially in a strictly physical sense: sap, gum, resin, and moisture exude from stems, pores, and rocks. For three centuries the word stayed close to that bodily image — it was a verb about trees and glands, about the slow sweat of matter.

  • The technical offshoot: in the 17th–19th centuries the derivatives exudation and exudate entered medicine for fluid that seeps through vessel walls into tissue or a wound, and botany for the resins and gums plants release — keeping the physical sense alive in scientific register.
  • The figurative turn: in the 19th and 20th centuries the verb was released onto the invisible. To exude confidence, charm, authority, menace — the metaphor keeps the old physiology: what is exuded is not said but perspired, passed involuntarily through the surface of the self. The key difference from radiate or emit is passivity: a confident person does not broadcast confidence but sweats it; the quality is a by-product of inner state, and it is precisely that involuntary, pore-level intimacy that makes the modern figure so vivid.
  • In the passage: the bright carvings, standing immobile all day, 'exuded a kind of darkness for all their colour.' The paradox depends on the physiology: colour is what the eye sees, darkness is what the objects perspire — an emanation of the inner life of the painted stone, given off slowly, hour by hour, with the sun's rotation, while the fantastic shadows shift and elongate on the wall behind. The word makes the darkness seem organic, spectral, involuntary — the secret of the objects leaking out through their substance.
Sound changes along the chain: PIE *sweid- → Latin sūd- (loss of *w between *s and vowel; *ei → long *ī → Latin ū); sūdāre + ex- → exsūdāre (the cluster -xs- simplified to -x- in spelling); Latin → Old French exsuder/exuder (regular inheritance of the infinitive); Old French → English exude (borrowing, 1570s). The English pronunciation stresses the second syllable — /ɪɡˈzjuːd/ — and the past form exuded preserves the full disyllabic ending, making the word feel slow, deliberate, and bodily, exactly as its meaning demands.
