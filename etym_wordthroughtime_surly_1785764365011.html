- [Middle English, c. 14th c.] sirly / syrly — "lordly, majestic, befitting a sir"; perception largely status-based, capable of positive grandeur or mild arrogance depending on context.
- [Late ME – early 16th c.] Transitional pejoration — "haughty, imperious, overbearing"; the lordly bearing is increasingly read as social offense; speakers begin to hear contempt rather than dignity.
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] Spelling/pronunciation surly stabilizes; dominant sense "arrogantly rude, churlish, uncivil"; strong presence in drama and moral characterization; positive "majestic" reading fades.
- [18th–19th c.] Fully temper-focused — "ill-humored, sullenly unfriendly"; applied to servants, hosts, strangers, and figurative weather; less tied to actual aristocratic status, more to mood and manners.
- [20th–21st c.] Modern perception — compact literary insult for grumpy hostility; slightly old-fashioned or bookish beside grumpy/rude; still vivid and unflattering; no residual polite link to "sir" for most speakers.
