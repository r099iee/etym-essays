Strained is the past participle of strain turned full adjective, and it carries the whole physics of pulling tight into every corner of life: things stretched to their limit, bodies hurt by over-pulling, attention screwed to its utmost, relations stretched thin, smiles that must be forced through tension. To be strained is to be under a pull that cannot quite be sustained — and the word always lets you feel the rope.

In the source sentence the hobbits wrap up against the night while their ears are strained for any sound above the creak of wheels and the slow clop of ponies' hoofs — hearing stretched to its utmost, every fibre of attention pulled taut toward the dark. It is the listener's equivalent of eyes straining through fog: the organ itself cannot stretch, so the word lends it the body's effort, and every reader who has lain awake listening for a footstep knows exactly the tautness it names.

Core meaning in detail: Physically, strained describes fibres, muscles, and structures pulled beyond comfort — a strained hamstring, a strained voice, a rope strained to snapping. Mentally, it describes faculties driven to their limit — strained ears, strained eyes, strained attention. Socially, it describes bonds under tension — strained relations, a strained atmosphere, a strained marriage. In each case the image is identical: something elastic pulled toward the point where it must either hold or give.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Pulled taut, stretched tight (founding participial sense): strained wires, strained canvas, ears strained to catch a sound. The bridge is the verb's oldest image — drawing tight — frozen into a state: the pull is still on.
  • Injured by overexertion (primary bodily sense, from the 16th c.): a strained muscle, a strained back, strained eyes after long reading. The bridge runs from tension to damage: pull a fibre past its limit and it complains, so strained becomes the athlete's and the clerk's diagnosis alike.
  • Produced with effort, forced (of voice, smile, politeness): a strained laugh, strained courtesy between rivals. The bridge is the effort made visible: what emerges only because tension wrung it out, the opposite of easy flow.
  • Tense, uneasy (of relations and atmospheres, 19th–20th c.): strained relations, a strained silence at the table. The bridge carries the taut rope into diplomacy and family life: the bond is stretched and may snap, and everyone in the room can feel the pull.
  • Filtered, passed through a sieve (the drained sense): strained broth, strained yoghurt. The bridge is the verb's second great branch — pressing liquid through cloth — where the tension belongs to the mesh and the result is clarity.
  • Excessive, overdone (archaic shades): strained interpretations, strained compliments. The bridge is effort overshooting its mark: meaning pulled so hard through the press that it comes out distorted.

Usage, grammar, and register: strained is neutral, clinical when medical (the physiotherapist's strained ligament), literary when perceptual (strained ears in the dark), diplomatic when social (strained ties between capitals). It collocates with effort words — strained to hear, strained through, strained by — and with damage words — strained muscle, strained voice — and with diplomacy words — strained relations, strained atmosphere. Note the spelling trap: strained (pulled tight) is not stained (marked with colour), though tired eyes have been both. The grammatical engine is the passive participle: something has been strained by effort, through a sieve, to its limit — and the preposition tells you which physics is running.
