• Nero: Similar because both were notorious early Julio-Claudian Roman emperors whose names became universal synonyms for monstrous tyranny, megalomania, and the bloody persecution of political opponents; differs because Nero is primarily remembered for artistic pretension, matricide, theatrical self-indulgence, and persecuting Christians after the Great Fire of Rome, whereas Caligula specifically evokes erratic, sadistic caprice, claims of personal divinity, and the squandering of state wealth on surreal architectural stunts.
  • Domitian: Similar because both ruled Rome autocratically, executed prominent senatorial elites, and demanded divine honors during their lifetimes; differs because Domitian was a disciplined, methodical administrator and austere moralist who stabilized the empire's frontiers, whereas Caligula ruled with flamboyant, unpredictable chaos and reckless financial abandonment.
  • Commodus: Similar because both were young emperors whose reigns degenerated into megalomania, gladiatorial delusions, and bloody terror against the aristocracy; differs because Commodus styled himself as the reincarnation of Hercules and physically fought beasts in the public arena, while Caligula focused his theatricality on senatorial humiliation, elaborate pontoon bridges, and personal deification.
  • Heliogabalus (Elagabalus): Similar because both shocked Roman society by subverting ancestral customs, promoting outlandish religious ceremonies, and indulging in transgressive, decadent spectacles; differs because Heliogabalus was driven by youthful devotion to the Syrian sun deity Elagabal and non-normative gender identity rather than the systematic political terror and murderous vengeance practiced by Caligula.
  • Despot: Similar because both denote an absolute ruler exercising unchecked and oppressive power over subjects; differs because despot is a generic political science descriptor without personal historical specificity, whereas Caligula is a vivid historical antonomasia charged with connotations of madness, sadism, and grotesque vanity.
  • Tyrant: Similar because both describe an oppressive, cruel, or illegitimate sovereign; differs because tyrant can apply widely to any harsh ruler, domestic bully, or overbearing boss, while Caligula connotes absolute imperial supremacy coupled with unhinged, homicidal theatricality.
  • Megalomanic: Similar because both characterize individuals consumed by delusions of personal omnipotence, divine status, and boundless grandeur; differs because megalomaniac is a psychological trait that may exist in anyone without the power to act upon it, whereas Caligula represents the catastrophic fusion of megalomania with the absolute authority of an imperial state.
