Ermine names the stoat (Mustela erminea) — a small, slender, agile carnivore of the weasel family — especially in its white winter coat, and, by extension, the costly, soft white fur of that coat, historically used to line and trim the robes of royalty, nobles, and judges. The word therefore carries an enormous load of symbolism that far exceeds its literal reference: the pristine white fur with its scattered black tail-tip spots became in medieval and Renaissance Europe the emblem of purity, incorruptibility, sovereignty, and judicial integrity. In the passage from the Dream of the Red Chamber, the single detail of a jacket “lined with ermine” places the wearer instantly among the wealthiest and most privileged figures of the novel, summoning the same visual and cultural code that clothed European monarchs and the bench of high magistrates.

Polysemy and secondary senses:

  • Zoological: the stoat in its white winter pelage — the sense used by naturalists and furriers; strictly, an “ermine” is the animal or its white coat, while a “stoat” is the animal in any season.
  • The fur and the garment: the white winter pelt, and by extension any garment, lining, or trimming made from it — “an ermine cape”, “a jacket lined with ermine”.
  • Metonymy of office: because judges and peers wore ermine, “the ermine” (and phrases like “to assume the ermine” or “to don the ermine”) names the judicial office or the peerage itself; “the ermine of the bench” is a stock phrase for judicial dignity and impartiality.
  • Heraldry: in armory, “ermine” is one of the two principal furs — a white field powdered with black “ermine spots” (the stylized black tail-tip); related tinctures include “ermines” (black field with white spots), “erminois” (gold field with black spots), and “pean” (black field with gold spots).
  • Figurative and literary: the ermine stands for spotless honour, for a reputation that must never be sullied — medieval bestiaries claimed the ermine would rather die or surrender than soil its white coat by crossing mud, a myth that made it the perfect emblem of incorruptibility; but precisely because of that claim, “the ermine” can also carry a hint of self-righteous pride or of a purity too jealously guarded.
  • Trade and imitation: “ermine” has historically been applied to other white furs and even to white-dyed wool trimmed in imitation of the true fur, and in old fashion vocabulary it shades into the general category of expensive white winter furs alongside miniver and vair.

Register and collocation. Ermine is a formal, historical, literary, heraldic, and legal-register word. Collocations: “ermine robe”, “robes of ermine”, “lined with ermine”, “trimmed with ermine”, “the judicial ermine”, “an ermine mantle”, “ermine spots”, “ermine fur”. Its boundaries against other furs are strongly connotative: sable is dark, somber, mature wealth; mink is twentieth-century glamour; chinchilla is softness and delicacy; while ermine alone is the fur of sovereignty and of incorruptible state authority — a spotless white that forbids, by its very mythology, any touch of impurity. This is why, in the novel’s world of status-signalling, ermine is not a mere luxury but a precise claim about the character’s unblemished standing.
