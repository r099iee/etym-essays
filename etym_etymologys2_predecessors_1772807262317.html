Chain for the first element “prae-” (the “before” prefix): - PIE *per- / *prō- / *prē- “forward, before, through, first” (attested across the family; sisters: Sanskrit prá “forth,” Greek pró “before, forward,” Latin pro “for, before,” Germanic *fur-, English fore / for / first, German vor). - Latin prae “before, in front of” (adverb / preposition; attested classical). - Latin praedēcessor “one who has gone before (in office)” (post-classical, c. 4th–6th century CE) — first element of the compound. - Old French predecesseur / prédécesseur (12th century) and Middle English predecessour (14th century). - English predecessor (15th century onward) — the pre- prefix. - In predecessors, pre- “before” is the first element. Chain for the second element “de-” (the separative prefix): - PIE *de “away from, down” (a widely reconstructed separative / deictic particle; the Latin preposition is its direct reflex). - Latin de “from, away, down” (preposition; attested classical). - Latin dēcēdere “to go away, depart, withdraw; to retire (from office); to die” (attested classical, e.g. in Cicero) — prefix de- + cedere. - Latin dēcessus “departure, withdrawal, death; retirement from office” (from the perfect stem cess-). - Latin dēcessor “one who departs; predecessor in office” (post-classical). - In praedēcessor — middle element; in English predecessors — the de- element. Chain for the third element “cedere” (the “go / yield” verb): - PIE *ked- “to go, yield, fall” (proposed; the ultimate ancestry of Latin cēdere is debated—some handbooks link it to *ked-, others regard the verb as isolated; state explicitly: the PIE stage, if it existed, is poorly attested, and the secure history of the word begins in Latin). - Latin cēdere “to go, walk; to yield, withdraw, give way” (attested from c. 2nd century BCE onward; Plautus, Caesar). - Latin dēcēdere “to go away, depart; to retire from office; to die” (attested). - Latin dēcessor “one who departs; predecessor in office” (post-classical). - Late Latin praedēcessor “one who has gone before” (c. 4th–6th century CE). - Old French predecesseur / prédécesseur (12th century). - Middle English predecessour (14th century, from Old French). - Modern English predecessor; plural predecessors “prior holders of an office” (the form in this note). Sisters: recede, concede, proceed, accede, secede, cession, cease, success, all built on the same Latin cēdere family. Composite gloss: prae- “before” + de- “away” + cedere “go” → “one who has gone away before” → predecessors “prior holders of a position.”
