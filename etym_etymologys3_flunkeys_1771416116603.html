At the root of flunkey stands the Indo-European base *klei- ("to lean, incline; side"), a root whose oldest image is the body propped against something — a tree, a wall, another person. Out of that image two great lines of meaning opened in antiquity. In the Indo-Iranian world the root produced the Sanskrit verb srayati ("leans on, resorts to") and the related liyate ("clings, rests, adheres"), together with the noun laya ("rest, dissolution"), which in Indian music theory became the word for tempo and lilt — the rhythmic "leaning" of time itself. In the Italic world the same root hardened into Latin clinare ("to lean, bend"), the source of inclinare ("to bend toward" — English incline), declinare ("to bend away" — decline), and reclinare ("to lean back" — recline).

The branch that matters for the servant-word, however, is the "side" offshoot. Handbooks usually trace the flank-words to a Germanic form *hlanka ("hip, side"), the West Germanic cousin of the leaning root, and although the exact equation with *klei- is traditional rather than certain (some scholars prefer a distinct base *kleng-), the whole family has long been kept under *klei-. From Frankish *hlanka came Old French flanc ("side, flank"), and from Old French came English flank in the sixteenth century, together with the verb to flank and the military flanqueur — one posted at the side of a line of battle. The leap from "the side" to "a servant" is the crucial semantic event, and it happens exactly where the word flunkey is first recorded.

Flunkey appears in Scottish sources in 1782, in the sense of a footman or liveried attendant. The traditional reading ties the birth of the word to the flank — the position of the running servant at the side of his master's carriage or horse:

  • The coach-side attendant: in the eighteenth century a footman's most visible duty was to run at the side of the coach, to open doors, and to precede his master; a "flunkey" was, on this reading, literally "the one at the flank" — a servant defined by his place at the side.
  • The uniform and the gate: by the nineteenth century the word had drifted from the moving carriage to the stationary gate; the flunkey became the liveried figure who stood in rows before great doors, the living ornament of aristocratic and then of grand bourgeois display, and the word acquired its characteristic note of condescension — the servant is admired for his dress and condemned for his idleness.
  • Satire and the social novel: the Victorians used the word with relish. Thackeray, Dickens, and the comic stage made the flunkey a stock figure of overdressed vanity — magnificent in livery, empty in service — and "flunkeyism" was coined in the nineteenth century to name the culture of grovelling deference to rank and title that the figure embodied. The word thus absorbed the class anxieties of industrial Britain: it mocked the great house while mocking even harder those who served it.
  • From household to politics: the figurative extension followed quickly. A "flunkey" need no longer wear livery; any servile follower, any minor official doing a patron's dirty work, any "placeman" of a party or an administration became a flunky, and in American political usage the word settled on the loyal, dependent office-holder — the "political flunky" — a sense that keeps the word alive in modern journalism.

Modern usage is thus tri-layered: concrete (a liveried footman, now almost wholly historical), figurative (any obsequious underling), and political (a compliant minor official). The word's own uncertain pedigree mirrors its mocking function: because it entered English in the 1780s with no secure attested ancestor, it has the flavour of slang or cant — a word born low, which is exactly what the nineteenth century needed it to be. Its survival in The Story of the Stone translation is therefore a choice with sharp irony: Hawkes uses a word that is itself servant-born, faintly ridiculous, and socially loaded, to render a tableau of ten liveried men sitting before the great gate — impressive, decorative, and, on closer inspection, faintly absurd.
