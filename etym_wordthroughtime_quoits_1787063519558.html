- [c. 1380–1450: Middle English 'coyte'] - Borrowed from Anglo-Norman 'coite' (flat throwing stone); played widely by English peasants and banned in 1388 to enforce archery.
- [c. 1500–1650: Early Modern English 'quoit'] - Shifted from flat stones to manufactured forged-iron rings; used by Shakespeare as both a sport noun and a verb for hurling.
- [c. 1700–1850: 18th & 19th Century] - Applied in Cornish archaeology to giant prehistoric portal dolmens (e.g. Lanyon Quoit) believed to be giants' throwing discs.
- [c. 1880–Present: Modern Era] - Adapted into shipboard 'deck quoits' using Manila rope rings, remaining a classic lawn game of aiming and hand-eye coordination.
