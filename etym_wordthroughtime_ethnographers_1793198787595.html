- 1830s — the words are coined: ethnography and ethnographer enter learned French and English for the new science of describing peoples, cousins of geography and biography.
- Victorian age — the collectors: gentlemen scholars, missionaries, and imperial officers gather tales, skulls, vocabularies, and customs; the ethnographer is often an amateur with a pith helmet and a questionnaire.
- Russian decades — the mythologists: scholars adapt peasant lore into grand mythological systems, reading fairy tales as fallen gods and winter rites, half science and half poetry.
- 1910s–1920s — the fieldwork revolution: Boas and Malinowski demand long immersion and the native tongue, turning the collector into a resident and the souvenir into a monograph.
- Mid-20th c. — the professionals: university departments, doctoral fieldwork, and the classic village study make the ethnographer a credentialed scientist with ethics codes and return tickets.
- 1970s–1980s — the reflexive turn: the gaze reverses, insiders write back, and the ethnographer confesses doubts, friendships, and colonial shadows inside the text itself.
- Today — everywhere and anywhere: hospital wards, trading floors, and game servers all host ethnographers, and the village hut has become any human world worth dwelling in.
