Litanies is the plural of litany, a noun borrowed from ecclesiastical Latin and Greek and used in English in two broad registers, the liturgical and the figurative. In the reading the capital L and the plural mark a specific set of authorised prayers: “Priests’ Litanies, read or chanted at fixed money-rate per hour”. Carlyle’s capitals treat the litanies as a named commodity, a fixed form sold by the hour; the word is doing satirical work, measuring the mechanical repetition of paid prayer against the spontaneity of grief.

1. The liturgical sense

  • A petitionary prayer-form: a litany is a sequence of short invocations sung or recited by a minister, each followed by a fixed response from the congregation — “Lord, have mercy”, “pray for us”, “hear us, O Lord”. Its defining features are repetition, alternation and a refrain, not a single collect or oration.
  • Named forms: the Litany of the Saints, the oldest Western type, sung at ordinations, processions and the Easter Vigil; the Litany of Loreto, the Marian litany standardised in the sixteenth century; the Great Litany of the Book of Common Prayer (1544–1549), the first English-language liturgy of its kind; and the Orthodox ektenia, the deacon’s litany of peace, supplication and thanksgiving.
  • Penitential processions: in the West, litanies were chanted on Rogation Days and in the Major Litany of 25 April, walking the boundaries of the parish; the word could therefore mean the procession as well as the text.
  • Occasional litanies: forms appointed for particular needs — for rain, for the sick, for the dying, for the harvest, for the dead. This occasional flexibility explains why the plural is so natural.

2. Why the plural, and why the capital

Plural litanies is natural because the Church possesses several forms and repeats them on many days: the Litany of the Saints, the litanies of the saints and angels, the litanies appointed for particular needs. Carlyle’s capitalisation is stylistic, not canonical: it turns the prayers into a proper commodity — “Priests’ Litanies” — and the phrase “at fixed money-rate per hour” completes the commercial image. The reader is meant to hear a tariff, not devotion. The plural also implies iteration: the same text, read or chanted over and over, hour after hour.

3. The figurative sense

The modern, and now dominant, use is figurative: a litany of complaints, a litany of failures, a litany of errors, a litany of excuses. A litany here is a long, repetitive recital — usually of grievances, faults or misfortunes — delivered or endured rather than prayed. The semantic bridge is exact: the liturgical litany is defined by many petitions with one repeated response, so any long uniform list recited in sequence inherits the shape. The tone is normally weary, ironic or accusing; the word implies that the items have become a formula, and that their repetition is itself the problem.

4. Extended and technical uses

  • Legal and journalistic: “a litany of charges”, “a litany of violations” — an enumeration read into the record, with an implied demand for response.
  • Memorial: “a litany of names” — the reading of the dead, which restores the intercessory function in a secular key.
  • Literary: the catalogue or list as a rhetorical figure; Whitmanesque enumeration has been called litanic, and modernist long poems use litany as a structuring device.
  • Music: litany settings (Mozart’s Litaniae Lauretanae, Schubert’s Litanei) and, loosely, any chant-like refrain repeated over a harmonic ground.

5. Register and connotation

In church use the word is solemn and technical, and it is normally capitalised only in titles. Outside church use it is almost always pejorative or at least resigned: a litany is something one is subjected to. It is a favourite of journalists and columnists because it promises length and monotony in one noun. Unlike list, it carries affect; unlike rant, it implies formality and repetition rather than heat; unlike catalogue, it implies a speaker and an audience.

6. In this reading

The litanies are the one sound that does interrupt the silence of Paris, and they interrupt it because they are paid for. Carlyle’s point is not that prayer is absent but that it has been reduced to a service sold by the hour — the anti-clerical sting of the passage. The plural and the capital together make the prayers sound like stock items in a catalogue, while the verb “chanted” keeps the ancient ritual form visible behind the transaction.
