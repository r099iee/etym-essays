An ancient Indo-European verb of motion. The verb FARE descends from one of the oldest and most productive roots in Indo-European, the reconstructed root *per-, meaning 'to go across, to pass over, to travel, to bring through'. This same root underlies an enormous family: Latin portare ('to carry'), Greek peiran ('to pass through'), Sanskrit piparti ('to bring across'), and the Germanic words for 'ford' (a place for crossing a river), 'ferry' (a boat for crossing), 'farewell', and 'far'. In the Germanic branch the root produced the reconstructed Proto-Germanic verb *faraną, 'to go, to travel, to journey', and from this descends the Old English faran, 'to go', one of the most common verbs of motion in the earliest English.

Old English and Middle English usage. In Old English, faran (with its many prefixed forms such as gefaran 'to fare, to undergo') was the ordinary word for travelling and going. It appears throughout Beowulf and the Anglo-Saxon chronicles in the sense of journeying. In Middle English it became faren / faren, retaining the motion sense but also beginning to shade into the sense of 'to get on, to act, to happen to one'. The crucial development of this period was the growing strength of the condition sense: because the trials and outcomes of a journey — food, shelter, fortune — were inseparable from the act of travelling, 'to fare' gradually came to mean 'to be situated, to fare well or badly'.

From motion to condition and to nourishment. The single most characteristic shift in the word's history is the transfer from 'going' to 'getting on'. This transfer is visible in the archaic meal sense, where 'to fare' meant 'to be fed, to have food placed before one' ('we fared sumptuously'), which is how the noun 'fare' came to mean food and provisions ('homely fare', 'simple fare'). The same inheritance gave the noun 'fare' its modern sense of the price paid for a journey: travelling on a road or a ferry cost a 'fare', the payment for being carried across — again the very root idea of crossing. Thus one single Proto-Indo-European root produced, in English, the verb of travelling ('fare forth'), the verb of condition ('how did you fare'), the noun of food ('fare'), and the noun of a journey's price ('bus fare').

The frozen blessing 'farewell'. An especially important fossil of the verb's old life is the salutation 'farewell', formed from the imperative 'fare well' — 'travel well', 'may it go well with you'. The compound farewell appeared in Middle English and has remained essentially unchanged, preserving through all subsequent centuries the archaic imperative of the verb. Related frozen formulas such as 'fare thee well' and 'to bid farewell' keep the verb alive in idiom even where the bare verb of motion has receded into poetry, fantasy, and historical fiction.

Modern English distribution. In the modern period the word's labour is divided unevenly. The bare verb's motion sense ('fare forth', 'wherever you fare') has retreated to a literary, archaic, or deliberately archaic register — Tolkien's prose is full of it, as in the very contexts of this card — while the condition sense remains completely current and neutral: one 'fares well or badly' in an exam, a fight, or an undertaking. Meanwhile 'fare' as a noun (the cost of a journey; food and provisions) is among the most common words in everyday English. The verb FARE is therefore a living monument to Indo-European unity: through 'ferry', 'ford', 'farewell', and 'what is the fare?', a single root meaning 'to cross over' still shapes how modern English speakers talk about journeys, fortune, and food.
