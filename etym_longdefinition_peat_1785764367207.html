PEAT: Spongy brown soil formed by the partial decomposition of organic matter — chiefly mosses, sedges, and other bog vegetation — in waterlogged, acidic, oxygen-poor conditions; used as fuel when dried, and as a soil amendment in horticulture. In physical character it is spongy, fibrous to amorphous, brown to black, and rich in carbon; when cut into blocks and dried, it has long served as a domestic heating fuel in northern latitudes, especially Ireland, Scotland, and Scandinavia. In the card’s sentence, the hikers trudge “through miles of spongy peat land”, their boots sinking deep into the waterlogged soil: the word names the material as a landscape, a terrain that gives underfoot, wet and yielding, so that the trek is not a walk but a labour. Tone in nature writing is earthy and material; in environmental discourse the word is increasingly freighted with carbon-cycle and conservation ethics. Senses and uses (full polysemy).
  • Sense 1 — the substance itself (primary): the partly decayed, carbon-rich organic accumulation of bogs, fens, and mires; the scientific and everyday name for the material, defined by its wet, acidic, anaerobic origin and its slow rate of decay.
  • Sense 2 — the landscape (metonymic extension): peat land, peat bog, peat moss — the wetland terrain where peat forms; the card’s sentence uses the word in this landscape sense, “peat land” being the ground itself rather than the substance in isolation.
  • Sense 3 — the fuel: dried and cut peat, burned on domestic fires for heat and cooking; a peat fire, peat smoke, and peat ash are long-standing fixtures of northern household life, and the acrid-sweet scent of peat smoke is a cultural marker of Ireland, Scotland, and Scandinavia.
  • Sense 4 — the horticultural material: peat moss and peat-based potting composts, valued for moisture retention, aeration, and acidity; the modern gardening sense that drove a global industry in the twentieth century.
  • Sense 5 — the geological stage: in earth science, peat is the first stage of coalification — the earliest, least compressed precursor of lignite and coal — and the word functions in geology as a technical stage-name along the coalification pathway.
  • Sense 6 — the conservation and climate subject (modern jargon): in environmental policy, peatlands are recognized as major carbon sinks and unique habitats; “peat extraction”, “peat-free compost”, and “peatland restoration” are now standard terms, and the word carries ethical weight about habitat destruction and carbon emissions.
Near-synonyms and semantic boundaries.
  • Turf: similar in Irish and Scottish fuel contexts, where both can name cut bog material for burning; differs because turf emphasizes the cut slab, often with surface vegetation, and also means grass sod generally, while peat names the organic deposit and fuel substance itself, including milled or non-slab forms.
  • Humus: similar as decayed organic soil matter valued for fertility; differs because humus forms mainly under oxygenated conditions in topsoils, while peat accumulates under waterlogged, anaerobic conditions and remains only partially decayed, with a distinct chemistry and structure.
  • Muck: similar in denoting dark, wet organic earth; differs because muck usually implies mixed mineral-organic sludge, farmyard manure, or messy mud, while peat is specifically bog-formed, fibrous or amorphous plant residue with fuel and horticultural identities.
  • Lignite: similar in sitting on the coalification continuum; differs because lignite is a soft brown coal, geologically further altered and compressed, while peat is the earlier, less compacted precursor still associated with living or recent wetlands.
  • Compost: similar in both improving garden soils; differs because compost is an intentionally aerated, managed human product, while peat is harvested natural wetland matter — and modern sustainability discourse increasingly contrasts peat-based with peat-free (compost, coir) media.
Collocations and register. The word collocates with bog, moss, land, fire, smoke, cutting, extraction, peat-free, and peatland. Register is earthy and concrete in everyday and regional use, scientific in geology and ecology, and increasingly political in environmental discourse; the word names both the humble fuel of the hearth and the endangered carbon sink of the global climate, and it moves easily between those registers because the material is both.
