- [Biblical use (14th–16th c.)] - Entered English from Latin-Greek "sycomoros" naming the fig-mulberry tree (Ficus sycomorus) of the ancient Mediterranean and the Bible; a learned, scriptural word.
- [Name transfer (17th c.)] - The same name is transferred to the great maple of Britain (Acer pseudoplatanus), so "sycamore" now names an entirely different temperate tree; the word's referent shifts as its exotic, biblical aura fades.
- [American adoption (18th–19th c.)] - In North America the name is applied to the native plane tree (Platanus occidentalis) with its mottled peeling white bark that grows along riverbanks; in American literature it becomes a rustic, riverine, wilderness landmark.
- [Contemporary sense] - Regionally split: in Britain a sycamore is the ubiquitous shade maple (often treated as a weedy self-seeder), while in America it is the majestic riverbank plane planted on streets and creeks; the word now carries plain, familiar, everyday associations rather than any biblical grandeur.
