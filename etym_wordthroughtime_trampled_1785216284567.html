- [Late 14th century: heavy walking] - trampen enters English from Low German as a neutral word for walking heavily, stamping the feet; it names a manner of gait with no victim and no moral colour — one tramps through mud, through a march, through life.
- [Mid-15th century: the crushing turn] - the transitive sense appears: to trample is to beat something down by continuous treading, to crush a crop under a herd or a person under a charge; the word acquires a victim and, with the victim, its first real violence.
- [16th–18th century: literal trampling in war and rout] - trampling becomes the ordinary word for what mobs, cavalry, and stampedes do to the fallen underfoot — a death without agency, the accident of being beneath a crowd; the tone stays bodily and grim.
- [19th century: the figurative trample] - the verb extends to rights, laws, and feelings: to "trample on" something is to treat it with brutal contempt; the heavy foot becomes the emblem of tyranny and carelessness, so the word now carries a ready moral charge alongside the physical one.
- [20th century: Tolkien's careless feet] - in The Hobbit (1937) Bilbo fears being "bumped into, ________ to death, or caught by feel" — the goblin horde would crush him as carelessly as men trample a law; the word fuses its old bodily weight with its modern moral overtone.
- [Contemporary sense] - trampled remains both concrete and figurative: stampede deaths are tramplings, and rights are still said to be trampled underfoot; the core — repeated, heavy, crushing feet — has not shifted, only widened its targets from grass to crowds to rights.
