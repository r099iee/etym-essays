Origin in the Old Norse bundle. The word wisp enters English from Old Norse, where visk denoted a small bundle or handful — of straw, hay, twigs, or similar loose material. Norse was the language of the Vikings who settled northern and eastern England from the ninth century, and this particular word crossed into English in the Middle English period (first attested in the fourteenth century as wisp or wips) as a practical, agricultural word for the kind of small sheaf a farmer might grab, use to wipe a surface, or light a fire with.

Germanic cognates and deep ancestry. Old Norse visk is cognate with Middle Low German and Middle Dutch wisch, and with German Wisch — "wisp, bundle of straw, rag" — pointing back to a Common Germanic noun. Beyond Germanic, the deeper Indo-European ancestry is disputed: some scholars connect the family to a root meaning "to grow, sprout," reasoning that a wisp is gathered vegetation, but this is uncertain, and the standard dictionaries simply label the pre-Germanic origin as unclear. The word's history is a reminder that not every English word can be chased back to a tidy Proto-Indo-European root.

From tool to wisp of smoke. The crucial semantic shift occurred between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. While still meaning "bundle of straw," wisp gained a subsidiary concrete use: a wisp of straw or tow, dipped in pitch or tallow, made a serviceable torch or taper. From this small burning bundle came the folk-belief word will-o'-the-wisp — the mysterious marsh-light that lured travelers astray, imagined as a sprite carrying a wisp of glowing straw — recorded in English from the late sixteenth century and used ever since for anything deceptively alluring and elusive. In the same period the word began to thin out: a wisp came to mean any loose, thin, or filmy portion — of hair, feathers, or grass — and, crucially, of smoke, steam, mist, and cloud. By the seventeenth century, wisps of smoke and wispy hair are fully established in the written record, and the concrete bundle sense begins to recede into dialect and historical usage.

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century refinement. The age of the novel found wisps irresistible for atmosphere: wisps of fog on a heath, wisps of chimney smoke, a wisp of hair escaping a bonnet. The word acquired its modern delicacy — faintness, evanescence, motion. In the nineteenth century it also developed its fully abstract use: a wisp of a smile, a wisp of memory, a wisp of a tune, where thinness itself has been metaphorized. Writers such as the Brontës, Dickens, and Hardy use the word to set a mood with a single touch.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century usage. In modern English, wisps is a standard stock descriptor for smoke, steam, cloud, and hair, while the adjective wispy has become the most common derivative in everyday speech. Fantasy literature and gaming revived the folkloric strain: wisps as ghost-lights, spectral wisps, will-o'-the-wisps — creatures of deceptive light — a direct inheritance from the old marsh-fire belief. The word has come full circle: from a farmer's handful of straw, through a torch that lures, to a ghost that glows and a curl of smoke above a dragon's nostril.

Summary. Old Norse visk → Middle English wisp "bundle of straw" → a straw torch → the marsh-light sprite (will-o'-the-wisp) → any thin drifting substance (smoke, hair, cloud) → a faint abstract trace — an unusually elegant widening of a homely Viking word into one of the most atmospheric words in the language.
