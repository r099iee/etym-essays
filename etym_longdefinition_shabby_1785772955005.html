Shabby describes what has fallen below a decent standard of wear, upkeep, or honour. In the material sense it names clothes, furniture, rooms, and streets that are worn, faded, frayed, stained, or meanly maintained — not necessarily torn to rags, but visibly tired, seedy, and neglected. In the moral and social sense it brands treatment, tricks, excuses, and behaviour as mean, ungenerous, underhanded, or beneath what loyalty and respect require. One and the same adjective spans threadbare sleeves and petty betrayal; literary English leans on it for genteel poverty (a shabby coat worn with dignity) and for ethical disappointment (a shabby way to repay a friend). In the example card, the elderly painter lives in a shabby, unheated apartment with peeling wallpaper yet retains an air of noble dignity — the word marks the visible decline of upkeep while the dignity survives intact.

Polysemy and sense structure:
  • Material sense (the primary, physical core): shabby clothes, shabby furniture, shabby apartments, shabby streets. The key quality is a visible decline in upkeep — faded colour, frayed edges, stains, worn pile — without necessarily dramatic tearing. It is the look of money and care running out.
  • Moral and social sense: shabby treatment, a shabby trick, a shabby excuse. The behaviour is mean, stingy, disloyal, or underhanded — contemptible in a small, mean key rather than monstrous. 'A shabby way to treat an old friend' is the ethical twin of the worn coat: both are beneath decency.
  • Phrase 'shabby genteel' (also 'shabby-genteel'): the condition of being poor while keeping up the appearances of gentility — a nineteenth-century staple of fiction about down-at-heel respectability, and exactly the world of the elderly painter in the example card.
  • Aesthetic reclamation 'shabby chic': a late-twentieth-century design style that deliberately cultivates the look of faded, distressed, worn elegance — an ironic inversion of the word's ordinary stigma.
  • Historical echo: the word's deeper etymology (see below) keeps a faint medical and animal residue — something scabrous, flaking, not cleanly whole — which still colours its feel even when speakers no longer hear 'scab'.
  • Figurative extensions: shabby condition of a market, shabby treatment of a subject, shabby little secrets — always pointing to meanness or neglect.
Semantic reasoning and bridges: the material sense comes first, from the ancient image of a flaking, diseased, or scabrous surface; the moral sense grows out of it in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries because a society obsessed with dress as class signal learned to read shabby cloth as a visible failure of respectability, and from the visible failure of things to the ethical failure of persons was a short step. 'Shabby chic' inverts the direction, valuing the look of neglect as style while the moral sense continues to condemn actual meanness.

Register and collocations: shabby ranges from neutral description of wear (a shabby jacket) to sharp rebuke (shabby treatment). Collocations: shabby clothes, shabby genteel, shabby treatment, shabby trick, shabby apartment / room / street, shabby chic, look shabby, a shabby excuse. Domains: fashion and poverty narrative, interior description, the ethics of manners, and design slang. Emotional range: pity, embarrassment, scorn, or — in 'shabby chic' — ironic stylishness.

Boundary work among near-synonyms:
  • Threadbare is textile-specific — fabric worn so thin the threads show — whereas shabby applies to whole outfits, interiors, and conduct.
  • Tattered stresses tears and hanging rags; shabby may be intact yet faded, ill-kept, or out-at-elbows without dramatic holes.
  • Seedy overlaps for disreputable, run-down appearance (hotels, persons) with a hint of moral seediness; shabby is broader and more ordinary for worn clothes and petty unkindness.
  • Dilapidated suits buildings and structures collapsing from disrepair; shabby is milder and more about appearance and social code.
  • Despicable is far stronger morally — vile, wicked — while shabby conduct is contemptible in a smaller, meaner key: stingy, disloyal, low.
  • Mean and ungenerous are partial synonyms for the moral sense; worn, frayed, faded, and grubby for the material. Antonymic poles: smart, spruce, trim, generous, honourable.
Morphology: shabby is a derived adjective (shab + -y), inflected shabbier / shabbiest; the adverb is shabbily, the noun shabbiness, and the word is pronounced /ˈʃæb.i/.
