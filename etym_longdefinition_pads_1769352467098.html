pads (plural noun): the soft, fleshy, cushion-like underparts of the foot of a dog, cat, bear, fox, or other four-footed mammal, which absorb the shock of the animal's weight as it walks, runs, or leaps and provide grip on ground surfaces. Each paw carries a set of pads: the large central metacarpal (forefoot) or metatarsal (hindfoot) pad, the smaller digital pads beneath each toe, and a carpal pad higher up the foreleg; together they form the rounded, hairless cushions that leave the distinctive footprints of padded-footed animals. Primary senses in detail: - In zoology, veterinary science, and everyday description, "pads" names these thick, elastic, hairless cushions of skin, fat, and connective tissue on the underside of an animal's paw. In John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men, "the spread pads of dogs from the ranches" refers to exactly this — the broad, round footprints a dog's soft foot-cushions press into damp riverbank sand — a usage that extends the word from the body part to the track it leaves. - By extension, "pads" can name the whole soft foot of an animal and, more loosely, any soft, flat cushion-like part. Secondary and modern polysemous senses:
  • Protective or comfort padding: any layer of soft material used to cushion, protect, or support, e.g. shoulder pads, knee pads, elbow pads, hip pads, saddle pads, mattress pads, floor pads, and the padding inside sports gear, armor, furniture, and packaging.
  • Writing pad: a flat stack or block of sheets of paper glued or bound at one edge — a legal pad, notepad, memo pad, sketch pad, or drawing pad.
  • Flat technical surfaces: a launching pad for rockets, a landing pad / helipad for helicopters and drones, a mouse pad for a computer, a touchpad and keypad on a device, a tablet computer marketed as a "pad" (as in iPad); also an ink pad / stamp pad for rubber stamps.
  • Absorbent and mechanical pads: a sanitary pad, a gauze pad, a protective pad worn in sports; brake pads in machinery and vehicles (thin friction or cushioning layers).
  • Slang for a home or room: "a bachelor pad," "a crash pad," "my pad" (counterculture slang of the 1960s-70s), from the notion of a soft place to lie down and rest; now informal and dated.
  • Verb "to pad": (a) to stuff, fill, or line with soft material ("a padded jacket," "padding"), and (b) to inflate with extra, often useless material ("to pad out an essay," "to pad an expense account") — a dishonest "stuffing" sense that adds moral coloring.
  • Verb "to pad along": to walk with a soft, steady footfall; the older noun "footpad" names a highway robber who roamed on foot (16th-19th centuries).
Historical and semantic bridge: the word entered English in the 1500s from Low German / Dutch, where pad meant both "path" and (transferred) "the soft sole of the foot," hence a soft cushion. Once English had the single image of a flat, soft, shock-absorbing layer — under an animal's paw, under a sleeping body, under a saddle — every later invention could be read through the same template: a flat cushion becomes a notepad, a launch pad, a mouse pad, a brake pad, even a bachelor's apartment. The home-slang sense grows directly from the oldest cushion idea, since a home is where one lies down on a soft pad to rest. Register: the anatomical sense is neutral, standard, and widely used in nature writing and veterinary texts; the "home" slang is informal and now dated; the technical senses (launch pad, mouse pad, brake pad) are ordinary modern English.
