curator (noun): an official charged with the care, custody, and management of a collection — a museum, library, archive, gallery, zoo, or, in Gormenghast, the Hall of the Bright Carvings. The word's core is Latin cura, 'care', and a curator is etymologically 'one who cares for'. In the passage the term is used with full ironic force: Rottcodd is the curator, the appointed caretaker, yet no one ever visits the hall and he sleeps 'during most of his life' in the hammock he has erected at the far end. The irony is not laziness so much as emptiness — a care whose object receives no visitors, a vigil kept for nothing, an office whose entire duty consists of two small tasks in 'that long and silent hall'. The single regular task we see — flicking dust from the Bright Carvings — is the outward ceremony of his care, performed as ritual even in the absence of any audience, and his sleep itself becomes a kind of curation, a guarding that continues in unconsciousness. The tone is gently absurd but never contemptuous: the dignified Latin word is lavished on a man who is both guardian and sleeper, and the title curator makes him the priest of a deserted shrine. Polysemy of 'curator' — the senses it carries today
  • Museum / gallery sense (primary, since the 17th c.): the official in charge of a collection — selecting, interpreting, conserving and displaying objects; the oldest and most dignified English use.
  • Library / archive sense (18th–19th c.): the keeper of a library or archival collection, responsible for its care, cataloguing and preservation.
  • Zoo / botanical sense (19th–20th c.): the officer in charge of the living collections of a zoo, aquarium or botanical garden — 'curator of reptiles', 'curator of the herbarium'.
  • Exhibition / art-world sense (20th c.): the professional who conceives and organises exhibitions — selecting artists, themes and works; the 'curatorial turn' made the curator a creative figure in contemporary art.
  • Digital / data sense (21st c., now very prominent): 'content curator', 'to curate a playlist or feed' — the selection, ordering and presentation of online content, images, links and data; care applied to streams of information rather than objects.
  • Legal / guardianship sense (historical, 14th–16th c.): a curator as a legal guardian or overseer appointed to care for the affairs of a minor, an estate, or an incapacitated person — the oldest English sense, now rare outside civil-law systems where a 'curator' still manages estates or trusts.
  • Religious neighbour: through the same cura root, 'curate' (a parish priest charged with the cura animarum, 'care of souls') is the spiritual sibling of the curator.
The semantic bridge is the root care itself. Every sense — museum, library, zoo, exhibition, digital feed, legal guardianship — is a different object of the same central act: taking responsibility for something and looking after it with attention. The modern digital sense is the latest chapter of a very old idea: the 'content curator' heeds and orders a stream of information, an overseer of attention itself. Rottcodd, curator of a hall no one visits, is the logical endpoint of the word's paradox: care whose object is invisible, a curation that has become pure ritual.

Register and distinction: 'caretaker' and 'keeper' are everyday words that emphasise physical custody; 'custodian' (from Latin custos, 'guard') stresses guarding against loss or theft; 'steward' implies managing resources on another's behalf, with overtones of the estate and the table; 'warden' is institutional; but 'curator' is the only one that makes care itself — cura — the essence of the office. In modern usage the word has widened from museums to zoos, archives and finally digital data, a semantic drift that is itself a chapter in the word's biography.
