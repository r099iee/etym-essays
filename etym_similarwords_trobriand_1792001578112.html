• Melanesian: Similar because both refer to the indigenous cultural and geographic populations of the southwestern Pacific; differs because Melanesian is a broad macro-regional classification encompassing New Guinea, the Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, New Caledonia, and Fiji, whereas Trobriand designates a specific, localized coral atoll archipelago and its unique matrilineal society.
  • Polynesian: Similar because both denote Pacific Island societies renowned for oceanic navigation, outrigger voyaging, and elaborate chieftaincies; differs because Polynesian societies (such as Hawaii, Samoa, Tonga, and New Zealand Maori) occupy the eastern Pacific triangle, speak distinct Central-Eastern Oceanic languages, and frequently practice patrilineal or ambilineal descent, whereas Trobriand culture is strictly matrilineal and Western Oceanic.
  • Papuan: Similar because the Trobriand archipelago is politically part of modern Papua New Guinea; differs because Papuan linguistically and anthropologically refers to the ancient, non-Austronesian indigenous peoples and language phyla of the New Guinea mainland, whereas Trobriand Islanders speak an Austronesian (Oceanic) language arriving via later maritime migrations.
  • Kiriwina: Similar because Kiriwina is the indigenous name of the largest island and language of the Trobriand group, frequently used interchangeably with the archipelago itself; differs because Kiriwina is the specific local geographic and linguistic endonym, whereas Trobriand is the historical European exonym applied to the entire administrative archipelago.
  • Massim: Similar because the Trobriand Islanders are the northernmost constituent group of the Massim cultural realm; differs because Massim encompasses the entire maritime cultural complex of southeastern Papua (including the Louisiade Archipelago, the D'Entrecasteaux Islands, and Woodlark Island), sharing the Kula ring and related artistic styles across multiple distinct island groups.
  • Andamanese: Similar because both served as celebrated island laboratories for pioneering early twentieth-century British social anthropology (Radcliffe-Brown in the Andamans, Malinowski in the Trobriands); differs because the Andamanese are indigenous hunter-gatherers of the Bay of Bengal belonging to an ancient Asian lineage, whereas Trobrianders are maritime horticulturalists possessing elaborate chieftaincies and complex ocean trade networks.
