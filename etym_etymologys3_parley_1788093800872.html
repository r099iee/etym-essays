Greek beginnings — a word thrown beside. The chain behind parley begins in ancient Greek with a compound verb: parabállein, "to throw beside, set alongside", from pará "beside, beyond" and bállein "to throw". (The deeply Indo-European preposition pará continues PIE *per-, "forward, through, beyond", the root also of Latin per and Sanskrit pára; the verb bállein is usually traced to a root *gʷelh₁- "to throw, strike", though its descent is debated and a Pre-Greek origin has been suggested.) From the verb Greek formed the noun parabolḗ — literally "a throwing beside" — which in rhetoric and mathematics came to mean "comparison, analogy, application": Aristotle uses it for a rhetorical comparison, and Euclidean geometry applied the word to the curve we still call the parabola, because its construction was conceived as the "application" of an area. It is the comparison-sense that matters here: a parable is, etymologically, a story thrown alongside a truth so that the truth is seen in its light.

The parables and the shift to "word". The New Testament made parabolḗ the technical name of the teaching stories of Jesus — the Parable of the Sower, of the Prodigal Son — and Latin Christianity borrowed the word as parabola. Then a momentous semantic shift occurred within Christian Latin: because the Gospel parable was the uttered word of God, parabola broadened from "parable" to "utterance, word, speech" in the spoken Latin of the church and the street. The shift is preserved, fossilised, in the Romance languages: Italian parola, Spanish palabra, Portuguese palavra, French parole — all meaning simply "word" — and English "parole" (a word given, a promise, and later the promise of a prisoner to behave).

Vulgar Latin and Old French — from word to speaking. From the noun came a verb: Late and Vulgar Latin made parabolāre, "to speak" (literally "to utter parables, to talk"), contracted in the spoken language to *paraulare, the form reconstructed behind all the Romance verbs of speech — French parler, Occitan paraular, and the -parl- stems of the Iberian languages. Old French parler (attested from the ninth century) was and remains the everyday French verb "to speak"; from it the same period coined parlement, "a speaking", an assembly where talk is the business — the word English borrowed by about 1300 as parliament — and parlour, the room for receiving guests and talking.

Middle French parlée and English parley (16th century). French formed from parler the feminine past participle parlée, used as a noun for "a talk, a discourse" — and English borrowed it as parley, first recorded in the mid-sixteenth century (the older short form parle, from the Old French infinitive, appears from the fifteenth century). In its first decades in English, parley meant simply "conversation, discussion", and the verb meant "to speak"; the narrowing that defines the word today — a conference with an enemy — happened within a generation, as Tudor and Stuart warfare gave it daily employment. "To beat or sound a parley" became the stock phrase for the drum- or trumpet-signal by which one side requested a conference under truce; Elizabethan and Jacobean drama, and the military memoirs of the Thirty Years' War and the English Civil War, are full of parleys held, granted, refused and — sometimes — treacherously abused.

The 17th–19th centuries — the law of the battle-line. By the 17th century the parley was a rule-governed institution of war: military manuals regulated the signal, the place of meeting, the flag of truce, and the safe conduct of officers who came to talk; abuse of a parley was a recognized war crime. Writers on the laws of war treated the parley as the standing proof that war itself rests on agreement — enemies who can agree to meet can agree to cease — and 18th-century siege literature is crowded with parleys before capitulations. The 19th century kept the word in the soldiers' vocabulary of sieges and colonial warfare while it faded from civilian speech; historians and novelists, from Scott's generation on, made it forever the word of the flag of truce and the drum at the gate.

The 20th century to today — fantasy and film. In the 20th century parley retired to the literary past — it is precisely in that costume that it appears in the source passage from J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), where Bard, the dragon-slayer and heir of Girion, demands "a parley and a council" at the mountain gate: the word summons an entire code of honor between enemies. In 2003 the film Pirates of the Caribbean revived the word on a global scale, giving the pirate's "parley" a safe-conduct right under the Code of the Brethren — cinematic law that made "parley" a meme of negotiation culture. Today the word is jocular and faux-antique in general use (a "parley" between rival companies), standard in fantasy and historical fiction, and still, in its oldest sense, the right name for that fragile moment at the boundary of a conflict when the talking starts instead of the fighting.

Italian parola, Spanish palabra, French parole); Merovingian parabolare; Old French parler, parlement (9th–11th c.); English parley (mid-16th c.), "beat a parley" (17th c.); Tolkien, The Hobbit (1937); Pirates of the Caribbean (2003).
  • Phonetic notes: Greek pará + bállein > parabolḗ; Latin parabola; Vulgar Latin *parabolāre → *paraulare (syncope of the unstressed middle syllables); Old French parler; French parlée; English parley (with loss of the French feminine ending's vowel, /ˈpɑːli/).
  • Semantic drift in one line: to throw beside → comparison, parable → (Christian Latin) word, speech → to speak → a talk → an enemy conference under truce → jocular literary relic and cinematic pirate-law.
