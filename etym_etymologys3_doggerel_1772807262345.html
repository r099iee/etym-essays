DOGGEREL: AN EVOLUTION ESSAY THE COMPONENTS AND THEIR PRE-HISTORY Doggerel is one of the few English literary terms whose ancestry lies entirely inside English itself. It has no PIE root, no Latin bridge, no French borrowing as a whole word; it is a compound coined on English soil out of two pieces. The first piece is "dog". Old English "docga" appears only around 1050, in the Leiden glosses, and it is famously mysterious: it has no certain cognates in the other Germanic languages, which use reflexes of "hund" (Gothic "hunds", Old Norse "hundr", German "Hund"). The most likely explanation is that "docga" began as an expressive or diminutive pet-name formation - perhaps a reduplicative nursery word - that happened to win out in England while the rest of Germanic kept "hund". By Middle English the form "dogge" was standard, and by the sixteenth century the dog had acquired a rich set of transferred, depreciative senses: a contemptible person, a spy or informer ("to dog"), a low or spurious thing ("dog Latin", "dog cheap"). It is precisely this climate of contempt that the coinage needed. THE SUFFIX AND THE COINAGE The second piece is the suffix "-rel", descended from Anglo-French and Old French "-erel"/"-arel", a pejorative-diminutive that English borrowed in the Middle Ages alongside "mongrel", "wastrel", and "pickpocket" compounds. The suffix gives the meaning "small or contemptible instance of X". Adding it to "dog" in the early seventeenth century produced "doggerel": verse that is dog-like, base, cur-like - the kind of rhyme one might expect a dog to howl, or verse so low that it befits a contemptible animal. The earliest attestations cluster in the 1630s, in literary quarrels: the word appears in the context of attacks on ballad-mongers and hack poets of Grub Street. From the very beginning the word is a weapon of critical abuse. SEVENTEENTH-CENTURY SATIRE Because doggerel was a pejorative, it was immediately usable as a satiric register. Its great triumph came with Samuel Butler's "Hudibras" (1663), a mock-heroic burlesque of Puritanism written in rhyming octosyllabics of deliberately hobbling, sing-song character. Butler's lines read like doggerel by design - irregularly stressed, comically rhymed - and this "Hudibrastic" style became the model for comic narrative verse for a century, so that what began as an insult ("doggerel") became, in careful hands, a legitimate comic technique. The word, however, kept its sting: critics used "doggerel" to dismiss anything they considered crude, while satirists exploited the crudeness as their medium. THE EIGHTEENTH AND NINETEENTH CENTURIES In the eighteenth century doggerel was the native idiom of Grub Street: the political lampoon, the election squib, the pamphlet rhyme, and the newspaper couplet. Addison and Swift both sneer at doggerel even as they wield rough comic verse; the word becomes standard vocabulary in periodical criticism for verse that fails Addison's ideal of polite ease. By the nineteenth century the semantic field had expanded: doggerel was used of the street ballads and broadsides hawked in cities, of nursery rhymes dismissed by serious critics, and of comic verse in magazines such as Punch. The Victorian era also generated the affectionate revaluation: the limerick craze and the nonsense verse of Edward Lear and Lewis Carroll showed that "bad" verse could be deliberately, skilfully bad, and reviewers began to speak of "good doggerel" as an oxymoron that named a real achievement. The negative core survives, but a comic-positive valence has been layered on top. THE MODERN PICTURE Today doggerel remains a working critical term and a colloquial dismissal. In criticism it still names metrically careless, tritely rhymed verse, and it is applied to amateur poetry, advertising jingles, and sing-song political rhymes. In everyday speech it is used self-deprecatingly ("a bit of doggerel") and contemptuously ("that's pure doggerel"). The word also carries a practical, mnemonic association that the source text exploits: doggerel is memorable, and rough rhyme is one of the oldest devices for fixing information - genealogies, family lists, political maps - in memory. The semantic bridge from "bad verse" to "useful verse" is exactly what the passage activates, when the usher's verses, though metrically contemptible, are the practical directory of the great houses of the district. SOUND AND FORM Because the word is an English-internal compound, it has no long chain of sound shifts; its pronunciation settled as /d|A|guhruhl/ in RP, with stress on the first syllable, a short stressed vowel in "dog", and a reduced final syllable in "-rel". The spelling was unstable in the seventeenth century ("dogrel", "dogerel", "doggerell"), settling on "doggerel" with the doubled "-gg-" by the eighteenth century.
  • c. 1050: Old English "docga" first attested in the Leiden glosses, origin unknown.
  • c. 1150: Middle English "dogge" becomes the standard word.
  • 14th c.: the pejorative suffix "-rel" is borrowed into English ("mongrel", "wastrel").
  • 1630: "doggerel" first attested as a label for rough, comic verse.
  • 1663: Butler's "Hudibras" turns doggerel-like octosyllabics into the respected "Hudibrastic" genre.
  • 18th c.: the word becomes standard critical vocabulary (Addison, Swift).
  • 19th c.: extended to street ballads, nursery rhymes, and magazine verse; the "good doggerel" revaluation appears.
  • 20th-21st c.: survives in criticism and colloquial speech, both contemptuous and self-deprecating.
LITERARY AND CULTURAL CONCLUSION The history of "doggerel" is therefore a history of critical taste: a word born as an insult in the pamphlet wars of the 1630s, turned into a comic genre by Butler's burlesque, institutionalised as critical vocabulary in the eighteenth century, revalued affectionately by the nonsense poets of the nineteenth, and retained today as a flexible, slightly mocking label for rhyme that is rough, memorable, comic, and useful. Its evolution shows a pejorative label repeatedly being reclaimed by comic practice, while never losing its core meaning of deliberately or inadvertently clumsy verse.
