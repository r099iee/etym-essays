Parlour is one of the least expected words in the English lexicon, because its biography runs from a Greek verb meaning “to throw” to a room full of sofas and conversation. The story shows how a word can cross languages, change syntactic class twice, and migrate from monastery to manor to high street, all the while keeping a single thread of meaning — speaking and receiving.

The oldest attested stage is Greek. The PIE root *gʷelH-, reconstructed as “to throw, to strike,” is standardly cited as the source of Greek bállein “to throw,” and from that verb Greek formed the compound parabolḗ, literally “a throwing beside” (παρά “beside” + βολή “a throw”). The concrete image — placing one thing beside another — became “a comparison, an illustration, a juxtaposition.” This is the word of the New Testament parable: Jesus’ parabolḗ is a story thrown alongside a truth. When Latin borrowed parabola from Greek, it took the comparison sense (whence English parable and the geometric parabola, the curve defined by the “comparison” of its parts) — but in Christian Latin, from the 3rd–4th century CE onward, parabola also came to mean simply “speech, a word,” the sense preserved in French parole and in the Italian parola.

From that speech-noun, the Romance-speaking world rebuilt a verb: Vulgar Latin *parabolare, later shortened to *parlare, “to speak,” the ancestor of French parler and the whole Romance family of “speak” words (Italian parlare, Spanish hablar from a different source, Portuguese falar). Now the socio-cultural pivot occurs. In medieval monasticism, where the Benedictine rule demanded silence, an exception had to be made for necessary conversation; monasteries therefore had a room where speech was permitted, called in Old French the parloir or parleur — literally “the speaking-place” — the architectural cousin of the cloister’s locutorium. The word entered Middle English as parlour around 1200, still meaning “a room where one speaks,” and from the monastery it secularized: in manor houses and then town houses it named the private room set aside for family conversation and for receiving guests, as opposed to the kitchen, the hall, or the bedchamber.

In the modern period the parlour became a social institution. In Victorian and Edwardian Britain, the parlour was the best-furnished room of a respectable household, kept for receiving visitors, where the parlour-maid served and families entertained one another at parlour games; it encoded the middle-class cult of gentility and display. From that domestic heart the word then migrated outward again into commerce, so that a “parlour” is now any shop or service room that receives the public — beauty parlour, ice-cream parlour, funeral parlour, tattoo parlour, pizza parlour — while the home’s formal room was renamed the living-room. The American spelling parlor reflects Noah Webster’s 19th-century reform of the -our ending; parlour had been standard on both sides of the Atlantic until then.

Timeline of parlour:

  • 5th–4th c. BCE: Greek parabolḗ, “a throwing beside, a comparison,” from bállein “to throw.”
  • 3rd–4th c. CE: Christian Latin parabola develops the sense “speech, word.”
  • Proto-Romance: *parabolare → *parlare “to speak”; Old French parler.
  • c. 1200: Old French parloir (the monastery speaking-room) enters Middle English as parlour.
  • Later Middle Ages–early modern: the word secularizes from monastery to manor and town house.
  • 19th century: the Victorian parlour of gentility, the parlour-maid, and the parlour game.
  • 20th century: commercial “parlours” (beauty, ice-cream, funeral) flourish while the home room is renamed the living-room.

In Tolkien’s scene the semantics are thematically neat: the dwarves occupy the very room whose name means “where one speaks and receives,” and fill it with trays, tables, and noise — hospitality rewritten as occupation. The word has come a long way from “throwing beside,” yet every step of the road is audible in its final meaning.
