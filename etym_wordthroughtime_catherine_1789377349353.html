- 4th to 10th Century - Ancient Greek *Aikaterínē* emerges as a Christian baptismal name associated with Saint Catherine of Alexandria; early Christian scholars link it by folk etymology to Greek *katharos* ("pure"), prompting the -th- spelling.
- 11th to 14th Century - Crusaders and pilgrims return from Saint Catherine's Monastery on Mount Sinai, igniting massive veneration across Western Europe; the name enters Old French and Anglo-Norman as *Caterine* / *Katherine*, becoming one of the most beloved medieval baptismal names.
- 15th to 16th Century - In England, Saint Catherine's iconographic attribute (the spiked breaking wheel) enters popular folklore; English queens (Catherine of Valois, Catherine of Aragon, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr) cement its royal prestige.
- 17th to 18th Century - British pyrotechnicians invent the spinning pinwheel firework and christen it the "Catherine wheel" after the saint's torture engine; simultaneously applied to architectural wheel windows.
- 19th Century - Victorian culture embraces the Catherine wheel as a holiday firework, immortalized by Charles Dickens and Oscar Wilde's pensive, philosophical Catherine Wheel in *The Remarkable Rocket* (1888); enters athletic slang for cartwheels.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues as an enduring, timeless personal name across the globe (Catherine, Katherine, Kate, Cathy) and remains the standard Commonwealth term for spinning pyrotechnic wheels and cartwheeling acrobatics.
