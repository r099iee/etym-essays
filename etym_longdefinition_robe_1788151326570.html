Definition. A robe is a long, loose outer garment that hangs from the shoulders to the knee, ankle, or floor, worn either as ordinary informal dress — especially a bathrobe or dressing gown in North American usage — or, far more characteristically, as a mark of office, rank, or ritual in ceremonial contexts: ecclesiastical vestments, academic gowns, judicial attire, and the investiture garments of monarchs and priests. The word’s core semantic content is flowing, enveloping coverage: unlike a fitted coat or jacket, a robe does not follow the body’s contours but conceals them beneath a swathe of fabric. Because the garment is so closely tied to institutions of authority, robe has generated a rich cluster of secondary and metaphorical senses that modern readers meet far more often than the garment itself.

  • Ceremonial and official garment (primary sense). Robes are the prescribed dress of priests (the priestly robe of Wordsworth’s line), judges, academics at graduation, kings at coronation, and members of learned societies. The robe is thus a visual carrier of office: it signals that the wearer acts not as a private person but as the occupant of a role. A crowned head, a bishop, and a barrister are all “robed” before they are anything else.
  • The robe as metonymy for a profession. In legal English, the long robe or simply the robe designates the legal profession — barristers and judges — exactly as the bar, the bench, or the ermine do. The semantic bridge is purely metonymic: the distinctive garment stands for the body of wearers and, by a further step, for the institutions they serve. A French parallel, gens de robe (“men of the gown”), shows the same logic in early modern Europe’s nobility of the robe, officials whose rank rested on office rather than birth.
  • Religious and literary vestment senses. In religious idiom, “robing” describes investiture with priestly or monastic dress, and by metaphorical extension with the authority the dress confers. Literary usage generalises the metaphor: to be “robed in silence,” “robed in moonlight,” or “robed in majesty” is to be clothed or enveloped by an immaterial quality as if by fabric. This figurative “robed in ...” pattern is among the most productive uses of the word in modern prose and poetry.
  • North American informal garment (bathrobe or dressing gown). In American and Canadian English, unqualified robe most commonly means a bathrobe — a soft, loose, absorbent garment worn after bathing or over nightwear. The bridge from ceremonial garment to bathrobe is the shared feature of a loose, wrap-around, unconstructed outer layer worn over other clothing; the two poles of the word’s range (solemn and domestic) show semantic drift along the single dimension of “loose enveloping garment.” In British English the everyday garment is normally called a dressing gown, and robe keeps more of its official weight.
  • Verb sense: to robe / robed.To robe, and the passive participle robed, means to dress someone or oneself in a robe, or more loosely in any flowing garment: robed in white, robed in purple, a robed choir. The participle is common in formal descriptive prose and in the language of liturgy (the celebrant “robes” for Mass). This verbal use extends the noun’s covering-image to an action: to robe is to invest, to wrap, to array.
  • Institutional and heraldic display senses. In heraldry, portraiture, and antiquarian usage, robes appear as symbols of dignity — ermine robes, coronation robes, aldermanic gowns — and phrases such as robe of state perfect the garment’s conversion into an instrument of institutional display rather than bodily protection.

Polysemy and its historical bridge. The word entered English from French after the Norman Conquest as a general word for a fine outer garment. Its subsequent development ran along two tracks. On the first, the garment narrowed and formalised into the ceremonial vestment of church, court, bench, and university, then passed by metonymy into names for the professions themselves. On the second, the sense drifted down the register scale to the homely bathrobe. Both tracks rest on the same ground — “a loose outer covering” — which is also exactly why the word is so readily available for metaphor of envelopment (“robed in ...”). A single Germanic root that originally meant “booty, clothes stripped from the slain” (“rob” is the same family) thus ends up draping both the Lord Chief Justice and a shower curtain: the word lost none of its covering function but gained every register in which a human being can be wrapped.
