The hyphenated nonce-noun death-where-is-thy-sting-fullness denotes a triumphant, exultant aura or manner - the air of someone behaving as if they have vanquished all opposition and can taunt defeat itself, echoing St Paul's victorious cry O death, where is thy sting? It is used ironically and disapprovingly in the source context to describe a self-satisfied, gloating fullness of manner that the observer finds distasteful.

In broader literary use, such Wodehousean hyphen-chains turn an entire clause or quotation into a single abstract noun in -ness, treating a fleeting tone as a settled quality. The core sense is therefore not theological but social and psychological: swaggering complacency, resurrection-like bounce-back, the flush of someone who believes nemesis has been cancelled. It implies abundance (fullness) of that spirit - not a momentary smile but a pervasive atmosphere surrounding the person, noticed at once and hard to ignore.

The register is consciously comic-highbrow: biblical grandeur applied to drawing-room triviality to create bathos. It belongs to comic prose, clubman narration and ironic character-sketch, never to formal exposition, and would be glossed in ordinary language as triumphalism, crowing or gloating self-congratulation. The narrator's distaste is part of the meaning in context: the same display admired as resilience by friends is condemned as smugness by rivals, and the long compound itself performs that inflation.

  • Triumphant manner and gloating aura (primary, contextual): A perceptible quality about her manner - self-congratulatory, exultant, insufferably pleased with herself, as if immune to reversal. Bridge: direct transfer of Paul's victory-over-death taunt to victory-over-social-circumstance.
  • Biblical allusion and Pauline victory (allusive sense): Evokes 1 Corinthians 15:55 and the Resurrection promise that death has lost its poison. Used at Easter, in Handel's Messiah, at funerals to proclaim hope. Bridge: retained by cultural memory; Wodehouse borrows its triumphant intonation while discarding doctrine.
  • Fullness as plenitude and repleteness (morphological sense): Fullness elsewhere means state of being full, satiety, completeness, spiritual abundance (fullness of time, fullness of joy). Here it nominalizes the taunt into a measurable aura. Bridge: productive -ness suffix that turns adjectives and phrases into character traits.
  • Distasteful complacency and smugness (pragmatic extension): In context the narrator's distaste adds a pejorative secondary reading: not admirable resilience but offensive smugness. Bridge: same triumphal display judged from the loser's perspective becomes crowing.

Domain notes: in theology the source phrase remains solemn and consolatory; in comic literature the hyphenated noun is mock-heroic and deflating; in everyday paraphrase it would be rendered as she was insufferably triumphant, full of herself. The form has no technical, scientific or legal sense and no currency outside this stylistic joke, but any literate reader decodes it instantly because the Authorized Version wording is still culturally alive, and the -ness ending signals character-trait abstraction familiar from goodness, darkness and heart-bowed-downness.
