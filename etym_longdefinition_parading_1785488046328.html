Core meaning. PARADING is the present participle and gerund of the verb parade: marching in a showy ceremonial procession, or — far more often in modern use — displaying, exhibiting, or showing something off conspicuously, "as if in a parade", with proud self-regard and an eye on the audience. The word always implies a performance: a parade is staged to be seen, and to parade something is to walk it out before onlookers for their admiration. It is, accordingly, a word of mild mockery in ordinary use — the person who parades his wealth, learning, or virtues is exhibiting them ostentatiously rather than simply possessing them.

In this passage. The sentence comes from a survey of the history of English metrical theory: the old schoolroom handbooks are accused of "parading lists of Greek terms and recherché metres culled from Swinburne and Bridges". The word is loaded with amused, gently mocking irony. These volumes do not merely list their learning; they parade it — they march their erudition out in full ceremonial display, as if on military review or in the pageant of scholarship, proud, self-conscious, and a little vain. The theatrical undertone is essential: a parade is always a performance staged for an audience, and the passage damns these books with faint praise by suggesting that they are performing erudition rather than quietly possessing it. Because it is a present participle, the word insists on duration and habit — the parading is a standing disposition of these handbooks, an ongoing ceremony rather than a single lapse — and its grammatical object, "lists", keeps the image at once concrete (lists are literally marched out for inspection) and figurative (the gesture is one of ostentatious display). The overall tone is that of an amused connoisseur surveying a curiosity-shop of dead scholarship: respectful of the effort, teasing about the vanity.

Polysemy: the senses of PARADE/PARADING today. The word family spreads widely from its military core:

  • Military sense. A parade is a formal muster and review of troops; to parade troops is to assemble and march them for inspection. The oldest English sense (17th century). "Parade-ground" is the physical place of assembly.
  • Public procession. A parade is a public procession or street spectacle — a carnival parade, a victory parade — staged for onlookers. In British English, "the parade" can be a row of shops or the seafront promenade (from the sense of a place where people walk to be seen).
  • Figurative display. To parade something (a quality, a possession, a fact) is to display it ostentatiously: "he paraded his knowledge", "she paraded her wealth". This is the dominant modern sense and the one in this passage. The participle parading names this habit: parading erudition, parading ignorance, parading virtue.
  • To walk about to be seen. To parade (intransitive) is to walk or march up and down in a showy way, to strut, to promenade — "she paraded up and down the street in her new coat".
  • A succession of things. "A parade of" something — "a parade of celebrities", "an endless parade of excuses" — means an unbroken, often tiresome series; the image is of a procession filing past.
  • Fixed phrases and modern jargon. "Rain on someone's parade" (to spoil their triumph); "hit parade" or "chart parade" (a list of popular records, from the sense of a marching list); "identity parade" (British, a police line-up); "parading" in fashion (the modelling of clothes on a catwalk).

Semantic bridge. All these senses descend from the same root image of soldiers standing ready and marching in formation for review. Latin parāre meant "to make ready, to prepare"; its participle parātus, "made ready", produced a word for troops drawn up in ordered array — a body prepared for display and inspection. From "an ordered standing-ready of troops" the meaning passed to "a military review", then to "any public procession", and then, figuratively, to "showing something off" — the internal review of one's own possessions or attainments staged for an audience. The modern parading of Greek terms in a handbook is therefore a direct descendant of troops marching past a general: the same display, the same audience, the same faint vanity.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Flaunt adds aggressive, shameless display and is morally charged — "flaunt one's wealth" accuses vulgarity rather than vanity. Showcase is modern, neutral, and commercial, suggesting benign presentation of a product without self-regard. Display is colourless and merely perceptual, unable to carry the procession-and-audience pageantry of a parade. Brandish is limited to flourishing a weapon or other hand-held object, and adds menace rather than showmanship. Strut, of people, means walking with an exaggerated, self-important gait — the strut is the bodily expression of the parade's vanity. Parade alone combines the ceremonial procession, the audience, and the self-conscious showing-off in a single word.

Grammar and register. As a participle/gerund, parading takes an object ("parading lists"), works progressively ("the handbooks are parading their learning"), and names the activity itself ("the parading of Greek terms"). Register: formal-to-literary, with a faintly satirical, mocking edge; comfortable in criticism, journalism, and scholarly prose — exactly the register of the amused survey in which it appears.
