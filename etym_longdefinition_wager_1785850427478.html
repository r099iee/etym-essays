WAGER (noun and verb): as a noun, an act of betting — the risking of money, goods, or something of value on the outcome of an uncertain event, or the sum or item so risked; a bet, a stake. As a verb, to risk something of value on an uncertain outcome; to bet; and, in a common weakened figurative use, to state confident conjecture ("I'd wager the storm will clear by evening"). The word's deep history lies in the legal tradition of the pledge: a wager was originally a solemn deposit, a security handed over as a guarantee, and the modern bet is the descendant of that ancient gesture of staking one's word and goods on a claim. To make a wager is thus, etymologically, to put something in pledge — to lay your stake down as security for your confidence. The word remains slightly formal: it is the polite, literary, or legal cousin of the everyday word bet. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • As a noun, the act of betting: to make a wager, to enter into a wager — the agreement by which two parties stake something of value on the outcome of an uncertain event; in legal terms, a contract of chance, an aleatory contract.
  • As a noun, the stake itself: the money or object risked — to place a wager of a hundred pounds, to double one's wager; the wager is the thing wagered as much as the act of wagering.
  • As a verb, the act of betting: to wager money on a horse, to wager a year's wages on the outcome; formal or literary where bet is everyday. The context sentence uses the noun sense: the grandmaster treats the match as a high-stakes wager that would define his legacy.
  • Figurative expression of confidence: "I'll wager that...", "I'd wager he never expected this" — the word means "I confidently predict, I'd bet"; the stake is mental, not monetary. This is the sense in one of the example sentences: "I would wager that the storm will clear before evening".
  • Philosophical and theological usage: Pascal's Wager (Pascal's pari) — the seventeenth-century argument of Blaise Pascal that belief in God is a rational wager, since the potential gain (infinite salvation) outweighs the potential loss (finite pleasure); the word wager here names a whole decision-theoretic argument and is a fixed term in the philosophy of religion.
  • Legal and historical usage: the wager of law and the wager of battle — medieval English procedures in which a defendant proved a case by oath-helpers (compurgation) or by trial by combat; wager retains these historical legal meanings in discussions of medieval law.
  • Colloquial and historical-gambling register: a wager can be the agreed condition or challenge of a bet — "their wager was that the loser would stand dinner"; the word keeps an aura of formality, of gentlemen's agreements and solemn undertakings.
  • Economic and financial extension: a speculative wager, to wager on markets — betting framed as investment strategy, where the language of the gaming table is applied to financial risk-taking.
HISTORICAL BRIDGE: Wager descends from the Germanic word for a pledge. The Frankish *waddi and its Germanic ancestor *wadja meant "a pledge, a security, a guarantee" — something deposited to ensure that an obligation would be met, whether appearance in court, payment of a debt, or the keeping of a promise. Old French and Anglo-French borrowed this word (as wage and wager), and in the medieval legal world a wager was a solemn security: a wager of law required oath-helpers, a wager of battle settled a dispute by combat, and a wage could be the security posted to guarantee appearance in court. From this legal core the modern meaning grew: when two parties wagered on an uncertain outcome, each posted a stake as a pledge of confidence — and the word shifted from the security deposited to the bet itself, from the pledge to the risk taken. The modern "I'll wager that..." simply generalizes the old gesture: to wager is to stake your confidence, to put your word in pledge. REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS:
  • make a wager, place a wager, accept a wager, win or lose a wager, double a wager, lay a wager.
  • wager + amount or object: wager a fortune, wager a year's wages, wager his reputation.
  • wager + proposition (verb + clause): "I wager that the storm will clear by evening" — expressing confident prediction.
  • Fixed and historical phrases: Pascal's Wager; wager of law; wager of battle; the wager of a challenge.
  • Register: slightly elevated, literary, or legal compared with bet; the two are near-synonyms, with bet fully everyday and wager retaining a residue of formal undertaking and legal guarantee. Related but distinct: stake (the sum risked), gamble (reckless risk-taking), pledge (a solemn promise without winnings), venture (risky enterprise), speculation (market risk).
