- Late 15th century - the word enters English from Middle French avant-garde as a purely tactical term (spelled vaward/vangard): the division that marches before the army; perception: technical, military, honourable.
- 16th-17th centuries - the figurative leap: preachers and pamphleteers speak of the vanguard of reform, learning, and the church militant; the word acquires prestige - the vanguard is brave, chosen, and exposed.
- 17th-18th centuries - solidification: vanguard standardises alongside van; naval usage fixes the fleet sense; the metaphor is by now routine but still soldierly.
- 19th century - the artistic and revolutionary turn: in France the avant-garde becomes the radical vanguard of art and politics (Saint-Simon's famous 1825 claim that artists are the avant-garde of society); English re-borrows avant-garde in the 1880s-90s, splitting the old word's family.
- Early 20th century - the party vanguard: Lenin's 'vanguard party' (1902) gives the word a precise organisational sense; later communist and liberation movements carry the term to every continent; perception: disciplined, strategic, slightly sinister.
- Mid-to-late 20th century - technology and business: 'the vanguard of science and industry' becomes journalistic idiom, and the investment firm The Vanguard Group (1975) makes the word a brand of trustworthy leadership.
- 21st century - everyday cliché and critique: 'in the vanguard of X' still confers praise but sounds slightly dated next to 'cutting edge'; perception: dignified, forward-looking, mainstreamed.
