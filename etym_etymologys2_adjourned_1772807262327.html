ADJOURNED is the past tense and past participle of English adjourn, a legal and parliamentary loan whose day-element is Latinate (not the Germanic word day). The chain runs: - PIE *dyew- "sky, heaven, daylight, day" (reconstructed root, source of day-words and sky-god names across Indo-European) → - Latin diēs "day; appointed day, date" (attested Classical Latin; from *dyew- with suffixation) → - Latin diurnus "of a day, daily; lasting a day" (adjective derived from diēs; attested Classical Latin) → - Late Latin diurnum / diurnāle "day, daily record" → Old French jour "day" (via Gallo-Romance; the d- palatalisation typical of the day-word family) → - Old French ajorner / ajourner "to fix a day; to summon to appear on an appointed day; to put off to a day" (a- < Latin ad "to" + jour "day"; attested from the 10th–11th centuries in legal usage) → - Middle English ajornen / adjournen "to summon to a day; to put off to another day; to suspend a sitting" (via Anglo-French; attested from the 13th century) → - English adjourn "to suspend (a formal body) to another day; to break off with the intention of resuming" (attested from the 14th century) → - ADJOURNED (past tense and past participle; also used as participial adjective). Notes on debated and nuanced points: - The Germanic day (English day, German Tag) is a different word from a different root (Germanic *dagaz), NOT the source of the day-element in adjourn; the day here is the Latinate diēs / diurnus path through Old French jour. - The same PIE *dyew- root yields Latin Iuppiter / Iovis (sky-father), Greek Zeus, and Sanskrit dyáuḥ "sky, day" — distant cognates, not the direct chain of adjourn. - French jour and journal come from Late Latin diurnum / diurnāle "daily (record)"; English journey "a day's travel" is from Old French jornée < *diurnāta "day's (travel)" — sisters in the same day-family. - The written d in English adjourn (absent from the pronunciation /əˈdʒɜːn/) reflects a spelling partly remodelled on Latin ad + diurnus; Middle English often wrote ajournen, and the d was restored in spelling rather than in sound.
