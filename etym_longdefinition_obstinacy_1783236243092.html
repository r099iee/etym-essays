obstinacy: In the famous opening of Macaulay's History of England, the American Revolution is explained as a fracture brought about by British policy: "how imprudence and obstinacy broke the ties which bound the North American colonies to the parent state." The word is loaded with moral judgment. Obstinacy here names the unyielding, self-defeating rigidity of the British ministry — a refusal to listen, to revise, or to compromise that the historian casts as culpable rather than admirable. In the sentence it is yoked to "imprudence," so that two vices jointly produce catastrophe: folly of judgement joined to hardness of will. The tone is diagnostic and admonitory, the voice of the Whig historian who reads the imperial breach as a failure of character, not merely of policy. Crucially, obstinacy is presented as a flaw with political consequences — stubbornness in power that snapped the bonds of empire. It is not firmness, not principle; it is rigidity mistaken for resolve.

Core meaning. Obstinacy is stubbornness: unyielding adherence to an opinion, purpose, or course of action in the face of argument, persuasion, or evidence. It is the noun corresponding to the adjective obstinate. Its defining feature is resistance to reason — persistence not because one is right but because one will not move. The word is formal and literary, always available for moral censure, and it names a disposition of the will rather than a single act.

Polysemy and shades of meaning.

  • Unreasonable stubbornness (the dominant, censuring sense): persistence against reason or advice — "obstinacy of the ministry," "she argued with him to no avail; his obstinacy was absolute." This is the sense in Macaulay.
  • Determination or tenacity viewed positively (rhetorically contested): obstinacy can be reclaimed as admirable grit when the cause is just — "the obstinacy with which the reformers clung to their principles." The moral polarity flips with the speaker's sympathy; the word itself is neutral as to the value of the cause, negative as to the unyieldingness.
  • Animal or mechanical refusal (extended): "the obstinacy of a mule," "the obstinacy of a jammed lock" — a transferred use from persons to things and animals that resist coercion, in which the moral censure is softened to mere picturesqueness.
  • Medical and pathological (archaic/specialised): obstinacy of the bowels, an older clinical term for stubborn constipation, showing the word's application to physical refusal to yield — now rare outside historical medical writing.
  • Legal and political (modern): "obstinate refusal," "obstruction" alongside obstinacy in the vocabulary of parliamentary and judicial deadlock, where the word names a party's refusal to consent or budge.

Historical bridge and semantic reasoning. The root is Latin stāre "to stand"; obstināre means literally "to stand firm against" (ob- "towards, against" + a making-stand stem). The physical image — planting oneself in the way — underlies the whole semantic history: from "standing firm" (honourable, of soldiers) the word shifted to "standing firm against persuasion" (culpable, of the wilful), and the moral censure hardened under Christian theology, where obstinacy became persistence against right, reason, or grace. The semantic core has never left the image of standing — a person who will not move.

Register and the synonymic field. Stubbornness is the plain, everyday synonym, morally almost neutral and describing a disposition rather than a disastrous policy; obstinacy is its formal, literary counterpart with a darker charge. Obduracy implies a hardening of the heart — obstinacy against what is right or good — and is more severe and theological in flavour. Pertinacity stresses energetic, clinging persistence in a purpose and can be admiring, whereas obstinacy always resists reason. Tenacity and perseverance are positive virtues; obstinacy is persistence without justification. Intransigence is the modern political term for an uncompromising refusal, often principled and therefore sometimes respectable; recalcitrance suggests open, defiant resistance; doggedness and pertinacity lean admirable; pig-headedness, bull-headedness, and mulishness are the colloquial, mock-animal equivalents. In Macaulay, obstinacy is chosen over all of these because it is weighty, formal, and morally decisive — the perfect noun for imperial rigidity.
