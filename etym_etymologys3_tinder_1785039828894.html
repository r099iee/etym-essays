The biography of TINDER begins among the fire-works of the Germanic peoples. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic verbal root *tund- "to kindle, set alight" lies beneath a wide family of kindling-verbs across the northern languages: Gothic tandjan, Old Norse tenda and tendra, Swedish tända, Danish tænde, Norwegian tenne, and Old English tendan — all meaning "to light, set on fire." From this verbal stock the noun *tundrą, literally "that which is kindled," was formed, and it is this noun that descends directly into Old English as tynder (also spelled tyndre), attested in the earliest centuries of English for the dry, scorched material — linen, fungus, rotten wood — kept ready at the hearth to catch a spark.

An important and honest caveat: unlike most fire-words, this Germanic family has no securely attested Indo-European ancestor. Etymonline and the OED describe *tund- as being "of unknown origin," and although some scholars have proposed a PIE base *dendh-/*dhundh- "to burn, blaze," the suggestion remains conjecture. This is itself a remarkable fact about a word so central to daily life: it suggests that the Germanic kindling-word may have been coined or reshaped within Germanic itself, or borrowed from a non-Indo-European neighbour of whom no trace survives. The etymology is thus genuinely debated at its deepest point, and the honest scholarly verdict is that we can trace the word with certainty only from Proto-Germanic onward.

  • Old English to Middle English: alongside the noun tynder, Old English had the verb tendan "to kindle." In Middle English the verb survived as tenden, teend, and tind (now obsolete or dialectal), while the noun stabilized in a range of spellings — tendre, tyndre, tinder, tyndir. The variation in spelling reflects pronunciation drift and dialect mixture; the modern form tinder won out by the Early Modern period.
  • The tinder-box revolution (16th century): by 1520 the compound tinder-box is recorded — a small metal or wooden box holding tinder, flint, and steel, the everyday fire-starter of every household before matches. The tinder-box was so universal that by 1590 it had already acquired a figurative sense: "anything easily set ablaze," a person or situation quick to anger, passion, or conflict.
  • Semantic drift outward: from the concrete hearth-object, the word extended by metaphor to any volatile situation. A political region ready to explode becomes a "tinder-box"; a crowd on the edge of violence is "tinder-dry." The bridge is the low ignition threshold: the property that makes tinder catch a single spark is exactly the property of a volatile crowd or region.
  • The match, the decline of the tinder-box, and the word's survival: in the 19th century, friction matches (Lucifer matches, safety matches) replaced flint-and-steel, and the tinder-box retreated from daily use. The word did not die: it survived in camping and survival craft, in the fixed phrase "tinder and flint," and in dialect and idiom.
  • The 2012 naming of the dating app: the dating application Tinder deliberately exploited the ancient metaphor — a quick mutual "spark" kindles a match. The name is a brilliant compression of the word's whole history: the app is a tinder-box of human attraction.
  • Tolkien's usage: in The Hobbit, "before you could say tinder and flint" is a compressed folk-proverb of speed. It draws on the genuine, age-old pairing of the two words — tinder and flint were the inseparable halves of fire-making — and on the broader family of "before you could say X" idioms that measure time by the utterance of a short familiar formula.

Sound-shift notes: the initial cluster of Old English tynder preserved the PGmc *t- intact (no Grimm's Law change applies here, *t being original); the later spellings tendre show a nasal/mannerism in Middle English before the -n- of the verbal forms (tendan, tenden) reasserted itself in the standard noun tinder. Modern RP preserves the stressed short /ɪ/: /ˈtɪndə/. The word has never shifted its central sense in a thousand years — it has only accumulated figurative layers on top of the literal one.
