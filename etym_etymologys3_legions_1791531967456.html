Legion is a Latin military word that outlived the army it described and then multiplied into a dozen figurative careers. Its history is unusually well documented, because the Romans wrote constantly about their own army and because the word was carried into the Bible, into medieval chronicles and into the modern languages of Europe.

Etymology and the Roman levy

Latin legio, genitive legionis, is a noun of action from legere “to gather, pick out, choose,” and means literally “a choosing out,” hence “a levy of troops.” The verb belongs to a large Indo-European family: Greek legein “to gather, say,” logos “word, account,” Latin lex “law,” legare “to appoint,” diligere “to value,” intellegere “to understand,” and English lecture, legend, lesson, select, collect and elegant. The underlying root is usually reconstructed as *leǵ- “to gather,” though a homophonous *leǵ- “to speak” has been proposed for the “read/say” senses; the two may be distinct.

Polybius and the manipular legion

Polybius, the Greek historian who described the Roman constitution in the second century BCE, gives the classic account of the manipular legion: 4,200 infantry and 300 cavalry, of whom 1,200 were hastati, 1,200 principes, 600 triarii and 1,200 velites. The three heavy lines fought in relays, the maniples of 120 men leaving gaps that the next line could fill. This flexibility, rather than sheer numbers, explains Roman success against the phalanx, and Polybius says so explicitly.

The Marian and Augustan legion

The reforms associated with Marius around 107 BCE abolished the property qualification, opened recruitment to the poor, and made the legion a professional career. The cohort of about 480 men became the tactical unit; ten cohorts made a legion, with the first cohort double strength. Augustus regularised the system, fixed the number of legions at 28, and settled veterans in colonies across the empire. After Varus lost three legions in the Teutoburg Forest in 9 CE, the total settled at 25 and rarely exceeded thirty. The legion was now a permanent institution with a treasury, a hospital, a workshop and a religious calendar.

Late Rome and the word’s survival

Under Diocletian and Constantine the old legion was fragmented into smaller units, and the Notitia Dignitatum lists legiones of greatly varying size. The word outlived the institution because Latin remained the language of law, war and the Church. It passed into Old French as legion in the twelfth century and into Middle English as legioun in the thirteenth, usually in translations of Roman history or of the Bible.

The Vulgate and the demonic legion

The most consequential non-military use is the Gospel story of the Gerasene demoniac. In Mark 5:9 and Luke 8:30 the possessed man says, “My name is Legion, for we are many.” Jerome’s Vulgate renders it Legio nomen mihi est, and the verse made “legion” a ready metaphor for a multitude of demons, sins or troubles. Medieval sermons and exorcism manuals repeated it; the phrase “their name is legion” passed into English as a dignified way of saying “they are innumerable.” The archaic use of legion as a predicate adjective — “their excuses are legion” — is a fossil of this biblical sense.

Early modern to nineteenth century

Renaissance historians and translators kept the Roman sense alive, and Shakespeare’s Roman plays helped fix “legions” in the English literary imagination as a word of immense, orderly power. In the nineteenth century the name was revived for new volunteer and colonial formations: the Polish Legions under Dąbrowski in 1797, the French Foreign Legion in 1831, and various “legions” in the wars of Italian and German unification. The choice was deliberate antiquarianism: a legion sounded Roman, disciplined and expeditionary, unlike a mere regiment.

Twentieth century: total war, veterans, disease

The First World War produced the Czech Legions and the Polish Legions again; the Spanish Legion was founded in 1920. Veterans’ organisations adopted the name — the American Legion in 1919, the Royal British Legion in 1921 — and the French Legion of Honour, founded by Napoleon in 1802, remains the best-known civilian use. In 1976 an outbreak of pneumonia among American Legion members in Philadelphia led to the identification of Legionella pneumophila and the naming of Legionnaires’ disease, a modern accident that gave the ancient word a new scientific life.

Contemporary figurative life

Today “legions” is most often figurative. Sports writing speaks of “legions of fans,” fantasy fiction of “legions of the undead,” and gaming of Space Marine legions and alien legions. The word retains a slightly archaic, grandiose flavour; it is more impressive than “many” and more organised than “horde.” The singular is also used attributively in journalism (“a legion of problems”), and the biblical phrase survives in formal prose.

Nouns of multitude compared

  • Legion vs horde: a legion implies discipline and command; a horde implies disorder and menace.
  • Legion vs army: a legion is a component; an army is the whole. Figuratively, “army” is more neutral, “legion” more literary.
  • Legion vs multitude: “multitude” is purely quantitative; “legion” carries the memory of soldiers and standards.
  • Legion vs myriad: “myriad” is numerical and Greek; “legion” is military and Roman.

The word’s trajectory is a lesson in semantic survival: an administrative term for a draft became the name of Rome’s most famous institution, then a biblical metaphor for plurality, then a reusable brand for armies, charities and diseases. Few Latin words have travelled so far on so little phonological change.
