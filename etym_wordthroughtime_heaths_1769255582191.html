- Old English hǣð: 'untilled waste land' - from the start the word names the open scrub country beyond the plowed fields, the wild, unenclosed margins of the settlement; out of it the church built heathen, 'dweller on the heath.'
- Medieval through Early Modern: heath is a real, everyday English landscape of heather, gorse, and bracken, and it spreads into the very names of the land - Hampstead Heath, Blackheath, Putney Heath - where the word will outlive the wilderness it names.
- 1800s: the Romantic and Victorian imagination adopts the heath as the great English setting of solitude and the sublime - Thomas Hardy's Egdon Heath makes the word a literary monument, 'a tract of unenclosed wild' where the prehistoric past still breathes.
- Contemporary: the ecological sense (heathland) persists, but the wild heaths shrink behind the city - the word survives in place-names, topographical writing, and a wistful, wild-romantic register that still pictures the purple-and-gold scrub of the traveler and the outlaw.
