- [Classical Greece (c. 6th–4th c. BCE)] - established as rhapsōidoi, professional staff-bearing reciters who traveled to festivals to chant Homeric epics in competition under civic regulations - [perception: revered as the indispensable living custodians of Homeric tradition and national cultural identity]
- [Hellenistic & Roman Era (c. 4th c. BCE–2nd c. CE)] - problematized by Plato in the Ion as irrational vessels of divine possession lacking true knowledge, while continuing to entertain imperial audiences in Rome - [perception: admired for dramatic performance skill, but critically viewed by philosophers as emotionally manipulative and intellectually ungrounded]
- [Renaissance & Early Modern English (c. 1600–1700)] - borrowed into English as rhapsodist, used by scholars to describe ancient Homeric chanters, but quickly adapted by satirists to mock bombastic, disorganized writers whose works were mere "loose stitchings" of borrowed verse - [perception: split between scholarly historical respect and literary satirical mockery]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romantic Era] - debated in Homeric scholarship (the "Homeric Question") as the literal stitchers of folk epics, while Romantic poets celebrated the rhapsodist as a sublime, visionary genius inspired by spontaneous feeling - [perception: elevated into a romantic archetype of wild, unfettered emotional expression and poetic ecstasy]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] - preserved in classical history for the ancient Homeric performers, while widely deployed in political and cultural criticism to characterize uncritical, rapturous champions of modern ideas or icons - [perception: recognized as an erudite, classical designation for epic reciters and a sharp descriptor for effusive panegyrists]
