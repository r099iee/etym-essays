Sicily is the English proper name of the largest island in the Mediterranean and, since 1946, an autonomous region of the Italian Republic. In the source sentence — “He became finance officer in Sicily and is credited with having engaged in archaeological work there...” — it denotes the Roman province of Sicilia, the Republic's oldest overseas possession, where Cicero served as quaestor in 75 BCE. The word is a place name: it has no plural, no common-noun sense, and its reference in any sentence is fixed by geography and history rather than by lexical definition.

What is denoted

  • The island: about 25,700 square kilometres, separated from Calabria by the Strait of Messina, dominated by Mount Etna, and occupied successively by Sicani, Sicels and Elymians, then by Greeks, Carthaginians, Romans, Byzantines, Arabs, Normans, Spaniards and Italians.
  • The Roman province: annexed in 241 BCE at the end of the First Punic War, governed by a praetor from 227 BCE and taxed largely in grain. It was Rome's first provincia in the strict sense and the laboratory in which Roman provincial administration was invented.
  • The quaestorship of 75 BCE: Cicero's “finance officer” is the quaestor, posted to Lilybaeum in the west of the island. The quaestorship was the first rung of the cursus honorum, and Sicily gave him the provincial experience and the provincial clients that later underpinned his prosecution of Verres in 70 BCE.
  • The modern region: the Regione Siciliana, with Palermo as capital, one of the five Italian regions holding special status under the constitution of 1948.

Secondary and derived uses

  • As a byword for dense antiquity: archaeologists and travel writers speak of “the Sicily of the north” or of a coastline “like Sicily” when a landscape is crowded with Greek temples. The comparison is metaphorical but always anchored in the island's real archaeology.
  • As political shorthand: in Italian public discourse Sicilia can stand for the structural problems of the Mezzogiorno — clientelism, emigration, organised crime. That connotation was produced by nineteenth- and twentieth-century history, not by the word itself.
  • In fixed phrases: Kingdom of Sicily; Kingdom of the Two Sicilies (1816–1860); Sicilian Vespers (1282); Sicilian Expedition (Athens' disaster of 415–413 BCE); Sicilian Defence, the chess opening first analysed in Palermo.
  • Adjectival and demonymic forms: Sicilian as adjective and noun; Siceliot for the Greek colonists of the island as distinct from the indigenous Sicels; Sicani and Siculi for the older populations.

Register, connotation and collocations

The name is neutral and frequent in historical prose. It collocates with ancient, Greek, Roman, province, grain, Etna, Syracuse and Palermo. Its emotional charge is warm in travel writing and heavily loaded in political commentary. English has no pejorative sense attached to the island name itself; modern insults attach to the adjective in criminal stereotyping, which is a separate and much later development.

Why it matters in this reading

The clause does two things at once. It records a career step — the quaestorship — and it places that step in the province that was at once Rome's oldest possession and its most thoroughly Hellenised one. Sicily explains the rest of the sentence: the archaeological work, the search for Archimedes' tomb at Syracuse, and Cicero's lifelong habit of quoting Greek science. The island is the bridge between Roman administration and Greek learning, and the name is the hinge of the whole passage.
