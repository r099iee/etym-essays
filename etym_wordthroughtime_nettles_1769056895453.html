- The stinging weed of Old English — 'nettle' names the same bristling, burning herb (Urtica dioica) from the earliest English to the present: a plain, useful, hedgerow word whose core sense has not moved in over a thousand years, the plant every country child learns to avoid.
- The poor man's useful plant — Through medieval and early modern Britain the nettle is a working household plant: young shoots eaten as a spring vegetable, brewed into tea, spun into fibre and cloth, its sting pressed into folk remedy — so the word carries a thrifty, cottage, and kitchen-garden register.
- The word learns to sting figuratively — From the 16th century 'to nettle' someone means to provoke or irritate secretly, transferring the plant's chemical sting to psychological annoyance; the noun sprouts the adjective 'nettlesome' ('irritating'), so the plant becomes the metaphor and the metaphor keeps the memory of the sting.
- Contemporary double life — Modern English keeps the literal weed, but the word now spins two ways: a nostalgic foraging revival (nettle soup, nettle tea, sustainable fibre) makes it quietly wholesome and fashionable, while the idiom 'to grasp the nettle' — tackle a difficulty boldly, since a nettle seized firmly stings less than one brushed lightly — turns the very sting into a proverb of courage.
