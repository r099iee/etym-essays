In Grandmother Jia's household arrangements, a muslin summer-bed is a sleeping place fitted with light, plain-woven cotton hangings or curtains — here specified as green — intended for the hot season. When she proposes putting Miss Lin (Dai-yu) in the green muslin summer-bed while shifting Bao-yu, the fabric name signals seasonal comfort, insect and heat management, and the family's refined material culture. The tone is domestic and practical rather than exoticizing: muslin is treated as an ordinary elite household textile, cool to the eye and the skin, not as a lecture on foreign trade. Colour ("green") adds visual coolness to the physical lightness of the cloth. The full sense inventory is as follows:
  • Primary sense — "lightweight cotton cloth in a plain weave": a fine, loose, translucent-to-semi-opaque cotton textile, the standard fabric name in modern dressmaking and household use; the sense given in the card's shortDefinition.
  • Fashion and dress sense: muslin dresses and garments, historically and today; in the 18th–19th centuries white and printed muslins were the fashionable dress fabric of warm weather, worn by women across Europe and colonial India.
  • Interior sense: muslin curtains, bed hangings, and summer-beds — the lightweight curtains of the Red Chamber passage, chosen for coolness, airiness, and soft light.
  • Dressmaker's sense — "toile muslin": the cheap, firm muslin used to make toiles, the trial garments in which a design is fitted before the final fabric is cut; "muslin" is the workhorse of pattern-making.
  • Kitchen sense — "muslin / cheesecloth": the open-weave cloth used to strain liquids, wrap herbs and poultry, and line moulds; in British usage "muslin" often names the kitchen straining cloth that American usage calls cheesecloth.
  • Baby sense: soft muslin swaddles, wraps, and burp cloths, prized for breathability and gentleness on infants' skin.
  • Historical colonial sense: the fine muslins of Bengal — above all the legendary Dacca muslin — imported to Europe as luxury goods in the 17th–19th centuries; "muslin" became the emblem of the East India trade in textiles.
  • Technical and trade senses: "book muslin" for bookbinding; "muslin" in theatre for gauze and scrim; "mull" and "jamdani" as fine varieties within the family.
  • Figurative sense: "muslin" as a byword for fineness, softness, and delicacy — a fabric name used metaphorically for anything light, gauzy, or airy.
The defining properties of muslin are weave and weight: it is a plain (tabby) weave of carded or combed cotton yarn, distinct from twills like denim or satins like sateen, and it is light enough to be translucent. Those two properties — lightness and breathability — explain the fabric's entire career: it is cool against the skin, easy to drape, cheap to produce at scale, and receptive to dye and print. Unlike calico, which is a heavier, coarser printed cotton, muslin is valued precisely for its fineness. The boundary against near-synonyms sharpens the sense:
  • Cotton: the fibre or any cloth made from it; muslin is a specific light plain weave, not cotton in general.
  • Calico: a heavier, typically coarser printed cotton; muslin is lighter and usually not the printed-coarse trade fabric.
  • Cambric: a fine, denser white fabric of linen or cotton with a different finish; muslin is looser and often translucent.
  • Gauze: a very open, transparent weave, often of cotton or silk; muslin is fuller, its weave tighter than true gauze though lighter than calico.
  • Cheesecloth: a very coarse, open muslin-type cloth; muslin proper is finer and used for garments as well as straining.
  • Lawn: a very fine, crisp cotton or linen used for shirts and handkerchiefs; muslin is softer and less crisp.
  • Organdy / organdie: a stiff, crisp fine cotton; muslin is soft and drapey, not stiffened.
  • Batiste: a fine, soft, closely woven cotton or linen; close to muslin, but batiste is denser and smoother, muslin more open and matt.
  • Voile: a sheer, semi-transparent fabric; muslin, though light, has a fuller body and clearer weave structure.
Collocations and phrases:
  • Bed and interior: "muslin summer-bed," "muslin curtains," "muslin hangings" — the pattern in the passage.
  • Fashion: "white muslin dress," "muslin gown," "printed muslin."
  • Dressmaking: "muslin toile," "a muslin of the jacket."
  • Kitchen: "muslin cloth," "to strain through muslin."
  • Baby: "muslin swaddle," "muslin squares."
  • Historical: "Dacca muslin," "Bengal muslin," "the muslin trade."
In the passage, the word quietly does the work of characterising a great household: the green muslin summer-bed is at once a practical response to the heat and a token of the family's refined, well-ordered domesticity — a fabric light enough for summer and fine enough for a Jia household.
