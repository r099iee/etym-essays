The plural noun falls, "waterfall", is the endpoint of one of the longest and best-attested chains in English: a prehistoric verb of descent that has travelled from the Indo-European homeland to Niagara. The root is reconstructed as PIE *pol- "to fall", on chiefly Baltic and Germanic evidence: the Baltic material is early and suggestive — Lithuanian puolti "to fall", Old Prussian aupallai "finds", literally "falls upon" — while the Germanic family is rich and consistent: Old English feallan, Old Saxon fallan, Old Frisian falla, Old Norse falla, Old High German fallan, Dutch vallen, German fallen, all "to fall". The Proto-Germanic etymon *fallaną was a strong verb of the seventh class — the class whose older strata show reduplication and whose Old English members show the characteristic vowel alternations instead — and Old English feallan preserves the pattern perfectly: present feallan, past feoll, past participle gefallen, with the breaking of the vowel before the double -ll- that the same class shows in weallan "to boil" and heall "hall".

The noun: from ruin to geography

The noun grew directly out of the verb, as nouns so often do in Old English. Old English feall and gefeall meant "a falling, a ruin, a death" — the moral and existential senses come first, centuries before any geography. To fall is to fail, to die, to lapse, and a "fall" is the event of that failure: the Fall of Man, the fall of kings, the fall of pride; the word carried the whole weight of the biblical and fatal sense from the beginning, and it kept it through Middle English (fal, fall: the fall of Troy, the fall of Adam, the fall of the year). Two concrete applications slowly thickened the noun's practical life. First, the season: "fall of the leaf" (16th century) named autumn, and American English — with its famous conservatism — pinned "fall" to the calendar from the seventeenth century on, the only major English dialect in which the season is called the fall. Second, water: rivers and rain "fall" from Old English onwards, and by the sixteenth century "a fall of water" is a standing phrase for a waterfall or a spate. The plural "falls" then crystallized as the geographic form — and it is the plural that won, for a good physical reason: a river's descent over a cliff is rarely a single drop but a series of plunges, chutes, rapids, and ledges, and a river names itself by its whole staircase of water.

The Falls, Niagara, and the sublime

The great witness to the plural is Niagara. In the seventeenth century French and English explorers described "the great Fall" or "the Falls" of Niagara — the Swedish traveller Peter Kalm visited in 1750 and wrote of "the Great Fall"; by the eighteenth century "the Falls" was the fixed English name. The nineteenth century made it the emblem of the sublime: the natural wonder that tourists, painters (Church, Bierstadt, Moran) and honeymooners travelled continents to see, the standing illustration in school geographies of what a "cataract" is. Niagara Falls fixed the rule that the plural is the default for waterfalls in English — Great Falls, Reichenbach Falls, Victoria Falls, the Falls of the Ohio — while the singular "fall" survives mainly in technical and poetic uses ("the fall of the river" = the gradient; "a fall of water" in engineering prose). In North American place-naming the plural travelled westward with settlement, and "the falls" also serves as a town-name element (Great Falls, Montana; Niagara Falls, New York): the word lives a double life as geography and as civic identity.

Sound changes and morphology

The phonological journey is classic Old English. West Germanic *fallaną descends to Old English feallan by breaking of *a to ea before the double -ll-; the past stem feoll shows the same colouring in the class-VII ablaut. Middle English smoothed the diphthongs (falle, fell), and modern English then split the reflexes neatly: the verb and the noun keep /fɔːl/ in Received Pronunciation, with some dialects — including the stereotypical Canadian and some northern English speech — showing the distinctive raising of the vowel before -l that gives "school", "pool" their modern sounds. The plural suffix -s is the ordinary English plural, and the word's morphology is otherwise unremarkable — which is precisely the point: falls is a perfectly ordinary plural of a superbly ancient verb-noun, and its whole individuality lies in what it names, not how it is formed.

Secondary and modern senses of the plural

  • Sport (wrestling): "a fall" is a throw or pin that wins a round of a bout; "the best of three falls" (or "two out of three falls") is the classic formula of the wrestling match — the plural names the rounds, a usage fixed in the 19th century and still the vocabulary of the sport.
  • Finance and journalism: "falls" in share, commodity, and price reports ("sharp falls on the market", "falls in output") is twentieth-century staple usage — the noun plural of a crash-adjacent verb, impersonal but carrying the ancient fatal undertone.
  • Weather: rainfalls, snowfalls, and "falls of rain/snow" continue the sixteenth-century pattern of "fall of water"; the compounds are fully independent words now.
  • Season: dialect American "the falls" for autumn (19th century) records the season word — itself from "fall of the leaf" — made plural; standard usage kept the singular, and the plural form survives only in dialect records and literature.
  • Verb agreement: "the falls is" and "the falls are" both occur freely; the plural form is treated as a singular place at will — the same fluidity shown by geographic plurals like "the Netherlands" and "the Straits".

The word thus spans the whole English timeline in one breath: a PIE verb of the second millennium BCE, an Old English noun of ruin and autumn, a sixteenth-century hydrological phrase, a seventeenth-century American place-name, a nineteenth-century tourist sublime, and a twenty-first-century hydrology term. The falls of English are many, and the word has fallen a long way from PIE *pol- without once losing its way.
