- Old English — brēr names the thorny shrub of hedges and wastes, a farmer’s word for what must be cut back.
- Middle English — brere spreads through Chaucer, Langland, and ballads, briars as both nuisance and proverb for hardship.
- 16th–17th c. — poets crown it, Shakespeare’s sweetbriars and the King James thorns and briars making it biblical and fragrant at once.
- 18th–19th c. — folklore fixes the briar patch as refuge, Brer Rabbit’s home and the Sleeping Beauty hedge, danger turned to shelter.
- 19th c. — French bruyère root lends its name to pipes, and English briar bowls become the gentleman’s smoke.
- Today — bog and briar still pair for pathless country, while gardeners plant briar roses on purpose and smokers polish briar pipes with love.
