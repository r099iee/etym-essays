The name Carthage belongs entirely to the Semitic world and has no Proto-Indo-European ancestry at all; its whole lineage runs through Phoenician and Punic, the language of the city itself. The Carthaginians wrote their city's name as Qart-ḥadašt, a compound of two thoroughly attested Semitic elements: qart, ‘city, town’, and ḥadašt, ‘new’ — literally ‘the New City’. The first element is the same root that appears in Hebrew qiryā ‘city, town’ (today in Israeli place-names like Kiryat Gat), in Arabic qarya ‘village’, and in Ugaritic qrt ‘city’, already present in Bronze Age Canaanite. The second element, the root √ḤDŠ ‘to be new’, surfaces in Hebrew ḥādāš ‘new’ (as in Rosh HaShanah, ‘the New Year’), in Arabic jadīd ‘new’, in Aramaic ḥadtā, and in Ge'ez haddis ‘new’ — the compound is thus transparent to any Semitic speaker of antiquity. The Phoenicians planted several colonies named Qart-ḥadašt across the Mediterranean — in Cyprus, Sardinia, and Spain — but the African one, on the coast of what is now Tunisia, so far outstripped its sisters that the name became famous. According to tradition (followed by Timaeus and Virgil), the colony was founded around 814 BC by Tyrian colonists led by Elissa, whom Roman myth called Dido; the ‘new’ of ‘New City’ distinguished the fresh foundation from the old mother-city of Tyre. The city grew into the greatest mercantile power of the western Mediterranean, its silver from Spain, its purple dye from the murex shell, its trade routes reaching Britain and West Africa; by the 3rd century BC it ruled an empire spanning North Africa, Sardinia, Corsica, and much of Iberia. When the Greeks met the Phoenicians of the west they adapted the Punic compound as Καρχηδών (Karkhēdōn), a single place-name whose inner meaning they did not translate; Herodotus already uses this form in the 5th century BC. The Romans took the name from Greek and Latinized it as Carthāgō, and from that Latin form descend all the modern European names — English Carthage, French Carthage, Italian Cartagine, Spanish Cartago. The English word, attested from the fifteenth century, thus rests on a chain of three adaptations: Punic compound, Greek loan, Latin loan, and finally an English proper noun. The semantic history of the name in the European tradition is inseparable from the city's fate. After the three Punic Wars (264–146 BC), Rome destroyed Carthage utterly in 146 BC — Cato's slogan “Carthago delenda est” (“Carthage must be destroyed”) preserved the political will to do so, and Scipio Aemilianus is said to have wept over the burning city, quoting Homer. The Romans later refounded the site as a colony (Colonia Iulia Concordia Carthago), and in 122 BC Caius Gracchus had even attempted a colony there; but the name never lost its double charge of fabulous wealth and total ruin. Rome also founded a Spanish city called Carthāgō Nova, ‘New Carthage’, a Roman Latinization of the very old Punic idea; it survives today as Cartagena in Spain, and its name was carried across the Atlantic to Cartagena de Indias in Colombia. From late antiquity to the modern era the word developed further layers. The Roman city became a centre of early Christianity — Cyprian and Augustine of Hippo were bishops of Carthage — so that the name also conjures the African church of the 3rd–4th centuries. In the nineteenth century the site was excavated, and “Carthage” acquired its archaeological sense: the Tophet, the Punic ports, the Antonine Baths, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site near Tunis. In political rhetoric the name remains the byword for a commercial empire destroyed by a rival — from Victorian parliamentarians to modern journalists — while dozens of towns across the United States quietly bear the name, so that a single Punic compound meaning ‘New City’ has become, in the full arc of its history, the most famous symbol of wealth, war, and annihilation in the western canon.
