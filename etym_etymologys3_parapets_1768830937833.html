The evolution of “parapet”: parrying the chest. Parapet is a compound of two Latin-descended Italian words, and its full biography must trace each half separately before the two meet in Renaissance military engineering.
  • First half — parare, “to make ready, to ward off.” The verb descends from the PIE root *perh₂- (also written *perə-), “to provide, produce, bring forth,” which survives in Sanskrit pṛṇāti, “he grants, bestows” — the often-cited cognate of Latin parāre, “to make ready, to prepare.” The Latin verb proved extraordinarily fertile: with the prefix prae- it became praeparāre, the source of Old French preparer and English prepare (14th century); with ad- it yielded apparatus, “things made ready,” the equipment of ceremony and, in the 20th century, the Soviet apparat; in Old French parer, “to prepare, adorn, defend,” it produced the English verb parry (17th century), the swordsman’s art of warding off a blow — for parer had come to mean “to ward off, fend off.” It is this last sense that matters for our word: Italian parare, borrowed from French and Latin, had become by the Renaissance a standard military verb meaning “to shield, to defend, to keep off,” used of men parrying missile and sword alike. When Italian military engineers of the 15th and 16th centuries needed a name for the low wall that protects a defender at the top of a fortification, they reached for exactly this verb and joined it to the Italian word for the chest, petto: a parapetto is a wall that “parries the chest,” that comes up to the breast and defends it. The idea of preparation is thus still faintly audible in the word: a parapet is a thing made ready against assault, an apparatus of defence, a stone parry aimed at the world.
  • Second half — pectus, “the breast.” Latin pectus (genitive pectoris), “breast, chest,” descends from the reconstructed PIE root *peg-, “breast,” whose certain cognates outside the Italic branch are remarkably few, so that the Latin word stands rather alone in its family, and an honest tree must stop at the root with a single solid daughter within Italic. Italian petto is the regular Romance descendant of the accusative pectum, “the chest.” From pectus English also has the learned adjective pectoral (“of the breast,” of muscles and cross-worn decorations) and the medical pectoralis.
  • The meeting and the English entry (16th century). The compound parapetto passed into French as parapet, and English borrowed it in the 1580s as a strictly military term: the low breast-high wall of earth or stone behind which defenders stood to fire and be sheltered. For the first two centuries it stays in the fortification lexicon — the defensive railing of the engineer’s trade, named alongside bastion, rampart, and breastwork.
  • Civilianisation (18th–21st centuries). In the 18th–19th centuries the defensive sense recedes and parapet becomes the architectural word for any low protective wall along roofs, balconies, bridges, and terraces, guarding against falling rather than against assault. The word’s literal heart — “breast-high” — has faded from most users’ awareness, so that a parapet is simply a low safety wall; freed of its military urgency, it can even take on an elegiac, poetic resonance as a relic of fortress glory, which is exactly the tone Vance’s passage strikes when a wizard climbs the parapets of his castle to breathe the ancient air under the far stars.
From a Sanskrit verb of giving, to a Latin verb of preparation, to a French swordsman’s parry, to an Italian chest-guard, to an English safety wall, the career of parapet is a study in how a compound can preserve both of its halves in a single image: a thing that is both made ready and raised to the breast — the wall between a man and the fall.
