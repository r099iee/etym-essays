[Root core] PIE *sker- "to cut, to shear, to clip" (c. 4000 BC; Pokorny *sker-) — the ultimate source of the Germanic mockery-word, if the standard reconstruction is right [Stage 2] Proto-Germanic *skeran- "to cut, to shear" (c. 500 BC–AD 200) — the root in its plain cutting sense [Stage 3] Proto-Germanic *skarnjan "to mock, to deride" (reconstructed; the derivation from "to cut at" is the standard scholarly guess) — attested indirectly in Old High German scern [Stage 4] Old High German scern "mockery, jest, derision" (9th c.); Middle Dutch schernen "to mock" (13th c.) [Stage 5] Old French escarn "mockery, derision" / escharnir "to mock, to deride, to insult" (11th c.) — a Germanic loan-word adopted into Romance [Stage 6] Middle English scornen "to treat with contempt, to mock" (c. 1200) — borrowed from Old French [Stage 7] English scorn (n.) "contempt, derision; a feeling that someone or something is worthless" and (v.) "to reject or refuse with contempt" — this card's word Note on the debate: The linking of *skarnjan to PIE *sker- "to cut" is the standard reconstruction but is flagged as uncertain by the OED. One rival, now rejected theory derived the word from Latin escorner "to break the horns off" (es- + corne "horn"), imagining mockery as the removal of an animal's horns; modern scholarship prefers the Germanic "cutting" etymology. Both Old High German scern and the French escarn point decisively to a Germanic donor.
