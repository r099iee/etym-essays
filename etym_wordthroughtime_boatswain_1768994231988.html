- 15th-c. nautical compound - "boat" + "swain" (boat's boy/servant) names the ship's officer in charge of the boat and its gear; the pronunciation already begins to compress toward "bosun."
- Age of sail establishment (16th–17th c.) - the boatswain is a fixed warrant officer commanding rigging, anchors, sails, and deck crew, his silver pipe (the boatswain's call) piping the crew to duty; a practical, hard-bitten, indispensable figure.
- Naval hierarchy formalization (18th–19th c.) - the office is codified in navy ranks below the lieutenants; in sea fiction the boatswain becomes a stock character — profane, weather-beaten, competent, the ship's disciplinarian of ropes and men.
- Steam-era continuity (19th–20th c.) - the role persists as boatswain's mate, but with sails gone the word grows historical, echoing the age of sail rather than the machine-age navy.
- Contemporary register (20th–21st c.) - "boatswain" survives mostly in nautical-historical and literary contexts, always pronounced "bosun" — a word that carries the cold professionalism and blue-collar dignity of the deck.
