- Old English hladan (to load, to pile up; also to draw up water) - the participle hladen names the state of being loaded, of goods piled upon a beast or into a vessel.
- Middle English - the participle laden attaches to the verb lade and is used literally of ships, wagons, and beasts "laden" with goods, and of trees "laden" with fruit.
- 15th-17th c. - the word detaches from the verb and hardens into a pure adjective: laden stands alone, meaning heavily loaded, weighed down; it keeps its old, formal, even scriptural tone.
- 16th-19th c. - heavy figurative freight: a soul or conscience "laden" with sins, a heart "laden" with cares - biblical and homiletic phrases that give the word a moral as well as a physical weight.
- Contemporary - laden remains a literary, antique adjective of dignified burden: a ship laden with treasure, a tree laden with fruit, a beast bowed under its load - public perception keeps it grave, passive, and enduring.
