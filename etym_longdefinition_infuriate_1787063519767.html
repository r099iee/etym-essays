Infuriate is a transitive verb meaning to make someone furiously angry — to enrage them to the point of fury, rage or loss of composure. In Tolkien's passage, the wise way to talk to a dragon is to flatter and hint rather than give “a flat refusal”, which would “infuriate them” — i.e., provoke the dragon's terrible, destructive anger. The word sits at the top of the English anger scale: ordinary verbs like annoy or irritate describe mild vexation; infuriate describes the maximum, explosive degree of anger, the state captured by the noun fury.

Core meaning. “To fill with fury; to make violently or incandescently angry.” It always involves a stimulus (the person or thing that infuriates) and a victim (the person who becomes furious). The emphasis is on the intensity and the suddenness of the resulting anger — the infuriated person does not merely dislike or resent; they burn. The word is causative: it names the action of causing fury in someone else.

Polysemy and shades.

  • Primary sense — to enrage: “His arrogance infuriated her.” The anger produced is extreme, bordering on blind fury.
  • Figurative / strong emphasis: infuriate is often used hyperbolically in everyday speech for any serious annoyance, though purists reserve it for genuine fury: “The constant dripping infuriated me” is a mild exaggeration, while “The betrayal infuriated him” is literal.
  • Adjectival sense (rare, formal): infuriate also exists as an adjective meaning “enraged, maddened, furious” (from the Latin past participle infuriātus): “the infuriate crowd”. In modern English this adjective is rare, largely displaced by infuriated or furious, but it persists in formal and literary prose.

Related nouns and adjectives.

  • Infuriated (adj.): made furious; the usual adjectival and participial form.
  • Infuriating (adj.): causing fury (“an infuriating delay”); the -ing form describes the cause.
  • Infuriatingly (adv.): in a manner that infuriates (“infuriatingly slow”).
  • Infuriation (noun, formal/rare): the state or act of being infuriated; rage.

Grammar. Infuriate is a regular transitive verb (infuriates, infuriating, infuriated). It is often used in the passive (“I was infuriated by the delay”) and with a causative subject (“It infuriates me that ...”). It is also used with by in passive constructions to name the provoking cause.

Register and usage. The word is standard in both speech and writing, and it is strong — careful writers use it when anger would be too weak and enrage too literary. It is frequent in journalism, criticism, political commentary and everyday complaint. Its Latin roots make it sound slightly more formal than madden, but it is not at all archaic. In Tolkien's context, the word carries real danger: to infuriate Smaug is to invite being burned alive, and the narrator's advice is that survival depends on never doing so. The semantic intensity of the word — fury, not mere annoyance — is precisely what makes the advice sensible.
