- c. 1200 – chateren means “to twitter”, of birds — a purely descriptive sound word.
- Middle English – extended to human speech: rapid, trivial talk; mildly to strongly dismissive from the very start.
- 17th–18th c. – applied to teeth clicking in cold and fear; chattering becomes a stock image of terror and shivering; chatterbox (1774) fixes the social type of the compulsive talker.
- 19th c. – in Victorian fiction chattering signals nervousness, gossip and comic triviality; the participial form is now the standard adjective for teeth.
- 20th c. – engineers borrow chatter for the vibration of cutting tools; “chattering classes” (British, 1980s) sneers at liberal commentators as mere talkers.
- Today – chattering spans birds, people, teeth and machines; in every human use it still means noise without much meaning.
