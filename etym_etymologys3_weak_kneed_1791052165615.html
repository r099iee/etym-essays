weak-kneed is a transparent Modern English compound of weak + kneed (having knees of a specified kind). Its history therefore requires two independent etymologies that converge only in the mid-19th century as a figurative insult for cowardice and irresolution. Both elements are ancient Germanic, but the compound and its moral sense are Victorian.

1. WEAK: Weak does not descend from Old English wac (pliant) directly but was reinforced or replaced by Old Norse veikr, meaning pliant, bending, weak, from Proto-Germanic *waikwaz, from PIE *weik- to bend, wind, yield. Cognates include Latin vicis (change, turn), Sanskrit vijate (recoils), English wicker (pliant twigs), vicar (one who turns in place of another), week (turn of days). The semantic core is BENDING INSTEAD OF RESISTING. In Old Norse veikr could describe a pliant willow or a yielding person. Middle English weik (c.1225) already means lacking physical strength, thin, watery. By the 14th century it covers arguments (weak reasoning), drink (weak ale), character (weak heart). The moral sense — unable to stand firm — is thus ancient.

  • Sound change: Old Norse ei / Proto-Germanic ai regularly becomes Middle English ei/ay then modern ee: *waikwaz > veikr > weik > weak. Spelling ea for /iː/ is Middle English convention. Initial w- is preserved.
  • Semantic drift: From physical pliancy (bends easily) to lack of force to lack of moral firmness. This bridge makes weak-kneed possible: knees that bend when they should lock.

2. KNEE / KNEED: Knee continues PIE *ǵenu- or *ǵenw- knee, joint-angle, one of the most stable body-part words in Indo-European: Sanskrit janu, Greek gonu, Latin genu, Gothic kniu. Proto-Germanic *knewą gives Old English cneow (with initial cn- pronounced /kn/), Middle English kne, modern knee after silent-k development (kn- > /n/ c.17th c., spelling retained). Kneed as second element means furnished with knees: knock-kneed (knees that knock together), bandy-kneed, stiff-kneed. It is almost never free-standing; it lives in compounds describing leg shape.

  • Physiology to metaphor: Knees buckle under fear, exhaustion, awe. Shaking knees, knocking knees, weak knees are universal somatic symptoms of fright. English since Shakespeare uses knees for courage: stiff knees = stubborn, bending knees = submission.
  • Compound formation: Weak-kneed first attested 1850s-1860s in British parliamentary and sporting press: a weak-kneed administration, weak-kneed sentimentalists. Modelled on knock-kneed (physical deformity) transferred to character. To be weak-kneed is to have knees that give way when firm stance is needed.

In the source sentence — considered it weak-kneed and unseemly to preserve food — the word means unmanly, cowardly, lacking hardihood. Storing food is seen, in the author's reconstruction of savage ethics, as timid foresight rather than brave improvidence. This is typical Victorian use: weak-kneed reproaches humanitarians, politicians, Christians who compromise. Later it broadens to any irresolute person: weak-kneed response to crime, weak-kneed liberals/conservatives depending on speaker.

Socio-cultural context: The compound flourishes in an age obsessed with manliness, empire, public schools, muscular Christianity. Weak knees imply effeminacy, over-civilisation. The hyphenated adjective is almost always attributive insult, rarely self-description. American English later prefers weak-kneed sibling lily-livered, chicken-hearted, spineless, but weak-kneed survives in politics.

Debate note: The precise relation of Old English wac (weak, miserable) to Old Norse veikr is debated: most authorities treat modern weak as a Norse loan replacing native wac under Danelaw influence, not direct descent. The PIE root *weik- vs *weig- (bend, turn) variants reflect laryngeal uncertainties but do not affect the Germanic chain. For knee, reconstruction varies between *ǵenu- and *ǵnew- with metathesis; meaning knee is undisputed.
