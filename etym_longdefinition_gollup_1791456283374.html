Gollup — far more often written gollop — is a British dialect and colloquial noun and verb for a big, rapid, usually noisy swallow of liquid, and for the act of swallowing in that rough, whole-hearted way. It belongs to the expressive, mouth-feel end of the English vocabulary of drinking. It is not the neutral sip, nor the clinical ingest, nor the polite taste: it is a word built to reproduce the sound and bodily effort of getting a large draught down quickly. The spelling with -u- is a dialect variant beside the commoner gollop; both are pronounced alike, with the first syllable rhyming with doll and a light, unstressed second syllable.

Primary verbal sense. To gollup is to drink something in one or more large, greedy draughts, often with an audible gulp. The emphasis falls on speed and quantity rather than on manners: a thirsty labourer gollups cold cider; a dog gollups water from a bowl; a comic character gollups a tankard of home-brew. The verb is frequently used of animals, where it suggests eager, unselfconscious drinking, and it can be extended to food eaten in the same bolting fashion, though liquids remain its natural territory. It is transitive in the ordinary way (“he golluped the lot”) and also intransitive (“she was golluping noisily”).

The noun sense. A gollup is the quantity taken in such a swallow — “a good gollup of ale” — or the swallow itself, including its sound. This is the sense that appears in the card’s context, where a first gollup of home-brewed beer is remembered with rapture. The noun is countable (one gollup, two gollups) and typically carries a positive, hearty colouring: it implies relish rather than mere thirst, and it is the kind of word a narrator uses when he wants to sound jovial, bodily, and unpretentious.

Figurative and extended uses. Because the word encodes a large, sudden intake, it transfers easily from liquid to air and breath. A runner at the end of a race, a swimmer surfacing, or a tired animal may take “great gollups of air”: the metaphor treats breath as a drinkable substance and the lungs as a vessel being filled. A related figurative use describes any large, greedy absorption — a reader who gollups a novel, a machine that gollups fuel — where the image is of something being swallowed whole rather than savoured. The word can also carry a faintly disapproving edge when applied to people who consume too fast, though the disapproval is usually affectionate rather than moralistic.

Register and regional distribution. Gollup/gollop is not standard written English. Collins treats gollop as a dialect variant of gulp, and the Dictionary of the Scots Language records gollop, “also gollup,” as a Scots verb meaning to swallow hastily or greedily, with citations from the early and mid twentieth century. It consequently feels at home in Scots, northern English, and rural or working-class speech, and it appears in literary dialogue when a writer wants to signal exactly that background. In neutral expository prose it would strike most readers as quaint, jocular, or deliberately rustic.

Semantic bridges: how the senses connect.

  • Sound to action: the word’s core is onomatopoeic — the “goll-” and the closing “-p” enact a wet, throaty swallow — so the physical noise becomes the name of the action.
  • Action to quantity: once the verb names one big swallow, the noun naturally names the amount contained in it, exactly as gulp, swig, and draught do.
  • Liquid to air: the body takes in air as it takes in drink, so the greedy-intake image is reused for breath and, by further extension, for any rapid consumption.
  • Literal to comic: because the word is homely and noisy, novelists use it to deflate pompous or genteel characters, turning a drinking noise into a small joke about dignity.

Related forms and derivatives. The present participle is golluping (also golloping), the past tense golluped (also golloped), and a habitual drinker may be called a golloper — a formation Collins lists alongside the verb. The spelling is unstable because the word lives chiefly in speech: gollop, gollup, and occasional gollap all occur, and dictionaries usually head the word at gollop while noting the -u- form.

Modern status and connotation. In the twenty-first century the word is rare in edited prose and essentially absent from technical or official language. It survives in regional speech, in Scottish literature and dialect writing, in historical fiction, and as a deliberate stylistic choice in humorous narrative. Its connotation remains warm, physical, and slightly old-fashioned: to call a drink a gollup is to suggest a world of tankards, farmhouse tables, and unashamed appetite rather than cocktail bars and measured tasting flights.
