endeared: made dear, beloved, or cherished; caused to be loved or liked by someone through gentle, attractive, or winning qualities. Core meaning. The past participle endeared describes a person (or a quality, or a thing) that has been made beloved or has won affection: "He endeared himself to the children," "her warmth endeared her to the whole office," "the little dog endeared itself to everyone." It is formed from the verb "endear" (en- "in, make" + dear "beloved"), literally "to make dear." The word always implies that affection was actively won — through kindness, charm, gentleness, or attractive qualities — rather than given automatically. In Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, Bingley is "endeared to Darcy by the easiness, openness, and ductility of his temper": Bingley's easy, open, flexible nature actively wins Darcy's love, even though the two men's characters are opposites. Primary sense — made beloved through winning qualities. This is the only living sense today. The focus is on the agent's attractive qualities or behavior: one is endeared to another by gentleness, generosity, openness, humor, or loyalty. The construction is typically "endear someone to somebody" or "be endeared to somebody." It is common in the passive participle form ("she was endeared to the community") and in the adjectival present participle "endearing" ("an endearing smile"), which is now far more frequent in everyday speech than the verb itself. Historical sense — made costly or expensive. When the verb "endear" first appeared in the late 16th century, it could also mean "to raise the price of, to make expensive or rare," because its base word "dear" then still carried the strong economic sense "costly, high-priced." To "endear" a commodity was to make it dear in the marketplace sense. This economic meaning has long since died out, but it explains the word's birth: the emotional sense "make beloved" and the economic sense "make costly" both drew on the two poles of Old English dēore, which meant simultaneously "precious, costly" and "loved." Secondary shade — to attach by affection. A related use of "endear" means "to bind or attach someone to another by affection," as in "their shared trials endeared them to each other." Here the focus is less on making someone lovable than on creating a bond of affection between people. The passive participle "endeared" therefore can describe both "made lovable" and "bound in affection." Grammatical notes and collocations.
  • Typical patterns: "to endear oneself to someone"; "to be endeared to someone"; "endearing qualities"; "an endearing trait/smile/habit."
  • The noun is "endearment" — "a term of endearment" being the standard phrase for a loving nickname or pet name.
  • Register: gentle, warm, literary-to-neutral; more intimate than "admired," less intense than "adored."
  • The present participle "endearing" has become the most common form in modern English, especially in journalism and informal speech, where it means "winning affection, charming."
Polysemy summary (all senses, living and historical).
  • Made beloved or cherished; caused to be loved — modern, dominant.
  • Bound or attached by affection — modern, literary.
  • Made costly, raised in price, made expensive — 16th–17th-century economic sense, now obsolete.
  • Brought into favor or esteem with someone — 17th–18th-century use, now rare.
The semantic bridge. The whole family turns on the double life of the word "dear." In Old English dēore meant both "costly, valuable" and "loved, cherished" — things that cost much were guarded and prized, and what we prize we love. When English formed "endear" with the causative prefix en-, it could therefore mean either "make expensive" or "make loved," and both senses existed for a time. As the economic sense of "dear" receded from modern English (surviving chiefly in phrases about high prices and the old vocative "dear sir"), the emotional sense of "endear" triumphed. Today "endeared" is purely a word of affection, but its economic ghost explains why being "dear" to someone still means being valued as something precious.
