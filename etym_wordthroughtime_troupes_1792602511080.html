- [13th–14th c. Old French] — Derived as troupe or trope, denoting a flock of animals, a scattered crowd of peasants, or an assembly of people — [perception: rustic, descriptive term for a rustic gathering or herd]
- [15th–16th c. Middle French / Renaissance] — Adopted into military administration to denote a company of horsemen or soldiers under a single banner; simultaneously borrowed into English as troop — [perception: organized military subdivision and disciplined martial fighting force]
- [17th c. Classical French] — Extended in France to celebrated royal and traveling theatrical companies (e.g., Molière's Troupe du Roi at the Palais-Royal) — [perception: prestigious royal and artistic cultural institution in France]
- [Early 19th c., c. 1820s Romantic English] — Deliberately re-borrowed into English preserving French orthography (troupe) to distinguish theatrical artists, dancers, and circus acrobats from military troops — [perception: bohemian, romantic, and prestigious designation for dedicated stage performers]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Standard international English term for dance, circus, comedy, and theatrical companies; used figuratively for synchronized political or corporate teams — [perception: polished artistic ensemble with deep collaborative chemistry]
