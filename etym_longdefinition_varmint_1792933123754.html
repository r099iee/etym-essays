Varmint is, in its oldest sense, a troublesome wild animal: the fox in the henhouse, the weasel among chicks, the rat in the grain, any small creature that preys, steals, or spreads disease and earns the farmer enmity.

In the source sentence a farmer glares at a young trespasser and growls that next time this young varmint sets foot on his land, the dogs may eat him. The boy is no animal at all, but the word demotes him to one: a poaching, crop-trampling pest to be chased off like a fox, with rough rural humour softening the threat.

Core meaning in detail: Varmint is the folksy American and dialectal English form of vermin, keeping the older broad sense that towns later narrowed. Where modern vermin mostly means rats, lice, and parasites, varmint stayed outdoors: foxes, raccoons, coyotes, weasels, crows, any creature the countryman shoots, traps, or curses. Applied to people it keeps the animal sting but adds affection: a young varmint is a scamp, a little rascal, scolded with a grin.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Troublesome wild animal (primary sense, from 16th c.): foxes, weasels, rats, and crows that raid crops and coops. The bridge is direct inheritance from vermin, the noxious creature, with the vowel shifted by rural speech.
  • Mischievous person, especially a boy (source sense, 19th c.): the young varmint on the farmer land, the little varmints next door. The bridge is metaphorical demotion: the trespasser annoys like a crop-raiding beast, so he borrows the beast name, half insult and half endearment.
  • Contemptible adult rogue (harsher extension): thieving, shiftless, or cruel characters dismissed as varmints in Westerns and rural fiction. The bridge hardens the boyish scamp into the grown pest: what was mischief becomes menace, and affection drains out.
  • Varmint hunting and varmint rifles (American sporting sense, 20th c.): the pursuit of coyotes, groundhogs, and prairie dogs with small-calibre rifles. The bridge turns the noun into a sport category: creatures too small for big game but too harmful for mercy get their own guns, cartridges, and magazines.
  • Varmints as creepy-crawlies (dialectal survival): in older rural use any small wriggling pests, worms and grubs included. The bridge reaches back to Latin vermis, the worm, preserving the word oldest and smallest sense inside the folksy form.

Register and connotation: varmint is unabashedly rustic: drawled vowels, frontier humour, farmers and cowboys rather than committees. Against the clipped official vermin of pest-control notices it sounds human and story-shaped, almost fond even when it threatens. The farmer who calls a boy a young varmint is performing a ritual older than fences: the mock-fierce warning that says you are trespassing, I am watching, and the dogs are hungry, while everybody understands no boy will actually be eaten.
