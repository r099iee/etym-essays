- 1547–1616 - The personal surname of Miguel de Cervantes Saavedra; during his lifetime, despite the rapid popularity of Don Quixote, he was viewed primarily by contemporaries as a witty, entertaining satirist and impoverished veteran rather than an untouchable literary monument.
- 17th to 18th Century - *Cervantes* and the derivative adjective *Cervantic* spread across Western Europe, where he was celebrated predominantly as the brilliant comedic parodist who delivered the mortal blow to the absurdities of chivalric romances, inspiring novelists from Henry Fielding to Tobias Smollett.
- 19th Century (Romantic Reinterpretation) - German and English Romantics (Friedrich Schlegel, Lord Byron, Heinrich Heine) fundamentally transformed the cultural perception of Cervantes, elevating him from a simple comedic satirist into a profound, melancholic philosopher of the human spirit whose masterpiece captured the eternal, tragic struggle between noble ideals and earthly disillusionment.
- 20th Century (Modernist & Formalist Analysis) - Championed by formalists, structuralists, and existentialists (Viktor Shklovsky, Mikhail Bakhtin, José Ortega y Gasset, Michel Foucault) as the definitive father of the modern novel, celebrated for inventing polyphonic dialogue, narrative defamiliarization, and self-conscious metafiction.
- 21st Century - Universally canonized in global literary consciousness as the archetype of humanistic storytelling, multi-perspectivism, and vernacular literary mastery, honored through prestigious institutions like Spain's Miguel de Cervantes Prize.
