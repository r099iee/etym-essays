Abstain is an intransitive verb signifying the voluntary, deliberate act of refraining or holding oneself back from an action, indulgence, physical gratification, or civic participation. Unlike passive neglect or external prohibition, abstaining requires an active exertion of willpower and self-command, intentionally choosing non-engagement in the presence of an available desire, privilege, or opportunity. Over its centuries of development, the word has established vital footholds across spiritual discipline, legislative procedure, judicial ethics, scientific methodology, and clinical medicine.

The verb's primary and polysemous modern senses include:

  • Voluntary Restraint from Bodily Pleasures and Consumption: The most enduring historical sense involves intentionally forgoing sensory appetites, notably intoxicating liquor, rich foods, sexual intercourse, narcotics, or tobacco. In medical regimens, a patient must abstain from caffeine, alcohol, or solid sustenance prior to diagnostic imaging, blood draws, or general anesthesia. In ethical and religious disciplines, such physical abstention constitutes ascetic practice (e.g., fasting during Ramadan, Yom Kippur, or Christian Lent) intended to purify the spirit by disciplining carnal desires.
  • Formal Parliamentary and Deliberative Non-Voting: In political science and parliamentary procedure, to abstain means to formally decline to cast a ballot either in favor of ("aye") or against ("nay") a proposed motion, bill, treaty, or resolution. In assemblies like the United Nations Security Council, an abstention allows a permanent member to permit a resolution to pass without formally voting for it, signaling calculated diplomatic neutrality, tacit acquiescence, or unresolved disagreement without invoking a disruptive veto.
  • Intellectual, Epistemological, and Critical Prudence: In philosophy, scholarship, and scientific discourse, to abstain means to intentionally withhold judgment, dogmatic assertion, or premature criticism until empirical verification has been completed. This is the precise sense exemplified in Carl Menger's foundational economic prose: an uncompromising commitment to scientific truth demands that scholars refuse to hold back or "abstain from applying criticism" even to the most revered doctrines of their intellectual forebears. Here, abstention would be a dereliction of critical duty; refusing to abstain represents the courage of independent judgment.
  • Judicial, Fiduciary, and Ethical Recusal: In legal and institutional governance, board directors, trustees, and judges abstain from voting, deliberating, or presiding over matters where an actual or perceived conflict of interest exists. While related to formal legal recusal, an ethical board member simply chooses to "abstain from deliberation" to safeguard institutional integrity.
  • Behavioral and Interpersonal Forbearance: In social interactions and diplomacy, the term denotes consciously withholding hostile commentary, retaliatory acts, or contentious gossip. Choosing to abstain from inflammatory online debates or family disputes demonstrates emotional maturity and conflict de-escalation.

Historical Semantic Bridges & Polysemy:
The architectural bridge unifying all these senses is the ancient physical metaphor embedded in its Latin ancestry: ab- ("away from") joined with tenēre ("to hold, grasp"). To abstain is literally to "hold one's hands off" an object or pursuit. In ancient Roman law and daily life, abstinēre manus meant literally keeping one's hands away from stolen goods or sacred relics. Over time, Roman moralists transposed this physical gesture of non-touching into an internal psychological posture: holding back one's appetites, holding back one's tongue, holding back one's vote, or holding back one's credulity. Consequently, whether an individual is declining a glass of wine, a diplomat is withholding a vote in Geneva, or a scientist is refusing to spare an obsolete doctrine from rigorous criticism, each is enacting the identical psychological motion: pulling the self back from entering into an action.
