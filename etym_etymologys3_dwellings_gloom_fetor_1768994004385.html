DWELLINGS — an essay. The history of dwelling begins not with homes but with wandering. The Proto-Indo-European root *dʰwel- meant "to wander, to go astray, to be confused or deluded," and its Germanic reflexes were verbs of errancy and deceit: Gothic dwals means "foolish," and Old English dwellan, the direct ancestor of our verb, meant "to mislead, to deceive, to hinder, to lead astray." In Old English literature, to dwellan someone was to trick them or delay them; the noun dwola meant "error" and "heresy," a straying from the true path in both mind and doctrine. The word that would become our "dwell" was thus, in its first attested English life, the opposite of a word about home: it was a word about being lost, about wandering, about tarrying where one should not, about error.

The great reversal happens in Middle English. The verb dwellen slid from "to tarry, to delay, to remain" toward "to stay put, to abide, to sojourn," and then — by the fourteenth century — to "to live in, to reside in, to inhabit." The semantic bridge is continuity of occupation: to keep remaining in a place is, eventually, to live there; a man who tarries long enough has settled. The noun dwelling was built on the settled sense, a verbal noun in -ing that hardened from the act of residing into the place resided in: by 1300 English had dwelling "a place of residence, an abode," and by the late medieval period it wore the formal, almost legal neutrality it still has — the word of leases, surveys, and church records of who lived where.

That neutrality has proved a double-edged legacy, and it explains the word's behaviour in our passage. Because dwelling names residence without warmth, it can be pointed at anything that inhabits: English today speaks fluently of cave dwellings, pit dwellings, cliff dwellings, and lake dwellings — the piled settlements of prehistoric peoples — and of the "dwellings of the dead," tombs treated as residences. It is the same neutrality that lets a novelist describe the "Aihai dwellings" of a bestial underground folk, and so to taint domesticity with lair. In modern fixed phrases — dwelling house, dwelling unit, residential dwelling — the word is a legal-administrative unit of housing; in literary and archaeological prose it is the architectural sign of settled life, whether human, bestial, or ghostly.

  • PIE *dʰwel- "wander, stray" → Old English dwellan "mislead, delude" (8th–11th c.): a word of errancy and error.
  • Middle English dwellen "tarry, abide, remain" (12th–14th c.): the hinge of the shift — staying becomes dwelling.
  • English dwell "live in, inhabit" + noun dwelling "residence" (14th c.): the act freezes into architecture.
  • Modern English: formal neutrality allows animals, primitives, and the dead to "dwell" — cave dwellings, dwellings of the dead, Aihai dwellings.

GLOOM — an essay. Gloom belongs to one of the oldest families of Germanic words, the *gʰel- "shine" group, and its paradox is that it names darkness by way of light. The root produced Germanic *glōaną "to glow" — whence Old English glōwan "to glow," still our word for the burning without flame — and beside it a derivative *glōm- meaning "dim light, twilight," the fading gleam of the sky when the sun has gone but the day has not quite died. Old English glōm "twilight, darkness" is attested, as is the noun glōmung, the source of our poetic "gloaming" for evening dusk. The original gloom was thus the gloaming: not blackness but the intermediate state, the world grown dim.

By Middle English, glome/gloum meant simply "darkness, dimness, shadow," and here the word's biography forks. In the fifteenth century a verb gloumen appears meaning "to look sullen, to frown, to stare darkly," and from it the family grew a new branch: the adjective gloomy (16th c.), "dark, dim" and also "sullen, moody," and the noun gloom extended to mean a dark mood, melancholy, despondency. The transfer from physical to emotional darkness is complete by the eighteenth century, and the two senses have reinforced each other ever since: a gloomy day (dim) and a gloomy man (melancholy) share the same word because they share the same felt quality — the mind darkened as a room is darkened, the spirit gone grey. The OED cautions that the origin of the modern word is "obscure," and some scholars prefer to derive the noun from the sullen-look verb (possibly of Scandinavian origin, cf. Swedish glo "to stare"), with the darkness sense arriving by transfer; either route, the final product is the same — a word that slides freely between twilight and sorrow.

  • PIE *gʰel- "shine" → Germanic *glōm- "dim light, twilight" → Old English glōm "twilight" (8th–9th c.): darkness as the residue of light.
  • Middle English glome "darkness, dimness" (13th–15th c.): the atmospheric core sense.
  • English gloom (noun) "darkness" (15th c.); verb gloumen "look sullen" (15th c.); gloomy (16th c.).
  • 18th c.: the emotional sense — gloom = melancholy, despondency; the mind darkened as a room is darkened.
  • Modern English: atmospheric dimness, overcast heaviness, and deep sadness all in one word.

FETOR — an essay. Fetor is a Latin word that came to English ready-made, and its history is short but revealing. Latin fētēre, "to stink, to emit a foul smell," is attested from the third century BCE — Plautus has a character sniff and cry fetet! "it stinks!" — and it is a strikingly isolated verb: it has no certain Indo-European ancestors and no sure cognates, and etymologists (including de Vaan) record it as an opaque, substrate-like word of Latin with no secure PIE reconstruction. Attempts to attach it to a root of "blowing" or "smoking" are speculative and unproven. Latin, in other words, simply had an old word for stinking whose deeper history is lost, and that lost stage is precisely the gap the correction protocol demands be stated explicitly rather than filled with invention.

From the verb came the noun fētor — "a bad smell, a stench" — attested in the first centuries BCE/CE in Cicero and Pliny, and beside it the adjective fētidus "stinking, foul-smelling." The spelling foetor is a Latin hypercorrection of the imperial age, an artificial diphthong written into a word whose vowel was simply long, and it survives into modern British medical print as a variant. English borrowed the noun directly in the fifteenth century as fetor (or fetour) "foul smell, stench," and the adjective fetid followed in the 1590s. For centuries fetor was the plain Latinism of prose, the dignified cousin of stench and stink; its real modern career, however, is medical and technical. Today fetor is the clinical word for diagnostic bad smells: fetor oris ("foulness of the mouth," the technical term for halitosis), fetor hepaticus (the sweet, musty stench of advanced liver failure), fetor uremicus (the ammonia odor of kidney failure) — a narrow, precise, hygienic vocabulary in which an ancient isolated Latin noun has found its perfect modern niche, while its literary use keeps the word alive for the "clinging" stench that taints the dwellings of the Aihai.

  • Latin fētēre "to stink" (3rd c. BCE, Plautus): an isolated verb with no secure PIE ancestry — origin unknown and stated as such.
  • Latin fētor "stench" (1st c. BCE–CE, Cicero, Pliny) and fētidus "stinking"; foetor as later hypercorrect spelling.
  • English fetor "foul smell" (15th c.), directly from Latin; fetid (1590s) from fētidus.
  • Modern medical use: fetor oris, fetor hepaticus, fetor uremicus — diagnostic stench; literary use for the clinging smell of corruption.
