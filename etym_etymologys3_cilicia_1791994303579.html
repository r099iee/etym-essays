The toponym Cilicia possesses an extraordinary etymological pedigree that spans over three millennia, linking Bronze Age Hurro-Luwian kingdoms, Neo-Assyrian imperial annals, classical Greek mythography, Roman administrative jurisprudence, and medieval Christian chronicles. The linguistic path of the word reflects how indigenous Anatolian geographical terms were adapted into the phonetic and orthographic structures of Ancient Greek, Classical Latin, and eventually the vernacular languages of Western Europe.

1. Bronze Age and Iron Age Anatolian Origins:

In the second millennium BCE, the region corresponding to eastern Cilicia was known in Hittite cuneiform records as the kingdom of Kizzuwatna, a prominent Hurro-Luwian cultural buffer state between the Hittite Empire and the Mitanni kingdom. In the early first millennium BCE (Iron Age), following the collapse of the Bronze Age empires, the western mountainous zone was recorded in Neo-Assyrian inscriptions (beginning with the annals of King Shalmaneser III in the ninth century BCE) under the Akkadian cuneiform transcription Ḫilakku (sometimes vocalized as Chilakku), while the fertile eastern plain was designated as Que (or Hiyawa, related to Homeric Achaeans).

Linguists and Anatolian philologists (such as Emmanuel Laroche and Ilya Yakubovich) trace Greek Kilikía directly to this indigenous Luwian/Anatolian root *Hilakku. Because Ancient Greek lacked a voiceless velar or uvular fricative /x/ or /h/ at word-initial position matching the Semitic and Anatolian ḥēt / ḫ, Greek speakers adapted the initial foreign consonant as an unvoiced velar stop kappa (/k/), transforming *Hilakku into Kílikes (Κίλικες, the inhabitants) and Kilikía (Κιλικία, the territory).

2. Greek Mythography and Hellenization:

Classical Greeks, characteristically seeking mythological explanations for foreign place-names, rationalized the name through the myth of Kilix (Κίλιξ). In Greek mythology (recorded by Herodotus and Apollodorus), Kilix was the son of the Phoenician king Agenor and the brother of Cadmus and Europa. When Zeus abducted Europa, Agenor ordered his sons to search for their sister and never return without her. Kilix eventually abandoned the search and settled on the southern coast of Asia Minor, bestowing his name upon the country and its inhabitants (the Cilicians).

Under the Achaemenid Persian Empire (546–333 BCE), Cilicia was governed as a semi-autonomous hereditary tributary kingdom under rulers bearing the dynastic title Syennesis, with Tarsus serving as their capital. The region gained legendary status in Greek historiography after Xenophon's Anabasis described Cyrus the Younger's army marching through the formidable Cilician Gates into Syria.

3. Roman Provincial Administration and Cicero's Governorship:

The Latin form Cilicia represents the direct transliteration of Greek Kilikía into the Roman alphabet, preserving the classical spelling with the letter C (which in Classical Latin was pronounced as a hard voiceless velar stop /k/, hence /kɪˈlɪ.kɪ.a/). In 102 BCE, Rome established a military command in Cilicia under Marcus Antonius the Orator to combat the rampant Mediterranean piracy that threatened grain shipments. Following Pompey the Great's decisive naval victories under the Lex Gabinia in 67 BCE, Pompey organized Cilicia into an expanded formal Roman province in 64 BCE.

Between 51 and 50 BCE, Marcus Tullius Cicero served as proconsular governor of Cilicia. Cicero's surviving letters to his friend Atticus and the Roman Senate provide an incomparable firsthand portrait of provincial governance in Cilicia: handling financial extortion by Roman moneylenders (such as Marcus Junius Brutus), administering equitable judicial assizes, and leading military skirmishes against mountain insurgents at Mount Amanus, for which his legions acclaimed him imperator.

4. Biblical, Medieval, and English Transmission:

With the rise of the Roman Empire, Cilicia gained enduring prominence in the New Testament as the birthplace of Saul of Tarsus (Saint Paul), who was a Roman citizen by birth. The name entered Old and Middle English directly through biblical scriptures and Latin liturgical texts. In the late Middle Ages, Cilicia became famous throughout Christendom as the seat of the Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia (1080–1375 CE), which provided crucial logistical and military support to the Crusades. The term also bequeathed to European languages the ecclesiastical word cilicium (haircloth), derived from the Cilician goat hair traditionally used for manufacturing sackcloth garments worn for Christian penance.
