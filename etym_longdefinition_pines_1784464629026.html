Pines here is the plural of the tree-name: evergreen coniferous trees of the genus Pinus (and, in loose literary use, closely similar conifers), characterized by needle-shaped leaves, resinous wood and sap, and usually tall, straight growth. The pines are the classic northern forest tree — dark, windswept, resin-scented, growing on heights and rocky slopes — and the word in the plural names not a single specimen but a whole stand or ridgeline of them: a landscape of trees that becomes, in poetry, a collective presence, almost a chorus. In the dwarves' lament — “The pines were roaring on the height” — the plural is doing atmospheric work. The trees do not whisper or rustle; they roar, and the poet makes a whole mountainside of pines an acoustic instrument of the storm. The winds moan below while the pines roar above; the forest is personified as a sound-producing power, stage machinery for approaching doom. Tone: ballad wilderness, northern and ominous; the pines are weather's instrument, not botanical inventory. Primary senses of pine / pines
  • Any of various evergreen coniferous trees of the genus Pinus, with needle-shaped leaves borne in clusters and typically resinous cones — the botanical sense.
  • In looser or literary use, any tall, dark, needle-leaved conifer — fir, spruce, and larch are sometimes loosely called pines, though botanically distinct.
  • The timber of such trees — “pine wood,” “white pine,” “pine furniture” — one of the world's most important softwoods, used for building, masts, matchsticks, and pulp.
  • The plural, collectively — “the pines,” a pinewood or pine forest; a ridgeline or stretch of country covered in pines.
Polysemy and secondary meanings; the homonym trap
  • The verb to pine: to waste away with longing or grief, to yearn deeply (“to pine for home”). This verb is a completely different word from the tree-name: it descends from Old English pīnian, “to torment, afflict,” ultimately from Latin poena, “punishment, pain.” The two are homonyms — same spelling, unrelated origins — and must never be confused etymologically.
  • Pineapple: the tropical fruit, so named in the 17th century because the fruit was thought to resemble a pine-cone; the word has no botanical connection to the pines of the genus Pinus beyond that old visual analogy.
  • Pine-nuts / pine seeds: the edible seeds of certain pines (the piñón), used in cooking — a genuinely ancient human use of the tree.
  • Pine resin / turpentine: pine pitch and its distilled oils were the ancient world's great source of resin and turpentine — the very thing the tree's name originally meant (see the etymology): the fat, sticky sap.
  • Pine as adjective and colour: “pine green,” the dark blue-green of pine needles, a standard colour-name.
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge of the senses The tree-name itself is a sap-name. Latin pīnus “pine” derives from a root meaning “fat, rich, swollen with juice” — the tree is named for its resin, the sticky, fatty sap that streams from its bark and fills its wood. This is why the word's original force is “the resinous tree,” and it explains the deep association of pines with pitch, tar, turpentine, and the whole maritime economy of masts and pitch-sealed ships. In English the word has been the tree-word since the Old English period, when pīn was borrowed directly from Latin. The verb homonym — to pine, to languish — entered English by a separate Latin route (poena, pain, punishment), and the two words have simply travelled side by side ever since, sharing a spelling but never a history. How pines differs from its near-synonyms
  • Fir and spruce — related conifers but different genera; firs have flattened needles and upright cones, spruces have four-sided needles and hanging cones; poets may blur them, but the words name different trees.
  • Conifers — the whole class of cone-bearing evergreens (pine, fir, spruce, larch, cedar, yew); a pine is a conifer, but most conifers are not pines.
  • Evergreens — trees that keep their leaves through winter; a descriptive class, not a species; many non-conifers (holly, laurel) are evergreen.
  • Woods / forest / pines as a collective — the plural “the pines” approaches these collectives, but names the tree-kind specifically, not the whole vegetation.
In the card's context The line keeps the plural PINES as the subject of the roaring: a mountainside of trees made voice in the storm. Only the tree plural matters here — roaring vegetation on the height as the soundscape of approaching catastrophe — and the word's deep history as “the resinous tree” adds its own ancient odour of pitch and wilderness to the ballad gloom.
