- Flight feather of the wing (14th c.) - The noun pinion enters English naming the long, stiff outer quill-feather that drives a bird through the air; a purely ornithological, technical term of falconry and the mews, carrying no cruelty.
- The falconer's verb (15th-16th c.) - From the feather comes the act: 'to pinion' a bird means to clip those flight feathers or bind the wing fast against the body, so that flight becomes physically impossible; the word begins its move from noun to verb, from object to deed.
- Transference to the human captive (17th-18th c.) - By a natural extension the verb is applied to people: to pinion is to pin the arms tightly behind the back with rope, cord, or a wrestler's grip; the sense settles into the vocabulary of capture and imprisonment.
- Modern literary charge (19th-20th c.) - The participle 'pinioned' becomes the stage-direction of accomplished capture in prose: to be pinioned is to be rendered flightless, stripped of escape, resistance, and dignity, the ornithological origin now working as pure metaphor.
