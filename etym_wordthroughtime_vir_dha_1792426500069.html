- [Epic Composition Era (c. 5th–3rd c. BCE)] — introduced in Vālmīki's Rāmāyaṇa as the first ferocious obstacle of the Araṇyakāṇḍa, establishing Rāma's divine role as the protector of forest ascetics and introducing the motif of liberation through combat (*śāpa-mokṣa*) — [perception: feared as the terrifying personification of primordial forest violence and cannibalistic savagery]
- [Classical Sanskrit Kāvya Period (c. 4th–8th c. CE)] — dramatized in Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa and Bhaṭṭi's Rāvaṇavadha (Bhaṭṭikāvya), where the battle with Virādha was celebrated as an essential demonstration of heroic martial improvisation (burying an invulnerable foe) — [perception: admired as a masterclass in epic heroism, warrior ingenuity, and mythological salvation]
- [Medieval Vernacular Rāmāyaṇas (c. 12th–17th c. CE)] — retold in Kamban's Tamil Irāmāvatāram and Tulsīdās's Rāmacaritamānasa, where the killing of Virādha was reinterpreted through deep Bhakti theology as an act of boundless divine grace (*anugraha*), transforming a terrifying execution into a soul's joyous return to heaven — [perception: venerated as a testament to Rāma's power to liberate even the most degraded sinners from worldly bondage]
- [Colonial & Modern Indological Studies (19th–20th c.)] — analyzed by comparative mythologists and historians (such as Hermann Jacobi and Arthur Anthony Macdonell) as an anthropological reflection of the dangerous frontier encounters between migrating Aryan hermits and indigenous forest-dwelling populations — [perception: interpreted as an allegorical narrative marking the southern expansion of ascetic civilization]
- [Contemporary Literary & Cultural Era (20th–21st c.)] — recognized in South Asian performing arts, Kathakali dance-dramas, and televised epic adaptations as the iconic opening trial of Rāma's forest exile, while his name remains a literary symbol of obstinate obstruction overcome by righteousness — [perception: permanent cultural fixture representing the inevitable triumph of divine dharma over monstrous ego]
