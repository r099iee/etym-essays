PIE *bʰren- 'to project, stand out' (reconstructed, unattested; the root is often invoked for Latin frōns, but the etymology is debated — some scholars derive frōns from *bʰren-, others treat its origin as uncertain or from a different formation; sister languages use unrelated roots for 'forehead' — e.g., Greek métōpon, German Stirn — so the reconstruction rests on thin comparative evidence and must be flagged as uncertain) → Latin frōns, frontis 'forehead, brow; face; front, foremost part; façade; outward show' (attested from the 3rd century BCE; derivatives include frontālis 'frontal' and the phrase frontem praebere 'to present a bold front') → Medieval Latin *frontaria, *frontāria 'borderland, frontier' (a formation on front- with the -āria suffix, the ancestor of the Romance frontier words; the precise Latin form is reconstructed rather than directly attested) → Old French frontiere, frontière 'borderland, the district that faces another power, frontier' (attested 12th century, formed on Old French front 'forehead, front') → Middle English frounter, frontier 'border of a country, frontier' (attested 14th–15th century) → English frontier, plural frontiers 'the border(s) of a country; an outer military or political edge' (modern senses include the American settlement frontier and, from the 20th century, the metaphorical frontiers of science). Note on debate: the Old French to English path is secure; the deep origin of Latin frōns is not. The *bʰren- root is a reasonable but not certain reconstruction, and false cognates (such as German Stirn 'forehead') must not be forced.
