- Purely factual, 'unknown' (Old English onward) - uncūþ simply means not known, unfamiliar, strange, with no judgement attached; cūþ, 'known,' is the past participle of cunnan, 'to know'
- The drift toward social sense (Middle English to 14th c.) - what is unknown is strange; what is strange is foreign; uncouth begins to mean unfamiliar, rough, awkward
- Settled as 'rude, unrefined' (16th-17th c.) - the word completes its migration from epistemology to etiquette: uncultured, unpolished, without grace, while the old 'unknown' sense withers away
- The modern irony (19th c. onward) - the word now means simply bad-mannered, and English coins couth (c. 1896) as its opposite, forgetting that the negative once contained the positive - what is couth is only 'known'
- Contemporary - an old-fashioned word of manners, often comic, yet capable of the older, bleaker force: the rude as the unknowing, excluded from the circle of the known
