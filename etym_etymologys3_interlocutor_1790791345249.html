The etymology of interlocutor unfolds across more than two millennia of linguistic evolution, stemming from an ancient Indo-European verbal root for speech and passing through Roman jurisprudence, canon law, theatrical dialogue, and modern international diplomacy.

1. Proto-Indo-European Verbal Roots (*tolkʷ- / *tlokʷ-): The foundational building block of interlocutor is the Proto-Indo-European root *tolkʷ- (or metathesized *tlokʷ-), signifying 'to speak', 'to utter', or 'to talk'. This root left traces in several Indo-European branches, including Old Church Slavonic tlŭkŭ ('interpretation, translation'), Old Irish tluchair ('he speaks, asks'), and most prominently, the Latin deponent verb loquī ('to speak, say, declare'). Through loquī, this root spawned an extensive family of English words including eloquent, loquacious, colloquial, circumlocution, soliloquy, and ventriloquist.

2. Latin Composition: Inter + Loquī (Interloquī): In Classical Latin, the spatial preposition and prefix inter, meaning 'between', 'among', or 'in the midst of' (derived from PIE *enter 'between, among'), was prefixed to loquī to create the compound verb interloquī. The primary meaning of interloquī was literally 'to speak between', 'to interrupt a speaker', or 'to interpose a spoken remark in the midst of a discussion'.

3. Late Latin Legal Specialization (Interlocutio and Interlocutor): During the Late Roman Empire and the emergence of Roman civil law, interloquī acquired a specialized juridical application. When a judge made an intermediate oral ruling or procedural determination during the course of a trial—before delivering the final definitive verdict (the sententia)—that provisional decision was termed an interlocutio (an 'interlocutory judgment'). Consequently, the agent noun interlocutor was formed using the masculine agentive suffix -tor to denote the judicial official who pronounced this intermediate judgment or intervened between the disputing litigants.

4. Medieval Canon Law and French Vernacularization: Through the medieval ecclesiastical court system, canon lawyers maintained the use of interlocutor and its related terms. In the 16th century, the word was borrowed into Middle French as interlocuteur. In French humanist literature and drama, the meaning broadened beyond courtroom judges to encompass any participant in a literary dialogue or theatrical debate—a speaker engaging with a conversational counterpart.

5. English Adoption and the Evolution of Modern Registers: The word was adopted into English in the 16th century, initially appearing in translations of continental legal and philosophical texts. By the 17th and 18th centuries, writers like John Locke and Jonathan Swift used interlocutor to refer to the characters representing distinct viewpoints in philosophical colloquies. In the 19th century, the term took an unusual theatrical turn in American minstrel shows, where the 'Interlocutor' served as the dignified master of ceremonies. In the 20th century, the word shed its minstrelsy associations and became firmly established in international diplomacy as the standard term for an accredited negotiating partner, as well as in sociolinguistics and ethnography to denote an active collaborator in communicative discourse.
