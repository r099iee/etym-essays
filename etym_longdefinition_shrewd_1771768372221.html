In the sentence about Horace Walpole — “The shrewd observer,” who “greeted the first volume of Gibbon’s history with surprise as ‘a truly classic work’” — shrewd praises a sharp, penetrating, unsentimental intelligence that is not easily deceived. The nuance fits Walpole exactly: it suggests a man who saw through pretension, weighed men and books with worldly exactness, and was no slave to fashionable enthusiasm, so that his surprise and his verdict carry the weight of a discerning, almost ruthless connoisseurship. Gibbon was famously gratified that so severe a critic was impressed, and the word shrewd is precisely what makes that surprise meaningful: an endorsement from a “shrewd observer” reads not as flattery but as measured, hard-won judgment.

Beneath the modern compliment lingers a faint undertone of the word’s dark past. Shrewd derives from the noun shrew, the tiny fierce insectivore, and in its earliest recorded use (c. 1300) the adjective meant “wicked, evil, malicious, cursed” — a person as dangerous as the venomous little beast of folk-belief. By the sixteenth century the meaning had turned: cleverness came to be re-read as a kind of dangerous sharpness, so that a “shrewd” person was cunning and knowing; and by the eighteenth century the negative sense had faded into the background, leaving the fully positive modern meaning of astute, sharp practical judgment. In a sentence like the Walpole one, that history is faintly audible: the compliment retains a cutting quality, a slight sharp edge, as if the insight itself had a bite — the observer is not merely clever but slightly ruthless, self-possessed, quick to exploit what he sees.

The senses fan out across time and register:
  • Sharp practical judgment — the central modern sense: keen, penetrating, worldly intelligence in weighing people and situations; applied to observers, investors, negotiators, and bargainers.
  • Cunning / crafty (slightly pejorative) — a residual sense where shrewdness shades into sly, calculating self-interest, especially in commercial contexts (“a shrewd deal” implying advantage taken).
  • Painful, piercing (archaic) — an older sense applied to blows, cold, or grief (“a shrewd knock,” “shrewd weather”), preserving the original “cutting, biting” image; now obsolete or dialectal.
  • Cursed, wicked (archaic) — the earliest recorded sense, from the past participle of the verb to shrew (“to curse”); extinct in modern use but decisive for the word’s etymology.
The boundary words each set off a distinct facet. Astute is the nearest synonym — both mean quick to perceive and to turn perception to advantage — but astute stresses intellectual quickness and the spotting of opportunity, while shrewd adds practical experience and a hint of cool, calculating self-interest. Canny (chiefly British and Scottish) is shrewdness tempered by caution and economy, a thrifty wariness of being cheated. Sagacious suggests profound, weighty wisdom, often philosophical and old; shrewd is faster, more street-level, more transactional. Sharp is purely quick-witted and may be cutting but lacks the worldly judgment that shrewd implies. Perceptive and insightful name the simple act of seeing accurately and omit both the practical know-how and the slight hard edge of shrewdness. Clever is intellectual agility without judgment. Crafty and sly are the pejorative partners, stressing trickery rather than wise perception, and mark the point where shrewdness tips into suspicion.
