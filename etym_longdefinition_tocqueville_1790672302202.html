Tocqueville in this context is shorthand for Alexis-Charles-Henri Clérel, comte de Tocqueville (1805–1859), French magistrate, deputy, minister, traveller and political sociologist, author of De la démocratie en Amérique / Democracy in America (1835, 1840) and L'Ancien Régime et la Révolution / The Old Regime and the Revolution (1856). The bare surname with noble particle — De Tocqueville noted it — invokes not merely a man but an authority-function: the disquieted liberal aristocrat who saw democracy from inside and outside at once and warned that democratic societies care for ideas while neglecting the language, forms and associations through which ideas live. To cite Tocqueville is to borrow premature classic status and mournful prescience.

Life and works must be known in outline. Born into a Norman legitimist family that survived the Terror — his parents narrowly escaped the guillotine — Tocqueville trained in law, served as juge auditeur at Versailles, and in 1831–1832 sailed with Gustave de Beaumont to America ostensibly to study penitentiaries. The nine-month tour from New York to New Orleans, via New England townships, the frontier, Washington and the South, yielded a mountain of notebooks, interviews with Jackson, John Quincy Adams, Sam Houston and settlers, and the two-part masterpiece that made him famous at thirty. Elected deputy for Valognes (1839–1851), briefly Foreign Minister in 1849, imprisoned after Louis-Napoleon's coup, he withdrew to write the unfinished history of the Revolution before dying of tuberculosis at Cannes.

Core ideas associated with the name form a portable doctrine:

  • Equality as providential fact: democratic centuries level conditions; aristocratic hierarchy gives way to the passion for equality, which becomes the master-motive, more ardent than liberty itself.
  • Tyranny of the majority and soft despotism: formal liberty may coexist with social omnipotence — opinion pressures conformity, while an immense tutelary state shepherds isolated individuals who have withdrawn into private life. The famous image of a network of small rules covering society like fine mesh.
  • Associations, mores and self-interest rightly understood: American township, jury, church and voluntary association school citizens in liberty; mœurs — habits of heart and mind — matter more than laws. Democracy survives only where art of association corrects individualism.
  • Language and form: Tocqueville repeatedly notes democratic impatience with form — abstract, general terms multiply, rhetoric swells, while precise craft of words decays. Hence the aptness of citing him in a plea for prose: he diagnosed the belief that only ideas matter as itself a democratic pathology.
  • Old Regime continuity thesis: the Revolution did not break centralisation but completed Bourbon state-building; administrative despotism persists under new masters. A warning against mistaking rupture of personnel for change of regime.

Use and tone require care. Tocqueville as cited authority carries high-liberal, melancholy, non-partisan prestige: left and right both claim him — the left for participatory association, the right for anti-statism and religion. The disquiet noted in the prompt is characteristic: Tocqueville rarely celebrates or condemns outright; he observes with aristocratic detachment and democratic sympathy, warning that the very passion for equality that redeems also threatens liberty through conformity, materialism and love of the present. Grammatically the name keeps its particle in French — Alexis de Tocqueville, M. de Tocqueville — but English often drops to bare Tocqueville after first mention; the prompt's De Tocqueville with capital D reflects older English convention for sentence-initial or reverential use. Pronunciation in RP borrows uneasily: /ˈtɒkvɪl/, sometimes /ˈtɒkəvɪl/, versus French /tɔkvil/. To write De Tocqueville noted it with disquiet is thus also a stylistic performance — displaying humane letters, invoking a witness who cannot be dismissed as mere stylist, because his subject was liberty itself.

Keeper status is uncontested. Unlike minor prefects or one-book pamphleteers, Tocqueville is a global classic, continuously in print, foundational to sociology, political science and American self-understanding. Deleting the card as an NPC would amputate a central node of modern thought; filling it must establish biography, bibliography, theses, reception and pronunciation so the reviewer meets not an empty name but a mind.
