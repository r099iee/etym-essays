A word of the water-trade. The verb moor is, as far as we can tell, a native of the North Sea. It surfaces in Middle English in the early sixteenth century as moren or mouren, in unmistakably nautical contexts, and the two most credible sources proposed for it are the Middle Low German mōren and the Middle Dutch māren, both meaning "to tie or fasten a ship." This is exactly the kind of word that travelled with the Hanseatic merchants, pilots, and shipwrights who dominated the Baltic and North Sea trade routes and brought their seamanship vocabulary into English ports. Scholars reconstruct a Proto-Germanic *mairjaną / *mōrijaną, "to make fast," and some — cautiously — reach behind it to a PIE root *mer-, "to bind, tie"; but every honest dictionary marks that final step as uncertain. The word's entire documented life is West Germanic; it is a word born of ropes, tides, and trade, not of Indo-European prehistory.

From rope to metaphor. In English of the sixteenth century the verb established its core nautical sense — to make a vessel fast with cables or ropes to a fixed point — and threw off the noun mooring (a place of anchorage, a fastening) by about 1490. Almost immediately the word began to drift inland and inward, developing the figurative sense "to fix, fasten, hold steady": a person could be "moored" to a place, a duty, or a belief, exactly as a ship is moored to her mooring post. This metaphorical extension is the quiet engine of the word's longevity: from the rope that holds a boat in a creek, English derived the image of anything — a life, a memory, a nation — held fast against drift. The past-tense form moored, a regular weak inflection, is the very shape Lewis chooses in the Narnia passage, where it closes the sea-journey with a knot tied tight.

The three moors. Any rigorous history must separate the verb from two look-alike nouns, and the protocol's anti-merging rule demands it here. The noun moor, "a tract of open, waterlogged wasteland," descends from Old English mōr (the word behind the Moors of Dartmoor and the Yorkshire moors, and kin to Dutch moer, "marsh"); it has nothing to do with tying ropes. The proper noun Moor, "a member of the Muslim peoples of North Africa," comes from Latin Maurus, through the Mauri of Mauretania; it too is unrelated. Only the nautical verb, from the Low German and Dutch stock, gives us moored. The accidental identity of sound is a classic homonym trap, and the three words continue to live side by side without ever sharing a root.

Modern career. In modern maritime English moor remains the general word for securing a vessel — alongside its technical derivatives mooring, mooring buoy, mooring rope, and mooring post — while the figurative sense has grown even stronger in prose: "moored to the past," "a life moored in habit," "firmly moored in evidence." The word's register is technical but never arcane: it is the ordinary seaman's verb, and its past form moored carries the calm, competent finality of a voyage ended and made safe — exactly the note Lewis strikes when the Dawn Treader's crew "ran the boat up into a little creek and moored her to a tree."

  • Semantic drift summary: "to fasten, tie" (Proto-Germanic *mairjaną) → "to moor a ship" (Middle Low German mōren / Middle Dutch māren) → "to secure a vessel" (Middle English moren, c. 1500) → "to fix, fasten, hold steady" (English figurative moor, 16th c. onward) → "made fast" (English moored).
  • Sound changes: the word entered English with the -ō- of the continental forms (mōren/māren → moren/mouren) and settled to modern /mʊə/; the weak past-tense -ed is the regular English inflection.
  • Geographical context: the word is a maritime loan from the Hanseatic world — its distribution across the North Sea coastlines (Low German, Dutch, Frisian, and English) is itself evidence of the trading network that carried it.
