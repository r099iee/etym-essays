PERIL means serious, immediate, and usually mortal danger - exposure to harm so grave that destruction or death is a real and present possibility. The word names not a mild risk but the active presence of threat: one is in peril, at peril, or surrounded by perils. In the sentence behind this card - 'And if we in the Keep are still in peril' - the word reports a state of mortal jeopardy continuing into the present: the speakers are still exposed, still hunted, still in danger, and the noun's gravity supplies the urgency of the question. Peril is a danger that one cannot shrug off; it is the danger that may end you.

CORE SENSES
  • Immediate mortal danger: the state of being exposed to likely harm, destruction, or death - the peril of a storm at sea, of a battlefield, of a collapsing structure.
  • Risk of loss or harm to a specific thing: the peril of losing a fortune, a reputation, or a life - the danger that threatens a particular valued object or state.
  • Legal and technical senses: in insurance, a 'peril' is the specific cause of loss covered or excluded by a policy - fire, flood, theft, earthquake, windstorm; the 'named perils' in a policy are the enumerated causes that the insurer will pay for.
  • Maritime and legal formula: 'perils of the sea' - the classic exemption in shipping contracts and marine insurance for dangers of navigation that no human skill can prevent, such as storms, rocks, and shipwreck.
  • Figurative and idiomatic extension: 'at your peril' - used as a warning that the person who disregards a condition does so at the cost of their own safety or wellbeing; 'do it at your peril' means 'the risk is entirely yours'.
POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY MODERN MEANINGS IN DETAIL
  • Insurance and risk management - 'named perils policy', 'all-perils cover', 'fire peril': the technical noun names discrete causes of loss. The semantic bridge is the old sense of peril as 'the dangerous thing itself' rather than merely 'the state of being endangered'; a policy does not insure the state, it insures against enumerated dangerous events.
  • Law - 'perils of the sea', 'inherent perils': the legal doctrine that certain dangers are not the fault of any party and therefore excuse liability; the word keeps its ancient gravity in the courtroom.
  • Literature and rhetoric - 'in grave peril', 'deadly peril', 'perilous journey': the high-literary use of the word for epic danger; perilous is its adjective, and the pair has always been a favourite of romance, epic, and adventure fiction.
  • Video games and design - 'peril' as a descriptor of extreme difficulty or danger zones: a casual extension of the literary sense into modern entertainment language.
  • Idioms - 'at one's peril', 'in peril of one's life': fixed phrases preserving the noun's oldest function of naming the thing one stands to lose.
HISTORICAL AND SEMANTIC BRIDGE
The bridge runs from trial to danger. Latin periculum first meant 'a trial, a test, an attempt' - putting something to the proof, as in an experiment or an ordeal - and only secondarily 'the danger inherent in such a trial'. The Romans knew that to test anything risky is to expose it to harm; periculum is cognate with experiri 'to test' (source of English experience and experiment), and both come from the PIE root *per-, 'to try, to venture, to risk, to lead across'. What begins as 'putting to the test' becomes 'the risk run in the test' and then simply 'danger'. English borrowed the word through Old French in the thirteenth century with the 'danger' sense already dominant, and it has never returned to the neutral 'trial' of its Latin youth.

REGISTER AND USAGE
  • Peril is more intense and more literary than danger; it implies immediacy, magnitude, and usually the threat of destruction or death, whereas danger may be mild, distant, or merely probable.
  • The adjective perilous ('full of peril', from Old French perilleus) keeps the same elevated register and is common in phrases like 'a perilous journey'.
  • The word is at home in law and insurance as a technical noun and in epic and adventure prose as a high literary one; everyday speech prefers danger, hazard, or risk.
  • Peril collocates with verbs of facing and escaping (face peril, brave the peril, survive the perils) and with intensifiers (grave, mortal, deadly, imminent, dire).
