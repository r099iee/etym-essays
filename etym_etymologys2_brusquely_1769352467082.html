Origin: a non-Indo-European (pre-Roman) Mediterranean substrate / Gaulish (Celtic) root *brusko-, "rough heath, prickly scrubland, thorny shrub" (reconstructed; a pre-IE or Gaulish element, not a PIE root) → Vulgar Latin / Medieval Latin bruscum (also *bruscus), "butcher's broom (Ruscus aculeatus), a stiff prickly shrub" (attested in medieval botanical and culinary Latin; its roughness and harshness become a stock metaphor) → Italian brusco (adjective), "tart, harsh, sharp, rough" — of wine that bites the tongue, then of rough, unpolished manners (attested by the 14th c.) → French brusque (adjective), "abrupt, rough, sharp, fierce" (borrowed from Italian brusco, 16th c., c. 1560s) → English brusque (adjective), "abrupt, blunt, rough in manner" (borrowed from French, 17th c., 1650s) → brusque + adverbial suffix -ly → brusquely, "abruptly, bluntly, curtly, to the point of rudeness" (adverbial use recorded from the 17th–18th c.) Note on debate: The origin of the word is uncertain. OED marks brusque's etymology as of uncertain origin; the derivation runs securely from Italian brusco and Latin bruscum, but the pre-Latin source is a reconstruction. A traditional connection to PIE *bhreg- "to break" (with bruscum read as "that which breaks/snaps") is sometimes proposed but is not generally accepted, since bruscum is better explained as a substrate or Gaulish borrowing. The form bruscum also blended with the similar Latin word ruscum, the classical name of the same plant (butcher's broom), so the two plant-names interfered in the written tradition.
