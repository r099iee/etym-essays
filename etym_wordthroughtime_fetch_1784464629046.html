“Fetch” has meant to go and bring something back since the earliest English — the verb of errands and retrieval, built on the round-trip of going away and returning with the object.
“Fetch” in Middle and early modern English was also the dialect noun for a spirit-double or apparition of a living person — a “fetch” that came to fetch the soul — so the plain verb shadowed a darker supernatural cousin.
“Fetch” through the 18th–19th centuries extended to the training of dogs (“fetch the ball”) and to brisk housekeeping commands, keeping the go-and-return schema in everyday errands.
“Fetch” today remains the plain, brisk verb of going to get — domesticated as a dog-command and household imperative, with the apparition sense all but forgotten except in dialect and folklore.
