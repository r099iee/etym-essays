Democratically packages 2,500 years of political thought into an adverb: from a Greek compound insulted by aristocrats, through Latin scholarship, French Revolution ideology, to modern English manner-adverb, with an ironic extension to animals treated as equals.

The first element begins with PIE *da-, to divide. From division comes apportionment of land, hence district, hence the people of a district. Proto-Greek *damos, Attic dêmos, thus meant the commons as opposed to chiefs: villagers, soldiers, citizens. The second element kratos, strength, rule, dominion, is usually traced to PIE *kret-, strong, seen in karteros. Their compound dēmokratía, coined in 5th-century Athens after Cleisthenes, literally people-power. Aristocratic writers like Pseudo-Xenophon used it sneeringly: rule by the rabble. Democrats embraced it: equality before law, lot-chosen juries, assembly pay.

  • Classical Athens: radical experiment. Dēmokratía denoted direct male-citizen rule, ostracism, demagogues and rhetoric. Philosophers were ambivalent: Plato scorned it as mob rule, Aristotle classified it as a deviant constitution. The adverbial idea dēmokratikōs already existed: acting in a popular manner.
  • Hellenistic to Late Latin transmission. Polybius and Cicero transliterated democratia as a Greek curiosity, contrasted with aristocratia and monarchia. Medieval scholastics preserved it in Latin manuscripts without live referent; monarchy and church were the realities.
  • Early Modern revival. Renaissance humanists, then 17th-century English parliamentarians and 18th-century French philosophes, revived democracy as ideal and insult. French démocratique (1670s) and démocratiquement provided the adjectival and adverbial models. The American and French Revolutions made democracy a rallying cry.
  • English democratic / democratically. English borrowed democracy c. 1570s, democratic c. 1790s under French influence, and democratically soon after by regular -ally adverb formation. It meant in a manner pertaining to popular rule: democratically elected, democratically decided. Spelling with -ally follows democratic + -ally, not -ly alone.
  • Extension to egalitarian manner generally. By the 19th-20th centuries the adverb broadened beyond voting procedure to any egalitarian, inclusive, brotherly manner: to share democratically, to treat children democratically, to admit animals democratically into society — the exact use here, where a beast becomes comrade, not property. This is metaphorical democracy: equal membership without ballot boxes.
  • Phonetic and morphological notes. Stress sits on -crat-: /ˌdem.əˈkræt.ɪ.kəl.i/. The long chain preserves Greek dēmo- and -crat- intact across Latin, French and English, showing learned borrowing rather than popular sound change. The suffixes -ic (adjectival), -al (relational) and -ly (adverbial) stack transparently.

Socio-culturally, democratically moved from pejorative to honorific. Nineteenth-century conservatives still warned against democratically levelling all distinction; twentieth-century liberals celebrated democratically run schools, workplaces and families. Durant's ironic use for a domesticated animal joining human society captures that honorific glow: to be accepted democratically is to be accepted as an equal, not as a slave or tool.

Today the adverb covers both procedural precision (elected, accountable, constitutional) and vague egalitarian ethos (open, inclusive, unpretentious). Its history is democracy's history in miniature.
