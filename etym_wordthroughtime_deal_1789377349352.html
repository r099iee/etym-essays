- Old English (Pre-12th Century) - Native Old English *dǣl* and verb *dǣlan* mean "part, portion, share; to divide, distribute"; unrelated to wood.
- 14th Century - Hanseatic League merchants import Baltic pine and fir planks into English ports; the Middle Low German / Dutch word *dele* ("plank, floorboard") enters Middle English joinery.
- 15th to 16th Century - The timber noun and the native sharing noun merge phonetically and orthographically as *dele* / *deal*. Carpentry statutes standardize "deal" as a pine board 9 inches wide.
- 17th to 18th Century - In gaming, "dealing" cards becomes standard; in commerce, "a deal" shifts from apportioning shares to negotiating a trade or business transaction. Concurrently, "deal" furniture denotes honest, plain cottage carpentry.
- 19th Century - Victorian social realism heavily features "deal boxes", "deal tables", and "deal coffins" as symbols of working-class humility and pauperism (satirized by Oscar Wilde in 1888); American slang popularizes "a square deal."
- 20th to 21st Century - Commercial and political senses ("business deal", "New Deal", "deal with it") dominate global speech, while the carpentry sense survives among antique restorers and timber trades.
