A simile is a figure of speech that explicitly compares two essentially unlike things by means of like, as, than, or equivalent markers: brave as a lion, ran like the wind, white as snow. Unlike metaphor, which fuses identities (he is a lion), simile keeps the comparison open and signalled, inviting the hearer to map qualities from vehicle to tenor while remembering they remain distinct.

Rhetoricians distinguish Homeric or epic simile, which extends the vehicle into a miniature scene (as when Milton compares Satan's host to autumn leaves), from simple simile, a single-point likeness. Grammarians distinguish closed similes with explicit grounds (sweet as honey in taste) from open ones leaving inference to context. In the Dickens source the word is used in its older Latin sense of a proverbial comparison or parable: the wisdom of ancestors preserved in a traditional saying, which unhallowed modern hands should not meddle with. That is the Mrs Gamp-like voice of stolid conservatism, treating a homely comparison as constitutional scripture.

  • Core literary sense: like/as comparison; e.g. her smile was like sunshine after rain.
  • Epic simile: extended developed comparison spanning lines; e.g. Homer's armies like swarms of bees.
  • Proverbial simile: fixed folk comparison; e.g. drunk as a lord, fit as a fiddle.
  • Logical/biblical sense (older): parable or dark saying; Latin simile in the Vulgate for parables, surviving in Dickens's mock-solemn use.
  • Pedagogical contrast: textbook opposite of metaphor, tested in schools by hunting for like or as.
  • Everyday softener: hedged comparison to avoid bluntness; e.g. it felt like a failure cushions bald assertion.

Effect depends on distance and aptness. Good similes yoke distant domains to reveal hidden likeness; dead similes (white as snow, busy as a bee) are idioms no longer felt as figures. Culture supplies stock vehicles: lions for courage, foxes for cunning, snow for whiteness. Writers refresh them by twisting the vehicle or literalising it. Theological and legal writing once used simile for any illustrative parallel, a sense now largely yielded to analogy and precedent.

Do not confuse simile with metaphor, analogy, or metonymy, though they overlap in practice. If you can insert like without breaking grammar, critics often call it simile; if identity is asserted, metaphor. Dickens's joke reminds us that before schoolroom definitions hardened, simile could mean any wise saw handed down, the very vehicle of ancestral wisdom. Hence the mock piety: to disturb the simile is to disturb the constitution.

Classroom practice cements the device: pupils underline like and as, convert similes to metaphors and back, and invent fresh vehicles to replace dead ones. Examiners reward aptness, economy, and surprise, penalising mixed vehicles that yoke incompatible domains. Computational stylistics now counts simile markers to measure figuration across corpora, confirming that like remains the commonest explicit flag, followed by as, as if, and as though. Whether epic or everyday, the simile advertises its own artifice, asking assent rather than commanding belief, which is why tyrants prefer metaphor and teachers prefer simile.
