• titular: Similar because both refer to an entity whose name appears in a title; differs because titular strictly means "holding a title or appearing as the title character" (and can also imply having a title without actual power or authority), whereas eponymous specifically highlights the historical or creative act of name-giving.
  • self-titled: Similar because both describe artistic works named after their creator; differs because self-titled is an informal, plain modern compound used almost exclusively in commercial music and media, whereas eponymous carries rich classical and literary heritage.
  • name-giving: Similar as a direct descriptive translation of the Greek root; differs because name-giving is a plain Anglo-Saxon descriptive phrase rather than a formal, unified lexical term.
  • homonymous: Similar because both involve words or entities sharing identical names; differs because homonymous relates to linguistic homonyms (two different words sharing spelling/sound without etymological connection), lacking the causal donor-recipient relationship of an eponym.
  • appellative: Similar because both deal with designations and naming; differs because appellative refers to common nouns (as opposed to proper names) used to classify general categories.
  • patronymic: Similar because both describe names derived from persons; differs because patronymic is restricted strictly to names derived from a father or paternal ancestor (such as *Johnson* or *Ivanovich*).
  • eponymic: Similar as an alternate adjectival variant; differs because eponymic is a rarer morphological twin, with eponymous being by far the dominant standard form in literature and criticism.
  • namesake: Similar because both denote an entity that shares another's name; differs because namesake is a noun referring to the person or thing named in honor of another, rather than an adjective describing the name-giving relationship.
  • cognominal: Similar in referring to names; differs because cognominal relates to a surname, nickname, or shared cognomen rather than a title-originating founder.
  • nominative: Similar because both concern names; differs because nominative is primarily a grammatical case indicating the subject of a finite verb.
  • toponymic: Similar in the domain of naming; differs because toponymic refers specifically to the names of geographical places rather than human name-givers.
