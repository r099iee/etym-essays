Besieged is the past tense and past participle of the verb besiege, "to surround a place with armed forces in order to capture it, to cut it off, or to force its surrender". It is also used as a participial adjective describing a place or person subjected to such encirclement. Etymologically the word means "to cause to be sat upon or sat around": Latin obsidēre is "to sit before a place" (ob- "against, in front of" + sedēre "to sit"), and a besieging army literally camps in a ring about the walls, waiting. In the source passage, Bard formally declares the Mountain besieged: "I declare the Mountain besieged. You shall not depart from it, until you call on your side for a truce and a parley." Here the word is a legal-political act — the public proclamation of a blockade which, by the code of the story (echoing medieval custom), forbids the dwarves to leave, threatens them with starvation ("we leave you to your gold"), but promises no immediate assault.

Primary senses.

  • Military sense: to surround a fortified place — a city, castle or garrison — with troops so that it cannot receive supplies or reinforcements, and to press it by blockade and assault until it surrenders. "Besieged" names the resulting state: the town was besieged for nine months. A siege typically combines encirclement, interdiction of food and water, mining, bombardment and assault; the participle covers the whole condition of being invested.
  • Of persons, not places: a person can be besieged — a traveller besieged by wolves, a commander besieged in his headquarters, a garrison besieged in a ruined keep. The word then stresses confinement and pressure rather than fortification.

Secondary and figurative senses.

  • Figurative pressure from all sides: "besieged with questions", "besieged by reporters", "besieged by suitors or creditors" — the modern idiom par excellence. The semantic bridge is transparent: as an army surrounds a city, so attention, demands or troubles surround a person, so that they can neither escape nor get relief. First recorded in the later sixteenth century, this use has grown steadily and now rivals the military sense in frequency.
  • Siege mentality and contemporary metaphor: the modern phrase “siege mentality” — a defensive, embattled frame of mind that perceives hostility on every side — is a direct extension of the participle: a person or group feels itself besieged even without walls or weapons. In journalism, politics and sport “besieged” now standardly describes presidents, teams, cities and brands under overwhelming external pressure, and a besieged incumbent, a besieged bill or a besieged market are metaphors one meets weekly.
  • Psychological and inner besieging: "besieged by doubt", "besieged by fears", "a conscience besieged" — the besieger is an emotion or a memory and the mind is the citadel; a favourite figure in devotional, Romantic and modernist prose.
  • Technical-historical and gaming use: in historical writing, military history and strategy games, "besieged" keeps its strict technical meaning, surrounded by the vocabulary of siegecraft, siege engines, blockades, and "raising a siege".

Boundaries and register. Besieged is more intense and more complete than surrounded (which is neutral and can be friendly), more structured than beleaguered (chiefly figurative, with a note of weary harassment), and more formal than hemmed in. Its connotation is of hostile, methodical, inescapable pressure; its register ranges from plain military report to dramatic political metaphor ("a besieged government") in modern journalism.
