sock (as used on this card — the verb): to hit someone forcefully, especially with a closed fist; to punch, to strike hard; and, as a noun, "a hard blow or punch." The card's definition is "To hit forcefully; a punch," and this is the sense George uses in Of Mice and Men when he threatens Lennie: "You gonna give me that mouse or do I have to sock you?" The verb is informal, blunt, and strongly physical: a "sock" is a heavy, thudding, decisive blow rather than a delicate tap — the word's sound, a short, closed, resonant monosyllable, itself seems to land. In the example sentences, "He socked the ball out of the park" extends the sense to hitting a ball with great force, and "I was socked by the news of his departure" uses the metaphorical extension of being struck hard by information. The nuance is heavy, forceful, blunt impact — a punch or blow with real weight behind it. Polysemy & the full range of the word
English sock is a striking case of two unrelated words that happen to share a spelling and sound — a pair of homonyms with entirely separate histories:
  • Sense 1 — the verb "to hit" (this card): informal, first recorded in English slang of the late 17th century; "to strike or punch hard"; also "a hard blow." Origin unknown; probably imitative of the thud of a heavy blow, though a connection to the sock-garment used as a weighted weapon has also been suggested.
  • Sense 2 — the garment: a knitted or woven covering for the foot and ankle, worn inside a shoe; from Latin soccus, a light low slipper worn by comic actors, itself from Greek sýkkhos of likely non-Indo-European origin; the garment sense reached English through Old English socc. This sense is a completely separate word with its own history.
  • Sense 3 — theatre metonymy: from the comic actor's soccus, "the sock" became the classical symbol of comedy, paired with "the buskin" (the high boot, cothurnus) as the symbol of tragedy — "to put on the sock" meant to write or act comedy.
  • Sense 4 — extended and idiomatic uses: "to sock it to someone" (20th c.) — to attack or impress forcefully; "sock it away" (saving money) — to stash a heavy load away. These idioms grow from the verb sense.
Historical semantic bridge
The two halves of the word have no bridge to each other — they are pure homonyms. The garment sock descends through Greek and Latin from an ancient Mediterranean word for a light slipper; the verb sock appears abruptly in English street slang of the 1690s. For the verb, etymologists propose three main theories: (1) imitative origin — the word mimics the dull thud of a fist landing on flesh, joining the English echoic strike-family (thump, whack, smack, bash, clout); (2) the weighted sock — a stocking stuffed with coins or a stone, swung as an improvised weapon, a real practice of 17th-century street-fighting and maritime brawling; (3) dialectal inheritance — from some dialect word of obscure source. The most widely accepted view is that the verb is an imitative coinage; the true source is honestly unknown. What is certain is that the semantic core is heavy impact — the word was born in the world of fists and brawls. Metaphoric shifts and modern extensions
  • Punch → any forceful blow: "he socked the ball," "socked the nail" — the verb extends from hitting people to hitting objects with force.
  • Physical → emotional: "I was socked by the news" — to be struck hard emotionally, a 20th-century metaphor drawn from the physical punch.
  • Idioms: "sock it to (someone)" — the 1960s catchphrase, made famous by the TV show Rowan & Martin's Laugh-In and Aretha Franklin's 1967 "Respect," meaning to give one's all, to attack or impress with maximum force.
  • "Sock away": to save money, as though stashing a heavy load away — a 20th-century American idiom.
  • Theatre symbol: "the sock and the buskin" remain the classical emblems of comedy and tragedy in dramatic criticism — a high-register survival of the ancient slipper.
Boundary against near-synonyms
As a strike-verb, sock is the informal, heavy, blunt end of the hitting spectrum: punch is the standard word for a closed-fist blow; thump stresses the dull sound; wallop and clobber add comic force; slap is open-handed and lighter; smack is sharp and stinging; strike and hit are neutral, formal, and broad. "Sock" uniquely combines heaviness, informality, and a thudding sound-image.
