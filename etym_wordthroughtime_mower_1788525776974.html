- Old English to Middle English (c. 8th–14th Century) - Denoted an essential agrarian laborer (māwere / mowere) who cut standing meadow grass and grain by hand with a scythe or sickle during the summer hay-harvest - Foundational agricultural profession.
- 15th–17th Century - Expanded into powerful religious, poetic, and mythological allegory as an emblem of Death (the Grim Reaper) and Father Time, mowing down mortal lives; celebrated in pastoral literature (e.g. Andrew Marvell's 'Mower Poems') - Allegorical and pastoral symbolism.
- 18th–19th Century - Maintained its pastoral agricultural sense while undergoing a technological revolution with Edwin Budding’s 1830 invention of the mechanical cylinder lawnmower, permanently expanding the word from human laborer to mechanical device - Industrial and mechanical transition.
- 20th–21st Century - Pervasively denotes domestic motor, electric, and autonomous robotic lawnmowers, while agricultural mowers (disc, drum, flail mowers) dominate commercial farming - Modern mechanization and turf-care ubiquity.
