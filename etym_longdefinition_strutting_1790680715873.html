Strutting is the present participle of the verb strut: to walk with a stiff, erect, and self-satisfied gait, chest out, steps measured, as though putting oneself on show. The peacock simile in the source sentence is the word’s natural habitat — strutting is parading, moving so as to be admired. A soldier struts on parade, a rooster struts across the yard, a footballer struts after scoring; in each case the walk announces confidence, pride, and a desire to be seen. Applied to people it is very often faintly mocking: to say someone struts is to grant the confidence while questioning the vanity behind it.

The core sense is proud, showy walking, and the word’s grammar spreads that meaning in several directions. Intransitively it describes gait (he strutted across the room); with around or about it suggests aimless display (strutting around like peacocks); transitively, in the established phrase to strut one’s stuff, it means to display one’s abilities or attractiveness, especially in dance or performance. As a noun, strut names both the walk itself (a confident strut) and — by a separate engineering history — a structural brace, though that technical sense lies outside this card’s concern.

A secondary, literary sense of strut means to stalk across a stage with grandeur, immortalised by Shakespeare’s struts and frets his hour upon the stage in Macbeth: the tyrant’s brief, pompous performance before oblivion. From this comes the transferred use for any pompous transit through public life — politicians strutting on the world stage, executives strutting through headquarters. The word also shades into sexual display: ethologists describe male birds strutting before females, and human uses readily borrow that colouring, presenting the walker as courting attention or asserting dominance. Dictionaries also record the participial adjective in extended use for anything that parades itself — a strutting facade, strutting prose — where architecture or style seems to show off. In all these uses the semantic bridge is the same: erect posture plus deliberate visibility equals a claim of importance, sincerely admired by allies and satirised by everyone else — exactly the double vision the source passage exploits, where the neighbours’ display is both peacock-fine and peacock-foolish.

  • Proud gait: walking stiffly erect with an air of self-importance; the primary literal sense.
  • Parading / showing off: moving so as to attract admiration, as in strutting around or strutting one’s stuff.
  • Theatrical stalk: crossing a stage or public scene with grandiose self-presentation, after Shakespeare’s Macbeth usage.
  • Courtship / dominance display: the ethological sense of ritualised male promenading, extended metaphorically to human swagger.
  • Figurative swagger: behaving with ostentatious confidence in any domain — strutting through a debate, strutting one’s wealth.
  • Engineering homonym (distinct): a strut as a load-bearing brace; historically connected through the notion of stiff, rigid posture, but a separate modern sense.
