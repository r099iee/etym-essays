- Viking Age borrowing - English takes the Norse phrase "á lopt" ("in the air") and fuses it into the single adverb "aloft", meaning "on high" (c. 1200).
- Neutral height - In Middle and early Modern English "aloft" simply means "high above", used of towers, trees, mountains and things raised up.
- The age of sail - From the 14th century the word becomes a technical order aboard ship: "aloft" = up in the rigging and yards, high above the deck.
- Poetic elevation - Poets and prose writers keep the word for grand, soaring images: birds, kites, flags, smoke and raised torches; it develops a lofty, dignified tone.
- Modern technical senses - Meteorology and aviation adopt it for the upper air ("winds aloft", "climbing aloft"), while ordinary speech prefers "up".
- Today - "Aloft" is felt as literary or technical, evoking rigging, heights and raised lights, exactly as in Tolkien's hobbit holding his lamp aloft.
