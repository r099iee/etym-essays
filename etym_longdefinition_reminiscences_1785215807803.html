REMINISCENCES is the plural of the noun reminiscence, denoting a body of personal memories, recollections, or memoirs drawn from one's own lived experience — and, in its literary use, a written record of such recollections. In the sentence behind this card — "Carlyle, in his reminiscences, described her with ambiguous epithets" — the word refers specifically to a published work: Thomas Carlyle's autobiographical volume Reminiscences (1881), assembled and published after the essayist's death, in which the famously caustic diarist set down his retrospective impressions of leading Victorian figures, including Harriet Taylor Mill.

Primary senses: memory and recollection

At root, a reminiscence is the act of recalling the past, or a particular memory summoned back into consciousness. The plural names the accumulated store of such recollections. In philosophy, the word has a technical history: following Aristotle, medieval thinkers distinguished passive memoria, the storage of impressions, from active reminiscentia, the deliberate mental effort of reconstructing what has been stored — and the Latin re- prefix of the word marks that repeated, willed return to past experience.

Secondary senses: the literary genre and modern extensions

  • The published genre: since the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, "reminiscences" has been the standard title and genre for a book of retrospective portraits and anecdotes — loose, episodic, and devoted to other people, places, and scenes rather than to a systematic life-narrative. Carlyle's Reminiscences is the classic Victorian instance.
  • The single reminiscence: in the singular, a reminiscence is one recalled episode, often with a nostalgic or anecdotal flavour: "a reminiscence of her childhood."
  • The verb reminisce: a back-formation of the nineteenth century, meaning "to recall the past," usually in a fond or conversational key.
  • Modern psychologized use: reminiscence therapy, in care of the elderly, deliberately encourages the sharing of memories; the "reminiscence bump" in psychology names the tendency to recall more events from adolescence and early adulthood.
  • Formal and retrospective tone: the word carries an elegiac, sometimes querulous tone — as in Carlyle's late-life recollections, which often filter his judgments of the dead and the living through eccentric, equivocal phrasing.

Metaphoric shift and historical bridge

The word entered English in the seventeenth century (attested c. 1658) as a learned borrowing from Latin reminiscentia, "recollection," from the verb reminīscī, "to call back to mind," which is built on the Indo-European root *men-, "to think, remember." The shift from a philosophical faculty (recalling what the mind once knew) to a literary genre (a published book of recollections) happened over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries as biography and memoir culture flourished; the plural form became the genre's badge of title.

Register, grammar, and usage

The noun is countable ("a reminiscence," "his reminiscences") and appears characteristically with the verbs publish, write, record, collect, and entertain with. The plural is the genre form, and it is often used with a possessive ("in his reminiscences") to name an author's published retrospective. The family includes reminisce (verb), reminiscent (adjective), and reminiscently (adverb), and the near-relative remembrance. In the Carlyle passage the plural carries a documentary weight: the word names not an ordinary private act of remembering but a written record intended for posterity.
