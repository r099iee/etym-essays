Whit descends from a PIE noun for "thing". The chain below runs from reconstructed PIE to the modern word. (5 attested stages.) 1. PIE *wekti- "thing, creature, being" — a nominal formation of the "thing / living thing" family; its ultimate wider links are debated, though it is often connected with PIE *weg- "to be vigorous, awake, alive" (the creature as "the living one"; cf. Latin vigēre "to flourish", vigil "awake"). 2. → Proto-Germanic *wihtiz "thing, creature, being" — the Germanic reflex of the noun. 3. → Old English wiht / wyht "creature, being, thing, person" — one of the most common nouns of Old English, used in the poetic epithets of warriors and monsters alike. 4. → Middle English wight "person, creature, thing" (Chaucer's "a worthy wight") and the weakened, reduced form whit "a very small thing, the least particle" — the reduced form developed beside the full one. 5. → English whit "a very small amount" — surviving chiefly in the fossil phrases not a whit, no whit, never a whit. Final node: English WHIT (rootWord) — the present-day form, preserved almost entirely inside negative idioms. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The ultimate PIE source of *wekti- is not settled; the link to *weg- "to be vigorous" is plausible but unproven, and some scholars treat the noun as an isolated formation. (2) The reduction of wiht to whit (stage 4) is a regular phonological weakening, well attested, not debated. (3) The same root, combined with nā "no", produced Old English nāwiht "nothing" — the ancestor of modern English not — a sister development, not a different root.
