- Old English - 'shoe' (Old English 'sceoh') is ancient Germanic, and its -ing noun 'shoeing' names the ordinary act of fitting footwear; in a mounted age the craft of fitting iron shoes to horses is a prized trade of every village and castle.
- Medieval-early modern - farriery hardens into a skilled craft: the shoeing-smith shapes and nails iron shoes, and 'shoeing' is workaday, unsentimental, and vital to travel, war, and trade alike.
- 18th-19th century - with better roads and horse transport at its height, shoeing is a routine, professional operation of stables and blacksmiths, its vocabulary fixed (farriery, smithing, shod).
- 20th-21st century - the motor car empties the word of urgency; 'shoeing' now belongs to specialist farriery and equestrian care, while the same word keeps its humbler human sense (shoeing a child). In verse and song it survives as a homely, practical, almost antique word — as in the Elves' teasing promise that the weary ponies need shoeing.
