PLIANT descends from a PIE root meaning "to plait, weave, fold," through Latin and Old French. The chain: 1) PIE *plek- "to plait, weave, braid, fold, twist together" (reconstructed; the root of Greek plékō "to weave," Latin plicāre "to fold," Proto-Germanic *flehtaną "to braid" [German flechten, English flax]) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin plicāre "to fold, bend, double up, roll up" (attested from the 2nd c. BCE) — the direct ancestor; the source of the great -plic- family in English: complicāre (complicate, "fold together"), explicāre (explicate, "fold out"), implicāre (implicate, "fold in"), applicāre (apply, "fold to"), replicāre (reply, "fold back"), multiplicāre (multiply, "fold many times"), and the -plex adjectives (complex, duplex, simplex, multiplex). 3) Vulgar Latin *plīcāre "to fold" (reconstructed; the stressed ā > ī shift) — the spoken form behind the Romance verbs. 4) Old French plier / pleier "to fold, bend" (attested from the 9th c.) — French plier, "to fold," still in everyday use. 5) Old French pliant (present participle of plier, used as adjective, attested c. 1300) "bending, yielding, supple." 6) Middle English pliant (late 14th c., the age of Chaucer) "bending, flexible; easily influenced." 7) English pliant (modern) — PLIANT. NOTE ON DEBATE: (1) The core chain Latin plicāre → Old French plier → pliant → Middle English pliant → English pliant is secure and uncontroversial. (2) The proposed Indo-Aryan reflex of *plek- (Sanskrit pṛc- "to join, mix") is debated and should be treated cautiously; it does not affect the Romance/Germanic chain. (3) The "double-faced" moral sense (praiseworthy adaptability vs. blameworthy subservience) developed within English and French from the start of the word's use for persons in the 14th century.
