beaker (noun):

A beaker is primarily recognized in modern standard English as a flat-bottomed, cylindrical vessel with a small pouring lip or spout, made of heat-resistant borosilicate glass, plastic, or metal, ubiquitous in scientific laboratories for mixing, stirring, heating, and holding liquid or chemical reagents. In broader cultural, historical, and colloquial contexts, it refers to a wide-mouthed, stemless drinking vessel, cup, or goblet made of horn, metal, wood, or ceramic, as well as an everyday British term for an open, handleless drinking cup or child's drinking tumbler.

Primary and Secondary Senses:

  • Laboratory Glassware (Scientific Apparatus): A standard cylindrical container featuring a flat base, wide opening, graduated volume markings, and a distinct pouring beak or spout (commonly a Griffin beaker or Berzelius beaker), designed for experimental chemistry, biology, and pharmacology.
  • Historical & Ceremonial Drinking Cup: A large, handleless, stemless drinking cup or goblet, typically made of silver, pewter, horn, pottery, or glass, utilized in antiquity, medieval banqueting, and early modern toasts.
  • Everyday & Colloquial Drinkware (British & Commonwealth English): A handleless drinking vessel made of plastic or ceramic, commonly used by young children (e.g., a toddler's spill-proof beaker) or colloquially for an informal mug or large cup of tea or liquor.
  • Archaeological Classification (Bell-Beaker Culture): An inverted bell-shaped ceramic drinking pot dating to the late Neolithic and early Bronze Age (c. 2800–1800 BCE) across Western and Central Europe, which lent its name to the prehistoric "Bell-Beaker culture."
  • Informal / Literary Alcoholic Drink (as in P.G. Wodehouse): Metonymically used to refer to the intoxicating contents of a large glass or mixed cocktail (e.g., I mixed myself a beaker).

Semantic Bridge & Polysemy Evolution:

The semantic trajectory of beaker traces a fascinating path through European material culture and linguistic convergence. Originating in ancient Mediterranean trade vessels (Greek bīkos, a wine jar), the term passed through Late Latin into Germanic languages as a general term for a wide, handleless drinking vessel or goblet (Old Norse bikarr, Middle Dutch beker). Throughout the Middle Ages and Renaissance, a beaker was an indispensable piece of secular tableware for communal drinking. In the 18th and 19th centuries, as the natural sciences formalized laboratory protocols, experimentalists repurposed these open cylindrical cups for chemical operations. The presence of the small pouring lip—coupled with an irresistible folk-etymological association with the anatomical beak of a bird—cemented the word in scientific nomenclature, while everyday speech retained its original heritage as a humble drinking vessel.
