- Old English — “efes” is the roof’s overhanging edge; the compound “yfesdrype” (eaves-drip) marks the strip of ground where rain falls, a line that mattered in Anglo-Saxon property law.
- Middle English — “eves” broadens to any edge or brink (eaves of a wood, of a cliff); “evesdropper” (15th c.) names one who lurks near a house under the eaves to overhear talk.
- 16th–17th c. — speakers re-analyse the inherited -s as a plural and begin saying “the eaves are”; a fresh singular “eave” is back-formed; the verb “eavesdrop” (1600s) is born and moves indoors.
- 18th–19th c. — building manuals settle “eaves” as the architectural term for the projecting roof-edge; the old “brink/edge” sense dies out.
- 20th–21st c. — “eaves” is a plural-only building word (with Canadian “eavestrough”), while “eavesdropping” keeps the old roof-side image alive in everyday speech.
