Molo is a proper noun, not a common noun. In this reading it names a person: Apollonius Molon of Rhodes (Greek Ἀπολλώνιος Μόλων), a Greek rhetorician of the first century BCE, head of a celebrated school of rhetoric on Rhodes and the teacher of two of the most consequential prose writers of antiquity, Cicero and Julius Caesar. The source sentence — “Under one of these, Molo the rhetorician, he was able to study once again during a visit to Greece and Rhodes” — uses the bare name with the apposition “the rhetorician”, exactly as a historian might write “under Aristotle, the philosopher”.

Who he was. Molon belonged to the Greek East that supplied the Roman elite with its higher education. Rhodes, a wealthy maritime republic with a long philosophical and rhetorical tradition, was one of the great finishing schools of the late Republic: young Romans went there to study with Greek masters before entering public life. Molon was active in the first half of the first century BCE, with a floruit around the 80s to the 60s, and his school attracted both Roman aristocrats and Greek students. Beyond the classroom he had a public career: Cicero recalls hearing him speak at Rome when he came on an embassy from Rhodes, and later sources treat him as a figure of sufficient standing to address the Senate.

The teacher of Cicero. Cicero's own testimony is the richest source. In 79 BCE, after his first forensic successes and a period of study in Athens, he crossed to Rhodes and placed himself under Molon, whom he calls the most distinguished rhetorician of the time. In the Brutus and the De Oratore Cicero describes how Molon set about correcting him: the young orator's style was exuberant, repetitive and Asiatic, and Molon pruned it back, teaching him to restrain his cadences and aim at a firmer, more disciplined manner. Cicero treats the correction as a turning point and speaks of Molon with warmth and gratitude — a rare thing in his remarks on Greek teachers, whom he often praises with a certain condescension.

The teacher of Caesar. According to Suetonius, Caesar sailed for Rhodes in 75 BCE specifically to study under Molon; captured by pirates on the way, he famously demanded that his captors raise his ransom and promised to crucify them, a promise he kept. Suetonius' notice confirms that Molon's reputation was not confined to a single pupil: by the 70s he was the rhetorician a Roman aristocrat would cross the sea to hear. Caesar's own prose — spare, lucid, apparently artless — is often read as the fruit of exactly the discipline Molon imposed on Cicero.

Works and doctrines.

  • Molon wrote on rhetoric, and later writers cite his views on invention, arrangement and the training of the voice.
  • He is credited with a work on Homer, part of the Hellenistic industry of Homeric criticism; his judgments are quoted in the scholia.
  • He is also named by Josephus, in Against Apion, among the Greek writers who had attacked the Jews, which places him in the wider world of Hellenistic ethnographic polemic.
  • Nothing he wrote survives intact. He is known through citations, through Cicero's letters and dialogues, and through the biographical tradition.
  • His method, as Cicero describes it, was declamation followed by detailed correction — the student performed, the master criticised, and the criticism was remembered for decades.

Why he matters in this reading. Molon embodies a whole mechanism of late Republican culture: the importation of Greek expertise into Roman public life. Cicero's education was not a private hobby but a career strategy, and the men who supplied it — Molon, the philosophers of Athens, the jurists of Rome — were the intellectual infrastructure of the Senatorial elite. To name Molon is to name the Greek side of Cicero's formation, just as “Mucius Scaevola” names its Roman, legal side.

The name and its homographs. As a personal name, Molo has no plural and no generic sense; it cannot be used as a common noun. English writers of the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries usually called him Molon, and that remains the standard form in classical scholarship; Molo is an older variant, reflecting the Greek accusative and the Latin habit of dropping the final consonant in some transliterations. The same string of letters occurs in wholly unrelated words: Italian molo, “pier, jetty” (from Latin moles, “mass, structure”), the place names Molo in Kenya and Molo in Iloilo City in the Philippines, and various surnames. None of these has any connection with the Rhodian rhetorician.
