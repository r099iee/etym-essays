• height: Similar because it is the modern standard spelling and pronunciation of the same word, sharing all core senses (vertical measurement, summit, utmost degree); differs because height is the regular phonological descendant of Old English hēahþu via heihte, while highth is an analogical re-formation from high + -th that today functions only as an archaic literary variant.
  • elevation: Similar because it denotes vertical distance above a reference level and also figurative rank or dignity; differs because elevation is a learned Latin loanword used technically in geography, surveying, and architecture, whereas highth is a native Germanic word used in concrete and poetic senses.
  • altitude: Similar because it denotes height above a fixed reference, usually the horizon or sea level; differs because altitude is a strictly technical term for celestial or aerial measurement, while highth is a general word for measurement, summit, and degree.
  • summit: Similar because both name the topmost point of a mountain or object; differs because summit is restricted to the physical apex (or, metaphorically, the apex of a meeting of leaders) and does not mean "the degree of being high" the way highth does.
  • peak: Similar because both denote the pointed or maximum top of a hill and, by metaphor, the maximum of an abstract scale ("the peak of a career"); differs because peak suggests a sharp or distinctive pointed top and is also a verb ("to peak"), while highth is a nominal only, and it retains the sense of a measurement that peak lacks.
  • pinnacle: Similar because both name the topmost point of a structure or of excellence; differs because pinnacle originally means a small turret or spire on a building and carries a strongly architectural, often ornate image, whereas highth is a plain abstract noun of measure.
  • acme: Similar because both name the culminating or most perfect point of a development or career; differs because acme is a Greek loanword used only of an abstract peak of perfection, not of physical vertical distance or a measurable summit.
  • climax: Similar because both can name the highest or most intense point of a process ("the climax of the story"); differs because climax is strongly sequential and rhetorical (the peak of a rising action), while highth is primarily spatial and only secondarily temporal.
  • zenith: Similar because both denote the highest point, literal or figurative ("the zenith of his power"); differs because zenith comes from Arabic and originally means the point of the sky directly overhead, giving it an astronomical and rather rhetorical flavor.
  • top: Similar because both name the highest part of an object; differs because top is the everyday, all-purpose word for the upper end of anything, with no sense of measurement or degree, whereas highth implies a scale of highness and its abstract maximum.
