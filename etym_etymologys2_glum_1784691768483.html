Origin ultimately UNKNOWN / DEBATED — no secure PIE or Germanic etymon is attested before Middle English; the earliest securely attested English form is the Middle English verb gloumen (see DEBATE below) → Middle English gloumen "to become dark" (recorded c. 1300; the verbal stem gloum- carries the sense of darkening or lowering) → Middle English gloumben / glommen "to look gloomy or sullen" (late 14th c.; the semantic shift from darkness of weather to darkness of countenance) → English glum (noun) "a sullen look" (attested 1520s) → English glum (adjective) "sullen, moody, frowning" (attested c. 1540) → Modern English glum "sullen, morose, dejected, silently gloomy" (with the derived forms glumly, glumness; the colloquial extension the glumps "a fit of sulkiness" from glump, 18th c.; and the Scottish variant glunch, 1719). DEBATE / NUANCE: The deeper origin is disputed. Closely similar words appear across the North Sea — Low German glum "gloomy, troubled, turbid", Dutch gluren "to leer", Norwegian dialectal glome "to stare somberly" — and it is impossible to say with certainty whether Middle English borrowed from the Continent or formed the word independently from a native or Scandinavian source. Some scholars have linked the family to Old English glōm "twilight" (source of English gloaming), but the OED and Etymonline treat that connection as uncertain or reject it. The chain above therefore begins at the first attested English form and marks the pre-English stage as unknown rather than inventing one.
