Cramp is a word of Germanic stock that travelled into English through French. The English noun and verb are first recorded in the 14th century, borrowed from Old French crampe, which meant both a "painful muscular contraction" and a "bent iron hook or clasp". Behind the French word stands the Frankish *krampa — again, as with tarnish, a Germanic contribution to Gallo-Romance — and behind that the Proto-Germanic noun *krampō and adjective *krampaz, "bent, crooked", a formation on the root *kremp- / *kramp-, itself an extension of the Indo-European base *gerbh- (also given as *grembh-), "to bend, twist, be crooked". The same family yields German Krampf (spasm, cramp) and Krampe (staple, clamp), Dutch kramp, Old High German kramph (bent, crooked) and krampf (hook, cramp), and Old Norse krappr (narrow, straitened) — a whole Germanic cluster in which the idea of bending and pressing together generates both the painful spasm and the iron tool.

Phonetic and morphological career. The English word has been remarkably stable in form since the 14th century: Middle English crampe, modern cramp. The participial adjective cramped is formed on the verb and is attested from the 16th century onward; the verb itself is a denominal formation, "to cramp" meaning originally "to contract, compress, or bend into a cramped shape", and then "to afflict with cramp". The early English spelling crampe, with a final -e, reflects the French source; the present spelling with -mp has been standard since the 15th century. The modern adjective can be compared with its close relative crampon (a climbing spike), which comes through French from the same Frankish root, and with the verb cram, "to stuff tightly", which descends from Old English crammian — the two words are not direct relatives in English but belong to the same broad Germanic family of pressing and squeezing.

Semantic development. The history of cramped is a clear three-stage expansion. (1) Bodily: the earliest senses concern the muscle cramp — a limb that is "cramped" is stiff, contracted, and painful, whether from cold, exhaustion, swimming, or sitting too long; this is the sense Tolkien uses when the sodden dwarves can hardly move after the barrel-ride down the river. (2) Spatial: by the 15th–16th centuries the adjective transfers from the cramping limb to the space that cramps it — a room, house, seat, or passage so tight that the body is pressed and bent as if by a cramp; the transfer is natural because the same root means "bent, crooked", so a cramped space is one that literally bends and confines the person within it. (3) Figurative: in the 17th–18th centuries the restriction becomes mental and social — a "cramped education", "cramped circumstances", a "cramped imagination"; the sense of being boxed-in and unable to develop extends from the body to the mind and the life. The idiom "to cramp someone’s style", meaning to inhibit a person from acting freely and naturally, is a 20th-century fossil of this spatial metaphor.

Socio-cultural context and register. In the 19th century, as industrial cities crowded workers into tiny lodgings, "cramped" became a key word of social criticism: the "cramped dwellings" of the poor were denounced by reformers, and the word carried the moral weight of unhealthy, stunting confinement. In the 20th century it moved into everyday domestic language — the "cramped flat", the "cramped office", the "cramped car" — while keeping a mild but persistent tone of discomfort and oppression. Writers of adventure and survival literature, of which The Hobbit is an exemplar, use the bodily sense for physical ordeal: soaked, bruised, and cramped, the dwarves are reduced to a state of almost infantile helplessness. The word’s long career thus runs from the muscle spasm of medieval medicine, through the architecture of the industrial slum, to the modern idiom of the inhibited person, all through the single image of being bent, pressed, and held too tightly.
