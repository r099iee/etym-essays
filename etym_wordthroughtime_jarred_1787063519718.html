- 1500s: to make a harsh, grating, discordant noise - “to jar” is first a sound-word, imitative of rough scraping
- 1600s: the sound becomes a vibration - transitive “to jar” means to shake, jolt, or set vibrating by a hard impact
- 1600s: the jolt turns figurative - jarring opinions, jarring natures; “to jar” = to clash, quarrel, or be in discord
- 1800s: the aesthetic sense flourishes - jarring colours, a jarring note; any harsh, unpleasing clash of style or mood
- 1900s–present: “jarred” is both bodily and emotional - a fall jarred his spine; the news jarred her awake; the building jarred with its setting
- Today the word keeps its double life: the physical jolt in the bones and the grating, discordant clash on the senses, one single image of harsh contact
