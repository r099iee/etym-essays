- Late 14th c. — snar growls first: Middle English has the short growl-verb for an angry animal sound, already imitative, already hostile.
- 1590s — the frequentative snarl arrives: with its -le ending it names the repeated, sustained growl rather than a single snap, and writers seize on it for quarrelsome humans as well as dogs.
- 17th c. — from throat to face: snarling spreads from the sound to the look — snarling lips, snarling visages — and to the tone of scolding voices in pamphlets and plays.
- 18th–19th c. — the novelist's adjective: snarling villains, snarling curs, and snarling winds fill Gothic and Victorian pages, always the audible signature of ill temper.
- 20th c. — headlines adopt it: snarling traffic disputes, snarling rows, snarling witnesses — the participle becomes journalistic shorthand for any sustained hostility.
- Today — still bared teeth: from guard dogs to comment sections, snarling marks hostility that keeps sounding, paired in the source with snivelling as misery's aggressive pole.
