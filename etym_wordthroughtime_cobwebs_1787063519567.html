- 14th c. - Coined in Middle English as coppeweb / cobweb, combining coppe (spider, from attercop) with web.
- 16th c. - Popularized in Elizabethan drama (Shakespeare), celebrated both as an atmospheric detail of ruined dwellings and as a folkloric styptic to bind cuts and wounds.
- 17th-18th c. - Expanded into moral and legal satire, describing obsolete laws, pedantic philosophies, and neglected institutions.
- 19th c. - Idiomatic phrases of mental refreshment crystallized ("brush the cobwebs from one's brain") across Victorian novels.
- 20th-21st c. - Adopted into macroeconomic theory (the Cobweb Model of cyclical price fluctuations) and computing/web culture as a metaphor for neglected digital archives.
