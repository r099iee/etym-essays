Derivation chain for ROOT 1: GAILY 1. Frankish *gāhi — “swift, sudden, impetuous” (reconstructed; Frankish of the 5th–9th centuries). The Germanic ancestry is debated: cognates (Old High German gāhi “quick, sudden”) suggest a Germanic source, but the word’s appearance in Romance as a word of joy and colour is unusual, and some scholars have proposed a Celtic or otherwise non-Germanic origin. 2. Old Provençal gai — “joyful, merry, brightly coloured” (attested 11th–12th century; the Romance adjective developed from the Frankish loan). 3. Old French gai — “joyful, happy, merry; splendid, bright” (12th century; borrowed into Middle English as gay). 4. Middle English gay — “joyful, light-hearted, splendid, brightly coloured” (borrowed 12th–13th century). 5. Middle English gaili / gaily — adverb “cheerfully, brightly, showily” (formed with the adverbial suffix -ly; attested from the 14th century). 6. Modern English gaily — “in a bright, cheerful, showy manner” (16th century onward; the modern sense of the adverb, with the colour sense dominant). Final node: GAILY. Derivation chain for ROOT 2: DRESSED 1. Proto-Indo-European *reg- — “to move in a straight line; to direct, rule, set straight” (reconstructed; the source of Latin regere). 2. Latin regere — “to rule, direct, guide, keep straight” (attested from the 3rd century BCE). 3. Latin dirigere — “to set straight, arrange, direct” (dis- “apart” + regere; attested from the 1st century BCE). 4. Latin directus — “straight, arranged, directed” (past participle of dirigere; classical). 5. Medieval Latin / Vulgar Latin directiare — “to direct, set straight, arrange” (reconstructed Romance formation). 6. Old French drecier — “to set up, arrange, direct; to dress” (attested 10th–12th century; the verb acquired the meaning “to prepare, put in order”). 7. Middle English dressen — “to set straight, arrange, prepare; (by the 14th century) to clothe, attire” (borrowed from Old French in the 13th century). 8. English dressed — “clothed, attired” (past participle, 14th century onward). Final node: DRESSED. Compound formation: GAILY + DRESSED — the two words were joined by hyphenation as a compound attributive adjective (a gaily-dressed X), attested in English from the 17th–18th century. Final compound node: GAILY-DRESSED. Note on the debate: only the Frankish ancestor of gaily is seriously debated (Germanic vs. other origin); the chain for dressed is secure throughout. Note also the modern sense-collision: since the 20th century the word gay has shifted to denote homosexuality, so the historical chain above describes the sense state required by this card’s period context.
