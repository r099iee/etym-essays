The ancient legend. The Pygmies (Greek Πυγμαῖοι, Pygmaîoi) were a fabled race of tiny men who enter literature in Homer: in the Iliad (3.3–6) the clamour of the Trojan host is compared to the cries of cranes fleeing winter rain, cranes that “bring slaughter and fate to the Pygmy men”. On this single simile Greek imagination built an entire ethnography: the pygmies, a dwarf people at the world’s southern edge (by the Nile’s sources, or in India, or in some accounts Thrace), were locked in an endless annual war with the cranes, riding to battle on rams and goats, armed with axes; the crane-fight (the geranomachía) was a favourite scene in Greek art. Aristotle (History of Animals 8.12) records the pygmy-crane war as sober fact of the Upper Nile; Herodotus (2.32) reports short-statured marsh-dwellers; Ctesias of Cnidus (5th c. BCE) described Indian “three-span” men. The name itself was built on size: pygmies were said to be one πυγμή (pygmē) tall — a fist’s length, the measure from elbow to knuckles, about 13 inches.

  • The modern peoples. From the later 19th century “Pygmy” (often capitalised) was applied to the real short-statured hunter-gatherer peoples of the central African rainforest — the Mbuti, Bambuti, Aka, Baka and Twa of the Congo Basin — and by some writers to short-statured peoples of Southeast Asia and Oceania (the Negritos, the Aeta of the Philippines). Adult stature typically averages roughly 137–150 cm, and the “pygmy phenotype” — small adult size with normal infancy — is studied as a likely adaptation to tropical forest life, linked to growth-hormone and insulin-like growth factor insensitivity. Modern anthropologists increasingly prefer the peoples’ own names (Baka, Mbuti, Aka), and “Pygmy” now functions as a loose umbrella term, sometimes carrying the flavour of the colonial catalogue.
  • Generalised and taxonomic senses. As an adjective “pygmy” means “of dwarfed size, the smallest of its kind”: the pygmy hippopotamus, pygmy marmoset, pygmy owl, pygmy shrew, pygmy elephant, pygmy sperm whale and pygmy goat all bear the name because they are the miniature members of their groups — zoology borrowed the mythic race to label biology’s smallest. Applied to people, “a pygmy” can mean a very small person (careful usage now prefers “little person”); applied figuratively, it means a person of small importance, competence or soul — “an intellectual pygmy” — a deliberately belittling metaphor.
  • Spelling. Both “pygmy” and “pigmy” have existed since the word reached English; “pigmy” dominated until the 19th century, when classicising usage restored the -y- spelling that reflects the Greek upsilon (πυγμή), and “pygmy” became standard. The plural is “pygmies”.
  • In Wilde’s story. “The pygmies who sail over a big lake on large flat leaves, and are always at war with the butterflies” recasts the crane-war motif as pure fairy tale: Pliny (Natural History 7.26–27) passed on the pygmies’ war with cranes; the butterflies are the Swallow’s storytelling flourish, shrinking a martial race to an insect-scale comedy — the ancient etymology of “one fist tall” made literal in a story about the size of wonder.

The semantic bridge. One pivot — size — turns the word through its whole career: fabled fist-high warriors (Homer) → legendary Africans (Aristotle, Pliny) → real short-statured peoples (19th-century explorers) → biological miniature species (zoology) → contemptuous epithet for the little-minded. “Pygmies” is simultaneously the oldest ethnonym in European literature and a living insult with a disputed capital letter.
