Shove is a blunt, physical verb meaning to push roughly, abruptly, or with the whole body — and in the imperative "Shove off!" it is a rude command to leave, a dismissal that began as a sailor's order to push a boat away from the shore. In the source text, the single cry "Shove off!" is the rowers' signal to push the barrels (and themselves) out into the current — the word sits at the exact point where physical pushing becomes social expulsion.

PRIMARY SENSE — ROUGH PHYSICAL PUSHING

  • To push something (or someone) with force and little ceremony: "he shoved the door open with his shoulder", "she shoved the papers across the desk".
  • The push is abrupt, direct, and often impatient or hostile: you shove when you do not care to be gentle.
  • Noun use: "give it a shove", "one last shove and it moved" — a single, decisive, effortful push.
  • Body parts do the work: hands, shoulders, elbows — "he shoved his hands deep into his pockets" (to thrust), "they shoved their way through the crowd".

NAUTICAL ORIGIN — SHOVING OFF FROM SHORE

  • "To shove off": to push a boat away from the bank, quay, or shore so that it can float free and be rowed or sailed away — the first act of departure by water.
  • From the 16th century this phrase crossed from boats to people: "shove off!" = "go away! leave!", with the impatience of a boatman tired of waiting.
  • The imperative keeps its rough, salty register: it is what you say to someone you are done with, not a polite goodbye.

SECONDARY SENSES — SOCIAL AND SPATIAL PUSHING

  • "Shove up / shove over / shove along": to make room by moving sideways — "shove up, there's room for one more" — the physical push becomes a small social negotiation.
  • "Shove it" (vulgar): an aggressive dismissal of an offer or demand — "he told them to shove it" — the unwelcome thing itself is the object of the shove.
  • "Push and shove": the jostling of a crowd — "there was a lot of push and shove at the gates" — the noun phrase for undignified physical competition.
  • "If push comes to shove" (20th c., US): "if matters are forced to the point of action" — the idiom takes the moment when polite pressure turns into physical force as the type of a last resort.

SLANG AND GAMES

  • "Shove-ha'penny" (19th c.): a pub game in which coins are shoved along a marked board — the word's only game sense, now the emblem of a certain kind of English pub nostalgia.
  • "Shove it down someone's throat": to force an opinion or product on someone — the push made violent and oral.
  • Australian/UK slang "shove off" remains current as a curt dismissal; "shove" is a favourite of football commentary ("shoved it over the line").

THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE

  • Every sense of shove is the same physical gesture viewed at different scales: the body pushes something away — a door, a boat, a person, an offer, an opinion.
  • The key step is "shove off": originally an instruction about a boat (push it away from the land), it became an instruction about a person (push yourself away from me) — the transfer from water to social space gives the word its characteristic rudeness, because the boating context is impatient and functional.
  • Register: shove is the least polite of the push-verbs; it is quicker than push, cruder than nudge, less forceful than ram, and more contemptuous than thrust.

REGISTER AND STYLE

  • Shove is short, blunt, Anglo-Saxon, and oral; it belongs to speech, sport, narrative action, and slang far more than to formal prose.
  • It is at home in adventure fiction (Tolkien's dwarves and elves shove barrels), in working-class dialogue, in pub idiom, and in the vocabulary of crowds and queues.
  • Its most famous literary uses are precisely where physical effort and impatience meet: the shove that starts a boat, clears a crowd, or ends an argument.
