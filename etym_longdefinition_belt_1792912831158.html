Belt is, in its most familiar sense, a narrow band of leather, cloth, or synthetic material worn round the waist to hold up clothing or to carry tools, weapons, and ornaments, fastened with a buckle or clasp.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses a landscape sense: a belt of trees, meaning a long narrow strip or band of woodland cutting across open country. The travellers come again to such a plantation-like band of tall oaks with elm and ash, a ribbon of timber in the wider farmland, and the word carries exactly this geographical meaning of an elongated zone distinct from what surrounds it.

Core meaning in detail: The clothing belt is a strip whose length greatly exceeds its width, encircling the body at the waist or hips. From Roman soldiers sword-belts to modern fashion belts, seat belts, and tool belts, the form is constant: flexible band plus fastening. The landscape and technical senses keep the same geometry: a thing longer than it is wide that binds, joins, or marks a zone, whether a belt of trees, a belt of rain, or a conveyor belt carrying goods.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Waistband fastening (primary clothing sense, from Old English): the strap worn round the waist; black belt, white belt, seat belt, safety belt, tool belt. The bridge is direct inheritance: Latin balteus, a soldier sword-belt, became the general waistband of every later century.
  • A long narrow strip of land or vegetation (source sense): a belt of trees, the corn belt, the green belt round a city, the Bible belt. The bridge is shape metaphor: a region that, seen on a map, lies like a strap or band across the country, binding it or marking it off.
  • An endless mechanised loop (industrial, 18th–19th c.): conveyor belt, fan belt, assembly line belt. The bridge is functional: the loop encircles pulleys exactly as a waistband encircles a waist, and it carries things along as a baldric once carried a sword.
  • A zone of sky, sea, or climate (geographical): the asteroid belt, the doldrums belt, temperate belts. The bridge extends the map-strip image upward and outward: astronomers and geographers saw rings and bands girdling planets and globes, and named them belts.
  • Rank or achievement grades (martial arts, 20th c.): black belt, brown belt. The bridge runs through the coloured waist-sash of judo and karate: the belt stopped merely holding clothes and began signalling mastery, so that the object now names the rank itself.
  • A blow or heavy gulp (verb and noun, colloquial): to belt someone, to belt out a song, to belt down a drink, a belt of whisky. The bridge runs from the belt as a strap used for striking to the force of the blow itself, then to any vigorous exertion of voice or appetite delivered with the same force.
  • Below the belt and tighten your belt (idioms): unfair blows from boxing rules where striking below the waistband is foul, and wartime thrift where a tightened belt means a narrowed waist from hunger. The bridge is the waistband as the body honour-line and hunger-line: what falls below it is foul, and what tightens it is hardship borne with resolve.

Register and connotation: belt is plain, sturdy, workmanlike English, at home in farms, factories, and fashion alike. The tree-belt is neutral descriptive prose; the industrial belt is technical; the idioms are earthy and vivid. As clothing it can signal authority (the guards belt), prowess (the black belt), or safety (fasten your seat belt). Few short words cover so much ground: the strap round a waist, the trees across a valley, the rocks between Mars and Jupiter, and the punch in a bar-room story are one geometry of binding bands.
