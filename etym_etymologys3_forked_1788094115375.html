FORKED is a word whose history is unusually transparent: it is simply the past participle of the verb to fork, which itself is the noun fork turned into a verb — and fork is one of the oldest Latin loanwords in English. The story begins with Latin furca, 'a two-pronged pitchfork, a forked pole used as a prop, a fork-shaped instrument'. Furca is one of those Latin words whose prehistory is lost: no Indo-European etymology can be reconstructed for it, and most scholars treat it as a loan from an unknown Mediterranean or Italic substrate source (a handful have pointed at Etruscan forms, without proof). It matters for English because furca entered Germanic very early — as a culture-word for an agricultural tool — and Old English took it directly as forca (attested in the 8th–9th centuries), meaning 'pitchfork, forked instrument'. It is thus a rare early Latin agricultural borrowing, of the same vintage as Old English strǣt 'street' from Latin strata and mil 'mile' from Latin milia.

  • Old English → Middle English: forca gives Middle English forke (13th century), covering the pitchfork, the hayfork, and any pronged implement; the word is reinforced from the 13th century by Anglo-French fourche/forque (from Latin furca via Old French), which is why the modern spelling fork shows the French vowel. Around 1300 the adjective forked(e) is coined — 'having the form or prongs of a fork' — first applied to tools, trees, and animals' horns.
  • 14th–16th centuries — the word goes natural: forked extends from implements to nature: a forked tree (a trunk dividing in two), a forked river, a forked tongue. The forked tongue of serpents was already proverbial in the Middle Ages, and the adjective inherits the association: to speak with a 'forked tongue' is to speak double — the word's only distinctly sinister colouring, still alive in English and, through the translation clichés of the 19th-century American frontier, in depictions of Native American oratory, though the famous phrase itself is a literary invention of that period.
  • 17th–18th centuries — the verb and the road: to fork appears in the 14th century ('to divide into branches'), but its modern career begins with roads and rivers: a road 'forks' at a junction, and the noun 'fork' comes to mean the junction or one of its branches ('the North Fork'). The table fork — the object that would make the word universal — is a late arrival: introduced to England from Italy around 1600 (Thomas Coryate's Crudities, 1611, famously describes the Italian custom), mocked for a generation as effeminate, then triumphant by the late 17th century, so that by 1800 'fork' is the most ordinary object in the language and 'forked' stays transparently connected to it.
  • 19th century — slang and the frontier: fork out / fork over / fork up ('to pay, hand over', 1830s) appears in American English — the image is of forking coins out of a purse with a fork-like motion, or of a reluctant parting; 'speak with a forked tongue' enters the literary language of the West; in science, 'forked' becomes a routine descriptor of bifurcate structures in zoology and botany (forked tails, forked antennae).
  • 20th–21st centuries — the digital fork: in 1971, Unix names its process-duplicating system call fork(), after the idea of a single process 'splitting in two like a fork in the road'; from the 2000s, version-control and open-source platforms (GitHub, 2008) adopt 'fork' for copying a codebase into an independent line of development. 'Forked' thus completes a 700-year journey: from the pitchfork of the Anglo-Saxon farm to the splitting of software projects — the same Y-shaped image of division throughout. Today 'forked' is ordinary in both worlds: a forked beard, forked lightning, a forked road, and a forked repository.

Phonetically the word is stable: Old English forca → Middle English forke → Modern English fork /fɔːk/, with forked /fɔːkt/ showing the regular participial -ed; the main change is the loss of the Old English final vowel and the regular development of Old English short o before r + consonant to modern /ɔː/. Orthographically, the spelling fork (with the French-style spelling) replaced earlier forke/forcke by the 16th century. Key literary anchors: Anglo-Saxon glosses of forca; Chaucer's fork in the Miller's Tale (c. 1387); Coryate's Crudities (1611) on the Italian table fork; Shakespeare's 'forked plague' (King Lear, c. 1605) on serpents' tongues; 19th-century frontier fiction's 'forked tongue'; and Unix's fork() (1971), which gave the oldest tool-word in the language a permanent home in the newest one.
