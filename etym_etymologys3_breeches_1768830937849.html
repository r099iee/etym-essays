breeches — The biography of this word begins with breaking. Its root core is the Proto-Indo-European verb *bhreg-, "to break, to split," which produced two great verbal families: in Italic, Latin frangere, "to break," with its many derivatives — fragment, fracture, fragile, frail, infringe, refract — and in Germanic, Proto-Germanic *brekaną, "to break," with Old English brecan and modern English break and breach. But the garment branch is a striking specialization of the same root: Proto-Germanic *brōkiz named the garment that covers the place where the body splits into two legs — breeches are, etymologically, "the split thing," the clothing of the fork. The word's plural shape is a fossil of that history: Old English brēc had the plural brēċ, the two leg-coverings taken together; Middle English, no longer recognizing brēċ as a plural, added the regular plural -es and produced breches, so that modern breeches is a double plural — the legs, so to speak, twice over. The Celtic and Roman world had their own version of the same word or of a close relative: Gaulish *bracā was borrowed into Latin as bracae — Roman writers treated the Gallic trouser as an exotic, barbarian garment, since Rome itself dressed in tunics and togas, and the loan's Romance descendants (Italian brache, Old French braies) kept the word for the divided leg-garment. Whether the Celtic and Germanic forms are independent creations from the same "split" root or one borrowed from the other in the Iron Age is debated; the family resemblance is real, but the two lines are best treated as parallel. Meanwhile the Germanic line runs Old Norse brók, Dutch broek ("trousers" to this day), and Old English brēc. In English, breeches designated the knee-length garment worn by men from the sixteenth to the early nineteenth century — the mark of the gentleman, the garment of the master of the house, so that "to wear the breeches" became the idiom for a wife who rules the household; the dialectal britches (eighteenth century) survives colloquially; and the reanalyzed singular breech went its own technical way into anatomy (breech-birth) and firearms (breech-loading). The garment's social history explains the word's connotations. In the early modern period knee-breeches were the everyday dress of respectable men, and with the rise of long trousers in the early nineteenth century the breeches became a marker of the old order — conservative, formal, and tied to riding and ceremony. "To wear the breeches" and "too big for his breeches" keep the word alive in idiom even after the garment left everyday wear, and the technical singular breech (the rear of a firearm, the position of a bottom-first baby) shows how the word split into specialized channels. In the passage, the rider wears "loose white breeches to the knee" — the garment has returned to its original habitat, the riding costume, for breeches were always the horseman's clothing: the very split that named them is what allows a leg on each side of the saddle, and the loose white cloth flapping about the rider's knees is the ancient garment doing its ancient job. Key developments:
  • Sound and form: PIE *bhreg- "to break" → Proto-Germanic *brōkiz "the split garment" → Old English brēc → Middle English brech/breches (re-pluralized) → modern breeches; the double plural fossilizes the two legs.
  • Semantic drift: "a breaking/split" → "the garment covering the split" → "knee-length leg-garment" → (singular breech) "the hind part, the rear of a gun."
  • The debate: whether the Germanic and Celtic garment words are parallel creations from the same root or reflect early borrowing remains an open question; the English word's descent is Germanic.
  • Social history: breeches were the mark of the master, the gentleman, and the horseman; long trousers displaced them in the 19th century, and the idioms preserved them.
  • Literary attestations: from the Robin Hood ballads ("the good yeoman's breeches") through Restoration comedy, Dickens, and modern adventure fiction, breeches is the word for the riding garment of those who sit astride.
