BLASTED is the past tense and past participle of the verb to blast, and a versatile adjective in its own right. Its meanings radiate from the noun blast, "a strong gust of wind or a violent current of air", and the verb "to blow violently". From this root grow three great branches: (1) the physical sense of being struck by wind, explosion, or force; (2) the agricultural and poetic sense of being withered or blighted, as if by a malevolent wind; and (3) the colloquial, exclamatory sense of being damned or cursed.

In The Hobbit, "all was befouled and blasted with the comings and goings of the monster": the halls of the Lonely Mountain are not only soiled but blasted — scorched, shattered, and laid waste by Smaug's fiery breath and the violence of his passage. Tolkien's blasted here is the strong, physical sense of ruin by fire and force, evoking a landscape blasted by dragon-fire.

Core meaning: Struck by a violent gust, explosion, or heat; withered, blighted, or ruined; colloquially, damned or cursed.

  • Primary sense (wind and explosion): Subjected to a violent current of air or to the shock-wave of an explosion; blown up, shattered, or pulverized by force. For example, "the rocks were blasted to rubble by the quarrymen". This is the dominant modern sense.
  • Sense of searing and blight (agriculture and nature): Withered, scorched, or destroyed by blight, drought, frost, or a "blasting" wind — a destructive dry heat that kills plants. A blasted heath is a barren, withered landscape. This sense, older than gunpowder, survives strongly in poetic and fantasy usage.
  • Sense of ruin and devastation: Laid waste, ravaged, desolated — applied to regions, buildings, hopes, and prospects. "Blasted hopes" or a "blasted career" are ruined irreparably.
  • Colloquial oath sense (damned): As a mild imprecation, blast means "damn", and blasted means "damned, confounded, cursed". For example, "that blasted door is jammed again!" This is the same family that gives the interjection "blast it!" and the intensifying "you blasted fool".
  • Slang and emphatic senses: In 19th-20th-century slang, "blasted" could intensify annoyance (a blasted nuisance) or mean extremely drunk ("he was blasted"); and the phrase "to have a blast" flipped the noun into an idiom of great enjoyment.

Connotations and register: The word is remarkably flexible across registers. In engineering and mining it is technical (blasted rock, blasted tunnel). In nature writing it is poetic and grim (blasted heaths, blasted trees). In everyday speech it is a colourful, slightly old-fashioned mild swear-word. Tolkien's usage blends the searing and the devastating senses, and the word sits naturally in the elevated, epic diction of his prose.

Grammatical notes: A regular weak verb; blasted functions as past tense, past participle, and attributive adjective ("a blasted tree", "the blasted lands"). The noun blast (a gust, an explosion, a loud sound, a strong start — "having a blast") is its source. The adjective is almost always negative except in the modern idiom of celebration.

Related semantic fields: blight, scorch, wither, ruin, devastate, ravage, explode, demolish, curse, damn.

In the context of the card: "befouled and blasted" — blasted completes the portrait of the dragon's work: filth (befouled) plus fire and force (blasted), the two together reducing the proud dwarf-halls to a desolation. The word foreshadows the "Blasted Lands" that became a fixture of later fantasy geography, where a blasted waste is a landscape ruined by fire, war, or sorcery.
