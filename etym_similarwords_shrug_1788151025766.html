• Shrug off — dismiss: similar because both mean to treat something as unimportant; differs because "dismiss" is the general verb of refusal to consider, while "shrug off" keeps the bodily image — a casual shoulder movement — and is warmer, more dismissive-by-default than deliberately reasoned dismissal.
  • Shrug off — brush off: similar because both mean to deflect criticism or rejection with casual ease; differs because "brush off" (like brushing dust off one's clothes) is slightly more deliberate and even insulting to the thing brushed, while "shrug off" is more about unconcern than contempt.
  • Shrug off — pooh-pooh: similar because both mean to dismiss as trivial; differs because "pooh-pooh" is overtly contemptuous and a little comic (redolent of the exclamation "pooh!"), while "shrug off" need not despise what it dismisses — it may simply not care.
  • Shrug off — disregard / ignore: similar because both mean to pay no attention; differs because "ignore" is passive and may be principled, whereas "shrug off" implies that the thing was felt — it pressed — and was then shed like a garment.
  • Shrug off — slough off: similar because both mean to cast off something clinging; differs because "slough off" (of a snake's skin, a habit) suggests something dead or obstructing being shed, whereas "shrug off" is lighter — the thing is unimportant, not poisonous.
  • Shrug (gesture) — raise the shoulders: similar because both describe the same movement; differs because "raise the shoulders" is the neutral anatomical description, while "shrug" is the cultural word that already encodes the meaning — indifference, ignorance, helplessness.
  • Shrug (garment) — bolero: similar because both are short jackets, often sleeveless or short-sleeved, worn over other clothing; differs because "bolero" is the Spanish dancer's short jacket with a front closure and distinctive ornament, while "shrug" is a loosely fitted, open, shoulder-hugging capelet with no fastening tradition.
  • Shrug (garment) — capelet: similar because both are short shoulder coverings; differs because a "capelet" hangs from the shoulders as a miniature cape, while a "shrug" is cut to hug and follow the arms and is typically of knit or fitted fabric.
  • Shudder / shiver (older sense): similar because the medieval "shrug" meant exactly this — a convulsive movement of the body; differs in that "shudder" and "shiver" survive today as involuntary reactions to cold or horror, whereas "shrug" has shifted wholly to the voluntary gesture of the shoulders.
  • Shake off: similar because both mean to rid oneself of attackers or ailments; differs because "shake off" (a pursuer, a cold) is more vigorous and physical, while "shrug off" is the suave, shoulders-only version.
