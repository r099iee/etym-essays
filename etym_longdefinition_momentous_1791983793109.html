momentous is an adjective meaning “of great importance or consequence”. It is applied to events, decisions, discoveries, meetings and occasions that are felt to change the direction of what follows, and it almost always carries a tone of gravity: a momentous event is not merely interesting or useful but weighty in its effects. In the source sentence — “During the period when Cicero was growing up in Rome, momentous historic events were occurring” — the word describes the collapse of the Roman Republic's political order: the civil wars of Marius and Sulla, which the passage goes on to call the handwriting on the wall for Senatorial government.

The semantic bridge: “of moment”. The adjective is a transparent English formation from the noun moment plus -ous, and it preserves an older sense of moment that most speakers no longer consciously command: importance, weight, consequence. That sense is still alive in the fixed phrases a matter of moment, of great moment, of no moment, and in the negative unmomentous. The bridge is Latin. Momentum meant “movement” — the noun of movēre, “to move” — but it also meant “weight”, because the pointer of a balance moves as the weight shifts, and so “the momentum of a thing” came to mean the pull it exerts, its importance in the scales of judgement. English borrowed the noun twice: as moment (a point of time, and also importance) and as the learned doublet momentum (moving force, later also the product of mass and velocity). Momentous is the adjective of the first, built on the “weight” sense: momentous = “full of moment”, that is, heavy with consequence.

The main senses.

  • Historically decisive: “a momentous year”, “the momentous events of 1989”, “a momentous shift in the balance of power”. The word points to large public consequences and is at home in history, politics and journalism.
  • Grave in prospect: “a momentous decision”, “a momentous choice about the future”. Here the emphasis falls on the burden of deciding, not on the outcome already known.
  • Ceremonial or institutional: “a momentous occasion”, “the momentous first meeting of the assembly”. This is the weakened, often semi-formal use: the event matters because it is marked and remembered, even if its consequences are modest.
  • Deliberate inflation or irony: because the word is grand, it is often used for small things with a wink — “a momentous decision about lunch”. The irony depends on the reader recognising the mismatch of scale.
  • Obsolete and rare: in the seventeenth century the adjective could still be felt as “of or belonging to a moment”, that is, momentary; that sense has been entirely lost, and the surviving word is the one meaning “important”.

Grammar and degree. The adjective is gradable: events can be more momentous or the most momentous, and the adverb momentously and the noun momentousness are both current, though the noun is clumsy and mostly academic. It is used attributively far more often than predicatively: a momentous event is natural, the event was momentous is possible but slightly literary.

Register and collocation. Momentous belongs to elevated written English — historiography, editorial prose, speeches, obituaries, commemorative journalism. Its regular partners are event, occasion, decision, year, day, change, shift, consequences, implications, undertaking and news. The tone is serious and slightly public; applied to private life it can sound pompous. It is stronger than important, more neutral than historic, and less ominous than fateful.

A common confusion. Momentous is constantly confused with momentary, “lasting only a moment”, because both derive from moment in different senses. The pair is a genuine trap: a momentary lapse is brief, a momentous lapse is disastrous. The distinction is worth fixing, since the two words sit close together on the page and have almost opposite implications.

What the word contributes to this reading. The passage is explaining why Cicero's generation produced such extraordinary oratory: rhetoric, it argues, thrives when a free society is under threat. Momentous does quiet work in that argument. It frames the Marian–Sullan contest not as one more factional quarrel but as a turning point in the history of the Republic, and so prepares the reader for the claim that Cicero was lucky — or doomed — to practise his art at the hinge of an era.
