The biography of “etching” is the story of how eating became engraving. It begins with one of the most ancient and widely attested words in all of Indo-European: the root *h₁ed-, “to eat,” which survives in Sanskrit átti (“he eats”), in Greek édō (“I eat”), in Latin edō, and in the Germanic *etaną that produced Old English etan, our eat, along with German essen, Dutch eten, Swedish äta, and Gothic itan. Tellingly, the same root also produced the word for tooth in nearly every branch — Greek odoús, Latin dēns, Sanskrit dánt, Germanic tanþs, our tooth — because a tooth is literally “the eater.” From this root Latin built a small family of civilized derivatives: edibilis gave edible; comedere, “to eat up,” gave comestible; and ēsuriēns, “hungering,” gave the rare esurient. None of these, however, points toward the printmaker’s workshop; that path belongs to the Germanic branch and to a single ingenious grammatical device, the causative. The decisive step was taken on the Germanic side. Proto-Germanic added a causative suffix to the verb “to eat,” producing *atjaną, “to cause to eat, to feed.” Old High German kept the feeding sense in ezzen, but in Middle High German the verb etzen began to take a sharper, technical turn: it came to mean “to cause to eat away,” to corrode, to let something gnaw into a surface. This is the seed of the modern word. In German the verb ätzen — “to cause acid to eat into metal, to corrode, to etch” — became the standard term of the printmaker’s workshop, together with its compounds Ätzung (“etching, corrosion”) and Ätzwasser (“acid,” literally “eating-water”). From German the verb passed into Middle Dutch as etsen, and the Netherlands, the great center of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century printmaking, transmitted it to English. English took up etch around the 1630s, in the context of the Flemish and Dutch engraving workshops that dominated European printmaking, and the noun etching followed in the seventeenth century. The word entered the language as a technical term of a new art, and from the first it carried the ancient image sealed into its root: the acid eats its way into the copper, and the print is the record of that eating. The linguistic geography matters here as much as the phonology — the word did not arrive through the literary Latin that furnished most of English’s learned vocabulary, but through the workshops and trading cities of the Low Countries, where the technique itself had been perfected and where the English went to learn it. The technique that the word names deserves a moment of its own, for its history explains the word’s social and artistic associations. In etching, the artist covers a copper plate with a thin, acid-resistant ground of wax or varnish, draws through the ground with a steel needle to expose the metal, and then immerses the plate in acid, which bites the exposed lines into the surface. The needle is freed from the muscular resistance of engraving — the artist draws as fluently as with a pen — and the bitten line, with its irregular, grainy edges and subtle variations of depth, gives etching its characteristic wiry, spontaneous, sketch-like quality. This combination of fluency and nuance made etching the chosen medium of the great draftsmen: Rembrandt above all, but also the printmakers of the eighteenth century, and among them Giovanni Battista Piranesi, whose Vedute di Roma — vast, visionary, wildly dramatic views of the ruined monuments of ancient Rome — are among the supreme achievements of the medium and precisely the etchings that illustrate the Gibbon edition named in this card. The eighteenth century was the golden age of the word as well as of the art. Piranesi’s enormous plates, with their plunging perspectives, their flickering light, and their obsessive rendering of the stones of antiquity, made etching the medium of romantic ruin and historical sublimity; a century later, the Etching Revival of the 1860s — led by Rembrandt’s rediscovered prints and the atmospheric plates of Whistler and the French etchers — made the word a byword for artistic spontaneity and prestige. Meanwhile the technique and the word kept expanding beyond the artist’s studio into industry: from the nineteenth century, acid etching was used to decorate glass and metal and to make printing plates for illustration; in the twentieth century it became a manufacturing process, biting circuits into silicon wafers and designs into glass and steel, so that the modern technical senses of “etch” and “etching” are everywhere in electronics and fabrication. The socio-cultural dynamics of the word’s career can be summarized:
  • The etymological core: acid “eats” the metal; the word etching preserves, after thousands of years, the primitive image of eating and corrosion.
  • The workshop transmission: the word travelled from German ätzen through Middle Dutch etsen into seventeenth-century English along the trade routes of the Low Countries, home of the period’s finest printmakers.
  • The artistic ascendancy: the medium’s fluency drew the greatest draftsmen — Rembrandt, then Piranesi, then Whistler — and made etching the print technique of choice for spontaneity and expressiveness.
  • The antiquarian halo: Piranesi’s etchings of Roman ruins linked the word forever to the romantic contemplation of decay — the very mood of Gibbon’s history, which the illustrated Modern Library edition sets before the reader.
  • The industrial afterlife: the same acid-biting process now etches glass, steel, and silicon circuits, extending the old printmaker’s word into the vocabulary of modern manufacture.
Literary and artistic attestations anchor the trajectory. Rembrandt’s etchings of the 1640s and 1650s are the medium’s summit; Piranesi’s Carceri and Vedute di Roma (1740s–1770s) carried it into the age of ruins; the Etching Revival of the 1860s gave it a Victorian prestige; and modern catalogues, galleries, and illustrated editions — like the one that says “illustrated from the etchings by Gian Battista Piranesi” — keep the plural form alive as the precise word for a body of such prints. In the Gibbon line the word is doing all of this at once: it names the technique, the artworks, and the historical atmosphere, and its ancient root — acid eating into copper — is the quiet metaphor behind the whole romance of ruins, decay, and decline that the edition exists to evoke.
