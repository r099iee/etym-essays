pother (noun; also verb): a commotion, fuss, uproar, or state of noisy disturbance and mental confusion — and, historically, a choking cloud of dust, smoke, or steam.

1. Primary sense — a commotion, fuss, or agitation

In ordinary use, a pother is a noisy disturbance or a flurry of activity and worry, especially one out of proportion to its cause:
  • "in a pother" — flustered, agitated, in a state of fuss ("She was in a pother about the guest list");
  • "to make a pother" — to create an unnecessary fuss over something small ("Why all this pother about a simple mistake?");
  • "what a pother!" — a standard exclamation of exasperation at a disproportionate commotion.
Key features of the primary sense:
  • Disproportion: pother typically describes a fuss that exceeds its cause — the word carries a dismissive, impatient flavour.
  • Noise + confusion: it mixes audible commotion with mental fluster and worry.
  • Register: old-fashioned, faintly bookish in modern English; common in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century prose.

2. Secondary sense — a choking cloud of dust, smoke, or steam (historical and dialectal)

At its first appearance in English (c. 1590) and still in dialectal use, pother denotes a dense, choking cloud of dust, smoke, or steam — the puff of debris kicked up in a dry road, or the smoke of a smoking hearth. Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the physical choking cloud of blinding dust or smoke transferred metaphorically to a noisy, blinding cloud of social fuss and mental confusion. The commotion is to society what the dust-cloud is to the eye: something kicked up that obscures clear sight and leaves one sputtering. This image survives in the word's tone even though the physical sense itself has largely receded.

3. The verb — to harass, perplex, or bustle about

  • "to pother someone" — to harass, worry, perplex, or bewilder them (to envelop them, metaphorically, in the choking cloud);
  • "to pother about" — intransitively, to bustle about making a fuss;
  • participle "pothering" — harassing, worrying; "pothering" can also be adjectival ("pothering cares").

4. Register, grammar, and collocations

  • Uncountable noun in most uses ("a great deal of pother," "much pother"), occasionally countable ("a pother" — a particular fuss).
  • Common patterns: "in a pother," "make a pother," "what a pother," "all this pother."
  • Frequently paired with near-synonyms for cumulative effect — e.g. C. S. Lewis's "bustle and pother."
  • Do not confuse with bother (the modern everyday word); the two are similar in sound and meaning but historically distinct.

5. Polysemy summary

  • (1) Commotion, fuss, uproar, agitation — primary modern sense.
  • (2) Choking cloud of dust/smoke/steam — the original 16th-century sense, now historical and dialectal.
  • (3) Verb: to harass/perplex, or to bustle about — derived from the noun's image.
  • The unifying bridge is the dust-cloud: a pother is something kicked up that chokes, blinds, and confuses — physically at first, socially and mentally afterwards.
