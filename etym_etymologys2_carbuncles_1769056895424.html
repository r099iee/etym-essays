Chain for CARBUNCLES (single root):
1. Proto-Indo-European *ker-, “to burn, heat, fire” (reconstructed; pre-3rd millennium BCE) — the root of burning and glowing heat. Note: de Vaan (EDL) regards the deeper connection of Latin carbo as uncertain; the *ker- “to burn” assignment follows the traditional line (Pokorny, Etymonline). Some scholars compare Greek κραῦρος (kraûros) “dry, brittle” as a possible but debated reflex.
2. Latin carbo, carbōnis m., “a coal, charcoal, glowing ember” (Roman Republic onward) — from PIE *ker- “to burn”; the coal of the hearth and the charcoal of the forge.
3. Latin diminutive carbunculus, -ī m., “a little coal; a glowing red gem; a burning boil” (Roman period) — the diminutive of carbo; the “little coal” sense names (a) the red garnet because it glows like an ember, and (b) the painful skin-tumour because it burns — both attested in Pliny’s use of the gem-word.
4. Old French / Anglo-Norman carboucle / carbuncle, “a red gem; a boil” (12th–13th c.) — the Gallo-Romance conduit into English.
5. English carbuncle, “a red gem; a boil” (13th c.) — the double sense carried into English.
6. English plural carbuncles — /ˈkɑːbʌŋkəlz/, the form in the Narnia treasure list ── OUR WORD.
Note: the French form escarboucle (with prosthetic e-, from *ex-carbunculus) kept the gem sense alive on the Continent; the plain English “carbuncle” retained both the gem and the medical senses throughout.
