Summation continues a Latin family built on the idea of the highest point becoming the total. Its story runs from a Proto-Indo-European spatial adverb through Latin social and rhetorical institutions to modern mathematics, law and everyday summary.

The ultimate root is PIE *uper-, also reconstructed as *upo-, meaning over, above, up from under. This prolific particle underlies English over, above, super, superior, supreme, summit and sum. In Italic it yielded Latin super above, superus upper, and by regular superlative formation summus (for *sup-mus, with assimilation of p-m to mm) meaning highest, topmost, greatest. The Romans nominalized the feminine summa, short for summa res the highest thing or summa linea the top line in adding, to mean the sum total, the main point, the gist. Hence phrases such as summa summarum the sum of sums, summa philosophiae the crown of philosophy. From summa came the verb summare to add up, sum up, and in Late and Medieval Latin the action noun summatio, summationis, a summing up.

  • PIE spatial stage: *uper- as adverb/preposition over. Cognates include Sanskrit upari, Greek hyper, German über, English over. The semantic core is vertical superiority.
  • Latin adjectival stage: superus upper contrasted with inferus lower; summus highest contrasted with imus lowest. Social metaphor already present: summus magistratus highest magistrate, summi viri leading men.
  • Latin nominal stage: summa as accounting and rhetorical term. Shopkeepers added columns to a summa; orators closed with a summa, a recapitulation. Quintilian uses summa dicendorum for the heads of a speech.
  • Medieval Latin scholastic stage: summatio and summa as genre. Thomas Aquinas Summa Theologica is a comprehensive compendium, and university disputations ended with a summatio, a summing of arguments. Old French took somme in this compendium sense (Somme théologique) but English kept summation distinct from sum and summary.

English borrowed summation relatively late. The OED records summation in the mathematical sense from the 1760s, later than sum (13th c.), summary (15th c.) and sum up (17th c.). Early uses are in arithmetic and legal rhetoric: the summation of a series, the summation of evidence. The suffix -ation (from Latin -atio) marks it as an action or result noun parallel to formation, culmination. Phonologically, Latin summatio /sumˈmaːtioː/ with geminate mm and /tio/ > /tsio/ passed into English with stress shift and yod-coalescence as /sʌˈmeɪʃən/, losing gemination and reducing the first vowel to /ʌ/. Orthographically the double m was retained, signalling the Latin superlative.

Semantic drift in English involved specialization in three domains while retaining the general sense:

  • General summary sense: a summation is a concise recapitulation, grand total of argument. The source sentence Not to think unless we have to — there is much to be said for this as the summation of wisdom exemplifies this aphoristic use where summation means distilled essence, culmination, not arithmetic addition.
  • Mathematical sense: from 18th-century analysis, summation denotes the operation of addition of a sequence, formalized by the capital sigma Σ notation introduced by Euler. Summation formulas, summation by parts, Borel summation extend the Latin adding metaphor into calculus, divergent series and signal processing.
  • Legal-rhetorical sense: especially in American English, the summation or summing-up is the closing argument, the prosecution summation, defense summation. British English more often says closing speech or summing-up, but summation is understood. This preserves the Quintilian sense of oratorical recapitulation.
  • Physiological-acoustic sense: neural summation, spatial and temporal summation of stimuli, where repeated subthreshold inputs add to threshold. Here the Latin total metaphor is literalized in biology.

Literary attestations show the widening: 18th-century textbooks on vulgar arithmetic, 19th-century Macaulay and Carlyle using summation for historical summing-up, 20th-century law reports and mathematics papers. Unlike summary, which stresses brevity, summation stresses completeness and culmination — the total into which parts have been gathered. That nuance explains why a maxim calls something the summation of wisdom rather than the summary of wisdom: it is the crowning total, the highest point (summus) of wisdom.

Today summation coexists with sum, summary, summation sign and summa without confusion, each occupying its niche. Its evolution from over to highest to total to closing argument is a classic climb from space to quantity to discourse.
