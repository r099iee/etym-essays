- Classical Antiquity (c. 5th c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Greek sīmós ('snub-nosed') applied to satyrs and monkeys, borrowed into Latin as sīmia to name apes, celebrated for their mocking mimicry.
- Medieval Bestiary Tradition (c. 1100–1500) — Latin simia allegorized as the 'ape of God' (simia Dei), symbolizing the devil, deceitful imitation, and spiritual vanity.
- Early Modern English (c. 1600–1750) — Coined as an English adjective from Neo-Latin simiānus, appearing in learned anatomical treatises and natural history compendia.
- 19th-Century Darwinian Era (1859–1900) — Centralized in evolutionary biology and fierce theological debates following Darwin's demonstration of human genealogical descent from simian ancestors.
- Modern Biomedical Era (20th c.–Present) — Codified in primatology (infraorder Simiiformes) and medical genetics (simian crease, SIV, SV40), while historical racist usage has made human application strictly taboo.
