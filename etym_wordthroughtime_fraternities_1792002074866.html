- Classical & Medieval Latin (1st c. BCE–12th c. CE) — Frāternitās designated both natural biological brotherly affection and spiritual kinship within early Christian monastic and lay communities.
- 14th–16th c. — Borrowed into Middle English (c. 1330) as fraternite, used primarily to designate guild-like religious confraternities, trade sodalities, and charitable burial societies.
- Late 18th c. — Attained monumental political prominence during the French Revolution as fraternité, establishing universal civic brotherhood as the third pillar of democratic republicanism alongside liberty and equality.
- 19th c. — Transformed in North America into the dominant designation for collegiate Greek-letter secret societies following the founding of Phi Beta Kappa, while fraternal mutual-benefit orders (Freemasons, Odd Fellows) swept civic life.
- 20th–21st c. — Retains universal recognition across American academia as collegiate Greek Life; concurrently functions as a standard metaphor in sociological and journalistic prose for any tight-knit professional cohort (e.g., the intelligence fraternity).
