Brim is the projecting edge or rim of a hat, or the upper edge (circumference) of a cup, bowl, goblet, or similar open vessel — the finishing line that defines the mouth of the container or the shade-giving flange of the headgear. In the opening scene of The Hobbit, Tolkien writes of Gandalf's "long bushy eyebrows that stuck out further than the brim of his shady hat": here the noun is concrete millinery, the wide circumference of the wizard's hat that would normally shade his face, yet even that generous projection is outdone by the brows — a comic measurement of wizardly hair before any magic is named. The register is visual, gently caricatured, affectionate; "shady hat" and overreaching eyebrows sketch the whole figure at Bilbo's door. The context fixes the hat-sense specifically, though the word belongs to a family whose other members — the brim of a cup, the verb "to brim" — share the same logic of edges and fullness.

Core senses:

  • Hat brim — the projecting rim that encircles the crown of a hat and casts shade (e.g. "the wide brim of her straw hat"). This is the sense in the card's context.
  • Vessel brim — the upper edge of a cup, bowl, bucket, goblet, or tub; "filled to the brim" means level with that edge and about to overflow.
  • Edge of water / brink — in older and poetic English, the brim is the shore-line or brink of a body of water, a survival of the word's sea-edge history (see the etymology).
  • Verb to brim — to fill to the edge ("tears brimmed in her eyes"); brimming "full to overflowing"; brimful / brim-full ("brimful of joy").
  • Figurative fullness — "brimming with confidence", "at the brim of the event": quantity or emotion rising to an edge.

Historical bridge & semantic logic: the Middle English noun brymme meant the edge of water — the shore or brink where water meets land; from that boundary the word generalized to any upper or outer edge, then specialized twice: to the mouth of a vessel (the rim liquid rises to) and, from the later 16th century, to the projecting rim of a hat that finishes and shades the crown. The verb and adjective forms grow out of the vessel image: liquid rising to the brim becomes emotional or quantitative fullness, so that "brimming" today is usually figurative rather than literal.

Usage notes & idioms: brim appears in the fixed phrases "full to the brim", "over the brim", "brimful", and "brimming"; the verb is almost always intransitive or followed by "with" ("brimming with tears", "brimming with enthusiasm"). In millinery the brim is contrasted with the crown and the band; in containers it is the level to which a vessel may safely be filled. The adjective is written variously as brimful or brim-full, and the old compound brimstone ("burning-stone", sulphur) is unrelated to the brim-word despite the identical first syllable.

Differentiate: rim is the close general synonym for a circular edge; edge and margin are broader boundary words; peak or visor is only a forward projection of a cap, not the full circle; crown of a hat is the upper head-covering; brink is the edge of something dangerous or steep. Brim keeps its own collocations — hats and overflowing vessels. (Unrelated near-lookalike: brimstone "sulphur" is from Old English bern-stan "burning-stone", not this word.) The card preserves the singular noun exactly as the comparison uses it: one hat-brim that the eyebrows still surpass.
