1. A Germanic word with no certain Indo-European pedigree. Fleisch is peculiar to the Germanic languages. Its immediate ancestry is secure: Old High German fleisk (8th–11th c.), Middle High German fleisch or vleisch, Early New High German Fleisch, modern German Fleisch. The reconstructed Proto-West Germanic form is *flaiski, and the Proto-Germanic form is usually given as *flaiskiją. The same word lies behind Old English flǣsc (modern English flesh), Old Frisian flāsk, Old Saxon flēsk, Dutch vlees and Old Norse flesk ("pork, bacon"). The Germanic family is solid; what lies beyond it is not.

Two competing explanations are usually mentioned. The first connects the word with the PIE root *pleik- "to tear, split", attested in Lithuanian plėšti "to tear" and Latvian plēst "to tear"; on this view *flaiski originally meant "a piece torn off", hence a cut or portion of flesh. The second connects it with the Germanic root of English flay (Old English flēan), but the phonology is difficult. Many dictionaries, including Kluge and the Oxford English Dictionary, treat the pre-Germanic origin as uncertain or unknown. The safest statement is that the word is a Germanic inheritance of uncertain deeper etymology, with *pleik- as a plausible but unproven source.

2. Old High German: the earliest record. fleisk appears in Old High German glosses and religious prose from the eighth century onward, where it renders Latin caro ("flesh") in biblical and doctrinal contexts. At this stage the word covers the whole semantic range of "flesh": the living body, human kinship, the material of which animals are made, and meat as food. The word had already acquired its characteristic High German shape: Germanic *fl- remains fl-, and the cluster *-sk- is written -sc-, pronounced [sk]. The diphthong written ei continues Germanic *ai; it is not the later New High German diphthongization of long ī.

3. Middle High German: courtly and mystical flesh. In Middle High German the word is written fleisch or vleisch, the initial v being a spelling variant of f. It is common in courtly literature, in religious prose and in the emerging urban records of markets and butchers. The formula vleisch und bluot ("flesh and blood") is already established, and mystical writers use vleisch for the bodily nature that the soul must discipline or transcend. The cluster -sc- develops into the modern -sch [ʃ], giving the characteristic German sound shape of the word.

4. Reformation and Early New High German: the theological peak. Luther's Bible gave the word one of its most famous German sentences: Und das Wort ward Fleisch und wohnte unter uns ("And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us", John 1:14). The incarnational sense, already patristic and medieval, became a fixed point of German religious language, and Fleischwerdung ("incarnation") remains current. At the same time, urban butchering and the market made the food sense increasingly dominant in ordinary speech. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries thus sharpen a division that the older language had left blurred: Fleisch as the body in theology, Fleisch as meat in the kitchen.

5. Enlightenment, lexicography and industrial meat. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought the systematic description of German. The Grimms' Deutsches Wörterbuch treats Fleisch at length, distinguishing the biblical, anatomical and culinary senses and recording a rich phraseology. In the same period, urban growth, railways and refrigeration turned meat into an industrial commodity. The butcher's trade split into regional names (Fleischer, Metzger, Schlachter), and compounds such as Fleischbank, Fleischmarkt and Fleischwaren multiplied. Anatomy and physiology adopted Muskelfleisch and Fleischfaser for precise description, while wildes Fleisch remained the surgeon's term for granulation tissue.

6. Twentieth-century rationing and mass consumption. The two world wars made Fleisch a political object. Ration cards, fleischlose Tage ("meatless days") and black-market trade gave the word a sharp practical edge. In the postwar Wirtschaftswunder, rising incomes made meat an everyday food and a symbol of prosperity; the Sunday roast and the butcher's shop became fixtures of West German life. In the German Democratic Republic, Fleisch was subsidized and periodically scarce, and the word acquired a quiet political charge in jokes and in the language of shortage.

7. The twenty-first century: ethical and ecological charge. Contemporary German uses Fleisch at the centre of debates about climate, health and animal welfare. Fleischersatz ("meat substitute"), Fleischsteuer ("meat tax"), Fleischatlas (a widely cited data report), Fleischkonsum and Laborfleisch ("cultured meat") show the word being redefined by its consequences. Vegetarian and vegan discourse often avoids the word or marks it with scare quotes; the food industry defends it as a protected designation. The semantic core remains "animal tissue eaten as food", but the connotations now include carbon footprint, animal suffering, health risk and cultural identity.

8. Sound and form in summary.

  • Consonants: Germanic *fl- is retained; *-sk- becomes -sc- in Old High German and -sch [ʃ] in Middle High German and modern German.
  • Vowels: Germanic *ai becomes Old High German ei; the modern ei is therefore inherited, not a New High German diphthongization.
  • Spelling: Middle High German v- and f- alternate; modern standard German fixes F-.
  • Morphology: a strong neuter mass noun, genitive Fleisches or Fleischs, no plural; the plural is supplied by Fleischsorten.
  • Semantics: the inherited double sense "living flesh" and "meat" is narrowed socially, with the culinary sense dominating everyday use and the theological sense dominating elevated use.

9. Literary and cultural attestations. The word's trail runs through Old High German glosses, Middle High German mysticism, Luther's Bible, the Grimms' dictionary, and the modern literature of war and scarcity. It is embedded in proverbs (weder Fisch noch Fleisch), in Pauline theology (das Fleisch ist schwach), in medical terminology (wildes Fleisch) and in the contemporary language of food politics. No other German word so neatly shows how a single inherited term can carry the body, the plate and the conscience at once.
