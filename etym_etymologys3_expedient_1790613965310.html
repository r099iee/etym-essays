Expedient begins with feet in a trap. Latin pes, pedis foot, from PIE *ped- foot (cognate with Greek pous, podos, Sanskrit pād, English foot), combines with prefix ex- out of, from PIE *eghs out, to form the verb expedire: literally to free the feet from a snare, to disentangle, to extricate. Roman soldiers used it concretely — expeditus meant light-armed, unencumbered for marching, without packs (impedimenta baggage, whence English impediment is literally something foot-shackling). From untying feet the verb generalized to clearing the way, getting ready, dispatching business quickly, and being serviceable: expedit mihi it is useful to me.

Classical authors already moralized the shift. Cicero contrasts honestum the honourable with utile / expediens the useful; the Stoic debate de officiis asks whether the expedient can ever conflict with the right. The present participle expediens, expedientis nominalized this usefulness into a quality: that which promotes an end. Ecclesiastical and scholastic Latin extended it to governance and salvation — expedit vobis it is expedient for you (Vulgate John 16:7) — giving it theological dignity.

Old French borrowed expedient in the 14th century as learned administrative vocabulary for fitting and needful measures; Middle English took it c. 1400 (Chaucer, Gower, Lydgate) both as adjective profitable, fitting and noun means, device. Early modern statecraft cemented the double edge: Bacon praises wise expedients, while Puritan divines warn against mere expediency. Swift’s Lagado Academy satire (1726), source of the present context, weaponizes the noun: projectors gravely dub carrying sacks of lumber as an expedient to abolish words, exposing how bureaucratic language sanctifies absurdity as practicality.

  • Phonology: Latin expedire /eks.peˈdiː.re/ → Old French /eks.pe.djɑ̃/ → Middle English /eksˈpiːdiənt/ with stress retraction and yod; RP /ɛkspˈiːdiənt/ preserves Latinate /ks/ + long /iː/, unlike popular French loans.
  • Morphology: ex- out + ped- foot + -ire verb + -ent participial adjective, later zero-converted to noun; family includes expedite, expedition, expeditious, impediment, impede, pedal, pedestrian, biped — a whole foot-based network of hindrance vs help.
  • Semantic drift: freeing feet → clearing path → being useful → being politically useful → being usefully unscrupulous; plus reification useful quality → useful thing (device). The pejorative branch grew as liberal ethics opposed utility to duty.
  • Cultural attestations: King James Bible It is expedient for us that one man should die (John 11:50) fixes the morally ambiguous flavour; Benthamite utilitarianism (expediency as greatest happiness) vs Newman/Arnold critique of expediency as philistinism; modern politics politically expedient almost always censure.

Thus a soldier’s untied sandal became philosophy’s most ambivalent compliment: to call something expedient is still to praise its feet while questioning its heart.
