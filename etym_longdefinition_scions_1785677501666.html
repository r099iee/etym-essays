SCIONS: The plural of scion — the descendants or heirs of a prominent family, dynasty, or house; or, in horticulture, young shoots or twigs cut from one plant to be grafted onto another. The two senses are bound together by one image: a scion is an outgrowth of a living stock, a new shoot that carries the life of the parent — in the garden, a cutting that will be grafted onto a rootstock; in a family, a child or heir grafted onto the family line. The word is formal, dynastic, and faintly botanical-poetic, and it dignifies the young heirs it describes. Nuance in this context. In the card's sentence — "The noble __________ of the old dynasty gathered to negotiate the terms of succession" — the plural noun casts the young heirs of an ancient house as living branches of a family tree. The word carries the whole force of its horticultural past: a scion is literally a shoot cut from one plant to be grafted onto another, and the metaphor applies that image to a dynasty whose members are, as it were, grafted onto the family stock — carrying its blood, its name, and its future. The tone is formal, dynastic, and faintly botanical-poetic; "scions of the old dynasty" dignifies the young negotiators, making them not mere claimants but natural outgrowths of a lineage that is itself the tree. In this context the word also does political work: because it emphasizes descent and family rather than law or contract, it frames the succession as a matter of blood and inheritance, of who the house will continue through, and the phrase "negotiate the terms" sets these living shoots in council — a new generation shaping the very branch it belongs to. The plural matters: a whole row of heirs, all outgrowths of the one old trunk, gathering to decide the family's future. Senses and uses.
  • Sense 1 — genealogical (dominant modern sense): a descendant or heir of a prominent family, dynasty, or house; "a scion of the banking empire," "scions of the old aristocracy," "the young scion of a famous political family." The word is used with admiration or at least elevation, and nearly always of families of rank, wealth, or fame.
  • Sense 2 — horticultural (the original sense): a young shoot, twig, or bud cut from one plant for grafting onto another; "graft the scion onto the rootstock," "healthy green scions for grafting." This is the literal, farming sense from which the genealogical metaphor grew, and it remains current in orcharding, viticulture, and plant breeding.
  • Sense 3 — figurative extension (modern): a product or outgrowth of anything regarded as a stock; "scions of the movement," "scions of the city's artistic tradition" — a looser, literary use in which anything with a lineage can have scions.
The semantic bridge. The word entered English in the fourteenth century from Old French as a purely horticultural term: a cion (or sion) was a shoot cut from one tree to be grafted onto another. The French word was borrowed from a Germanic source, ultimately from a Frankish word for "sprout, shoot," a word cognate with Old English cīþ, "sprout, germ." The genealogical sense grew naturally out of the graft metaphor in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: as a grafted scion carries the life of one tree onto another, so a child carries the life of a family into the next generation, and the word "scion of the family" was applied first to princely and noble offspring, then to heirs of any prominent house. The English spelling scion, with its initial s-, stabilized in the seventeenth century out of earlier forms cion and sion. The two senses have lived side by side ever since — the farmer's cutting and the family's heir — united by the image of the living shoot. Collocations and register. The noun collocates with "of" and a family, dynasty, empire, or house: "a scion of the Habsburgs," "scions of the old dynasty," "scions of a merchant banking family." In horticulture it collocates with "graft," "rootstock," "stock," "twig," "shoot": "a scion for grafting," "scion wood." The register is formal and literary for the genealogical sense — the word is at home in history, biography, and high journalism, and carries a slightly old-fashioned, admiring flourish — while the horticultural sense is plain technical vocabulary. There is no common plural alternative with quite the same force: heirs is legal, descendants is neutral, and scions alone carries the botanical image of the family tree.
