The verb and noun gush possesses a vigorous, onomatopoeic Germanic heritage, originating in ancient expressive sound-symbolism for rushing, bursting water and evolving through Scandinavian influence into a multifaceted English term spanning hydrology, petroleum booms, Victorian sentimentality, and avant-garde linguistic theory.

Linguists trace gush to an expressive, imitative Germanic root *geusaną ("to pour out, surge, burst forth"), which is closely connected to the Proto-Indo-European root *gʷʰew- / *gʷʰeu-d- ("to pour, shed"), the ultimate source of Latin fundere ("to pour" > English fuse, foundry, confound) and Ancient Greek chéein (χέειν, "to pour" > English chemistry):

  • In the North Germanic branch, the root gave rise to Old Norse geysa ("to rush furiously, chafe, burst out in anger or water"), which also gave Icelandic geysir, the proper name of the famous erupting hot spring in the Haukadalur valley borrowed into international English as geyser.
  • Related cognates include Middle High German giezen ("to pour"), Low German gusen ("to gush"), and Dutch gijzen.

The word entered recorded English literature in late Middle English around 1400 as guschen (also written gousen), appearing in chivalric romances and medical chronicles to describe the sudden, copious outflow of blood from battle wounds or the violent rushing of floodwaters breaching a dam. The phonological structure of the word—initiating with the voiced velar plosive /ɡ/ and terminating in the voiceless postalveolar fricative /ʃ/—inherently mimics the acoustic sensation of pressurized fluid rushing forcefully through a narrow opening.

During the Early Modern English period (sixteenth and seventeenth centuries), gush became standard in descriptive poetry and drama (appearing in Shakespeare, Spenser, and Milton) to depict torrential storms, weeping, and volcanic eruptions. By the early nineteenth century, the word initiated an important metaphorical shift:

  • In the 1820s and 1830s, English essayists and satirical commentators began using gush to characterize an excessive, cloying, or hyperbolic display of emotion, praise, or enthusiasm. A person who spoke with unrestrained, theatrical sentimentality was said to "gush," leading to the Victorian formation of the adjective gushing and the noun gushiness.
  • In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the American oil boom introduced the iconic term gusher for an oil well drilled into a high-pressure reservoir that spontaneously blew drilling mud, oil, and gas hundreds of feet into the air.
  • In the mid-to-late twentieth century, poststructuralist literary critics such as Roland Barthes adopted the term metaphorically to describe experimental literature (such as Philippe Sollers's Lois), where traditional syntactic grammar is completely abandoned in favor of a continuous, intoxicating, breathless "gush of words"—an unfiltered torrent of raw language and affective desire.
