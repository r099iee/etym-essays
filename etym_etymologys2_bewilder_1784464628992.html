PIE *wel- "to wish, choose" or "to tear, injure" — debated; the pre-Germanic ancestry of *wild is uncertain (c. 4500–3000 BCE; see note) → Proto-Germanic *wilþijaz "wild, untamed, uncultivated" (c. 500 BCE–200 CE; reconstructed; cognate with Welsh gwyllt "wild") → Old English wilde "wild, untamed, waste, uncontrolled" (8th–11th c.) → Middle English wild "wild" (12th–15th c.) → English wilderness (Old English wildernes) "wild land, waste" → English wilder (verb) "to lose one's way; to lead astray" (17th c., first recorded 1610s–20s) → English bewilder (be- intensive + wilder) "to lead into the wilds; to confuse utterly" (late 17th c., first recorded c. 1689) → Modern English BEWILDER Note on the debated stages: (1) the PIE ancestry of *wilþijaz is not settled; proposed connections include PIE *wel- "to wish, want" (the "self-willed, uncontrollable" reading of wild), and various others, but no single reconstruction is standard — several handbooks simply mark the Germanic adjective as of uncertain origin; (2) the verbs wilder and bewilder are English formations of the 17th century, so the entire later half of the chain is firmly attested, but the deep root must be labelled uncertain; (3) be- in bewilder is the intensive prefix from Proto-Germanic *bi- (PIE *ambhi "around, on both sides").
