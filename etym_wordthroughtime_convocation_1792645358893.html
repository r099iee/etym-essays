- [Late 14th–15th c. (c. 1380s–1400s)] — Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Latin (convocacioun), initially used in ecclesiastical and royal law for the formal summoning of clergy synods (Canterbury and York) or sovereign assemblies - [perception: revered as a solemn, authoritative instrument of archiepiscopal governance and royal decree]
- [16th–17th c. Reformation & Stuart Era] — Deeply entangled in constitutional and religious battles; the Convocations of the Clergy debated the Thirty-Nine Articles, canon law, and prayer book revisions amidst fierce friction between Crown, Bishops, and Parliament - [perception: viewed as a potent, often contentious theological parliament governing spiritual orthodoxy and clerical rights]
- [18th–19th c. Academic Institutionalization] — Adopted formally by Oxford, Cambridge, and emerging Scottish, North American, and colonial universities to designate the supreme legislative assembly of resident Masters and Doctors, and the solemn ceremonial gathering conferring degrees - [perception: acquired distinguished academic prestige, symbolizing scholarship, intellectual lineage, and collegiate tradition]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Global Era] — Universally embraced across Commonwealth nations, North America, and international universities as the primary designation for university commencement exercises, matriculation ceremonies, and fraternal meetings - [perception: widely recognized by the public as a festive, dignified milestone of educational achievement and graduation]
