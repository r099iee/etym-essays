No PIE ancestry; the word is a Sino-Tibetan compound borrowed into English through the southern ports of China. 1. Proto-Sino-Tibetan: *rənh “man, person” + *srəm “the ginseng plant” — a proposed reconstruction of the compound's elements; the exact Old Chinese phonology is debated (handbooks differ; cf. Baxter–Sagart), but the sense — “man-plant,” the root that resembles a human figure — is certain. 2. Old Chinese: 人 *njən (later *niŋ / *njin per Baxter–Sagart) “man, person” + 蔘 *srəm “the ginseng plant” — later written 參 / 蔘, the plant-character carrying the “grass” radical 艹. 3. Classical Chinese: 人參 rénshēn “ginseng” — recorded among the top-grade (上品) tonics in the Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng, the “Divine Farmer's Materia Medica” (Han dynasty). 4. Southern Min / Hokkien (Min Nan): jîn-sim / lîn-sim (人蔘) — the coastal reading of the compound, the form European traders encountered. 5. English ginseng (1654, earliest recorded gensing; later ginshang, jenseng) — borrowed via Dutch and Portuguese traders who dealt with the southern ports; English ginseng, the root word. Notes: Korean 인삼 insam is a direct Sino-Korean loan keeping both characters; Japanese 人参 ninjin was borrowed with the Sinic reading but later shifted to mean “carrot” (modern Japanese uses 朝鮮人参 chōsen-ninjin, “Korean ginseng,” for the herb); the Manchu name for the wild root, orhoda, is a native Tungusic word, unrelated to the Chinese compound. The scientific name Panax (Linnaeus, 1753) is a separate Linnaean graft from Greek pan “all” + akos “remedy,” not an ancestor of the English word.
