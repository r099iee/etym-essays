THE EVOLUTION ESSAY OF AFFRIGHTED 1. THE GERMANIC ROOT OF FEAR 'Affrighted' is a Germanic word through and through, and its story begins in the Proto-Germanic root *furht-, whose sense was 'fear' or 'dread.' That root appears across the whole of the early Germanic world with remarkable consistency: Old English fyrhtu and fyrhto, 'fear, trembling'; Old Saxon forhta; Old High German for(a)hta; Gothic faurhtei, 'fear, terror.' The direct German descendant is Furcht, still the ordinary word for 'fear,' with its verb fürchten, 'to fear,' and its adjective furchtsam, 'timid.' The English simplex developed in parallel: Old English fyrhtu gave Middle English frighte and modern English fright, 'a sudden scare,' which in the seventeenth century produced frighten by means of the inchoative suffix -en — the same suffix that gives us 'deepen' and 'strengthen' — and the adjective frightful, with its peculiar adverbial form frightfully, 'exceedingly, terribly.' Whether the Germanic root reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European *preḱ-, 'to fear,' as suggested by Avestan pərəsaiti, 'to fear, to dread,' remains an open question among scholars; the safest statement is that the word's attested history is entirely Germanic, and no PIE ancestry can be asserted with confidence. 2. THE BASE VERB AND THE INTENSIVE PREFIX The English verb line diverges from the noun line. The base verb was Old English fyrhtan, 'to frighten, terrify,' from the Proto-Germanic causative *furhtijaną, 'to make afraid.' Causative verbs in Proto-Germanic are formed on the past-tense (or o-grade) stem and mean 'to cause to be in the state named by the root': *furhtijaną is literally 'to cause fear to exist,' 'to make afraid.' Old English then strengthened this verb with the intensive prefix ā-, which goes back to Proto-Germanic *uz-, meaning 'out, utterly, completely.' The prefix, which appears in many Old English verbs to mark completion or thoroughness, produced āfyrhtan, 'to terrify utterly, to strike with dread.' That intensified verb came down through Middle English afrighten and affrighten into Early Modern English as affright, 'to frighten violently, to strike with sudden and great fear.' The prefix is the crucial agent: where plain 'frighten' suggests a moderate scare, 'affright' names a fear laid on with full force and completeness — the person is not merely startled but utterly overcome, the mind filled to the brim with terror. 3. FROM VERB TO PARTICIPIAL ADJECTIVE From the verb came the past participle affrighted, 'terrified, filled with dread,' and from the participle a full participial adjective of the same shape. The participial adjective is the form that has proved most durable. It appears in the poetry and prose of the sixteenth century onward, describing persons, animals, and their faculties seized by complete terror: affrighted crowds, an affrighted horse, affrighted eyes, an affrighted imagination. The word quickly acquired its characteristic register: formal, grave, and literary, the language of the pulpit, the epic, the history, and the tale of terror. It was never a word of the street; it was the word of solemn narration, and it carried with it the weight and dignity of the Old English intensive from which it came. 4. THE EIGHTEENTH CENTURY AND GIBBON By the eighteenth century 'affrighted' had settled as the stately, archaic-coloured word for complete terror — the word a formal historian would choose over the plainer 'frightened.' Gibbon's use is the literary summit of this development. In his account of the great earthquake that shook the cities of the eastern Roman Empire, he observes that 'their affrighted imagination enlarged the real extent of a momentary evil.' The choice of word is precise and psychological. A momentary evil — the earthquake itself, brief in its violence — is enlarged by the imagination that perceives it, and the engine of that enlargement is fear. Because the imagination is 'affrighted' — filled, possessed, and clouded by complete terror — it cannot measure; it multiplies. The archaism of the word is part of its effect: it lends the observation gravity and dignity, and it insists that this is not the mere 'fright' of a passing alarm but the total possession of the mind by dread, the very condition in which reason abdicates and exaggeration begins. The sentence is a miniature treatise on the psychology of panic, and the word that carries it is one of the oldest and strongest words in the English vocabulary of fear. 5. THE NINETEENTH CENTURY: POETRY, GOTHIC, AND ARCHAISM In the nineteenth century 'affrighted' retreated into elevated verse, Gothic fiction, and historical narrative. The Romantics and the Victorians used it freely — it is a natural word for the poetry of terror and the supernatural, for the ballads and the tales of the Gothic, where it sits beside 'aghast,' 'appalled,' and 'horror-struck.' Beside the plain, everyday 'frightened,' the archaic 'affrighted' sounds consciously literary, and its very old-fashionedness became part of its value: it could be used for solemn dignity, for Gothic atmosphere, or, increasingly, for a faintly ironic mock-heroic effect. The noun 'affright' ('terror, sudden fear') travelled with it in the same registers — 'in great affright,' 'to the affright of all' — though it was always the rarer member of the pair. 6. THE MODERN WORD: RARITY AND RESONANCE In the twentieth century and the present, 'affrighted' has become rare in speech and survives mainly in historical, mock-archaic, and deliberately literary writing. It keeps its solemn, complete-terror sense, but it has yielded everyday currency to 'frightened' and 'terrified.' A modern writer who uses 'affrighted' is reaching for the eighteenth century or the Gothic novel; the word is chosen precisely because it is not the ordinary word, and it carries with it a full cargo of literary memory — the sermons, the epics, the histories, and above all Gibbon's affrighted imagination enlarging the real extent of a momentary evil. Across its whole career, from the Old English āfyrhtan to the modern historical novel, the word has never lost the force its intensive prefix stamped upon it: affright is not the start of fear but its fullness, fear laid on utterly, the mind filled, possessed, and — in Gibbon's great sentence — made to magnify its own terrors.
