Gazette is one of the few English words whose entire biography is a transaction. Its life begins not in the press but in the marketplace of sixteenth-century Venice, where the first rudimentary news-sheets were hawked to merchants, bankers, and diplomats for the price of a single small coin. That coin was called a gazzetta, a diminutive whose earlier history is itself a mystery. The most widely repeated theory, supported by many standard dictionaries, derives the coin's name from a Mediterranean–Iranian chain: Greek gaza (γάζα), "royal treasury," appears to be a loanword from an Old Iranian word for "treasure," and the chain therefore runs through Iranian rather than through Indo-European proper — so the word has no secure Proto-Indo-European root, and no tree of Indo-European cognates can honestly be drawn for it. A rival and equally old theory prefers the bird: Venetian gazza, "magpie," the bird famous for its chattering, would have given gazzetta the sense of "idle talk, small news." Either way, the decisive event was not the word's origin but its pricing: the earliest Venetian news-sheets cost one gazzetta, and within a generation the name of the coin had become the name of the product itself.

The spread of the word maps the trade and diplomatic routes of early modern Europe. From Venice, Italian gazzetta passed into French as gazette in the sixteenth century, and English borrowed it around 1600 through French. For its first decades in English a "gazette" was simply a small newspaper, an unremarkable periodical of news — a trade name, not yet a title of office. The transformation into an official, governmental instrument came in 1665, when The London Gazette was founded as the journal of record of the British crown; from that moment a "gazette" meant, above all, the authoritative printed organ in which official appointments, promotions, honours, bankruptcies, and legal notices are published. The verb to gazette ("to announce or appoint officially") and the adjective gazetted grew directly out of this institution and remain standard in British and Commonwealth usage — a general or a peer is still said to be "gazetted" to his rank. The noun also reached the wider world of print: Spanish gaceta and Portuguese gazeta are direct Italian loans, German took Gazette via French, and in Russia French gazette gave the everyday word for "newspaper," газета (gazéta) — so that the name of a Venetian coin became the Russian term for the whole industry of the press.

The word's later career shows how an institutional name can colonise a language. In English the noun generated gazetteer — first "a writer for a gazette," then, through an eighteenth-century bestseller, Laurence Echard's The Gazetteer's or Newsman's Interpreter (1693), "a geographical dictionary" — a strange but well-documented semantic drift from newsman to atlas, a fitting close to the word's circle, since the voice that reports the news becomes the book that maps the world. The socio-cultural engine of the whole story is the rise of state publication itself: gazettes were the instruments by which early modern governments made their acts public, authoritative, and permanent, the printed guarantee that stood against rumour. Key stations:

  • Venetian gazzetta, the small coin (16th c.) — the price names the news-sheet bought with it (c. 1563).
  • Italian → French → English gazette (c. 1600) — the coin's name becomes the generic word for a small newspaper.
  • The London Gazette (1665) — the generic newspaper becomes the official journal of record of the state.
  • to gazette / gazetted (1660s) — the institution spawns a verb and an adjective of official announcement.
  • gazetteer — "writer for a gazette" → "geographical dictionary" (1693), the newsman's voice becomes the atlas.
  • Russian газета "newspaper" (18th c.) — the Venetian coin becomes the everyday Russian word for the press.
