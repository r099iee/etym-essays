Quintus is one of the oldest and plainest of Roman names: an ordinal numeral turned into a personal name, and then into a family inheritance. Its history runs from the Indo-European counting system to the Latin textbook, and it shows better than almost any other name how Roman nomenclature fossilised ordinary words.

The numeral behind the name

Latin quīnque “five” continues Proto-Italic *kʷenkʷe, which in turn reflects PIE *pénkʷe. The initial qu of the Latin word is the result of a regular assimilation: the labiovelar at the end of the word pulled the initial labial towards itself, so that *penkʷe became *kʷenkʷe. The same numeral is visible in Greek πέντε, Sanskrit pañca, Gothic fimf and Old English fīf, and the family resemblance is one of the standard demonstrations of the Indo-European relationship.

The ordinal is older still in formation: PIE *pénkʷtos “fifth” underlies Greek πέμπτος, Sanskrit pañcama- and Latin quīntus. Latin preserves an older spelling quinctus, with the nasal still written, and the classical quīntus shows the regular loss of the nasal before t with compensatory lengthening of the vowel. The same alternation appears in the month name Quīntīlis, the fifth month of the old Roman calendar, later renamed July.

  • Numerical kin — quattuor “four”, sex “six” and septem “seven” belong to the same inherited series; the ordinals quārtus, quīntus, sextus and septimus were all available as names.
  • Derived vocabulary — quīnta essentia, the “fifth essence” of Aristotelian cosmology, gives English “quintessence”; quīntus also lies behind “quintet”, “quintuple”, “quintile” and the card-game “quint”.
  • Camp and calendar — quīntāna was the fifth street of a Roman camp and survives in “quintain”; Quīntīlis survived as a poetic name for July long after the imperial renaming.

From numeral to praenomen

Early Rome used a very small stock of praenomina, and several of them were ordinals: Primus, Secundus, Tertius, Quartus, Quintus, Sextus, Decimus. The origin of the fashion is obscure, but the obvious explanation — that the name recorded the child's place in the family — is supported by the existence of the whole series and by the later Roman habit of naming a fifth son Quintus and a sixth Sextus. By the historical period, however, the practice had become hereditary: the same praenomen recurs in a family for generations, and Quintus is found as the first son's name. The ordinal sense had become a fossil.

The name was especially common in plebeian and municipal families, and it is frequent in inscriptions: Q. Fabius Maximus, Q. Hortensius, Q. Sertorius, Q. Caecilius Metellus. Its abbreviation Q. is one of the most familiar marks of Latin epigraphy. The feminine Quinta and the derivative Quintinus extended the family; the latter, through the martyr Quentin of Amiens, became the French and English given name Quentin.

The Cicerones

The most famous bearer is not a general or a magistrate but the younger brother of an orator. Quintus Tullius Cicero was born about 102 BCE, four years after Marcus, and was educated with him. His public career — praetor in 62, governor of Asia from 61 to 59, legate in Gaul from 54 to 52 — was respectable but never independent of his brother's reputation. His literary remains are modest and disputed: the Commentariolum Petitionis, a practical manual for the consular canvass, and four tragedies said to have been written in sixteen days. His marriage to Pomponia, sister of Atticus, and his death in the proscriptions of 43 BCE give him a place in the family tragedy that surrounds Cicero's own murder.

The name's later history is one of learned survival rather than natural transmission. Latin schools kept Quintus alive as a paradigm of the second declension; Renaissance humanists gave it to their sons; the classical revival of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries made it a recognisable English given name; and historical fiction used it as a marker of Roman setting — Quintus Arrius in Ben-Hur, Quintus in the Cambridge Latin Course. In none of these uses does the ordinal meaning survive except as an etymological footnote.

Modern perception

Today Quintus is rare, slightly bookish and unmistakably classical. It carries no religious or political charge, unlike Augustus or Caesar, and its main connotation is “Roman” — a schoolboy's Rome of togas and declensions. The name's long descent from a PIE numeral is invisible to its bearers, which is precisely what makes it a good example of how thoroughly names can forget their meanings.
