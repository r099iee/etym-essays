Torches, the plural of torch, are in their primary sense hand-held sticks or clubs with combustible material at one end, carried burning to give light — among the oldest portable lamps known to humanity, predating candles, lanterns, and every form of electric light.

In the source sentence the word appears inside Gandalf's firework distribution — ‘squibs, crackers, backarappers, sparklers, torches, dwarf-candles…’ — where torches are festive hand-flames, waved and carried amid the celebration: part illumination, part spectacle, part ceremony. Tolkien the philologist surely savoured placing so ancient a word among his invented elvish pyrotechnics.

Core meaning in detail: A traditional torch is a wooden shaft wound or dipped at the head with oil-soaked rags, pitch, wax, or resin, burning with a large open flame. From castle sieges to Olympic ceremonies, the form has barely changed in millennia: a handle plus a flame. Modern torches extend the idea — the Olympic torch, the tiki torch, the flashlight (which British English still calls an electric torch).

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Hand-held flame for light (primary, from c. 1300): the burning brand carried in processions, castles, caves, and celebrations; the torch of the Statue of Liberty is its most famous image.
  • A source of guidance or inspiration (figurative): ‘to carry/pass/hand on the torch’ — keeping a cause, tradition, or body of knowledge alive. The bridge is the ancient relay: the flame physically passed from runner to runner becomes the mission passed from generation to generation.
  • An electric torch / flashlight (British): the battery lamp. The bridge is functional analogy — a hand-held giver of portable light — and the name preserves the older technology inside the new.
  • A welding or cutting torch (technical): the oxyacetylene torch. The bridge is the controlled jet of flame at the end of a handled tool — same shape, same fire, industrialised.
  • Arson slang — ‘to torch’ (verb, 20th c. American): to set fire to a building, especially for insurance fraud (‘they torched the warehouse’). The bridge runs from the noun's flame to the act of applying flame criminally.
  • Carrying a torch for someone (idiom, 1920s–30s): to harbour an unrequited love, as in the torch song. The bridge is the flame as emblem of a love that keeps burning though never returned — the singer holds the flame alone in the dark.

Register and connotation: torches carries ceremony and antiquity — processions, monuments, angry mobs in old films — but also warmth and hope (Liberty's torch, the Olympic flame). The mob-with-torches image gives it a faint edge of menace in modern metaphor (‘torch-bearing crowds’), while ‘passing the torch’ is wholly noble. Few words span arson and idealism so completely. The flame that warms the celebration and the flame that razes the warehouse are, linguistically, one fire.
