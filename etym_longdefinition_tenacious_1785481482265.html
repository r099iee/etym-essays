In this sentence, tenacious describes the ways of dealing with metre that have "proved most tenacious in their hold on the English literary consciousness": metrical habits so firmly established that they have gripped the English literary mind for centuries and resisted every attempt to dislodge them. The context is a historical account of English prosody - of the systems of handling metre that have survived and dominated - so the word does the work of both praise and warning. The tone is admiring but also slightly wary: the hold is so powerful that later poets and readers are still governed by it "whether we realise it or not". Tenacity here is not mere persistence; it is a physical, clinging, vine-like grip, as if the metrical traditions had hands that will not let go. At its root the word is about the act of holding. English tenacious comes directly from the Latin adjective tenax (stem tenac-), "grasping, holding fast, clinging", which in turn is formed from the verb tenere, "to hold, keep, grasp". The core literal sense is that of a firm physical grasp or adhesion: a tenacious grip, a tenacious vine clinging to a wall, a tenacious coating that will not peel away. Everything else the word means today is an extension of that single physical image of holding on. PRIMARY SENSES OF TENACIOUS TODAY:
  • Physical adhesion or firm grip: the oldest living sense in English, describing things that hold or stick with unusual firmness - a tenacious grip, tenacious mud, a tenacious adhesive, a tenacious weed whose roots cannot be pulled up.
  • Of the mind and memory: a tenacious memory is one that retains facts and impressions stubbornly and for a very long time; a tenacious idea or opinion is one that refuses to be removed or argued away. This is the mental extension of the physical grip: the mind "holds on" to what it has grasped.
  • Of character and resolve: a tenacious person or a tenacious pursuit is one that will not give up in the face of difficulty; the word carries strong approval here - tenacity as dogged, unyielding endurance - while remaining distinct from mere obstinacy.
  • Of habits, traditions and institutions: the sense used in the present passage - a tenacious tradition, tenacious custom, a tenacious literary convention that dominates succeeding generations long after its origins are forgotten.
  • Of a hold over people or power: tenacious can describe a hold, influence, or claim that cannot be shaken - a tenacious hold on power, a tenacious grip on the popular imagination.
  • In medicine and physiology: a tenacious sputum or tenacious mucus is thick, sticky and hard to clear from the lungs or airways; tenacious describes viscous, adhesive body fluids. This is the clinical descendant of the physical-adhesion sense.
  • In materials science and engineering: tenacity is the technical property of toughness or resistance to rupture - the ability of a material or fibre to withstand pulling forces; a "tenacious" alloy or coating is one with high tensile strength or strong adhesion to a substrate.
  • In biochemistry and microbiology: a "tenacious biofilm" is a microbial layer that adheres strongly to a surface and resists removal; "tenacious binding" describes a molecular or protein interaction of unusually high affinity and slow dissociation.
  • In computing and networking: a "tenacious connection" is a network or client-server link that automatically persists and retries rather than dropping after a failure; tenacious retry logic in fault-tolerant systems keeps an operation running despite transient errors. This is the most recent extension, borrowed by engineers for the idea of a system that "refuses to let go".
  • Of diseases and conditions: a tenacious infection or a tenacious symptom is one that persists stubbornly and resists treatment - again the image of something that will not be shaken off.
THE SEMANTIC BRIDGE: The historical logic is beautifully simple. Latin tenere, "to hold", gave the adjective tenax, "given to holding, holding fast", built with the suffix -ax, which expresses a habitual tendency (the same suffix appears in audax, "bold", from audere, "to dare", and in rapax, "grasping", from rapere, "to seize"). A tenax thing is not merely held; it is by nature a holder, a thing that is constantly and characteristically inclined to grip. When English borrowed tenacious in the early seventeenth century, it borrowed both the physical image and the habit of holding. The move from "physically clinging" to "mentally persistent" is a transfer from the body to the mind: a person who "holds fast" to a belief treats the belief as something physically grasped and protected. The modern technical senses - in medicine, materials science, biochemistry and computing - are all modern re-applications of the same ancient metaphor of adhesion and persistence to new domains of knowledge. Distinctions against near-synonyms are worth making precise, because each competitor stresses a different facet. "Persistent" is the neutral term for continuing in the face of difficulty; it describes duration of effort without any image of a grip. "Persevering" is positive and moral, suggesting virtuous continued effort against setbacks. "Dogged" is grim and slogging, suggesting dull unyielding determination. "Obstinate" and "stubborn" both imply unreasonable refusal to yield, with a note of censure. "Pertinacious" is tenacity taken to a fault - annoying, clinging insistence (it is in fact a close relative of tenacious through the same Latin root, Latin pertinax, "holding on persistently"). What tenacious alone contributes is the image: it celebrates the sheer adhesive strength of the thing that sticks. That is why we can say, naturally and without strain, "a tenacious memory", "tenacious weeds", and - exactly as in our passage - tenacious ways of dealing with metre.
