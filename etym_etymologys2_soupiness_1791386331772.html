Proto-Indo-European (reconstructed)
*seu- / *sew-
"to take liquid, suck, seep"
↓
Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BCE)
*suppō / *sūpaną
"to sup, drink, sip, soak bread"
↓
Late Latin / Vulgar Latin (c. 6th c. CE)
suppa
"piece of bread soaked in broth"
↓
Old French (12th c.)
soupe
"broth poured over bread; sop"
↓
Middle English (c. 13th–14th c.)
soupe / sope
"broth poured over bread; soup"
↓
Modern English (17th c.)
soup
"liquid food of meat and vegetables"
↓
Modern English (19th c. adjective)
soupy
"resembling soup; foggy; sentimental"
↓
Modern English (late 19th–early 20th c.)
soupiness
"soupy state; murk; mawkish sentimentality"
Note: The Germanic root *suppō was borrowed into Late Latin during the post-Roman migrations, displacing classical Latin *ius* (broth, whence *juice*). In medieval dining, *soupe* originally designated the trenchers or crusts of bread onto which boiling broth was ladled, before shifting semantically to the liquid itself. The abstract noun *soupiness* was created in Modern English by attaching the native Germanic suffix *-ness* to the colloquial adjective *soupy*.
