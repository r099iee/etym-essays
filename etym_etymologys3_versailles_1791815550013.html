A place name of uncertain origin. Versailles is first a field name, not a palace. The site lay in wooded and marshy country south-west of Paris, with a hamlet, a church and farmland, and the name is recorded in medieval charters from the eleventh century. Its origin is disputed, and the dispute is genuine rather than merely technical. The most widely repeated explanation derives it from Latin versare, “to turn over, turn again and again”, a frequentative of vertere, “to turn”; the sense would be land that has been turned by the plough, a ploughed field, and the name would be a collective or plural of such a term. On that reading the hamlet was named for the tilled fields around it, exactly as French semailles “sowing” comes from Latin seminare.

Competing explanations. Other accounts connect the name with Latin versus “a slope, a turning” or with versor “to dwell, to stay”, and some local writers have proposed a personal name. None of these has won general assent, and no pre-eleventh-century form is attested. What can be said with confidence is that the name belongs to the large family of French toponyms built on Latin vertere and its derivatives, and that the meaning “ploughed land” fits the agricultural setting better than “dwelling” or “slope”. The derivation should be presented as probable, not certain — a caution that applies to most French field names.

From fields to palace. The hamlet became a royal site in the seventeenth century. Louis XIII built a small hunting lodge there in 1623; Louis XIV began transforming it in 1661, enveloped the old château in a new one in 1668, and moved the court there in 1682. The name then underwent a rapid semantic ascent: from a few fields and a church, it came to denote the greatest palace in Europe and the entire system of absolute monarchy that radiated from it. This is one of the clearest cases in French of a toponym becoming a political abstraction.

The name as metonym.

  • The court: “Versailles” in the eighteenth century means the king’s entourage, its factions, favours and etiquette; to be well at Versailles is to be powerful.
  • The regime: revolutionary writers use “Versailles” for the old order itself — its luxury, secrecy and distance from the people — and the word acquires a polemical edge.
  • The treaty: the peace signed in the Hall of Mirrors in 1919 gives the name a diplomatic sense; in Germany “Versailles” became a byword for a dictated peace and a grievance.
  • Opulence: in modern usage any vast, gilded interior can be called “a Versailles”, and colloquial French uses “ce n’est pas Versailles” to mean “it is not luxurious”.

Phonetics and spelling. French Versailles is pronounced /vɛʁ.saj/: the final s is silent, the -ille is a palatal glide rather than the /ij/ of fille in some varieties, and the vowel of the first syllable is open. English borrowed the written form and anglicised the pronunciation to /veəˈsaɪ/, with the stress on the last syllable; the spelling with -ailles looks plural because it is — toponyms of this type often generalise a field name. The English adjective “Versailles” (as in “Versailles style”) is invariable and capitalised.

Why it matters here. Carlyle’s “his own Chateau of Versailles” uses the name at the height of its political meaning. The king is carried from the small private Trianon back to the immense public palace, and the sentence measures the distance between the shepherd and the flock. By 1774 Versailles is still the centre of Europe, but the word already carries the luxury and isolation that will be held against it in 1789; Carlyle, writing in 1837, lets that future hang over the deathbed.
