Latvian is the youngest of the names treated here as a national label, even though the people and their language are ancient. For most of its recorded history the word was an outsiders’ term for tribes and peasants; only in the nineteenth century did it become the name of a nation, and only in 1918 did it become the name of a state.

First attestations

The ethnonym appears in Latin sources of the thirteenth century. Henry of Livonia’s chronicle, written around 1225, uses Lettones and Lettigalli for the Baltic peoples of the region, alongside Livones for the Finnic Livs and Semigalli for the Semigallians. The forms were taken into Middle Low German as Lette and into Latin as Lettus, and it is from these that English Lettish descends. The land itself was usually called Livonia, after the Livs, not Latvia.

The etymological question

The origin of the root Lat- is uncertain, and the proposals are best treated as hypotheses:

  • From the Latgalians. The name is usually explained as a shortening of Latgali, the tribe of the eastern region. Latgale may contain gals “end, border,” giving “the land at the end” or “the borderland of the Lats.”
  • From a river name. Some scholars connect Lat- with a hydronym, by analogy with other Baltic tribal names derived from rivers.
  • From a root meaning “to flow.” A Baltic verbal root has been proposed, but the phonology is not decisive.

The modern national name Latvija is a nineteenth-century formation built on latvis “Latvian” with the suffix -ija. Its coinage belongs to the Young Latvian movement, which needed a name for the whole people rather than for a province.

Medieval and early modern background

German crusaders and merchants arrived at the end of the twelfth century; Bishop Albert founded Riga in 1201, and the Livonian Order and the Hanseatic League shaped the coast for centuries. After the Livonian War the region was divided between Poland-Lithuania, Sweden and Denmark, and by the eighteenth century most of it was in the Russian Empire: Livonia in 1721 by the Treaty of Nystad, Courland in 1795 after the third partition of Poland. Throughout this period the Baltic German nobility owned the land, German was the language of administration and education, and Latvian was the language of the peasantry.

Serfdom, reform and revival

Serfdom was abolished in Courland in 1817 and in Vidzeme in 1819, earlier than in much of the Russian Empire. The resulting social mobility, together with literacy and the influence of the Lutheran church, produced the conditions for a national awakening. In the 1850s and 1860s the Jaunlatvieši (“Young Latvians”) — Juris Alunāns, Atis Kronvalds, Krišjānis Valdemārs and others — standardised the literary language, collected folklore and argued for education in Latvian. Krišjānis Barons later assembled the monumental collection of Dainas, the folk songs that became the canonical expression of national identity.

1918: the state and the name

On 18 November 1918, amid the collapse of the Russian and German empires, the People’s Council of Latvia proclaimed independence. The new state adopted Latvija as its name, and “Latvian” became a citizenship as well as an ethnicity. Riga flourished as a cosmopolitan capital, and the country remained a parliamentary democracy until Kārlis Ulmanis’s coup in 1934.

Occupation, exile and return

Under the Molotov–Ribbentrop pact, Soviet troops entered in 1940 and Latvia was annexed as a Soviet republic. Nazi Germany occupied the country in 1941–1944, and the Holocaust destroyed the Jewish community, including the Riga ghetto. The Soviet return in 1944 brought deportations — especially the mass deportations of March 1949 — collectivisation and Russification. Many Latvians fled west and formed a vocal diaspora, which kept the name “Latvia” alive in exile and refused to recognise the Soviet annexation.

Singing Revolution and independence

In the late 1980s the reform movement Tautas fronte and the song festivals became vehicles of national mobilisation. On 23 August 1989 the Baltic Way linked Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius in a human chain of some two million people. Latvia declared the restoration of independence on 21 August 1991 and joined the European Union and NATO in 2004.

Language history

The first printed Latvian texts are a Catholic catechism of 1585 and a Lutheran Enchiridion of 1586; Georg Mancelius produced an important dictionary in 1638. The written language long followed German orthography, until a new orthography devised by Kārlis Mīlenbahs was adopted in 1908 and standardised in the 1920s. Latvian has three main dialect areas — Livonian, Middle and High Latvian — and the standard is based on the Middle dialect. Livonian, a Finnic language spoken on the coast, influenced the vocabulary and phonology and is now effectively extinct as a native language.

Literature and culture

The national literature begins with folk songs and religious texts, and comes of age with the poet and playwright Rainis and the poet Aspazija in the late nineteenth century. The novelist Jānis Jaunsudrabiņš, the poet Imants Ziedonis and the contemporary writer Inga Ābele carry the tradition into the modern period. In architecture, Riga’s concentration of Art Nouveau buildings is among the finest in Europe.

From a chronicler’s term for tribes, through a German province and a Russian guberniya, to a national name and an official EU language: Latvian records the slow transfer of the power to name from outsiders to the people themselves.
