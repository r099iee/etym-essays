Milch belongs to one of the oldest and most widely attested semantic fields in Indo-European: dairying. The reconstructed root *h₂melǵ-, “to milk, to wipe or draw off”, underlies Latin mulgere “to milk”, Greek amelgein “to milk”, Welsh blith “milch, giving milk”, Old Church Slavonic mlěsti “to milk”, and the Germanic verbs represented by German melken and English milk. The root is therefore older than any attested Germanic language, and its survival across branches shows that milking was a well-established practice in the prehistoric communities that spoke Proto-Indo-European.

From verb to noun. Proto-Germanic formed a noun from the verb: *melukz, the substance obtained by milking. The formation is archaic, and its exact morphology has been debated, but the noun is securely attested across the family: Old English meolc, Old Frisian melok, Old Saxon miluk, Old High German miluh, Old Norse mjolk. English milk and German Milch are direct continuations of the same Proto-Germanic word, which is why they look and sound so alike — the resemblance noted in the card is not a coincidence of borrowing but the residue of a common ancestor.

  • Grimm's law. The PIE voiced palatovelar *ǵ of the root becomes Proto-Germanic *k; hence Latin mulgere with l-g corresponds to Germanic forms with l-k. The same correspondence is visible in dozens of other words and is one of the signatures of the Germanic branch.
  • The High German consonant shift. In the southern dialects, including Old High German, Germanic *k in certain positions became h or ch. Thus *melukz yields miluh, with final h, and later Middle High German milch, with the affricate-like ch that survives in modern Standard German.
  • Morphology. The noun was feminine in Proto-Germanic and remains feminine in German (die Milch), while the related verb melken is strong and shows ablaut. The Modern German plural is effectively unused in ordinary speech, the substance being treated as a mass noun.

Old and Middle High German. Old High German miluh is attested from the eighth century in glosses and monastic texts, where milk appears both as food and as an allegorical substance. Middle High German milch is common in courtly and religious writing. The word's semantic range in this period is already the modern one: the secretion, the drink, the product of the cow or ewe, and by extension whatever is white and nourishing. The great medieval German preachers used Milch for the elementary nourishment of the soul, a trope inherited from the New Testament.

Reformation and literary fixation. Luther's Bible gave the word two canonical contexts that every German speaker still recognises. The first is the Promised Land “flowing with milk and honey” (ein Land, darin Milch und Honig fließt), an image of abundance drawn from Exodus and Deuteronomy. The second is 1 Peter 2:2, where believers are told to desire “pure spiritual milk” (lautere Milch) as newborn infants. From these passages Milch acquired a settled double character: bodily food and elementary doctrine. In the eighteenth century Schiller exploited the softer side of that inheritance in Wilhelm Tell (1804), where the phrase Milch der frommen Denkart — “the milk of pious disposition” — describes a peaceful temper as milk turned to dragon's venom. The line became a quotation-book commonplace and gave the word a permanent literary association with sentimental gentleness.

Science, industry and the Milky Way. The scientific revolution added new compounds. Milchstraße, the Milky Way, is a loan-translation of Latin via lactea and Greek galaxías kyklos, literally the “milky circle”; the German word treats the galaxy as a road or street of milk. Chemistry produced Milchzucker (lactose) and Milchsäure (lactic acid), and dairying produced Magermilch, Vollmilch, Kondensmilch and Milchpulver. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word became inseparable from public health, infant feeding, refrigeration and agricultural policy. The Milchmädchenrechnung, the naive calculation of the milkmaid who counts her profits before she has sold the milk, turned the household word into a warning against wishful economics.

Contemporary extension. The latest chapter is commercial and legal. Cosmetics use Milch for white lotions (Sonnenmilch, Reinigungsmilch), and the boom in plant-based drinks has produced Hafermilch, Sojamilch, Mandelmilch and Reismilch. Because EU labelling rules reserve the dairy designation for products of animal origin, manufacturers often print Haferdrink while shoppers continue to say Hafermilch. The tension is a textbook case of semantic change outrunning regulation: the metaphor of whiteness and function is more powerful than the legal definition. Through all these shifts the ancient root remains audible, and the word still carries the double inheritance of the dairy and the nursery — nourishment, whiteness, mildness and, when the metaphor turns cynical, the ease with which the giving of milk can be turned into the milking of a cow.
