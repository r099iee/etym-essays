grunting — the present participle of the verb to grunt, "to make the short, low, guttural throat-sound typical of pigs and of brute effort" — is used in this passage to dismiss a whole category of human speech: "Men whose speech sounds like the grunting of apes." In this literary context the word's tone is dehumanizing and animalistic. The speaker refuses the men the status of language-users; their words are reduced to reflex noise, bodily and unarticulated, the sound of effort rather than of meaning. The nuance is not mere mockery but a cold taxonomic contempt — the grunt sits below speech the way appetite sits below thought — and the word carries a faint shudder of the ape-house, of the zoo, of the primitive. It converts a linguistic difference (foreign tongues) into a biological difference (animal bodies), and so it does double rhetorical work: it alienates the foreign sailors and, by contrast, reassures the speaker of his own humanity and articulation. There is also, quietly, an admission of menace — the ape-like sound belongs to a threat one cannot converse with.

Primary sense: the verb grunt names the short, low, guttural sound produced deep in the throat, characteristic of pigs and, by extension, of humans under strain. As a present participle, grunting names the act itself or its audible result — a continuous or repeated low throat-noise.

  • Animal sense: the pig's characteristic noise; also the croak of the grunt-fish (a family of fish named for the noise they make when hauled from water) and the harsh call of certain birds.
  • Human effort sense: the involuntary sound of physical strain — the weightlifter's grunt, the labourer's grunt under a load — a noise of the body, not of the mind.
  • Human speech sense: an inarticulate utterance that has abandoned words; a wordless acknowledgment (a grunt of assent) or the reflex sound of displeasure. This is the sense at work in the passage.
  • Technical/jargon senses: in military slang a "grunt" is a low-ranking infantryman (U.S. Vietnam-era), and "grunt work" is menial labour; in computing, "Grunt" is a JavaScript build tool, and a "grunt" can be a low-level worker process.
  • Metaphoric shifts: the word has drifted from sound to social rank — the "grunt" is the lowest member of a hierarchy, the one who does the bodily work — and from noise to meaninglessness: "grunting" speech is speech stripped of mind, the speaker's depreciation of others as mere animals.

Boundary words sharpen the effect. Groan is longer, sustained, and implies pain, sorrow, or protest — the sound of the sufferer; grunt is short, explosive, breathless, the sound of effort, impact, or brute acknowledgment, and is rarely an appeal for sympathy. Snort forces air through the nose and implies contempt, mockery, or a horse's alarm; the grunt stays in the throat and is unconscious. Mumble is indistinct but remains intended as speech, mouthing words badly, whereas grunting has abandoned words entirely. Growl is threatening, low, and angry, aimed at another — the grunt is self-contained, the by-product of force. Squeal and gurgle sit at the other ends of the vocal range, the one high and panicked, the other liquid and throaty. What distinguishes grunt from all of these is its brevity and its refusal of communication: it is the noise of the body acting, not of the person addressing anyone.

In collocation the word is vivid and concrete: "to grunt with effort", "a grunt of pain", "grunting pigs", "grunting and groaning", "grunt work", "a grunt in the army." The register is plain, physical, and slightly coarse, which makes it perfect for the dehumanizing rhetoric of the passage — and explains why the speaker reaches for it to describe speech he regards as animal rather than human.
