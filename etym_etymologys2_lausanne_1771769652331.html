Lausanne is a toponym (a place-name), not a common noun, so it has no PIE vocabulary root in the way that ordinary English words do; the correct derivation is a chain of attested place-name forms, with scholarly debate noted rather than resolved by guesswork.

Celtic local settlement name on the north shore of Lake Geneva (region of the Helvetii; pre-Roman, exact Celtic base debated — often associated by scholars with a local watercourse or locative formation, e.g. the Flon stream, but the precise morphemes are uncertain) → Latinized Lousonna / Lousonnae (a significant Roman vicus on the lake; attested in Roman itineraries and by archaeology, 1st–3rd c. CE) → Medieval Latin Lausanna / Lausanne (graphic and phonetic evolution of the Roman name in local Latinity, early medieval onward) → Old French / Franco-Provençal transmission in the region of Vaud (medieval) → French Lausanne (fixed medieval–modern form) → English Lausanne (unchanged loan of the French/local form; used in English from the 16th–18th c., e.g. in travel and biographical writing; the proper noun entered English as a foreign place-name and was naturalized without adaptation).

Note on the debate / nuance: The pre-Roman base of Lousonna is not transparently analyzable. Earlier scholars proposed a Celtic hydronymic or locative element, but modern toponymic research treats the exact Celtic morphology as uncertain and does not claim a secure PIE etymology for it. The chain from Lousonna to Lausanne is secure as a continuous name-history; the deeper pre-Roman stage is a fossil about which responsible scholarship states uncertainty explicitly. Do not merge the name with unrelated Latin words (e.g. lux "light" or laus "praise") that merely resemble it; they have no connection to this toponym.
