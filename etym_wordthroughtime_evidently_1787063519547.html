- 14th c. (Scholastic Latin & Middle English) - Borrowed via Old French from Latin *ēvidēns* / *ēvidenter*, meaning "clearly visible to the eye, manifest in physical reality."
- 16th c. (Legal & Forensic codification) - Employed in English common law and philosophical treatises to describe conclusions established through demonstrable testimony and proof.
- 18th c. (Enlightenment empiricism) - Broadened in scientific discourse to denote deductive conclusions drawn from rigorous empirical observation.
- 19th c. (Narrative & psychological inference) - Widely used in Victorian novels (Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes) to narrate deduction from physical clues.
- Contemporary (Conversational epistemic marker) - Operates in modern speech both as a sign of empirical deduction and as an epistemic adverb expressing mild assumption or ironic obviousness.
