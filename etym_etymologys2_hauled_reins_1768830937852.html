Chain for HAULED: - PIE *kelh₁- / *kel- 'to call, summon; to drive, urge, cause to move' — the source also of Latin calare 'to call' and English call, hold, and (via the 'drive' sense) some verbs of movement; the exact root and its primary sense are debated — see note. - Proto-Germanic *halōną 'to call, fetch, summon; to drag, draw' — the verb from which Old English halian 'to bring, fetch, drag' and the later nautical sense descend. - Frankish / Old High German halon, holon 'to fetch, bring, drag, draw' — the Continental Germanic form borrowed into early Romance. - Old French haler 'to pull, haul, drag' (12th c.) — the direct source of the English verb. - Middle English halen 'to pull, drag, haul' (14th c.). - Modern English haul, past tense hauled. Note on debate: The origin of Old French haler is disputed. Most authorities (OED, Etymonline) trace it to the Germanic family of 'to call, summon, fetch,' with the sense shifting from 'summon' to 'draw toward one' to 'drag'; others derive it from Old Norse hala 'to haul, drag,' related to a PIE *kel- 'to drive, urge.' Either way the word is Germanic in origin, and the 'drag' sense is native to the Germanic verb, not an English invention; only the deepest PIE root and its primary sense remain uncertain. Chain for REINS: - PIE *ten- 'to stretch' — the oldest reconstructable source of the notion of holding via stretching out; the same root gives Latin tendere 'to stretch' and English thin, tender, tense. - Latin tenēre 'to hold, keep, grasp' (1st c. BCE onward) — from the stretch-sense: to hold something out and keep hold of it. - Latin retinēre 'to hold back, retain, restrain' — compound of re- 'back' + tenēre 'to hold'; the root that also gives English retain, retention. - Latin retina 'a band, cord, leash, bridle-rein' — a noun derived from retinēre, literally 'that which holds back.' - Old French resne / reine (later also reigne) 'rein' (11th-12th c.) — the direct source of the English word. - Middle English rene / reyne / reyn 'rein' (14th c.), plural reynes. - Modern English reins (plural) — the form in which the word is almost always used. Note on nuance/debate: Some authorities note a parallel influence of Old French reigne/reigne 'kingdom, rule' (from Latin regnum) and of regere 'to rule' on the medieval spelling, a folk-etymological interference that reinforced the 'rule' senses. The plural is idiomatic, the matched pair conceived as a single instrument; the -eins spelling settles in the 16th c.
