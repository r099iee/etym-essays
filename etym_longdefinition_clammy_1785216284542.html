Clammy is an adjective meaning unpleasantly damp, cold, and sticky to the touch — a moisture that clings to the skin rather than dripping, felt as a cold, soft adhesive wetness. In The Hobbit, the word is a moment of involuntary sensory truth: Bilbo, fumbling for the answer to the riddle of the hand, touches his own flesh and recoils — "Ugh!" he said, "it is cold and clammy!" — and so guesses the answer. The nuance is tactile revulsion: clammy does not mean simply wet (that would be damp or drenched); it names a moisture that is cold, adhesive, and unclean — neither dry nor cleanly wet, clinging rather than dripping. It is the classic word for the skin of fear, fever, and nausea, and in the scene the disgust transfers from the hand to the riddle itself: the very flesh that feels is the answer, and Bilbo's revulsion is at once at the clamminess and at the uncanny discovery that the answer has lain under his hand all along.

Core meaning. Clammy fuses two physical qualities — coldness and clinging dampness — into a single disagreeable sensation. A clammy surface is cold to the touch and slightly adhesive, with the soft, unpleasant wetness of sweat or dew rather than the clean wetness of rain.

Exhaustive polysemy.

  • Of skin — the body under stress (most common sense). Clammy skin, a clammy palm, a clammy forehead, a clammy hand: the cold, damp, slightly sticky moisture of fear, anxiety, fever, nausea, shock, or faintness. In medical and first-aid usage, clammy skin is a recognized warning sign (of shock, hypoglycaemia, heat exhaustion), and "cold and clammy" is almost a formula of the clinical and the Gothic alike. The bridge from the core sense is the skin's own cold sweat, which clings like clay.
  • Of things — cold damp surfaces. A clammy wall, a clammy cellar, clammy linen, a clammy stone: cold, damp surfaces that feel adhesive and unpleasant to the touch. The sense grows naturally from the Middle English "sticky, adhesive" meaning of clammy (of earth, of hands), with coldness added from the 16th–17th centuries.
  • Of weather and air. A clammy mist, a clammy morning, clammy air: weather that is damp and chill, the cold wetness of fog, drizzle, or a sea mist. Here the word describes an atmosphere that clings to the skin as if the air itself were damp cloth.
  • Figurative and metaphoric shifts. "A clammy dread", "a clammy feeling", "a clammy atmosphere", "something clammy in his manner": the word extends to anything that produces the physical sensation of unease — creeping fear, revulsion, the horror of the uncanny. A "clammy" situation is one that makes the skin prickle with cold discomfort; a "clammy" silence is oppressive and unsettling. This is the Gothic sense, beloved of horror and sensation fiction, where "cold clammy dread" is a stock phrase.
  • Medical and diagnostic use (technical). In medicine, "clammy" describes skin that is cool, pale, and moist — the classic presentation of shock and of certain fevers; it is a descriptive symptom, not a diagnosis. The technical register preserves the word's core: the cold, clinging sweat of the body under threat.
  • Culinary and physical negatives. In less common extended use, food or substances can be "clammy" when unpleasantly soft, sticky, and cold (a clammy dough, clammy bread) — the sense of soft adhesion without pleasantness.

Distinctions and near-synonyms. Clammy is set apart from damp and moist (neutral, and for moist often positive — a moist cake is good), soggy (soaked through, of food or ground), sticky (adhesive from sugar, gum, or resin — not necessarily cold), sweaty (explicitly covered in perspiration), slimy (wet and oozing, actively disgusting), dank (wet, cold, and musty — chiefly of places), and viscous/glutinous (technical words for thick adhesive fluids). Clammy uniquely fuses coldness with clinging dampness — a wetness that sticks — and it is this combination of chill and adhesion that makes the word the signature of revulsion.

Historical semantic bridge. The word is formed from Middle English clam "mud, sticky earth" plus the adjectival suffix -y, and its deep family includes clay, glue, and glutinous — all from an ancient root meaning "to stick together". At its root, then, clammy means "like clay: soft, sticky, adhesive". The modern sense adds coldness and unpleasantness: from the 15th-century "sticky to the touch", the word concentrated (16th–17th c.) on cold, unpleasantly moist contact — clammy skin, clammy air, clammy dread — until the "cold and clammy" of fear and fever became, as in Bilbo's riddle, a single unmistakable sensation of revulsion.
