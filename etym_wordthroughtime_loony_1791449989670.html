- 13th century: lunatic enters English from Latin lūnāticus, “moon-struck,” naming periodic insanity and epilepsy — a learned medical and legal category.
- 16th–18th centuries: lunacy is a legal status; “lunatic” is a formal, sometimes compassionate, sometimes stigmatizing word for a person whose mind is ruled by the moon.
- 1850s: loony appears as a slang clipping of lunatic — casual, colloquial, and faintly dismissive, used in asylum and street talk.
- Early 20th century: loony bin (1919) and cartoon Looney Tunes (1930) spread the word through popular culture, giving it a comic, childish colour.
- Mid–late 20th century: the adjective generalizes from clinical insanity to any wild foolishness (“a loony idea”); British politics gives us “the loony left.”
- 21st century: mainly informal and hyperbolic, often playful among friends but recognized as stigmatizing toward mental illness; formal prose prefers “absurd,” “reckless,” or “eccentric.”
