PIE *reuh₁- "open space, room" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of space opened out and made available; genuine cognate in Avestan ravah-, "open space, open field."
│
└──▶ Proto-Germanic *rūmaz "space, room, open place" — the same root that gives Old English rūm, Old Norse rūm, Old High German rūm, and Modern English room.
────▶ Frankish *rūm "space" — loaned into Gallo-Romance.
────────▶ Old French rimer "to put in place, to stow"; prefixed arrimer "to stow cargo in a ship's hold" (a- "to" + rimer).
────────────▶ Old / Middle French arrimage "stowage of cargo in a ship's hold."
────────────────▶ English rummage (noun) "the arranging or stowing of a ship's cargo" (first attested 1520s).
────────────────────▶ English rummage (verb) "to search a ship for contraband" (1560s); rummage (noun) "a customs search of a vessel" (1570s).
────────────────────────▶ English rummage (verb) "to search messily, to turn everything over" (broadened from the 18th century).
────────────────────────────▶ Modern English RUMMAGED (past tense and past participle; Tolkien, 1937).

Note on debate: the reconstruction of the PIE root is not perfectly secure: some scholars write it *reuh₁- "to open, make room," others *rew(h)-, and while the connection to the Germanic "room" family is generally accepted, the exact ablaut grade is debated. The stages from Frankish onward are fully attested. The customs-search senses (verb 1560s, noun 1570s) grew directly out of the cargo-stowage sense of the same noun, as the customs officer's examination of the stowed cargo took the name of what he searched.
