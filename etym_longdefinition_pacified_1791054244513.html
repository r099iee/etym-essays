Pacified is the past tense and past participle of pacify, meaning made calm, quieted, appeased or brought under control. In this passage it is a stark euphemism for killed by drowning: Australian hunters swimming with a reed snorkel seized ducks by the legs, pulled them under and gently held them there till they were pacified — that is, till they stopped struggling and lay still in death. In short, pacified here means subdued into motionlessness by drowning.

Outside this hunting context the word covers a wide spectrum from tender soothing to military subjugation, and that doubleness is its defining polysemy. To pacify a baby is to soothe it to sleep; to pacify a province is to conquer and disarm it; to pacify anger is to appease emotion; to pacify opponents in debate is to conciliate them. All share the core schema of turning agitation into calm, differing only in means — rocking, reasoning, bargaining or force — and in moral colour.

Exhaustive senses and bridges:

  • 1. Core appeasement sense — to calm anger or agitation: to pacify fears, wrath, conscience or a storm; implies active soothing by words, gestures or remedies; the historical bridge is Latin pax as fastened treaty — agitation is unfastened strife, pacifying re-fastens agreement.
  • 2. Conciliation sense — to reconcile opponents: to pacify quarrelling parties, factions or creditors; implies mediation and concession; differs from mere calming by aiming at lasting settlement rather than momentary quiet.
  • 3. Military-administrative sense — to subdue and govern: to pacify a region, tribe, frontier or village; means to suppress resistance by force or threat and impose order; inherits directly from Roman pacare provincias; the bridge is peace-through-victory, where calm follows disarmament.
  • 4. Hunting euphemism sense — as in this passage: to hold ducks under till pacified equals to drown quietly without splash; the bridge is motionlessness as calm: dead ducks no longer thrash, so they count as pacified; the adverb gently heightens the irony by framing killing as careful technique to avoid alarming the flock.
  • 5. Infant-care sense — to soothe to sleep: to pacify a crying child by rocking, feeding or giving a pacifier (dummy teat); the noun pacifier lexicalises this in American English; the bridge is oral soothing as peace-making with the infant body.
  • 6. Psychological and medical extension: to pacify anxiety with reassurance or sedation; overlaps with sedate, tranquilise but retains a verbal-moral tone rather than purely pharmacological one; a pacified patient is cooperative, not merely unconscious.
  • 7. Political euphemism and counter-insurgency: pacified areas, pacification programmes; 20th-century coinage for villages cleared of guerrillas and counted as loyal; the bridge is statistical calm — absence of incidents — presented as consent; nowStudiously avoided by careful writers because of Vietnam- and Algeria-era associations.
  • 8. Adjectival resultative use: a pacified crowd, a pacified province, ducks pacified; denotes the state after action, calm and compliant; often carries residual irony because the calm was imposed rather than chosen.

Register ranges from intimate (pacify a baby) through diplomatic (pacify creditors) to brutally official (pacified zone). Tone depends entirely on object: with emotions the word is warm, with peoples it is cold, with ducks in this Edwardian anthropology it is coolly technical. Recognising which pole is active — soothing versus subduing — is the key to reading any instance, and here the underwater grip leaves no doubt that subduing has shaded into killing.
