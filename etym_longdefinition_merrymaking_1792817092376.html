Merrymaking is the activity of celebrating joyfully with others: feasting, drinking, dancing, singing, and laughing in company, the collective business of having a good time. Where mirth names the feeling and merriment names the atmosphere, merrymaking names the doing, the gerund's -ing keeping the emphasis on action in progress: people are merrymaking out on the field right now, audible from afar. In the source sentence Bilbo hears the sounds of merrymaking in other parts of the field, and the word choice tells us the celebration has spilled beyond the pavilion into the open air, a whole landscape faintly ringing with other people's happiness, which he enjoys at one remove with a host's quiet satisfaction.

The word's range runs from the innocent to the rowdy, and the surrounding context decides where on that spectrum a given instance falls. At its gentlest it means wholesome festivity: Christmas merrymaking, wedding merrymaking, the village fete's games and dances, where families and neighbours rejoice together and no disapproval attaches. At its roughest it shades toward revelry and carousing: late-night merrymaking that keeps the street awake, tavern merrymaking fuelled past midnight, where the same joy becomes someone else's nuisance. The semantic bridge is volume plus drink: celebration expressed loudly and lengthily, especially with alcohol, slides from charming to objectionable without changing its nature, only its hour and its witnesses, so that one generation's merrymaking is the next morning's apology.

Grammatically the word is a compound gerund, merry plus making, and that structure explains both its warmth and its slight old-fashionedness. To make merry is one of English's oldest festive idioms, recorded since Middle English, and the fused noun preserves the idiom's biblical and ballad flavour: it sounds like carols, morris dances, and Shakespearean clowns rather than nightclubs and playlists. Modern speakers reaching for the contemporary equivalent say partying, celebrating, or having fun, all cooler and more neutral; choosing merrymaking instead wraps the event in nostalgia, archaism, or literary colour. Journalists exploit this deliberately, writing of New Year's Eve merrymaking to dignify street crowds with a storybook glow, and novelists of fantasy use it constantly for taverns, feasts, and festivals that should feel timeless rather than modern.

Related senses and collocations complete the picture. Merrymaker names the person, one of the celebrants, slightly comic in tone, as if the word itself were smiling: the merrymakers spilled out into the square. The adjective merry contributes its own penumbra of senses, from cheerful to tipsy: a merry evening may mean only a happy one, but to get merry has meant to get drunk since the sixteenth century, and that alcoholic undertow tugs gently at merrymaking too. The fixed phrase eat, drink, and be merry, echoing the biblical invitation to feasting, stands behind countless uses, lending the word a sanction of ancient wisdom: merrymaking is not frivolity but the proper human answer to harvest, wedding, and homecoming, joy taken communally and aloud.

For the learner, the practical rule is register: use merrymaking when the occasion should feel traditional, hearty, and communal, and prefer celebration or partying for modern neutral description. Its plural is rare but possible, the merrymakings of the season, usually with an arch or journalistic wink. It governs the prepositions of and among loosely, sounds of merrymaking, merrymaking among the tenants, and it pairs naturally with verbs of hearing from a distance, since the word so often describes joy perceived across space: heard, distant, echoing merrymaking is almost a formula, placing the listener outside the circle looking fondly in, exactly where Bilbo stands on his birthday night.

  • Joyful celebration (core sense): festive activity in company, as in sounds of merrymaking; the central meaning, built directly on the idiom to make merry nominalized into an ongoing collective action.
  • Wholesome festivity (approving sense): innocent communal rejoicing, as in Christmas merrymaking; bridged by tradition, where harvest, wedding, and holiday customs sanctify the noise.
  • Rowdy revelry (disapproving edge): loud late celebration, as in drunken merrymaking; bridged by excess, the same joy revalued once volume, hour, or drink offend the neighbours.
  • Literary and nostalgic colouring (register sense): deliberately old-world festivity, as in fantasy taverns and New Year's Eve merrymaking; bridged by the word's ballad flavour, chosen to make events feel timeless.
  • Merrymaker (agent sense): one who takes part, the merrymakers danced till dawn; the compact personal noun the gerund naturally spawns for its participants.
  • Tipsy undertow (associated sense): the alcoholic hint inherited from merry and make merry; bridged by centuries of feasting idiom in which wine and joy arrive together.
