- [Old English (pre-10th c.)] - līhtan (past: līhte) means to dismount from a horse, disburden oneself of weight, or descend to earth - [perception: essential equestrian and military action of warriors and riders]
- [Middle English (12th–14th c.)] - lighten / lit expands from dismounting horses to birds descending softly onto branches ("lit upon a bough"); the separate verb līehtan (to ignite) begins sharing the past form lit - [perception: lyrical, avian, capturing both physical landing and hearth fires]
- [16th–18th c.] - Early Modern English grammarians debate lit versus lighted; idiomatic phrases like light upon (to discover unexpectedly) become established in literary prose - [perception: standard literary and conversational past tense]
- [19th c.] - Victorian literature (Wilde, Tennyson) uses lit for the graceful landing of birds and the radiance of faces - [perception: elegant, concise, rhythmically nimble aesthetic standard]
- [20th–21st c.] - slang expansions: early 20th-century jazz slang for intoxicated ("lit up"), evolving in 21st-century youth culture into an adjective for wildly exciting and vibrant - [perception: dynamic, multi-register word spanning ancient literature and modern slang]
