Part of speech and denotation. Bastille is a proper noun naming a specific building: the fortress on the eastern edge of Paris, in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine, whose capture by a revolutionary crowd on 14 July 1789 became the emblematic act of the French Revolution. The name is also used, with a lower-case b and in older texts, as a common noun meaning “fortified tower, small fortress, bastion”—the architectural sense from which the building took its name.

What the building was. The Bastille was begun in 1370 and completed about 1383 under Charles V, as part of the new wall enclosing Paris. It guarded the Porte Saint-Antoine, the gate through which traffic from the east entered the city, and it was built as a detached fortification—a bastille in the military sense—rather than as a royal residence. It had eight towers, a moat, drawbridges and thick walls, and its silhouette dominated the approach to Paris from Vincennes.

The prison. From the seventeenth century the fortress was increasingly used as a state prison. Under Louis XIII and Louis XIV it received prisoners held by lettre de cachet—a royal warrant that bypassed ordinary courts—and it became notorious as the place where the king could imprison anyone without charge or trial. Its most famous inmate was Voltaire, confined there twice, in 1717 and 1726. The Marquis de Sade was held there from 1784 until a few days before the storming. Conditions varied enormously with rank and money, but the idea of the Bastille mattered more than its practice: it stood for arbitrary power.

The fall. On 14 July 1789, with the Estates-General in deadlock and troops gathering around Paris, a crowd estimated in the hundreds and then the thousands converged on the fortress, chiefly to seize the gunpowder stored there. After hours of confusion and negotiation, the governor, the Marquis de Launay, surrendered; he was killed by the crowd, and his head was carried through the streets. The prison held only seven prisoners at the time—four convicted forgers, two men confined as insane, and one nobleman—a fact that later historians would use to puncture the myth. The myth proved stronger than the fact.

Why it matters. The fall of the Bastille was read across Europe as the collapse of despotism. It gave the Revolution a date, a place and an image; it entered the calendar, the iconography and the rhetoric of every subsequent revolution. The building was demolished between 1789 and 1790, and its stones were worked into souvenirs and memorial objects, some carved with a plan of the fortress. The site became the Place de la Bastille; the July Column, commemorating the revolution of 1830, was raised there in 1840; the Opéra Bastille opened on the square in 1989 for the bicentenary. Since 1880, 14 July has been the French national holiday, known in English as Bastille Day.

Metaphorical and cultural life. “Storming the Bastille” has become a general metaphor for a symbolic assault on an entrenched institution, and “Bastille” alone can stand for any prison of arbitrary power. The building also has a literary afterlife: Carlyle devotes a celebrated chapter of The French Revolution (1837) to its fall, written in his present-tense, participatory manner, and Dickens opens the revolutionary plot of A Tale of Two Cities (1859) with the release of Doctor Manette from the Bastille after eighteen years of unjust imprisonment.

In this reading. Carlyle writes as though he and the reader were “observers, indeed almost participants, on the streets of Paris at the fall of the Bastille.” The phrase treats the event as a scene one could stand inside—a deliberate rejection of the detached historiography exemplified by Gibbon. The Bastille is the perfect object for that technique: an image so charged that the reader needs no explanation to feel its weight.

Key facts at a glance

  • Built: 1370 to about 1383, under Charles V, to guard the Porte Saint-Antoine.
  • Function: fortress, then state prison holding prisoners by lettre de cachet.
  • Stormed: 14 July 1789, chiefly to seize the gunpowder stored there; seven prisoners were found inside.
  • Demolished: 1789–1790; the stones became relics and building material.
  • Commemorated: Place de la Bastille, the July Column (1840), the Opéra Bastille (1989), and the French national holiday since 1880.
