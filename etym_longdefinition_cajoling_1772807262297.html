In this passage from Dream of the Red Chamber, cajoling names the soft, sustained pressure of speech used on the terrified girl Ying-lian: not a barked order, not a legal interrogation, but patient coaxing that wears down fear until confession and tears break through. Paired with "persuading," it paints a technique of emotional management — kind tone, repeated appeals, flattery or reassurance — aimed at drawing a frightened person into cooperation. The literary tone is compassionate yet instrumental: the speaker "kept on at her," so cajoling here includes persistence, not a single sweet phrase. It is soft power applied to a traumatised child who must be coaxed past silence and terror of her kidnapper.

To cajole is to persuade by coaxing and flattery — sustained, artful, sweet-talking pressure aimed at getting someone to do what one wants. The participle cajoling, as here, highlights ongoing process: the repeated, soothing, wheedling speech that continues until resistance breaks. The word always implies speech (usually gentle in tone, whatever its purpose) and a goal: cajoling is persuasion with charm as its instrument. The method may be warm and protective, or sly and self-interested; the manner is constant — softness repeated until the will yields.

Polysemy — range of senses and shades.
  • Persuade by flattery/coaxing (core): "cajole someone into doing something"; the standard transitive construction in which the object is drawn toward an action by sweet speech.
  • Soften a reluctant or frightened person (protective shade): the novel's sense — patient, kind coaxing of a child past fear; ethically neutral or warm, a use in which the coaxer's aim is the other's own good.
  • Wheedle, sweet-talk, with self-interest (pejorative shade): when the speaker's motive is manipulation — "he cajoled her out of her money"; here the word approaches inveigle and begins to condemn.
  • Chatter, prattle (historical/obsolete): the verb's earliest French sense, "to chatter like a bird in a cage"; now lost in English but the root of the whole acoustic image.
  • As adjective of manner: a "cajoling tone/voice," describing wheedling sweetness of manner; the participle used attributively to qualify the delivery of speech.
  • Gerundial noun: "endless cajoling," naming the act or habit itself; the abstract noun cajolery is the same idea as a practice, a stock of sweet-talking devices.

The bridge runs from the caged bird's chatter (Old French dialectal cageoler — to prattle like a jay, or to chatter at a bird to lure it) → the bird-like coaxing speech that soothes or entraps → any flattering, wheedling persuasion → the modern psychological sense of gentle-but-persistent social pressure. The word kept its acoustic, bird-cage flavour for centuries before settling into pure persuasion-talk.

Coaxing is the closest synonym: gentle urging, often physical or verbal, but less automatically charged with flattery; cajoling usually implies artful, sweet-talking persuasion. Wheedling stresses sly, sometimes self-interested flattery and can sound more manipulative or childish. Flattering is praise alone and need not aim at getting someone to act; cajoling always has a persuasive goal. Inveigling suggests enticing into something unwise or improper by deception — darker than the protective rescue tone of this scene. Urging or pressuring can be blunt or moral; they lack the bird-cage charm historically associated with cajole. Sweet-talking is the colloquial synonym, blunter and more obvious in its intent.

Typical collocations: cajole someone into doing something, cajoling tone, cajole with promises/flattery, stop cajoling me. Register is standard literary and conversational English — slightly more bookish than plain "talk into," less formal than "induce." The present participle cajoling highlights ongoing process, which matches the narrative's repeated effort until the girl breaks down and cries.
