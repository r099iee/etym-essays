Throes (plural of throe) are violent, often prolonged pangs or spasms of pain — most familiarly the convulsions of a dying creature ("death throes") or the labour pains of childbirth ("birth throes", "labour throes"). The word is almost never used in the singular: agony, in the English imagination behind this word, comes in waves and convulsions, not as a single point, and so throe survives chiefly as the tail of its plural. In the source sentence, "His last throes splintered it to sparks and gledes", the dying dragon Smaug's final convulsions are violent enough to shatter wood into glowing splinters — the word carries the full weight of a great creature's death-agony, and its plurals multiply the violence: not one pang but a storm of them.

1. Core senses.

  • Death throes: the convulsive final struggle of a dying person or animal — "the death throes of the whale", "he watched the wounded beast's throes". Since the early modern period the collocation "death-throes" has been fixed, and it is now applied as readily to institutions and regimes as to bodies.
  • Birth throes: the pangs and contractions of childbirth — "the throes of labour". The pairing of the two great bodily struggles, birth and death, made the plural natural: both are wave-like, recurrent, overwhelming; both end in a transition that cannot be refused.
  • General agony: intense pain or distress of any kind, archaic or literary — "in his last throes", "racked with throes".

2. The figurative idiom: "in the throes of". From the 19th century the word made a decisive abstract leap: to be "in the throes of" something is to be helplessly caught in the middle of a violent, painful or all-consuming process. A nation is in the throes of revolution or civil war, a company in the throes of a takeover, an artist in the throes of creation, a schoolchild in the throes of adolescence. The image is always the convulsion: the process grips you as a spasm grips a body, and you cannot step out of it until it is finished.

3. Secondary and figurative extensions.

  • Cultural and political upheaval: "the throes of change", "the throes of a crisis", "the throes of transformation" — standard headline and editorial language for any period of violent transition.
  • Creative and intellectual struggle: "the throes of composition", "in the throes of writing a novel" — the artist's labour treated as a body in labour; the process is painful, absorbing and cannot be hurried.
  • Emotional extremes: "throes of passion", "throes of remorse" — exaggerated, literary, slightly archaic; the word lends intensity and drama to any overwhelming feeling.

4. A caution about homophones. Throes is pronounced exactly like throws (as in "he throws the ball"), and the two are frequently confused in writing — "the last throws of the match" (wrong) versus "the last throes of the match" (the agonising final phase). The -oe- spelling of throe is itself the product of this old confusion: the word was historically spelled throwe, throu, throo and was respelled throe in the 16th–17th centuries on the model of throw, because the death-agony looks like the body throwing itself about. The spelling is thus a fossil of a medieval association, and the homophone remains a standing trap for modern writers.

5. Register and nuance. Literary, dramatic, deliberately heavy. Throes is grander than pains, more physical than agonies, more urgent than suffering. It retains its medieval core — the pang that comes in waves — and adds the modern theatrical overtone of a great struggle reaching its final convulsive phase. In journalism it is a favourite for the dramatic middle of a crisis; in epic fiction (the Tolkien passage) it is exactly right for a dragon's end: enormous, violent, terminal.

6. Summary. Throes are the violent, wave-like pangs of pain, above all of death and childbirth, and by extension any overwhelming convulsive process — a nation, a company, an artist or a dragon in the throes of its last, most violent struggle.
