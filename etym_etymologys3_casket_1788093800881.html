1. The Indo-European root: *kap- “to grasp”. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *kap-, “to grasp, seize, hold”. Its reflexes are spread across the family: in Latin it yields capere “to take, seize, hold” (the source of hundreds of English words from capture, captive, capacity and capable to catch, via Old French), and it is plausibly connected with Germanic *habjaną “to have, hold” (English have, German haben) and with Greek verbs of holding and taking. The root’s fundamental sense of seizing and containing explains everything that follows: a container is, etymologically, a thing that holds.

2. Latin capsa: the box that holds. From capere Latin formed capsa, “a box, chest, case” — literally something that contains. In classical Rome the capsa was above all the cylindrical box in which books and scrolls were carried (the poet Horace speaks of the boy-slave carrying his master’s capsa of books), and the word also named money-boxes, jewel-boxes and boxes of incense. The formation is the ordinary Latin noun in -sa built on the supine stem; it is attested in comedy and satire of the 2nd–1st centuries BCE and remained in learned and popular Latin through antiquity, where it produced the diminutive capsella “little box” and passed into Christian Latin for reliquaries and pyxes. Latin capsa is thus the joint ancestor of English case (via Old French casse / chasse), French caisse and cassette, Italian cassa, and two separate English words: case and casket.

3. Old French casse and its diminutives. In Old French the Latin word became casse “box, chest” (attested from the 12th century), and on this base French formed the diminutive cassette, “a little box”, in the 14th century — the same diminutive suffix -ette that gave English cigarette, kitchenette and the modern audio cassette. English appears to have borrowed the word in the mid-15th century directly from a northern French form pronounced with a hard initial k, and spelled it casket; the OED and Etymonline both note that the exact continental source — Parisian cassette with an s sound, or a northern cassete with k — is not entirely settled, and the English spelling with -k- remains a small unsolved mystery of historical borrowing. (What is certain is that the word is not a native English diminutive of case, though it behaves like one.)

4. The English casket: jewels, relics and romance (15th–18th centuries). From its first attestations in the mid-1400s, English casket meant “a small box for jewels or valuables” — at first especially for jewels, rings and precious objects, soon also for money, letters, relics and documents. It is the word of romances and wills: kings and merchants bequeath their caskets; church treasuries keep reliquary caskets; brides receive caskets of gems. The greatest literary fixing of the word is Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice (c. 1596–98), in which Portia’s suitors must choose among the gold, silver and lead caskets to win her; the leaden casket contains the image of Portia, reversing expectation and making “casket-choice” a permanent fable of substance over show. Throughout the 17th and 18th centuries the word keeps this double character of treasure-chest and moral emblem; Johnson’s Dictionary (1755) defines it simply as “a small chest or box for jewels”.

5. The 19th-century American transformation: the euphemism. The great turning point comes in mid-19th-century America. Faced with enormous death tolls — cholera epidemics and then the Civil War — American undertakers and funeral merchants began to market elaborate burial containers with the softer word casket, reserving coffin for the plainer, blunter article. The transfer, attested from the 1840s onward, is a masterpiece of commercial euphemism: the casket, the most precious box in the language, becomes the last container of a beloved body — treasure has become the dead, the box has become a shrine, and the mortuary industry has acquired a word that dignifies its product. By the late 19th century “open-casket” funerals, casket showrooms and casket catalogues are part of American life, and the usage has hardened into the standard dictionary sense: “in the United States, a coffin, esp. an elaborately ornamented one”. The funeral industry even codified a technical distinction — a casket is rectangular with a hinged lid; a coffin tapers at the shoulders — a difference invisible to most mourners but policed by the trade.

6. The two modern lives. Today the word is cleanly split by the Atlantic. In American English casket is the ordinary, expected word for the burial container — the word of news reports, funerals and war memorials — while the jewel-box sense survives only in historical and literary contexts. In British English the coffin sense is understood as American, and the native weight of the word remains with the treasure-chest: the romance, the reliquary, the strongbox of jewels. It was this older, gleaming sense that Tolkien revived in The Hobbit — the strong casket of iron-bound wood in which the Arkenstone is borne before Bard and the Elvenking is a deliberately archaic treasure-chest, and the word carries six centuries of jewel-box glamour into the scene. Modern commerce has even revived the old sense at street level: jewellery retailers sell “caskets” as keepsake boxes, and the cremation industry offers “cremation caskets” for ashes, quietly bridging the two meanings again.

7. Phonology in brief. Latin capsa (with p before s) passed into Old French as casse, the cluster simplifying to -s-; the English word’s initial c- is pronounced /k/ as in French casse, and the internal -sk- spelling of casket records the northern pronunciation that English borrowed. The diminutive suffix -et (French -ette) gives the word its final unstressed syllable, and the Great Vowel Shift did not touch the short a in the open stressed syllable, so modern casket is pronounced much as it was spelled in the fifteenth century /ˈkɑːskɪt/. The word has never changed its sound machinery — only its cargo, from jewels to the dead and back to keepsakes.
