- 14th c. — Middle English architectural borrowing: adopted from Middle Dutch and Middle Low German trade carpentry as the noun schore (a heavy timber prop, stay, or strut) and verb schoren, used by builders to brace leaning walls and ship hulls.
- 15th–16th c. — Mining and siegecraft expansion: widely utilized across Europe in military engineering (sapping, trenching) and subterranean mining to hold up tunnel roofs and prevent earth collapses.
- 17th–18th c. — Naval dockyard standardization: codified in maritime architecture for drydock bilge blocks, slipways, and shipboard emergency damage control to brace holed bulkheads during naval warfare.
- 19th c. — Emergence of the phrasal verb "shore up": transitioned into general literary English as an established idiom for physically propping sagging structures and repairing dilapidated buildings.
- 20th–21st c. — Macroeconomic and diplomatic hegemony: became an indispensable journalistic staple to describe central bank currency interventions, emergency government bailouts of failing banks, and geopolitical efforts to preserve disintegrating alliances.
