Woden (proper noun) — the Old English name of the chief god of the Anglo-Saxon pagan pantheon, the god of wisdom, war, poetry, magic, inspiration, and the dead, cognate with Old Norse Óðinn (Odin) and Old High German Wuotan (Wotan), and the divine ancestor claimed by the royal genealogies of several Anglo-Saxon dynasties. THE HISTORICAL FIGURE Woden stood at the head of the Old English pantheon much as Odin stood at the head of the Norse one. His domains were the lofty and dangerous ones: battle-frenzy and victory, runic and poetic inspiration, magical knowledge and the shamanic journey to the dead, and the royal charisma that kings claimed by descent from him. The genealogies of the royal houses of Wessex, Kent, Mercia, and others traced their lineage back to Woden, making him the divine progenitor of Anglo-Saxon kingship. The name itself means 'the frenzied one', from a Proto-Germanic adjective *wōdaz 'mad, furious, inspired, possessed' — the god is literally the lord of the divine frenzy that possesses the seer and the battle-mad warrior. That root, PIE *weh₂t- 'to blow, inspire, excite', is the same root that produced Latin vātēs, 'seer, prophet', and English vaticinate, 'to prophesy', so Woden is etymologically the god of breath and inspiration as much as of battle. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Calendar sense — the most durable trace of the god: the weekday name Wednesday, from Old English Wōdnesdæg, 'Woden's day', itself a calque pattern from Latin diēs Mercuriī, 'Mercury's day'. Roman observers, applying the interpretatio romana, equated the Germanic god of speed, cleverness, and communication with the messenger-god Mercury, and the Germanic peoples substituted their own god of the same profile. Woden survives, hidden, in the middle of the modern English week.
  • Royal-genealogy sense — in Anglo-Saxon historical writing, Woden is the legendary ancestor of royal lines (especially Wessex, the dynasty of Alfred and the very kings Macaulay imagines in the passage), so the name also functions as a term for the divine source of Anglo-Saxon kingship.
  • Comparative-religion sense — in modern scholarship Woden is used as the standard English form of the god's name when speaking specifically of Anglo-Saxon paganism, as opposed to Norse Odin or German Wotan; the three are cognate names of one deity.
  • Literary and historical-prose sense — in Macaulay and the historians of the 18th and 19th centuries, Woden appears as a deliberate archaism, the emblem of stubborn, savage Germanic paganism against the Christianity of the converted continental courts; 'the temples of Thor and Woden' is shorthand for pre-Christian English religion.
  • Modern cultural sense — Woden is revived in modern heathenry and popular re-imaginings of Anglo-Saxon England, in place-names (e.g., Wensley, Wednesbury, Wednesfield, and other 'Woden's ...' compounds), and in the general public's familiarity with the god under the Norse name Odin.
  • Philological sense — Woden is a standard citation form in Old English lexicography and etymology, used whenever the English reflex of the god-name is discussed in dictionaries and grammars.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGES The semantic bridge from root to full cultural weight is remarkable. The PIE root *weh₂t- 'to blow, to inspire, to excite' produced, on the Italic-Celtic side, the 'seer' words (Latin vātēs, Gaulish uātis), and on the Germanic side the adjective *wōdaz 'mad, furious, possessed, inspired'. From that adjective the god-name *Wōdanaz was formed — 'the frenzied or inspired one' — and the god of frenzy then attracted the attributes of poetry (inspired utterance), wisdom (the knowledge of the ecstatic seer), magic (the power of the possessed), war (battle-frenzy), and the dead (the shamanic traveller). Each later sense — Wednesday, royal genealogy, antiquarian emblem, modern revival — is a different survival of that single root meaning, the divine breath that blows the world into speech and madness.
