BANNERMEN is a transparent compound of two ancient Germanic words, banner and men, and its history is therefore really the history of its two halves meeting in the feudal and post-feudal world. The first half, banner, travelled a long continental road. The traditional etymology takes it back to a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *bhendh-, “to bind”, the same root that lies behind band and bind; the banner, on this view, is “something bound or tied”, and the idea of binding carried over to the binding of soldiers under a sign. This root stage is uncertain — some scholars prefer simply a Germanic root with no clear Indo-European antecedent — but the Germanic formation is clear: Proto-Germanic *bandwa- or *bandwō meant “a sign, an ensign, a banner”, a derivative of the verb “to bind”.

The continental journey of “banner”.

  • During the early Middle Ages the Germanic word passed into Late Latin as bandum, “a banner, a standard”, adopted by the imperial and then the barbarian armies of Europe.
  • From late Latin bandum came Old French baniere, “a banner, a standard”, which entered Middle English in the thirteenth century as banere.
  • English banner is therefore a word of Germanic origin that returned to English through Latin and French — a typical early medieval migration, in which a Germanic war-word was latinized and then re-borrowed by a Germanic language.

The Germanic descent of “men”. The second half is simpler. The word man descends from Proto-Indo-European *man-, “man, human being” — traditionally, though not certainly, connected to the root *men-, “to think”, which would make a man “the thinking being”. Proto-Germanic had *mann-, Old English mann or mon, Middle English man; the plural men is one of the oldest plural formations in the language, formed by vowel mutation (umlaut) and surviving unchanged into modern English.

How the compound was born. The marriage of the two words was natural. In feudal Europe the banner was the heart of the army: a lord’s men gathered under his banner, and to fight “under the banner of” a lord was to fight as his sworn man. English coined bannerman for the man who bore a banner — a standard-bearer or ensign — and the plural form bannermen names the body of men who rally beneath a standard. Unlike vassal, which stressed land tenure, the banner-word stressed military loyalty: the bannerman is defined not by the land he holds but by the flag he follows.

The historical and institutional senses.

  • The historical English word is bannerman, “a standard-bearer”, attested in feudal and military contexts.
  • A distinct but related medieval rank was the knight banneret, a knight entitled to carry a banner in the field; the formation banneret is separate from bannerman.
  • Independently, the military organization of the Qing dynasty used “bannermen” (Manchu: gūsa) for the hereditary troops of the Eight Banners — a coinage of the same shape from an entirely different tradition.

The modern fantasy revival. In modern English the word has been revitalized by epic fantasy. In A Song of Ice and Fire the great houses of Westeros have “bannermen” — the sworn houses and their armies bound to follow their liege lord’s banner into war — and the word has become the standard term for feudal military followings in fantasy literature, games, and historical fiction. The sentence “Their bannermen are wavering” uses the word in precisely this revived sense: the armed loyalty of subordinate houses is faltering, a political and military crisis expressed through the imagery of banners and sworn men.

Sound and form. The compound has kept the phonology of its parts: banner with its short open vowel from Old French, men with its mutated plural vowel. The word is essentially plural in practical use, and its transparent structure — “men of the banner” — is the reason it can serve equally well in medieval chronicles and in modern fantasy.

Summary. A Germanic sign tied to a pole travelled through Latin and French to become banner; an Indo-European word for the thinking being came down through Germanic and Old English as man; the feudal world tied them together, and modern epic fiction has given their union a new and vivid life.
