- [17th century: Latin scholarly loan] - 'Enumerate' enters English as a learned verb meaning to count or list things one by one; connotation is formal, bookish, and confined to mathematics, law, and scholarship.
- [18th-19th century: administrative spread] - The word enters census-taking, inventories, and official records; to enumerate becomes the precise verb of official counting - a population is enumerated by the state - and connotation is bureaucratic, systematic, and thorough.
- [Modern usage] - 'Enumerate' means to mention a number of things one by one, often with urbane irony when one declines to do so ('it would be superfluous to enumerate'); connotation is still formal and slightly elevated, used where plain 'list' would be too flat.
