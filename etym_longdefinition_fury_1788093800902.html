Primary sense. Fury is intense, violent, often uncontrollable anger — rage in its most explosive form, frequently imagined as a force that seizes a person (“flew into a fury”, “in a fury he smashed the cup”). Unlike mere annoyance or indignation, fury implies loss of restraint and destructive energy; the word carries the physical image of a storm or a beast. In this card's context — “the hatred of their race for the dwarves had been rekindled to fury” — it names the end-state of hatred raised to full destructive intensity: not anger, but a conflagration of emotion.

The mythological sense. Capitalised, the Furies (Furiae in Latin) were the Roman goddesses of vengeance, identified with the Greek Erinyes — the chthonic avenging spirits born, in Hesiod's Theogony, from the blood of the castrated Uranus, who hunted down murderers, perjurers and those who violated kinship and natural order. The Latin name means “the Raging Ones”. The singular “a fury” is a furious, avenging woman — a raging, violent female figure — and this usage explains the word's long association with feminine wrath in drama and fiction, from Aeschylus's Eumenides to the melodramas that put “a fury” on stage as the raging heroine or villainess.

Force of nature. By transparent metaphor, fury extends to the violence of impersonal forces: “the fury of the storm”, “the fury of the battle”, “the fury of the waves”. This sense, common from the 17th century on, treats natural catastrophe as an angry agent — a personification inherited from classical poetry's storm gods.

Secondary and modern senses:

  • Furor / furore: Latin furor “madness, frenzy” and its Italian descendant furore (“a craze, public excitement”) are closely related borrowings; “fury” and “furor” are doublets from the same Latin family (furere, “to rage”).
  • Collocations and idioms: “fly into a fury”, “in a fury”, “blind fury”, “fury at/over” (journalistic), “the fury of the elements”, and the colloquial “like fury” (“with great violence”, 19th c.).
  • Proper-name uses: Nick Fury, the Marvel Comics spymaster (1963), and “Fury”, the British nickname for the American M10 tank destroyer of World War II (revived by the 2014 film Fury), show the word's modern career as a tough label for righteous violence.
  • Contemporary rhetoric: “righteous fury”, “public fury”, “internet fury” — modern usage often grants fury moral legitimacy, describing justified collective anger at injustice, a shift from the older view of fury as blind madness; the 2015 film Mad Max: Fury Road sums up this reclamation of the word as a title of honour.

Etymology and semantics in brief. From Latin furia “rage, frenzy” (and the plural Furiae, the avenging goddesses), from furere “to rage, be mad”, the verb behind furious (Latin furiōsus). The word entered English in the 14th century via Old French furie. The semantic bridge from goddess to emotion is the oldest fact about the word: fury was never merely a feeling but a personified, divine force that pursued and punished — which is why the word still suggests anger that seems to come from outside the self.
