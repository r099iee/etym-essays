Two morphemes compose the word: cynic + -ism. Each is traced, together with the full chain. Chain 1 — the core “cynic”: - PIE *ḱuṓn “dog” (genitive *ḱunós, stem *ḱun-; well attested across the family: Greek kýōn, Latin canis, Sanskrit śván, Old English hund) → Greek kýōn “dog” (attested in Homer) → Greek kynikós “doglike, of a dog, currish” (adjective in -ikós from kýōn; first the epithet of the philosophical school) → the school-name: the followers of Antisthenes and Diogenes, called Kynikoí “the Cynics, the dog-men” (the nickname explained in antiquity both by Diogenes' shameless, “dog-like” plainness and by the gymnasium Kynósarges “White Dog” where Antisthenes taught) → Latin cynicus “of the Cynics; a Cynic philosopher” (Classical Latin) → English cynic (noun/adjective, 16th century, first recorded c. 1540) “a follower of the ancient Cynic school” → English cynic + -ism = cynicism (17th century, first recorded 1674) “the doctrine of the Cynics” → the modern sense “a disposition to distrust human motives” develops in the 18th–19th centuries → Modern English CYNICISM. Chain 2 — the suffix -ism: - PIE verbal/adjectival suffix *-ye- (forming denominative verbs, “to act as …”) and the abstract-noun suffix *-mos (of which the exact PIE shape is only generally reconstructable) → Greek -ízein (denominative verb suffix, “to do, to act in the manner of”) + -mos → Greek -ismós (abstract noun of practice, doctrine, or attitude) → Latin -ismus → English -ism “doctrine, practice, attitude,” as in cynicism. Note on debate: the origin of the name “Cynics” is itself debated in antiquity: ancient sources offer two explanations — the shameless “dog-like” lifestyle of Diogenes (with the story that he proclaimed himself “a citizen of the world” and barked at convention) and the gymnasium of Kynósarges where Antisthenes taught. Both are reported in Diogenes Laertius; modern scholarship regards both as possible and notes that the school may have embraced the dog-name defiantly. The PIE root *ḱuṓn “dog” is one of the most secure reconstructions in the field. The shift from “Cynic philosophy” to “general distrust of motives” is English-internal and undisputed in its broad outline, though its precise 18th-century trigger is a matter of scholarly discussion.
