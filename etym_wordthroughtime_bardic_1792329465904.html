- Classical Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — Sacred Celtic Caste: Described by Greek and Roman writers (Posidonius, Strabo, Diodorus Siculus, Lucan) as one of the three elevated orders of Gaulish society alongside Druids and Vates, singing heroic praise-songs and satirical curses to the accompaniment of lyre-like instruments.
- Medieval Celtic Nations (c. 6th–16th c.) — Hereditary Court Aristocracy: Flourished in Wales and Ireland as highly trained, protected hereditary orders (such as the Welsh beirdd and Irish baird) who recorded dynastic pedigrees, advised royalty, and adjudicated poetic disputes.
- Early Modern & Scots Repression (c. 15th–17th c.) — Criminalized Vagrancy: Severely demonized under English and Scottish statutes as rebellious, masterless instigators of unrest, reducing the term in common English parlance to vagabond rhymers and insolent beggars.
- Romantic Antiquarian Revival (18th c.) — Mythic & Sublime Elevation: Reimagined through Thomas Gray's ode "The Bard", James Macpherson's Ossianic poems, and the rediscovery of Celtic lore as the ultimate embodiment of untamed, inspired, primordial national genius.
- 19th Century to Present — Canonical Honorific & Oral-Epic Theory: Expanded honorifically to designate the greatest national poets (most famously William Shakespeare as "The Bard of Avon" and Robert Burns as "The Bard of Ayrshire"), while modern folklore and anthropology adopted "bardic" to analyze oral-traditional performance across world epics.
