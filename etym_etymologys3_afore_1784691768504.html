Afore is a transparent little compound whose history illustrates how ordinary words become fossils, and how a single dialect word can do the work of a whole portrait in fiction. It reaches back to one of the most ancient and productive particles of the Indo-European family, *per-, "forward, through, in front of." This single root gave Latin per and pro and prae, Greek pró and pará, and Sanskrit purás, "before, in front," and purā, "formerly." In the Germanic family, Grimm's Law turned the initial *p of the root into *f, producing Proto-Germanic *fura-/*fora, "before, in front of," the ancestor of English fore, for, first, forth, and former, and of the Germanic cognates Gothic faúra, Old Norse fyrir, Old Saxon fora, and Old High German fora.

Old English inherited two related words: fore, "before, in front of," used as a preposition and adverb, and foran, "in front, before," a slightly extended form of the same stem. To make the spatial meaning unmistakable, speakers compounded the prepositions æt ("at") or on ("in") with foran, yielding ætforan and onforan, literally "at the front of," "in the front." This is the same emphatic compounding that later produced English before from be- + fore, and it shows how Old English built spatial prepositions by piling up small directional particles for clarity. In Middle English the compounds contracted: the unstressed vowel of on- or æt- reduced to the single vowel a-, and onforan/ætforan became afore, exactly as on-forms gave a-bed, a-new, a-hold, and a-while in later English.

Once contracted, afore took on a double life. Its first recorded sense was spatial — "in front of" — and from that concrete meaning the temporal sense grew by the ancient metaphor in which what is before the eyes comes first in time: afore came to mean "previously, before this time." For centuries afterward it was a perfectly ordinary word of the standard language. It appears in the English Bible of 1611 and in the prose and poetry of the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries, where it competed on equal terms with before. Its decline began as before — the newer compound of be- + fore — won out in educated speech from the late Middle Ages onward, and by the nineteenth century afore had withdrawn from the standard language, surviving in two quite different refuges: the fixed professional formulas of law and the sea, and the living regional dialects of England, Scotland, and traditional American speech.

The law preserved aforementioned, afore-said, aforenamed, and aforethought, as in malice aforethought, the legal term for a killing planned in advance. The navy preserved afore the mast, the traditional phrase for the forecastle quarters of common sailors — the part of the ship before (in front of) the mainmast, and by extension the life of the ordinary seaman as opposed to the officers. In the dialects of northern England and Scotland, and in the folk speech of the American countryside, afore remained an everyday word meaning "before," and it still appears in dialect writing and in tales that want an old-fashioned, fireside, rural voice.

It is precisely that voice that Tolkien summons in The Hobbit when Bert the troll says "as I've said afore this evening." Tolkien, a professional philologist, chose such forms with care. Afore at once signals the trolls' rustic, unlettered speech and gives their dialogue a flavour of older, rural England — exactly the flavour appropriate to three rough creatures squatting in a wood. Set against the narrator's smooth standard English, the single dialect word does a remarkable amount of characterisation, placing the trolls socially as well as morally, and it suits the tale's fireside, storytelling register, in which archaic turns of phrase feel perfectly at home. A word that began as a PIE particle meaning "forward" thus ends its recorded life doing duty as a badge of rustic speech in one of the best-loved novels of the twentieth century.

  • Sound changes: PIE *p > PGmc *f (Grimm's Law); the reduction of the Old English compounds onforan/ætforan to Middle English afore is the key sound change, driven by the unstressed position of the prefix.
  • Morphology: afore is a frozen compound of a- (reduced on/æt) + fore; the parallel compound before (be- + fore) is its successful rival.
  • Semantic drift: "in front of" (space) > "previously" (time) — the ancient spatial-to-temporal metaphor — and then retreat into fossil phrases (law, sea) and dialect.
  • Socio-cultural factors: the rise of a standard educated English after the late Middle Ages pushed afore to the margins; law and the navy, as conservative institutions, preserved it; dialect speech kept it alive in the countryside.
  • Literary attestations: the Authorized Version (1611); seventeenth- and eighteenth-century prose; nineteenth-century dialect literature; and Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), where Bert's "afore this evening" is a philologist's precise marker of rustic speech.
