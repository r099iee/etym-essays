- Colonial entry (17th c.) - "cot" comes into British English from India (khāṭ), naming the light, woven Indian bedstead carried by travellers and servants; the word arrives already meaning portability and transience.
- Army and servants' spread (17th–18th c.) - the sense of a light portable bed hardens in military and household use; to sleep in a cot is to be lodged briefly, at minimal cost, without belonging.
- British–American divergence (19th c.) - British English drifts the word toward a small child's bed (later the crib), spawning "cot death" and the nursery compounds; American English keeps the military folding field bed.
- 20th-c. institutional flavour - "cot" comes to evoke barracks, camps, hospital wards, and charity rooms — a bed of utility lent to a body, not owned by it.
- Contemporary usage - the word remains plain and functional, its single clipped syllable still suggesting something light enough to be folded up and left behind.
