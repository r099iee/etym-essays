Shoots are the young, newly grown stems of a plant that push up — that “shoot” — out of a bud, seed, or root: the newest, tenderest growth of the growing season, still green, still flexible, still elongating. In the verse from The Story of the Stone (David Hawkes's translation of Cao Xueqin's Hong lou meng) that describes Lin Dai-yu — “In motion she called to mind tender willow shoots caressed by the wind” — the word condenses an entire theory of her grace. A willow shoot is slender, flexible, unforced: it does not resist the breeze but bends with it, so that its beauty consists precisely in its yielding. The adjective tender reinforces the youth and delicacy of the growth, and the whole image makes Dai-yu's motion seem organic rather than studied — she moves the way a shoot moves, because to move otherwise is not possible for her. There is also a hint of fragility: shoots are easily bruised, easily broken, and their beauty is inseparable from their vulnerability. The comparison quietly sets her apart from the sturdier, more assertive characters of the novel: she is the willow, not the oak — beauty defined by suppleness and by susceptibility to the wind rather than by resistance to it. The primary botanical sense. In botany a shoot is any new stem arising from a seed (the plumule), from a bud, or from a root; it may be a leafy stem, a sucker from the root system, a runner that creeps along the ground, or a green sprout from a cut stump. Shoots are the plant's engine of expansion: they are the parts that elongate, that seek the light (phototropism), and that carry the developing leaves. The word must be kept distinct from its botanical neighbours, each of which names a different stage or organ: a bud is the embryonic swelling from which the shoot emerges, not the growing stem itself; a sprout stresses the act of emerging from seed and carries a humble, edible connotation (bean sprouts); a twig is the older, hardened small branch of the second year onward, structural and stiff; a tendril is a specialised climbing organ that coils about a support; a runner (stolon) creeps horizontally and roots at the nodes; a sucker springs from the root or lower stem, often unwanted; and a scion is a shoot deliberately cut for grafting onto a rootstock. The boundary is one of age, flexibility, and purpose: a shoot is the newest, greenest, still-growing part of the plant. The verb and the semantic bridge. The noun is historically a back-formation from the verb to shoot, and the verb is the key to the whole polysemy, because every sense of shoot rests on one image: something darting outward with sudden force. (1) To shoot a gun, bow, or missile is to discharge a projectile — the root sense, with its strong verb morphology (shoot – shot – shot). (2) To shoot is to move suddenly and rapidly: to dart, to spurt, to flash across space (a pain shoots up the arm; a star shoots across the sky; a boat shoots the rapids). (3) To shoot is to grow with speed: a plant shoots up, as if growth itself were a kind of propulsion — and it is exactly this sense that produced the botanical noun, a shoot being a stem that has been “shot out” of the bud. (4) Since the late nineteenth century, to shoot has meant to photograph or film: a camera “takes a shot”, and a film crew “shoots a scene”, the photographic sense borrowing the vocabulary of the marksman and the arrow. (5) In ball games to shoot is to drive the ball at the goal or basket, the same darting image transferred to sport. Other noun senses. The noun shoot has spun off further senses beyond botany. (1) A shoot is a shooting expedition — a party of people going out to shoot game, or the estate ground over which the shooting takes place (“the grouse shoot”). (2) A shoot is a chute: a steeply inclined passage, pipe, or trough down which water, coal, grain, or rubbish slides (“a rubbish shoot”, “a coal shoot”). (3) In slang the plural shoots can mean roughly “the lot” or “the whole affair”, as in “the whole shoot”. (4) Bamboo shoots — the young edible sprouts of bamboo — are a staple ingredient across East Asian cuisines, and it is precisely the tender, fast-growing quality of the shoot that makes it edible. Idioms and fixed collocations. The verb is extraordinarily productive in idioms, all preserving the darting image:
  • Green shoots — since about 2009, journalistic shorthand for early signs of economic recovery, transferred from the green shoots of spring to the first, fragile positive indicators in a recession.
  • To shoot the breeze (or shoot the bull) — to chat casually; the image of idle talk drifting like wind.
  • To shoot for the stars — to aim very high.
  • To shoot straight / to shoot square — to be honest and fair.
  • To shoot from the hip — to speak or act impulsively, without reflection (from the cowboy trope of firing without aiming).
  • To shoot oneself in the foot — to harm one's own cause by a careless act.
  • A shooting star — a meteor, darting across the sky; a shooting pain — a sudden, darting pain.
  • Shoot! — a mild euphemistic oath, standing in for a stronger vulgar word, in use since the late nineteenth century.
  • To shoot up — to rise rapidly in price or height; in slang, to inject a drug intravenously.
  • A photo shoot / a shoot — a session at which a photographer or film crew takes pictures.
Modern technical and professional uses. In photography and filmmaking a “shoot” is the whole session of taking photographs or filming footage — the word has become a noun of the industry (“we wrapped the shoot at six”). In computing and gaming, shoot-'em-up (or shmup) names a genre of action game built around shooting; a screenshot is etymologically a “shot” of the screen. In forestry and agriculture, shoot growth is measured to gauge a tree's vigour, and “thinning the shoots” is a standard horticultural practice. The word has thus carried its darting, propulsive image from the bow and the gun into photography, film, sport, medicine, meteorology, cookery, finance, and the video game — an unusually wide radiation for a word that began with a missile.
