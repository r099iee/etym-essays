- Late 14th c. — scholastic entry: enters Middle English from Anglo-Norman as significacioun, denoting prophetic portents, celestial omens, and the allegorical meaning of scripture (attested in Chaucer and Wyclif).
- 15th–16th c. — grammatical narrowing: adopted by humanist educators and logicians to denote the precise literal definition or semantic value of a Latin vocabulary word.
- 17th c. — empiricist scrutiny: John Locke and the British empiricists establish signification as the foundational term for how arbitrary verbal sounds become connected to internal ideas in human consciousness.
- 19th c. — romantic aesthetics and theology: John Ruskin and Victorian art critics use the term to describe nature and visual art as symbolic languages bearing divine and moral instruction.
- 20th c. — structural semiotics: Ferdinand de Saussure, Roland Barthes, and post-structuralist theorists make signification the central technical term for the dynamic production of meaning between signifier and signified.
- Contemporary — formal and scholarly: preserved in high-register literary criticism, linguistics, and philosophy, strictly distinguished from general 'significance' (importance).
