- [Late 15th c. learned entry (1483)] - English took the word (spelled paradigme) through French from Latin paradigma, itself from Greek parádeigma 'that which is shown alongside'; from the start it meant 'a pattern, model, or illustrative example', and the grammatical sense - a model of inflections set beside the learner's forms - arrived immediately. It was a modest scholar's word.
- [Four centuries of quiet learned use (15th-19th c.)] - For roughly 400 years 'paradigm' lived unobtrusively in rhetoric (an example adduced as proof), in grammar (the declension pattern amo, amas, amat), and in philosophy (Plato's ideal Form); ordinary speakers rarely met it, and it carried no wider resonance.
- [Kuhn's explosion (1962)] - Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions relaunched the word as the whole constellation of assumptions, methods, and exemplars governing a scientific community, and its phrase 'paradigm shift' became one of the most borrowed intellectual slogans of the century; public perception swung from a classroom grammar term to a headline for revolutionising thought.
- [Modern register] - Today 'paradigm' is ubiquitous and, in careless hands, loosened to mean almost any typical model, approach, or worldview ('a new paradigm for business'); critics complain it is overworked jargon, while the precise scientific sense and the old grammar sense both remain alive. Its public register has gone from obscure and technical to fashionable, faintly grandiose, and slightly debased - an example of a word both enriched and diluted by fame.
