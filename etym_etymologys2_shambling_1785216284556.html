Proto-Indo-European *skembh- “to prop, support” (reconstructed; a debated variant *skambʰ- “crooked, bent” is sometimes posited to explain Greek σκαμβός) │ └── Latin scamnum “bench, stool, step” (from PIE *skembh-; Italic branch, attested 1st c. BCE) └── Late Latin scamellum “low stool” (diminutive of scamnum; attested in Late Latin) └── Old English sceamol / sceamel “stool, bench, table” (borrowed from Latin scamellum with the furniture of church and monastic life; 8th–10th c.) └── Middle English schamel “stall, bench, table” (12th–14th c.) └── English shambles (plural) “butcher’s stalls; slaughterhouse; scene of carnage” (13th c. onward; plural of shambel) └── English shambling (adjective) “of legs: crooked, straddling, splayed” (16th c.; from the image of the spread trestle-legs of a bench or butcher’s stall) └── English shamble (verb) “to walk with an awkward, dragging, slovenly gait” (1590s; back-formation from the adjective shambling) └── English shambling = shamble + -ing (present participle and participial adjective, 16th–17th c. onward) └── Modern English: shambling Debated stage: the Greek adjective σκαμβός (skambós) “crooked, bent” is often connected to a variant root *skambʰ-, but the relationship between *skembh- “to prop” and *skambʰ- “crooked” is debated and not secure; the English chain above relies on the well-attested *skembh- → scamnum → scamellum → sceamol line. The sense-shift from “bench/stool” to “crooked legs” and then to “awkward gait” is semantic, internal to English, and fully documented in the 16th century.
