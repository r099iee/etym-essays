Flanders is a proper noun naming a region of the southern Low Countries: the low, sandy coastal plain and its hinterland along the southern shore of the North Sea, centred historically on Bruges, Ghent and Ypres, and today constituting the Dutch-speaking northern half of Belgium. In this reading it is the theatre of war: Carlyle's Louis XV, “hastening from one end of his kingdom to the other, and suspending his conquests in Flanders that he might fly to the assistance of Alsace”, is the king of France in 1744, at the height of the War of the Austrian Succession, breaking off a successful campaign in the Austrian Netherlands to meet an Austrian threat on the Rhine.

The name is not a description of national character, as nineteenth-century writers sometimes imagined, but a very old territorial label of the coastal marshlands. Its likely sense, “flooded land” or “marsh-land”, fits a landscape of tidal flats, dunes, polders and storm surges, a landscape that the inhabitants had to reclaim from the sea yard by yard.

Historical referents

  • The county. The County of Flanders emerged in the ninth century, when Baldwin I “Iron Arm” was installed as margrave in the coastal march; by the eleventh and twelfth centuries it was one of the richest principalities of Europe, its wealth built on the cloth industry and on the North Sea trade.
  • The towns. Bruges, Ghent and Ypres formed a network of manufacturing cities whose guilds and aldermen exercised unusual power; the Battle of the Golden Spurs at Kortrijk in 1302, in which Flemish militia defeated a French army of knights, became a founding myth of Flemish identity.
  • The Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands. Through the marriage of Margaret of Male to Philip the Bold in 1369, and her inheritance in 1384, Flanders passed to the dukes of Burgundy, and through Mary of Burgundy to the Habsburgs in 1477; it was thereafter a province of the Spanish and then the Austrian Netherlands.
  • The French and Belgian periods. Annexed by revolutionary France in 1795, included in the United Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1815 and in Belgium in 1830, Flanders became the Dutch-speaking north of the Belgian state, a fact formalised by the language laws and the federal reforms of the twentieth century.

What “Flanders” denotes today

  • The Belgian region. The Flemish Region and the Flemish Community, with their own parliament in Brussels, administer the north of Belgium; “Flanders” in the news almost always means this political entity.
  • The historical county. Historians also use it for the medieval county, whose borders extended into what is now France (French Flanders, around Lille and Dunkirk) and the Netherlands (Zeelandic Flanders).
  • A cultural and linguistic label. “Flanders” can denote the Dutch-speaking area as a whole, including Brussels in some usages, and “Flemish” is the adjective for its language, art, literature and institutions.
  • A battlefield. Since the First World War the name has been inseparable from the Western Front: the Ypres salient, Passchendaele, and John McCrae's poem In Flanders Fields (1915), which fixed the poppy as the emblem of remembrance.

Derived and habitual expressions

  • Flanders Fields. A phrase from McCrae's opening line, now used for the war cemeteries and the commemorative landscape of western Belgium.
  • Flanders poppy. The field poppy that colonised the disturbed soil of the battlefields and became the symbol worn on Armistice Day.
  • Flanders campaign. A standard military-historical label for the campaigns of 1744–48, 1793–95 and 1914–18 in the same low, wet terrain.
  • Flanders lace. The bobbin lace industry of the old county, still a marker of the region's early modern craft economy.

Grammar and register

As a proper noun, Flanders takes no article in normal English (in Flanders), resists pluralisation and has no common-noun senses. It is neutral in register but heavily charged in context: in British memory it is a landscape of war, in Belgian politics a community, and in economic history a byword for the first industrial cloth economy of medieval Europe. The adjective is Flemish, the inhabitant is a Fleming, and the Dutch name is Vlaanderen. Carlyle's use is territorial and military: Flanders is simply the theatre from which the king must withdraw.

Why it matters in this reading

The phrase “suspending his conquests in Flanders” compresses a whole strategic situation. In 1744 the French had been winning in the Austrian Netherlands; the diversion to Alsace, and the illness at Metz that interrupted it, turned a triumphal progress into a crisis of the monarchy and gave Louis XV the nickname le Bien-Aimé. Flanders is thus the starting point of the sequence that runs through Alsace and Metz in the other cards of this batch.
