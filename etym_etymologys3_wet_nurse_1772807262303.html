PART ONE — THE WET ELEMENT (PIE *wed- 'water, wet'). The first element of wet-nurse, wet, descends from one of the most ancient and widely attested roots in the Indo-European family, PIE *wed-, 'water, wet.' The root is so old and so stable that it survives recognisably across the whole family, always attached to water: Greek hýdōr 'water' (the source of English technical loans hydrate, hydrant, hydraulic), Sanskrit udán 'water,' Latin unda 'wave' (whence undulate), Hittite watar 'water' — the earliest attested Indo-European form, in second-millennium-BC cuneiform — and Russian voda 'water.' No other concept in the vocabulary of these languages has produced such a consistent constellation of forms; the root simply means water, everywhere. In the Germanic branch the root underwent a characteristic vowel alternation. The full-grade form lies behind water itself (Old English wæter, Proto-Germanic *watōr), while a zero-grade form produced the adjective wet, moist. Proto-Germanic *wēta-, *wētaz, 'wet, moist,' gave Old English wæt, 'wet, moist, liquid,' attested from the earliest English texts; Old Norse vatr and Old Frisian wet are exact cognates. Old English also used the word as a noun, 'moisture,' and built a verb wǣtan, 'to wet.' The adjective wet, with its plain, physical meaning 'covered or saturated with liquid,' is one of the most basic words of the language, and it is that word, in its most concrete sense, that the compound wet-nurse presses into service. In the compound, however, wet is not weather vocabulary. Its function is to specify the presence of milk — the moist, life-giving nourishment that defines the role. The phrase wet-nurse is an exact and economical invention: it names a nurse who is 'wet' in the sense of lactating, who gives milk to the child, in explicit contrast with the dry-nurse who minds a child without breastfeeding. The adjective does not describe the woman's state so much as her function; it is a marker of the milk. Etymologically, then, the first half of wet-nurse points back to the primal Indo-European water words, and in the compound it harnesses the most basic sense of wet — liquid present, liquid given — to the most intimate of human acts. The choice of wet to mark this role has a long vernacular logic. In the early modern period, when the compound was coined, English already distinguished wet and dry as a pair everywhere — wet and dry weather, wet and dry goods — and the nursery borrowed the same binary: wet-nurse and dry-nurse are first attested together in the seventeenth century as a strict contrast on the giving of milk. The water-root etymology thus underwrites a social distinction that mattered enormously in premodern households: the difference between the woman whose body fed the child and the woman who only minded it. Every use of wet-nurse, from the seventeenth century to Nannie Wang in the Red Chamber translation, silently invokes that deep water vocabulary and turns it to a biological and social purpose. The word is also a reminder of how concrete old compounds can remain: five thousand years after *wed- meant water, an English hyphen joins it to the woman who feeds an infant. PART TWO — THE NURSE ELEMENT (Latin nūtrīx 'wet-nurse'). The second element, nurse, has a longer and more dramatic career. The word is a direct descendant of Latin nūtrīx, 'wet-nurse' — she who gives suck — and its verb nūtrīre, 'to suckle, nourish, foster.' The Latin formation is an agent noun built on a PIE base reconstructed as *nu-tri-, 'she who gives suck,' tied to a root *(s)nau- / *(s)na-, 'to flow; to give suck, to suckle.' The reconstruction is repeated in many handbooks, though some scholars treat the inner structure of nūtrīre as obscure; what is certain is the chain of meaning: to nourish, to feed at the breast, to foster. From the same Latin stock come English nourish, nutrition, nutrient, and nutritious, all of which preserve the feeding core without the occupational title. In Roman households the nūtrīx was a household institution: the enslaved or hired woman who suckled the children of her mistress, often deeply attached to the children she raised. Latin also formed nūtrīcius, 'that nourishes,' and from its feminine and neuter uses Late Latin built *nūtrīcia, a noun meaning 'nurse, governess' — the woman who raised children even after weaning. That Late Latin form passed into Old French as norrice, nourrice, 'foster-mother, wet-nurse, nanny,' and from Old French into Middle English in about 1200 as norice, nurrice, still meaning primarily 'woman who suckles or fosters an infant.' For the first several centuries of English usage, nurse and wet-nurse were one and the same thing: the word needed no qualification because the feeding function was its default sense. The great change came when English nurse began to broaden. From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the word spread to any person who tends the sick, the infirm, or the young — the hospital nurse, the sick-nurse, the day nurse — until the feeding sense was no longer guaranteed by the bare word. At that point English did something characteristic: it built explicit compounds to hold the old meanings apart, and the pair wet-nurse versus dry-nurse, attested from the seventeenth century, was the result. The milk-giving function, which had once been the whole meaning of nurse, now had to be spelled out with an adjective; wet-nurse keeps alive, in a single hyphenated phrase, the original nūtrīx sense that plain nurse had outgrown. The social history wrapped in the word is long and contested. Wet-nursing was an elite and often criticised institution: in early modern and eighteenth-century Europe, hiring a wet-nurse was standard practice among the wealthy, and it was also the subject of moral debate — Rousseau and other reformers urged mothers to nurse their own children, and medical and moral writers blamed wet-nurses for the mortality and waywardness of infants. In elite Chinese households, as in the Red Chamber, the wet-nurse (nai-ma, 'nursing mother') was a trusted quasi-family member who often retained influence over the child for life — exactly the figure Nannie Wang is: 'her old wet-nurse,' the woman who fed Dai-yu as an infant and still travels with her, a living link between nourishment and loyalty. Modern English, where breastfeeding practices have changed and the occupation has all but vanished, keeps the word for historical and literary settings; contemporary discourse prefers 'milk donor' or 'surrogate breastfeeder.' But the etymology is unbroken: nurse, from the woman who gives suck, and wet, from the primal water root — together, the woman whose body feeds another's child, a compound in which two of the oldest words in the language meet to name the oldest of human roles.
