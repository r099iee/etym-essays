christened is the past tense and past participle of christen, whose core modern sense is to baptize a person, especially an infant, into the Christian faith with water in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and by extension to give a name to a person at baptism, and still more broadly to name, designate, dub or launch anything for the first time with ceremony.

The primary ecclesiastical sense remains central: to admit to the Christian Church by the sacrament of baptism. In Anglican, Catholic, Orthodox and many Protestant traditions this involves water, Trinitarian formula, godparents and entry in a parish register. A child is christened, meaning both baptized and named. Adults converting are also said to be christened. The rite confers Christian identity, original-sin remission in Catholic theology, and communal belonging.

A second major sense is purely nominative and secularized: to give a name or title to. Ships are christened with champagne smashed on the bow, buildings are christened with dedication ceremonies, bands, products, hurricanes and scientific phenomena are christened. Konrad Lorenz christened vacuum behavior in exactly this sense: he did not baptize animals, he coined and bestowed a technical term. This naming sense carries inaugural, authoritative and often public overtones: the namer claims definitional authority.

  • Religious baptism: to administer baptism, to make Christian, to receive into the Church with water and prayer.
  • Bestowal of a personal name: to name at baptism, e.g. she was christened Mary; extended to any formal naming.
  • Ceremonial naming or launching: to dedicate and name a ship, building, enterprise or product with ritual, e.g. the liner was christened Queen Mary.
  • Coinage of a term or nickname: to dub, label or designate, e.g. Lorenz christened the behavior, journalists christened him Iron Man.
  • Figurative initiation by ordeal: to introduce to a harsh experience, e.g. christened by fire, christened in battle, meaning initiated through first trial.
  • Humorous misuse for non-Christian rites: loosely used for any naming ritual, even where baptism is theologically inappropriate, reflecting secularization.

Polysemy bridges are clear. From make Christian by baptism to give a baptismal name is metonymic: naming happens during the rite, so the rite verb absorbs the naming act. From sacred naming to secular naming is metaphorical generalization during early modern English, as civic launches imitated church ritual. From naming to initiating by trial comes from the idea that baptism is a first immersion: first combat or first soaking becomes a christening. The Lorenz usage sits in the coinage branch: scientific baptism as authoritative labeling, with faintly playful irony since animal vacuum activity is far from church.

Register and nuance matter. Christened is warmer, more traditional and more ceremonial than named, designated or termed. It implies witnesses, intention and permanence. Called is neutral, dubbed is journalistic, termed is technical, coined is linguistic, christened is inaugural and slightly solemn or whimsical. It can sound quaint or Anglican in American contexts where baptized is preferred for the rite itself. In academic prose christened often signals a self-aware coinage, acknowledging that the label is stipulative rather than discovered.
