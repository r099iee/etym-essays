Single root word: brambles (plural of bramble). 1. PIE *bʰrem- 'to growl, buzz, roar' (onomatopoeic; c. 4500–3500 BCE; also with the sense 'to project, bristle') → Proto-Germanic *brēm- / *brām- 'thorn-bush, bramble, broom' (c. 500 BCE; the semantic bridge from 'buzz/roar' to 'thorn-bush' is DEBATED — either the buzzing of bees in the blossom or the bristling projection of thorns) → *brēm-ilaz 'little bramble' (diminutive, Proto-Germanic; the plain form *brāmō gave English 'broom' and German Brombeere 'blackberry', lit. 'bramble-berry') → Old English bræmel / brēmel 'bramble' (attested 8th–9th c. CE) → Middle English brembel / bremble 'bramble' (13th–14th c.; metathesis of the liquids -m- and -l- also gives dialectal 'brumble') → Modern English bramble /'bræmbəl/ 'a prickly shrub, typically a blackberry' → plural brambles /'bræmbəlz/ 'prickly shrubs, typically blackberries.' NOTE — the root *bʰrem- is onomatopoeic, so it has no further Indo-European ancestor in the ordinary genealogical sense; the debated step is the sense-shift from 'noise' to 'plant', and both proposed bridges are given above.
