- 1590s–1610s (Late Elizabethan Architecture) - Borrowed into English from French frontispice (and Medieval Latin frontispicium) as an architectural term denoting the principal decorated facade, entrance portico, or pediment of a classical temple or manor house.
- 1600s–1650s (Jacobean Printing & Folk Etymology) - Adopted by London book publishers whose elaborate title pages were engraved as miniature triumphal arches or architectural portals; the terminal syllable was altered through folk etymology from -spice to -piece, linking it in the popular mind with a 'piece of art'.
- 1700s–1850s (Enlightenment & Victorian Publishing Standard) - Shifted from the title-leaf itself to denote specifically an engraved plate (frequently an author portrait, allegorical scene, or landscape) placed on the left-hand page (verso) directly facing the printed title page.
- Modern Era - Established in bibliophilia, typography, and art history as the premier opening illustrative plate of a fine volume, and employed metaphorically to describe any public, introductory 'face' or symbolic display of an institution or movement.
