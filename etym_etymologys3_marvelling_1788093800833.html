The PIE root of wonder. The chain begins with the PIE root *smei- "to smile, laugh, be astonished" — a root that links wonder to the face: Sanskrit smáyate "smiles", Greek meidán "to smile", Old Church Slavonic smějǫ "to laugh". In Latin the root produced mīrus "wonderful, marvellous, extraordinary" and the verb mīrārī "to wonder at, admire, be astonished" (whence English admire, miracle, and mirror via the notion of looking at oneself in wonder). The semantic bridge is the open, astonished face — wonder was conceived as the mind's smile.

Latin: mirabilia and the literature of wonder. From mīrus Latin built mīrābilis "wonderful, astonishing" and its plural mīrābilia "wonderful things, marvels". Roman and late-antique culture produced a whole genre of wonder-literature — paradoxography, catalogues of mirabilia: strange beasts, distant peoples, engineering marvels. The medieval tradition of "Mirabilia" books (such as the Mirabilia Urbis Romae, a guide to the wonders of Rome) kept the word alive, and medieval pilgrims and travellers "marvelled" at what they saw exactly as tourists do today.

Vulgar Latin and Old French. Popular speech reshaped mīrābilia into Vulgar Latin *mīribilia, which became Old French merveille "a wonder, amazement, miracle" — attested from the earliest Old French texts (10th–11th centuries) and frequent in the great epics and romances, where knights ride out to see "les merveilles du monde". The Old French verb merveillier (se merveillier de) "to wonder at, marvel at" (12th century) is the direct ancestor of the English verb.

Middle English: merveillen. English borrowed the noun merveille around 1300 (marvel "a wonder, miracle") and the verb merveillen shortly after (early 14th century), from Anglo-Norman and Old French. Chaucer uses the noun richly (a "merveille" of nature, of art), and Middle English devotional and romance texts make "marvelling" the stock response to God's works, magic, strange lands and beautiful women — wonder is both a religious emotion and a courtly one. The spelling marvel (with -a-) reflects the Anglo-Norman pronunciation of merveille.

Renaissance and the King James Bible. The King James Version (1611) fixes the verb in memorable phrases: "When Jesus heard it, he marvelled" (Matthew 8:10), "And Pilate marvelled if he were already dead" (Mark 15:44), "Marvel not at this" (John 5:28). In the Renaissance, wonder becomes an intellectual virtue: the age of exploration, the wonder-cabinets (cabinets of curiosities) and the new science all made "marvelling at nature" a respectable and even pious activity — wonder at creation was wonder at the Creator.

Modern career.

  • 18th–19th centuries: science and industry domesticate wonder; people marvel at machines, railways, landscapes and discoveries; the word shifts from sacred awe to delighted curiosity.
  • 20th century: "marvellous" becomes a universal adjective of approval in British speech, while the verb keeps a more literary register; "I was marvelling at..." is warm, reflective, slightly old-fashioned.
  • Spelling divergence: marvelling (British, doubled -l) versus marveling (American) — a visible marker of dialect since the 19th-century codification of spelling rules.

Summary. Marvelling has travelled from the astonished smile of the PIE root, through Latin wonder-literature and medieval miracle, into the King James Bible and the cabinets of curiosities, and down to modern English, where it still means what it always meant: to look at something wonderful with open eyes and a wondering mind — the oldest and most human of intellectual emotions.
