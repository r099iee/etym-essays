1. From grasping to taking in: the Latin base. The history of conceiving is the history of one image — taking hold — turned toward the mind and the body at once. Its root is the Indo-European base *kap- "to grasp, seize" (reconstructed, and itself debated in shape), which gives Latin capere "to take". Compound capere with con- "together, with" and you get concipere: literally "to take in with oneself", to receive into one's own hold. From the earliest Latin literature the verb carries both of its great senses side by side: the mind receives and contains an idea or a plan; the womb receives and contains a child. Cicero writes of concipere in argument and of women conceiving in the same sentence of the language — the double life is not an English accident but is present at the root.

2. Romance and Middle English: one word for two embodiments. Old French conceivre transmitted both senses to Middle English conceiven in the thirteenth century, where they flourished together in religious, legal, and literary use. The theological vocabulary of the Virgin and the incarnation ("conceived of the Holy Ghost") kept the two senses in constant, deliberate contact: the mind that conceives a purpose and the womb that conceives a child were pictured as parallel acts of receiving. Chaucer and the mystery plays use the word in both directions without any sense of strain. Conception was thus, for medieval culture, a single metaphor of taking in with two celebrated instances — the idea and the child.

3. The early modern expansion: conceiving as creation. Renaissance philosophy and poetics made conceiving the master-word of mental creation. In the phrase of the Italian concetto and the English "conceit", an artistic or intellectual work was held to be conceived before it was made — the mind's interior act of formation, the inward shaping of argument, image, and design. Shakespeare's plays are full of hearts and minds conceiving wonders; Bacon and Descartes give conception its modern philosophical sense of a notion or concept; the word becomes indispensable to epistemology ("our conception of matter", "the conceiving mind"). Meanwhile the physical sense never flagged in medicine and midwifery, where "to conceive" remained the exact clinical word.

4. The nineteenth century: nervousness and differentiation. The Victorians inherited the word with its two senses fully live, but sensibility was changing. Open talk of conception withdrew from polite writing into medical textbooks, while the mental sense absorbed the new vocabulary of psychology and evolution — "conceiving a species", "conceiving the mind as an organ". The result was a visible asymmetry: the mental sense grew philosophically freighted and slightly precious, the physical sense grew private and clinical. It is in this climate that Pater writes of "conceiving life": his usage leans wholly on the intellectual sense and yet, because the bodily sense is still audible beneath it, the phrase gathers a discreet resonance — conceiving life as the mind takes in, and as the body brings forth, something new.

5. The twentieth century and today: reversal of weights. In the twentieth century the balance tipped decisively. Everyday speech now hears conceive and conceiving first of all as words about pregnancy; the abstract sense survives in the idiom "conceive of" (to imagine the existence of something) and in formal or literary prose. The result is a word with two sharply separated registers: the plain biological sense and the loftier intellectual one, divided — fittingly — by the same act of taking in that has bound them together for two and a half millennia. Any reader of older prose must keep both senses in play, for an author such as Pater could still count on the word to mean forming, imagining, and understanding at full intellectual pitch — with the fertile echo sounding underneath.
