In the source passage — “right between the arms of the Mountain” — the word is used in its extended, almost personified sense: the projecting ridges or spurs of a mountain, imagined as limbs reaching out from the massif. This is a literary-topographic use of the plural of arm, the same word family that gives us the arm of a chair, the arm of the sea, the arm of a crane and the arm of the law.

Arms, however, is one of the most polysemous plurals in English, and it is important to separate its three great strands:

  • The limbs (plural of arm) — the upper limbs of the human body, from shoulder to hand; in the plural, “arms” is the anatomical word (“he folded his arms”, “she carried the child in her arms”). By extension, anything that projects like a limb is an “arm”: an arm of the sea (an inlet), an arm of a chair (the side support), an arm of a lever or crane (the projecting member), an arm of a mountain (a ridge or spur), an arm of the law (the enforcing power of authority), and the “long arm” of an organisation or of coincidence.
  • Weapons (from Latin arma) — “arms” meaning weapons of war, collectively: firearms, side arms, small arms; “to take up arms” (to begin fighting), “to bear arms” (to serve as a soldier; in American usage, the right to keep and bear arms), “a call to arms”, “man-at-arms” (a medieval soldier), “arms race” (competitive military build-up). This sense is a distinct word historically — it entered English from Old French armes, from Latin arma “weapons, equipment of war” — though it happens to be spelled and pronounced identically to the plural of “arm”.
  • Heraldic bearings — “arms” as the emblems of a family, city or institution: a “coat of arms”, “the College of Arms”, “to grant arms”. This sense developed within the weapons strand, because the knight's heraldic device was originally displayed on his shield and armour.

The verb “to arm”. From the weapons strand comes the verb: “to arm” means to supply with weapons or equipment (“arm the garrison”), to prepare for action (“armed with facts”, “armed with patience”), and, in modern technical use, to make a device ready to fire or detonate (“arm the missile”, “arm the alarm”). The derived adjective “armed” contrasts with “unarmed”, and the nouns “armament” (military equipment, weapons collectively) and “armour” belong to the same family.

Idioms and set phrases. “With open arms” (warmly), “arm in arm” (linked together), “at arm's length” (kept at a distance; also of a transaction between independent parties), “up in arms” (angry and protesting), “the long arm of the law” (the far-reaching power of justice), “in arms” (of a baby held in the arms), “a babe in arms” (a very young child), “arm's reach”.

The semantic bridge. All three strands ultimately trace to the same Indo-European root meaning “to fit, join” — the arm is the limb that joins the body to what it grasps, and weapons are the “fitted equipment” of the warrior. The English situation is a happy accident of history: the limb-word came down the Germanic line (Old English earm), while the weapons-word came down the Latin line (arma), and the two converge on the identical form “arms”, each reinforcing the other's imagery of reach and power.
