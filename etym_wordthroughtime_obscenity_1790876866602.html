- Classical Antiquity (Latin) - obscēnus denoted that which was ill-omened, foul, repulsive, or inauspicious according to religious augury.
- Late 16th Century - Borrowed into English via French obscénité to designate gross moral foulness, lewdness, and offensive speech.
- 18th to 19th Century - Codified into Victorian legal statutes (Lord Campbell's Act 1857, Hicklin Test 1868) to censor published literature and erotic art.
- 20th Century - The center of fierce constitutional battles over free speech and censorship in modern democracies (Roth, Jacobellis, Miller tests).
- 21st Century - Continues as a crucial legal doctrine in internet regulation, a designation for spoken profanities, and a moral metaphor for extreme societal injustices.
