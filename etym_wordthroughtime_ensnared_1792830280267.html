- Old Norse — the loop: snara names the noose and cord-trap of hunters, a humble working word of woodcraft.
- Middle English — the hunter's noun: snare enters English for wire loops and net-traps, literal and ruthless.
- Late 16th century — the verb is armed: ensnare is built with en- meaning put into, and preachers at once set it for souls, warning that sin ensnares like wire.
- 17th–18th century — love and law borrow the wire: lovers are ensnared by eyes, debtors by bonds, readers by plots; the trap goes indoors and psychological.
- 19th century — empire and melodrama: savages, spies, and heroines are ensnared in novels; missionaries warn of drink and doctrine ensnaring converts.
- Today — systems set the snares: debt, fine print, addiction, and surveillance ensnare modern victims, while Tolkien's Rings remain the archetypal ensnaring gift — power offered, freedom taken.
