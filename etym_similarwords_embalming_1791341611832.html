• Mummification: Similar because both involve the preservation of a dead body against decomposition; differs because mummification emphasizes the complete desiccation, drying out, and wrapping of the body (either naturally through aridity or ritually as in ancient Egypt), whereas modern embalming centers on fluid chemical fixation of soft tissues via arterial perfusion.
  • Taxidermy: Similar because both involve treating dead organic specimens to maintain lifelike form; differs because taxidermy is applied exclusively to animals and involves skinning the specimen and mounting its treated hide over an artificial armature, whereas embalming preserves the intact human body internally.
  • Plastination: Similar because both are preservation techniques for anatomical specimens; differs because plastination is a high-tech modern method that replaces body water and lipid tissues with curable polymers (such as silicone or epoxy) to create rigid, dry anatomical models, whereas embalming is temporary or medium-term chemical preservation using liquid fixatives.
  • Preservation: Similar because both denote actions taken to prevent decay and decay-causing processes; differs because preservation is an overarching, general umbrella term applicable to food, documents, landscapes, or biology, whereas embalming is specifically restricted to mortuary treatment of dead bodies.
  • Conservation: Similar because both protect artifacts from deterioration; differs because conservation applies to cultural heritage, artwork, and historical items, focusing on stabilization and ethical restoration, rather than mortuary biology.
  • Petrification: Similar because both result in a body or organism being arrested in time; differs because petrification is a geological process where organic matter is mineralized into stone over millennia, rather than chemical treatment by human agency.
