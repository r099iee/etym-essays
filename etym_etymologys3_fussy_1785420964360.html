FUSSY descends from the noun fuss, and its biography is the biography of an expressive, sound-symbolic coinage — one of those English words that lexicographers cannot trace to an Indo-European root because it was never built on one. The noun fuss first surfaces in colloquial English around the end of the seventeenth century, with the earliest recorded uses appearing c. 1701, meaning "needless commotion, bustle, or agitation over trifles." Its phonetic shape — a short cluster of stops and a sibilant, fuss, with the doubled s suggesting a prolonged hissing or puffing — seems designed to evoke a puffing, blowing, or fidgety motion, and comparative evidence points to similar expressive formations across North and West Germanic: Middle Low German fuss "tumult, commotion, bustle," Dutch and Flemish vuzen "to blow, puff, vent," and Old Norse fúss "eager, willing, desirous," which conveys an eager, forward-leaning attitude. Both groups suggest that the underlying image was one of restless, audible energy — a person huffing and bustling about in disproportionate excitement, blowing air out in agitation. Because these are expressive roots rather than regular inherited words, the usual rules of Germanic sound change do not apply, and specialists emphasize that no secure PIE etymon exists for fuss. The word is best described as an onomatopoeic or ideophonic coinage, reinforced in English by dialect and low-register borrowing; the cluster of similar words across the Germanic languages — fuss, fidget, fuzz, futz, hustle — belongs to a family of sound-symbolic formations that Germanic speakers seem to have created repeatedly for the restless, fussing, fidgeting world of small anxieties. This is worth stating explicitly: the etymological tree of fussy cannot begin at a PIE root, because none exists, and any attempt to force the word onto a reconstructed Indo-European base would be fabrication. The honest chain therefore begins with the expressive noun of the seventeenth century. In early eighteenth-century English the noun fuss signified a "tumult, bustle, or needless commotion," especially the exaggerated concern shown over unimportant matters, and by the same period a verb to fuss arose meaning "to bustle about or to fidget restlessly." Around the 1770s the adjective-forming suffix -y (from Old English -ig, meaning "having the nature of, characterized by") was attached to the noun to produce FUSSY, initially in the sense "given to making a fuss." The adjective quickly developed its two main branches of meaning. Applied to persons, it described someone finicky, hard to please, habitually anxious over small matters — the fussy eater, the fussy old lady, the fussy parent who cannot let a child out of sight. Applied to things and processes, it described the over-elaborate and the cluttered — a fussy design, fussy ornament, fussy prose — the quality of work that tries too hard and accumulates unnecessary detail. The two branches share the core image: disproportionate attention to the small, the marginal, the trivial. The semantic drift of the word in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries is a drift of register and extension rather than of core meaning. In the nineteenth century fussy broadened from persons to aesthetics and to domestic material culture, so that a room, a dress, a piece of furniture, or an ornament could be fussy if it were cluttered and over-decorated; the word gained its comfortable place in the vocabulary of interior decoration and fashion criticism, always carrying the mild disapproval of excess. In the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries it entered literary and editorial criticism: fussy editing, fussy style, fussy scholarly apparatus — a text or edition so laden with qualifications, cross-references, and minor emendations that the apparatus threatens to outweigh the argument. This is precisely the sense of the present card, where a "fussy process" of footnotes and qualifications is confessed with gentle self-deprecation, the writer insisting that the finicky apparatus was a deliberate safeguard against over-cutting. The social history of the word tracks the history of its connotations. In the eighteenth century fuss was a word of complaint about trivial commotion, belonging to the domestic world of servants, children, and anxious mothers; the adjective fussy inherited that homely, exasperated warmth. In the nineteenth century, as the culture of respectability and of domestic order reached its height, fussy named the excess that threatened order — the over-decorated parlour, the over-arranged household, the finicky guest — and the word became a mild social weapon, the polite way of saying that someone was taking trifles too seriously. In the twentieth century the word loosened further, becoming equally at home of a crying baby, an exacting eater, an over-decorated room, or an over-annotated page, and settling into an informal-to-neutral register far less formal than fastidious or meticulous and distinctly less condemnatory than pedantic. The derived forms — fussiness, fussily, fusser, and the American fussbudget — extend the same image of the restless, trifle-obsessed spirit. In the specific context of this card — the editorial process with its conflicting footnotes — fussy is deployed with perfect accuracy. The word carries all its history at once: the homely commotion of the eighteenth-century noun, the aesthetic disapproval of the nineteenth-century adjective, and the scholarly extension of the twentieth century. The writer's "this may seem a fussy process, but I did not want to cut too much" is a small rhetorical masterstroke, because the adjective both concedes the reader's probable judgment — yes, this is fussy — and re-frames it: the fussiness is not neurosis but scholarly conscience, the refusal to let a single qualification be lost. The word thus does exactly what its etymology predicts: it names the making of a fuss, the disproportionate, careful, anxious attention to detail that is the fussy mind's whole character.
  • Expressive noun fuss (c. 1701) "needless commotion over trifles" — no PIE ancestry; sound-symbolic, imitative of puffing, bustling agitation.
  • Parallel Germanic expressive forms: Middle Low German fuss "tumult," Dutch/Flemish vuzen "to blow, puff," Old Norse fúss "eager, forward" — sound-symbolic kin, not regular cognates.
  • Verb to fuss (early 18th c.) "to bustle about, to fidget, to make much of small matters."
  • Adjective fussy (c. 1770s) = fuss + -y (from Old English -ig, "characterized by"); first "given to making a fuss."
  • Extension (19th–20th c.): from persons to things, aesthetics, design, and scholarly apparatus — "burdened with minor qualifications."
  • Derivatives: fussiness, fussily, fusser, fussbudget (American).
