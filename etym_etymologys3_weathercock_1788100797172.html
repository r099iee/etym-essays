1. The blowing root. The first ingredient of weathercock is weather, and every European word for the sky's moods trails the same reconstructed Indo-European root: PIE *h₂weh₁- (also written *we-), "to blow", the root of Latin ventus, Greek aētēs, and English wind. From this root the Germanic languages built a noun for the blowing conditions themselves: Proto-Germanic *wedrą, "wind, weather", which became Old English weder. The Old English word is wider than the modern one: weder meant not only "weather" in our sense but "air, sky, breeze, storm" — the whole aerial element in which people lived. When the Anglo-Saxons prayed for good weder they were praying for fair sky and safe passage; the poem Beowulf speaks of foul weather driving ships on the whale-road. Middle English narrowed the spelling battle through weder, wedir, wether to the standardised weather of the 16th century, while the sense narrowed from "sky and storm" to "the atmospheric state at a given place and time".

2. The crowing bird. The second ingredient, cock, is older in English than any compound using it — Old English cocc, "male domestic fowl", is attested in the 10th century and even earlier as a personal name, Coc. Its origin is a celebrated puzzle: the OED describes it as of uncertain etymology, probably imitative of the cock's crow — an onomatopoeic name for the loudest voice of the farmyard. Old Norse kokkr and Old French coq look like relatives but no sound-change law connects them cleanly, and no common Germanic–Romance ancestor has been established; the bird may simply have been named by the same call all over Europe. Whatever its prehistory, by the later Middle Ages cock was the ordinary English word for the rooster, rich in folklore: the crowing cock that scatters the powers of darkness at dawn, the cock of St Peter's denial, the barnyard tyrant of Chaucer's Nun's Priest's Tale.

3. The church-cock. The custom that welded these two words together is the medieval church-cock. Early medieval Christianity — with its vigils, matins and the imagery of the soul waking from sin — made the rooster the emblem of wakefulness; Luke 22:34, where Jesus foretells Peter's denial "before the cock crow", turned the bird into the conscience that calls sinners to repentance, and medieval legend attributed the placing of cocks on church towers to a decree of Pope Nicholas I in the 9th century, though the fashion certainly grew out of older Carolingian and Byzantine practice. The surviving bronze cock of Brescia, cast in the 9th century, shows how early the gilded bird crowned north-Italian towers. From the church, the practical innovation spread to castle, guildhall and farmstead: a pivoted metal cock that turned with the wind and told the people — sailors, farmers, travellers — which way the weather came. The word weathercock (Middle English wedercok) names exactly that object: the weather-telling cock, attested from the later Middle Ages and ubiquitous in English from the 16th century.

4. The figurative turn — the time-server (16th–18th centuries). The object's perpetual pivoting made it the perfect Renaissance emblem of inconstancy. From the second half of the 16th century, weathercock names a person — usually a politician, preacher or courtier — who turns with every prevailing wind. The Reformation and the wars of religion, when whole clergy and councils swung between creeds and dynasties, gave the word its teeth: a "weathercock" was a time-server who had no fixed principle, only a pivot. The satirists of the 17th and 18th centuries — the age of the "trimmer", of shifting ministries and changing fortunes — fixed the phrase in political prose: the proverbial "as changeable as a weathercock" applied to ministers, critics and lovers alike. This is the sense in which Wilde's The Happy Prince (1888) deploys the word, and the joke is double: the statue is literally beautiful but useless, while the councillor who says so is himself a human weathercock.

5. The technical re-borrowing (19th–20th centuries). As the literal vane receded into quaintness — plain "weathervanes" with arrow pointers and compass roses displacing the cock on new buildings from the 1800s — the exact sciences re-borrowed the old image. Seamen speak of a boat "weathercocking" into the wind when its bow swings to align with the blast; pilots and aerodynamicists speak of "weathercock stability", the natural tendency of an aircraft to turn its nose into the relative airflow, exactly as a steel cock turns into the breeze; even missiles and drones are tuned with reference to the ancient barn-vane metaphor. The word has thus completed a circle: from church emblem to farm instrument, from political insult to aerospace formula — always meaning, in every register, that which points where the wind blows.
