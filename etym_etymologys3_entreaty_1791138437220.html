Entreaty is a textbook example of a concrete handling verb softening into emotional pleading. It begins not with prayer but with pulling. PIE *tragh-, to draw or drag, yields Latin trahere, to pull, whose frequentative tractare meant to keep dragging, hence to handle, manage, treat. From handling things to handling people to treating them well or badly was a short Roman step: tractare negotium, to handle business; male tractare, to mistreat.

Old French inherited this as traitier, to treat or deal with, and with the intensifying prefix en- formed entraitier, to treat earnestly, to deal with someone pressingly. When the Normans brought the word to England after 1066, Middle English entreaten carried both strands: to treat or entertain, and to beseech. For a century entreat could still mean to treat medically, to negotiate, to invite — I entreat you to dinner once meant I invite you — as well as to beg. The noun entreaty, formed with -y by the 16th century on the model of treaty, specialty, narrowed to the pleading sense only: an earnest request, a humble prayer.

  • Sound shifts: Latin tractare with hard k + t cluster simplifies in Old French to traitier, losing the c before t and palatalizing ti to i. English borrows the French vowel and stress, then shifts stress in the noun: entreat /ɪnˈtriːt/ verb versus entreaty /ɪnˈtriːti/ noun, with the -y pronounced as long e. Spelling retains ea for the French ai.
  • Semantic drift: pull → handle → treat → deal pressingly with → beg earnestly → formal humble petition. Each step keeps the idea of drawing someone toward you, now emotionally rather than physically. To entreat is etymologically to try to pull another's will toward your own.
  • Socio-cultural context: In feudal and early modern hierarchy, entreaty was the correct language upward: subjects entreated kings, children entreated fathers, lovers entreated ladies, litigants entreated courts. It implied acknowledged inferiority without abjectness, unlike begging, which could imply destitution, or praying, which implied worship. Legal phrases like at the entreaty of preserve this formal petition sense.
  • Literary attestations: Shakespeare is full of entreaties — lovers, courtiers, and condemned men entreat. The King James Bible uses entreat for gentle persuasion. Dickens in A Christmas Carol uses less open to entreaty to measure Scrooge against nature: even pitiless rain is merely deaf, while Scrooge actively refuses the social duty to be moved by pleading. Victorian readers heard entreaty as dignified, slightly elevated, not slangy.
  • Modern narrowing: Treaty kept the negotiation sense while entreaty kept the pleading sense, a clean split of twins. Today entreaty is formal and literary; we speak of pleas, appeals, requests in ordinary life, reserving entreaty for solemn, repeated, or moving appeals. Plural entreaties is common: despite her entreaties.

What entreaty never lost is earnestness. A request can be casual; an entreaty by definition is heartfelt, repeated, humble. It assumes the granter is free to refuse and must be moved, not compelled. That is why Dickens pairs it with pelting rain: rain cannot be entreated because it has no will, Scrooge will not be entreated though he has one, and the comparison condemns him. From dragging nets to dragging hearts, the word's long pull ends in persuasion.
