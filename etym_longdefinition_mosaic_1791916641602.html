Mosaic, with a capital M, is an adjective meaning “of or relating to Moses”: Mosaic law, Mosaic covenant, Mosaic dispensation, Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch. It is formed from the proper name Moses on the model of Latin adjectives in -aicus, and it belongs almost entirely to theology, biblical criticism and legal history. In the source passage the word appears in quotation marks — the “Mosaic” demand of “an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth” — because the author is citing a technical label: the retributive formula belongs to the law traditionally ascribed to Moses, and the inverted commas mark it as a term of art rather than a description.

The quotation is apt. The talion formula occurs three times in the Pentateuch, in Exodus 21:24, Leviticus 24:20 and Deuteronomy 19:21, and in each case it is set among provisions about injury, false witness and asylum. Read historically, the formula functions at least partly as a limitation — one eye for an eye, not a vendetta — which is why legal historians are uneasy when “Mosaic” is used as a synonym for pure revenge. The essay’s argument requires the opposite emphasis: it wants the three ancient systems, Roman, Babylonian and Mosaic, to stand together as witnesses to a single retributive instinct.

Senses of Mosaic (of Moses)

  • Belonging to Moses personally: the Mosaic writings, the Mosaic vision, the Mosaic rod; rare outside scriptural commentary.
  • Belonging to the law attributed to Moses: Mosaic law, the Mosaic code, Mosaic legislation, Mosaic precepts. This is by far the commonest use, and it is the one in the source.
  • Belonging to the religious system of the Pentateuch: the Mosaic covenant, Mosaic worship, Mosaic ritual, the Mosaic priesthood, Mosaic Judaism.
  • Theological periodisation: the Mosaic dispensation or Mosaic economy — the era of law between the Abrahamic covenant and the Christian dispensation, a staple of covenant theology.
  • Authorship and dating: Mosaic authorship, the traditional attribution of the Pentateuch to Moses, contested since the seventeenth century and rejected by mainstream biblical criticism.
  • Chronology and cosmogony: Mosaic chronology (the biblical timetable favoured by Ussher and his successors) and Mosaic cosmogony (the creation account of Genesis read as literal history).
  • Modern scholarship: the “Mosaic distinction”, Jan Assmann’s term for the monotheistic separation of true from false religion — a deliberate extension of the adjective into cultural theory.
  • Variants: Mosaical, common in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and still occasionally used; post-Mosaic for material dated after Moses.

The separate word mosaic (art)

English also has a noun and adjective mosaic — usually lowercase — naming a picture or pattern made from small pieces of stone, glass or tile, and by extension any variegated composite (“a mosaic of cultures”). That word is etymologically unrelated to Moses. It comes through French and Italian from medieval Latin mosaicus, which descends from late Latin musivum opus, “work of the Muses”, from musa, a Muse. The resemblance to Mosaic is a coincidence of Latin spelling, though it has caused centuries of confusion, and the occasional claim that mosaic pavements are called after Moses is folk etymology.

  • Art sense: a surface decoration composed of tesserae; the technique itself, as in Roman floor mosaics or the gold-ground mosaics of Ravenna.
  • Figurative sense: a patchwork or composite — “the mosaic of immigrant neighbourhoods”, “a mosaic of memories”.
  • Biological sense: an organism or tissue containing genetically different cells, hence mosaicism; also mosaic development in embryology.
  • Plant pathology: mosaic disease, a viral mottling of leaves, first described in tobacco in the 1890s.
  • Technology: NCSA Mosaic, the 1993 web browser that helped popularise the graphical World Wide Web.
  • Materials: mosaic gold, a pigment of stannic sulphide; mosaic glass and mosaic tile as trade terms.

Register and collocation

Capitalised Mosaic is bookish and confessional: it appears in sermons, theological essays, and histories of law and religion, and it usually signals that the writer is talking about a system rather than a person. Lowercase mosaic is neutral and technical, at home in art history, biology and journalism. The capital letter is a useful but imperfect signal, since sentence-initial mosaic art is also capitalised, and since many publications lowercase the theological adjective. In the source the inverted commas do the disambiguating work that the capital cannot.
