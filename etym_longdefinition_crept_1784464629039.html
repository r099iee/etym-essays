Crept is the strong (irregular) past tense — and, in modern standard English, also the past participle — of the verb creep. As a verb form it denotes movement that is slow, careful, low to the ground, and frequently stealthy or hesitant: a manner of advancing that avoids noise, speed, and visibility, and that hugs the surface over which it moves. Where walk implies an upright, deliberate stride and run implies rapid propulsion, crept insists on a low, gradual, close-to-the-ground traverse — the motion of a cat stalking, a child sneaking downstairs, ivy spreading across a wall, or fear spreading imperceptibly through a mind. The word is native Germanic, one of the oldest strata of English, and that deep age is felt in the odd, strong inflection and in the stubborn physical image that never quite detaches from the verb. In the sentence "After a while and a drink he crept nervously to the door of the parlour," the strong past tense performs precise narrative work. Bilbo does not stride, march, or hurry to the parlour door as a confident host would; he edges toward it in a low, cautious, almost apologetic motion, approaching the noise of the dwarves like a trespasser in his own home. The adverb nervously locks the verb's semantic core: fear and reluctance shape the gait. The form crept (rather than the nonstandard *creeped) marks the action as a single, completed, discrete cautious traverse from the relative safety of the kitchen toward the ordeal of the parlour. The tone is comic-pathos: the narrator offers physical characterization through a verb whose long history carries connotations of animality, lowliness, and unease. The register is plain narrative, vivid in manner, exactly suited to a moment of quiet social comedy. Primary senses of creep / crept
  • Of persons and animals — to move slowly with the body close to the ground, on hands and feet or on the belly, quietly and cautiously ("the cat crept toward the bird").
  • Of plants and growth — to spread or grow along a surface, advancing low instead of standing upright ("ivy crept up the wall"); the sense stresses gradual, clinging advance.
  • Of time and abstract forces — to advance slowly, relentlessly, and often imperceptibly ("the hours crept by"; "a creeping dread"); the emphasis falls on gradualness and inevitability.
  • Of behaviour (older uses) — to act servilely or fawningly, to cringe or curry favour, e.g. to "creep to" a superior; a derogatory sense of abject subservience.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern jargon senses
  • Feature creep / scope creep (software, project management): the gradual, uncontrolled expansion of a product's features or requirements beyond the original plan. Historical bridge: the core image of slow, stealthy, unperceived advance is transferred from physical crawling to the insidious growth of specifications — a project "creeps" past its own design.
  • Mission creep (military, political): the gradual expansion of an operation beyond its original objectives, built on the identical image of incremental, easy-to-miss advance.
  • Creep (noun, informal): an unpleasant, obnoxious, or predatory person; "to give someone the creeps" = to cause unease or disgust. Bridge: the skin-crawling sensation of small things moving over the body, extended first to uncanny dread and then to a repellent person.
  • Creepy (adjective): eerie or repellent, from the same skin-crawling metaphor; a 19th–20th-century development that has become fully standard.
  • Creep of sound, light, fog, and mist: slow diffusion or spread ("a creeping mist"; "creeping dawn").
  • Creeper (noun): a creeping plant or animal; in modern gaming, the Minecraft mob that creeps up on the player and explodes — a striking illustration of how far the root has travelled into digital culture.
  • Creep (noun, engineering): the slow plastic deformation of metal under sustained stress; a 20th-century scientific jargon use of the same gradual-advance image.
  • Creeping (adjective, colloquial): of prices or costs rising slowly but steadily ("costs keep creeping up").
Grammar and form notes
  • Crept is irregular: creep / crept / crept, inherited from the Old English strong-verb system; *creeped is nonstandard in careful prose, though it appears in informal speech and in children's overregularization.
  • The -t ending reflects historical reduction of an older participle cluster; the form was standardized in literary English by the early modern period (16th–17th centuries).
  • Crept serves both preterite ("he crept") and participial ("has crept", "had crept") functions in modern English.
Register and collocation Crept belongs to plain, vivid narrative register and collocates readily with cats, thieves, shadows, fog, time, fear, and ivy; it combines with directional and manner adverbs and prepositions (crept into, crept up, crept toward, crept away, crept silently, crept nervously). Because it marks a completed motion, it is the narratological past: the finished traverse from safety to ordeal that this card's sentence records.
