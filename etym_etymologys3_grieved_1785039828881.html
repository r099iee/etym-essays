The past form GRIEVED grows from one of the most concrete images in the whole Indo-European lexicon: the root *gʷreh₂-, 'heavy, weighty,' written *gwer- in older handbooks. Whatever was heavy pressed down on whatever bore it, and from that physical pressure grew an entire family of words for burden and sorrow that spans the Indo-European world. The Hellenic branch produced Greek barús, 'heavy,' and báros, 'weight,' which English later borrowed into science and music as barometer and baritone. The Indo-Aryan branch produced Sanskrit gurú, 'heavy, weighty, venerable,' which passed into Hindi as guru, 'teacher, master' — a weighty person, one whose words carry gravity — and thence back into English as guru. The Germanic, Celtic, and Balto-Slavic branches all show related forms. But the direct ancestor of our word is the Italic branch: Latin gravis, 'heavy.' From gravis Latin built a small but productive family. The abstract noun gravitas, 'weight,' gives English gravity and gravitate. The adjective itself was borrowed into English through French as grave, 'solemn, serious' — someone whose gravity of manner is itself a kind of weight. And the denominative verb gravare, 'to burden, weigh down,' was passed into Gallo-Romance as Old French grever, 'to afflict, burden, oppress.' English met the word in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, in the legal and feudal vocabulary of complaint: to grieve was to oppress or burden someone, and a grief was a burden or injury laid upon them. The noun grievance and the adjective grievous — 'heavily injurious, atrocious' — preserve this oldest stratum to this day: a grievous wrong is a heavy wrong, and a grievance is a weight of complaint laid at another's door. The crucial change — the internalization of the weight — happened over the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Where greven had meant 'to press down physically or oppress,' it came to mean 'to sadden, to weigh down in spirit': the body's heaviness became the heart's. Middle English greven is attested from around 1300, and the sense 'to feel sorrow' is established by the Early Modern period. The noun grief travelled the same road: from 'a burden or injury' to 'the feeling of deep sorrow,' a meaning fixed in English by the sixteenth century. The physical image was never wholly lost, however. We still speak of being 'weighed down by grief,' and the whole family — grief, grievance, grievous, aggrieve — carries the pressure of the PIE root at its core. The related aggrieve arrived through Old French agrever from Latin aggravare ('to make heavier'), and its participle aggrieved names the state of one who has been wronged — a person made heavy by injustice. The socio-cultural history of the word is a history of mourning itself. In medieval society, grief was public and communal — mourners wore black, observed formal laments, and performed their sorrow in ritual. The word grieve participated in that display: to grieve was to enact loss in the presence of the community. In the modern period, as mourning became more private and psychological, the word followed: grieving is now understood as an inward, prolonged process of adjustment, the subject of 'grief work' and therapy. Tolkien's use stands at the older end of this spectrum. When Elrond 'grieved to remember the ruin of the town of Dale,' the word carries both registers at once — the ancient heaviness of the root, pressing on the spirit, and the elegiac, long-memoried sorrow of a culture that knows how to mourn. The grief of an immortal Elf for a town of mortal Men, 'its merry bells' and 'the burned banks of the bright River Running,' is the grief of memory itself: weight without outlet, the PIE root of heaviness doing its quiet, eternal work.
