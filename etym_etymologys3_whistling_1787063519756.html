A word born from its own sound. Whistle is an onomatopoeic or sound-symbolic formation: the initial hw- cluster mimics the breathy rush of air and the sibilant -stl- the shrill edge of the note, so the word practically describes itself. It can be reconstructed only as far back as Proto-Germanic, as *hwistlōjan "to whistle", with close Germanic kin in Old Norse hvisla "to whisper" and Old English hwisprian "to whisper" — all sharing the hissing, blowing *hw- opening. No reliable PIE ancestor can be established because such sound-mimicking words are coined independently in every language (compare Latin fistula "pipe", which some have loosely linked, though the connection is doubtful and must not be asserted).

Old English: whistling in the heroic age. The verb hwistlian "to whistle" is attested from about 725, in the world of Beowulf, where the participle hwistlende describes weapons and air moving shrilly — an early literary life that already values the word for its piercing, martial sound. Alongside it stood the closely related hwisprian "to whisper", showing the early Germanic mind heard a family of breathy sounds together.

Middle English: voice, instrument, and proverb. In Middle English whistlen covered both the lip-whistle and the whistling instrument — a small pipe or horn. The sound became socially meaningful: proverbs and folklore began policing who might whistle, and the 16th-century saw the famous rhyme "a whistling woman and a crowing hen are neither fit for God nor men" — a folk judgement, now archaic and rejected, that marked whistling as a masculine privilege. Yet the same period made the whistle a cheerful emblem of the craftsman and labourer, the whistling boy and the whistling ploughman appearing constantly in songs and jest-books.

The 17th–18th centuries: signal, stage, and sea. In the age of sail, the whistle became a working signal aboard ship and in the workshop; stage and ballad made the whistler a lively, cheerful stock figure. The word also built its great idiom bank: "to whistle for" something (to want it in vain, from the sailor's whistle for a wind that never comes) is recorded from the 16th–17th centuries and is still current today.

The 19th-century steam revolution. The railway and factory age mechanised whistling. The steam whistle — a device for converting pressurised steam into a piercing alarm — made "whistling" an industrial sound of departure, shift-change, and danger, and the whistling kettle carried the same physics into every kitchen. This is the era that made the sound feel mechanical and elemental rather than merely human, preparing the ground for Tolkien's "fierce whistling steam".

The 20th century: bullets, idioms, and psychology. Two world wars made "whistling" a sound of projectiles and falling bombs, while popular speech added the defiant idioms "whistling in the dark" and "whistling past the graveyard" — whistling as the sound of manufactured courage. In acoustics and aerodynamics the word became technical: "whistling" now names the high-frequency tonal noise of air through gaps, reeds, and tubes, from wind instruments to vehicle aerodynamics.

Modern status and the whistle-blower. In the 21st century whistling spans the whole range — birdsong, the casual tuneful lip-whistle, the referee's signal, the boiler's scream, the wind in the rigging, the shriek of steam — and it has gained a wholly modern civic descendant: the whistle-blower, whose name comes from the referee's whistle that stops the game. In Tolkien's sentence the word is used in its fiercest elemental register: the rising steam whistles as it escapes under pressure, and the sound-word tunes the reader's ear for the blaze that follows — one more demonstration of how an imitative sound-word has carried a single, clear acoustic image unchanged from the heroic age to the industrial and on to modern fantasy.
