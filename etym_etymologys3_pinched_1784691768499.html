The biography of pinched runs from a prehistoric root for pricking, through a French verb of squeezing, into one of the most useful small verbs of English — and out again into the street slang of theft and the sleek gesture-commands of the smartphone. It is a word that never lost its hand.

The uncertain origin. The verb enters Middle English around 1200 as pinchen, borrowed from Old North French pincier (Old French pincer), "to pinch, squeeze, nip". Where the French verb came from, no one can say with certainty. The most popular hypothesis derives it from a Vulgar Latin *punctiāre, "to prick, puncture", built on Latin punctum, "a point, a prick", the past participle of pungere, "to prick, sting" — and pungere in turn is agreed to continue the Proto-Indo-European root *peuĝ-, "to prick, stab, mark", the same root that lies behind English puncture, punctual, point, and poignant. But the handbooks are honest: the OED labels the whole Romance verb "of unknown origin", and the *punctiāre stage is reconstructed, not attested. An honest chain therefore marks the pre-French stages as the probable-but-unproven hypothesis and keeps the attested story — French in the twelfth century, English from about 1200 — as its solid spine. What is certain is the picture: the word has always carried two fingers closing on something.

Sound and form. Within English the changes are modest: Middle English pinchen → modern pinch, with the -en infinitive ending dropped and the vowel settling; the past participle pinched is a regular weak formation. The French side shows the characteristic Old French reshaping of the Latin cluster, and the borrowing gives English the verb ready-made. From the start, to pinch was to close a thumb and forefinger on something — and everything the word has ever meant has been an echo of that gesture.

  • Semantic drift — the wheel of the squeeze. From the physical squeeze the meanings radiate like spokes from a hub. (1) Pain and privation: to be "pinched with cold" is to be nipped by frost; a "pinched face" is thin and drawn, squeezed hollow by want or worry; "the pinch of poverty" is the pressure of need. (2) Money: to be "pinched for money" is to be short; the "pinch-penny" miser squeezes every penny; "to feel the pinch" is to suffer financially. (3) Quantity: "a pinch of salt" is what the fingers can hold — the gesture itself becomes a measure, and the word slides into the kitchen as a noun. (4) Theft: in seventeenth-century slang "to pinch" came to mean to steal — a quick, deft, hand-to-hand snatch, exactly what a pickpocket does, and the sense in which Tolkien uses it. (5) Arrest: from the thief's capture by the hand of the law, "to pinch" also came to mean to arrest, "to get pinched" being to be arrested, recorded from the nineteenth century. (6) Difficulty and emergency: "in a pinch" means if pressed, if necessary — the squeeze re-read as pressure and constraint. (7) Modern technology: the touchscreen "pinch" gesture, two fingers closing together to zoom out, and the engineer's "pinch point", a place where a limb can be trapped between moving parts — the ancient hand-gesture still naming the newest machines.
  • The registers of theft. The narrator's sentence in The Hobbit — "picked the trolls' pockets, pinched the very mutton off the spits, purloined the beer" — is a tiny tour of the English verbs of stealing: picked (plain, literal), pinched (slang, quick, manual), purloined (formal, literary, from Old French porloigner "to put away"). Tolkien lets the escalating diction do comic work: the legendary burglar, the sentence suggests, is a master of every register, from street argot to the grandest theft-vocabulary, and he does it all without being noticed.
  • Socio-cultural factors. The slang senses grew in the street life of early modern London, where the pickpocket's trade was the great urban crime and where "to pinch" — the nimble finger-squeeze — named the nimblest kind of stealing. The police sense followed the growth of professional policing in the nineteenth century, and "the pinch" entered the vocabulary of arrest. In the twentieth century "pinched" as "stolen" crossed the Atlantic and settled into casual American English, while "pinch" kept its kitchen life in the small measure of a pinch of spice or salt.
  • Literary attestations. The word appears in the everyday prose and drama of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries ("he pinched her cheek"); in the comic writing and slang lexicons of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in the theft sense; in the nineteenth-century novel for the drawn face and the pinch of poverty; in detective fiction and police slang for the arrest; and in Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937) in the mock-heroic catalogue of the ideal burglar — where "pinched" sits perfectly between the practical "picked" and the grand "purloined".
  • Register today. Modern English uses pinched in all three of its chief lives at once: literally of fingers and faces, informally of theft and arrest, and technically of gestures and pinch points. Its range — from nursery play to police slang to the smartphone screen — is a remarkable demonstration of how one hand-gesture word can keep branching for eight hundred years without breaking.
