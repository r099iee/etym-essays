Leaping is the present participle of the verb leap, denoting the act of springing or bounding suddenly into the air — upward, forward, or from place to place — with force and momentum, covering a considerable distance in a single impulse. In The Hobbit the word is stripped of every athletic, joyful, or graceful association: the creature whose shaggy coat has caught fire is leaping to and fro, yelping horribly, and the verb now conveys frantic, explosive, uncontrolled motion, the convulsive, panic-driven flailing of a being in searing pain. The continuous aspect is essential: this is not a single jump but a sustained, violent to-and-fro jerking, a creature convulsing in its own fire, unable to escape, and the reader feels the motion as involuntary and chaotic — the predator's menace dissolved into a pathetic, almost comic spectacle of suffering, the great Warg reduced to a burning thing that can only bound and shriek. Tolkien chooses the participle precisely because its unbroken action stretches the agony across the whole sentence, joining the leaping to the yelping in one continuous loop of pain. PRIMARY SENSE — PHYSICAL SPRINGING AND BOUNDING At its centre, to leap is to propel the body off the ground by a sudden muscular effort, springing upward, forward, or across, so as to cover a distance or height greater than ordinary walking or running allows. A dancer leaps across the stage; a salmon leaps out of the rushing river; a cat leaps onto a wall. The noun leap names the act or the distance covered. The distinctive feature of leaping among the verbs of leaving the ground is range and drama: a leap implies a longer, higher, more forceful trajectory than a jump, often executed at speed and covering real ground. SECONDARY SENSE — THE METAPHORIC LEAP From the physical spring, leap extends richly into figurative language, where the image is of a sudden, dramatic, often risky transition:
  • a leap of faith — an act of trusting or committing to something without evidence or certainty, the abandonment of safety; the phrase (from Kierkegaard's 'leap of faith', in Danish springet af troen) reframes religious trust as a bold jump into the unknown.
  • a leap in the dark — a hazardous or blind venture, an action taken without knowing the outcome.
  • leap to conclusions — to reach a judgement hastily, skipping intermediate steps of reasoning; to leap at an opportunity — to seize it eagerly and at once.
  • leap to one's feet — to rise suddenly, springing up; a leap of the imagination — a sudden, creative jump between ideas.
  • quantum leap — literally, in physics, the abrupt transition of an electron between energy levels, a discontinuous jump; colloquially, a dramatic and sudden advance or change (a quantum leap in technology), the word borrowing the prestige of physics for any great bound forward.
THIRD SENSE — TIME, CALENDAR AND NOMENCLATURE The leap into time is institutionalised in English: a leap year is a year with 366 days, the extra day (leap day, 29 February) 'leaping' the calendar forward so that it stays aligned with the solar year; in the traditional leap-year custom, women were said to be entitled to propose marriage, leaping over the usual convention. The game leapfrog names the children's sport of vaulting over a stooping playmate; the expression by leaps and bounds means with rapid, dramatic progress, the image of an animal covering ground in huge bounds. MODERN JARGON AND SPECIALISED USES
  • Physics: 'quantum leap' (quantum jump) is the technical term for the discrete transition of an atomic or subatomic particle between energy states, famously discontinuous — the model for the colloquial sense of a sudden advance.
  • Timekeeping: a 'leap second' is an extra second inserted into Coordinated Universal Time to keep atomic clocks aligned with the Earth's rotation, a borrowing of the leap-image for calibration.
  • Athletics and dance: 'leap' is the standard term for the long bound in ballet (a grand jeté is a great leap) and in athletics for jumps of range and elevation.
  • Zoology: 'leaping' names the saltatorial (jumping) locomotion of animals — leaping frogs, leaping lemurs, the leaping ability of fleas, and the North American 'leaping hare' (the jackrabbit), all built on the participle's image of unbroken bounding.
REGISTER AND SEMANTIC HISTORY Leap is the older, more literary word; jump arrived in English only in the 16th century, so for most of the language's history leap was the ordinary verb of leaving the ground, the word of Beowulf, of dancers, and of chroniclers. In modern English it keeps an archaic or dignified colouring — the verb of epic motion and graceful athletics — while the participle leaping, with its long open vowel and soft consonants, extends the motion in time, describing not one bound but a continuous, sustained flight. In The Hobbit that dignified verb is turned on its head: leaping to and fro is the grammar of agony, the same word that launches salmon and ballet dancers now stitched into the frantic to-and-fro of a burning beast, and the contrast between the word's grace and the creature's helplessness is exactly what makes the passage so dreadful and so memorable.
