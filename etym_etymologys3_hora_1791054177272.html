The word in this card is not the familiar Latin hora meaning hour, but the Tahitian plant name hora, denoting a fish-stupefying plant used with the huteo nut to intoxicate fish. Its history therefore belongs to Austronesian, not Indo-European, linguistics, and to the ethnobotany of the Pacific fish-poison complex centred on Barringtonia asiatica, known across its range as hutu, hotu, futu, putun, butong, and, in the Tahitian source behind this passage, huteo and hora.

The deep background is the Austronesian expansion. Speakers of Proto-Austronesian (Taiwan, c. 3000 BCE) carried a rich tree vocabulary into Island Southeast Asia and Oceania. Comparative evidence reconstructs Proto-Oceanic *putun for Barringtonia asiatica, a large littoral tree with buoyant square fruits dispersed by ocean currents from Madagascar to Tahiti. The fruit, seeds, bark and roots are rich in oleanane triterpene saponins that stun fish, which then float and are gathered by hand. Because the tree is water-dispersed, its pan-tropical distribution is natural, but its use as ichthyotoxin diffused with Austronesian voyagers through the Nicobars, Celebes, Philippines, Marianas, Solomons, Fiji, Samoa and eastern Polynesia. Early European navigators and missionaries recorded the Tahitian forms hutu, hotu, huteo and hora, reflecting regular Polynesian sound shifts where Proto-Polynesian *f becomes Tahitian h, and *t remains t, with vowel variation by dialect and transcription.

Phonetically the development is straightforward and needs no Indo-European sound laws:

  • Austronesian to Oceanic: Proto-Austronesian *p and *q weaken and reshape into Proto-Oceanic *p in *putun, retaining the disyllabic tree-name shape.
  • Oceanic to Polynesian: *p lenites to *f, giving Proto-Polynesian *futu, attested in Samoan futu, Tongan futu and Tahitian hutu after the further Tahitian shift *f to h.
  • Within Tahitian: hutu, hotu, huteo and hora coexist as dialectal or transcriptional variants for the same fish-poison complex; hora in this text denotes the leafy or herbaceous admixture pounded with the huteo nut to make the intoxicating mixture.
  • Borrowing into English: nineteenth-century travel, missionary and anthropological literature borrowed the Tahitian form unchanged as hora, italicised or lower-cased as a foreign plant name, never nativised with English plural or stress shift.

Semantically there is no drift in the European sense, only ethnobotanical specification. In Tahitian the word always denoted the plant and, by metonymy, the stupefying preparation made from it. In English it has only ever been a quoted exoticism, appearing in anthropological catalogues of narcotic fishing alongside derris, mundulea, tephrosia and Barringtonia. It never acquired the temporal senses of Latin hora — hour, season, horoscope, Horae — and dictionaries that list only hora = hour simply omit this homograph because it belongs to Pacific ethnography rather than the classical lexicon.

Socio-cultural context explains why the word survives at all. Narcotic fishing — damming a stream or lagoon pool, pounding saponin-rich nuts and leaves, and scooping up the floating fish — was widespread, efficient and ritually regulated across Polynesia. Colonial observers, including the source of this passage, catalogued it as evidence of primitive ingenuity in the war of species. The pairing huteo nut plus hora plant is thus a recipe, not a taxonomic pair, and hora here likely covers a secondary stupefacient herb (compare Tahitian horahora for Lepidium bidentatum and hora recorded for several shore plants) pounded to release poison slowly while sinking.

Literary attestation is thin but consistent:

  • Forster and early Tahiti vocabularies (late 18th c.): hutu / hotu for Barringtonia, establishing the root.
  • 19th-c. missionary dictionaries: Tahitian-French wordlists record hutu as fish-poison tree and hora as a plant name, sometimes glossed generally as herb or shrub.
  • Anthropological fishing literature (early 20th c.): comparative lists of fish poisons from Madagascar to Tahiti cite Barringtonia asiatica with its string of vernaculars, preserving hora exactly in this recipe context.
  • Modern botany: standardises on Barringtonia asiatica (syn. B. speciosa, Mammea asiatica) with vernaculars hutu, hotu, futu, putat, butong, fish-poison tree, leaving hora as a historical Tahitian variant rather than an accepted common name.

The crucial lexicographic caution is homograph separation. Latin hora — from Greek hōra season, period, hour, from PIE *yeh₁r- year, season, source of English hour, horoscope, horology — is one of the commonest classical words and dominates all European dictionaries. Tahitian hora has no genetic relationship to it whatsoever; the identity of form is pure coincidence across unrelated families. Any derivation that traces the fish-plant through Latin, Greek or PIE is therefore false. The correct lineage is Austronesian *putun to Polynesian *futu to Tahitian hutu / hora to English quoted borrowing, with residual uncertainty over whether hora denoted Barringtonia itself or a companion stupefacient herb mixed with the huteo nut, a nuance the source text leaves unresolved.
