English MALINGER (verb, first attested in the 18th c., c. 1780s, in British military and naval usage, meaning "to feign illness or incapacity to avoid duty") — derived by back-formation or adaptation from the French adjective malingre "sickly, ailing, puny, weak" (a genuine description of ill health, which English converted into the verb meaning "to act sickly") — of debated origin, traditionally analyzed as mal- "bad, evil" + an obscure second element. Stage chain (single linear derivation, with the origin of the French base flagged as debated): 1) PIE *mel- "bad, wrong, evil" (reconstructed; one of the proposed sources of Latin malus "bad, evil"; the PIE pedigree of malus is debated, and some scholars instead derive it from an Italic substrate or from *malo- "deceived" — see note below) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE. 2) Latin malus "bad, evil, wicked" (adjective, attested from the 3rd c. BCE), with adverb male "badly, ill" — the source of the Romance prefix mal-. 3) Old French mal "bad, evil, ill" (adjective/adverb/noun, attested from the 9th c.) — the first element of the French word. 4) French malingre "sickly, ailing, puny, emaciated" (adjective, attested from the 12th–13th c.; formation mal- "bad, ill" + second element of uncertain origin, traditionally connected with Old French haingre "haggard, thin, lean" of Germanic origin, or with maigre "thin, meager" from Latin macer "thin" — the second element is debated, see note) — the direct donor to English. 5) English malinger "to feign illness or incapacity in order to avoid work or duty" (verb, first attested c. 1783–1785 in British military usage; also the noun malingerer and gerund malingering from the 19th c.) — the target root word, MALINGER. Note on debate: The etymology of malingre is not settled. The traditional analysis reads it as mal- "bad" + a second element; the two main proposals are (a) Old French haingre/heingre "haggard, thin, lean," of Germanic origin (cf. Middle Dutch henger etc.), giving "badly lean, ill-thin," and (b) association with maigre "thin, meager" (from Latin macer). Modern handbooks often describe the whole as "of uncertain origin." The PIE source of Latin malus is likewise debated: *mel- is frequently cited, but the reconstruction is insecure, and some authorities posit a non-Indo-European substrate word. The English stage is secure: the verb is first attested in British military documents of the 1780s, and the semantic shift from the French adjective "sickly" to the English verb "to pretend to be sickly" happened in English, not French.
