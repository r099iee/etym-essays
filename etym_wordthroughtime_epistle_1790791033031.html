- Classical Antiquity (5th–1st c. BCE) - Greek *epistolē* (*epistellō*, "to send to, dispatch by message") denoted any message, oral dispatch, or written letter; developed in Hellenistic rhetoric into a distinct literary and philosophical genre (Epicurus, Plato's letters).
- Roman Era (1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) - Latinized as *epistula*; Horace, Seneca, and Pliny the Younger perfected the verse epistle and philosophical letter as polished literary art forms.
- Early Christian & Medieval Church (1st–14th c.) - Became sacred terminology for the New Testament apostolic letters (Paul, Peter, John); in Western Christian liturgy, the *Epistle* designated the specific scripture reading preceding the Gospel during Mass.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1400) - Entered English via Old French *epistle*; used for the liturgical reading ("singing the epistle") and formal written communications (Chaucer, Wycliffe).
- Renaissance & Enlightenment (16th–18th c.) - Flourished as a major poetic genre (Alexander Pope's *Epistles to Several Persons*, *Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot*) and the epistolary novel (Richardson).
- 19th–21st Century - Retained in liturgical rites, academic biblical criticism, and used playfully or archly in modern English to refer to an unusually long, serious, or elaborate personal letter.
