BAR-TAILED is a hyphenated attributive adjective used chiefly in ornithology to describe a bird whose tail bears transverse bars or bands of contrasting colour, most famously the bar-tailed godwit, Limosa lapponica. In the source context it identifies one wader among a mixed migratory flock with curlew, knot and plover, signalling a precise field mark rather than a casual flourish. The term belongs to the technical vocabulary of field guides, museum labels and conservation literature, where barred tail, barred rump and barred under-tail coverts separate closely related species at distance.

The primary sense is strictly diagnostic. A bar-tailed bird shows alternating dark and light transverse markings across the rectrices or tail-coverts, visible in flight or when the tail is fanned. In Limosa lapponica this contrasts with the solid black tail with white base of the black-tailed godwit, Limosa limosa, allowing observers to distinguish two otherwise similar large godwits. The same compounding logic produces bar-tailed lark, bar-tailed trogon, bar-tailed cuckoo-dove and bar-tailed godwit as fixed common names, each capitalised in checklist orthography when part of the official name. The hyphen marks a unified attributive modifier: a bar-tailed godwit is a godwit that is bar-tailed, not a tailed godwit that is bar.

  • Primary ornithological sense: having a tail crossed by bar-like bands, used as a diagnostic plumage character in waders, raptors, larks, trogons and pigeons.
  • Secondary general descriptive sense: of any animal or object, having the tail or tail-like rear marked with transverse stripes, for example a bar-tailed squirrelfish, a bar-tailed kite model, or a bar-tailed aircraft livery.
  • Tertiary extended and figurative senses: in heraldry, textiles and design, barred tail-like projections; in aviation and engineering, informally, a tailplane or rear section with striped markings; metaphorically, anything trailing a barred pattern behind it.

Polysemy here is shallow but instructive. The general descriptive sense derives by broadening the ornithological prototype: once birders learn bar-tailed as barred tail feathers, the pattern is analogically extended to fish, insects and artefacts. The heraldic and textile bridge is older: Middle English barre already meant a transverse stripe in heraldry and cloth, so bar-tailed revives a medieval visual sense inside a modern compound. What it never means is tailed by a bar in the sense of confined or barred from entry; the stress pattern BAR-tailed and the ornithological context block that parsing. Register is technical-neutral, never slang, and connotation is precise and admiring in birding culture, where finding a bar-tailed vagrant among common waders confers prestige.

Domain-specific nuances matter. In taxonomy, Bar-tailed with capitals is part of a proper common name governed by checklist authorities such as the IOC, while lowercase bar-tailed is a free descriptive adjective applicable to any individual with that pattern. In field identification, bar-tailed implies assessment in flight, when the tail pattern shows, and is often paired with companion marks such as streaked back, long upturned bill and barred underwings. In conservation writing, bar-tailed godwit has become shorthand for extreme migration endurance, so the adjective carries an aura of athleticism and vulnerability to habitat loss at Yellow Sea stopovers that a purely morphological term would not normally bear.
