- 16th c. (Medical pathology) - Borrowed via Latin from Greek *leikhēn*, initially applied in English medical texts to refer to scaly, spreading cutaneous eruptions and ringworm-like skin diseases.
- 17th–18th c. (Early botanical taxonomy) - Adopted by naturalists like Ray and Linnaeus to designate cryptogamic, crustaceous plants encrusting stones and bark, often conflated with mosses.
- 19th c. (Symbiotic revolution) - Radically reclassified after Simon Schwendener demonstrated in 1869 that lichens are dual composite organisms rather than independent plants, sparking intense scientific debate.
- 20th c. (Ecological indicator & air monitoring) - Recognized globally as sensitive bioindicators of atmospheric pollution (especially sulfur dioxide) and microclimate health.
- Contemporary (Aesthetic & philosophical) - Evokes ancient wilderness, geological timescales, endurance in extreme environments, and symbiotic collaboration in philosophical discourse.
