Grumpy is an adjective meaning bad-tempered, surly, touchy and easily annoyed, disposed to sullen silence or terse, grumbling replies rather than to open rage or violence. It belongs to a distinctly colloquial register of English, and, unlike angry or enraged, it nearly always carries an undertone of amused, affectionate tolerance: a grumpy person is more comic than threatening, a mood rather than a menace. Senses and usages:
  • Settled temperament. A person may be grumpy by nature — a habitual, low-grade bad temper, touchy and quick to take offence, expressed in muttering and terse replies.
  • Passing mood. One may merely be grumpy for an afternoon — temporarily irritable, out of sorts, cross without a cause. The word covers both a permanent disposition and a transient state.
  • Sounds, expressions and looks. A grumpy face, a grumpy answer, a grumpy look, a grumpy tone: the adjective transfers from the person to what the person says or shows.
  • Transferred to things. A grumpy old car, a grumpy kettle, a grumpy engine — inanimate things that are cranky, unreliable or uncooperative, treated with the same affectionate irony.
  • Related forms. Grump (noun): a surly person, or a fit of ill humour; also a verb, to grump, to be sulky or complain in a low voice. Grumpish (adjective, now rare or archaic): surly, ill-tempered. Grumpily (adverb) and grumpiness (noun): the state of being grumpy.
  • Register and colour. Grumpy is informal and homely, at home in speech, children's books and light fiction; it softens bad temper into a slightly comic, forgivable human failing, which is exactly how the context sentence uses it.
In the context sentence from The Hobbit, the word condenses a whole catalogue of physical discomfort into a single moral-psychological state. After tea-time, in a day-long downpour, with a hood dripping into the eyes, a cloak full of water, a tired pony stumbling on stones, the company are "too grumpy to talk" — not angry with one another, but worn into a sullen, sodden silence in which conversation is no longer worth the effort. The homely, faintly comic word keeps the tone of the narrative playful even in discomfort, allowing the reader to smile at the drenched travellers while sharing their misery. Historical semantic bridge: the word is purely English and recent. It grows out of grumble (adopted c. 1580, itself echoic of a low rumbling sound) through the clipped dialectal back-formation grump (early 18th century — first a verb meaning "to sulk", then a noun for a fit of ill humour or the surly person who suffers one), from which grumpish (c. 1725) and then grumpy (1778) were formed with the ordinary adjective suffix -y. The movement is from sound to disposition: what begins as an audible growl becomes, by degrees, a silent state of being. By the time grumpy reaches modern English it no longer needs a noise at all — a grumpy person may say nothing whatsoever, and the word names the mood rather than the mutter.
