- 17th–18th c. — the parade square: right about! is barked as two beats, the recruit’s pivot to the rear over the right shoulder, exact and terrifying.
- 19th c. — fused in print: drill books and memoirs write rightabout as one word for the single turn and rightabouts for the exercise in general.
- Mid-19th c. — westward loosening: soldiers turned settlers carry the word to waggon-trains, where the rightabouts softens into the ground off to the right, scouted by riders.
- Late 19th c. — dime-novel colour: frontier fiction loves the shout to send a rider to the rightabouts, fixing the phrase as shorthand for practical command.
- 20th c. — drill modernises: armies standardise about-face and right face, leaving rightabouts to sound quaint, rural, and historical.
- Today — a period flavour word: alive in westerns, re-enactment, and horsemanship, signalling dust, discipline, and decisive leadership.
