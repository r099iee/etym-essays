foreboding (noun) — a powerful, uneasy presentiment that something bad or unpleasant is coming; a sense of impending doom. In the Hobbit passage, Bilbo's 'foreboding grew' as the dwarves talk and the shadows lengthen: his vague discomfort hardens into the certainty that something evil is approaching the group.

Core meaning: foreboding is a feeling, not a prediction. It is an emotional and instinctive anticipation of danger or misfortune rather than a reasoned forecast, and it is characteristically unspecific: one has a foreboding that something is wrong without yet being able to say what.

  • 1. Noun — presentiment of evil (primary sense): a creeping dread of what is to come, e.g. a foreboding of disaster. It collocates strongly with grow, creep in, fill, and weigh on.
  • 2. Attributive adjective — ominous: a foreboding silence, i.e. a silence that feels like a warning; a real but rarer adjectival use that describes the thing which produces the feeling rather than the feeling itself.
  • 3. As a mass noun describing atmosphere: the room was full of foreboding; the word often behaves as an uncountable noun describing a mood that hangs over a scene or a place.
  • 4. Related forms with shifted senses: forebode (verb, 'to portend; to have a presentiment'), foreboded, and forebodingly (adverb). A warning: foreboding (anxiety) and the verb bode ('to promise well or ill', as in this bodes ill) share a root, but bode as a noun for 'omen' has largely disappeared from ordinary speech.

Semantic bridge: the word is built from fore- 'before, ahead' + bode 'to announce, portend' + -ing. Its root meaning is literally 'an announcing beforehand', and the shift from 'the thing that is announced' to 'the feeling that arises when one reads the announcement' gives the modern noun its emotional colour: it is knowledge of doom before doom arrives, carried not as fact but as unease.

Register: literary and serious; rare in casual speech, where bad feeling or dread are commoner. It carries a distinctly gothic, suspenseful flavour and is a favourite of fantasy and horror writers precisely because it can hang over a scene without naming the danger.

Grading the feeling: foreboding sits between the mild nagging of misgiving and the paralysis of terror. It is a fear of the known-to-be-coming rather than of the unknown in general: the person who has a foreboding has already accepted that harm is on its way and is simply waiting for it. It can be low and steady — 'a sense of foreboding' — or sharp and sudden: 'foreboding seized her'. Both are legitimate, and the difference in English is signalled by grammar and collocation rather than by a change in the word itself.

How it is used in narrative: in fiction, foreboding is the signature device of foreshadowing. A writer plants foreboding in a character's mind so that the reader anticipates the reveal; the word therefore often appears at the end of a calm passage, just before the danger strikes. Tolkien's sentence — Bilbo's foreboding growing as the shadows lengthen — is a textbook instance: the external darkness and the internal dread rise together, so that by the time the disaster comes the reader was already braced for it. This literary habit is the reason the word is so common in high fantasy, ghost stories, and thrillers and so rare in conversational speech.

Collocations worth learning: a deep / growing / terrible foreboding; a foreboding that something is wrong; filled with foreboding; with a sense of foreboding; foreboding hung over (place/event). Note also the related adjective foreboding in attributive position ('a foreboding landscape'), which overlaps with ominous and sinister and shows the word stretching from a feeling into a quality of the world that causes it.
