- [Earlier English: the plain strong verb] - Smote was once simply the ordinary past tense of the strong verb smite, meaning "to strike or hit", with no special elevation; a blow was a blow.
- [17th century: the biblical register] - Through the Authorized Version ("he smote the rock", "the LORD smote the land"), smote hardens into a solemn, fateful, scriptural word for a blow or a divine judgment, while strike becomes the everyday term for hitting.
- [18th-19th century: literary survival and the smitten idiom] - Smote lives on in elevated poetry and prose as a dignified verb for overwhelming force, while the participle smitten survives separately in the romantic idiom "smitten with love", keeping a faint warmth attached to the smiting.
- [Contemporary sense] - Smote now sounds deliberately archaic and literary: writers reach for it to lift a moment out of colloquial prose into a biblical, fateful register, naming either a mighty blow or a sudden overwhelming emotion that strikes a person down.
