Banquets (plural noun; singular: banquet) are large, formal meals — ceremonial dinners held to celebrate an occasion, to honour a person, or to mark a public event. A banquet is a feast with protocol: it has invitations, a seating plan, a set order of courses, and often speeches and toasts. In the passage from J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the sneezing Bilbo finds his speeches at the banquets of the Wood-elves of the Elvenking limited to "Thag you very buch" — the word chosen precisely because a banquet is where one gives formal speeches, a grand indoor feast of a great hall, in contrast to a simple meal.

Primary meaning. At its core, a banquet is a large and elaborate feast, especially one arranged for a public or ceremonial purpose: a wedding banquet, a state banquet, a victory banquet, a banquet to honour a retiring colleague. The defining features are scale (many guests, much food), formality (organisation, ceremony), and purpose (celebration or honouring). It is always a seated meal with courses, not a standing buffet.

  • State and diplomatic banquets. The state banquet is the grandest fixed form: a head of state or government entertains a visiting monarch or president, with strict protocol, an official menu, and formal toasts. These are rituals of international courtesy.
  • Civic, political and institutional banquets. In the nineteenth century the civic banquet became an institution of public life — mayors, guilds, universities and societies held banquets to raise funds, honour members, and conduct politics over dinner; the after-dinner speech was born in this world.
  • Wedding and family banquets. In many cultures the celebration meal is called a banquet: the Chinese wedding banquet, the Indian wedding feast, the diaspora "banquet hall" of North America. Here the word crosses into everyday life as the name for a large celebratory meal.
  • Religious and historical feasts. Historically the banquet shades into the religious feast (the feast of a saint, the Passover Seder), but the English word banquet is reserved for the secular, formal meal; religious meals are called feasts.
  • Metonymic and extended uses. The word extends by metonymy to the building or room where such meals are held (a banqueting hall, the banqueting house), and figuratively to any lavish sensory treat ("a banquet for the eyes"). The verb to banquet, "to feast sumptuously", is chiefly literary and archaic.

Grammar and usage. Banquet is a regular countable noun (singular banquet, plural banquets). It takes the usual verbs: hold a banquet, give a banquet, attend a banquet. It is modified by occasion adjectives (wedding banquet, state banquet, victory banquet) and place adjectives (a palace banquet). The person who gives it is the host; those who eat are the guests. In modern commercial English the word has spawned the "banquet hall" and "banqueting suite" — rooms hired for weddings and conferences — and the phrase "banquet facilities".

Secondary meanings and semantic bridges. The word has never lost its core sense of "grand feast", but it has drifted in two directions. Upward, into ceremony: from a rich man's dinner it became the fixed state and civic ritual, where the meal is subordinate to speeches, honours, and diplomacy. Downward, into commerce: the "banquet hall" of the modern hotel is a business venue as much as a dining room, and "banquet" now also names any large, set-menu group dinner sold by caterers. The bridge between "little bench" (the original Italian sense) and "grand feast" is the surprising historical link described in the etymology: medieval diners ate light refreshments on benches between the courses of a meal, and the word for that small snack on a little bench (banchetto) climbed the social scale until it named the feast itself. Today no speaker feels any trace of the bench; the word is pure gold-and-candlelight ceremony, which is exactly why Tolkien uses it for the Elvenking's hall.

In the reading context. "His speeches at banquets were limited to 'Thag you very buch.'" The joke depends on the word's formality: banquets are where one makes speeches, and the great celebrator of feasts — Bilbo — is reduced, by a bad cold, to a comic sneeze of a speech in the presence of the Elvenking. The plural banquets also tells us these are repeated, ceremonial occasions, part of the court's rhythm, and thus how long poor Bilbo's embarrassment lasts.
