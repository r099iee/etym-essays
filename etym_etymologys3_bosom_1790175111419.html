A secure Germanic word with an insecure ancestry. The English bosom comes from Old English bōsm, and that in turn comes from Proto-Germanic *bōsm-, a form with one of the most evenly distributed cognate sets in the language: Old Saxon bōsom, Old Frisian bōsa, Middle Dutch bōsem, modern German Busen, and Old Norse bósi, which additionally means the upper lip and, by extension, the chest. That distribution is not in dispute, and it establishes the word as inherited Germanic and old. Beyond that point the dictionaries are careful. The standard convention carries the word back to an Indo-European root *bheh2- ‘to be warm, to heat’, on the reasonable enough ground that a word for the chest might have arisen from the warmth of the body or of the hearth, as the place where one sits close and is comfortable. But that root is thinly attested elsewhere, the semantic bridge to ‘breast’ cannot be demonstrated, and other proposals, connecting the word with Latin bustum and Greek buston ‘breast’, have been made and not accepted. The honest summary is that the word is securely Germanic and that its prehistory is open.

The two Old English lives. Old English bōsm already had both of the lives that the modern word has: the physical chest, and the inner, sheltered, hollow place. The second sense is the crucial one, because it is what made the figurative expansion possible, and Old English uses it of the landscape. A valley, a dell, a sheltered hollow between hills, is a bōsm, and so is the inward curve of the chest; one word covers both, on the ordinary Germanic principle that the metaphor goes from the container to the contained and back again. It is from this early pair that the modern phrases descend, including the biblical bosom of the earth, which the translators of the seventeenth century found ready-made in English and used with great effect.

The medieval turn to the heart. During the Middle Ages the word’s centre of gravity moves decisively from the body to the feelings. A thing kept in the bosom is a thing kept in the heart, and by the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the word is at home in the language of the inner life: the bosom of a person, the bosom of a family, the bosom of the Church. Two consequences follow. The first is the birth of the attributive adjective, in the fifteenth century, in the phrase bosom friend. The phrase is one of the most durable in English, and it survives because it says something the words close friend do not: that the friendship is one in which a person is held against the chest, shielded and known. The second consequence is the great theological expansion. The Vulgate and the later devotional tradition gave English in the bosom of Abraham, and the fourth-century creeds gave it the language of the Son received into the Father’s bosom; from the sixteenth century onwards the bosom of God is a fixed English phrase, and it is doing serious doctrinal work as the translation of a Greek and Latin formula rather than as a metaphor.

Seventeenth and eighteenth century: the technical and the domestic. The word does not stay in the devotional register. In the same period it picks up three technical lives. In Freemasonry and in the clandestine societies that preceded it, a bosom is the concealed lodge or meeting chamber, the inner room where the mysteries are kept, and the term survives in the vocabulary of the craft. In heraldry and metalwork, the bosom of a shield is its concave inner face, the natural home of the image of the shield’s occupant, and the verb to bosom survives a little longer in the language of the craft, meaning to hollow or to curve. In the language of clothing, the bosom of a shirt or coat is the part covering the chest, and a bosom pocket is an inside pocket. All three of these are obsolete, and all three are ordinary in their own fields. The eighteenth century also completes the secular phrase in the bosom of, meaning inside the shelter of, which by then can take a family, a Church, a nation, a season, a library, or a life: everything from in the bosom of the Church to in the bosom of plenty and in the bosom of one’s family.

The poets, and the demotion of the literal word. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the phrase the bosom of the earth and its variants were put to intensive work by the Romantic and Georgian poets, who found in it a way of describing landscape as something held and warmed rather than merely seen; the sense of shelter, verdancy and enclosure in the modern phrase is largely theirs. At the same time, and by a slow process with no single date, the ordinary literal word for the chest changed. Breast took over the neutral, everyday sense, chest took over the anatomical and military one, bust the old decorative one, and bosom was left with an increasingly marked register: elevated, poetic, decorous, faintly erotic when applied to a woman, and increasingly used for its fixed phrases rather than for fresh application. That demotion is not a loss but a specialisation, and it is why the word sounds grand in a modern sentence even when it means something small.

Status today. Bosom survives in a small number of live constructions and a large number of quotations. Live are the physical sense in the language of blouses and bodices, the phrase bosom friend and its relatives, the ecclesiastical phrases, and the general figurative sense of an inmost or sheltered part in literary prose. Quoted are bosom sins, the bosom of Abraham, and the eighteenth-century poetry in which the word still sounds like the ordinary name of a place. The attributive use in the source sentence belongs to the family of bosom friend: the word functioning as an adjective for closeness, which is the oldest figurative sense of them all, since to hold someone in your bosom and to be a bosom friend are the same gesture seen from two sides.

  • Old English: bōsm, the chest, and already by extension a hollow or sheltered place in the landscape.
  • Medieval: the word turns inward, becoming the heart, the inner life, the protected core of a family or a church, and producing the fifteenth-century phrase bosom friend.
  • Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: theology supplies the grand senses, the bosom of God, of the Father, of Abraham, while the secret society, the heraldic hollow and the pocket of a shirt add three technical lives.
  • Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: the poets take over the landscape sense, the bosom of the earth, while breast and chest capture the ordinary bodily senses and bosom becomes a mark of elevation.
  • Twentieth century to present: a word of fixed phrases and deliberate archaism, alive in bosom friend, in the ecclesiastical vocabulary, and in the figurative language of shelter and inner life.
