BUTTERCUP is the familiar popular name for any of several small, glossy yellow cup-shaped wildflowers of the genus Ranunculus, especially the meadow buttercup Ranunculus acris, the creeping buttercup Ranunculus repens and the bulbous buttercup Ranunculus bulbosus. They are low to knee-high perennials of damp meadows, pastures, ditches and garden lawns, with divided leaves and five-petalled, varnished-yellow flowers that shine as if oiled. The shine is structural: a paper-thin upper petal layer reflects light like a mirror, producing the intense gloss that makes a buttercup glow under the chin in the children's game.

Ecologically buttercups are colonists of grazed and mown ground, flowering from April to August and persisting by rhizomes, runners or corms. All parts contain the glycoside ranunculin, which when bruised releases protoanemonin, an acrid irritant poisonous to livestock and humans, causing mouth blisters and dermatitis. Cattle usually avoid them, so buttercups increase in overgrazed pasture as unpalatable survivors, a paradox of a cheerful flower signalling poor grazing. Bees and hoverflies nevertheless visit them for pollen and nectar.

  • Primary botanical sense – the Ranunculus flower: any glossy yellow crowfoot. Example: a field of buttercups. Bridge: compound of butter, for the rich yellow colour and gloss, plus cup, for the bowl-shaped corolla; a visual simile lexicalised in Middle English.
  • Colour and pollen sense – buttercup yellow, buttercup dust: the intense mustard-egg-yolk yellow of the petals, extended to pollen, dust or light of the same hue. In the source context buttercup dust is yellow pollen or peaty dust the colour of buttercups coating black feet. Bridge: metonymy from flower to its characteristic colour and then to any powder of that colour.
  • Horticultural and cultivar sense: named double or white forms grown in cottage gardens, and by analogy unrelated ornamentals such as the Californian buttercup or winter aconite sometimes called buttercups. Bridge: analogy of colour and cup-shape rather than taxonomy.
  • Figurative and affectionate sense: a term of endearment for a child or sweetheart, my little buttercup, and in American slang a cheerful, wholesome person. Bridge: metaphor from the small, bright, innocent flower to human brightness and innocence, reinforced by songs and films.
  • Specialist compounds: tall buttercup, small-flowered buttercup, water crowfoots formerly called water buttercups. Bridge: taxonomic qualification within the same folk genus.

Literary and cultural nuance is unusually rich for a weed. Buttercups connote childhood summers, hayfields before the cut, innocence and abundance, but also acridity hidden under sweetness. Poets from Wordsworth to Larkin use them as shorthand for remembered meadows; parents teach the chin-glow test for liking butter; farmers read them as a sign of wet, acidic soil needing lime. The dust in Baker's simile works because buttercup pollen is copious, dry and deeply yellow, clinging to dark feathers and leather-like feet like flour.

To know BUTTERCUP here is therefore to see not only a flower but a colour and texture: mustard-yellow powder half-covering black, the contrast of gloss-yellow above and jet below that makes the plovers summer dress startling. It contrasts with daisy, dandelion and celandine, fellow spring composites and ranunculids, by its varnished cup, divided leaves and poisonous sap.
