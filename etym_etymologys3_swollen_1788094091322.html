Swollen is the past participle of the strong verb swell, and its history is the history of a single physical fact — distension by internal pressure — expanding, over more than a thousand years of English, into a moral, medical, and quantitative vocabulary. The chain begins in Proto-Indo-European *swel- "to swell, rise" (Pokorny's root set), whose Indo-Iranian cognate is among the most cited in comparative grammar: Sanskrit śvayati "to swell, grow" appears in the Rigveda, giving the root a documented life of nearly four thousand years. From *swel- the Germanic languages built the strong verb *swellaną: Old English swellan (class III strong verb), Old Norse svella, Old Frisian swella, Old Saxon and Middle Dutch swellen, Old High German swellan, German schwellen, Swedish svälla — a compact family in which every member agrees on both shape and sense. One caution belongs in every account of the root: PIE *swel- "to swell" is a different root from the homophonous *(s)wel- "to burn, shine" (the family of Latin sōl "sun" and English sultry), and the two are routinely confused in popular etymology; they are homophones at a distance of millennia, not kin, and the confusion is a standing warning that reconstructed roots can look alike while being unrelated.

The Old English participle

Old English marks the past participle of strong verbs with the prefix ge-: geswollen is the regular form, used of wounds, tumours, sinews, the tongue, the belly, and of waters rising in flood. The class III morphology is the old machinery of the verb: the stem vowel runs through the strong paradigm (e in the present, o in the participle), and it is precisely this o-grade that survives into modern "swollen" — the participle is thus the one member of the paradigm to preserve the ancient vowel unchanged, while the past-tense forms (swelled, and dialectal "swoll") took the regular weak path or conserved the older shape in dialect. Middle English dropped the prefix (swollen, i-swollen), and by the early modern period "swollen" was both participle and free-standing adjective, exactly as today.

The figurative leap

The moral extension is biblical and early. The English Bible translators and the devotional prose that followed them used the word of inward excess: the man "swollen with pride", the heart "swollen with anger" — the image is of a pressure rising inside the self that distends it past its proper shape, the Old Testament motif of the proud heart that swells against God and is brought low. By the seventeenth century "swollen" is a standard word for arrogance and dangerous self-inflation in sermons, satire, and epic alike; Milton's poetry and prose exploit the image of swollen pride and the swelling of empire. The nineteenth century added the fixed phrase "swollen head" (with the variant "swelled head" and the adjective "swollen-headed"), conceit as a pathological enlargement of the ego — the perfect coinage for an age that was simultaneously producing the exact clinical vocabulary around it, and a reminder that the moral and medical senses of the word grew up side by side and reinforced each other.

Medicine, rivers, and the quantitative age

In the same century, clinical English systematized the physical senses: "swollen glands", "swollen joints", "swollen spleen" became routine diagnostic language, and the noun "swelling" took on its modern specific sense of a lump or oedema. The river sense — water swollen by rain or flood, exactly as in the present context ("Great floods and rains had swollen the waters that flowed east") — is ancient and continuous: it is there in Old English descriptions of rivers in spate, and it runs unbroken through Romantic poetry (the "swollen stream" of spring thaw is a standby of the picturesque) into modern flood reporting. The twentieth century's contribution is quantitative: "swollen" applied to numbers, ranks, budgets, profits, and bureaucracies — the inflation of any countable quantity, from "swollen government" to "swollen profits", always with the same undertone of excess and distension the word has carried since the sermons. Even the sciences admit it: "swollen" belongs to hydrology (a swollen river), to pathology (swollen tissue), and to meteorology (swollen clouds) without changing its register.

Phonology and the modern split

Phonologically the word has been stable in form and mobile in sound. Middle English swollen had the short o; early modern /ˈswɔlən/ (the vowel of "gone" in conservative dialects) was lengthened and raised before /l/ at the close of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — the same change that turned "folk", "whole", and "gold" into their modern forms and gave Received Pronunciation /ˈswəʊ.lən/, with the "goat" vowel in the stressed syllable and a schwa in the second. The dialect form "swole" — preserving the older vowel — escaped the change and survived in regional speech; the twentieth century then gave it a strange second life: African American Vernacular English used "swole" for muscular or well-built, and 1990s bodybuilding culture adopted it as slang ("he's swole"), a conscious reclamation of the participle's ancient o-variant as a badge of muscle. The participle's grammar, too, settled into its modern distribution: "swelled" remains as the simple past and as a competing participle ("he swelled with pride"; "swelled head" is the fixed phrase for conceit), but "swollen" is the mandatory form whenever the swelling is physical and visible, and for the adjective function generally. The medieval geswollen, the sixteenth-century "swollen with pride", the nineteenth-century "swollen glands", the twentieth-century "swollen profits", and the twenty-first-century "swole" are all the same word at five points on a single continuous line — one participle whose pressure has never run out.
