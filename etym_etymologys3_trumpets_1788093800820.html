An imitative beginning in Germanic

Unlike most musical instruments, whose names travel with the instruments from civilisation to civilisation, the trumpet's Germanic name appears to have been coined on the spot, from the sound. The reconstructed Germanic base *trump-/*trumba is generally regarded as onomatopoeic or expressive — it imitates the short, booming blast of a horn — and it underlies Old High German trumba/trumpa "trumpet" (9th century), Old Norse trumba "trumpet" (also "pipe, tube"), and the early English trump borrowed in the 13th century. No Indo-European ancestor can be recovered, and none is needed: the family is a Germanic expressive creation, and its spread across the continent followed the spread of the instrument and its martial use. The same imitative base lies behind words for other resonant tubes — compare Middle Dutch tromme "drum" and German Trommel — showing how closely trumpet and drum were associated in medieval military music.

  • Proto-Germanic *trumpa (imitative) → Old High German trumba, Old Norse trumba, Middle English trump(e).
  • No PIE root: the base is expressive, like "bang" or "toot".

The Romance detour: Frankish into Old French

The modern English word, however, does not descend from an Old English ancestor; it was re-borrowed through French. Old English had its own native words for horns and trumpets, but the modern family is entirely a French import. The Frankish conquerors of Gaul carried the Germanic word *trumpa into the Romance-speaking world, where it became Old French trompe — "a long, tube-like musical instrument" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries) — and from trompe the language formed a diminutive, trompette, literally "little trumpet", attested in the 12th–13th centuries. The diminutive suffix -ette (feminine of -et) was itself a productive French coinage, and it is this affectionate "little horn" form that English adopted in the 14th century as trumpet — while also keeping the shorter trump from the same French word (hence "the last trump").

  • Frankish *trumpa → Old French trompe → Old French trompette (diminutive) → Middle English trumpet.
  • English thus owns two descendants of the same Germanic base through French: trump and trumpet.

The 14th century: the herald's voice

Middle English trumpet arrives with the full ceremonial apparatus of chivalry. In 14th-century literature — the romances and Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, whose Knight's Tale already sounds "pypes, trompes, nakers, clariounes" at the tournament — trompes and trumpets ring out at sieges, royal entries, and weddings; the word enters English precisely as the instrument of announcement and command. The trumpet is not yet an orchestral instrument but a signal instrument: it proclaims the arrival of a king, calls soldiers to battle, and marks the great festivals of the Church. The Bible's apocalyptic trumpets — the seven trumpets of Revelation and "the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible" of 1 Corinthians 15:52 — give the plural a permanent eschatological register: "trumpets" are the voice of judgement and resurrection.

The 16th–17th centuries: stage and state

In Elizabethan and Jacobean drama the trumpet is everywhere in stage directions — "flourish", "alarum", "sennet" are all trumpet signals — and Shakespeare's plays are studded with "trumpets sounded" as the audible grammar of power. "Blow your own trumpet" (self-praise) is recorded in this period: the herald who announces a knight's deeds becomes the man who announces his own. The 17th century also sees the trumpet enter art music: the Baroque natural trumpet — played with the lips alone and limited to the harmonic series — becomes the virtuoso instrument of Bach and Handel, and Handel's Messiah (1741) crowns the old biblical phrase with the aria "The trumpet shall sound", in which the instrument literally dialogues with the soloist. Trumpets in this era mean majesty, war, and the divine, and the plural "trumpets" in ceremonial contexts carries all of that weight.

The 19th–20th centuries: valves, jazz, and widening metaphor

Two revolutions reshape the word's world. First, the invention of valves (patented in the 1810s–1830s by Stölzel, Blühmel, and others) makes the trumpet fully chromatic; by mid-century the valved trumpet is standard in orchestras and the new military bands. Second, the 20th century makes the trumpet the leading voice of jazz — Louis Armstrong, Dizzy Gillespie, Miles Davis — so that "trumpets" now conjures not only heraldic ceremony but the blues, swing, and bebop. In the same centuries the verb "to trumpet" widens to any loud public assertion ("trumpeting the company's success"), and "trumpeting" becomes the standard word for an elephant's call. The plural in the context sentence — "warning trumpets were suddenly sounded, and echoed along the rocky shores" — shows the oldest function intact: the trumpet as alarm, its blast carrying over water and stone to warn a town of the dragon's approach.

  • c. 1815–1830s: valve patents → chromatic trumpet; orchestral and military expansion.
  • 20th century: jazz trumpet; "to trumpet" = to proclaim loudly; the elephant's cry.
  • Idioms: "blow one's own trumpet"; "the last trump"; "trumpet call".

Conclusion

TRUMPETS thus concentrates a remarkable history: an imitative Germanic coinage, a Frankish loan into French, a French diminutive borrowed into English in the age of chivalry, a biblical and heraldic career, a Baroque virtuoso life, and a modern jazz and digital afterlife — while the plural form itself has always meant "the sounding of the instruments": the acoustic event of alarm, glory, and summons.
