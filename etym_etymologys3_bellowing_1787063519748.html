Origins of the sound. The English verb bellow is first attested in Old English as bylgian, recorded chiefly in glosses (bilingual word lists), where it translates Latin verbs for roaring and making a loud noise. The word is universally treated by the standard etymological dictionaries as imitative or onomatopoeic: the string of sounds bel-w imitates the deep, breathy, vibrating roar of a large animal, exactly as "moo", "baa", and "cuckoo" imitate other animal calls. No secure pre-Germanic (PIE) etymon has been established. An older suggestion linked the word to a PIE root meaning "to swell" — the notion being that the animal "swells" its voice or chest — but this is now generally regarded with suspicion, because the semantics are weak and no verifiable sound correspondences support it.

The Germanic family. What is certain is that the word belongs to a tight-knit Germanic family of loud-noise verbs built on the base bel-/belw-, all of them probably independent imitations of the same sound:

  • Old English bylgian "to bellow, roar" — the direct ancestor.
  • Old English bellan "to bark, roar, bellow" — a sibling formation, source of the dialect verb bell "to roar" and connected with the noun "bell" through the resonant ring of a struck metal cup.
  • Old Norse belja "to roar, bellow"; Middle Low German and Middle Dutch bellen "to make a loud noise, bark"; Middle High German bellen "to bark".

These forms point back to a Proto-Germanic base *bel(w)- whose exact reconstruction is uncertain (it is reconstructed only on the strength of the daughters), and beyond Germanic the trail goes cold. The resemblance of the base to words for "ball" and "to swell" (the *bel- of "belly", "ball") has tempted some scholars to see a connection — the swollen-chested roar — but this is speculation, and the OED keeps the origin as imitative, "of obscure or onomatopoeic origin".

Middle English. The verb survived the Norman Conquest and appears in Middle English from about 1300 as belwen (also belghen, bilwen), meaning "to roar, low, cry loudly". At this stage it is a thoroughly rural word: it belongs to the farmyard and the forest, describing oxen, bulls, and deer, and it is recorded in regional texts, sermons, and romances where animals roar in battle or beasts are driven to slaughter. It is also used of the human voice when it drops to animal depth — warriors "belwen" battle-cries, grief-stricken men "belwen" their laments — but always with the sense of a throat pushed to its loudest, least articulate extreme.

Early Modern English. In the 1500s the spelling settles as bellow (with variants bellou, belowe), and the verb begins its long extension outward from animal cries to sounds of every kind. The Authorised Version of the Bible (1611) uses it memorably of a suffering animal ("the young lions ... bellow, do they catch their prey"), and poets from Shakespeare onward put it in the mouths of furious men and the throats of storms. It is exactly in this period that the word crosses from "the cry of a beast" to "any deep, resonant, swelling sound": thunder bellows, cannon bellow, the sea bellows, and a great bell "bellows" its iron note — the transfer being driven by the shared quality of a low, powerful, long-drawn vibration.

Industrial age. The 19th and 20th centuries, which filled the world with new and terrible noises, found the word ready-made. Steam engines "bellowed", factory furnaces "bellowed", ships' sirens and, later, loudspeakers and amplifiers "bellowed". Journalism and fiction used it for anything vast and deafening, and the participle "bellowing" became a stock adjective for industrial and military uproar. At the same time the word kept its farmyard heart: in hunting, natural history, and country writing, "bellowing" remains the precise technical term for the rutting roar of a stag or bull.

The modern word. Today "bellowing" straddles the animal and the mechanical, the literal and the metaphorical, without ever losing its deep, sonorous, threatening core. It is used of bulls and thunder, of enraged men and loudspeakers, of dragons in fantasy literature and of subwoofers in home audio. Across a thousand years it has never drifted into a mild or positive sense: every bellow, real or figurative, is too loud, too deep, too much like an animal's throat — which is precisely why the word is so effective in the passage from The Hobbit, where a single "bellowing" from far below makes a tunnel of stone shake with dread.
