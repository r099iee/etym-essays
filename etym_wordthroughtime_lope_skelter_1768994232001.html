- Lope enters Middle English as a northern variant of 'leap,' meaning simply to leap or spring.
- By the 15th–16th c. it narrows from any jump to the long, easy, bounding stride of a large animal, the unhurried ground-covering gait of wolf, fox or horse.
- Through the 17th–19th c. the verb settles into its modern shape — running with long bounds and no strain, power held in reserve — and also serves as a noun, 'an easy lope.'
- In modern usage 'lope' is a literary and naturalist word, mildly archaic and faintly wild; it belongs to open country and poetry, and it keeps its contrast with panic words like 'skelter.'
- Skelter, by contrast, appears mainly inside the reduplicated phrase 'helter-skelter' from the 16th c., meaning in hurry and confusion; alone it names a scurry, a headlong rush.
- In the modern register 'skelter' survives chiefly in that disorderly compound, so where 'lope' is poise, 'skelter' is panic.
