- 14th–16th century rural register — 'heather' names the low, wiry moorland shrub as the practical resource of poor, wild land: bedding, thatch, brooms, and fuel; the word smells of the northern fells and of hard living.
- 17th–18th century — the plant is a staple of the highland economy (heather honey, cattle fodder, rope), and the word stays strictly utilitarian, with no glamour attached.
- Late 18th–19th century Romantic turn — 'heather' becomes an emblem of wild, sublime landscape: purple moors, the Scottish Highlands, nostalgic freedom; poetry and tourism recast the poor-man's shrub as an icon of wildness.
- Contemporary English — 'heather' now also names a purple-pink colour, a heather-textured tweed, ornamental garden cultivars, and the enduring moorside image; its register has shifted from utility to soft nostalgia.
