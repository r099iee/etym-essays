Linear derivation chain (real attested forms with dates; 8 stages): 1. PIE root *sel- "to leap, spring, bound" (reconstructed, c. 4500-2500 BCE; some handbooks give *sal- - see debate note). 2. Latin salire "to leap, jump, spring forth" (attested Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE). 3. Latin adsalire / assalire "to leap upon, spring at, attack" - ad- "to/toward" + salire (attested 1st c. BCE-2nd c. CE). 4. Vulgar Latin *assalire "to assault, make an attack" (spoken Latin, c. 4th-8th c. CE). 5. Old French assaillir "to attack, besiege, beset" (attested c. 9th-12th c.). 6. Anglo-Norman / Middle English assailen "to attack with force or words" (attested 13th c.). 7. Modern English assail "to attack violently; to criticize harshly" (16th c. onwards). 8. English un- (Old English un-, privative "not", from PIE *n-) + assailed (past participle of assail) - UNASSAILED "not attacked, unswayed" (attested in English literature from the 16th c.; prominently in Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, 1813). Debate note: the PIE etymon of Latin salire is usually reconstructed as *sel- "to leap" (Pokorny *sel-), though some handbooks give *sal-. The root is also credited to Greek hallein "to leap" and Sanskrit sarati "runs, flows". This uncertainty concerns only the reconstructed root; the attested Latin-to-English chain above is secure.
