The name Macedonia reaches back to one of the plainest words of the Indo-European inheritance — the adjective for “long” or “tall.” At the head of the chain stands the Proto-Indo-European root *meh₂k-, an adjectival root of height and length whose reflexes span the family: Greek μακρός (makrós) ‘long’ (whence English macro-, macron), Greek μῆκος (mēkos) ‘length’, and Latin macer ‘thin, lean’ (whence English meagre, emaciate). The Greeks themselves felt the etymology: the ancient Macedonians were named, in the most natural reading, from the adjective μακεδνός (makednós) ‘tall, slender, tapering’, so that the Μακεδόνες (Makedónes), the Macedonians, were — literally — “the tall ones,” or, as many scholars prefer, “the highlanders,” the lofty mountain-folk of the Pierian and Olympean uplands. From that ethnonym came the territorial name. Greek formed Μακεδονία (Makedonía), “the land of the Macedonians,” with the common -ια (-ia) suffix of place that also gives Arabia and Italia. The word then followed the fortunes of the land. In the fifth and fourth centuries BC it named a semi-barbarous northern border kingdom whose Greekness was disputed at Athens — Demosthenes sneered at Philip II as a man “not even a Greek” — and the name carried that slighted, frontier colouring in Attic oratory. Then came the seismic shift the context records: in the second half of the 4th century BC, under Philip II and Alexander the Great, the kingdom of Macedon rose into an empire that stretched to India, signaling the start of the Hellenistic period. Suddenly Macedonia was the name of the ruling power of the Greek world, and the word's connotation swung from northern obscurity to the centre of Mediterranean history. The name then migrated across the political map of the ancient world. In 146 BC, after the Fourth Macedonian War, Rome annexed the kingdom and established the province of Macedonia, with its capital at Thessalonica; the passage's sentence “in the 2nd century BC Macedonia became a Roman province” records exactly this administrative transformation, in which the name passed from a kingdom to a province while retaining its territory. Under the Empire the province was a key military district on the Via Egnatia, and the New Testament preserves the name in Acts and the Epistles — “the churches of Macedonia” (2 Corinthians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians) — so that through Christianity the toponym entered the wider European inheritance. In the Middle Ages the name detached from its ancient geography. The Byzantine theme of Macedonia (established in the 9th century) actually lay to the east of the old kingdom, centred in Thrace, so that for centuries “Macedonia” officially named a district far from Mount Olympus. Ottoman rule then blurred the boundaries further: from the 19th century “Macedonia” designated the whole contested Balkan region — roughly modern Greek Macedonia, the Republic of North Macedonia, and adjacent parts of Bulgaria and Albania — the battleground of the Macedonian Question, of competing Bulgarian, Greek, and Serbian claims, and of the Balkan Wars of 1912–13. The twentieth century gave the name its most acute political charge. When Yugoslavia dissolved in 1991, its southernmost republic — which occupies the northern half of the ancient region — declared independence under the name “Macedonia.” Greece objected that the name implied a claim on its own northern province of Macedonia, and for nearly three decades the country was known internationally as the “Former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia” (FYROM). The dispute was settled by the Prespa Agreement of June 2018, ratified in 2019, under which the state took the official name the Republic of North Macedonia. In modern English, therefore, “Macedonia” must be handled with care: it names an ancient kingdom, a Greek administrative region centred on Thessaloniki, a modern nation-state (now qualified as North), and the wider historical region that overlaps all of them — a single Greek word, ‘land of the tall ones,’ carrying two and a half millennia of political history.
