PERVERSITY is a Latin-derived word built on two PIE roots: *per- "through, forward, across" (the prefix element) and *wert- "to turn, to bend" (the verb element). The chain below is the single linear derivation of the noun itself; the two roots enter at its base. Chain: 1. PIE *per- "through, forward, across" (attested Sanskrit pári, Greek perí, Latin per; a fully Indo-European preposition) + PIE *wert- "to turn, to bend" (attested Sanskrit várte, Latin vertere, Old English weorðan "to become"); together these produce Latin pervertere. 2. Latin pervertere "to turn upside down, to overturn, to corrupt, to pervert" (composed of per- "thoroughly, completely" + vertere "to turn"; attested classical, e.g. Cicero, 1st c. BCE) — literally "to turn (something) all the way over," hence "to twist out of the proper course." 3. Latin perversus "turned the wrong way, inverted, contrary, perverse" (past participle of pervertere; attested classical) — the adjective describing what has been twisted aside. 4. Latin perversitās "perversity, wrong-headedness, perverseness" (abstract noun in -tās formed on perversus; attested in Classical and Late Latin, e.g. in patristic writers, 1st–4th c.) — the quality of being turned the wrong way. 5. Old French perversité "perversity, wickedness, wrong-headedness" (from Latin perversitātem, accusative of perversitās; attested 12th c.) — the word passes through Gallo-Romance with the religious-moral sense already present. 6. Middle English perversite "perversity" (borrowed from Old French, attested 14th c., e.g. in Chaucer's usage of the family) — entering English with the sense of contrariety and moral wrong-headedness. 7. Modern English PERVERSITY (14th c. onward) — the sense of deliberate contrariness; the narrowed sexual-clinical sense of "perversion" developed in the late 19th c. as a separate formation, while perversity retains the broader meaning. Note on debated stages: the derivation from PIE *wert- through Latin vertere is standard and uncontested (de Vaan, LIV, Pokorny). The only nuance worth flagging is that the -tās abstract formation is productive throughout the history of Latin, so no single "first attestation" for perversitās can be given with precision; the word is securely attested in the imperial period and is common in the Latin of the Church Fathers, where the moral-religious sense was fixed. No stage is invented.
