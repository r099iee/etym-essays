Root core: Unknown / expressive. West Germanic *muffl- / *muff- "something soft, puffed, rounded; a thick glove or mitten" — an expressive root whose doubled, rounded consonants (m…f) suggest softness and padding. No accepted Proto-Indo-European etymon exists; the family is classed as an expressive (sound-symbolic) formation. Linear chain to MUFFLED: 1. West Germanic expressive base *muffl- / *muff- "soft, padded, rounded; thick glove or mitten" (early medieval; no reconstructible PIE ancestor). 2. Old High German moufa "muff, thick glove" (attested in early glosses). 3. Medieval Latin muffula "thick mitten, glove" (Carolingian-era glosses) — itself a loan from the West Germanic base, not a Latin root. 4. Old French moufle "mitten, thick glove" (from Latin muffula), with the derived verb enmoufler / moufler "to wrap up, to cover as with a mitten or cloak." 5. Middle English muflen "to wrap up, cover the face (and body)" (late 14th century), borrowed from Old French. 6. English muffle (v.) "to wrap up; to deaden sound," and its past participle / adjective muffled (15th century onward) — the exact form on this card. Debated / nuanced notes: - No PIE etymology is accepted for this cluster; scholars treat it as an expressive creation, which is why the chain begins at a West Germanic base rather than an Indo-European root. - The sound-symbolic siblings muffle / mum / mumble / mutter all build on labial m- for "indistinct sound made behind closed lips"; they overlap with *muffl- by sound-symbolism rather than by strict descent. - English also borrowed the noun muff "a fur tube for warming the hands" (c. 1580) from Middle Dutch mof, a parallel member of the same soft-padding family; it is a cousin, not the ancestor of the verb muffle.
