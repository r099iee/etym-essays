Gin is in its dominant modern sense a clear distilled spirit flavoured chiefly with juniper berries — the botanical liquor of the martini, the gin and tonic, and the cocktail cabinet. In the source sentence it is the drink of the desperate: the shabby Middlesex labourers have every appearance of drinking gin, which in Cobbett’s moral arithmetic means they have every appearance of ruin, since gin was the cheap oblivion of the English poor.

Core meaning in detail: Gin is made by redistilling neutral grain spirit in the presence of juniper (Juniperus communis) and other botanicals — coriander, angelica, citrus peel, orris — then diluting to bottle strength. London dry gin, the classic style, takes all its flavour from distillation with no sweetening after; Dutch genever, the ancestor, is maltier and often aged; sloe gin steeps sloe berries in the spirit. The juniper gives the piney resinous snap no other white spirit owns, which is why gin marries tonic, vermouth, and citrus as vodka never quite can.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Juniper spirit (primary, from early 18th c.): geneva shortened to gin, the drink of the Gin Craze, the Empire’s sundowner, and the cocktail age. The bridge is clipping: Dutch genever worn down by English throats to one syllable.
  • A trap or snare for game and vermin (noun, from 13th c., now rustic): a wire gin for rabbits, a spring gin for poachers. The bridge is a second clipping: gin from engine in its old sense of a cunning device — an engine for catching, shrunk to three letters.
  • The cotton gin (noun, 1793): Eli Whitney’s machine for tearing cotton fibre from seed. The bridge runs through the same engine: a cunning device for a harder job — one machine that did the work of fifty hands and re-founded slavery’s economics.
  • To gin — to trap, to clear of seed (verb): to gin rabbits, to gin cotton. The bridge is plain denominal formation from each noun in turn: to work the trap, to work the machine.
  • Gin rummy (noun, 20th c.): the two-handed card game. The bridge is the bar-room: players named the game for the drink (as rum and whisky poker took theirs), and the name stuck through radio tournaments and Hollywood.
  • Mother’s ruin and demonic epithets (cultural): mother’s ruin, blue ruin, Madam Geneva. The bridge is moral panic made slang: the Gin Craze christened the bottle with the names of the destruction it dealt.

Register and connotation: No spirit has travelled further socially than gin: from seventeenth-century Dutch cordial to the demon of Hogarth’s Gin Lane, from the Victorian officers’ mess to Prohibition bathtub poison, from 1980s joke (the gin-soaked dowager) to today’s craft-gin respectability, all copper stills and foraged botanicals. Cobbett uses it at its nadir of repute — gin as the visible seal of degradation — while a modern menu uses it as a promise of connoisseurship. The trap and cotton senses live on unembarrassed in the countryside and the history books, unaware of the spirit’s scandals.
