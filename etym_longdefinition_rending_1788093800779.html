Rending is the present participle of the verb rend: the act of tearing something apart violently and forcibly so that it separates into jagged, irregular pieces. Where tearing is the neutral everyday verb, rending implies great force, distress, or ferocity: cloth rends in a struggle, flesh rends under claws or weapons, storms and explosions rend sails, roofs, and the silence itself. In the source context the word describes wolves turning upon their own dead and wounded and tearing the bodies apart — the perfect picture of the violent, bestial sense.

Literal senses:

  • Of cloth and garments: "to rend one's garments" is the ancient Near Eastern gesture of mourning, horror, and penitence — a gesture biblical in register and still alive in fixed phrase.
  • Of flesh and tissue: rending describes wounds that are torn, not cut: claws, teeth, hooks, and shrapnel rend; surgeons cut, but beasts rend. The participle is the natural word for an animal's tearing of prey or corpses.
  • Of materials and structures: storms rend sails and rigging; earthquakes and avalanches rend the land; lightning rends a tree or the sky; struggling prisoners rend their bonds.

Figurative and transferred senses:

  • Of sound: a scream rends the silence or the night; "rending cry" is the stock poetic phrase for a piercing, agonised noise.
  • Of emotion and community: grief and conflict "rend" the heart or a nation; the passive participle rent ("a country rent by civil war", "a family rent apart") is a literary fixture; heart-rending (from the late eighteenth century) means heartbreaking, agonisingly pitiful.
  • Of veils, curtains, and barriers: "rending the veil" — the temple curtain torn at the crucifixion (Matthew 27:51) — has become a general metaphor for revelation, the abrupt disclosure of what was hidden; "to rend the veil of secrecy" is a live collocation in journalism.

Semantic bridges: all the extended senses run along two rails: violent force and irreparable damage. Once the verb was entrenched for cloth and flesh (Old English rendan), the extension to what is "torn" immaterially — silence, hearts, covenants, societies — was natural: the same violence that rips fabric rips the intangible, and the participle "rending" lends a high, archaic, or melodramatic register precisely because daily speech abandoned it in favour of tearing and ripping. The "veil" metaphor adds the religiously crucial note that what is rent is not merely damaged but opened — hence the shift from destruction to revelation.

Grammar note on "rend" and "rent": rend has a double past: regular rended and the older strong-style rent (Middle English rente), the latter still alive as a literary past and participle ("the veil was rent", "rent asunder"). "Rending" itself is the ordinary progressive/participial form, carrying the historical participial suffix that continued Middle English -ende.

Register: in contemporary English "rending" is firmly literary, biblical, or journalistically dramatic; it signals drama and finality, and it survives in fixed phrases (heart-rending, earth-rending, rend the veil, rend the silence, rent asunder) far more than in free composition. It is the verb a writer chooses when a tear must feel final.
