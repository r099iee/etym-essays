Contemporaneous is a learned Latinism whose parts are far older than the whole. The prefix con- together, with- comes from Proto-Indo-European *kom- beside, with, preserved in Latin cum with and English co-, com-, con-. The base tempus, temporis time, season, moment descends, via de Vaan and Pokorny, from PIE *ten- to stretch, extend — time conceived as a stretch or span. From the same root come temple (forehead, the stretched thin place), temper, temple (building for marking time?), tense (stretched time of verb), tend, tent, thin, tenet. Latin temporaneus timely, temporary and contemporaneus living at the same time are Late Latin, Christian-era formations (4th-6th centuries) needed for hagiography and chronicles to align saints, emperors and councils.

  • Morphology: con- (assimilated com-) + tempor- (stem of tempus) + -aneus belonging to, as in mediterraneus, extemporaneus; English replaces -aneus with -aneous on analogy of spontaneous, instantaneous, miscellaneous.
  • Phonetics: Latin /kontemporaːneus/ → English /kənˌtɛmpəˈreɪniəs/ with stress shift to -raneous, reduction of con- to /kən/, and Great Vowel Shift raising of a.
  • First English: 1630s-1650s contemporaneous and contemporary together; Sir Thomas Browne, Ussher chronology, ecclesiastical histories.

Seventeenth-century antiquaries used contemporaneous and contemporary interchangeably for kings, authors and witnesses living together. Johnson (1755) lists Contemporary but not the longer form, hinting the shorter was commoner. Nineteenth-century science separated them: geologists (Lyell) needed contemporaneous for strata deposited in the same epoch though miles apart; historians (Ranke) prized contemporaneous sources over later compilations; lawyers admitted contemporaneous memoranda as reliable. The longer word thus specialized as evidential-technical, the shorter drifted to mean modern, up-to-date (contemporary art, contemporary furniture).

Anthropology gave the word its most loaded use. Victorian evolutionists from Tylor to Frazer treated living foragers as contemporaneous ancestors — windows onto the Stone Age. Placed in scare quotes, as in our contemporaneous ancestry, the phrase already betrays unease. By the 1910s-1920s Boas, Malinowski and later Radcliffe-Brown urged sparing use of savage and barbarous and rejection of the living-fossil idea. The sentence in the prompt belongs to that reform: do not call present peoples our ancestors, even with a polite Latin adjective. Post-colonial anthropology keeps contemporaneous for method (fieldnotes, measurements) but drops it for peoples.

Today corpus data show contemporaneous is Tier 4 rare, confined to judgments, audits, histories and exam rubrics, while contemporary is Tier 1-2 core. The evolution illustrates division of labour: the cumbersome Latinate survives where precision about co-timing matters, the handy form takes everyday modernity.
