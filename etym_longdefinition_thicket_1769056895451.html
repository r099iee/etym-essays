thicket is a noun meaning "a dense, tangled growth of shrubs, bushes, or small trees", a place where branches knit together so tightly that passage is difficult. In the context from C.S. Lewis's Prince Caspian the word is doing emphatic, physical work: the children "struggled out of the thicket", and the narrator specifies the cost — "stings from nettles and pricks from thorns". The thicket here is the island's biological resistance, a green barrier that scrapes and bites before letting the children through to the unexpected brightness of the beach. The tone is one of mild ordeal: the word's heavy interior consonants and its blunt short vowel picture a place packed solid with vegetation, a crowded green wall you must force yourself through. PRIMARY MEANING:
  • A dense growth of shrubs and small trees. A thicket is thicker than a clump and thicker than a spinney: the plants grow so closely together that their stems interlace, blocking light and passage. It is a natural tangle, not a cultivated plantation.
  • Habitat sense. In ecology, a thicket is a dense stand of undergrowth that shelters game birds, small mammals and nesting birds — cover for hiding and for hunting alike.
SECONDARY AND FIGURATIVE MEANINGS:
  • "A thicket of" — a dense, confusing mass. Since the 19th century the word is used metaphorically for anything densely tangled or hard to get through: "a thicket of rules", "a thicket of regulations", "a thicket of lies". The image is of pushing through obstruction.
  • Literary and Romantic uses. In 18th–19th century nature-writing and fiction, the thicket is the unmanaged wild middle of a wood — obstacle and refuge at once, alive with scratches and hidden life.
  • The -et teaching point. Though it looks like the French diminutive ending (as in coronet, cigarette), the -et of thicket is a native Old English collective suffix that forms nouns from adjectives or nouns — thick + -et = "a thick growth". A thicket is therefore not a "little thick thing" but "the place where things grow thick".
MODERN TECHNICAL USES:
  • In ecology and land management: "scrub thicket", "riparian thicket", "thicket-formation" as a stage of vegetation succession.
  • In military and survival vocabulary: a "thicket" is cover that hides movement but slows advance — the ambush-maker's green wall.
  • Figurative journalism: "the thicket of complexity", "a regulatory thicket" — standard modern idiom for any dense, obstructive system.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGE: The word is transparently built from thick, and its history is the history of that adjective plus a tiny native suffix. Old English þicce ("thick, dense, crowded") combined with the collective suffix -et (also seen in Old English place-words) to form þiccet — literally "a dense growth". The meaning has been remarkably stable for a thousand years: from the Anglo-Saxon glosses to modern dictionaries, a thicket is a thick, tangled mass of small woody plants. The adjective thick itself reaches back through Proto-Germanic *þikkuz to the Indo-European root *tegu-, "thick, dense", so the whole word is a native, Germanic formation — unlike the courtly French -et words, thicket has never left home. In the novel it does the work of pure physical obstruction: the nettles and thorns are the price of passage, and the bright beach beyond is the reward. Exhaustively: thicket = (1) a dense, tangled growth of shrubs, bushes or small trees; (2) such growth as cover and habitat for wildlife; (3) metaphorically, any dense, confusing, obstructive mass ("a thicket of rules"); (4) in ecology, a vegetation stage and habitat type; and (5) a native Old English formation, þicce "thick" + collective -et, whose diminutive-looking ending is not French but a Germanic collective suffix.
