Primary sense: to shake or wobble. Shimmy is above all a verb of agitated motion. It describes a rapid, quivering shake of the body, or of a part of it, and by extension any object that vibrates or wobbles in a way that seems alive. A dancer shimmies her shoulders; a dog shimmies out of a wet coat; a bicycle shimmies on a wet road. The motion is characteristically lateral, repetitive and slightly uncontrolled, which is why the word so often carries a suggestion of comic or sensual abandon rather than mechanical precision.

The dance. The original shimmy was a jazz dance that swept the United States and Europe around 1918. It consisted essentially of a rapid shaking of the shoulders, hips and torso while the feet stayed comparatively still, and it was danced to early jazz and blues records. It was regarded as scandalous precisely because the movement isolated the torso and made the body's shakings the centre of attention. The dance gave the word its glamour and its slightly disreputable charge, and that charge survives whenever the verb is used of a person.

  • Shimmy as performance: stage and cabaret dancers used the step as a display of control and stamina, holding the shoulders still while the torso shook.
  • Shimmy as social dance: it belonged to the same post-war explosion of ragtime and jazz dances that produced the foxtrot, the Charleston and the black bottom.
  • Shimmy as moral panic: newspapers and clergy denounced it as indecent, which only increased its popularity.

The garment. Before it was a dance, shimmy was an American dialectal pronunciation of chemise, the loose sleeveless undergarment or slip worn by women. The dance was named for the way the chemise shook and slipped during the movement, and the garment sense survives in older American usage and in descriptions of vintage clothing. This is a striking case of a word for a piece of clothing becoming a word for the motion that the clothing makes visible.

Technical sense: wheel shimmy. Engineers use shimmy for a self-excited oscillation of a wheel or landing gear about its steering axis. A car or aircraft wheel can begin to oscillate violently at certain speeds, a phenomenon driven by tyre elasticity, caster geometry and gyroscopic coupling. The cure is a shimmy damper, a device fitted to aircraft nose gear and to some vehicle steering systems to absorb the oscillation. This is the same word as the dance, transferred by metaphor: the mechanical motion looks like the human shake.

The climbing sense. In informal English, especially American, to shimmy up or shimmy down something is to climb it by gripping with the hands and legs and wriggling upward or downward, as a child does on a pole or a sailor on a rope. This sense is probably influenced by shinny, to climb by shinning, and the two words have partly merged in speech.

Secondary and modern senses. The word has continued to spread into new domains, always by the same metaphor of rapid lateral shaking:

  • Dress: to shimmy into a dress or a pair of trousers is to wriggle into a tight garment with small shaking movements.
  • Movement: to shimmy across a floor or along a bench is to move sideways in a wriggling fashion, often while sitting or lying down.
  • Signal and machinery: in computing and audio a shimmy can be a small jitter or wobble in a signal or a mechanical part.
  • Dance revival: retro and jazz-dance companies still perform the original shimmy, and the word keeps its faintly theatrical air.

The unifying thread is always the same: small, rapid, repetitive lateral movement, performed by a body or by something that behaves like one.

The semantic bridge. The chain runs from garment to dance to generic shaking to mechanical oscillation. Each step is a metonymy or a metaphor: the chemise is the thing that shakes, the dance is the shaking of the chemise, the verb generalises the dance's motion, and the engineer borrows the human motion to describe a wheel. The word's persistence is a tribute to the vividness of the original image.
