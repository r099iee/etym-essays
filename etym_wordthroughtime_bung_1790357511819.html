- 15th c. [Late Middle English bonge] - Entered the language via the Hanseatic wine and beer trade as a specialized cooperage term for the wooden stopper sealing a barrel's bung-hole.
- 16th–17th c. [Elizabethan Underworld Cant] - Adopted into rogue and criminal slang to mean a purse or pocket ('bung-nipper' for cutpurse), reflecting a tightly closed container for coins.
- 18th–19th c. [Colloquial & Pugilistic expansion] - Developed the verb sense 'to bung up' (to seal or bruise closed, as in a 'bunged-up eye' from bare-knuckle boxing) and the slang sense of throwing or thrusting roughly into a space.
- 20th c. [Modern Commonwealth Slang] - Broadened widely in British, Australian, and New Zealand English for tossing items casually ('bung it in'), paying illicit sporting/political kickbacks, and the Australian idiom 'go bung' (to fail or die).
