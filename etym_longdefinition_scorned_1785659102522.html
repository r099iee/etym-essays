SCORNED: The past tense and past participle of the verb scorn — rejected, refused, or treated with intense contempt, disdain, or mocking derision; regarded as unworthy of respect, consideration, or regard, and made the object of deliberate humiliation or dismissal. As a participle it works in three grammatical roles: the simple past ("He scorned all offers of compromise"), the perfect ("she has scorned him before"), and the passive adjective ("a scorned woman," "the scorned lover"). The core sense: contemptuous refusal. To scorn is to reject something with an emotion that goes beyond mere refusal: the thing scorned is felt to be beneath consideration, unworthy of a moment's weighing. In the card's sentence — "He scorned all offers of compromise, preferring honorable failure to a hollow peace" — the word is charged with moral dignity. The scorn is directed at compromise and at the "hollow peace"; the contempt is an act of self-respect, because the speaker refuses what would cheapen him. This is the classic heroic use — the prophet who scorns the bribes of the powerful, the principled man who scorns a peace that would dishonor him. Its tone is cold, proud, and decisive, a sharp downward judgment passed from a position of real or imagined superiority.
  • Sense 1 — to refuse with contempt (active verb): "he scorned the offer," "she scorned his advances" — rejection plus derision, the sense that the thing was not worth taking seriously.
  • Sense 2 — to despise, to hold in contempt (attitudinal verb): "they scorned everything foreign," "a man who scorns flattery" — a settled attitude of disdain rather than a single act of refusal.
  • Sense 3 — to mock, to deride publicly (the oldest, literal sense): from Old French escarnir, "to mock, jeer at"; "the crowd scorned him in rhyme" — verbal ridicule with contempt in it.
  • Sense 4 — the passive participle as a badge of humiliation (adjectival): "the scorned woman," "a scorned lover" — one who has been publicly judged worthless; the memory of the judgment is what converts contempt into fury and sets the machinery of revenge in motion. This is the sense most exploited in tragedy and romance.
  • Sense 5 — the noun of the same word: scorn (noun) "intense contempt or derision; the object of such contempt" — "a look of scorn," "the object of their scorn." The verb and noun were borrowed together from Old French escarn in the early 1200s.
The historical bridge. The word entered English c. 1200 from Old French escarn, "mockery, derision, insult," of Germanic origin — ultimately from a root meaning "to cut," the image being the cutting of a reputation to shreds, a verbal shearing. Over the centuries the mocking, noisy sense softened and deepened into the proud, silent disdain the word has today: one can scorn with a raised eyebrow and a cold silence, with no words at all. The participle scorned concentrated the passive half of that story — the one who has been cut down — and it is this passive sense, with its lingering charge of injury and possible revenge, that makes the word so potent in fiction. Register and usage. The verb is somewhat formal and literary; casual speech prefers "turned down," "rejected," or "snubbed." In prose it is a strong word, usually saved for moments of principled refusal or public humiliation. It appears in set constructions — "scorn to do something" (to refuse to stoop to it), "pour scorn on" (idiom: to heap contempt on someone or something). Derived forms: scorner (one who scorns; in older biblical English, "a mocker"), scornful (adjective, "full of contempt"), scornfully (adverb), scornfulness (noun). Related words and traps. Scorn is NOT related to thorn (a Germanic word for a plant spine); the resemblance is coincidental. It is also not related, despite a popular folk-etymology, to Latin cornu "horn" (via Old French escorner, "to cut off horns"); the accepted etymology is the Germanic cutting-root described above. True cousins include shear (to cut) and, through the same Old French noun, the obsolete English scarn, meaning "mockery".
