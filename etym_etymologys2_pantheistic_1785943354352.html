PANTHEISTIC is a compound adjective built from two Greek roots, and its etymology therefore divides into two separate chains — one for pan- ("all") and one for theos ("god") — before the two are fused, via the Latin-influenced coinage of the Enlightenment, into a single English word. Both chains are set out here in isolation, with their uncertainties stated. Chain 1 — the root word pan- (all, whole): - PIE *pant- "all, whole, entire": a reconstructed root that rests almost entirely on the evidence of Greek, and its Proto-Indo-European status is therefore debated — some scholars treat pâs as a Greek-internal formation or as a substrate word, and the exact shape of the etymon is uncertain. State: the PIE stage is a reconstruction of uncertain standing. - Greek pâs, pâsa, pân "all, every, the whole", attested from Mycenaean Greek (pa-si, "to all", in the Linear B tablets, c. 14th–13th century BCE) and in Homer (8th century BCE); the stem is pant-, and the nominative forms show the loss of the final stop (*pant-s → pâs). - Greek combining form pan- "all, whole, entire", productive in compounds from the classical period (pan-theos "of all the gods", 1st century CE, used of temples such as the Pantheon in Rome). - Modern Latin pantheista "a pantheist", coined by the English philosopher John Toland in 1705 (in Socinianism Truly Stated), from Greek pan- "all" + theos "god" + Latin agent suffix -ista. - English pantheist (noun), 1705 (Toland's own English reworking of his coinage); English pantheistic (adjective), recorded from the 1730s. - PANTHEISTIC Chain 2 — the root word theos (god): - Greek theós "god", attested from Mycenaean Linear B (te-o, c. 14th–13th century BCE) and in Homer. Etymology uncertain and not securely Proto-Indo-European: the traditional derivation from a root *dhes- "to do, put, place" (thought to appear also in Latin feriae and festus, "festival days") is now widely doubted — Beekes, in his Etymological Dictionary of Greek, prefers a pre-Greek (substrate) origin. State this debate explicitly. - Greek theós "god", with combining form theo- and the noun-form used in the compound pantheos "of all the gods" (1st century CE). - Thereafter the chain merges into Chain 1: Greek theos + Greek pan- → Modern Latin pantheista (Toland, 1705) → English pantheist (1705) → English pantheistic (1730s) → PANTHEISTIC. Note on the suffix: the -istic ending of pantheistic comes from Greek -istikos, via Latin -isticus, and marks adjectives "of, or pertaining to, a doctrine or its adherent" (cf. theistic, polytheistic, deistic). Note on the coinage: the word is a deliberate Enlightenment creation, not a natural growth; it entered English as a label for a philosophical position before it ever described a mood or a religion.
