all-but:
- Free 'all except' phrase (Old & Middle English) - 'but' (= Old English būtan, 'on the outside, except') withholds an exception, so 'all but forgotten' means everything short of forgotten, the final step still untaken
- Crystallising into an adverb (late medieval - early modern) - the loose phrase hardens into a single adverbial unit meaning 'very nearly', while the buried 'except' keeps its excluding force
- Formal & legal dignity (16th - 19th c.) - used in statutes, formal prose and set phrases ('all but impossible'), where the ceremonial pairing of 'all' with 'except' lends an almost legal finality
- Contemporary - a strong, emphatic near-synonym of 'almost'; the hyphenated compound 'all-but-' is now fully idiomatic, and its paradox (totality minus one step) still lets it hover exactly at the threshold of completeness

recrudescent:
- The medical coinage (19th c.) - formed on Latin recrudescere, 'to become raw again', a pathologist's word for a wound or fever breaking open afresh after healing
- The career in pathology (19th c.) - fixed in medical writing for ulcers, fevers and inflammations that recur with renewed severity after apparent healing
- The metaphorical widening (19th - 20th c.) - transferred from wounds to evils: wars, plagues, revolts and memories 'recrudesce' by bursting open their scar
- Contemporary - a rare, formal, literary adjective reserved for evils that return with painful force; beneath it still lies crudus, the raw, bloody, undressed flesh
