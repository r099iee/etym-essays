• Meaning: Similar because both denote the concept, idea, or message intended by a sign or expression; differs because meaning is the broad, everyday Germanic default used across all registers, whereas signification is highly formal, analytical, and specifically focuses on the systematic mechanism of representation.
  • Significance: Similar because both derive from the same Latin base and can relate to importance or hidden meaning; differs because significance primarily denotes consequence, momentousness, or importance (‘a matter of great significance’), while signification refers strictly to the specific semantic content or meaning signified.
  • Import: Similar because both refer to the substantial meaning, underlying drift, or implication conveyed by an utterance or event; differs because import emphasizes the gravity, consequence, or emotional weight borne by the message (‘the grave import of his words’), whereas signification emphasizes formal semantic clarity and definition.
  • Sense: Similar because both denote an intelligible meaning or interpretation of a word or passage; differs because sense is brief, flexible, and frequently used to distinguish specific nuances or polysemous definitions within a dictionary entry (‘in a figurative sense’), whereas signification treats the concept as a structured semiotic act.
  • Denotation: Similar because both pertain to the explicit, objective meaning of a term; differs because denotation is paired specifically against connotation to indicate the literal dictionary reference stripped of emotional associations, whereas signification encompasses the broader act and substance of signifying.
  • Connotation: Similar because both concern what a sign conveys beyond its bare acoustic form; differs because connotation refers exclusively to secondary, emotional, or cultural overtones, while signification refers primarily to the core referential meaning itself.
  • Purport: Similar because both describe the general meaning, scope, or intended drift of a document or speech; differs because purport often implies an apparent or professed meaning that may conceal an underlying motive or lack complete verification (‘the purported aim’).
  • Tenor: Similar because both describe the prevailing course or meaning of an extended discourse; differs because tenor describes the continuous tone, general drift, or ongoing mood of a communication rather than an explicit, itemized definition.
  • Intendment: Similar because both refer to the true or intended meaning of words or actions; differs because intendment is a specialized legal archaism referring specifically to the presumed intention or legal understanding of a statute, contract, or testator.
  • Interpretation: Similar because both involve assigning intelligible content to signs or texts; differs because interpretation focuses on the subjective cognitive process or hermeneutic act of the reader/observer, whereas signification focuses on the communicative property inherent in the sign itself.
