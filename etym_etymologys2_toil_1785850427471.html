CHAIN 1: FROM PIE TO TOIL (THE LABOUR SENSE, THE SENSE OF THIS CARD) — 7 stages

  1. Proto-Indo-European *tewd- "to strike, thrust, beat" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; the exact shape is debated — older handbooks group it under *teu- "to push, strike, beat"; the root is the source of Latin tundō).
  2. Latin tundō, tundere "to strike, beat, pound, crush" (attested from Plautus, early 2nd century BCE; also of stamping, of the beating pulse, of crushing grain and olives).
  3. Latin tūdicula "a small hammer or pestle; a machine for crushing olives" (attested in the agricultural writers, 1st century CE; a diminutive formation from tundere).
  4. Late Latin tūdiculāre "to crush, stir, beat about, agitate" (verb built on tūdicula, attested in late and medieval Latin).
  5. Old French toiller / toiler "to stir, trouble, fight, struggle, drag about" (attested from the 12th century; from tūdiculāre, with the internal syllable worn down).
  6. Anglo-French toiler "to struggle, contend, labour" → Middle English toilen "to exert oneself, labour, struggle, fight" (verb recorded from the 13th century; the noun toil "strife, severe exertion, battle" appears in the 14th century).
  7. English TOIL (noun) "hard, exhausting, continuous labour" and (verb) "to work hard; to move with painful effort" (the modern labour sense is firmly established from the 16th century, moralized as "honest toil" in Early Modern English).

CHAIN 2: FROM PIE TO "TOILS" (THE HOMONYM "NETS, SNARES") — 5 stages

  1. Proto-Indo-European *teks- "to weave, fabricate" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; the root of Latin texere "to weave" and, through Latin, of English text, textile, tissue).
  2. Latin tēla "warp, web, woven fabric; the loom" (attested classical; formed from the root of texere with a tool-suffix, literally "that with which one weaves" or "the woven thing").
  3. Old French toile "cloth, canvas, net, web, snare" (attested from the 11th–12th centuries; from Latin tēla; modern French toile still means "cloth, canvas, web").
  4. Middle English toile "cloth, linen; a net or snare" (borrowed from Old French in the 14th century; the snare sense appears especially in the plural toils).
  5. English toils (plural noun) "snares, nets, entanglements" — the phrase "in the toils of" (16th–17th centuries onward). The spelling is identical to TOIL (the labour word), making the two a homograph pair of separate origins.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The exact shape of the PIE root behind Latin tundō is debated: de Vaan treats the etymology of tundō as uncertain in its details, and older handbooks vary between *teu-d- and *steu-d- "to beat"; the chain from tundere onward is, however, secure and uncontested.
  • The hunting-snare sense is a separate borrowing that happens to share the spelling; the two etymologies are never confused in scholarship but were felt in English as a single word with dual imagery (struggling labour + entrapment).
  • The noun toil in the labour sense is a back-formation from the verb toilen in Middle English; the verb is the older of the two.
