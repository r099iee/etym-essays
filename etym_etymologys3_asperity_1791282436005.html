Asperity descends from Latin asperitas, itself from asper "rough, uneven, harsh," plus the abstract-noun suffix -itas. Its deeper pre-Latin origin is disputed, which must be stated plainly rather than smoothed over.

Latin foundation. Classical Latin asper (feminine aspera, neuter asperum) described rough ground, shaggy fruit, grating sounds, severe winters, and, by metaphor, harsh character and style. Asperitas therefore meant both physical ruggedness and moral/aesthetic severity from the start — Cicero writes of asperitas verborum (harshness of words) as readily as of rough roads. The suffix -itas (-tās) formed quality nouns exactly as English -ity does now. Phonologically the stem is stable: asper- / asperitāt- with predictable case endings.

The disputed deeper root. Unlike transparent Latin formations, asper has no secure Indo-European etymology. De Vaan (Etymological Dictionary of Latin) marks it as of unknown origin. Older conjectures segmented it as ab- + sper- or linked it to Greek spora, but these lack phonological regularity. Etymonline and the OED therefore stop at Latin, noting "of uncertain origin." This uncertainty is itself informative: asper may be a substrate or expressive formation whose very harshness is iconic rather than inherited. No PIE root should be asserted as fact.

  • Late Latin to Old French: Late Latin asperitatem > Old French aspreté, âpreté (12th c.), with regular loss of intervocalic -s- voicing, syncope, and suffix reduction -itatem > -eté. Old French kept both physical and moral senses: rough paths and sharp tempers.
  • Middle English borrowing: asperite appears c. 1200–1400 in moral and devotional prose ("asperite of penaunce"), borrowed via Old French but quickly re-Latinised in spelling to asperity in the 16th century under Renaissance influence. Early use leaned religious — austerity, mortification — before broadening to general harshness.
  • Early Modern expansion: 16th–18th-century writers apply it to voice, style, weather, and fortune. Shakespeare, Bacon, and later Gibbon use it for both landscapes and polemics, cementing the double physical/moral range that Latin already contained.
  • Modern narrowing and technical revival: everyday use contracts toward manner and tone, while geology and tribology revive the plural asperities for surface micro-geometry (Amontons–Coulomb friction theory, 20th c.). Wodehouse's comic use — wounded pride producing a momentary asperity — shows the word fully domesticated as a polite term for being snappish.

Thus asperity has travelled with remarkable semantic stability for two millennia: roughness outside, roughness in speech, roughness of fate. Its form was French-filtered and Latin-restored; its meaning barely moved, because human skin and human feelings are scratched by the same kind of unevenness.
