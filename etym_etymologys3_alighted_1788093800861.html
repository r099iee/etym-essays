Root and earliest stratum. The verb alight goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *legʷʰ-, "light in weight, easy", the same root that produced Latin levis "light", Greek elaphrós "light, nimble", and Sanskrit laghú "quick, light". Proto-Germanic inherited the adjective as *linhtaz "light (of weight)", which survives in English light and Old Norse léttr. Crucially, the Germanic family also built a derived causative verb, *līhtijaną, "to make light, to alleviate, to lift a weight off".

The great semantic leap — dismounting. In the horse-centred societies of the Germanic world, "to make light" found a daily, concrete application: when a rider dismounted he made his horse lighter. Old English līhtan therefore carries both "to lighten" and "to dismount, to come down from a horse"; the two senses are recorded side by side, and from "dismount" it is the shortest of steps to "come down from the air" — a bird or an arrow "dismounts" onto its target. English then reinforced the simplex verb with the prefix ā-, an intensive/away prefix, producing ālīhtan "to alight, to come down, to land", attested in Old English from the earliest prose and glosses onward.

Middle English and the age of chivalry. In Middle English the verb appears as alighten, alyghte, alihten. It is the routine word for the ritual of dismounting in medieval romance: knights "alight" from their war-horses before combat, before oratory, before entering halls. Malory's Le Morte Darthur (c. 1470) uses the word constantly of knights lighting down from their horses; the formula "he alighted and…" signals courtesy and deference. The -gh- spelling reflects the old velar fricative, which later fell silent, while the vowel continued to develop: Middle English long ī underwent the Great Vowel Shift, giving Modern English alight /əˈlaɪt/.

Early modern register and the carriage age. Through the 16th–18th centuries alight remains standard, but chiefly in the sense of stepping down from vehicles and horses. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novels — Richardson, Austen, Dickens — constantly describe travellers "alighting from a carriage" or "alighting at an inn". It is in this period that the verb acquires its decorous, formal colouring, since the common speech of the street preferred "get down", "step out", "get off".

Aviation and the figurative turn. With the coming of aviation in the early 20th century, alight took on a new technical life: pilots and journalists speak of aircraft "alighting" on runways, fields and water, and the phrase "alight on the water" remains standard in emergency-landing discourse. Meanwhile the older prepositional use alight on/upon broadened in the 19th century from physical settling to mental settling: the gaze, attention or fancy "alights" on a person, an object or an idea — i.e. "singles out, lands upon". This is the source of modern "she alighted on a solution": attention imagined as a bird that flies and finally settles.

The homograph whirlpool. The learner's real difficulty is the adjective alight ("burning, glowing"), first appearing in the 16th century from the phrase on light ("in the light") — an entirely different etymology rooted in the Indo-European "shine" root *lewk-, not *legʷʰ-. Poets from the Romantics onward loved the adjective ("the sky was alight", "her eyes were alight"), and its popularity constantly tempts readers to misparse the verb. Modern dictionaries keep the two words separate; the participle alighted unambiguously belongs to the "descend and settle" verb.

Literary and socio-cultural picture today. In contemporary English alighted is a marker of considered style: it appears in formal fiction, in nature writing about birds, in ceremonial prose and in railway notices. Its decline from everyday speech — the story of countless English verbs displaced by shorter, brisker competitors — has left it with a dignified, old-world flavour, exactly the flavour Tolkien exploits when the raven Roäc alights stiffly before the dwarves in The Hobbit (1937).
