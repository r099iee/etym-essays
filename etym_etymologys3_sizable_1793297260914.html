The history of sizable is the story of a courtroom word that walked out of the law courts and learned to measure the whole world. It begins in ancient Rome with the verb for sitting in judgment, passes through medieval ordinances that fixed honest weights and measures, becomes the English noun for sheer physical bulk, and finally — as late as the seventeenth century — grows an -able tail and sets up as an independent adjective of considerate magnitude.

The deepest root is the Proto-Indo-European base *sed-, to sit, one of the stablest roots in the family, furnishing Latin sedēre, to sit, and its compound assidēre, to sit beside someone, especially to sit beside a defendant as assessor or judge. From the feminine past participle of that compound, *assisa, Vulgar Latin fashioned a noun meaning a judicial sitting and hence an ordinance or decree — the word we still meet as assize, the sittings of travelling judges. Old French inherited it as assise, and Middle English borrowed it around 1300 as sise, first meaning a legal regulation fixing standard weights, prices, and measures, as in the celebrated assize of bread and ale that set the lawful weight of the farthing loaf.

  • Roman law — sitting in judgment: Latin assidēre names the assessor who sits beside the magistrate; the sitting itself, assisa, becomes the name of the court’s decree, judgment turned into edict.
  • Old French — the ordinance: assise and its clipped everyday form sise denote fixed standards imposed by authority — the official size that loaves, gallons, and ells of cloth must match on pain of fine.
  • Middle English — from regulation to dimension (c. 1300–1500): sise slides from ‘the regulation prescribing magnitude’ to ‘the magnitude prescribed’, and then to magnitude in general; by the 1500s size means bulk and dimension outright, helped along by phrases like ‘of what size’ and by the tailor’s, shoemaker’s, and shipwright’s daily trade.
  • Early Modern English — the adjective is born (1610s): size takes the all-purpose suffix -able, from Latin -ābilis via French, arriving first as sizeable, ‘of considerable dimensions’, applied to ships, estates, fishes, and portions in mercantile prose.
  • The great spelling split (19th–20th c.): American dictionaries after Webster regularise the form to sizable on the analogy of sized and sizing, while British usage keeps the etymological e in sizeable; both forms remain fully standard today and are pronounced identically.
  • Twentieth century — the abstract career: the adjective migrates from warehouses and whales to majorities, investments, samples, literatures, and differences, becoming the social scientist’s favourite unit of respectable magnitude, exactly as in the source sentence’s sizable literature.

Phonetically the word is a miniature of regular English development: the long i of size keeps its Great Vowel Shift diphthong /aɪ/, the suffix vowel reduces to a schwa, and the whole settles as /ˈsaɪzəbəl/ — three easy syllables whose very effortlessness explains part of the word’s popularity, since it sounds precise without demanding precision. Literary attestations track the migration neatly: seventeenth-century writers apply it to fleets and fortunes, Victorian novelists to appetites and country houses, and twentieth-century academics to samples, effects, and bodies of literature. The moral of the evolution is quietly democratic: a word invented to let kings police the weight of loaves now lets any reviewer praise any library, and the judge’s seat has become, four thousand years later, everybody’s ruler.
