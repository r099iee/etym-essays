Origin and Latin foundations.

The story of solemnity begins with the Latin adjective sollemnis (also spelled solemnis, sollennis), traditionally analysed as a compound of sollus "whole, entire, unharmed" and annus "year": a sollemne sacrum was a rite fixed to recur each year — "annual, established by religious custom." On the traditional reading, a sollemnis observance was performed sollō annuō, "with the whole year," that is, on its due yearly cycle. This etymology, however, is not secure: some scholars propose an Etruscan source, and others analyse the word as sol- plus the suffix -emnis without a second element "year." The "whole year" explanation is the inherited school-book account and rests partly on folk-etymological neatness, so the debate deserves an explicit flag.

Whatever its prehistory, in classical Latin sollemnis had a rich semantic range: "fixed and established (of days and customs), appointed by religious law; customary; solemn, ceremonious; festive." Its neuter plural, sollemnia, was substantivised to mean "religious ceremonies, festivals, solemn rites" — precisely the sense of "solemnities." From this noun Latin built the abstract sollemnitas (Late Latin also solemnitas), "a solemn observance, a ceremony, a solemnity." The word thus carried both a concrete ceremonial sense and an abstract quality-sense from very early on.

The Romance and Middle English transmission.

Latin sollemnitas passed into Old French as solempnete/solemnité and into Middle English as solempnete, solempnite, first attested around 1300. Chaucer uses the word in the Canterbury Tales and Troilus and Criseyde for both "ceremony, festivity" and "solemn seriousness." Two strands coexisted in medieval usage: (1) the concrete plural "solempnities" for the Church's high feasts and the ceremonial celebrations attached to them; (2) the abstract sense of grave, earnest seriousness. Both strands descend from the same Latin word, diverge in Middle English, and continue to the present.

Religious and liturgical specialisation.

  • In the Roman Catholic liturgical calendar a "solemnity" is the highest class of feast (Latin sollemnitas), ranking above "feasts" and "memorials": Christmas, Epiphany, Easter, Ascension, Pentecost, Corpus Christi, All Saints.
  • The medieval English "solempnyte" of a feast day involved mass, processions, and public pageantry; the plural was natural, since a single feast produced many observances.
  • This liturgical usage kept the word alive and respectable through the Reformation, when Protestant translators and divines nonetheless retained "solemnity" for religious ceremonies and grave assemblies.

Secular broadening and literary colour.

From the 16th century onward "solemnity" broadened to any formal, dignified public proceeding: coronations, funerals, state receptions, university ceremonies. By the 18th and 19th centuries the plural "solemnities" regularly named the whole imposing machinery of a formal occasion. Literary authors exploited a latent irony: solemnities can be grand or merely long-winded. Dickens, Thackeray, and Trollope use the plural for ceremonies that are impressive but sometimes pompous. Tolkien continues this line in The Hobbit: Fili's impatience "at these solemnities" plays the archaic grandeur of the word against a dwarf's practical urgency, the plural neatly suggesting a whole programme of grand formalities he would rather be done with.

Modern senses and phonetic notes.

  • Modern English: "solemnities" = formal ceremonies / a programme of observances; "solemnity" (uncountable) = gravity, earnest seriousness.
  • Legal-historical: "solemnities" = the formalities required to validate a legal act (e.g., the solemnities of marriage).
  • Pronunciation: Middle English /səˈlɛmpnətiː/ showed the cluster -mpn-; the p was later lost, giving modern /səˈlɛm.nə.ti/. In the adjective solemn the final n is silent (/ˈsɒl.əm/), while in solemnity it is pronounced — a classic trap for learners.
  • Register: formal and literary; the plural is commoner than the singular and frequently appears as "the solemnities."
