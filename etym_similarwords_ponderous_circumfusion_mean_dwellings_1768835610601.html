• Heavy (ponderous): Similar because both denote great weight; differs because heavy is a neutral physical adjective with no necessary sense of sluggish labour or laboured movement.
  • Massive (ponderous): Similar because both suggest great size and bulk; differs because massive describes dimensions without implying slowness, clumsiness, or motion at all.
  • Weighty (ponderous): Similar because both carry the idea of weighing much; differs because weighty is usually transferred to abstract things — arguments and reasons of importance — not to physical motion.
  • Cumbersome (ponderous): Similar because both imply difficulty of handling; differs because cumbersome stresses awkwardness due to bulk, not the slow, solemn, laborious progress of the ponderous.
  • Laboured (ponderous): Similar because both describe effortful, heavy motion or style; differs because laboured emphasises strain and effort rather than the mere dead weight of mass.
  • Slow (ponderous): Similar because both imply lack of speed; differs because slow is neutral and could be gentle or deliberate, while ponderous explains the slowness by weight.
  • Diffusion (circumfusion): Similar because both describe something spreading; differs because diffusion is the scattering of a substance in any direction, while circumfusion specifically means pouring or spreading around a central point.
  • Dispersion (circumfusion): Similar because both describe spreading outward; differs because dispersion implies scattering and separation, while circumfusion suggests an enveloping flow that surrounds.
  • Envelopment (circumfusion): Similar because both describe being surrounded; differs because envelopment is general wrapping, while circumfusion keeps the fluid, poured, quasi-liquid sense.
  • Embrace (circumfusion): Similar because both name a surrounding presence; differs because embrace is warm and human, while circumfusion is a cold, learned term of poured envelopment.
  • Poor (mean): Similar because both describe lack of wealth or quality; differs because poor is a neutral, sympathetic word, while mean adds the judgment of inferiority and squalor.
  • Shabby (mean): Similar because both describe worn, low-quality things; differs because shabby stresses visible dilapidation, while mean stresses smallness and low rank of condition.
  • Common (mean): Similar because both descend from the idea of what is shared by all; differs because common today is neutral or positive (a common room), while mean carries the historical slide into inferiority.
  • Squalid (mean): Similar because both describe filthy, wretched conditions; differs because squalid emphasises dirt and neglect, while mean emphasises poverty and lowliness of station.
  • Vulgar (mean): Similar because both descend from 'of the common people' into 'low'; differs because vulgar means tasteless and coarse, while mean means inferior and shabby.
  • Home (dwellings): Similar because both name places where people live; differs because home is warm and emotional, charged with belonging and sentiment, whereas a dwelling is deliberately flat and functional.
  • House (dwellings): Similar because both refer to a place of residence; differs because house is purely physical, the building itself, with no administrative or legal neutrality.
  • Residence (dwellings): Similar because both denote a place where one lives; differs because residence is official and slightly pompous, the word of forms and registers, not of ordinary speech.
  • Abode (dwellings): Similar because both mean a place of dwelling; differs because abode is literary and suggests something temporary, a resting-place, not the fixed, settled, legal permanence of a dwelling.
  • Habitation (dwellings): Similar because both name a place of living; differs because habitation stresses the act or fact of inhabiting a wild or empty place, while a dwelling is a settled residence.
