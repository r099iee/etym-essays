Eaves are the lower, projecting edges of a roof — the parts that overhang the walls of a building and stand out beyond them, so that rainwater running down the roof is shed clear of the wall and foundation rather than streaming down the face of the wall. The word is virtually always plural in modern English: one says “the eaves”, as one says “the outskirts” or “the depths”, and grammarians class eaves among the pluralia tantum — nouns that occur only (or mainly) in the plural. This is a grammatical accident with an interesting history: the -s is not and never was a plural marker; Old English efes was a singular noun, and the final -s belonged to the stem itself. As the word’s structure became opaque, speakers re-analysed the -s as the familiar plural ending, began saying “the eaves are”, and eventually — in the 18th century — back-formed a new singular eave that is still occasionally heard.

Primary sense: the roof’s overhang. In building, the eaves are the overhanging lower edge of a roof plane, usually finished with a fascia (the vertical board or metal edge), a soffit (the underside, often ventilated), and, in modern practice, a gutter, which catches the runoff the eaves were built to throw clear. The functional logic is ancient: shed the water beyond the wall-foot, keep the timber and masonry dry, and keep open space at the wall-head. Eaves vary enormously across traditions — wide, deep eaves with exposed rafter tails in Alpine and Japanese buildings; minimal parapeted eaves in classical architecture, where the roof is hidden behind a wall; deep bracketed eaves in Craftsman houses; the flared eaves of Chinese and Japanese temple roofs, whose upturned corners are among the most recognisable roof forms in the world.

Secondary senses and derivatives.

  • The eaves as habitat — the space under the eaves is classic nesting territory: swallows, house martins, wasps, and bats live “under the eaves”, and the phrase carries a settled domestic warmth in English.
  • Eaves-drip and property law — Old English yfesdrype, “eaves-drip”, names the strip of ground where rain falls from the roof; in Anglo-Saxon charters and medieval law this drip-line defined boundaries and building rights — one might not build so close that a neighbour’s eaves-drip fell on one’s land, or one’s own on a neighbour’s.
  • Eavesdropping — the most famous derivative: one who stood under the eaves to overhear talk inside was an eavesdropper (15th century), and the verb eavesdrop (1600s) has entirely outlived its building context — to eavesdrop today is to listen in secretly anywhere, and few speakers still picture the roof.
  • Extended edge-uses — historically the word (as eves) could mean any edge or brink, including the edge of a wood or a cliff; this broad sense is now archaic, surviving only in dialect and the odd literary echo.

Register and common collocations. Eaves is a neutral, technical-but-household word: roofing manuals (eave height, eave depth, eave overhang) and everyday speech about dripping roofs both use it. Canadians say eavestrough for the gutter hung at the eaves, and North American builders use eave in the singular in compounds like “eave height”. In the story the word does its classic job: “long icicles like crystal daggers hung down from the eaves of the houses” — the eaves are where winter hangs its fringe, the exact place where the drip and the freeze meet.
