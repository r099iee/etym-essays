• Emblem: Similar because both are objects standing for an idea, identity, or office; differs because "emblem" is the broader, looser term and does not necessarily imply official authorization, rank, or institutional regulation — an insignia is always an emblem, but an emblem need not be an insignia.
  • Badge: Similar because both are small physical marks of membership or rank; differs because a badge is typically worn on the person and is smaller and more casual, whereas insignia can be larger, ceremonial, and hung or displayed (plaques, banners, standards) and carries a more formal, institutional register.
  • Crest: Similar because both denote identifying heraldic emblems; differs because "crest" is specifically the device atop a coat of arms belonging to one family or lineage, while insignia cover all marks of rank and office beyond a single heraldic family.
  • Ensign: Similar because both name official identifying marks of rank or body (the military ensign was a standard, and an ensign is also a rank); differs because "ensign" today most often means a national flag or a junior naval officer, whereas insignia is the general noun for all such marks.
  • Seal: Similar because both authenticate and identify office; differs because a seal is specifically an impressed device used for legal or official authentication of documents, whereas insignia are visible tokens worn, flown, or displayed rather than impressed on wax or paper.
  • Symbol: Similar because both are signs standing for something beyond themselves; differs because a symbol is an abstract, conventional representation of an idea, while insignia are concrete, regulated marks of rank, office, or membership with an institutional character.
  • Sign: Similar because both are marks that carry meaning and the word is etymologically embedded in insignia (in + sign); differs because "sign" is the broadest possible term for any indicator, with none of the official, honorific, or institutional weight of insignia.
  • Token: Similar because both are physical evidence or marks of something (rank, membership, recognition); differs because a token is generally a smaller, more personal or exchangeable object (a love token, a token of gratitude), lacking the formality and public regulation of insignia.
  • Mark: Similar because both denote a visible distinguishing feature; differs because "mark" is generic and neutral — a mark can be accidental or private — whereas insignia are deliberately designed and officially authorized signs of status.
  • Device: Similar because both name an emblem or design adopted as an identifying badge; differs because "device" leans heraldic and poetic, often denoting a motto-bearing design or a personal impress, while insignia denote institutional marks of rank and office.
  • Regalia: Similar because both belong to the world of official ceremonial emblems of office; differs because regalia is the full panoply of ceremonial dress, crowns, sceptres, and ornaments of office, whereas insignia are the specific identifying marks (badges, plaques, standards) within or apart from that panoply.
  • Decoration: Similar because both include medals and awards worn as marks of honour; differs because a decoration is specifically a conferred honour (a medal, cross, or ribbon), while insignia include all marks of rank and membership, honorary or otherwise.
  • Medallion / Medal: Similar because both are metallic marks of distinction; differs because medals are specific conferred awards, while insignia is the umbrella term for all official distinguishing marks of rank, office, and membership.
  • Accoutrements: Similar because both can denote the visible trappings of a role; differs because accoutrements are the supplementary equipment and effects of a role or uniform, not the identifying marks themselves, and carry no semiotic function.
