• Khan: Similar because Cham is simply an archaic spelling of it, denoting the same steppe ruler; differs because Khan is the modern historical term, neutral and literal, with none of the mock-heroic irony of the English epithet.
  • Khagan: Similar because it is the fuller title of the supreme ruler over subordinate khans, the sense the “Great” in “Great Cham” gestures towards; differs because it is a technical term of steppe history, never used as a literary nickname.
  • Great Khan: Similar because it names the Mongol emperor literally; differs because the phrase is historical and descriptive (Genghis, Kublai), whereas “Great Cham” is a deliberate joke about an Englishman.
  • despot: Similar because it denotes absolute, unaccountable rule; differs because it is a political term of abuse applied to real governors, without the exotic colour or the affectionate exaggeration.
  • autocrat: Similar because it describes one who rules alone; differs because it is a neutral constitutional label, lacking any suggestion of grandeur or cultural tyranny.
  • tyrant: Similar because it implies power exercised without appeal; differs because it is strongly condemnatory and suggests cruelty, whereas the Johnsonian epithet is largely affectionate.
  • dictator: Similar because a “dictator of letters” is the nearest English equivalent of the idea; differs because it evokes Roman or modern politics and carries no image of an eastern court.
  • arbiter: Similar because Johnson arbitrated taste and usage; differs because an arbiter persuades rather than commands, and the word implies no coercive power at all.
  • oracle: Similar because Johnson’s pronouncements were received as authoritative; differs because an oracle is consulted for wisdom, not obeyed as a sovereign.
  • doyen: Similar because it denotes the senior, pre-eminent figure of a profession; differs because it is respectful and institutional, with no trace of despotism or irony.
