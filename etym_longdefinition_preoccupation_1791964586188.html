preoccupation is a noun with two closely linked senses: the state of being engrossed in thought or by a subject, and the subject itself that holds that attention. In the source — “The preoccupation with Cicero in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance was not limited to his style” — it is the second, more object-like sense: the intense, collective attention paid to Cicero, an absorption that extended beyond stylistic imitation to his ethics, politics and authority as a thinker.

Morphologically it is pre- + occupation, a loan from French préoccupation resting on Latin praeoccupātiō, “a seizing beforehand”. The history of the word is the history of a military metaphor applied to the mind: what first described ground taken before an enemy arrives comes to describe a subject that has taken possession of the attention before anything else can.

Senses of the noun

  • Absorption, engrossment: the condition of having one’s attention claimed so fully that other things go unnoticed — “he answered with the vagueness of preoccupation”.
  • A ruling concern or obsession: the thing that so claims the mind — “her preoccupation with status”, “Cicero was a lifelong preoccupation”. This is the sense in the source.
  • Prior occupation (literal, rare): the state of being occupied or taken beforehand; a legal or military sense that is now effectively obsolete.
  • Prepossession, bias (17th–18th c.): a mind already “occupied” by an opinion, hence prejudice or partiality. The sense survives only vestigially, chiefly in the older word prepossession.
  • Rhetorical anticipation: in classical rhetoric praeoccupatio is the figure of answering an objection before it is raised, also called procatalepsis or antipophora. English “preoccupation” never fully naturalized this technical sense, which is usually rendered as prolepsis.

The related adjective

  • Preoccupied: “lost in thought, abstracted”. This is now the commonest member of the family and has drifted toward a temporary, almost trivial state (“sorry, I was preoccupied”), while the noun retains a stronger and more durable sense.
  • Preoccupied in the older sense: “already occupied or taken” — a preoccupied seat, a preoccupied position. The literal sense survives in formal and legal registers.
  • Preoccupying: “claiming the mind in advance”, used of a worry or question that will not let go.

Connotation, register, collocations

The noun is neutral to mildly critical. To call something a preoccupation is to say that it takes up a disproportionate share of attention; to call a person preoccupied is gentler, suggesting abstraction rather than rudeness. It collocates strongly with with (“a preoccupation with money, hygiene, death, detail”) and with possessives (“his lifelong preoccupation”). Unlike interest it implies exclusivity; unlike obsession it can be respectable and intellectual. In the plural, preoccupations often means a person’s or an age’s characteristic themes — “the preoccupations of Victorian novelists” — and there the word is descriptive rather than diagnostic.

Semantic bridge. Latin occupāre means “to seize, take possession of”; praeoccupāre is to seize before someone else, to occupy the ground first. Applied to the mind, the metaphor is exact: a preoccupation is a subject that has taken possession of the mental ground before other claimants arrive. The prefix thus does double work — priority in time (“beforehand”) and priority in rank (“before all else”). French préoccupation shifted the military image toward anxiety and solicitude in the seventeenth century, and English followed: to be preoccupied is to be held by something, whether worry or study.

The word in this reading

In the history of scholarship the “preoccupation with Cicero” is a genuine cultural phenomenon: medieval readers valued him as a moralist and a model of Latin prose, while Renaissance humanists made him the centre of grammar-school education, imitation exercises and the theory of eloquence. Petrarch’s letters to Cicero, the Ciceronian controversies of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and Nizzoli’s Thesaurus Ciceronianus all belong to that long absorption. The source’s point is that this attention was not merely stylistic — it embraced Cicero’s philosophy, his civic ideals and his authority as a guide to life, which is why the word preoccupation, with its suggestion of a mind seized in advance, is so apt.
