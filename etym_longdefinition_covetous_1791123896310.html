Covetous is an adjective meaning greedily desirous of something, especially something belonging to another person; grasping, avaricious, and envious in wanting to possess it. It is a moral condemnation, not a neutral description of desire.

The core biblical sense dominates: to covet is to desire illicitly what is your neighbour's — house, spouse, servant, goods, status. A covetous person does not merely want more in the abstract; he wants yours. Hence the envy component distinguishes covetous from merely greedy or ambitious. A greedy child wants a second cake; a covetous child wants your cake precisely because it is yours.

  • Avaricious and grasping for wealth: the covetous miser, the covetous landlord, Dickens's squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner. Here covetous summarizes a whole career of pinching and hoarding. Hands, ledgers, and rent-books are its emblems. It implies persistent, calculating acquisition, not impulsive spending.
  • Envious desire for status, love, or possessions: covetous eyes on a promotion, a covetous glance at a neighbour's garden. The desire includes resentment that another has it. Modern psychology would call this upward social comparison turned hostile.
  • Theological sense — the sin of covetousness: in catechisms covetousness is avarice, one of the Seven Deadly Sins, breach of the Tenth Commandment. It denotes inordinate attachment to worldly goods as an orientation of the soul, not a single act. To be covetous is to be spiritually diseased, idolizing Mammon.
  • Extended use for any inordinate craving: covetous of praise, covetous of power, covetous of time. Here the of-complement broadens beyond property. Even then the tone stays negative: a covetous appetite for applause is not healthy ambition but hungry vanity.
  • Positive-bleached use in commercial language (coveted, not covetous): strictly, the past participle coveted (a coveted prize, most-coveted role) has softened into marketing praise meaning highly desired. Covetous itself resists this bleaching; applied to a person it remains an insult. Calling a collection coveted flatters it; calling the collector covetous damns him. Learners must not confuse the two.

Connotation is always censorious when applied to character. While ambitious can praise, aspiring can encourage, and desirous can sound neutral, covetous judges. It imagines narrowed eyes, clutching fingers, a cold heart. Dickens deliberately piles verbs of manual seizure before it to make the abstraction physical.

Grammar: attributive (a covetous man), predicative (he is covetous), with complement covetous of + noun (covetous of wealth). Adverb covetously, noun covetousness. Formal register, literary and moralistic; everyday speech more often says greedy, jealous, grabby. Using covetous signals you are invoking a biblical or Dickensian moral frame.

Domain notes: theology and ethics use it technically; law uses covet rarely for mens rea toward another's property; economics contrasts covetous hoarding with productive investment; literature uses it as character shorthand for Scrooge-type misers whose low temperature freezes all human warmth.
