WROUGHT: The archaic past tense and past participle of the verb "work," meaning "made, fashioned, executed, or effected," and by extension "worked into being"; as an adjective, "worked or shaped by effort," as in "wrought iron"; and, in the fixed phrase "wrought up," "emotionally stirred or agitated." In the card's sentence — "The defeat had __________ a deep and lasting change in his temper, and he was no longer the man he had once been" — the word does far more than report causation. It reaches back to the smithy: to have wrought something is to have hammered it out, beaten it into shape, moulded a raw material into an enduring form through sustained, patient effort. The tone is grand, biblical, almost geological; it suggests slow, painful labour and durable consequence rather than a momentary shock, and it drapes the prose in a stately, archaic dignity that the everyday verb "worked" would instantly flatten. The choice of "wrought" signals that the change was not a sudden event but a forging — an alteration so deep that it became part of the very grain of the man's temper. Core features of the concept.
  • Archaic form of work: wrought is the old strong past and participle of work, displaced in ordinary use by "worked" but preserved where dignity, craft, or age is wanted.
  • Effort and craft: unlike "caused" or "made," wrought insists on labour — the thing has been shaped, hammered, or moulded by sustained work, not merely produced.
  • Formative force: in figurative use (as in the card's sentence) wrought describes change that alters the very substance of a person or thing — a deep and lasting alteration.
  • Register: archaic, poetic, biblical, and elevated; the word belongs to solemn prose, liturgy, and the language of craft, and it is deliberately chosen for gravity.
Polysemy and related senses. Wrought has three broad uses, all historical outgrowths of its status as the old participle of work:
  • Sense 1 — the verb form (made, effected): the archaic past tense and participle of work, meaning "made, fashioned, performed, or brought about," as in "wrought a change," "wrought havoc," "wrought destruction," "wrought a miracle." This is the sense of the card's sentence.
  • Sense 2 — the craft adjective (shaped by work): "wrought" modifying a material means "worked, beaten, or shaped by hand or tool," as in "wrought iron" (iron worked and shaped, as opposed to cast iron), "wrought silver," "wrought gold," "fine-wrought metal." Here the smithy image is literal.
  • Sense 3 — the emotional participle (agitated): in the fixed phrase "wrought up" (and the compound "overwrought"), the word means "emotionally stirred, agitated, keyed up" — one's nerves have been "worked into a knot." This sense turns the image of hammering inward: the agitation is the emotions worked out of shape.
All three senses share the single idea of working: something is made, shaped, or stirred by the application of force. The historical bridge from the smithy to the temper is direct — the change in the man's character has been hammered into him by the defeat, as iron is hammered into shape by the smith. Set against its near-synonyms.
  • Caused is abstract and neutral, naming a bare relation of cause and effect with no suggestion of effort or craft; to say the defeat "caused" a change is to report a fact, whereas to say it "wrought" a change is to describe a process of shaping.
  • Effected is formal and almost administrative, suggesting the orderly execution of an intention, but it is bloodless and bureaucratic beside "wrought."
  • Produced is industrial and mechanical, implying assembly or generation on a mass scale rather than the craft of a single hand.
  • Made is the plain, colourless everyday word.
  • Fashioned emphasizes design and the form given to material, but lacks the muscular image of pounding and beating that wrought carries.
  • Worked is the regular, neutral past of the same verb — it reports that work was done without the archaic weight.
Only "wrought" implies pounding, folding, beating, and moulding — the action of hands, or of time, upon resistant material — and it is precisely this muscular concreteness that makes it the favoured word when a writer wishes to present change as a form of manufacture. Collocations and register. The word survives idiomatically in a small but tenacious set: "wrought iron," "wrought havoc," "wrought destruction," "wrought a change," "wrought a miracle," "wrought up," and the compound "overwrought." It collocates with nouns of deep change ("change," "transformation," "havoc," "ruin," "devastation") and with the preposition "upon" ("wrought upon him"). Its register is deliberately archaic and elevated: the word is the choice of solemn prose, of the King James Bible, of historical fiction, and of any writer who wants the reader to feel that a change has been forged, not merely made. In modern English it is no longer a working verb form; it is a stylistic resource.
