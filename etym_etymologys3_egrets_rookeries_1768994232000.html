egrets — a biography of the plumed heron. The story begins in the Germanic north with a bird's cry: the heron, whose harsh, grating call caught the naming ear, was called *haigrô or *hraigrô — two metathesized twins of a single echoic root that survive in Old English hrāgra, Old High German heigaro, and Old Norse hegri. No Indo-European root can be reconstructed behind it with confidence; the word is one of those old Germanic animal names made of the animal's own voice. What matters for English is the southern branch of that Germanic stock. When the Lombards carried their Germanic speech into Italy, *haigaro became garza, the Italian word for heron; a diminutive garzetta, "little heron," slipped into Old French as aigrette — a name for the bird and, soon, for its delicate feather plume. Middle English took the word about 1400 as egret.

Semantic narrowing. The narrowing is the interesting shift: in the Germanic stock the word meant any heron, but in the Romance chain it settled on the white, plume-carrying members of the family — the birds whose breeding plumes ("aigrettes") became the great prize of the nineteenth-century millinery trade. The plumes, grown only in the breeding season and displayed in nuptial display, were shot by the tens of thousands for women's hats, and the fashion nearly exterminated the egrets of the American South. The word thus passed through three registers in quick succession: the fowling-and-heraldry word of the gentry (14th–17th c.), the scientific and aesthetic name of the white wader (18th c.), and the conservation cause célèbre (late 19th–20th c.), when the Audubon movement and protective legislation — the Migratory Bird Treaty Act of 1918 — turned "egret" into a symbol of the feather trade's cruelty. "White as candles" is therefore not mere metaphor but etymology: the whiteness is what the word has meant for centuries, from aigrette plume to snowy wader to poet's votive flame.



rookeries — a biography of the croaker and his colony. Here the root is the very old Indo-European cawing root *ker- "to cry hoarsely," which also produced Greek korax and Latin corvus, the raven of both languages. Germanic made two birds from it: *hrabnaz "raven" (Old English hræfn, our raven) and, with the o-grade and a -k- suffix that seems to catch the cry itself, *hrōkaz "rook." Old English hrōc names the Corvus frugilegus, a crow that nests in huge, noisy colonies; the rook is above all a social bird, and its name went on to build the rookery.

From bird to colony to slum. English tacked the place-suffix -ery (Old French -erie) onto rook in 1725 to make rookery, "a breeding colony of rooks." The eighteenth century extended it to any gregarious bird's nesting colony — penguin rookeries, gull rookeries, heronries — and in the nineteenth the word made its most striking leap: the packed, overcrowded tenement districts of Victorian London, warrens of lodging-houses, courts, and thieves' dens "packed like nests," were called rookeries. The image of dense, close-packed, disorderly crowding migrated from tree-tops to slums, so that a single word named both the noisy colony of birds and the desperate housing of the poor. In the twentieth century the slum sense receded to historical usage, and "rookery" returned to its root meaning — the communal nesting site where the birds themselves still gather and caw. The passage's "egrets in their rookeries" quietly exploits the whole range: the marsh birds stand in nests whose name once belonged to the rook and later to the slum — a word that has spent three centuries migrating from one crowded community to another.
