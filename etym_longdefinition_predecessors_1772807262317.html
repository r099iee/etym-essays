In the usher’s worldly briefing to magistrate Yu-cun, predecessors names the earlier holders of the same provincial office—men who, though shrewd enough to see the justice of the Xue case, deliberately left it untouched. The plural is not a neutral administrative label; it is an indictment of a chain of calculated cowardice. Each prior magistrate chose silence over confrontation with the local magnates listed in the “life-preserver,” so the unsettled case becomes evidence that corruption is hereditary in the post itself: power and fear pass from one official to the next like furniture left in a yamen. The word therefore frames Yu-cun’s dilemma as institutional rather than personal—he is invited to join a lineage of non-action rather than to break it. Core meaning. Predecessors are the people who held a job, office, position, or title before the current holder—the earlier occupants of the same role. The singular predecessor likewise names one such person. The relation is structural (same office, different time), not genetic or merely chronological: your predecessors in a post are its earlier holders, whoever they were, whether or not you ever met them. Etymological and conceptual bridge. The word is Latin through and through: prae- “before” + de- “away” + cedere “to go,” so that a praedēcessor is literally “one who has gone away before”—one who has gone (departed from the post, retired, or died) at an earlier time. The semantic bridge runs through the idea of stepping down: the office is a place, and those who held it before you have “gone off” from it ahead of you. Predecessor thereby encodes succession as a double motion—departure (de-cedere) plus priority (prae-). English has used the word for office-succession since the 15th century, and has extended it from people to things: a predecessor model of a phone, a predecessor system, a predecessor version. Polysemy and secondary senses (exhaustive):
  • Office-succession sense (dominant): the person who previously held the same job, office, or position; “my predecessor in the magistracy.”
  • Genealogical / archaic sense: a forebear or ancestor; earlier English usage could apply predecessor to a lineal ancestor, a sense now largely absorbed by ancestor and forebear.
  • Of things (20th century): an earlier model, product, version, or system that a newer one replaces; “the new turbine’s predecessors were gas-fired.”
  • Institutional and legal sense: the prior holder of an office, title, estate, or corporate seat, used especially in legal and bureaucratic contexts of succession and liability.
  • Chronological / abstract sense: anything that comes before in a sequence—an earlier event, stage, or arrangement seen as the forerunner of a later one.
  • Plural force: the plural predecessors accumulates the whole chain of prior holders, stressing the weight of precedent—as in the passage, where an entire line of officials has avoided a case.
Register and collocations. Standard, formal, and neutral; equally at home in legal, bureaucratic, historical, and everyday speech. Common collocations: immediate predecessor, my / your / his predecessor, predecessor in office, predecessors and successors, to inherit from one’s predecessor, a predecessor of the current model. The word carries none of the emotion of ancestor (blood) or the drama of precursor (anticipation); it is the sober administrative word for succession, and in this passage its very sobriety makes the indictment colder: not romance, not genealogy, just the plain fact that office after office chose fear.
