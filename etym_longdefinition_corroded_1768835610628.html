corroded (adjective; past participle of corrode) - eaten away gradually, as by chemical action (rust, acid, salt air) or by slow external abrasion; pitted, roughened, and diminished by a gnawing process. The Latin source, corrōdere, is the intensive of rōdere, "to gnaw": corrosion is gnawing - the invisible teeth of chemistry and time working from the surface inward. Primary sense: chemical and physical consumption. In its technical core, corrosion names the slow destruction of a material - most often metal - by chemical reaction with its environment: iron rusts, lead blackens and pits, stone is eaten by acid rain. The corroded object is characteristically smaller (mass has been consumed), darker (oxide film and tarnish), rougher (pitting and cratering), and weaker (structure undermined) than it was. The word's force is the slowness and the invisibility of the agent: corrosion works steadily from the surface inward, with the patience of a rodent at a beam. Nuance and tone in the literary context. The passage from Mervyn Peake's Titus Groan describes Rottcodd's skull as "dark and small like a corroded musket bullet". The simile fuses the martial with the entropic: the skull is compared to a spent lead ball, ancient, blackened, its once-smooth surface eaten into pitted ruin by age. "Corroded" does the work of several adjectives at once - it states the darkening (corroded lead goes black), the shrinking (corrosion consumes mass), the roughening (pitted, no longer round), and the slowness (the erosion of decades). The tone is gothic, precise, funereal: a head so worn by time that it has become an antique object of weaponry, dense with the residue of its own history. Polysemy and secondary modern meanings.
  • Chemistry and metallurgy. The literal, scientific sense: oxidative or acidic attack on metal - rusting, verdigris, pitting. Corrosion is the technical noun; corrosive the adjective naming the agent.
  • Medicine. A corroding ulcer or corrosive acid burns tissue - the old "eating away" of flesh, the organic original of the metal sense.
  • Moral and psychological metaphor. "Corroded trust", "corroded hopes", "corroding envy" - emotions and institutions gnawed from within, exactly as the Latin poets imagined curae rodunt, cares gnawing the heart. The metaphor transfers the mechanics of rust to the invisible erosion of feeling and value.
  • Figurative diminishment. Anything slowly eaten away, worn down, or undermined - a corroded authority, a corroded memory - retaining the sense of diminution: the thing becomes smaller, darker, and rougher than it was.
Differentiation from similar words.
  • Rusted is metal-specific and names the brown oxidation of iron, without the sense of consumption.
  • Eroded belongs to wind, water, and landscapes - surfaces worn by external friction.
  • Decayed and decomposed describe organic rot.
  • Tarnished is a superficial loss of sheen - the moral dimming of reputation.
  • Dissolved is the complete liquid dispersion of a solid.
  • Eaten away is plain but lacks the Latinate weight.
Corroded uniquely implies an active, deliberate agent of gnawing - acid, rust, or time itself - a slow consumption that leaves the object smaller, darker, and rougher than it was, exactly the mineral devastation Peake's simile requires. Etymology and semantic bridge. The word descends from PIE *red- ("to gnaw, to scrape, to chew"), through Latin rōdere ("to gnaw") and its intensive corrōdere ("to gnaw away entirely"), into Old French corroder and Middle English corroden (14th c.). The bridge from teeth to chemistry was made when the "gnawing" of living creatures was extended to the "eating" of sharp substances, and finally to the chemical action of acids and rust. The same root produced rodent (the gnawing order of animals) and, probably, rat ("the gnawer").
