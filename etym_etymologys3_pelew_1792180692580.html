The historical English proper noun Pelew is an eighteenth-century anglicized phonetic transcription of the native Micronesian endonym Belau, the indigenous autonym for the Palauan archipelago. The linguistic journey of the term illustrates how Pacific Austronesian phonology was mediated through European maritime navigation, colonial cartography, and Enlightenment romantic literature before being formally replaced by the modern international spelling Palau.

Indigenous Austronesian Etymology and Mythological Roots:

  • The Palauan Endonym (Belau): The indigenous name Belau possesses two principal etymological explanations within Palauan linguistics and oral history:
    • Descent from Proto-Malayo-Polynesian (*banua): Linguistically, Belau is closely cognate with the Palauan common noun beluu ("village, inhabited land, country"), which traces directly back to Proto-Austronesian and Proto-Malayo-Polynesian *banua ("inhabited territory, homeland, village", cognate with Polynesian whenua / fenua). In this analysis, Belau simply signifies "the homeland" or "the inhabited settlements."
    • Mythological Derivation (Aidebelau): In traditional Palauan oral mythology, the name is tied to the creation epic of the giantess Chuab, whose body formed the islands. A legendary phrase aidebelau ("indirect replies" or "making fire with a fire-drill") is cited in traditional folklore as commemorating the cleverness and cultural resilience of the islands' original inhabitants.
  • Phonological Transmission into European Tongues: The initial consonant in Palauan Belau is a voiced bilabial stop [b] that frequently lenites to a voiced bilabial fricative [β] or is pronounced with weak voicing. When early sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Spanish navigators (such as Ruy López de Villalobos in 1543) first encountered or recorded accounts of the islands, they transcribed the name as Islas Palaos or Palao, substituting the voiceless bilabial plosive [p] for the native Palauan sound.

The British Shipwreck and the Coining of "Pelew":

  • Captain Henry Wilson and the Antelope (1783): The specific English spelling Pelew entered the language in the aftermath of August 9, 1783, when the East India Company packet ship Antelope was wrecked on a reef off the Palauan island of Ulong. Captain Henry Wilson and his crew lived among the Palauan people for three months under the protection of the High Chief (Ibedul) of Koror. In recording the native pronunciation of their island realm, English sailors phonetically transcribed [bəˈlaʊ] as Pelew (pronounced /pəˈluː/), rhyming in contemporary British English with "anew" or "canoe".
  • George Keate’s Bestselling Narrative (1788): In 1788, London man of letters George Keate compiled Wilson’s ship logs into An Account of the Pelew Islands, Situated in the Western Part of the Pacific Ocean. The work captured the imagination of Western Europe, portraying the Pelewese as paragon of innate moral virtue and gentle benevolence, untainted by the corruptions of civil society. The tragic death of the Ibedul's son, Prince Lee Boo, from smallpox in Rotherhithe, London, cemented the name Pelew in British romantic consciousness.
  • Colonial Succession and Modern Cartography: Throughout the nineteenth century, British naval power ensured that Pelew remained the dominant English cartographic label. However, following the 1899 sale of the archipelago from Spain to the German Empire, German colonial cartographers popularized the form Palau (closer to the Spanish Palaos and native Belau). Following the Japanese administration and subsequent American trusteeship after WWII, Palau became the universal international standard, while the newly independent republic adopted Belau in its indigenous constitutional texts in 1981, leaving Pelew as a prized relic of eighteenth-century literary history.
