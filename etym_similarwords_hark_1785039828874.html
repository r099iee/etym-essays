• Hearken: Similar because both mean attentive listening; differs as hark's longer doublet, now chiefly biblical or rhetorical, while hark keeps the imperious hunting-field urgency.
  • Listen: Similar because both mean paying attention to sound; differs as the neutral, modern word of everyday speech, without hark's commanding, archaic force.
  • Hear: Similar because both belong to the perception of sound; differs because hear is the passive perception of sound arriving at the ear, whereas hark commands active, strained attention.
  • Attend: Similar because both mean focused attention; differs because attend implies deliberate, disciplined focus, often on something other than sound alone.
  • Heed: Similar because both mean attending to what is said; differs because heed carries a warning of consequence — heeding advice — rather than the mere summons to listen.
  • Overhear: Similar because both involve hearing; differs because overhear means hearing accidentally or without being addressed, the opposite of hark's deliberate summons.
  • Give ear / lend an ear: Similar because both mean listen attentively; differ as archaic rhetorical phrases that lack hark's crisp, commanding, one-syllable urgency.
  • Pay attention: Similar because both mean direct one's attention; differs as the general modern phrase for any kind of focus, while hark is specifically the imperious call to listen.
