XENOHORMESIS (n.) — A biological hypothesis, formulated at the intersection of evolutionary biology, molecular nutrition, and aging science, proposing that organisms have evolved to detect and respond to chemical stress signals emitted by OTHER species in their environment. The paradigm case is dietary: plants under drought, ultraviolet radiation, pest attack, or other adversity cannot flee, so they defend themselves biochemically, synthesizing stress compounds such as polyphenols (resveratrol, quercetin). The xenohormesis hypothesis holds that the animals that eat those stressed plants have co-opted those very signals as a pre-emptive warning system, mounting protective, adaptive stress responses of their own — before the animal itself has encountered the hardship. Because the signal is foreign and the response is a beneficial low-dose stress response, the coinage fuses xeno- ("foreign") with hormesis ("beneficial stress kick").
  • Primary sense (evolutionary biology): the co-evolutionary claim that dietary stress molecules from one species act as signals that trigger adaptive (hormetic) responses in another. It was first articulated by the molecular biologist David Sinclair and colleagues (Howitz, Bitterman, Cohen, Lamming, Lavu, Wood, Zipkin, Chung, Kisielewski, Zhang, Scherer & Sinclair, 2003, in the journal Nature), offered as a hypothesis to explain why modest amounts of plant stress compounds confer health benefits on animal consumers.
  • Longevity and anti-aging research: in gerontology and molecular nutrition, xenohormesis denotes the mechanism by which dietary polyphenols and related phytochemicals activate conserved longevity pathways — most prominently the sirtuin family of proteins (especially SIRT1) and AMPK — recapitulating the benefits of caloric restriction.
  • Pharmacology and the nutraceutical industry: in the commercial world of supplements and "longevity" products, the term legitimizes the health claims of resveratrol, quercetin, and other plant stress metabolites, framing them as hormetic dietary stressors.
  • Popular-science and wellness discourse: in journalism and wellness media, xenohormesis has been simplified into the slogan that "stressed plants make healthy eaters" — a vivid metaphor for dietary hormesis that often overstates the directness of the causal link.
Etymology and the semantic bridge: the word is a modern scientific coinage (2003) built from two Greek elements. The prefix xeno- (from Greek xénos, "stranger, foreigner, guest") contributes the sense of "other species" — the same element as in xenophobia ("fear of foreigners"), xenotransplantation, and xenolith. The second element, hormesis, is itself a twentieth-century technical term (coined 1943 by the plant pathologists Chester Southam and John Ehrlich) built on Greek hormē ("onset, impulse, rapid motion, eagerness") from the verb hormāein ("to set in motion, urge on, excite"). The conceptual bridge is transparent: a beneficial stress signal ("kick") is received from a foreign source — hence "foreign + beneficial-stress = xenohormesis." Polysemy, jargon, and metaphoric shifts:
  • Hormesis (the base term): a dose-response phenomenon in which a low dose of a stressor or toxin produces a beneficial effect while a high dose is harmful — the "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger" pattern, applied in radiation biology, toxicology, exercise science, and fasting research.
  • Xenohormesis narrows this to the inter-species version: the stress signal originates in one species and is perceived by another — "signal eavesdropping" across the plant-animal boundary.
  • Metaphoric extension: in diet-culture and productivity writing, the concept is used loosely as a metaphor for "useful adversity" or "borrowed resilience" — eating foods from plants grown in harsh conditions, or even adopting deliberately "stressful" habits for gain. This is a diluted, non-technical offshoot of the original biological claim.
  • Contrast with related coinages: mitohormesis (adaptation driven by mitochondrial stress signaling), cytohormesis, para-hormesis, and the related longevity concepts of caloric restriction and caloric-restriction mimetics. The "French paradox" and the "resveratrol paradox" (the puzzle that moderate red-wine consumption correlates with health benefits) are the popular frames in which xenohormesis is usually discussed.
  • Debated status: in strict science, xenohormesis remains a hypothesis, not an established law. Critics note that the concentrations of resveratrol used in many experimental studies far exceed realistic dietary intake, and that the adaptive-signaling interpretation competes with simpler antioxidant or anti-inflammatory explanations for the observed benefits.
Usage notes: uncountable in technical contexts ("xenohormesis explains the resveratrol effect"); the adjective xenohormetic and the noun xenohormetin (a plant stress compound mediating the effect) are attested in the specialist literature. It is a learned, bookish term of molecular biology, virtually never used in ordinary speech; its register is that of a scientific neologism whose reach has been widened, and its edges blurred, by the modern longevity industry.
