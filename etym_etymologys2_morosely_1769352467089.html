Single root word: morosely (the adverb formed from the English adjective morose plus the adverbial suffix -ly). The whole family descends from the Latin noun mōs, "custom, manner, disposition." Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *me- (with apophonic variants *mē- and *mō-), "to measure, set bounds, evaluate, determine proper limits" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Ancient Greek metron "measure" (source of meter, geometry), Vedic Sanskrit mā "to measure" and mātrā "measure, quantity," and Latin mētior "to measure." 2. Latin mōs (genitive mōris), "custom, manner, habit, inner disposition; the settled way one does things" — attested in Classical Latin from c. 1st c. BCE (Cicero, Caesar, etc.). NOTE: the derivation of mōs from *me- "to measure" (via the sense "one's own measured, fixed way of behaving") is the traditional account but is debated; de Vaan and others treat the origin of mōs as uncertain. 3. Latin mōrōsus, "capricious, self-willed, hard to please, peevish, wayward" — an adjective formed from mōs + the suffix -ōsus ("full of"), literally "full of one's own fixed customs or humours" — attested from Plautus onward, c. 200 BCE–1st c. BCE. 4. English morose, "gloomy, sullen, ill-tempered, sourly despondent" — borrowed into English c. 1560s, per the OED directly from Latin mōrōsus (some accounts note reinforcement or mediation through French, where morose was itself a learned Latinism). NOTE: the exact route Latin-to-English, with or without a French stop, is the minor debate here. 5. English morose + the adverbial suffix -ly (from Old English -līce, "in a ... manner") —> morosely, "in a sullen, gloomy, ill-humoured manner" — attested from the 17th c. onward. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two points deserve explicit flagging. First, the step from PIE *me- "to measure" to Latin mōs "custom" is a recognised but not universally accepted semantic derivation — it rests on the idea that a person's custom is their "measured," fixed way of behaving, and some scholars prefer a separate origin for mōs. Second, the English borrowing may have been directly from Latin or routed through French; both paths give the same result. No stage is invented: where the record is uncertain, that uncertainty is stated.
