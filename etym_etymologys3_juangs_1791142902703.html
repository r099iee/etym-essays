Unlike words inherited through Latin or Sanskrit, Juangs is an ethnonym taken from living speech in the hills of what is now Odisha. The stem Juang is the community’s own autonym, recorded by British officers, missionaries, and the Linguistic Survey of India in the 19th century as Juang, Juango, Juanga. Within Munda comparative linguistics it is generally understood to mean “man, person, people” — the common pattern in which a group calls itself simply “the men” or “the people” while neighbours give a different exonym. Cognate ideas appear across Munda: Santali hor “man,” Mundari horo “man,” Kharia leburemo “people.” Whether Juang juang continues a Proto-Munda root *juʼ or a later local formation remains debated, because Juang-Kharia phonology shows irregular developments and borrowing from neighbouring Dravidian and Indo-Aryan languages complicates reconstruction.

Phonetically the Juang form is roughly [dʒuaŋ] with a palatal affricate, diphthongal vowel, and velar nasal. Odia speakers render it similarly, while English writers added variant final vowels (-o, -a) before settling on Juang. The plural Juangs with English -s is purely an English morphological overlay: neither Juang, Odia, nor Hindi marks the ethnonym this way. Colonial texts freely alternate “the Juang,” “the Juangs,” and “the Juangas” for the people, and “Juang” for the language — a fluidity still visible today.

  • Pre-literate period to c. 1800: oral autonym: hill swidden cultivators call themselves Juang in contrast to neighbouring Bhuiyans, Bathudis, and Oriya caste villagers; no written attestation.
  • 1800s–1900s: colonial fixation: Dalton’s Descriptive Ethnology of Bengal (1872), Risley’s surveys, and missionary vocabularies catalogue physical traits, leaf-dress, dormitories, and broadcast sowing, cementing “Juang” as the textbook primitive agriculturist.
  • 1900s–1940s: diffusionist use: German and British culture-historians (Hahn, Schmidt, Werth) cite “the Juangs threw the seeds” to illustrate the stage before hoe-culture and plough-culture; the sentence in the source comes from exactly this literature.
  • 1947–2000: constitutional tribe: the Constitution (Scheduled Tribes) Order lists the Juang; linguists (Pinnow, Zide, Anderson) document Juang as North Munda, splitting northern Gonasika and southern Nagada dialects.
  • 2000–present: PVTG and endangerment discourse: Odisha records some 40,000+ Juangs; UNESCO and Indian surveys flag Juang as vulnerable as Odia-medium schooling spreads, while mining in Keonjhar threatens land.

Semantically there has been little drift in denotation — Juang has always meant this community — but great drift in connotation: from colonial “savage” to diffusionist “survival” to constitutional “beneficiary” to contemporary “knowledge-holder.” The English plural Juangs itself encodes an outsider’s grammar, which is why many community advocates and linguists now prefer the uninflected Juang for both singular and plural, reserving Juangs for popular English. Etymologists explicitly warn against fanciful Sanskrit derivations; the name has no demonstrated connection to Indo-Aryan roots and must be explained inside Austroasiatic, however uncertain the deeper reconstruction remains.
