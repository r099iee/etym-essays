The verb forego embodies a captivating narrative of Germanic comparative philology, morphological prefix rivalry, and orthographic evolution. The history of the word is defined by the centuries-long convergence, confusion, and eventual partial re-differentiation of two separate Proto-Germanic verbal compounds that shared the same base verb but carried radically different prefixes.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Germanic Bases:

The base element of the compound is the universal Germanic verb of motion, Proto-Germanic *gānan (or *gēn-), meaning "to walk, step, or go." This Germanic root derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰēy- (or *ǵʰē-), signifying "to release, leave, go, or pass" (cognate with Sanskrit jíhīte "he goes, departs", Ancient Greek kikhanō "I reach, find", and Old High German gān). In Old English, this developed into the irregular verb gān (modern English go).

The prefixation, however, represents two distinct Indo-European spatial and directional particles:

  • The Prefix *for- (Privative and Ablative): Traced to Proto-Indo-European *per- (in its ablative sense of "away, through, beyond"), this yielded Proto-Germanic *fur-. In Germanic linguistics, this prefix functioned as an intensive, privative, and pejorative marker conveying destruction, loss, departure, or omission (identical to German ver- and Gothic fra-). In Old English, this gave for-, which formed verbs denoting actions turned away, ruined, or refused: forbēodan ("to forbid"), forlēosan ("to lose, abandon"; whence forlorn), forswarian ("to forswear"), and forgān. The Old English verb forgān meant literally "to pass by, go away from, leave untouched, neglect, or abstain from."
  • The Prefix *fore- (Locative and Temporal Precedence): Traced to Proto-Indo-European *pró / *péri ("in front of, forward, before"), this yielded Proto-Germanic *fura- / *furai (cognate with German vor-, Latin prae, and Greek pro). In Old English, this gave the preposition and verbal prefix fore-, signifying chronological priority or spatial advancement: foresēon ("to foresee"), foretellan ("to foretell"), and foregān. The Old English verb foregān meant literally "to walk in front of, precede, or outgo."

2. Middle English Phonological Reduction and Orthographic Merger:

In Anglo-Saxon England, forgān ("to abstain from") and foregān ("to go before") were kept strictly distinct in both pronunciation and orthography. However, following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Middle English experienced profound phonetic restructuring. Unstressed prefixes lost their distinct vowel timbres, undergoing reduction to a neutralized schwa (/ə/).

Because the initial consonants and vowels of for- and fore- sounded virtually identical to Middle English ears, scribes began treating the two prefixes as interchangeable graphic variants. In manuscripts of the thirteenth, fourteenth, and fifteenth centuries (such as the works of Geoffrey Chaucer, John Gower, and John Trevisa), forms such as forgan, forgon, foregan, and foregoon appear with both meanings without systematic differentiation. By the time printing was introduced to England by William Caxton in 1476, the spelling with an -e- (forego) had become the fashionable, prestigious standard for both concepts, largely because the silent -e- was frequently added by typesetters to justify lines of movable metal type.

3. Early Modern Literature and Shakespearean Coinage:

During the Renaissance and the Elizabethan era, forego reigned supreme. In William Shakespeare's dramatic canon, forego is used repeatedly for both "abstain/relinquish" and "precede." Most famously, in Othello (1603, Act III, Scene 3), Shakespeare utilized the past participle in its temporal sense to coin one of the most enduring idioms in the English language: when Iago fabricates Cassio's dream, Othello cries out, "O monstrous! monstrous!" to which Iago deceitfully counsels patience, observing: "Nay, this was but his dream... But this denoted a foregone conclusion."

4. Modern Prescriptivism and Contemporary Usage:

In the mid-eighteenth century, the emergence of prescriptive lexicography led scholars like Samuel Johnson to attempt a retrospective correction of the language based on historical etymology. Johnson lamented the confusion between for- and fore-, and subsequent nineteenth- and twentieth-century grammarians (most notably H.W. Fowler in his 1926 Dictionary of Modern English Usage) urged writers to resurrect forgo for "abstain" while restricting forego to "precede." While modern publishing houses often follow Fowler's rule for editorial consistency, the long literary history of forego ensures that it remains an authoritative, historically authentic variant for voluntary renunciation across modern English prose.
