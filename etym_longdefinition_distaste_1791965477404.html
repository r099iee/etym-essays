Distaste is a noun, and archaically a verb, naming a mild but decided aversion — the mental equivalent of the mouth turning down at a flavour. It is quieter than disgust and more personal than disapproval: one may have a distaste for politics, for cant, for a particular writer’s manner, without feeling sickened or morally outraged. In the source sentence, “their distaste induces doubt of reasonable judgment,” the word describes the critics’ antipathy towards Cicero, and the argument turns on the idea that such antipathy casts doubt on the critics rather than on Cicero. The distaste in question is aesthetic and temperamental — a matter of taste — and that is precisely what the word’s history explains.

1. The dominant modern sense: aversion of feeling

In present-day English distaste is the ordinary word for a restrained, often principled dislike. It implies that the object strikes one as unappealing or unworthy rather than actively vile, and it usually carries a suggestion of fastidiousness: the person of distaste has standards, and the thing in question offends them. Because the word is understated, it can be more damning than a louder synonym; a single “with distaste” can convey a whole verdict of refined contempt.

  • Core lexicographic wording: “a feeling of dislike or aversion; displeasure or repugnance aroused by something regarded as unpleasant or offensive.”
  • Typical constructions: distaste for (an activity, a person, an idea), distaste at (a remark or sight), with distaste, in distaste; “a distaste for publicity,” “handle the matter with distaste.”
  • Degree: weaker than disgust or revulsion, stronger than indifference or a mere preference against; it implies an active, felt reluctance.
  • Register: neutral-formal; at home in essays, reviews, biography, and political commentary, less common in casual speech.

2. The literal sense: a bad flavour

The oldest recorded uses of the noun are gustatory. A distaste was a disagreeable taste, either in the mouth or in a substance, and the verb meant to give something a bad flavour or to find it unpleasant on the palate. This physical sense has largely been taken over by aftertaste, unpleasant taste, or dislike, but it survives in the metaphorical background of every modern use: when we say we have a distaste for something, we are saying it leaves a bad taste.

  • Literal use: “a distaste in the mouth,” “the distaste of the medicine” — now archaic or literary.
  • Transferred use: a distaste for a food or drink is still idiomatic, though dislike is more common.
  • Survival in idiom: “to acquire a distaste for” something retains the image of the palate learning to reject.

3. The archaic noun: something distasteful

Between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries distaste could denote not the feeling but its cause: a distasteful quality, an offensive feature, or an unpleasant thing. One might speak of “a distaste in the arrangement” in the sense of an ugliness or impropriety. This objective sense has disappeared, leaving only the subjective one, a familiar pattern in the history of words for dislike: first the quality that repels, then the reaction of the repelled.

4. The verb: to dislike, and to spoil the taste of

The verb distaste is now archaic but was once common in two senses. The earlier, transitive sense was “to deprive of taste, to make distasteful” — to spoil a dish or a passage. The later, intransitive or transitive sense was simply “to dislike, to regard with distaste.” Both are recorded from the sixteenth century, and both are now confined to historical prose and poetry. The modern language prefers the noun, or the phrase “to have a distaste for.”

  • Sense 1 (obsolete): to impair the flavour of; to render unpleasant.
  • Sense 2 (archaic): to dislike, to be averse to — “he distasted the whole business.”
  • Modern replacement: dislike, take against, be averse to.

5. The semantic bridge: from palate to judgement

The word’s development rests on one of the oldest metaphors in European thought: taste as discernment. Latin sapere meant both “to taste” and “to be wise,” and English has always spoken of good taste, bad taste, and a taste for something in the sense of a cultivated preference. Distaste inherits the whole metaphor. If taste is the faculty by which the mind discriminates among experiences, then distaste is that faculty’s negative verdict — the mind rejecting what the palate would reject. This is why distaste can describe an opinion about a style, a doctrine, or a person’s manners with no reference to food at all, and why it sounds more considered than disgust, which remains anchored in the body.

6. Register, connotation, and company

  • Connotation: fastidious, restrained, slightly superior; it suggests a person whose standards are offended rather than a person who is physically revolted.
  • Common collocates: deep, evident, marked, obvious, particular, growing, mutual; verbs: feel, express, show, conceal, register, voice.
  • Contrast with disgust: disgust is a bodily reaction to the contaminating or vile; distaste is a judgement of the unsuitable or unworthy.
  • Contrast with disapproval: disapproval is moral and public; distaste is personal and often unspoken.

7. In this reading

The critics’ distaste for Cicero is presented as a fact about them, not about him: the sentence treats their aversion as evidence that their judgement is unreliable. The word is well chosen, because distaste names a reaction that claims to be a matter of taste while revealing a temperament. In the long history of Cicero’s reception, such distaste has alternated with admiration — the same oscillation that the neighbouring card, revulsion, describes at a higher pitch of feeling.
