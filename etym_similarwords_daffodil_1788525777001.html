• narcissus: Similar because daffodil is the common English name for plants belonging to the genus Narcissus; differs because narcissus is the formal botanical and Latinate term (often applied specifically to small-cupped, white, or paperwhite varieties), whereas daffodil is the warm, vernacular English name typically associated with large yellow trumpeted flowers.
  • jonquil: Similar because jonquils are a specific species within the daffodil genus (Narcissus jonquilla); differs because true jonquils have rounded, reed-like dark green leaves and clusters of highly fragrant small yellow flowers, whereas common daffodils have flat strap-like leaves and single large trumpets.
  • buttercup: Similar because both are beloved wild spring flowers displaying brilliant yellow petals in meadows; differs because the buttercup belongs to the genus Ranunculus with small, glossy five-petaled cups, lacking the bulbous growth habit, strap leaves, and central corona trumpet of the daffodil.
  • asphodel: Similar because the word daffodil is historically derived etymologically from asphodel; differs because true asphodel (genus Asphodelus) is a Mediterranean plant with tall spikes of white or pale pink starry flowers associated with ancient Greek underworld mythology, rather than the yellow trumpet of the spring daffodil.
  • tulip: Similar because both are popular spring-flowering geophytes growing from underground bulbs in the same seasonal window; differs because tulips belong to the lily family (Liliaceae) with cup-shaped or goblet-shaped multi-colored blossoms without a central projecting corona trumpet.
  • crocus: Similar because both are early spring bulbous/cormous heralds blooming in late winter meadows; differs because crocuses are much smaller, stemless members of the iris family (Iridaceae) with goblet-shaped purple, yellow, or white flowers blooming at ground level.
  • primrose: Similar because both are iconic English spring wildflowers celebrated by poets for delicate yellow blossoms; differs because the primrose (Primula vulgaris) is a low-growing perennial with five-lobed petals and wrinkled leaves, lacking the bulbous trumpet structure of the daffodil.
  • Lent lily: Similar because Lent lily is the traditional English dialect name for the wild native daffodil (Narcissus pseudonarcissus); differs because Lent lily is an archaic, regional folk designation referencing the Christian liturgical calendar.
  • sunflower: Similar because both feature prominent, vibrant yellow floral displays associated with solar warmth; differs because sunflowers are towering, late-summer composite flowers (Asteraceae) with massive disc florets and ray petals, entirely distinct from spring bulbous daffodils.
  • dandelion: Similar because both are ubiquitous bright yellow spring plants blooming across lawns and meadows; differs because the dandelion is a composite weed with jagged toothed leaves and disc-like heads that turn into feathery seed clocks, lacking the bulbous elegance and trumpet corona of the daffodil.
