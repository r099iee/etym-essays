Sterne famously builds the entire philosophical comedy of his novel upon the premise that the mental distractions, humours, and mechanical disruptions of his parents at the precise physical moment of procreation permanently marred Tristram's constitution, genius, and worldly fortune. The verb carries an evocative blend of physical procreative mechanics, moral duty, and existential consequence.

Grammatically and lexicographically, begot is the past tense form of the strong verb beget (principal parts: beget, begot or archaic begat, begotten or archaic begot). Derived from the Germanic verb get compounded with the intensive prefix be-, the verb historically moved from a general meaning of "to obtain, acquire, or seize" to an exclusive specialized meaning of procreating, siring, or generating. In modern English, while the present tense beget and past participle begotten remain recognized, the past tense form begot is primarily encountered in literary, biblical, philosophical, and historical registers.

The word displays significant polysemy and semantic range across multiple domains:

  • Biological Procreation and Siring (Primary Physical Sense): To procreate or father offspring; historically used predominantly of the male parent (sire) who generates the child, though in early modern literature (and specifically in Sterne's passage) it is expanded symmetrically to both parents in the procreative act ("when they begot me"). This sense emphasizes the physical origination and transmission of genetic, temperamental, and constitutional traits.
  • Biblical and Genealogical Formula: Historically immortalized in the genealogical chapters of the King James Bible (e.g., Genesis 5, Matthew 1), where the archaic variant begat (and in subsequent translations/commentaries, begot) establishes unbroken patrilineal descent and dynastic succession ("Abraham begat Isaac; and Isaac begat Jacob..."). This usage imbues the verb with solemn antiquity, patriarchal authority, and sacred historical continuity.
  • Theological and Christological Distinction: In Christian theology and the Nicene Creed, the concept of being begotten (Greek monogenēs, Latin genitum non factum) is rigorously contrasted with being created or manufactured: the Son is "begotten of the Father before all worlds, not made," meaning possessing the identical divine essence rather than being an external creature. The past tense and participial forms undergird core trinitarian doctrine.
  • Figurative and Causal Generation (Cause-and-Effect / Historical Sense): To produce, cause, generate, or bring into existence as a necessary consequence or byproduct. Common rhetorical and philosophical expressions include "violence begot violence," "familiarity begot contempt," "suspicion begot paranoia," and "economic despair begot political radicalism." In this register, the procreative metaphor is applied to ideas, societal movements, and psychological reactions, depicting one historical or emotional condition as the direct parent of another.
  • Literary and Dramatic Stylization: Employed by dramatists and novelists (from Shakespeare and Milton to nineteenth-century romanticists and modern historical fiction) to impart an elevated, deliberate, or ironized archaic gravity to human creation, ambition, and psychological parentage.

Stylistically, begot occupies an elevated and reflective register. In everyday conversational English, speakers substitute fathered, conceived, created, or caused. When a writer chooses begot today, it signals deliberate literary resonance, invoking the grand biblical cadences of lineage or the profound philosophical cause-and-effect explored by authors like Laurence Sterne.
