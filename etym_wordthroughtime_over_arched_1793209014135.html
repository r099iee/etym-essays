- Old English — the first half waits: ofer meaning over and above is already everywhere, while the arch has not yet entered the language.
- 13th c. — the arch arrives: Old French arche brings the curved Roman masonry home to England, and builders begin speaking of arched doors and bridges.
- 17th c. — the compound rises: overarch appears as a verb for making an arch over something, and over-arched bowers and walks fill the new landscape poetry.
- 18th–19th c. — the picturesque loves it: guidebooks praise over-arched drives and grottoes; the hyphen marks taste, leisure, and old trees.
- 20th c. — the metaphor takes the roof off: overarching detaches into committee-speak for all-embracing themes and strategies, usually unhyphenated and unironed.
- Today — two words in one skin: hyphenated over-arched still shades fantasy gullies and garden walks, while overarching spans agendas in meeting rooms.
