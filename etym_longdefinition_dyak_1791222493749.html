Dyak (variant of Dayak, also Dutch Dajak) is a collective exonym for the Austronesian Indigenous peoples of the interior of Borneo — some 200 riverine and hill groups in Malaysian Sarawak/Sabah, Indonesian Kalimantan, and Brunei. In the source sentence a Dyak with beeswax barters in a bazaar, illustrating forest-product trade before coined money. The term has no precise tribal meaning: it distinguishes non-Muslim interior longhouse dwellers from coastal Malays.

Core ethnographic content includes:

  • People sense: A member of any interior Bornean group: Iban (formerly Sea Dyak), Bidayuh (Land Dyak), Kayan, Kenyah, Ngaju, Murut, Lun Dayeh, etc. Traditionally rice growers, fishers, hunters, longhouse residents in communities of a few hundred, formerly practising intervillage warfare and headhunting, animist with elaborate omen and spirit cults, now majority Christian (Anglican, Catholic, Protestant) with many urban migrants. Population roughly 2.2 million.
  • Language sense: Any of the endemic Bornean Austronesian (Malayo-Polynesian) languages spoken by those groups — e.g. Iban, Bidayuh — as opposed to Malay/Indonesian. Example: Dyak dialects differ river by river.
  • Cultural / historical sense: Longhouse architecture (apartments for up to 100 families), boat mobility, brass, beads and jars as wealth, beeswax-rattan trade for salt and guns. Colonial literature fixed the image of fierce, mostly naked warriors in brilliant war-coats; modern writing stresses adat law, forest stewardship, and political mobilization.
  • Adjectival use: Dyak custom, Dyak.longhouse, Dyak headhunting — pertaining to that world.

Further nuances: in Indonesian Borneo Dayak applies to any non-Muslim interior native; in Malaysian Borneo it often narrows to Iban + Bidayuh. Capital D marks the ethnonym; lower-case is not used. Older English plural is Dyaks or collective Dyak; modern standard prefers Dayaks/Dayak. The form Dayuh occurs as a variant.

Polysemy is thus taxonomic, not metaphorical: people → their languages → their customs. The bridge from direction (upriver) to ethnicity is social othering by coastal observers, then bureaucratic convenience, then self-reclamation. Unlike canoe or caravan, Dyak never became an object word; it remained human. Its near-synonyms are river-specific names (Iban, Kayan) which differ by denoting real endogamous groups with distinct dialects, whereas Dyak denotes an outsider's umbrella.

Register caution: 19th-century dictionaries glossed savage, reflecting colonial contempt; current usage is neutral to proud, especially in Dayak congresses and cultural festivals. Using Dyak today is intelligible but dated; Dayak is preferred.

Economically the classic Dyak bazaar encounter — forest wax, rattan, and hornbill products exchanged for salt, iron, cloth, and beads at negotiated barter rates — became a textbook illustration of pre-monetary trade, exactly as quoted in the source with knives, stockings, blankets, guns, horses, and elk-teeth serving as units of account before coinage.
