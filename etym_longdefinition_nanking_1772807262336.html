Nanking — the older English romanization of the Chinese city 南京 (Nánjīng), 'Southern Capital'; the historical spelling long used in English sinology, trade records, treaties, and classic literary translations. In the source text, a letter has just arrived 'from Nanking' and finds Wang Xi-feng closeted with Lady Wang, attended by two women bearing a message from Lady Wang's elder brother and sister-in-law: the name is not a map label but the signature of the southern ancestral sphere of powerful families.

Literary and social role. In Dream of the Red Chamber Nanking is the ancestral power base of major houses, including the Xue line of the famous 'Nanking Four', and the backdrop to fortunes and scandals that travel north along kinship lines. A letter from Nanking reorders household priorities in the capital; the name tones the narrative with distance, old money, and the pull of the south against the Jia household's northern capital world. It is geographic, political, and social at once — 'home base' for a migratory elite, and a proper noun dense with pedigree in English.

Polysemy — senses in English:
  • The city (proper noun): the great city on the lower Yangtze, historically a capital of numerous Chinese regimes and modern capital of Jiangsu province; in English the spelling 'Nanking' was standard until pinyin took over.
  • Romanization variant: 'Nanking' is the Postal Map romanization (and its predecessors), contrasted with standard modern pinyin 'Nanjing'; the two spell the same place. Older English also used 'Nan-king', 'Nankin', and French 'Nankin'.
  • Attributive political/social use: 'Nanking families', 'Nanking Xue', 'the Nanking Four' — the city as a marker of clan alliance and regional power in the novel and in period writing.
  • Trade-product sense: 'Nankin' / 'nankeen' designates a durable yellowish or buff cotton cloth associated with the city's manufacturing and export fame, and by extension other goods styled 'Nanking ...' (e.g. 'Nanking silk'). The place-name became a trade label for the product.
  • Diplomatic-historical sense: 'the Treaty of Nanking' (1842) closed the First Opium War and is a standard historical reference point in English; the name here is fixed by the treaty's own text and subsequent historiography.
  • Modern usage: contemporary English overwhelmingly uses 'Nanjing'; 'Nanking' survives in older translations, historical prose, treaty references, and period fiction, carrying a deliberately archival flavour.
Compared with related designations. Nanjing is the standard modern pinyin; Peking/Beijing is the contrasting 'Northern Capital'; Jiangnan names the broader 'south of the Yangtze' cultural region rather than the city; 'the southern capital' is a transparent gloss of the Chinese morphemes that loses the proper-name force 'Nanking' carries as a seat of families and correspondence.

Register and connotation. In modern English 'Nanking' is literary-historical, slightly archaic in tone, and valued by translators for internal consistency with other period spellings (Peking). In the novel it functions as social magnetism: the very arrival of a letter from Nanking is enough to set the household's most powerful women conferring. The name is thus a key of the narrative's southern axis — old money, kinship duty, and the pull of ancestral estates against the capital world.
