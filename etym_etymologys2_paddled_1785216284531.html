The etymology of PADDLED is a chain of shape-transfers that runs through the kitchen before it reaches the boat, and its deepest stage is uncertain. Greek πατάνη (patánē) "shallow dish, plate" (attested in classical antiquity; of uncertain origin — Beekes suspects a Pre-Greek substrate word, so no secure Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed) → Latin patina "broad shallow pan, cooking dish" (the standard Roman kitchen vessel, 1st c. BCE onward) → Medieval Latin padella / patella "a small pan" (diminutive of patina; the voiced intervocalic -t- > -d- is the regular Romance sound change) → by shape-transfer: padella "a small spade" (because a small digging spade has a broad, flat, pan-shaped blade; Late Medieval Latin) → Middle English padell "a small spade" (15th c., borrowed from the Medieval Latin/Romance tool-word) → English paddle, noun, "a short, broad-bladed oar" (15th c.; the flat, pan-shaped blade of the spade is lengthened and adapted to stroke water) → English paddle, verb, "to propel with a paddle" (17th c., a regular weak verb formed from the noun) → English paddled (past tense and past participle, formed with the ordinary -ed suffix; attested in compounds such as paddle-wheel 1688, paddle-boat 1787, paddle-steamer 1843, dog-paddle 1850s). NOTE ON DEBATE / NUANCE: the OED records the etymology of "paddle" as uncertain. The chain above, through Greek patánē → Latin patina → Medieval Latin padella, is the most commonly given line, but an alternative borrowing path has been proposed: the word may instead descend from Old French pale or Latin palla "spade, shovel" (a word of obscure origin), and some scholars therefore keep both lines open. The Greek stage itself is doubtful — Beekes argues that patánē belongs to the Pre-Greek substrate, so no Proto-Indo-European ancestor should be forced for this word. The verb sense "to propel a boat" (17th c.) is clearly secondary to the noun "the oar" (15th c.), and the "to wade, dip the feet" sense (17th c.) and the chiefly US "to beat with a flat board" sense (19th c.) are later offshoots of the same image of a short, flat, stroking blade.
