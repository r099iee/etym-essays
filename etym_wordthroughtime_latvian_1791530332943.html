- 13th–16th centuries — Latin and German chronicles write of Lettones and Lettigalli; “Lettish” names tribes and peasants rather than a nation, and the land is called Livonia.
- 16th–18th centuries — Under Polish, Swedish and Russian rule the word remains a regional and social label; German is the language of towns and nobility, Latvian that of the countryside.
- 1817–1860s — Serfdom is abolished and the Young Latvians begin a national revival; “Latvian” starts to mean a modern nation with a literary language.
- 1870s–1918 — Latvija and “Latvian” circulate as national names; the word acquires a progressive, anti-German and increasingly anti-imperial charge.
- 1918–1940 — The name belongs to a sovereign republic; Riga flourishes as a cosmopolitan capital, and “Latvian” becomes an official nationality and citizenship.
- 1940–1991 — Under Soviet rule the official frame is the Latvian SSR; “Latvian” survives in émigré circles as a badge of non-recognition and cultural survival.
- 1989–2004 — The Singing Revolution and the Baltic Way restore the national name; EU and NATO accession make “Latvian” a modern European identity.
- 2000s–present — The word is routine in EU institutions and diaspora networking, while tourism branding leans on Riga’s Art Nouveau, amber and the song festival.
