thee (pronoun, second-person singular objective; accusative and dative) — The objective case form of the archaic, dialectal, and liturgical second-person singular personal pronoun thou, corresponding in modern standard English to "you" (when referring to one person as the direct object, indirect object, or object of a preposition). In Oscar Wilde’s fairy tale The Selfish Giant, the Giant gazes upon the wounds of the Christ Child and exclaims, "Who hath dared to wound thee?", employing the pronoun in its elevated, intimate, and emotionally charged literary register.

Primary grammatical function — direct and indirect object. In traditional Early Modern English grammar, the second-person pronominal paradigm preserved a strict morphological division between grammatical cases and numbers. Thou served exclusively as the nominative singular subject ("thou seest"), while thee functioned exclusively as the oblique/objective singular object ("I see thee", "I give thee my word", "come unto thee"). This mirrored the first-person distinction between I (subject) and me (object). When functioning as the indirect object (the historical dative), thee expresses the beneficiary or recipient of an action without requiring a preposition ("give thee strength").

Historical sociolinguistic function — the T/V distinction. From the Middle English period through the seventeenth century, English operated a nuanced system of social address modelled on the Romance T/V distinction (such as French tu versus vous). In this dynamic, thou/thee served as the intimate, informal, or downward-directed pronoun (used toward children, social inferiors, servants, close intimates, or romantic partners), whereas ye/you served as the respectful, polite, and formal address toward social superiors and strangers. To address a superior as thee was considered a serious insolence or deliberate provocation, whereas addressing an intimate companion or spouse as thee signified profound tenderness and emotional closeness.

Polysemy and specialized domain usages:

  • Religious and liturgical register — Paradoxically, while thee fell out of ordinary secular conversation, it became permanently enshrined in the solemn liturgy of the Western Church through the Coverdale Psalter, the Book of Common Prayer (1549/1662), and the King James Authorized Version of the Bible (1611). In hymns, prayers, and devotional addresses to God ("Nearer, My God, to Thee", "I praise Thee, O Lord"), thee conveys reverence, awe, and an unmediated, deeply personal spiritual relationship between the believer and the divine.
  • Quaker (Society of Friends) plain speech — In the seventeenth century, George Fox and the early Religious Society of Friends adopted thou and thee as a radical testament of universal Christian equality, refusing to bow, remove hats, or use the honorific plural you to social superiors. Over time, colloquial Quaker usage underwent a syntactic merger wherein thee came to be used as both the subject and object pronoun ("How does thee do?", "Thee knows best"), creating a distinctive communal sociolect that survived into the twentieth century.
  • Regional and dialectal English — In rural dialects of Northern England (particularly Yorkshire, Lancashire, Cumbria, and parts of the West Midlands) and West Country dialects, thee (frequently pronounced /ðiː/ or reduced to /ði/) persisted in everyday spoken speech among working-class communities well into modern times to express local solidarity, familial warmth, and unpretentious directness.
  • Poetic and romantic archaism — In eighteenth-, nineteenth-, and twentieth-century romantic poetry (such as the works of Shelley, Keats, Wordsworth, Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s "How do I love thee? Let me count the ways", and Oscar Wilde), thee functions as an elevated poetic device to establish emotional intimacy, lyrical intensity, and timeless aesthetic gravity.
  • Reflexive and ethical dative — In archaic idiom, thee was often used reflexively ("get thee gone", "fare thee well", "stand thee still") or as an ethical dative indicating personal interest or emotional involvement in an action.

Syntactic position and phonological behaviour. Thee typically appears following transitive verbs or prepositions ("beside thee", "for thee", "with thee"). In connected speech and regional dialects, it undergoes unstressed cliticization and vowel reduction, blending with verbal auxiliaries ("I tell thi"). Today, standard English employs the single generalized pronoun you across all numbers and cases, leaving thee as an evocative historical marker of intimacy, sanctity, and poetic solemnity.
