disengage - single chain: PIE *wadh- "to pledge, to bind" (an ancient juridical verb of giving one's word; earliest reconstructable stage) to Proto-Germanic *wadjō "pledge, security, earnest money" (1st millennium BCE) to Frankish *wadja "pledge, security, pawn" (5th-8th c. CE) to Old French gage "pledge, security, pawn" (9th-11th c.) to Old French engagier "to bind by a pledge, to pawn" (en- "in" + gage; 11th-12th c.) to English engage "to bind by promise, to commit, to join battle" (16th c., via Middle English and Anglo-French) to English disengage "to release from an engagement, to detach" (dis- "apart" + engage; 17th c.; the rootWord). Note: the same PIE root produced Latin vās, vādis "surety, bail" and Old English wedd "pledge" (source of English wed, "to marry", the pledge-word par excellence), confirming the root's ancient juridical character. Sanskrit vadh- "to strike, slay" is a homophone of this root, not a cognate of the pledge-word, and is therefore omitted. overall - two independent chains, one for each element: Chain A - over: PIE *uper "over, above, beyond" (earliest reconstructable stage) to Proto-Germanic *uber "over, above" (1st millennium BCE) to Old English ofer "over, above, beyond" (7th-11th c.) to English over (11th c. onward) Chain B - all: PIE *al- "beyond" (the traditional source of the Germanic "all" words; the derivation is debated, some authorities preferring *h2el- "to grow, nourish") to Proto-Germanic *allaz "all, whole, entire" (1st millennium BCE) to Old English eall "all, every, whole" (7th-11th c.) to English all (11th c. onward) Compound: over + all to English overall (17th c. adverb/adjective "taken as a whole"; 1759 noun "overalls", protective trousers; the singular "an overall", a one-piece garment, 20th c.; the rootWord). descended - single chain: PIE *skend- "to jump, leap, spring" (the base of Latin scandere; earliest reconstructable stage) to Latin scandere "to climb" (the leap re-read as an upward spring - the exact formation of scandere from the root is debated; classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) to Latin descendere "to climb down" (de- "down" + scandere; classical Latin, 1st c. BCE) to Latin descensus "a going down" (past participle used as noun; classical Latin) to Old French descendre "to go down" (9th-12th c.) to Middle English descenden "to go down, to descend" (13th c.) to English descend (14th c.) to English descended "moved or hung downward" (past tense / past participle; the rootWord). Note: the same root *skend-/*skand- produced Sanskrit skándati "he leaps, jumps" and Greek skándalon "stumbling-block, snare" (source of English scandal) - the leap and the trap that makes one leap - confirming the root's leap-oriented core; Latin scandere developed the climb-sense from the notion of springing upward.
