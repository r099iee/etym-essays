Insinuating continues Latin insinuare, to push into the sinus, the curved fold of the toga over the breast that served as pocket, bosom, and embrace. From that intimate fold comes a whole family of winding, creeping meanings that English has preserved for 500 years.

Latin sinus meant curve, bay, hollow, then the hanging fold of the toga, then by metonymy bosom, lap, embrace, shelter, and secret place. Sinuare meant to bend or wind. Prefixed in- gives insinuare: to bend or push something in, to work one's way into a winding passage, to get into someone's bosom or confidence. Cicero writes se insinuare in familiaritatem, to worm oneself into intimacy; Caesar uses it for troops filing into a gap; Pliny for water seeping into earth. The core image is never blunt entry but curving, gradual, almost imperceptible penetration.

  • Physical winding: rivers, paths, snakes insinuate - they wind in. This is the oldest layer, still felt in insinuating roots and streams.
  • Social creeping: to insinuate oneself into favour, office, or affection by artful, gradual steps. Tacitus delights in freedmen insinuating into emperors' confidence.
  • Verbal hinting: Late Latin and medieval rhetoric extend the creep to words: to insinuate an idea is to introduce it obliquely, without stating it, letting it seep into the hearer's mind.

French insinuer (14th c.) carries all three into the vernacular, especially the rhetorical one: exordium treatises advise orators to insinuer their case when the audience is hostile. English borrows insinuate in the 1520s in all senses: Skelton has winds insinuating, More has heretics insinuating errors, courtiers insinuating into favour. The present participle insinuating is adjectival from the start: an insinuating fellow is one who creeps into liking; an insinuating remark is one that hints without asserting.

Phonology is conservative: Latin /insinuˈaːre/ becomes French /ɛ̃sinɥe/ becomes English /ɪnˈsɪnjueɪt/. Stress on sinu. Spelling stable. The -ing form does double duty as progressive (he is insinuating that I lied) and adjective (an insinuating voice).

  • 1500s-1600s: dominant sense to creep or worm in physically and socially. Shakespeare's Iago is the archetype without always using the word.
  • 1700s: hinting sense overtakes: to insinuate a doubt, scandal, or accusation means to imply something discreditable indirectly. Chesterfield warns against insinuating manners as low cunning.
  • 1800s-present: two adjectival poles stabilize: (a) ingratiatingly worming, winning confidence artfully; (b) suggestively hinting something bad. An insinuating bacilli in the prompt fuses both: germs creeping into bodies and hinting at corruption.

Culturally, insinuating has always been morally ambivalent. Winding persistence can be praised as tact, diplomacy, or gentle persuasion, but English usually hears stealth and malice. To insinuate is the opposite of to declare: it evades responsibility while planting suspicion. Hence defamation law watches insinuation closely; a newspaper that insinuates fraud without stating it can still libel. The prompt exploits this slipperiness: bacilli do not attack openly like lions but insinuate - seep, creep, imply decay - until man is devoured from within.
