The story of commendation begins, literally, with the human hand. Its deepest root is the Proto-Indo-European noun *man- "hand," one of the most productive roots in the whole family: it produced Latin manus "hand" and, through it, a vast modern wardrobe — manual, manuscript (written by hand), manage (to lead by the hand), manicure (care of the hand), manufacture. In Rome the hand was the legal emblem of authority and possession: a father held his household in manu, and to place a person or thing into someone's hand was to transfer real power. The second element is the Indo-European "give" root *deh₃-, Latin dare "to give" (our data, date, tradition, edit).

Fusing hand and give produced the Latin verb mandare — "to put into someone's hand," hence "to entrust, commit, order." From mandare Rome built the great administrative vocabulary that English still uses: mandate, command (com- + mandare), demand, remand, commend. The next step was the prefix. Latin writers attached the intensive com- ("thoroughly") to make commendare: to hand something over completely, to entrust a person or thing wholly to another's protection or care. In the Roman world of patronage and recommendation, commendare was a social verb of enormous weight — a patron commended a client to a friend, a letter of recommendation introduced a stranger and staked the writer's honour on him. To commend someone was thus to place them in good hands.

Here the word performed its great semantic pirouette. Entrusting a person to another's care demanded vouching for them — speaking well of their character, honesty, and worth. The verb commendare therefore slid from "hand over to care" to "recommend, praise," and the action noun commendatio came to mean both the entrustment and, increasingly, the spoken or written praise that accompanied it. This shift was complete within classical Latin: Cicero commends and commends-people-as-carefully as any modern manager. Early Christianity gave the word a further career — the epistolae commendatriciae, letters of recommendation for travelling clergy, and the "commendation of the soul" in the burial rite, by which the dying were entrusted to God.

French and English carried the word forward. Old French commendacion reached Middle English in the 14th century as commendacion, meaning entrustment and praise; the "entrust" sense slowly yielded to the "praise" sense as English usage settled. By Austen's time the word was firmly the official approval that socially "authorizes" — exactly the force it has in Pride and Prejudice (1813), where the sisters' commendation of Jane as "a sweet girl" gives Bingley permission to think of her as he chooses. In modern English, a commendation is typically an official citation — a military medal, a letter from an employer, a public award — the direct descendant of that Roman gesture of placing someone in good hands.

  • Sound evolution: PIE *man- → Latin manus → mandare → commendare → commendatio → Old French commendacion → Middle English commendacion → English commendation [kɒmənˈdeɪʃən]; note the French loss of final -t in commendatio and the modern -tion suffix.
  • Semantic drift: "to give into the hand" → "to entrust to care" → "to recommend by vouching" → "to praise" → "an official award of praise."
  • Socio-cultural factors: Roman patronage made recommendation a social necessity; the church institutionalized commendatory letters; modern bureaucracies and militaries converted praise into recorded citations.
  • Literary attestations: Cicero's letters (commendatio); the Vulgate and burial-liturgy commendations; Chaucer's uses; Austen's Pride and Prejudice ("authorized by such commendation"); the modern "letter of commendation."
  • Modern situation: dominant sense is official praise or award; the entrusting sense survives in commend ("commend to your care") and in religious "commendation of the soul."

Few modern words preserve a whole social institution in their syllables: commendation still means, at bottom, "handing someone into good hands" — a Roman gesture of trust that three thousand years of usage turned into a medal.
