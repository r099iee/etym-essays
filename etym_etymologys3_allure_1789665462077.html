The evolution of ALLURE is a textbook case of a technical falconry term rising to general aesthetic vocabulary. Its deepest securely reconstructible base is Proto-Germanic *lothran, to call, to invite, reflected in Middle High German luoder and Middle Low German loder, lure or bait, modern German Luder (bait, carcass-bait, then scoundrel by pejoration), German laden, to invite or summon, and Old English lathian, to call or invite. From this verb the Germans of Gaul supplied Frankish *lothr or *lōthr, a call-device or decoy, the object swung to call back a hawk. Romance speakers borrowed it as Old French loirre, later leurre (modern French leurre), meaning the falconer's lure: a bundle of leather, feathers and meat trailed to reclaim the bird. Etymonline, the OED and the Anglo-Norman Dictionary converge here; the FEW places it under *lōthr.

Prefixed with à, to or toward, this gave Anglo-Norman aleurer, alurer (attested 1235–55 in the sense to lure hawks: les gentils faucouns aluirer), Old French aleurer, to attract or captivate, and parallel forms alerrer, alorir and enleurer in continental dialects. The OED etymology stresses this: allure is a borrowing from French, from Anglo-Norman aleurer from a- plus leurer. The falconry sense is primary and dated; the transferred sense to entice people by advantage or flattery follows within the same century. Middle English aluren, alure appears around 1400 meaning to tempt by offering something desired, with past participle allured and agent nouns following. Caxton and Malory use it of both hawks and honours; by Shakespeare's time (Venus and Adonis, Sonnets) it is fully naturalised for love and ambition.

  • 13th c. — hawking technology: aleurer is what a falconer does with leure: patient swinging, feeding, recall. Sense is skilled and affectionate, not sinister; the lure saves the hawk's life.
  • c. 1400–1600 — moralisation: preachers and poets transfer the device to the devil, the court and the lover: the world allures. The noun lure (early 14th c.) and verb allure run in parallel; allure keeps a slightly loftier register.
  • 17th–18th c. — aestheticisation: Dryden, Pope and the novelists use allure and alluring for beauty, music and landscape. Samuel Johnson defines to allure as to entice. The sinister edge dulls; allure becomes charm.
  • 19th–20th c. — commercialisation and psychology: advertising discovers allure; cosmetics brand Allure (founded 1991 as magazine) caps the process. Simultaneously naturalists reclaim the Hawking sense innocently: the allure of the wild is the honourable lure.

Phonetically the path is smooth: Frankish o lengthened in Old French oi, reduced to eu; English stress settles on the second syllable /əˈljʊə/ in RP, spelled all- by assimilation of ad- to al- before l. The double-l is etymologically the prefix plus stem-initial l merged in writing. Morphologically allure yields allurement, alluring, allured without umlaut, typical of French loans. Semantically the drift is tool-to-act-to-quality: object swung, act of enticing, property of being enticing. Falconry's prestige — the sport of kings — helped the rise; what kings did with hawks, lovers and advertisers could do with glances and slogans.

Debate concerns only the deepest root. Wiktionary tentatively links PGmc *lōthr- to PIE *leh₂-, to hide, via the notion of concealing bait, but marks it as perhaps and probably related to *lathōn, invitation. Etymonline is more cautious, stopping at Frankish and PGmc *lothran, to call. The OED stops at French. Following zero-guesswork, the chain tops at PGmc with PIE noted as tentative. There is also a distinct French allure meaning gait or way of walking (from aller, to go), which Etymonline notes may have influenced the spelling of our word but did not supply its sense. The falcon in Baker's Essex thus flies through the etymology: the bird once recalled by allure now embodies it, and the field naturalist willingly plays the hawk, returning daily to be caught by place.
