Unmethodical means not done or acting according to a method; lacking an orderly, systematic procedure; proceeding without a fixed plan, sequence, or principle of organization. It describes work, thought, behaviour, or arrangement that is irregular, unsystematic, or haphazard rather than step-by-step.

The core sense is procedural: a method is a repeatable way of going on — steps in order, criteria made explicit, results checkable. To call an inquiry, a textbook, a set of terms, or a person unmethodical is to say that this repeatability is missing. Traditional terms for prose (sincere, lucid, terse, turgid) are unmethodical in that they were not generated by one consistent theory with defined criteria; they accumulated from reviewers, teachers, and common talk, overlap one another, and cannot be applied by rule. Yet, as the source sentence insists, they may still work well enough for beginners, because unsystematic does not mean useless.

  • Procedural sense (central): lacking a fixed plan or order. An unmethodical search looks everywhere twice and somewhere never; an unmethodical essay jumps between points without signposts; unmethodical revision corrects commas before structure. The emphasis is on sequence and completeness rather than intelligence: clever people can work unmethodically, and dull people methodically.
  • Characterological sense: describing a person habitually averse to system. An unmethodical colleague loses files, misses steps, improvises brilliantly and forgets to write down how. Here the word softens moral judgment: it suggests temperament rather than vice, unlike negligent or sloppy, which accuse.
  • Stylistic and taxonomic sense: describing vocabularies, classifications, or descriptions that lack consistent criteria. Critics call impressionistic labels unmethodical because they mix metaphors (lean, urbane, hard-hitting), levels (sound, sense, moral tone), and degrees. The source passage uses the word exactly in this sense, conceding the charge before defending the terms pedagogically.
  • Weaker occasional sense: merely informal, casual, or spontaneous. An unmethodical ramble through a library or an unmethodical conversation is not defective; it is prized for serendipity. Context decides whether the absence of method is fault or freedom.

Register and connotation deserve care. Unmethodical is formal and a shade old-fashioned; everyday speech prefers unsystematic, disorganized, haphazard, or random. Its Latinate stem plus native un- gives it a bookish, slightly rueful tone — the word of examiners, reviewers, and preface-writers apologizing for or forgiving disorder. It is milder than chaotic (which implies confusion and breakdown), milder than slipshod (which implies carelessness), and less technical than non-systematic. The concessive construction in which it so often appears — unmethodical though they are, unmethodical as it was — signals that the speaker has noticed the fault, weighed it, and decided to proceed anyway. In prose analysis that is an important move: it lets a teacher keep vivid traditional labels while promising something more rigorous later, exactly the textbook strategy of the source passage.

Do not confuse unmethodical with immethodical (essentially non-existent), unmethodological (not concerning methodology as a theory), or unscientific (a stronger charge of violating scientific norms). Something can be methodical without being scientific (a filing system) and scientific without being fully methodical in presentation (a brilliant but rambling lecture). Unmethodical names the middle failure — absence of orderly steps — and leaves open whether deeper insight compensates, which in the humanities it very often does.
