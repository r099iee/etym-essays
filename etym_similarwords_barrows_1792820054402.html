• Wheelbarrows: Similar because a wheelbarrow is exactly what vehicle-barrow usually means; differs because wheelbarrow names the one-wheeled pushed form explicitly, while barrow alone can also mean the older two-man hand-barrow.
  • Handcarts: Similar because the source sentence pairs them as fellow hand-pushed load-carriers blocking the road; differs because a handcart is larger, usually two-wheeled and pulled, while a barrow is smaller, pushed, and tipped to empty.
  • Carts: Similar because both are small human- or animal-drawn load vehicles; differs because cart is the broad family word (often horse-drawn and two-wheeled), while barrow is strictly the hand-pushed garden and market member.
  • Trolleys: Similar because both are small hand-pushed carriers for loads; differs because a trolley typically has four wheels or castors and is pushed on flat floors, while a barrow has one wheel and is built for rough ground and tipping.
  • Stretcher-barrows: Similar because the hand-barrow is literally a stretcher for loads; differs because stretcher now suggests carrying people (the injured), while barrow carries soil, goods, and market stock.
  • Trugs: Similar because gardeners fill both trugs and barrows with harvest and clippings; differs because a trug is the shallow wooden basket carried in the hands, while a barrow is the wheeled vehicle the trug is emptied into.
  • Skips: Similar because both receive garden and building rubbish; differs because a skip is the giant static steel bin collected by lorry, while a barrow is the small mobile feeder that fills it trip by trip.
  • Tumuli: Similar because ancient burial barrows are also called tumuli; differs because tumulus names only the mound-twin (Latinate, archaeological), never the garden cart, while barrow confusingly names both.
  • Wagons: Similar because both haul loads on wheels; differs because a wagon is large, often horse-drawn or railed, for bulk freight, while a barrow is the smallest hand-scale carrier of all.
