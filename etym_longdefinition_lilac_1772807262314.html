In the Dream of the Red Chamber line about Xi-feng sending 'lilac-coloured hangings' for Dai-yu's bedding, lilac functions first as a refined colour term—pale violet, soft blue-purple—rather than as the shrub itself. The choice is atmospheric: lilac is delicate, cool, and slightly melancholy, a tint associated with elegance and understated luxury rather than the aggressive splendour of scarlet or gold. For Dai-yu, whose temperament is poetic, fragile, and grief-tinged, lilac hangings help stage an interior of subtle beauty; they belong to the same sensory world as brocade, satin, and carefully graded dyes. The word thus contributes both to material culture (Qing elite textiles) and to character colouring: the room is dressed in a hue that matches her refinement. At its core, lilac names (1) a genus of ornamental shrubs (Syringa, especially Syringa vulgaris, the common lilac) with fragrant clusters of small flowers, and (2) the pale violet or light purple colour of that blossom. The two senses are inseparably linked: the colour word is derived from the flower, and the flower's bloom cycle—brief, perfumed, and early-summer—gives the colour its distinctive emotional associations of freshness, transience, and delicate melancholy.
  • Plant sense: the lilac is a hardy shrub of the olive family (Oleaceae), native to southeastern Europe and eastern Asia, famed for dense pyramidal panicles of tiny four-petalled flowers in shades of pale purple, violet, white, or pink, and for a heady, sweet fragrance. In horticulture: common lilac (Syringa vulgaris), Persian lilac, Japanese tree lilac, lilac bush, lilac blossom.
  • Colour sense: pale violet or light purple, 'the colour of lilac blossom'—a soft, greyed or slightly pinkish purple, lighter and more delicate than violet and deeper than lavender. In design, fashion, and textiles it is a standard pastel: lilac-coloured, lilac-tinted, pale lilac, soft lilac, dusty lilac.
  • Victorian and literary associations: in nineteenth-century England lilac was a fashionable colour of spring, sentiment, and mourning-adjacent delicacy; in poetry it became shorthand for fragile beauty and passing youth, as in Whitman's 'When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd', where the lilac mourns Lincoln. The colour sense carries these overtones of tender grief.
  • Metonymy and compounds: lilac is used adjectivally in colour compounds (lilac-coloured, lilac-grey, lilac-pink), in botanical names (Persian lilac for Melia azedarach in some regions), and loosely for similar pale-purple flowers; the word also names perfumes and dyes associated with the flower.
  • Register: standard to literary; as a plant name it is concrete and botanical; as a colour it is decorative and fashion-adjacent. In textile and interior description it is precise and genteel—exactly the register of the novel's gift of bedding.
Colour near-synonyms mark the boundaries. Violet is the broader spectral name and can be deeper or more jewel-like; lilac is specifically the pale, greyed or pinkish purple of lilac blossom. Lavender is softer still, often greyer or more powdery, and strongly scented in association. Mauve (from mallow) is a nineteenth-century aniline fashion colour, often pinker or duskier. Purple historically ranged from imperial Tyrian dye to ecclesiastical solemnity and can feel heavier than lilac. Lilac sits in the pale-violet niche: gentle, ornamental, and slightly sentimental in modern English taste.
