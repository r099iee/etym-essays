The biography of chagrin is one of the great curiosities of etymology, because it begins not in a root language but in the saddle of a horse. The word has no Indo-European pedigree at all. Its deepest origin is the Turkic noun *sağrı, which named the rump or croup of a horse and, by extension, the tough skin of that region. In the horse-centered cultures of Central Asia the sağrı was a valued material: the hide from the hindquarters, when dressed untanned, produced a stiff leather covered in small, hard, granular nodules - rough enough to serve as an abrasive for polishing wood and metal, the sandpaper of the pre-industrial world. This rough leather travelled west along the trade routes that carried silk, horses, and hides from the steppe to the Mediterranean. By the 16th century the material word had entered the Romance world - Italian zigrino and, through it or a parallel path, Middle French chagrin - where it named this pebbled, granular leather, now also manufactured from the skin of sharks and rays. In French the word was a purely material term, the leather of bookbindings, knife handles, and scabbards, prized precisely because it was rough enough to grip and to polish. The same material sense was borrowed into English in the 17th century, where it survives today under the spelling shagreen - a decorative, pebbled leather often dyed green. The extraordinary leap from leather to feeling happened inside French. Alongside the material word, French had a word chagrin (of uncertain native origin) meaning "melancholy, sorrow, vexation". At some point in the 16th-17th centuries the two merged in the popular mind, and the merger was powered by a perfect metaphor: grief and disappointment are felt as a chafing, an abrasive grinding against the heart, exactly as rough leather grinds against a surface. The material word's roughness became the emotional word's sting. When English borrowed chagrin in 1656, it took the whole merged bundle - the emotion now wearing the leather's image of chafing. Some scholars hesitate over this account, suggesting the grief-word may be an independent native development, but the folk association between the abrasive and the emotion is at least as old as the merged usage, and it has shaped the word's meaning ever since. Early modern English used the word broadly. Samuel Johnson's dictionary and the novelists of the 18th century used chagrin for melancholy, ill humour, and gnawing disquiet - a heavier, more diffuse distress than today's. The narrowing to "embarrassment at one's own failure" came gradually through the 19th and 20th centuries. As the word tightened, it also lightened: modern chagrin is frequently used with a wry, half-ironic affection ("much to his chagrin", "to her chagrin"), describing the smart of wounded pride rather than full-blown grief. The archaic material sense split off permanently as shagreen, so that English today has two words - one for rough leather, one for ruffled pride - where French and Turkish each had essentially one. The context sentence captures the modern word perfectly: the man feels "shame and chagrin at his public failure". The shame is the moral weight of the failure; the chagrin is the sharp, chafing sting of pride exposed - the discomfort of having to "face his superiors" while still smarting. Behind that sting, if the etymology is right, lies the ghost of a horse's rump and a piece of rough leather, a reminder that even our subtlest emotions can be named after the most concrete of objects.
