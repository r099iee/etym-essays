brine. The biography of brine begins in uncertainty — an honest starting point, because the word is one of those Germanic formations whose deeper origin scholarship cannot fix. Old English brīne, "salt water," appears in the earliest English texts and glosses, and Old Norse brími, a poetic word for "the sea" or "brine," shows that the Germanic world possessed an old, dignified word for salt water that never made it into the other Indo-European branches — no Greek, Latin, Sanskrit, or Celtic cognate stands beside it. Faced with that isolation, etymologists have proposed the traditional hypothesis that brīne belongs to the Proto-Indo-European root *bhreu-, "to boil, to bubble, to seethe," the image being of salt water "brewing" and effervescing, churning and bubbling against the shore. The root's other reflexes are secure: Latin fervere, "to boil," gives English fervent, fervid, and effervesce; Germanic *brewwaną gives brew and *bruþą gives broth; and in India, Sanskrit bhurvan, "restless, agitated," may preserve the churning sense, though the equation is debated. But the OED remains cautious: the connection, though traditional and plausible, cannot be proved, and brine is best described as a word of uncertain, possibly expressive origin. What is certain is the meaning history, and it is rich. In Old English and throughout the medieval period, brīne denoted the salt water of the sea and the pickling bath — the sharp saline liquor used to preserve meat and fish by making it uninhabitable to decay. The word has always carried that sharpness: the sting of salt, the bitterness of water that cannot sustain life. In medieval and early modern English it was the everyday pickling bath and the bitter wash of the shore, and its Old Norse relative was even a poet's word for the sea itself, dignified and epic. The word's career since has been a steady splitting into technical and poetic registers. In cookery and industry, brine is the saturated solution that preserves food and yields salt and chemicals; in refrigeration and drilling, brine is the dense saline fluid of engineering; in geology and oceanography, brine is the residual water of dying seas and salt lakes, and the deep, dense brine pools that settle at the bottom of ocean basins where nothing lives. Each of these uses keeps the root condition: water so salted that life fails in it. The literary transfer completes the journey. Because brine is what an ocean becomes when it dies — reduced, concentrated, sterile — the word carries a desiccated, elegiac tone in writing: the sea's bitter residue, the dregs of a departed flood. Peake exploits exactly this when he writes that the sky is "dark as the brine of desert seas" that have "ebbed to desert pools": the heavens take on the colour of a dead ocean-bed, lethally saline, ancient, abandoned — the sharpness of the word, twenty or more centuries deep, still stinging in the simile. The mnemonic given in the card — Hindi namkīn (नमकीन), "salty" — points to the same sensory core in the Indian culinary world, where the saltiness of the word and the taste meet; a salt that preserves, a water that kills, and a darkness that is the colour of a sea that has died.
ebbed. The biography of ebbed is the story of a preposition that became a tide. Its ultimate ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *apo-, "away, off, from," one of the most widespread prepositions in the entire family. Its branches wandered far and productively. In Indo-Iranian it produced Sanskrit ápa, "away," which appears in compounds such as apakrama, "departure," and survives in Hindi words like aparādh, "crime" — literally "acting against" — the prefix hardened into a moral particle. In Greek it produced apó, "from, away," the source of a whole vocabulary of English: apology, literally a speech "away from" the charge; apogee, the moon "far from" the earth; apostle, one "sent away" on a mission; apocalypse, the "uncovering." In Latin it produced ab, "away from," the ancestor of abduct, abstract, absent, and even aborigine, "from the beginning." The root's core is pure direction: motion out and away from a point. The Germanic branch did something peculiar and beautiful with it. There the abstract "away" was specialized to the sea: the Proto-Germanic formation *abjōn- named the receding water, the tide that moves away from the land, and *abjāną was the verb "to flow back, to ebb." Old English inherited these as ebba, "low tide," and ebbian, "to flow back," and from the earliest English texts ebb and flood name the two halves of the tidal cycle — the going out and the coming in, the withdrawal and the return. The related forms in the continental Germanic languages — German Ebbe, Dutch eb, both via Low German — show that the tidal word belonged to the whole Germanic world, which is natural for a sea-faring people for whom the rhythm of the tide governed harbours, shipping, and fishing. The figurative extension came early and proved permanent. By the later medieval period, to ebb was already describing not only water but the withdrawal of anything that had been full: the ebbing of strength, of life, of hope, of fortune. The metaphor never lost its tidal rhythm — ebbing is slow, inexorable, regular, and above all hopeless, a going out that will not be turned. In the phrase "the ebb and flow," the two tidal halves became the standard figure for the rise and fall of events, business, and luck — gain and loss as the sea's own pulse, the rhythm of fortune itself. In the passage, the participle works at full geological and elegiac weight: "desert seas that have ebbed to desert pools" — whole seas, once vast, gone out over geological time and never returned, leaving only stagnant remnants and their bitter brine. The ancient preposition of "away," after four thousand years, is still saying what it always said: the water is moving away, and it will not come back. The mnemonic — Hindi utar gayā (उतर गया), "has gone down" — catches the same downward withdrawal, the tide gone out and the land left dry.
