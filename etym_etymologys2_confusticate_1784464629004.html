CONFUSTICATE is a nineteenth-century English humorous coinage: it has no PIE, Latin, or other ancient ancestor. The honest linear record therefore documents (a) the two source words the coinage blends, and (b) the coinage itself. Chain 1 — the confuse element: PIE *ǵʰew- 'to pour, libate' (reconstructed) → Latin fundere 'to pour' (root fūd-, attested Classical Latin) → Latin confundere 'to pour together, mix up, bewilder' (con- 'together' + fundere; 1st c. BCE) → Latin past participle confūsus 'poured together, mingled' → Old French confus 'mixed up, bewildered' → Middle English confusen (14th c.) → English confuse 'to mix up mentally, bewilder' (16th c.) → blend element of the coinage. Chain 2 — the confound element: PIE *ǵʰew- 'to pour' → Latin fundere → Latin confundere 'to pour together, confound' → Old French confondre 'to ruin, destroy, confuse' → Anglo-Norman confoundre → Middle English confounden 'to confuse, discomfit' (13th–14th c.) → English confound 'to bewilder, baffle' and, as a mild oath, 'to damn slightly' ('confound it!', 17th c.) → blend element of the coinage. Chain 3 — the coinage itself: English confuse + confound, blended with the mock-Latinate verbal suffix -icate (imitating complicate, fabricate, vindicate) → English confusticate 'to confuse, confound, put into a muddle', humorous slang coinage (mid-19th c., recorded c. 1840s–1850s) → derived participial adjective confusticated → CONFUSTICATE (base/imperative form in the oath “Confusticate and bebother these dwarves!”, Tolkien, The Hobbit, 1937). Note on the debate: no attested Latin or Medieval Latin verb *confusticare exists — the -icate ending is borrowed as playful morphology, not inherited. The PIE root *ǵʰew- is widely reconstructed (Pokorny, LIV) but its precise shape and cognate set are debated; it is the background of confuse/confound, not a parent of the coinage.
