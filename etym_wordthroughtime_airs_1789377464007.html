- 14th Century (Late Middle English, c. 1300–1380) - Borrowed from Old French *air* (atmosphere, breeze, weather, also outward appearance/manner), from Latin *āēr*, from Ancient Greek *ā́ēr*; initially denoted the invisible elemental gas of the atmosphere and meteorological breezes.
- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan & Renaissance Era) - Absorbed the musical sense *air* / *ayre* via French *air* and Italian *aria*, designating a solo melodic tune, vocal song, or lyrical movement with lute accompaniment (John Dowland).
- Early to Mid-17th Century (Jacobean & French Court Influence) - Semantic transfer from personal mien/bearing (*an air of nobility*) into behavioral affectation; influenced by French *se donner des airs* ('to give oneself airs'), the plural *airs* began denoting pretentious manners and inflated self-importance.
- 18th to 19th Century (Augustan & Victorian Eras) - Deeply popularized in satirical comedy and novels of manners (Sheridan, Austen, Thackeray, Wilde); *putting on airs* and *airs and graces* became standard drawing-room idioms mocking pretentious parvenus; musical *airs* flourished in opera and suites.
- 20th to 21st Century - Continues universally in English idioms criticizing arrogance (*don't put on airs with me*); preserved in historical musicology (*Air on the G String*); extended into broadcasting and electronic media (*on the air*).
