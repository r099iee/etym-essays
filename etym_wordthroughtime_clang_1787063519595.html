- Late 16th Century - Emerges in Early Modern English as a vivid onomatopoeic borrowing (partly via Latin clangor and Dutch klang), denoting the piercing blare of brass trumpets, the harsh cries of large migrating waterbirds (like cranes), and the striking of metal weapons in battle.
- 17th to 18th Century - Broadens into neoclassical poetry and literature to depict majestic auditory shocks: the ringing of heavy church bells, the resonant slamming of bronze gates, and the catastrophic clash of shields during heroic epic combat.
- 19th Century - Undergoes a major industrial semantic shift with the advent of the Industrial Revolution, becoming the signature auditory descriptor for steam engines, railway switchyards, boiler factories, and hydraulic steel forges.
- Early 20th Century - Enters psychiatric nomenclature through Kraepelin and Bleuler as clang association (or clanging), designating a severe thought disorder where speech is governed by rhyming and phonetic puns rather than logic.
- Modern Usage - Retains its evocative acoustic force across mechanical, environmental, and fantasy literature to convey resonant, jarring, and unyielding metallic impact.
