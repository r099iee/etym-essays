A door-nail is a large-headed nail or stud driven through a wooden door for strength, ornament, or as a knocker plate, hammered dead or clinched flat on the inside so it cannot work loose, and in modern use almost exclusively the benchmark in the simile as dead as a door-nail, meaning utterly, unmistakably lifeless. The source sentence is Dickens musing on that very cliché at the opening of A Christmas Carol.

The hardware sense is medieval. Plank and ledge doors were built of vertical boards held by horizontal ledges, and long hand-forged nails were driven through board and ledge then clinched over a rove. The broad round head outside formed a decorative row and spread load, while the flattened point inside locked the joint. Such nails also served as anvils for knockers: visitors rapped iron on the nail head. Inn and church doors bristling with studs displayed wealth and deterred battering.

  • Hardware sense: clench nail, stud, boss for doors and gates. Still listed in restoration joinery alongside rosehead and cut-clasp nails. Example: the wicket was hung with hand-forged door-nails.
  • Idiomatic sense dead as a door-nail: completely dead, broken beyond repair, or fast asleep. Applied to Marley, to batteries, to phones, to debates. The humour lies in treating deadness as gradable by hardware.
  • Intensifier sense: dead as an absolute intensifier, as in dead certain, dead silent. Door-nail supplies concrete image to an abstract absolute.
  • Literary and seasonal sense: shorthand for Dickensian gloom and comic pedantry. To quote door-nail is to summon Marley, Scrooge, fog, and Christmas haunting.
  • Knocker-plate sense: the striking plate under a knocker, sometimes called a door-nail plate, receiving the blow. Antique dealers retain this narrow use.

Grammatically door-nail is a count compound noun: one door-nail, several door-nails. Spelling varies among doornail, door-nail, and door nail, with the hyphenated form in the source preserving both elements and matching dictionary headwords for the idiom. Attributive use appears in door-nail simile and door-nail deadness.

Domain usages include architectural conservation, where surveyors specify wrought door-nails for listed buildings; literary criticism, where the simile exemplifies dead metaphor revived by Dickens; and everyday slang, where dead as a door-nail describes discharged devices and exhausted people. The tone is wryly emphatic rather than mournful: speakers choose door-nail over deceased precisely to sound blunt and proverbial.

Polysemy is narrow because hardware gave rise to idiom and idiom swallowed hardware. The bridge is clinching dead: a nail hammered dead cannot be reused, just as a corpse cannot be revived. Alliteration of dead and door reinforced memory, and Dickens canonised the phrase for every subsequent Christmas. No other nail carries this charge; window-nail or floor-nail remain purely technical, proving that cultural quotation, not form alone, made door-nail proverbial.
