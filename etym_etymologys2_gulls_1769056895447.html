Chain for "gull" (seabird) — the direct ancestry of gulls': 1. Proto-Celtic *weliannā (reconstructed; before c. 1000 BCE) — "seagull". The Celtic bird-name; no secure Indo-European etymology can be reconstructed for it. It may be a native Celtic formation of unknown origin, possibly sound-symbolic of the bird's cry, but this is unproven — state this uncertainty rather than inventing an ancestor. 2. Old Welsh guilann / Old Cornish guilian (attested early medieval period, c. 800–1100 CE) — "seagull". The recorded Celtic forms: Welsh gwylan, Cornish guylan. 3. Welsh gwylan / Cornish guylan (medieval to modern; attested from the early medieval period) — "gull, seagull". These are the direct source of the English word. Welsh gwylan, Cornish guylan, Breton gouelan all continue *weliannā. 4. Middle English gulle (late 15th century, first attested c. 1460–1500) — "a gull, a seabird". Borrowed into English from the Celtic forms above, almost certainly via the Cornish-speaking south-west of England, where the birds are abundant. Earlier English sometimes used "mew" for the same birds. 5. English gull (16th century onward; modern form) — "any seabird of the family Laridae, grey-and-white, with a raucous cry". The standard natural-historical name. 6. English plural gulls — the plural of gull. 7. English plural possessive gulls' — "belonging to gulls". The plural genitive, formed by adding only an apostrophe after the final -s (from the old genitive singular -es reduced to -'s; for plural -s nouns only the apostrophe remains). THIS IS OUR WORD. NOTE ON THE HOMONYM: The English verb "to gull" ("to trick, cheat, dupe") and the noun "a gull" (a dupe) are a DIFFERENT word. OED treats this verb as of uncertain origin — possibly from an old sense of "gull" meaning an unfledged or newly-hatched bird (something easily caught), but this connection is itself uncertain and is rejected by some authorities. The adjective "gullible" derives from this verb, NOT from the seabird. Do not conflate the two chains. DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: The ultimate origin of the Celtic bird-name is unknown. Some scholars have suggested an onomatopoeic origin echoing the gull's cry, and a few have tried an Indo-European root meaning "to howl", but neither is securely established. The English borrowing point (Celtic → English, c. 15th c.) is likewise inferred rather than directly attested in a single document: the word simply appears in late-15th-century English with no native Germanic ancestor, and the south-west Cornish connection is the accepted explanation.
