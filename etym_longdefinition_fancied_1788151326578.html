Fancied (/ˈfænsɪd/) is the past-tense and past-participle of the verb fancy, and a fully independent adjective. Its two great senses mirror the history of "fancy" itself: (1) imagined - formed in the mind, imaginary, supposed, idealized; and (2) liked, desired - held in fancy, wanted, or (British) found attractive. In the source context - "while upon the fancied scene I gazed" (Wordsworth, The Prelude) - the word is the first sense: the scene exists in, and is transfigured by, the speaker's imagination.

Sense 1 - imagined, imaginary, supposed.

  • Core: "a fancied slight", "a fancied grievance", "fancied dangers" - things believed or imagined to exist that have no objective reality, or that the mind has exaggerated.
  • Literary: "the fancied scene", "fancied worlds" - an idealized or poetically heightened picture, pleasant precisely because it is the mind's own creation. In The Prelude, Wordsworth sets the fancied (imagined) scene against the real one, and the word is central to Romantic theories of imagination, in which fancy is the image-making faculty.
  • Distinct from "fanciful": fancied is passive (the object of imagining), fanciful active (the mind that invents in whimsy).

Sense 2 - liked, desired, favoured (British).

  • The verb "to fancy" = "to like, want, be attracted to": "I rather fancy that hat", "she fancies him". Hence fancied = wanted or desired: "a fancied transfer target", "a coveted and fancied role".
  • Romantic register: a "fancied colleague" is the object of one's attraction. The bridge is old: in Shakespeare, "fancy" means love or amorous inclination, so to fancy someone is to hold them inside the heart's imagination.

Sense 3 - sporting and betting: heavily expected to win. "The fancied favourite", "a strongly fancied runner" - a horse, team, or candidate that the public and the experts believe will succeed, the one people "fancy", that is, back and wish for. The bridge runs through the 19th-century racecourse: to fancy a horse is to imagine it winning, and the word migrated from the imagination to the betting slip.

Semantic bridge uniting the senses. "Fancy" is the aphetic shortening of "fantasy", from Greek phantasia, "appearance, imagination", itself from phainein, "to make visible". To imagine something and to want something were, for centuries, the same activity: desire was a "fancy" - a picture the mind paints of its wished-for object. That is why "take a fancy to", "fancy that!", "fancted slights", and "fantasy" all descend from one Greek root meaning "to show".

Grammar and usage. As a verb, regular: fancy, fancied, fancied ("He fancied he heard footsteps"). As an adjective, attributive and predicative: "a fancied theory" (imagined), "the most fancied entry" (favoured). In British English the verb sense predominates; in American English "fancy" survives chiefly as an adjective ("fancy dress") and "fancied" is rarer and means mainly "imagined". Typical collocations: fancied slights, fancied grievances, a fancied favourite, highly fancied, little fancied, he fancies himself a poet.
