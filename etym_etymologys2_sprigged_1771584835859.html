[Root core] PIE *spergh- "to move with haste, to spring, to burst forth" (c. 4000 BC; Pokorny *sperǵʰ-) — the probable, though not certain, ultimate source of English sprig via the native Germanic idea of a twig that "springs" from a branch; the connection is flagged as uncertain by the OED [Stage 2] Proto-Germanic *springan- "to leap up, to spring forth, to burst" (c. 500 BC–AD 200) [Stage 3] Old English springan "to leap, to burst forth, to sprout" (8th–9th c.) — the verb from which the noun's idea of a "sprouting twig" derives [Stage 4] English sprig (late 14th c.) "a small shoot, twig, or spray" — origin obscure; possibly from a Scandinavian source (Norwegian sprik "a forked twig") and reshaped under the influence of spring, or a native formation on the model of spring [Stage 5] English to sprig "to decorate with sprigs; to plant or fix as a sprig" (16th–17th c.) [Stage 6] English sprigged (17th c.) "decorated with small sprig-shaped motifs" — past participle of to sprig, used adjectivally of fabrics and ceramics — this card's word Note on the debate: The OED marks the origin of the noun sprig as obscure. Two rival accounts are current: (a) a Scandinavian loan (Norwegian sprik "forked twig," cf. dialectal spreg) reshaped by analogy with spring; (b) a purely native formation on the model of spring, a sprig being "that which springs" from a branch. Both point to the same Germanic idea, and neither is certain. The PIE *spergh- link is therefore indirect and is presented here as the probable, not guaranteed, ultimate source.
