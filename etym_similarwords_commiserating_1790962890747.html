• sympathizing: Similar because both involve feeling and expressing sorrow for another's distress; differs because sympathizing is a broader, more common term that can describe ideological or intellectual agreement (e.g., sympathizing with a political cause), whereas commiserating strictly denotes shared sorrow, distress, or mutual misfortune.
  • condoling: Similar because both involve expressing sorrow for another's grief; differs because condoling is far more formal and restricted to solemn rituals of mourning and bereavement (offering formal condolences to a grieving family).
  • empathizing: Similar because both involve emotional attunement; differs because empathizing focuses on internally simulating and feeling another's precise cognitive and emotional state, whereas commiserating emphasizes the communicative act of sharing and lamenting misery together.
  • pitying: Similar because both involve feeling sorrow for another's suffering; differs because pitying can easily carry an asymmetric, patronizing, or condescending undertone from a position of superiority.
  • consoling: Similar because both are supportive responses to distress; differs because consoling actively aims to alleviate, cheer up, or comfort the sufferer, whereas commiserating focuses on joining them in their sorrow.
  • comforting: Similar because both provide emotional support; differs because comforting actively provides warmth, physical soothing, and reassurance to ease emotional or physical pain.
  • lamenting: Similar because both involve sorrowful expression; differs because lamenting is an outward, vocal bewailing or passionate mourning of a tragedy or loss, often done individually.
  • bemoaning: Similar because both express discontent or sorrow; differs because bemoaning often carries a negative, complaining, or self-pitying nuance regarding unfavorable circumstances.
  • compassionating: Similar because both denote active compassion; differs because compassionating is an archaic, rarely used verb with a strongly theological or formal flavor.
  • grieving: Similar because both involve deep sadness; differs because grieving is the profound internal psychological process of enduring deep loss, rather than sharing someone else's trouble.
