Centum (English /ˈkɛntəm/; Latin [ˈkɛn.tũː]) is the Latin numeral meaning "one hundred", and in modern English a technical adjective and noun in Indo-European linguistics. It names the western group of Indo-European languages in which the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European palatovelars merged with the plain velars, producing a plain /k/ rather than a sibilant. The word is a learned borrowing from Latin, first used in this technical sense in the 1890s.

1. The Latin numeral. Latin centum is indeclinable and means "a hundred"; its symbol is C. It is one of the most stable items of the Latin lexicon and survives in the Romance languages (Italian cento, French cent, Spanish ciento, Portuguese cento, Catalan cent). In Roman culture the number structured the army (a centuria of roughly a hundred men, commanded by a centurio), land surveying (centuriatio), the calendar and law. English has inherited a large family of derivatives through Latin and French: century, centurion, cent, percent, centennial, centipede, centigrade, centilitre, centime and many more.

2. The linguistic label. In Indo-European studies, centum is used adjectivally ("a centum language") and as a noun ("the centum languages"). The label comes from the fact that the numeral "hundred" is a convenient diagnostic: Latin centum begins with /k/, while Avestan satəm begins with /s/. The centum languages include Greek, Italic, Celtic, Germanic, Tocharian and probably Anatolian; the satem languages include Indo-Iranian and Balto-Slavic. In a centum language, PIE *ḱ, *ǵ and *ǵʰ merged with *k, *g and *gʰ; in a satem language they became sibilants or affricates.

  • Hellenic: Greek hekaton "hundred", with a velar reflex of the initial palatovelar.
  • Italic: Latin centum, the type specimen, with initial /k/.
  • Celtic: Old Irish cét and Welsh cant, both showing a velar outcome.
  • Germanic: Gothic hund and English hundred, where Grimm's law later turns the velar into /h/.
  • Tocharian: Tocharian B kante "hundred", a centum form in the eastern part of the family.
  • The satem contrast: Sanskrit śatám and Avestan satəm show sibilants where the centum languages show velars.

3. The centum–satem division. The division was once treated as the oldest split in the Indo-European family. It is now understood as an isogloss, a bundle of shared phonological developments rather than a genealogical branch. The discovery of Hittite and Tocharian in the early twentieth century was decisive: Tocharian is geographically eastern but phonologically centum, and Hittite shows a centum-like treatment of the dorsals. Albanian preserves a three-way distinction and is neither straightforwardly centum nor satem. The terms remain useful as descriptive labels, but they do not define a family tree.

4. History of the concept. August Schleicher's 1871 Compendium recognized a single series of velars. Karl Brugmann's 1886 Grundriss distinguished palatal and velar series and grouped languages by labialization. In 1890 Peter von Bradke defined the division in the terms that stuck, using centum and satem as shibboleths; Brugmann adopted the terminology in the 1897 edition of the Grundriss. The English adjective centum is attested from the 1890s, at first in translations and summaries of German comparative philology.

5. Secondary and technical senses. The word has a second, unrelated life in South Asian English. A centum can mean a set of one hundred verses in the satakam tradition, and, by extension, a perfect score of 100 in a school or board examination. This sense is a calque of Sanskrit śataka ("a hundred, a century of verses") and is pronounced /ˈsɛntəm/. It is a regional usage, not part of general British or American English. The two senses are homographs, not semantic relatives: one is Latin "hundred", the other is a South Asian English development of the same numerical idea.

6. Semantic bridge. The path from numeral to linguistic label is a classic case of a word being recruited as a diagnostic. Because the numeral "hundred" happened to preserve the crucial consonant, philologists used the Latin and Avestan forms as names for the two groups. The label then took on a life of its own, generating centumization, centumize and the phrase centum language. The numeral's everyday meaning survives in the derivatives, but the simplex centum is now almost exclusively technical or historical in English.

7. Usage and register. The linguistic term is usually printed in roman in English prose, sometimes italicized as a Latin word; the branch name is capitalized in headings ("the Centum group"). The Latin numeral is italicized in classical contexts. The South Asian sense is informal and regional. The pronunciation /ˈsɛntəm/ for the linguistic term is often proscribed in favour of /ˈkɛntəm/, because the Latin word had /k/; the spelling-pronunciation with /s/ is nevertheless widespread.

8. Cultural and historical resonance. The Latin numeral carries a long cultural afterlife. The Roman centuria gave English century, which now means both a hundred years and a Roman military unit; centurion names the officer; percent and percentage carry the idea of "per hundred"; centipede and centaur (from Greek, not Latin) evoke the number in different ways. In modern philology, centum is a reminder that a single ordinary numeral can become a taxonomic key, and that the history of a word can turn on one consonant. The term is also a useful case study in how nineteenth-century classifications were later reinterpreted: what began as a family tree became a map of overlapping sound changes.
