larder: (1) a room or large cupboard in a house for storing food, especially meat, dairy, and other provisions that need to be kept cool; (2) by extension, any store of provisions, or figuratively an abundant supply of anything. Sense in this literary context. In the passage "The shrimps were taken off to the larder, but Rover was given straight away to the little boy he had been bought for," the word names the household food store, a cool domestic room where the freshly caught shrimps will be kept before eating. The sentence is doing a small piece of moral staging: the shrimps, ordinary edible commodities, are sent to the cold practicality of the larder to await their culinary fate, while Rover the dog — a living creature and a companion — is given immediately to his new boy. The larder is thus the emblem of ordinary domestic supply, the place of provisions and daily housekeeping, set in deliberate contrast to the nursery and the heart. The word's tone is warm and old-fashioned, evoking a traditional kitchen economy of carefully managed stores. Primary sense — a room or cupboard for storing food. At its core, a larder is a cool, dry, well-ventilated room or large cupboard in a house used for storing food before it is cooked and eaten. Historically it stored meats, fish, dairy, game, and other perishable provisions, relying on coolness, shade, and air-flow rather than refrigeration. In modern usage, especially in British English, it can mean a large food cupboard or pantry, and the phrase "larder fridge" refers to a fridge designed for storing food at a slightly higher temperature than a standard fridge. Historical sense — the meat store of a great house. In the medieval and early modern household, the larder was one of a suite of specialized domestic offices. It was specifically the storeroom for meat — and its very name preserves the memory that the most prized preserved provision was cured pork fat, lard. The larder was typically a cool, north-facing masonry room where butchered meat, game, fish, and dairy were kept, and the office was run by a dedicated servant (the "larder" or "larderer"). It was a sign of a prosperous, well-run household. Figurative and extended senses.
  • "A well-stocked larder" — a plentiful store of food; by extension, abundance and readiness.
  • Figuratively, any ample reserve: "a larder of ideas," "a larder of talent" — a storehouse of resources.
  • In restaurant and catering jargon, "the larder" can mean the cold-room section or the area where raw meats and produce are prepped.
  • In British domestic usage, "larder" increasingly means the same as "pantry" — a general food cupboard, even one that is not cold.
Full polysemy map (all senses):
  • A cool room or large cupboard for storing food, especially meat, fish, and dairy — primary sense, especially British.
  • The meat storeroom of a medieval or early modern great house — historical sense.
  • A general food store or pantry — modern, often used interchangeably with "pantry".
  • "Larder fridge" — a modern appliance designed to store fresh food slightly warmer than a standard fridge — technical modern usage.
  • Figurative: an abundant supply or store of anything ("a well-stocked larder of anecdotes").
  • In professional kitchens: the cold-storage and prep area for raw provisions — trade jargon.
The semantic bridge. The word comes from Old French lardier, "a room for keeping meat," which itself is built on larde, "bacon, cured pork fat," from Latin lardum, "cured pork fat." The chain of meaning is deliciously concrete: the room took its name from its most valuable contents. In a world before refrigeration, cured fat was among the most precious preserved provisions — edible, long-lasting, and full of energy — so the room that held the meat was named for the lard that symbolized its wealth. As domestic life changed, the room widened from "meat store" to "food store," and then from "cold room" to "cupboard," while the Latin ancestor lardum itself gave English the word lard. Register, collocation, and confusables.
  • "The larder was well-stocked" — British domestic, neutral-to-warm; slightly old-fashioned and cozy.
  • "Go to the larder" — everyday British usage, now often meaning a food cupboard.
  • "Larder" vs. "pantry": historically a larder was for meat and cold provisions (named for lard), a pantry for bread, dry goods, and linens (from Latin panis "bread"); modern usage merges them.
  • Confusables: lard (the fat), larder (the store), buttery (a storeroom for casks of drink), scullery (the washing-up room).
In the test sentence larder is used in its domestic primary sense: the shrimps are taken to the cool household food store, and the ordinary practicality of that destination throws the warmth of Rover's fate into relief.
