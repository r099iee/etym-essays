- 16th–17th c. - "wall-eye" names a pale, whitish or bluish-opaque eye, first recorded of horses; a veterinary term read as a blemish, a clouded "white eye" that can barely see
- 18th–19th c. - extended to people: the eye turned visibly outward, or fixed askance (what doctors would call exotropia); blunt physical portraiture, faintly comic or coarse in tone
- 19th–20th c. - "to look walleyed" widens into a dazed, glassy, or skewed stare; the word slides from medical label toward a comic register of confused, wandering gaze
- Contemporary - a quiet literary adjective of oddness: a gaze that will not settle, "slightly walleyed," inviting distrust of the eye while belief rests in the intention — paired with "earnestly" to sketch a man hard to read but sincerely watching
