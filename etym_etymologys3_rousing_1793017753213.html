The history of rousing opens in the mews rather than the bedroom: its parent verb rouse is first recorded as a term of falconry, describing the moment a hawk shakes out and settles its feathers — ruffling the plumage, half-mantling, preparing for flight. Late-medieval hawking manuals are thick with such specialist verbs, most of which never left the hunt, but rouse escaped because the image it named — a creature shivering itself from rest into readiness — proved too useful to remain the property of falconers.

From feathers to feet: through the sixteenth century the verb widened step by step, and each step is datable in the record:

  • Hawking (late 15th c.): the hawk rouses its feathers — the narrow technical sense, shaking and ruffling.
  • Hunting (early 16th c.): game is roused from cover — the stag started from its lair, birds flushed from thickets; the shaking creature becomes the startled one.
  • Waking (mid-16th c.): sleepers are roused from bed — Shakespeare's generation uses it freely for reveille, alarm, and morning calls, and the falconry origin is already forgotten by most speakers.
  • Bestirring (late 16th c.): people are roused to action, spirits roused to courage — and the present participle hardens into the adjective rousing, describing whatever performs this waking: rousing strains of music, a rousing summons.

Shakespeare and the rhetoric of waking: the Elizabethan stage gave the verb its great showcase. Drums and trumpets rouse armies; ghosts rouse vengeance; conscience is roused from slumber in sermon after sermon. The moralised sense — to rouse is to recall the negligent to duty — spread through Puritan preaching, where congregations were roused from spiritual sleep as soldiers from blankets, and the participle-adjective came to praise any eloquence that performed the same office on an audience.

Sound change and form: phonologically the word is unremarkable, which is itself evidence of its native naturalisation: Middle English rousen with its French-looking spelling settled into the modern pronunciation with the same vowel shift that carried house, mouse, and louse from medieval to modern vowels. The spelling with ou never wavered, and the -ing participle has been spelled rousing since its first adjectival appearances.

The nineteenth century — oratory, anthems, and applause: mass politics, revival religion, and the modern concert hall made rousing one of the age's favourite adjectives. Election reports praised rousing speeches; hymnody prized rousing tunes that filled chapels; music critics reached for rousing whenever finales, choruses, and marches lifted audiences cheering. The colloquial intensifier followed: a rousing success, a rousing cheer, a rousing dinner — anything done with full-blooded spirit earned the falcon's old word.

Modern stasis: twentieth-century usage changed the word little, which is why it still sounds fresh rather than dated. Sportswriters, reviewers, and speechwriters keep it in constant service, and its etymological mystery — no certain parent language, no agreed PIE root — has never hindered its vigour. From a hawk shaking its feathers to a crowd shaking a stadium roof, rousing has described one continuous gesture for five hundred years: the shiver by which rest becomes readiness.
