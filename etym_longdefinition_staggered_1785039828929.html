**STAGGERED — unsteady, reeling, and off-balance** At its core, *staggered* is the past participle of *stagger*: it means to have walked, moved, or lurched unsteadily, reeling and tottering off-balance as if about to collapse at any step. The word always pictures a body that has begun to lose command of itself — muscular control eroded by weight, heat, exhaustion, age, drink, injury, or terror — so that forward motion becomes a series of wobbling, swaying recoveries. In *The Hobbit*, chapter 4 ("Over Hill and Under Hill"), the word attaches to poor Bombur during the company's desperate flight through the caverns of the Misty Mountains: he is "fat," and he **staggered** along "with the sweat dripping down his nose in his heat and terror." The nuance is pitiable and comic rather than heroic: Bombur does not simply walk or hurry; he reels onward as an overburdened body barely held upright, each step threatened by a fall, and Tolkien's phrasing makes the reader feel his roundness, his drenching sweat, and the fear that weighs on him as heavily as his own flesh. **The near-synonym field: how each unsteady verb is bounded** - To *totter* emphasizes a shaky, teetering stance of one about to topple, usually from weakness or age; it stresses the wobble in place rather than progress. - To *stumble* points to a specific catching of the foot that interrupts progress — a near-trip — not a continuous sway. - To *lurch* describes sudden, violent, jerky pitching, often of a ship or a heavy body heaving from side to side. - To *reel* suggests spinning or staggering as from a blow, dizziness, or strong emotion — a giddier, more swirling motion. - *Stagger* uniquely combines a rhythmic, side-to-side sway with forward (or aimless) progress and the constant imminence of a fall; it is the only one of the set that also works transitively and figuratively — to stagger a person is to shock or bewilder them so thoroughly that their mental footing gives way. **Exhaustive polysemy: every sense the word wears today** - **Sense 1 — literal physical unsteadiness (the card's governing sense).** To move unsteadily as if about to fall, from weakness, exhaustion, injury, drink, or terror: "he staggered under the heavy load," "Bombur staggered along." - **Sense 2 — transitive and figurative: to shock, bewilder, or overwhelm.** From the 1550s, to stagger a person is to make their mental footing give way: "the news staggered him." As an adjective, *staggering* means astonishing, overwhelming — "staggering wealth," "staggering numbers." The semantic bridge is perfect metaphor: physical loss of balance transferred to mental loss of composure. - **Sense 3 — to cause to waver in purpose or belief.** To stagger someone's faith or confidence is to make them doubt or hesitate: "nothing could stagger their resolve." This is a narrowing of Sense 2 toward doubt rather than shock. - **Sense 4 — technical: to arrange in offset or alternating positions.** In industry, planning, and design, to stagger something (or have it staggered) is to offset rows, payments, shifts, or timetables so that they do not coincide: "staggered working hours," "staggered payments," "brickwork with staggered joints." The bridge is the image of rows stepping alternately like a drunken walk, each unit out of line with its neighbour. - **Sense 5 — *the staggers*: a veterinary disease.** From the 1570s, "the staggers" names a disease (especially of horses) that makes the animal reel; the bridge is again the unsteady walk. The related plant-name *staggerwort* preserves the same association. - **Sense 6 — military and aviation jargon.** In aeronautics, *stagger* names the fore-and-aft offset between the wings of a biplane; in formation flying and marching, staggered ranks offset soldiers so each can see forward. Both are Sense 4's precise technical descendants. - **Sense 7 — noun senses.** The noun *stagger* (c. 1600) means an unsteady walk or a state of shock; the plural *stagger(s)* also names the bewilderment or the disease. **How the senses hang together** Every modern sense of *staggered* radiates from one physical image: a body or line that has lost its alignment with the vertical or the straight. The figurative senses (shock, bewilderment, wavering faith) transfer the loss of physical footing to the loss of mental or moral footing; the technical senses (staggered rows, payments, wings, shifts) transfer the same image of "out of line, alternating, unsteady" to the orderly world of engineering and scheduling, where staggering deliberately produces a rhythm of offsets. The word thus runs from the pathetic, sweating figure of Bombur to the neat alternation of a factory shift, always keeping its root image of a thing that cannot hold the straight and level.
