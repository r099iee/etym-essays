Expulsion is the noun of action from the verb 'to expel': the act of forcing someone or something out of a place, a group, a body, or an institution. It is a word of hard edges and strong verbs — driving, thrusting, ejecting — and it always implies a boundary being crossed by force from within, or an external power acting decisively to remove what is unwanted. Unlike mere removal or departure, expulsion is deliberate, often final, and usually carries the weight of judgment, whether medical, legal, political, or social. In the reading context supplied with this card — 'The sudden and violent ______ of the evil humour left the patient weak but finally on the path to recovery' — the word is used in its oldest and most physical sense: the forcible driving of corrupt matter out of the body, the medical drama of the purge. That context is not peripheral; it is the etymological and physiological heart of the word, from which all its institutional senses descend. The severity of the word is structural. Expulsion is never accidental: it is the deliberate exertion of force designed to eject that which is deemed toxic, rebellious, or incompatible. It marks a rupture — the student thrown out, the diplomat sent home, the demon cast out, the humour driven off — and it leaves a permanent change in the relation between the expelled and the expeller. This is what separates it from gentler words: dismissal can be routine, eviction can be administrative, but expulsion is the language of final judgment. The full polysemy of the word can be set out exhaustively:
  • Sense 1 — physical and medical, 'the forcing of matter out of the body': the expulsion of humours, phlegm, breath, blood, or waste from the bodily cavities; the sense in which the card's sentence uses it, and the oldest continuous sense of the English word.
  • Sense 2 — institutional and disciplinary: removal from a school, college, club, party, or professional body as a punishment — expulsion from the university, expulsion from parliament, expulsion from the party. Here the boundary is membership, and the act is a public verdict.
  • Sense 3 — political and diplomatic: the forcible removal of a person from a territory or the severing of a country's presence — the expulsion of diplomats, the expulsion of foreigners, the mass expulsion of populations. This sense carries the full weight of state power.
  • Sense 4 — religious and theological: the casting out from a sacred community or place — the expulsion from Eden, the expulsion of demons, expulsion from the church; the word's moral register is at its most absolute here.
  • Sense 5 — mechanical and technical: the discharge or driving out of matter by a machine — the expulsion of exhaust gases from an engine, the expulsion of a spent cartridge from a firearm, the expulsion of material in industrial processes.
  • Sense 6 — legal and administrative, 'eviction / ouster': the removal of a person from property, office, or legal possession, as in the ejection of a tenant or the ouster of an official — the institutional cousin of banishment.
The semantic bridge between the physical and the institutional is direct. Latin expellere meant literally 'to drive out' — ex- 'out' + pellere 'to drive, push' — and Roman writers used it of driving cattle, driving out enemies, and expelling noxious substances from the body alike. The word crossed into English twice over, through Old French expulsion in the 14th century, and it has kept both registers side by side ever since: the physician expels the evil humour, the state expels the traitor, and the machine expels its waste. What unites the senses is the image of force applied at a boundary — the body's wall, the city's gate, the institution's rolls — so that expulsion always describes a decisive, and usually final, act of rejection.
