- [Early 15th c. Late Middle English] — Borrowed via Middle French resolut from Latin resolūtus, used initially in medical, alchemical, and natural philosophy treatises meaning "dissolved, melted, liquefied, thinned, or reduced to basic constituents" — [perception: a physical and medical descriptor for fluid matter or decomposed humours, lacking moral or psychological connotations]
- [Late 15th–16th c. Renaissance & Reformation] — Under the influence of scholastic logic and Renaissance humanism, applied to intellectual processes where a thesis or dilemma was "resolved" into first principles, describing a person whose mind was free from doubt and definitively convinced — [perception: celebrated marker of philosophical clarity, scholarly certainty, and the elimination of intellectual vacillation]
- [Late 16th–17th c. Elizabethan & Jacobean Eras] — Fully crystallized into its modern psychological and moral meaning of "firm, unwavering, courageous, and determined in purpose," popularized by Shakespeare and military chronicles — [perception: an esteemed martial, civic, and moral virtue representing dignified constancy and indomitable courage in the face of peril]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Romantic Eras] — Frequently paired with heroic, spiritual, and philosophical quests, such as Wordsworth's "Pilgrim resolute" in The Prelude, signifying a profound inner dedication to an idealized calling — [perception: poetic emblem of solemn spiritual vocation, heroic self-mastery, and noble persistence]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Ubiquitous in political, diplomatic, and executive discourse to denote unyielding collective determination or firm policy implementation against crises, terrorism, or economic turmoil — [perception: standard high-register adjective conveying authoritative firmness, moral integrity, and dependable leadership]
