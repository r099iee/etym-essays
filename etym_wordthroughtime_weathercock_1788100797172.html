- 9th–14th centuries - the church-cock is a Christian symbol of wakefulness and repentance on steeples, its orientation already practical; perception: sacred ornament as much as instrument.
- c. 1400–1500s - the compound 'weathercock' appears in Middle English for the vane itself; the cock form is so standard that word and object fuse in the popular mind.
- 1550s–1600s - figurative use blooms: whoever turns with the wind of opinion is 'a weathercock'; in Reformation-era religious and political feuds the word becomes a taunt for trimmers, apostates and time-servers.
- 17th–18th centuries - the proverbs 'as changeable as a weathercock' and the 'weathercock of state' fix the connotation: instability, want of principle, wanton versatility; Dryden and the satirists apply it to ministries and ministers.
- 1800s - barns and farmsteads adopt plainer vane shapes; 'weathervane' begins to compete as the plain word, while 'weathercock' keeps its rustic, churchyard flavour.
- 20th century - sailing and aviation borrow the word as exact technical metaphor (weathercocking, weathercock stability); in general speech it recedes toward archaism and literary colour.
- Today - mostly figurative and nostalgic: journalists still call politicians 'weathercocks', and the gilded cock remains a beloved landmark on old steeples.
