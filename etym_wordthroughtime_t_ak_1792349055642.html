- [c. 500 BCE–200 CE] — Attested in Valmiki's Sanskrit Rāmāyaṇa (Bāla-kāṇḍa) as a cursed Yakṣiṇī-turned-Rākṣasī, functioning as the quintessential narrative and moral catalyst for Rāma's transition from sheltered prince to righteous protector of cosmic order.
- [c. 1100–1600 CE] — Reinterpreted in medieval vernacular bhakti traditions (Kamban's Iramavataram, Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas, and Adhyatma Ramayana) not merely as a monstrous enemy, but as an allegorical embodiment of avidya (spiritual ignorance) whose destruction by Rama grants her instantaneous liberation (moksha).
- [c. 1700–1900 CE] — Cemented in popular theatrical performance traditions across the Indian subcontinent (Kathakali, Yakshagana, Ramlila) as an iconic dramatic villain archetype featuring exaggerated grotesque makeup, terrifying roars, and dynamic stage combat.
- [20th–21st c.] — Transitioned into widespread colloquial use in Modern Indo-Aryan languages (Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali) as an idiomatic, pejorative figure of speech describing an aggressive, domineering, ferocious, or quarrelsome woman.
