From receiving to teaching. The plural noun doctrines descends from Latin doctrīna, "teaching, instruction, the body of knowledge that is taught," itself a derivative of doctus, the perfect participle of the verb docēre, "to teach." Behind docēre stands the Proto-Indo-European root *dek-, whose fundamental sense was "to take, receive, accept." The conceptual movement from "taking" to "teaching" is one of the oldest and most instructive semantic shifts in the history of education: what the teacher has himself received, he passes on to the next generation. The same root, with the same logic of reception, appears across the family — Hittite takki-, "to take up a matter, to reconcile"; Greek déchomai, "to receive, accept"; and, in the Germanic world, the stem behind Gothic taikns, "sign, token," something received and recognised. The chain of meaning is thus already complete in the prehistoric root: knowledge is something taken into the mind, and teaching is the handing-on of what has been taken in.

In classical Latin, docēre produced a remarkably rich family. Doctor, "one who teaches" (whence English doctor, originally a learned man, and so the teacher of the church, the university, and eventually the physician); docilis, "capable of being taught," source of English docile; documentum, "a lesson or example," source of English document; and above all doctrīna itself. The noun carried two overlapping senses from the start: the act of teaching, and the systematic sum of what is taught — the body of received knowledge. In Roman usage doctrīna could describe philosophy, rhetoric, medicine, or any organised branch of learning; Cicero speaks of the doctrīna of the Greeks as the whole inheritance of systematic knowledge and skill.

The Christian colouring and the word's entry into English. The decisive medieval development came when the Christian church took the word for its own. In Christian Latin, doctrīna became the official teaching of the faith — the deposit of truths handed down by apostolic authority, articulated in the creeds and councils. It is this religious sense that passed through Old French and Anglo-Norman French into Middle English in the fourteenth century: the earliest English doctrine is a theological word, meaning the authoritative teaching of the church. The plural doctrines appears naturally in this religious setting, naming the several articles of the faith — the doctrine of the Trinity, the doctrine of grace, the doctrines of the sacraments — each an item in the received body of truth. The Reformation intensified the word's life: sixteenth-century controversy is full of "the doctrines of the church" versus "the doctrines of the reformers," and the word carried the full weight of the deepest religious divisions in European history.

Secularisation and expansion. From the seventeenth century onward the term expanded far beyond theology. Political writers spoke of "the doctrines of government"; philosophers of "the doctrines of the schools"; lawyers of "the doctrine of precedent" and "the doctrine of consideration." In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — the age of political economy — "doctrine" became a term of art for the asserted principles of an intellectual system, and it is precisely in this secular, systematic sense that J. S. Mill uses the plural in the source sentence, delimiting with quasi-legal care exactly which ideas of his published work were his own. The nineteenth century also saw the birth of the named doctrine: the Monroe Doctrine (1823), declaring the Western Hemisphere closed to new European colonisation, made "doctrine" a word of statecraft, a label for a country's declared principle of foreign policy; later came the Truman Doctrine, the Bush Doctrine, and the long series of political and military principles that bear the name. In this sense a doctrine is not merely taught but proclaimed — an authoritative statement of how a power will act.

Meaning and form through time. Phonologically the word has been remarkably stable: Latin doctrīna → Old French doctrine → Middle English doctrine (fourteenth century) → modern [ˈdɒktrɪn]. The stress pattern, the -ctr- cluster, and the -ine ending all survive essentially intact. Semantically, the history is one of steady secularisation and broadening: from religious creed, to learned system, to political and legal principle, to any firmly asserted body of belief. The word has, however, never lost its core idea of authority and reception — a doctrine is always something received and handed on, never merely invented by the speaker, and it is this inherited authority that gives Mill's careful sentence its force: to say that his work owed nothing to her "in its doctrines" is to say that the received analytical content was his own. The plural form adds a final nuance: "doctrines" distributes the authority across a set of distinct principles, each with its own weight and history, exactly as the doctrines of political economy were, for Mill, a body of specific and separate claims.
