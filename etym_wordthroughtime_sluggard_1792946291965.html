- Late 14th c. — the insult is coined: slugge, a sluggish fellow, takes the scornful -ard tail and becomes a moral category.
- 15th–16th c. — pulpit and press: Proverbs in English Bibles fixes thou sluggard in every ear, and reformers wield it against monks and beggars alike.
- Shakespeare's age — stage and street: rogues, apprentices, and Falstaff's crew are taxed with sluggardliness; the word is comic, not clinical.
- Industrial age — the secular sin: factory bells and school bells make sluggardliness an economic crime, and conduct books preach early rising as capital virtue.
- 20th c. — the joke retires it: psychologists speak of motivation, managers of performance, and sluggard survives in cartoons, pantomimes, and affectionate rousings.
- Today — archaic but alive: shouted at teenagers and holiday lie-abeds, kept warm by Tolkien, Proverbs, and everyone who ever had to wake a Fatty.
