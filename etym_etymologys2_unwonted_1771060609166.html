Derivation of wonted (the base of unwonted) PIE *wenh₁- "to desire, to strive for, to love, to win, to be satisfied" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *wunēną "to be satisfied, to be content, to dwell (contentedly)" (c. 500 BCE – 100 CE) → Old English wunian "to dwell, to remain, to stay; gewunian 'to be accustomed, to be wont, to have a habit'" (attested from the 8th c.; the compounding prefix ge- formed *ge-wunian, the source of 'wont') → Middle English wonen / wunien "to dwell, to be used to; wont (past participle) 'accustomed, in the habit of'" (12th–15th c.) → wonted "accustomed, habitual, usual" — back-formation from 'wont' with the adjectival -ed (14th c., e.g. 'wonted haunts') → (with the negative prefix un-) unwonted "not habitual, unaccustomed, unusual" (recorded from the late 15th / early 16th c.; the sense 'unusual, remarkable' consolidates in the 16th–17th c.) → unwonted (Modern English) Derivation of the prefix un- PIE *ne- "not" (primary negative particle, c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *un- "not" (zero-grade prefixed form, c. 500 BCE – 100 CE) → Old English un- "not, lack of" (productive negative prefix; e.g. ungewunelic 'unaccustomed', 8th c.) → un- + wonted → unwonted (Modern English) Debated / nuanced points. The traditional assignment of 'wont' to PIE *wenh₁- 'to desire/strive' is generally accepted in OED and Etymonline, with 'wunian' having developed 'to dwell' from 'to be content/satisfied in a place'; a minority view instead separates the Germanic dwelling-root *wunēn- from the desire-root *wenh₁-, in which case the 'habit' sense arose within Germanic alone from the semantics of 'remaining, staying.' Both analyses converge on the attested Old English-to-Modern English sequence above, which is secure. The spelling 'unwonted' (with -o-) reflects the old past-participial spelling of 'wont' and is fixed in Modern English; the variant 'unwonted' is not standard.
