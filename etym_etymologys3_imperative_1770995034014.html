The history of imperative is the history of a word that rose from humble domestic preparation to the absolute command of empire and then descended again into everyday urgency. The chain begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *per-, "to produce, procure, bring forth," a root of practical, forward-moving energy that generated verbs and nouns of provisioning across the Indo-European world. Ancient Greek póros, "way, passage," and peirā́ō, "to try, attempt," show the root at work in the sphere of effort and getting things done. In the Italic branch the root produced the Latin verb parāre, "to make ready, prepare, procure, furnish" — the language of equipping a household, provisioning a ship, stocking a market. It is attested from the earliest Latin literature, in Plautus and Cato, and it was extremely productive: English words as diverse as prepare, apparatus, apparel, compare, separate, and parade all descend from it.

The decisive leap happened when Roman military and administrative culture attached the intensive prefix in- ("upon, toward") to parāre. The compound imperāre is attested from the same early period (Plautus, Cato) and meant "to command, order, dictate." The semantic logic is concrete: to "prepare upon" a person or a province was to impose order upon them, to marshal them into readiness, to arrange their world from above. The word quickly shed its domestic associations and became the central verb of Roman authority. From it rose the great nominal family: imperium, "command, supreme power, the right to command" — which in English became empire — and imperātor, "commander, emperor." The Roman emperor was, etymologically, "the one who commands"; an empire was "a thing commanded." All of imperial Latin civilisation's vocabulary of rule flows from this single compound verb.

The grammatical turn came in the late classical schools. Latin grammarians, systematising the verb's inflectional moods, named the mood of commanding modus imperātīvus — the "mood of commanding," from the adjective imperātīvus, "relating to a command," itself formed on the past participle of imperāre. This grammatical sense travelled with the Latin educational tradition into the vernaculars. Old French had imperatif; Middle English borrowed imperative in the fifteenth century, and for several centuries the word's primary English life was grammatical: "the imperative mood" was the schoolboy's name for the verb-form that issues commands. From the sixteenth century the adjective broadened to describe persons, decrees, and tones of voice — an "imperative manner," an "imperative gesture" — always carrying the aura of absolute, unquestionable authority that had accumulated in Rome.

The modern sense of "absolutely necessary, vital" grew out of this authority in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries. When writers said that a course of action was imperative, they meant it commanded obedience like a general or a law: the necessity was not a preference but an order issued by circumstances. The philosophical re-charging of the noun occurred in 1785, when Immanuel Kant published the Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten and distinguished the "hypothetical imperative" (a command conditional on a desired end) from the "categorical imperative" (the unconditional moral law). Kant's term entered English philosophical vocabulary in the 1790s and spread into general moral and public language: "a moral imperative," "the categorical imperative," "economic imperatives."

By the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, imperative had settled into its current double life. As an adjective it is the strong, formal synonym for crucial and vital in business, policy, emergency, and everyday serious speech — "it is imperative that we act now" — with the imperial and moral weight reduced to a managerial urgency but never quite erased. As a noun it names binding duties of conscience, survival, and strategy.

KEY SOUND CHANGES

  • PIE *per- → Latin parāre: the PIE e-vowel in a non-initial syllable reduces to a in the Italic formation; the root-final r is retained.
  • Latin in- + parāre → imperāre: the nasal of the prefix assimilates to m before the following labial p; the prefix is stressed in the compound (ímperāre), distinguishing it from simple parāre.
  • Latin imperātīvus → Old French imperatif → English imperative: loss of the Latin case endings and of the final -us/-um; the Latin participial suffix -ātīvus yields French -atif and English -ative.

LITERARY AND PHILOSOPHICAL ATTESTATIONS

  • Plautus and Cato, 3rd–2nd c. BCE — imperāre in the full sense of military and domestic command.
  • Latin grammarians, 4th–6th c. CE — modus imperātīvus, the grammatical term.
  • Middle English, 15th c. — the grammar-book borrowing imperative.
  • Kant, 1785 — "categorical imperative" (in English from the 1790s), fixing the moral sense.
  • Modern journalism and business — "it is imperative that..." as the standard urgent-collocation.

The word is a perfect example of how a single root can travel: from the PIE preparation of daily provisioning, through the marshalling of Roman armies and the machinery of empire, into the grammarian's classroom, into Kantian ethics, and finally into the urgent prose of modern life — every stage leaving a layer of meaning that the contemporary word still silently carries.
