Ecclesiastic is, in its commonest adjectival sense, anything of, belonging to, or administered by the Christian Church as an institution — its clergy, government, laws, revenues, buildings, and rites — set over against the secular, civil, or lay world. An ecclesiastic court tries clergy by canon law; ecclesiastic revenues endow parishes and bishoprics; ecclesiastic history narrates the life of the Church rather than the deeds of the state.

In the source sentence the word colours a whole literary culture: in Russia, until the seventeenth century, written literature served only religious themes, ecclesiastic purposes, and the Church Slavonic language, so the Russian fairy tale grew up unlettered and free, never pruned by clerical taste from above. Here ecclesiastic means the purposes of the organised Church — edification, liturgy, doctrine, the salvation of souls — as opposed to entertainment, curiosity, or inquiry.

Core meaning in detail: The adjective proper, largely interchangeable with ecclesiastical, marks institutional churchliness: ecclesiastic vestments, ecclesiastic discipline, an ecclesiastic prince such as a prince-bishop, an ecclesiastic state such as the Papal States. As a noun, an ecclesiastic is a member of the clergy, a churchman or churchwoman from parish priest to patriarch — a slightly formal, faintly old-fashioned synonym of cleric, much used by historians of all periods.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Of the Church as an institution (primary adjectival sense): ecclesiastic law, polity, patronage, and office. The bridge runs from the Greek assembly of the faithful to the organisation that assembly built: what belongs to the summoned people belongs to their Church, and in time to its courts and coffers.
  • A clergyman or clergywoman (primary nominal sense): as in a synod of younger ecclesiastics or a wily old ecclesiastic of the curia. The bridge is the metonymy of service: the person consecrated to church work takes the name of the work itself, just as a musician takes the name of music.
  • Composed in or for the Church register (contextual and literary sense): ecclesiastic purposes, ecclesiastic Slavonic, an ecclesiastic chronicle. The bridge is functional: texts and tongues that serve worship, preaching, and doctrine are named for their master, exactly as military history serves war.
  • Churchmanship and party temper (technical sense in Anglican and historical theology): high ecclesiastic views, a sternly ecclesiastic temper. The bridge narrows the general churchly sense to principle and party: how strongly one rates bishops, sacraments, vestments, and canon law against the claims of the freethinker and the sectary.
  • Privilege and control seen from outside (polemical shading): ecclesiastic censorship, ecclesiastic landlordism, the ecclesiastic yoke. The bridge is the same institution viewed by its critics: where the insider says order, charity, and pastoral care, the reformer or satirist says power, profit, and supervision, and the adjective carries the charge.

Register and connotation: ecclesiastic the adjective is bookish and precise, favoured by historians and theologians where plain church would sound casual or parochial; ecclesiastic the noun is dignified but slightly dusty, and modern writers usually prefer cleric, priest, or minister for living persons. Beside its twin ecclesiastical, it is the shorter, older, and somewhat rarer form. Eighteenth-century purists once tried to reserve ecclesiastic for the noun and ecclesiastical for the adjective, but both offices have long since merged in practice, and only rhythm and habit now choose between them.
