The ancestry of PROPRIETY runs through the great Indo-European root *per-, “forward, through, in front of, first,” one of the family’s most productive elements. Its reflexes fan out across the ancient world: Sanskrit prá and purá, Greek pró, Hittite parā, the Germanic *fur- that underlies English for, fore, forth, first, and former, and the Latin prō and per. In Latin the root also produced the superlative prīmus, “first,” the source of English prime, primary, and primate. From this “first, foremost” stratum Latin built a small family of adjectives expressing what belongs to a single person: prīvus, “individual, one’s own,” whence English private and privilege, and, beside it, proprius, “one’s own, personal, peculiar.” The internal morphology of proprius is debated — it appears to be a derivative of the *per- family formed with a *-prio- suffix, perhaps originally meaning “belonging to oneself first or primarily” — but its sense is secure: what is proprium is what is peculiar to a person or thing, the quality that is exclusively one’s own. From proprius Latin formed the abstract noun proprietās, which carried two related strands of meaning: “ownership, the holding of property,” and “a distinctive quality or peculiar nature.” Both strands passed into the Romance languages — Old French proprieté, Italian proprietà, Spanish propiedad, all meaning “property” and “peculiar quality.”

The word entered Middle English in the fifteenth century through Old French as propriety, and for its first two centuries it meant “ownership” and “a distinctive characteristic” — in this it was virtually the doublet of property, which arrived at the same time through the Anglo-Norman form propertie. The two words grew apart: property kept the sense of the owned thing, while propriety kept the sense of the essential nature, and then made the decisive move toward conduct. In the seventeenth century a great semantic shift took place. Writers began to apply propriety to language first — the rhetorical notion of proprietās verborum, “the propriety of words,” using exactly the right expression for the thought — and then, by a natural extension, to conduct: the propriety of a behaviour was its fitness to the situation, the station, and the expectations of society. Dryden and the critics of the age made “propriety” a standard term of both rhetoric and manners; Addison and Steele praise it in the Spectator; and by the eighteenth century it had become the great word of polite society — the very word Gibbon uses, coolly, in his Memoirs.

• Sound change: the passage from Latin proprius through Old French proprieté to English propriety involves only regular Romance and French developments (the -tās abstract in -té), and the English word settled its modern spelling by the 17th century; the drama is semantic, not phonetic.
• Semantic drift: “one’s own thing” (proprius) → “ownership / peculiar quality” (proprietās) → “fitness of words” (proprietās verborum) → “fitness of conduct to social rule” (modern propriety). The thread is the idea of rightness — what fits is what belongs.

In Gibbon’s sentence, “prudence and propriety” are the two halves of eighteenth-century respectability: prudence the inner calculation, propriety the outer conformity. The word’s social career tracks the rise of the middle classes and the codification of manners in the age of the drawing-room and the assembly — a culture in which conduct, dress, and speech were all judged by their “propriety.” Jane Austen’s heroines navigate a world where propriety is the air one breathes; the word appears in her pages precisely because it names the invisible rulebook of her society. In the twentieth century propriety came to sound dated and formal, used with irony or in period and legal contexts; the plural “the proprieties” survives to describe the set of conventions one observes, and the word retains its double life as both a technical term of rhetoric and the great noun of social conformity. Its whole history is compressed into a single idea: what is proper is what belongs — to the role, the station, and the world that expects it.
