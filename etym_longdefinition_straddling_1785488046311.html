In this context. The Foreword praises an author as “a true philologist, effortlessly straddling literary values and linguistic technicalities, convincingly showing the relevance of each to the other”. The metaphor is drawn from posture. To straddle is to stand with the legs astride, one foot firmly on each side of something — a horse, a fence, a ditch — so that you command both sides at once without having to choose between them. The author is pictured bestriding two disciplines, keeping a foothold in each, so that the literary critic and the linguist within him are simultaneously active and in balance, each informing the other. The adverb “effortlessly” is crucial: straddling normally demands strain and precariousness, but he does it without visible effort, proving that the two pursuits are naturally one. The tone is admiring, almost athletic — praise rendered as physical poise.

Core meaning. Straddling is the present participle and gerund of the verb straddle: standing or sitting with a leg on each side of something, or (of a thing) spanning, bestriding or lying across an object or area so as to touch both sides. Figuratively it means simultaneously spanning, bestriding, or holding two domains, positions or commitments in balance. The word is built on stride plus the frequentative suffix -le, which gives the action a repeated, spread-out, or ongoing character.

Polysemy: the full range of senses.

  • Literal posture. To sit or stand astride: straddling a horse, a bicycle, a fence, a ditch, or a seat — legs wide, one on each side. Also of an object: the bridge straddles the river; the town straddles the border.
  • Figurative balance. To occupy both sides of a divide at once, holding two roles, disciplines, opinions or cultures in balance without surrendering either — the sense in our sentence.
  • Hedging / non-commitment (chiefly American English, eighteenth–nineteenth centuries). “To straddle the fence” — to avoid taking sides, to keep one’s options open; hence a straddler is an equivocator. Here the image is evasive rather than athletic.
  • Finance (options market, twentieth century). A straddle is an options position that buys (or sells) both a call and a put on the same underlying security at the same strike price and expiry, profiting from movement in either direction — a strategy that deliberately “straddles” the market’s direction.
  • Poker. A straddle is a voluntary blind bet placed before the deal, usually double the big blind, which “straddles” (bestrides) the normal betting structure.
  • Military / ballistics (historical). To straddle a target with artillery is to bracket it, firing ranging shots both short and long so that the range is “bestridden”; straddling shots fall on both sides of the target.
  • Computing / networks (loosely). Straddling firewalls, straddling IP addresses or straddling administrative domains — configurations that sit astride two zones or boundaries.

Semantic bridge. The core image never changes: a body with its weight distributed across a gap, commanding both sides. From the concrete posture the word slides into the figurative balancing act, then (in American English) into the fence-sitter’s hedging, then into technical uses where a structure or position “bestrides” two alternatives at once. The professional senses all retain the root idea of holding two sides simultaneously — the trader’s straddle, the poker straddle and the straddling intellectual are the same gesture in different costumes.

Boundaries against near-synonyms. Bridging stresses building a link between two fixed, separate banks while keeping them distinct; spanning stresses width and extent, as of an arch, without the sense of a body doing the spanning; combining implies merging the two things into one, which is not what happens here; balancing stresses counterweight and the constant danger of tipping over; bestriding is grander and more conquering, implying domination from above rather than equal partnership. Straddling is distinct in exactly this: both sides remain fully separate, and the straddler holds them in a single, easy posture, neither merging them nor ranking one above the other.
