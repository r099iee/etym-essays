Choke is a native Germanic word, one of the oldest English verbs for suffocation. The earliest attested form is Old English ācēocian (10th century), "to choke, suffocate", used in glosses and homiletic prose. It is almost universally taken to be a derivative of Old English cēoce "cheek, jaw", with the prefixed intensive ā-; the underlying image is of something that lodges in the cheek, jaw, or throat and blocks the breath. The verb thus encodes, from its birth, a concrete bodily picture: the mouth and windpipe closed.

Pre-history and the problem of the root

  • Old English cēoce "cheek, jaw" points back to a Proto-Germanic *kaukō (reconstructed), and some scholars connect this to an Indo-European root *geu- / *gēu- meaning "to bend, curve, hollow", the cheekbone and jaw being curved bones that frame a hollow.
  • This Indo-European ancestry is plausible but insecure: the Germanic family is small and isolated, and the word may have been formed independently on a sound-symbolic base — a "k" cluster suggesting a catch or grip in the throat.
  • Scholars therefore flag the whole chain above Old English as reconstructed; what is certain is only the record from Old English onward.

Middle English and the rise of the modern form

  • In Middle English the verb appears as choken (13th century), "to choke, strangle, suffocate", with the same senses as today: being unable to breathe from an obstruction, or being strangled by external force.
  • The form shows the characteristic Middle English changes: loss of the ā- prefix, shortening and lowering of the root vowel, and the initial consonant becoming the affricate ch under the influence of palatal environments and dialectal variation.
  • By the 14th century the figurative use is already present: to "choke" can mean to suppress, check, or stifle something (a plan, a laugh, a protest) — the metaphor of throttling applied to words and intentions.

Early modern expansion

  • In the 16th–18th centuries the mechanical senses appear: a "choked" pipe, drain, channel, or river-mouth is one stopped up with silt, weeds, or debris; plants "choke" the ground they cover.
  • The engine sense is modern: with the internal combustion engine (late 19th–early 20th century), the "choke" valve — which starves the engine of air to enrich the mixture on starting — inherits the old image of constricting a passage to control flow. The noun then leapt into electronics as an "RF choke", an inductor that blocks high-frequency current.

Semantic drift in the 19th–21st centuries

  • Emotional sense: "choked with emotion", "choked up", "a choked voice" — a voice stopped by feeling — becomes common in 19th-century fiction and stays current; in British English "choked" alone came to mean "very upset or disappointed".
  • Performance sense: the great 20th-century innovation. American sports slang of the mid-20th century (baseball, golf, basketball) produced "he choked", "a choke artist" — an athlete who fails at the decisive moment because the pressure constricts performance. This sense spread into business and exams, so that "to choke" now often has nothing to do with breathing.
  • Intensifiers and compounds: "chokehold" (a neck restraint, 19th century, given legal and technical fame in the 2010s in police-use discussions), "chokepoint" (a narrow strategic or traffic point), "chokeberry", "chokecherry" (plants whose fruit contracts the throat) — the word keeps generating new compounds out of its root image of constriction.

Literary and documentary attestations

  • Old English glosses render Latin suffocare and strangulare with ācēocian, showing that the word was the standard vernacular for "suffocate".
  • Shakespeare exploits the literal and figurative registers of choke with full force; Chaucer uses it of being stifled and drowned in drink. Both poets keep the physical and emotional senses in play at once.
  • Modern use keeps all these layers alive simultaneously: a news report may describe a "choked" rescue victim, a "choked" port, an athlete who "choked", and a family "choked with grief" — four senses of one image in a single paragraph.

In sum, choked shows a native English word moving outward from the throat to the world: from blocked windpipes to blocked drains, engines, and electronics, to blocked emotions and frozen athletes — every step a transfer of the same bodily metaphor of a passage closed against the flow.
