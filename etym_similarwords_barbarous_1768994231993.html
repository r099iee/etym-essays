• barbaric: Similar because both denote the savage and uncultivated; differs because barbaric tilts toward the primitive and grand — barbaric splendor, barbaric music — admiring what it also fears, while barbarous is the stronger, unambiguously moral condemnation.
  • savage: Similar because both denote ferocity and rawness; differs because savage stresses violent wildness and applies easily to landscape and weather, while barbarous carries civilization's judgment of cruelty and unintelligible foreignness.
  • primitive: Similar because both denote the early or crude; differs because primitive is descriptive and anthropological, implying an early stage of development without moral charge, while barbarous condemns.
  • uncivilized: Similar because both denote a lack of civilization; differs because uncivilized is the plain literal term, neutral and factual, while barbarous adds savagery, cruelty, and the ancient sense of speech man cannot parse.
  • uncultured: Similar because both denote a lack of cultivation; differs because uncultured is polite and descriptive, naming a want of refinement in manners and taste, while barbarous is harsher, implying wildness and threat as well as ignorance.
  • uncultivated: Similar because both denote the unrefined; differs because uncultivated can be neutral, of land or of a mind left to run wild, while barbarous always carries the judgment of civilization against its outside.
  • unrefined: Similar because both denote a lack of polish; differs because unrefined belongs to the aesthetic register — crude manners, crude art — while barbarous covers moral cruelty as well as roughness.
  • rude: Similar because both denote roughness and lack of polish (the archaic sense of rude); differs because rude now chiefly means impolite, a social failing, while barbarous retains its strong sense of wildness and savagery.
  • boorish: Similar because both denote coarse manners; differs because boorish is confined to social conduct — a clod, a lout — while barbarous is cosmic and moral, of peoples, acts, and landscapes.
  • brutish: Similar because both denote sub-human coarseness; differs because brutish stresses the animal, physical, insensitive side (brutish strength), while barbarous emphasizes civilization's exclusion and judgment.
  • brutal: Similar because both denote cruel treatment; differs because brutal stresses physical violence and hard-heartedness in the agent, while barbarous adds the dimension of being outside civilized order.
  • cruel: Similar because both denote infliction of suffering; differs because cruel is the plain general term for causing pain without compassion, morally simple, while barbarous is historically loaded with the whole civilization-versus-wilderness opposition.
  • inhuman: Similar because both denote conduct below humanity; differs because inhuman stresses the loss of human feeling or limits, while barbarous stresses the outsider's lack of civilization.
  • ferocious: Similar because both denote fierce violence; differs because ferocious describes the savagery of attack or temperament (a ferocious beast, a ferocious temper), while barbarous judges whole conditions and cultures as uncivilized.
  • wild: Similar because both denote the untamed; differs because wild is the neutral descriptive word for what is not domesticated or controlled, while barbarous adds moral and historical condemnation.
  • untamed: Similar because both denote what resists domestication; differs because untamed is factual — an animal, a region, a spirit not yet subdued — while barbarous is the value-laden word of civilization's frontiers.
