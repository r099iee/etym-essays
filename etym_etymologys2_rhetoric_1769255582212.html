PIE *wer- (also written *wre-, *wreh₁-) "to speak, say" (reconstructed, c. 4500–3500 BCE; the same root gives Latin verbum "word" and English word)
→ Greek rhētōr "public speaker, orator" — built on the root-noun rhēma "word, utterance" and the verb eírein "to say"; the Greek reflexes show the aspirated initial rh- characteristic of this root's outcome in Hellenic (attested from the 5th c. BCE)
→ Greek rhētorikḗ (tekhnē) "the orator's art, the art of public speaking" — the feminine adjective rhētorikós "of the orator" applied to tekhnē "art, craft"; coined in the age of the Sophists and Isocrates (5th–4th c. BCE) and systematised by Aristotle's treatise (c. 335–323 BCE)
→ Latin rhētorica (ars) "the art of rhetoric" (attested 1st c. BCE)
→ Old French rethorique, retorique "the art of rhetoric" (attested from the 12th c.)
→ Middle English rethorike, rethoryk "the art of rhetoric" (attested c. 1350s)
→ English rhetoric ◄ TARGET (present day)

DEBATE / NUANCE: The Greek cluster rhēma "word, utterance", rhētōr "speaker", and rhētorikḗ is securely derived from the verbal root in Greek eírein/ereîn "to say" and rhētos "spoken, stated", all from PIE *wer- "to speak" — one of the best-attested roots, witnessed in Hittite weriye- "to call", Sanskrit vra-tá (the solemn vow or utterance), Latin verbum, and Germanic *wurdą "word". The main scholarly nuance is the laryngeal detail of the reconstruction (*wreh₁- vs *wer-) and the internal aspiration of the Greek cluster, matters of reconstruction rather than of attested form. The modern English spelling rhetoric, with the internal -h-, restores the Greek aspiration through Latin scholarship; the earlier Middle English forms rethorike and rethoryk kept it too, and the h was sometimes dropped in old scribal spelling.
