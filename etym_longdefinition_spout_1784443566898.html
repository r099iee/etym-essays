SPOUT (noun and verb, /spˈaʊt/) — as a noun: (1) a tube, pipe, lip, or trough through which liquid is discharged, especially the projecting lip of a kettle, teapot, urn, or vessel; (2) a sudden, forceful stream or jet of liquid, fire, or other matter forced through a narrow opening; (3) a column or fountain of water — the whale's blowhole spray, a waterspout; (4) by extension, the fixtures and channels that guide outflow, such as a rain downspout or a spout of a gutter. As a verb: (1) to issue, gush, or pour forth in a stream ("water spouted from the broken pipe"); (2) transitive, to pour or discharge through a spout; (3) figuratively, to utter or declaim at length and with force ("to spout poetry," "spouting nonsense"); (4) of whales, to eject a stream of water from the blowhole.

1. Core senses and usage

  • The physical spout (vessel): the projecting lip or tube by which a vessel pours — the kettle's spout, the teapot's spout — known in English since the 14th century.
  • The spout as outlet or downpipe: a gutter's spout, a drain spout, the downspout carrying rainwater from the roof — the "broken spout of the gutter."
  • The spout as stream: a jet or gush of liquid ("a spout of water," "a spout of flame"), where the noun names the stream itself, not the fixture.
  • The whale's spout: the spray ejected from the blowhole when a whale surfaces — a sense fixed in the vocabulary of whaling and natural history since the 17th century.
  • The waterspout: a tornado-like rotating column of air and water over a sea or lake, named from the appearance of water being sucked and spouted into the sky.
  • The figurative speech sense: to "spout" is to pour out words copiously, rapidly, or pompously — "spouting opinions," "spouting verse" — attested since the 18th century, the human mouth imaged as a pipe.

2. Historical bridges and semantic development

The word is part of the great expressive family of spit, spew, spatter, sputter, spurt, and spout, all built on an imitative Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to eject fluid from the mouth." The physical gesture — lips as an orifice, sudden expulsion — is the concrete image at the bottom of every sense. From the human mouth the image passed to the mouths of vessels (the lip of a kettle that pours like a lip spitting), to the blowholes of whales (a mouth that spouts), to the atmospheric column of the waterspout (a storm that spouts), and finally back to the human mouth in the figurative sense: the declaimer's mouth becomes the spout, pouring out words as a pipe pours water. The "spout of flame" in Tolkien's verse belongs to the same image extended to fire: the dragon's maw is the spout, the flame the stream forced through it.

3. Modern technical and jargon uses

  • Meteorology: waterspout — a non-supercell tornado over water or a funnel-shaped cloud-water column; landspout by analogy.
  • Marine biology and whaling: the whale's spout (the "blow") — a diagnostic feature for identifying species, since each whale's spout has a characteristic shape and height.
  • Brewing and dispensing: the spout of a beer engine, tap, or urn; in Britain, a "long-pull" spout terminology of the pub trade.
  • Geology and oil: an oil spouter — an oil well that spouts or gushes, hence the American slang "spouter" for a gushing well; a geyser is a spout of hot water and steam.
  • Fishing and netting (historical slang): of fish, to "spout," to leap or spurt.
  • The idiom "up the spout": British slang, "pawned, in pawn, ruined, done for, broken," attested since the 19th century — originally of goods "gone up the spout" to the pawnbroker, then of anything lost or ruined ("the plan is up the spout").
  • Figurative declamation: "to spout" = to deliver a speech, recite verse, or preach at length, often with a hint of emptiness or pomposity; a "spouter" is a bombastic speaker.

4. Distinctions from near-synonyms

  • Spurt names the sudden short jet, usually a burst; spout can name either the fixture or a sustained stream.
  • Jet is the compact, high-pressure stream (technical); spout is broader, covering fixtures and gentle vessels' lips.
  • Gush emphasizes volume and abruptness of outflow; spout emphasizes the channel that directs it.
  • Spew and spit are earthier, suggesting the mouth and disgust; spout is neutral in the physical senses.
  • Nozzle and pipe are the mechanical fixtures; spout can be the fixture or the stream itself.

5. Usage notes

Spout is a word of both the workshop and the wild: it serves for the humble teapot and for the sublime spectacle of a whale rising to blow, for the domestic gutter and for the apocalyptic "spout of flame" of a dragon's breath. Its double life as noun and verb mirrors its double ancestry — the concrete mouth of a vessel and the expressive act of spitting — and its figurative sense (declaiming) keeps the whole family's root image alive in everyday speech.
