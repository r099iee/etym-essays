Inordinate is an adjective meaning “excessive in amount, degree or intensity; lacking proper measure or moderation”. In the source it modifies “love”: Cicero’s affection for his daughter Tullia is called inordinate — not merely great, but beyond the bound the biographer thinks proper. The word is doing open moral work in that sentence, and the clause that follows, “who somehow never seemed to have merited it particularly”, confirms that the excess is being judged rather than measured.

The dominant modern sense

  • Excessive, disproportionate. The ordinary use today: an inordinate amount of time, inordinate delay, inordinate influence, inordinate pride, inordinate risk. It attaches to abstractions and quantities, hardly ever to physical objects, and it implies that a norm has been overrun.
  • Typical collocations. Inordinate delay is a staple of legal and bureaucratic complaint; inordinate amount of time is close to a cliché; inordinate power and inordinate influence belong to political commentary; inordinate fondness and inordinate appetite keep the older language of desire.
  • Adverbial use. Inordinately is often more idiomatic than the adjective: inordinately proud, inordinately long, inordinately fond of something. The adverb softens the bookishness and is common in journalism.

The literal sense and the semantic bridge

Etymologically the word means “not ordered”: Latin inordinatus, from in- “not” plus ordinatus “arranged, set in order”. That literal sense — disordered, irregular, not governed by rule — survives in archaic and technical English (“inordinate conduct”, “an inordinate appetite” in older moral prose), but it has largely been displaced. The bridge between the two senses is the idea of measure. Latin ordo is a row, a rank, a due arrangement; to be inordinate is to be out of that arrangement, and what is out of arrangement is, by a natural extension, out of proportion. Mediaeval Latin made the step explicit by speaking of inordinata dilectio, love that does not observe its proper place in the order of loves. “Too much” is therefore a special case of “not ordered”: a passion that has broken out of its rank.

Secondary, technical and historical senses

  • Theological and moral. The Authorised Version’s “inordinate affection” (Colossians 3:5) translates Greek pathos and became a fixed phrase in English devotional writing. In scholastic moral theology inordinatio is the disorder of an appetite that exceeds or defects from right order; the phrase amor inordinatus is standard. This is the most important historical bridge between the literal and the modern senses.
  • Legal and administrative. “Inordinate delay” is a formula in judgments and procedural complaints, meaning delay so great that it amounts to a denial of justice. The word here retains a faint flavour of the literal sense: the proceedings have fallen out of their proper order.
  • Archaic general. Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century prose can use inordinate simply for “irregular, disorderly, unruly”, of weather, government or conduct, without any quantitative implication.
  • Rare derivatives. Inordinateness and the seventeenth-century inordination exist but are literary curiosities; the adjective and adverb carry the word’s life.

Register and connotation

Inordinate is formal, bookish and mildly censorious. It does not merely report size; it alleges impropriety. That makes it stronger than “large” and more judgemental than “considerable”, but less emotional than “obsessive” or “pathological”. It suits written argument, editorial prose and legal drafting, and it can sound prim in speech. Because it presupposes a norm, it is a favourite of critics: to call someone’s influence inordinate is already to argue that it ought to be smaller.

In this passage

The biographer might have written “his great love for his daughter”. Choosing inordinate converts a virtue into a symptom and prepares the reader for the unkind aside about Tullia’s failure to merit it. There is an additional irony: Cicero himself wrote the Tusculan Disputations on the control of grief and passion, and the word charges the philosopher of moderation with a passion out of measure. Whether the charge is fair is another matter — the letters show a father devastated by Tullia’s death — but the lexical choice is unmistakably evaluative.
