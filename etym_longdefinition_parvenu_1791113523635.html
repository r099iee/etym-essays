Parvenu denotes a person who has recently risen to wealth, power or high social position but is regarded by established elites as lacking the manners, taste or breeding appropriate to that position — an upstart, a nouveau-riche climber.

The core sense is sociological and pejorative. A parvenu has arrived economically or politically but not culturally in the eyes of judges: money is new, accent or decorum betrays origin, display is thought ostentatious. Classic contexts are 19th-century industrial fortunes, post-war profiteers, and modern celebrity wealth crashing old hierarchies. The feminine form in French is parvenue, occasionally preserved in English for women, though parvenu is now generic. Plural parvenus or French parvenus.

  • Social-climber sense: newly rich or powerful person scorned as vulgar. Collocations: mere parvenu, rich parvenu, political parvenu, parvenu manners, parvenu taste. Verbs: dismiss as, sneer at, betray oneself as. Register is literary, journalistic and snobbish; it is an insult, not a neutral description of mobility.
  • Attributive/adjectival sense: characteristic of such a person — showy, ostentatious, insecurely grand. One speaks of parvenu luxury, parvenu suburbs, parvenu art-collecting. Here the noun modifies taste rather than naming a person.
  • Philosophical-zoological twist in context: that timid parvenu, man applies the social sneer to the human species in deep time: late-arriving, timid among mightier beasts, now insecure master of earth. Timid and parvenu collide ironically — an upstart who is also fearful — capturing early humanity huddling in camps while megafauna roamed. This is metaphorical, not literal class insult, but it borrows the newcomer-not-yet-secure logic.

Polysemy is narrow; the bridge from arrived to upstart is pejoration by envy and gatekeeping. To arrive implies a prior absence; elites convert absence into illegitimacy. The secondary adjectival use follows naturally: if parvenus decorate in a certain way, that style is parvenu.

Near-synonyms sharpen boundaries (see similarWords), but parvenu uniquely preserves Frenchness, worldliness and a whiff of salon contempt. It is less brutal than upstart, more personal than nouveau riche as a class, and less political than arriviste. Use requires care: calling someone a parvenu reveals the speaker's snobbery as much as the target's novelty.

In short, parvenu means newly arrived and resented for it — whether a railway king in Thackeray or timid Homo sapiens newly secure on earth.

Stylistically, parvenu is a deliberate Gallicism: writers choose it over plain upstart to perform their own insider cultivation while wounding the target. Pronunciation keeps French nasality softened for English ears, and the feminine parvenue still appears in novels of manners for precision. To use the word well is to balance description and judgment: acknowledge real ascent in wealth or power, then mark the gap between arrival and acceptance as judged by incumbents. In Durant historic-philosophical use, that gap becomes existential — humanity newly arrived at mastery, still timid, still proving it belongs on earth.
