unrivalled (British spelling; American unrivaled) is a formal adjective meaning “having no rival, competitor, or equal; peerless, matchless, supreme in quality or degree.” It denies the existence of anyone or anything that can match the person, thing, or quality described, and it does so in the language of contest and supremacy: the unrivalled performer stands alone in the arena because no opponent can run the same stream. In the present context the word applies to the wisdom of Harriet Taylor as mourned by John Stuart Mill: her wisdom was “all but unrivalled” — that is, almost entirely without equal — and the near-qualifier “all but” lets Mill claim for her a peerlessness that still stops short of divine arrogance. The nuance is grief-stricken veneration: the thoughts and feelings “buried in her grave” were so far above ordinary human attainment that Mill could never adequately interpret them to the world.

  • Sense 1 (of persons): “unmatched in a given field or quality,” e.g. an unrivalled master, unrivalled orator, unrivalled surgeon — implying that no competitor can match the person's skill.
  • Sense 2 (of things and qualities): “without equal in degree or excellence,” e.g. an unrivalled collection, unrivalled service, unrivalled beauty, unrivalled wisdom — the standard use in descriptions of achievement, collections, reputations, and natural wonders.
  • Sense 3 (marketing / hyperbole): in advertising and commercial language, a stock superlative — “unrivalled quality,” “unrivalled value” — where the claim of peerlessness is conventionalised and often taken as mere emphasis.
  • Historical bridge: the word is formed in English from rival (Latin rīvālis, “one who shares the same stream,” a competitor) with the negative prefix un- and the adjectival suffix -ed. To be “rivalled” is to be met by an equal in a contest; to be “unrivalled” is to be met by none. The image is of streams and waterways: the rival was originally the neighbour who drew water from the same brook, and the unrivalled person owns the river alone.
  • Distinction from near-synonyms: unmatched is the plainest word, saying only that nothing equals it; unparalleled stresses that nothing parallel or analogous exists, so it suits historical uniqueness (unparalleled cruelty); peerless is the poetic, lofty synonym (peers are equals, so the peerless have no equals); unsurpassed admits there may be equals but none greater (the emphasis falls on the ceiling of quality); incomparable says the thing is of a different order entirely, not to be set alongside others; matchless is a poetic variant of unmatched.
  • Register and connotation: middle-to-high register, formal and admiring; it preserves a faint adversarial image — the arena where no opponent can match the champion. It is used of qualities, achievements, collections, reputations, and skills; its natural domain is panegyric and superlative description, as in Mill's “all but unrivalled wisdom.”

Antonyms and contrasts: rivalled, matched, surpassed, second-rate, inferior, ordinary, common. The word's force lies in its absolute claim: it does not say “very good” but “without equal,” and the careful writer softens it with qualifiers (“all but,” “virtually”) when modesty demands. A full treatment of unrivalled thus moves from the Roman stream-sharing neighbour, through the language of chivalric and academic supremacy, to the Victorian biographer's tribute to a woman whose wisdom, buried in her grave, remained all but unrivalled in the world.
