Scurry has no single linear ancestry: it is a back-formation from the reduplicative compound hurry-scurry (1732), whose two elements have separate histories. Chains for each element in isolation: CHAIN 1 — the hurry element (imitative/Germanic, no secure PIE root): Proto-Germanic *hurza- "to move with haste, whir" (reconstructed; imitative, cf. German hurren "to whir, move fast," Old Swedish hurra "to whirl round") → Middle English hurren "to vibrate rapidly, buzz (of insects)" (attested) → English hurry "to move or act with haste" (attested from the 1590s, first in Shakespeare; probably a variant of harry or springing from the buzzing, whirring sense — the origin is uncertain and may be independent of *hurza-). CHAIN 2 — the skurry / scour element (PIE): PIE *kers- "to run" (reconstructed) → Latin currere "to run" (attested) → Latin excurrere "to run out, sally forth" (attested) → Old French / Anglo-French escourre "to run out, range over" (attested) → Middle English scour / skouren "to move about hastily in search, range" (attested; the modern English verb scour) → the rhyming element skurry, coined or reshaped under the influence of scour within the phrase hurry-skurry. MERGE — the modern word: Reduplicative compound hurry-scurry / hurry-skurry "confused haste, bustle" (attested 1732) → back-formation scurry (verb) "to hasten along, move precipitately, scuttle" (attested 1810) → scurry (noun) "a hurried, bustling movement" (attested 1823) → present participle scurrying → **SCURRYING** (Modern English). Notes and debates: - The OED and Etymonline treat scurry as probably from hurry-scurry (i.e. a back-formation), and hurry-scurry itself as a reduplicative formation whose second element was influenced by or coined under scour; the word is partly imitative, and some authorities emphasize the sound-symbolic (rhyming) value of the pair over a precise etymology. - The hurry element may derive from Middle English hurren (the whirring, buzzing sense) rather than directly from a variant of harry; Shakespeare's hurry (1590s) is the first attestation of the verb, and its exact relationship to the Germanic *hurza- base is uncertain. - There is no single governing root for the whole word because it is a reduplicative blend; the honest description is two elements (Germanic haste + Latin-derived scour) fused by rhyme and then back-formed into one verb.
