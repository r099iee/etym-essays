Buffeting continues a small, noisy French family built on the syllable buff-, imitative of puffed cheeks and dull thuds. The story begins not in Latin but in sound symbolism: across Germanic and Romance, buff-, puff-, baff-, bouff- clusters denote swelling (cheeks puffed to blow) and soft impact (a blow on puffed flesh). Old French lexicalised this as bufe, buffe (c.1200, “a blow with the fist, a slap, a puff of air”), probably echoic, with diminutive buffet (c.1250, “a little blow, a slap, a cuff”). The verb buffeter (“to box, to slap repeatedly”) follows regularly. Middle English borrowed the whole set after the Conquest (c.1300): noun buffet “a blow”, verb buffeten “to beat, to box”. Early attestations are martial and judicial: knights “buffet” foes, Christ is “buffeted” in Wycliffite gospel translations (Matthew 26:67, “they buffeted him”), establishing a solemn-religious aura of unjust suffering that still colours figurative buffeted by fate.

Phonologically the development is straightforward. OF buffet /by.fɛt/ was anglicised to ME buffet /buf.et/ with stress retraction to the first syllable (/ˈbʌfɪt/ in modern RP, with STRUT vowel /ʌ/ replacing French /y/). The verb’s inflection regularised as weak: buffet–buffeted–buffeted, with participial buffeting formed by adding -ing (from OE -ung via ME -ing, originally nominal, later progressive). Spelling doubled the t before -ed/-ing in American style (buffeted, buffeting) but single t often in British (buffeted still doubled by rule for short vowel). No Great Vowel Shift effect beyond regular shortening. The food noun buffet /bʊfeɪ/ (“sideboard, refreshment table”, borrowed 1710s from French buffet “bench, cupboard”, itself possibly from buff- “protuberance” or Germanic *buff-) is a later re-borrowing with French stress preserved; its divergence in pronunciation prevents merger, though spelling identity causes modern confusion that lexicographers must explicitly flag.

  • Medieval (1200–1500): Literal fist-blows. Buffet as countable blow; to buffet as to box. Collocates with knights, fists, palms, Christ’s passion. Perception: violent, honour-bound, or martyrological.
  • Early Modern (1500–1700): Extension to weather and waves. Shakespeare (“buffeted by waves”, Othello; “buffets of fortune”) and the Authorized Version fix the metaphor: nature and fate box as men box. Perception widens from brawl to providence.
  • 18th–19th centuries: Generalised adversity (“buffeted by misfortune”) plus pugilistic survival in boxing manuals. The noun buffeting (a beating, plural buffetings) becomes common in novels (Defoe, Scott, Brontë) for storms endured.
  • 20th century technical: Aviation adopts buffeting as term of art for aeroelastic vibration (buffet onset, tail buffet, 1920s–1940s NACA reports). Meteorology and oceanography follow. Simultaneously journalism clichés buffeted markets (1970s onward). Perception splits: precise-engineering versus formulaic-metaphorical.

Socio-cultural context explains persistence. A culture that prizes endurance needs a verb for survivable repeated blows that is neither lethal (to crush) nor trivial (to tap). Buffet fills that middle: to be buffeted is to be tested but not destroyed, to retain moral standing while losing comfort — ideal for saints, sailors, airmen, and economies. Pulp adventure, as here, leans on the literal-weather sense to make alien planets bodily legible: readers who have never felt Martian wind have felt a gale buffeting the face, and the participle’s ongoingness (-ing) puts them inside the blast rather than after it. The PIE pedigree is therefore shallow by design: no ancient root is needed because the word is built from medieval sound symbolism, French diminutive morphology, and English progressive aspect — a reminder that some of the most durable English words are young and echoic rather than ancient and inherited.
