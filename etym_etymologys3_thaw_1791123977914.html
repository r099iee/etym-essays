Thaw continues one of the most stable weather verbs in Germanic, barely changed in sound or sense from Proto-Germanic to Dickens to the present. Its ultimate source is PIE *teh₂- (also cited as *ta-) to melt, dissolve, flow. Cognates include Latin tabere to waste away, melt, Sanskrit toyam water, Old Church Slavonic tajati to melt. The core image is solid loosening into liquid.

Proto-Germanic formed *þawjan / *þawōn to thaw, with the characteristic voiceless interdental þ /θ/ preserved in modern English th-. Cognates: Old Norse þeyja, Dutch dooien, German tauen, all meaning to thaw. Old English þawian, þawan meant to melt (of snow, ice) and already figuratively to soften in mind. The Peterborough Chronicle notes thaws that broke frozen rivers and famines.

Middle English thawen, thawien kept both transitive (to thaw meat, to thaw frozen ground) and intransitive (it thaweth, the river thawed) uses. The Great Vowel Shift lengthened the vowel to modern /θɔː/ (RP), spelled aw. No French overlay touched it; it remained a blunt monosyllabic North-Sea word, opposite in register to Latinate liquefy, dissolve, deliquesce.

Semantic expansion followed the universal COLD = UNFEELING / WARM = KIND metaphor. Already in Old and Middle English one could thaw in feeling: a frozen heart thaws. By early modern times this is commonplace: Shakespeare has frozen allegiance thaw, Milton has thawed charity. Dickens inverts it brilliantly: Scrooge didn't thaw it one degree at Christmas — the office that he iced in the dog-days remains iced. The pronoun it refers to his office/atmosphere, and thaw is transitive-causative: to make less cold, to warm. The joke is physical-moral: dog-days (hottest summer) and Christmas (deep winter) are climatically opposite, yet Scrooge's cold is constant; neither season thaws him.

Modern science sharpened but did not replace the folk sense:

  • Meteorology: thaw as noun means a spell of warm weather that melts snow (a January thaw), also called thawing. Distinguished from melt (general solid→liquid) by implying winter ice/snow and relief.
  • Food technology: to thaw frozen food, thawing instructions, thaw overnight in fridge. Here thaw = defrost, with safety connotations.
  • Geopolitics: the Thaw (Khrushchev Thaw, 1950s) for liberalization after Stalinist freeze; thaws in relations, diplomatic thaw. Cold War language revived the heart metaphor at state scale.
  • Interpersonal: to thaw toward someone, to thaw out at a party, a frosty reception thawed. Intransitive with out or toward, describing geniality returning.

Grammar preserves the causative alternation: The ice thawed (intransitive, it melted) vs He thawed the office (transitive, he warmed it) vs He thawed out the pipes (phrasal). Dickens uses transitive with degree complement: didn't thaw it one degree — wonderfully measurable cold, as if warmth were degrees on a thermometer he refuses to grant.

Literary attestations cluster around moral winters: Chaucer's frozen counsel thawing, Shakespeare's winter-во, Wordsworth's thawing lakes, Frost's spring thaws, and Dickens's unthawable Scrooge whose eventual redemption is itself described as a thaw — he becomes as giddy as a schoolboy when his heart thaws. The arc from unthawing to thawed structures A Christmas Carol.

Note on form: present thaw/thaws, past thawed, participle thawed/thawing. No strong past *thew survives. Pronunciation RP /θɔː/, General American /θɔː/ with r-colour in thawing. The etymology from PIE *teh₂- via PGmc *þaw- is consensus; alternative citation as *ta- is a simplified notation of the same root, not a rival.
