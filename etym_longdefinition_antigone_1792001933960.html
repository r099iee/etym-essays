Antigone (Ancient Greek: Ἀντιγόνη) is one of the most celebrated figures of Greek mythology and classical literature, renowned as the devoted daughter of Oedipus and Jocasta, and the eponymous tragic heroine of Sophocles' landmark drama (c. 441 BCE). In Western cultural, legal, and philosophical history, Antigone stands as the universal archetype of moral conscience, filial piety, and conscientious objection, embodying the irreconcilable conflict between the eternal, unwritten laws of divine justice (agraphoi nomoi) and the arbitrary edicts of secular state power.

The dramatic, ethical, and intellectual dimensions of Antigone are defined across several profound arenas:

  • Mythological Background and the Theban Cycle: Antigone belongs to the doomed royal house of Thebes (the Labdacids). When Oedipus discovered that he had unwittingly killed his father Laius and married his mother Jocasta, he gouged out his eyes and went into exile. Antigone faithfully guided her blind father in his wanderings until his miraculous passing at Colonus. Following Oedipus's death, civil war erupted between her two brothers, Eteocles and Polynices, over the kingship of Thebes. In the ensuing clash (the "Seven Against Thebes"), the brothers killed each other in single combat, leaving their maternal uncle Creon as autocratic ruler of the city.
  • The Sophoclean Conflict and Sacred Burial Rites: Creon immediately decrees that Eteocles shall receive full, honorable military burial, while the rebel brother Polynices shall be left unburied outside the city walls, his corpse exposed to be ripped apart by dogs and scavenger birds—the ultimate desecration in ancient Greek religion. Creon imposes the penalty of public stoning to death on anyone who dares bury the traitor. Fully aware of the fatal consequence, Antigone defies the royal decree. Guided by sacred duty to her ancestral dead, she secretly performs the funeral libations and sprinkles the prescribed three handfuls of earth over Polynices’ body.
  • The Confrontation and Unwritten Laws: Captured by Creon’s sentries and dragged before the king, Antigone displays fearless moral conviction. When Creon asks how she dared break his law, she delivers one of the supreme speeches in world literature:
    "For it was not Zeus who proclaimed these to me, nor Justice who dwells with the gods below... Nor did I think your edicts had such force that you, a mere mortal, could override the unwritten and unfailing statutes of heaven. For their life is not of today or yesterday, but from all time, and no man knows when they first came forth."
    Enraged by her defiance, Creon sentences Antigone to be entombed alive in a subterranean rock cavern. Before Tiresias's prophecies convince Creon to reverse his command, Antigone hangs herself in her tomb, prompting the grief-stricken suicides of Creon's son Haemon (her betrothed) and Creon's queen Eurydice, leaving the tyrant utterly shattered.
  • The Infamous Fraternal Monologue (Lines 905–912): As she is led to her living tomb, Antigone delivers a controversial speech that stunned nineteenth-century scholars (including Goethe). She declares that she would never have defied the state for a deceased husband or child, because a woman may find another husband and bear another child; but with her parents dead in Hades, no other brother could ever be born. This speech directly mirrors the historical dilemma of the wife of Intaphernes recorded by Herodotus (Book 3.119), highlighting the intense cultural reality of archaic sibling kinship over affinal marriage ties.
  • Philosophical and Political Legacy: In modern political and legal philosophy, Antigone has generated extensive theoretical analysis:
    • G.W.F. Hegel: In The Phenomenology of Spirit, Hegel regarded Antigone as the highest masterpiece of human art, viewing the tragedy not as a simple clash between good and evil, but as a dialectical collision between two equally valid ethical powers: the divine law of the family (embodied by Antigone) and the human law of the state (embodied by Creon).
    • Modern Resistance: Antigone became an iconic symbol of anti-fascist and anti-colonial resistance during the twentieth century, powerfully adapted by Jean Anouilh under Nazi-occupied France (1944), Bertolt Brecht (1948), and Athol Fugard (The Island, 1973, in apartheid South Africa).
    • Feminist and Psychoanalytic Theory: Theorists such as Jacques Lacan (The Ethics of Psychoanalysis) and Judith Butler (Antigone's Claim) explore her as a radical figure challenging the patriarchal state and kinship norms.
  • Scientific and Astrological Eponyms: In astronomy, 129 Antigone is a large M-type main-belt asteroid discovered in 1873. In ornithology, Antigone is a recognized genus of large cranes, including the majestic Sarus crane (Antigone antigone), named for its elegant stature and solemn demeanor.

Through two and a half millennia, Antigone has remained the supreme literary personification of individual conscience speaking truth to tyrannical power.
