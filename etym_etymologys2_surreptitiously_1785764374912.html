English SURREPTITIOUSLY (adverb, formed in the 16th–17th c. by adding the adverbial suffix -ly to the adjective surreptitious, first attested late 15th c.) — from Latin surreptitius / surrepticius "stolen, taken secretly; done by stealth; clandestine" — from Latin surripere "to steal, snatch away secretly" (also spelled subripere) — a compound of the preverb sub- "under, from under" (assimilated to sur- before r) + rapere "to seize, snatch, carry off." Stage chain (single linear derivation, merging two PIE roots): 1) PIE *upo "under, below, beneath" (reconstructed; the source of Latin sub) — projected c. 4500–3000 BCE; cognate with Sanskrit úpa "toward, near," Greek hypó "under," Gothic uf "under." 2) Latin sub "under, below, beneath" (preposition and prefix, attested from the 3rd c. BCE onward) — regular development of PIE *upo in Italic; before r the b assimilates to give sur-/su(r)- in compounds. 3) PIE *h₁rep- / *rep- "to snatch, seize, tear" (reconstructed; the second etymon entering the compound) — cognate with Greek harpázō "to snatch" in the same semantic field. 4) Latin rapere "to seize, snatch, carry off" (verb, attested from the 2nd c. BCE; past participle raptus) — development of PIE *rep-; the root of English rape (historical sense "seize, abduct"), rapid, rapacious, rapture. 5) Latin surripere / subripere "to take away underhandedly, steal, pilfer" (compound verb, attested in classical Latin prose, 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE) — literally "to snatch from under," the source of the English adjective. 6) Latin surreptitius / surrepticius "stolen; done by stealth; clandestine; fraudulently obtained" (adjective derived from the past participle surreptus of surripere, imperial/legal Latin, 1st–3rd c. CE) — used of documents, privileges, and writs obtained by concealment. 7) English surreptitious "obtained by stealth; secret, clandestine" (adjective borrowed from Latin in learned legal-ecclesiastical usage, late 15th c.). 8) English SURREPTITIOUSLY "in a secret, stealthy manner" (adverb formed with -ly, 16th–17th c.) — the target root word. Note on debate: The PIE reconstruction of the first root is given as *upo (also written *úpo with stress); the second is reconstructed both as *rep- and, with laryngeal, as *h₁rep-; handbooks differ on the laryngeal. The assimilation of sub- to sur- before r is regular and undisputed. The English chain from surreptitious (15th c.) to the adverb (16th–17th c.) is secure; the adverb's earliest attestations cluster in the 1600s in legal and literary prose.
