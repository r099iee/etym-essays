- The noun cock (the male fowl) is in English from Old English; its expressive, bird-call origin is felt for centuries.
- The verb to cock (16th–17th c.) borrows the rooster's erect, alert posture: to set something up, turn it up, raise it jauntily — cocked hat brims, cocked heads, cocked ears.
- The gun sense follows the same metaphor: the hammer drawn back rises like a comb, and a cocked firearm is raised to readiness (17th c.).
- The idiom half-cocked (17th c.) transfers the misfiring gun to premature action and becomes a permanent part of the language.
- Today the rooster is forgotten; cocked head is neutral description, cocked gun technical, and half-cocked a durable idiom for acting before all is ready.
