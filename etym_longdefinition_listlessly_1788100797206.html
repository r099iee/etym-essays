Listlessly is the adverb formed from the adjective listless, and it describes the manner of someone — or something — that acts, moves, or waits without energy, interest, or desire. To do a thing listlessly is to do it slackly, mechanically, and without appetite: the hands go through the motions, the eyes rest on nothing, the voice carries no intent. The word is built from the obsolete noun list, meaning “desire, pleasure, inclination” (a relative of modern lust), plus the privative suffix -less, “without”; so a person who is listless is literally “without desire”, and anything done listlessly is done without wish, motive, or zest. The whole family — list, listless, listlessly — therefore encodes an absence: not the absence of ability, but the absence of wanting.

Primary sense: absence of energy and interest. In current prose, listlessly describes conduct drained of vigour and purpose: staring listlessly out of a window, turning pages listlessly, dragging listlessly through chores, or wagging a tail listlessly. It is a behavioural word, not a medical one; it records how someone appears to an observer, not what is wrong inside. It differs from slowly, because the slowness is charged with apathy; it differs from tiredly, because the fatigue seems to reach the will as well as the muscles; and it differs from indifferently, because it keeps a note of pathos — the listless actor is pitiable or worrying, not merely rude.

Secondary senses and the semantic bridge. Historically the word travelled from “heedless, careless, paying no attention” (its first Middle English sense) to “lacking vigour and desire” (its modern centre), the bridge being the visible body: when desire fails, attention wanders, limbs slacken, and the outward signs converge. Modern secondary uses include:

  • Physical lassitude — limbs, gestures, and whole bodies move listlessly; animals, invalids, and the exhausted are its natural subjects.
  • Mental and sensory drift — a gaze, an ear, or an attention that wanders listlessly: hearing without listening, looking without seeing.
  • Clinical and reportorial register — doctors and journalists use listlessly of the ill, the depressed, or the failing: a listless economy, a listless child, a listless campaign; the word observes behaviour rather than diagnosing a cause.
  • Critical and sporting idiom — actors, teams, and governments act listlessly when they perform without commitment; here the word shades into “without spirit, without fight”.

Register and boundaries. Listlessly is standard English with a distinctly literary flavour: it is less clinical than apathetically, less physiological than lethargically, and less hostile than indifferently or half-heartedly. It is the adverb of the blank stare. In Oscar Wilde’s The Happy Prince it appears at the exact emotional centre of the story — starving children looking out listlessly at black streets, desire and hope used up, and only the empty, unmotivated waiting of listlessness left behind.
