Old Norse gnastan 'a gnashing, the grating of teeth' (echoic/onomatopoeic noun; attested; built on the verb gnesta, and felt across the Germanic north as natural mimicry of the sound of grinding teeth — no PIE ancestry, see note below) → Old Norse gnesta 'to crack, snap, gnash' (echoic verb; attested) → Middle English gnasten 'to grind or strike the teeth together' (attested from c. 1250; borrowed via Anglo-Scandinavian contact in the northern dialects) → Middle English gnashen 'to grind the teeth' (variant with -sh-, attested from c. 1375; felt as a truer mimicry of the drawn-out grating sound, and gradually displacing gnasten through the 14th–15th c.) → Modern English gnash 'to grind the teeth in rage, grief, or pain' (weak verb) → English gnashed (regular past tense and past participle in -ed; the weak suffix seals the act as one finished event) → GNASHED NOTE / NUANCE: Gnash is sound-symbolic (echoic, onomatopoeic). It has NO Proto-Indo-European ancestry and no Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognates. The Germanic languages independently invented nearly identical words for the same sound — Middle Low German gnisten, Dutch knarsen (knarsetanden), German knirschen (Zähneknirschen) — but these are siblings by sound, not descendants one of another. The Old English native word for the same act was gristbītan 'to bite a grinding', from grist 'a grinding' and bītan 'to bite', which the Norse loan eventually displaced.
