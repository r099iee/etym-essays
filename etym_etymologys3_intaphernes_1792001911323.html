The historical personal name Intaphernes represents a Greek transcription of the prestigious Old Persian aristocratic name Vindafarnā (cuneiform: 𐎻𐎡𐎭𐎳𐎼𐎴𐎠), attested authentically in the rock inscriptions commissioned by Darius I at Mount Behistun around 520 BCE. In column IV, line 83 of the monumental trilingual text, Darius lists the names of the six Persian grandees who assisted him in overthrowing the Magian Gaumāta: "Vindafarnā nāmā Vāyaspārahyā puça Pārsa" ("A man named Vindafarnā, son of Vāyaspara, a Persian"). The name is an aristocratic theophoric compound deeply grounded in Indo-Iranian religion, ideology, and heroic onomastics.

Philological analysis reveals the dual Indo-European constituents of the original Old Persian name:

  • The First Element (*vinda-): Derived from the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *weyd- ("to see, know", and in its nasalized present stem *wi-n-d-, "to find, discover, acquire"). In Sanskrit, this root yields vindáti ("he finds, acquires, gains") and the sacred noun Veda ("knowledge"). In Old Persian, the participle or present stem vinda- signifies "finding", "attaining", or "he who obtains".
  • The Second Element (*farnah-): Reflects the Proto-Indo-Iranian religious concept of *pʰar-nas-, cognate with the sacred Avestan term xᵛarənah- (Old Persian: farnah-, Middle Persian: xwarrah, New Persian: farr). In Zoroastrian theology and ancient Iranian royal ideology, farnah denotes the mystical, luminous "divine grace", "royal aura", or "glory" bestowed by Ahuramazda upon legitimate kings and favored champions. A ruler who possessed the farr was victorious, prosperous, and divinely sanctioned; if he committed grave sins or hubris, the farr departed from him like a bird.
  • The Compound Synthesis: Synthesized together, Vinda-farnā signifies "He who attains divine glory", "He who finds royal splendor", or "Recipient of divine luster". It was a name befitting an elite member of the imperial high command.
  • Phonological Adaptation into Ancient Greek: When fifth-century BCE Greek historians, most notably Herodotus of Halicarnassus, transcribed Persian court names into the Ionic and Attic Greek alphabets, they encountered major phonetic discrepancies. Ancient Greek lacked the voiced labiodental fricative /v/ or labial approximant /w/ (the archaic digamma having disappeared from eastern dialects). Consequently, the initial Old Persian syllable Vi- was Hellenized using the vocalic onset Ἰ- (I-), accompanied by an epenthetic nasal dental cluster resulting in Ἰντα- (Inta-). The Iranian aspirate and liquid cluster -farnā was assimilated into the Greek second-declension nominal system as -φρένης (-phrénēs) or -φέρνης (-phérnēs) (under the influence of Greek phrēn, "mind/spirit"), yielding the classic Greek form Ἰνταφρένης (Intaphrénēs).
  • Transmission into Latin and Modern Western Languages: In Roman historiographical compendia and classical Latin translations, Greek Intaphrénēs was regularized into third-declension Latin as Intaphernes (genitive: Intaphernis). Through humanist Latin translations of Herodotus during the Renaissance—most notably by Lorenzo Valla in the fifteenth century—the name was transmitted directly into English, French, and German classical scholarship.

The survival of the name Intaphernes illustrates the remarkable journey of an authentic Achaemenid royal name across linguistic frontiers. From an Old Persian rock inscription celebrating aristocratic glory to a Greek moral drama of kin loyalty, the name remains permanently inscribed in world history.
