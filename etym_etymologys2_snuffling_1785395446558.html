CHAIN for SNUFFLING (non-PIE; imitative origin): 1. Expressive imitative nasal base *snu- / *snuf- "transcription of a wet, congested nasal sound" — no PIE etymon is reconstructable; the OED classifies the whole family as "expressive of nasal sounds", and the base recurs independently across Germanic as parallel formations. 2. Middle English snuffen "to draw air in through the nose" (attested from the 14th–15th c.); the sense behind snuff-taking and the transferred phrase snuff out "to choke out a flame". 3. English snuff (v.) "to inhale through the nostrils" (hence snuff-box, snuff-taking). 4. English snuffle (v.) "to draw air in noisily and repeatedly through the nose" (16th c.) — formed from snuff + the frequentative suffix -le (as in waddle, crackle, wrestle), marking repeated or continued action. 5. English snuffling (present participle) — sense 1 "breathing noisily through a congested nose" (16th c.); sense 2 "smelling about, nosing out a scent" (the sense in which the Wargs go snuffling about each tree). 6. SNUFFLING "breathing noisily through the nose, as while smelling something" (present participle used as verb or adjective). NOTE ON PARALLEL FORMATIONS: the frequentative pattern is not borrowed. Dutch snuffelen "to snoop, sniff" and German schnüffeln "to sniff, snoop" are independent imitative formations with the same base and the same frequentative suffix; they are parallel cousins, not ancestors. Related English family members — snout, snot, sniff, snore, snort, snivel — share the same expressive base but are not ancestors of snuffle.
