COAGULATION is the process by which a liquid changes into a solid or semi-solid mass, typically because particles dispersed or dissolved in it clump together and separate from the surrounding medium. The prototypical and most famous instance is blood: when a wound bleeds, blood coagulation (clotting) turns the liquid blood into a gel-like plug that seals the break. But the same word describes the curdling of milk into curds, the setting of egg white when cooked, the congealing of sap or latex, and the clumping of colloid particles in chemistry. The common thread in every sense is a change of physical state achieved by particles coming together into a dense mass, rather than by simple cooling or evaporation.

In the supplied literary context - 'The sudden [coagulation] of the vital humours led to a serious disruption of the body's internal balance' - the word is used in its archaic medical sense. Galenic medicine taught that health depended on the free, balanced flow of the four humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile); if those humours coagulated, they ceased to flow, clogged the vessels, and destroyed the body's harmony. The sentence therefore turns a precise scientific term into an image of catastrophic internal stasis: life's fluidity hardening into rigid blockage. The metaphor remains available today whenever we speak of the 'coagulation' of ideas, of society, or of institutions - a fluid vitality congealing into immobility.

The full polysemy of the word is best surveyed sense by sense.

  • Medicine / haematology (primary modern sense) - The cascade of biochemical events by which blood changes from liquid to gel, sealing wounds and stopping haemorrhage (haemostasis); failure of coagulation causes bleeding disorders such as haemophilia.
  • Chemistry / colloid science - Coagulation is the destabilisation of a colloid so that its dispersed particles clump into larger masses and settle out, as river mud flocculates or latex coagulates when acid is added.
  • Food science - The curdling of milk by rennet or acid in cheese-making, the setting of egg protein when heated, the firming of tofu from soymilk, and the gelation of sauces and custards.
  • Archaic medicine / alchemy - The thickening and solidifying of the body's humours; a concept central to humoral theory, where coagulated humours were blamed for tumours, gout, blockages, and 'melancholy'.
  • Metaphorical use - Any process by which something fluid - an idea, a group, a system - becomes fixed, rigid, and unchangeable; 'the coagulation of doctrine', 'the coagulation of a crowd into a mob'.

Coagulation is distinguished from its near-synonyms by three features: it is (1) a phase change in which a liquid actually passes into a solid or semi-solid state; (2) driven by the coming-together of particles, proteins, or molecules, usually by chemical or biological action; and (3) in its technical uses, essentially irreversible. Simple thickening, by contrast, merely raises viscosity and can be reversed; congealing suggests solidification by cooling; solidification is the general term for any liquid-to-solid change (including freezing), of which coagulation is a chemically specific subset.

Usage: in ordinary speech coagulation is now chiefly scientific, and for blood the everyday word is clotting; for milk it is curdling; for fat and gravy it is congealing. The abstract noun coagulation is preferred wherever the process is discussed as a process - in haematology, chemistry, food technology, and, with deliberate archaic colour, in historical or literary evocations of humoral medicine, exactly as in the supplied sentence. Its clinical formality is what gives such literary uses their eerie, antiquarian flavour.

The word is a survivor of an older scientific vocabulary: its history begins in Roman cheesemaking, where rennet - Latin coagulum - was the mysterious substance that 'drove together' the dispersed curd. That agricultural origin survives in the word's very structure, and it explains why coagulation, despite its clinical sound, still carries the memory of farmhouse dairies and shepherd's cheeses in its syllables.
