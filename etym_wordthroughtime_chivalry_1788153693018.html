- [12th–13th c.: Old French chevalerie = the body of knights, the knightly estate] - [Perception: a military and social fact of the feudal world, nothing moralistic yet]
- [13th–14th c.: English chivalrie — "knightly deeds, the profession of arms"] - [Perception: heroic, aristocratic, a little rough; Richard the Lionheart is its type]
- [14th–15th c.: the code crystallises] - [Perception: orders like the Garter, the Golden Fleece; the ideal of honour, loyalty and courtesy becomes a written field of conduct]
- [16th–17th c.: the satirical turn] - [Perception: Cervantes mocks it in Don Quixote; the word begins to smell of the old-fashioned and the absurd]
- [18th c.: the sentimental revival] - [Perception: chivalry becomes the polite courtesy of the gentleman — Burke praises "the age of chivalry", and almost everyone is ironic about doing so]
- [19th c.: romantic restoration] - [Perception: Scott, Tennyson and the Pre-Raphaelites re-crown the knight errant; chivalry is again noble, spiritual, picturesque]
- [20th–21st c.: the contested ideal] - [Perception: "chivalry is dead" is a male lament or a female verdict; historians use the word coldly, and manners debates argue whether the courteous protector is a gentleman or a patriarch]
