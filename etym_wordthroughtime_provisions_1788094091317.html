- Foresight (14th c.): ME 'provisioun' means prudence — seeing ahead; also the Pope's 'provision' of a benefice, a flashpoint of medieval English politics (Statute of Provisors, 1351)
- Things provided (15th c.): the abstract act becomes a concrete thing — a store, a supply, something laid in beforehand
- Victuals (15th–16th c.): the plural 'provisions' hardens into the word for food and drink for journeys, armies and ships; exploration narratives make it a staple of the sea
- The legal clause (17th c.): 'under the provisions of...' — a stipulation provided for in advance in a law or contract; meanwhile 'providence' has split off as the theological word
- The accountant's provision (19th–20th c.): finance sets aside 'provisions' for future liabilities; 'provisional' means temporary, pending confirmation
- Today: the concrete plural sounds slightly old-fashioned — 'supplies' and 'groceries' dominate daily speech — which gives 'provisions' a literary, adventurous flavour (Tolkien's boats 'laden with... many provisions') while the singular thrives in law and finance
