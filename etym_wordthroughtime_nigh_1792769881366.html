- [Old English Era (c. 700–1100)] — Attested as nēah, functioning as the foundational positive adverb, adjective, and preposition for spatial, temporal, and social closeness — [perception: core everyday vocabulary of rural life, landscape navigation, and heroic Anglo-Saxon poetry]
- [Middle English Period (c. 1150–1500)] — Spelled variously as nigh, negh, neigh, or ney; formed an essential morphological triad with comparative near (nearer) and superlative next (nearest) — [perception: universal, unexceptional descriptor of physical, emotional, and spiritual proximity in Chaucer and Wycliffe]
- [Early Modern English (c. 1500–1700)] — Its own historical comparative form near gradually usurped nigh as the primary positive descriptor in everyday speech; nigh retreated into biblical prose, high poetry, and dramatic verse — [perception: acquired an elevated, scriptural, and solemn tone, celebrated in the King James Bible and Shakespeare]
- [18th–19th c. Industrial & Victorian Eras] — Receded from standard urban spoken English into Northern English, Lowland Scots, and Appalachian rural dialects; widely used in literature for rustic color and ominous prophecy (the end is nigh) — [perception: recognized as rustic, dialectal, or portentously archaic, evoking ancient country wisdom or doom-laden preaching]
- [Modern & Contemporary Usage (20th–21st c.)] — Primarily survives in fossilized idioms (nigh on, well-nigh, draw nigh) and fantasy/historical literature (such as Tolkien's Shire dialogue) — [perception: charmingly archaic, evocative of pastoral heritage, venerable age, and atmospheric suspense]
