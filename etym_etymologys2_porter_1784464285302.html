Chain for the carrier / doorkeeper / beer cluster. Because the modern word converges from two distinct Latin sources, each root is given its own linear chain, followed by the metonymy that created the beer sense. Chain 1 — Latin portāre 'to carry' (the carrier; and, by metonymy, the beer) 1. Proto-Indo-European *per- 'to lead, pass over, go across' (reconstructed, c. 4500–3000 BCE; Pokorny; de Vaan). The same root also underlies Latin porta 'gate' and portus 'harbor', and Greek peírein 'to traverse'. 2. Latin portāre 'to carry, bear, convey' (first-conjugation verb; Classical Latin, attested from the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE, e.g. in Plautus); derivative portātor, later portitor 'carrier' (Late Latin acc. portitōrem). 3. Old French porteour / porteor 'carrier, bearer' (12th century), from Late Latin portātōrem. 4. Anglo-Norman portour / porter 'carrier of loads' (12th–13th century). 5. Middle English porter 'one who carries burdens' (attested from the 13th century). 6. Early Modern English porter 'luggage-carrier, attendant' (16th–17th century). 7. 18th-century London: porter's ale, then porter 'the dark ale drunk by street and river porters' (first recorded c. 1721–1722) — a metonymic transfer of the occupational name to the beverage. 8. Modern English PORTER (the beer sense, alongside the carrier sense). Chain 2 — Latin porta 'gate' (the doorkeeper) 1. Same Proto-Indo-European *per- 'to lead, pass over' (see Chain 1). 2. Latin porta 'gate, city gate, door' (Classical Latin); Late Latin portārius 'gatekeeper' (attested in Late Latin texts). 3. Old French portier 'gatekeeper, doorkeeper' (10th–12th century). 4. Anglo-Norman / Middle English porter 'doorkeeper, gatekeeper' (attested 13th century; the form merges with the carrier word of Chain 1). 5. Modern English PORTER 'gatekeeper, doorkeeper'. Notes on debate and nuance: The two Latin etyma (portāre and porta) are historically distinct words that converge on the identical English spelling, which is why dictionaries list them as separate homonymous entries (porter n.1, n.2, n.3). Both nevertheless descend from the same PIE root *per-. The beer sense is securely documented as a transfer from the occupation ('porter's ale', 1722), although the popular story that brewer Ralph Harwood 'invented' porter at the Bell Brewhouse in Shoreditch around 1722 is traditional and historically debated. Old French manuscript spellings vary (porteour, porteur, portour, portur); English stabilizes on porter.
