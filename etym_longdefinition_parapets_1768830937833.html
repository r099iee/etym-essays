parapets (plural noun; singular parapet) — /pˈaɹəpɪts/ — low protective walls or breast-high barriers along the edge of a roof, bridge, balcony, terrace, rampart, or other elevated structure, serving to guard against falling, to screen from view, or, historically, to shelter soldiers from enemy fire.
  • Primary sense — architectural safety walls. A parapet is the low wall that runs along the outer edge of a roof, bridge, balcony, or parapet-walk, protecting those behind it from falling. It is distinguished from a railing by being solid masonry or stonework rather than bars; it may be plain, battlemented, or pierced. In the card’s passage — “He climbed the parapets of his castle and stood under the far stars” — the word names the chest-high walls of a castle’s high places, the edge between safety and the void, where a man can stand and look out at the world.
  • Secondary sense — military breastwork. In fortification, the parapet is the low wall or mound of earth at the top of a rampart or on a defensive line behind which defenders stand to fire and be sheltered: the “breastwork” that comes up to the chest. The English word is borrowed from Italian parapetto, literally “chest-warder” — parare “to defend, ward off” + petto “breast” — a wall that guards the breast. This military sense is historically the earliest in English (1580s) and remains standard in military engineering.
  • Tertiary and extended senses. The word extends to analogous protective structures: the low wall at the edge of a bridge, the guarding stonework above a cornice, the raised edge of a ship’s deck (in naval usage, the bulwark), and, in surveying and mountaineering, the low wall or ridge on which a person stands or on which an instrument is mounted. Figuratively, a “parapet” may be any protective barrier, though this use is rare.
  • Semantic bridge. The compound is transparent: Italian military engineers of the 15th–16th centuries named the chest-high defending wall parapetto — a thing that “parries the chest” — joining the verb parare (“to prepare, to shield, to ward off,” from Latin parāre) to petto (“breast,” from Latin pectus). The defensive military image came first; the modern architectural safety-rail sense is a peaceful extension of the same chest-high wall from rampart to rooftop.
  • Distinguishing boundaries. Parapets differ from ramparts (massive broad embankments surrounding a fort), from balustrades (rows of small pillars or balusters supporting a rail), from bulwarks (heavy defensive works or the sides of a ship), and from battlements (parapets with alternating solid merlons and openings, or crenellations, specifically designed for shooting). A parapet is the plain, chest-high, solid wall — the quietest and most elementary of defences.
  • Usage and register. The word is technical but widely understood, at home in architecture, military history, travel writing, and poetry. The plural form used in the card — “parapets” — is the natural word for the low walls running around the heights of a castle, and its Latin-derived gravity suits the archaic, high style of the passage, in which a wizard stands alone among the far stars above his castle’s edge.
The parapet is thus the simplest of boundaries: a wall high enough to guard the chest, low enough to look over — at once protection and vantage, shelter and lookout. In the reading passage it is both at once: the wall that keeps the climber safe, and the place from which he can stand and breathe the air of ancient Earth.
