**HORDES — vast, disorderly, and menacing multitudes** At its core, *hordes* (the plural of *horde*) denotes vast, menacing, and undisciplined crowds or masses of people or creatures pressing together as a single hostile swarm — numbers so great that individuals blur into a faceless, violent tide, together with a wildness or lack of civilized order that makes them frightening. The word arrives in English trailing menace: it was first applied, in the travel-writing of the 1550s, to the nomadic tribes of the Asiatic steppe — above all the Mongols and Tatars of the Golden Horde — and from that beginning it has carried an unshakeable overtone of alien, barbaric, overwhelming numbers. In J. R. R. Tolkien's *The Hobbit*, chapter 3, Gandalf runs his anxious roll-call — Thorin, the twelve dwarves, and Mr. Baggins, fourteen in all — and then weighs their predicament: "No ponies, and no food, and no knowing quite where we are, and **hordes** of angry goblins just behind! On we go!" The plural does far more work than "crowds" would: it images the pursuing goblins as an overwhelming, loosely organized flood of enemies, uncountable and barely humanized, pressing at the company's heels. The tone is dry and almost humorous — a wizard's calm, appalled military report — and the word stands as the paragraph's one ominous flourish, collapsing every danger the company faces — lost, hungry, horseless, trapped — into a single noun. **The family of crowd-words, and how horde stands apart** - A *crowd* is a neutral gathering of persons; a horde is vast, disordered, and menacing. - A *throng* is a dense, jostling press of people, but without the wild, uncivilized menace of a horde. - A *mob* is a disorderly, potentially violent crowd, but with urban, European associations (from Latin mobile vulgus, "the fickle multitude"). - A *swarm* is a moving mass of creatures acting as one, like insects; a horde is more human or creature-like and more disorderly. - A *host* is an archaic word for a great army, orderly in conception; a horde is organized by nothing but appetite and pressure. - A *legion* is a disciplined body of troops; a horde has no discipline at all. - A *herd* is a group of animals; a horde, even of creatures, reads as quasi-barbaric and threatening. - *Hordes* alone combines vast numbers, menace, and near-barbaric disorder — which is why it is the perfect noun for angry goblins at a fleeing company's back. **Exhaustive polysemy: every sense the word wears today** - **Sense 1 — the historical, original sense: a steppe nomadic tribe.** From 1550s English, a *horde* was "a tribe of Asiatic nomads living in tents," specifically the Tatar or Mongol tribes; the historical *Golden Horde* (the Mongol-Tatar khanate of the 13th–15th centuries) keeps this sense alive in historiography. - **Sense 2 — the transferred, pejorative sense: any uncivilized gang.** By 1610 the word had broadened to "any uncivilized gang or troop," the semantic bridge being the European perception of the steppe nomads as wild, undisciplined, and rapacious; this is the seed of all modern use. - **Sense 3 — the modern everyday sense: a vast, disorderly, menacing crowd or swarm.** This is Tolkien's sense and the governing sense of this card: an overwhelming mass of people or creatures, always with an edge of threat and disorder, as in "hordes of tourists," "hordes of locusts," "hordes of zombies." - **Sense 4 — jargon and subcultural uses.** In gaming, a *horde mode* is a combat mode pitting players against endless waves of enemies; in biology and ecology, *hordes* of insects (locusts, army ants) denotes migrating masses acting as a single organism; in fantasy and horror fiction, "hordes of the undead" is a stock phrase. All of these run straight down from Sense 2. - **Sense 5 — the linguistic side-branch: Urdu.** The same Turkic root *ordu* ("camp") produced Hindustani *urdū*, literally "language of the camp," so that *Urdu* the language-name is a distant cousin of *horde*; this is etymology, not a modern sense of the English word, but it shows the family's reach. **How the senses hang together** The semantic history is a straight line of generalization plus pejoration. A word that named a specific, real people — the mobile camp-and-army of the Tatar khan — became, through fear and contempt, a word for anything like them: vast, wild, alien, and numerous. The plural *hordes* does the heaviest work of all, because the plural form insists on multiplicity: not a horde but hordes, wave after wave, a tide of enemies that no wall and no count can stop. Every modern sense, from zombie hordes to hordes of tourists, still carries that original freight of the Asiatic steppe: overwhelming numbers, disorder, and menace.
