Hilt is the handle of a sword, dagger, or similar bladed weapon — the grip, together with the guard (and often the pommel assembly), by which the weapon is held, as opposed to the blade itself. It is a precise piece of weapon vocabulary with an archaic-heroic flavour and one famous idiom.

Core sense. The hilt is the whole hand-held assembly at the base of a blade. In a typical sword it comprises the grip (the part actually clasped), the guard or crossguard (the bar protecting the hand), and the pommel (the counterweight knob at the end). By metonymy, hilt can name just the grip, or the handle-complex as a whole; "the hilt of a sword" is the standard phrase.

Register and idiom. Hilt is the technical and literary word of swordcraft, distinct from the everyday handle. Its most famous life is in the idiom to the hilt — "completely, fully, without reserve" — which pictures a blade driven home until only the handle is left showing: "he is committed to the hilt," "armed to the hilt." The idiom preserves an ancient combat image of total penetration.

How it differs from its neighbours.

  • handle — the general term for any tool's grip (a door handle, a cup handle); hilt is specifically the sword or dagger handle-complex.
  • haft — a handle, often of an axe, knife, or spear; overlaps with hilt but is not the sword-default.
  • grip — the holding surface alone, or the way something is held; hilt includes guard and pommel.
  • pommel — the knob at the end of the hilt, only one part of it.
  • guard / crossguard — the bar protecting the hand, only one part of the hilt.
  • blade — the steel cutting part, the opposite end of the weapon from the hilt.
  • stock (of a gun) or shaft (of a spear) — related handle-words for other weapons, not swords.

Polysemy: senses of hilt today.

  • Swordcraft / weaponry (primary): the handle assembly of a sword, dagger, or knife.
  • Metonymic use: "hilt" standing for the whole sword, or for the act of drawing or using it (chiefly poetic and archaic).
  • Idiomatic "to the hilt": completely, to the maximum extent, without reserve; also "armed to the hilt" (fully armed) and "to the hilt" as an intensifier of commitment, cost, or enjoyment.
  • Tool use: occasionally of the handles of sword-shaped tools and knives in craft vocabulary.
  • Figurative modern use: "hilt" appears in fantasy, gaming, and historical writing as a marker of authentic swordcraft; "hilted" (having a hilt, e.g., "a gold-hilted sword") is a poetical adjective.

Historical bridge of the senses. The word is native Germanic, unchanged in shape from Old English hilt/hilte to modern hilt — a rare stability across a thousand years, helped by the fact that French, for all its influence on English weapons-vocabulary, never displaced this native sword-term. Old English could treat the hilt as a multi-part object, which explains occasional plural forms (hiltas, hilte) for the handle-gear of a single sword — the guard, grip, and pommel seen as a set. The idiom to the hilt, attested from the seventeenth century, arose because a sword plunged to the handle has reached its absolute limit: from the full burial of a blade came the idea of doing something fully.

In the card's context. In the dwarves' song — "to hide in gems on hilt of sword" — craftsmanship does not stop at the cutting edge: the handle becomes a jewelled setting where caught light is stored in stone. The bare singular in the formula is generic-epic ("hilt of sword"), the place where wealth meets war-gear in dwarf and elvish light-craft.
