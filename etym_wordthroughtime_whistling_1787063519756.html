- [c. 725, Old English] 'hwistlian' — to whistle; an onomatopoeic verb living in Beowulf's world; 'hwistlende' (whistling) appears of weapons and air
- [13th–14th c., Middle English] 'whistlen' — both the lip-whistle and, newly, the whistle instrument (a pipe or small horn); 'whistling' becomes an action noun
- [16th c.] Proverbs and manners: 'a whistling woman and a crowing hen' — whistling girls judged unfeminine and unlucky; social disapproval sharpens around the act
- [17th–18th c.] Urban and naval life: whistling as a signal among sailors and labourers; on stage and street, the whistler is a lively comic figure
- [19th c.] Steam century: the railway whistle, factory whistle, and whistling kettle are born; 'whistling' becomes mechanical, piercing, and industrial
- [20th c.] Figurative idioms: 'whistling in the dark', 'whistling past the graveyard' (defiant cheer amid fear); bullets, wind, and bombs whistle
- [21st c.] 'Whistle' grows a new civic sense: the whistle-blower who stops play — 'whistling' now spans birdsong, sports, boilers, wind, and the exposing of scandal
