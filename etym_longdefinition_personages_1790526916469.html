Personages (the plural form of personage) in the source passage from Laurence Sterne's A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy (1768) denotes prominent, distinguished individuals of elevated social, cultural, or administrative status whom an eighteenth-century traveller on the Grand Tour was expected or obliged to pay formal court to, call upon, and flatter. Sterne satirically itemises the arduous social and intellectual duties that burden the conventional traveller—sifting traditions, picking up anecdotes, reconciling historical accounts, calling upon local dignitaries (Personages), and managing political eulogies and satires—all of which his own donkey and the unpretentious wanderer are blissfully spared. In this specific register, the word carries a gently pompous, theatrical aura, capturing individuals whose public stature, noble titles, or self-importance make them institutional landmarks rather than ordinary human beings.

In broad contemporary English, Personages refers to individuals who possess notable importance, distinction, fame, or elevated public standing, or alternatively, the characters inhabiting a drama, narrative, or historical tableau. Unlike the plain noun persons or people, Personages is an elevated, Latinate term imbued with gravitas, formal prestige, historical weight, or dramatic detachment. It elevates the subjects above the common crowd, presenting them either as pillars of institutional authority or as figureheads acting out distinct roles upon the societal stage.

  • Primary Socio-Political Sense: Eminent, illustrious, or noble individuals: Men and women of commanding social rank, high public office, royal lineage, or exceptional achievement whose presence commands deference and public recognition (e.g., diplomatic envoys were received by several royal personages; the banquet was attended by leading political and cultural personages). The semantic bridge from Latin persona (an actor's mask) reflects the social reality that eminent individuals wear a formalized public mantle or ceremonial persona that defines their official existence.
  • Literary and Dramatic Sense: Characters or dramatis personae in a work of art: The distinct fictional, allegorical, or historical figures who populate a play, novel, epic, or opera (e.g., the allegorical personages in Spenser's Faerie Queene; the historical personages woven into the historical drama). Here, the word preserves its ancient theatrical root intact: a personage is an embodied role created by an author to enact specific dramatic functions before an audience.
  • Historical and Archival Sense: Celebrated figures from antiquity or bygone eras: Notable historical actors viewed retrospectively through the lens of chronicle, biography, or historiography (e.g., the monumental personages of the Italian Renaissance; venerated personages whose decisions shaped the modern republic). In historiography, the plural personages often carries an air of monumental distance, treating past human actors as emblematic representatives of their epoch.
  • Archaic / Ecclesiastical Sense: Parsonages and benefices (Etymological doublet): Historically in Middle and Early Modern English, personage was frequently used interchangeably with parsonage to signify the benefice, living, tithe-right, or official residence of an ecclesiastical parson (Latin persona ecclesiae). Over centuries of orthographic and phonetic sorting, parsonage was restricted to the clerical residence, while personage ascended to describe secular nobility and social stature.
  • Ironical, Satirical, or Pompous Sense: Self-important or inflated figures: Used with wry, sardonic, or mocking inflection to describe individuals who affect great dignity, pretension, or unearned majesty (e.g., local personages parading their petty authority at the town council). The gap between ordinary humanity and the grand theatrical 'mask' creates immediate comic potential.
  • Physical Appearance Sense (Archaic): Imposing bodily stature, mien, or carriage: In sixteenth- and seventeenth-century literature, a man of goodly personage denoted an individual possessing tall stature, handsome features, and dignified physical carriage. While largely archaic today, traces linger when describing the commanding physical presence of theatrical actors or statesmen.

Stylistically, Personages occupies an elevated, formal, and literary register. It is chosen deliberately over people, notables, or VIPs when a writer wishes to evoke historical ceremony, classical dignity, or ironic detachment. In Sterne's prose, its juxtaposition with a humble mule highlights the absurdity of artificial human hierarchy.
