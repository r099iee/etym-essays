Alsace is a name that describes people, not landscape. Its Old High German prototype, Alisāzzo, is a compound of ali “other, foreign” and sāzzo “sitter, dweller, settler”, a noun from the verb “to sit” that survives in modern German sitzen and in the dialect word Sasse “settler”. The plural Alisazzon would mean “the foreign settlers”, and the territory took its name from them.

The two elements

  • Ali- continues Proto-Germanic *aljaz “other”, from the Indo-European root that also gives Latin alius and alter, English else and alien, and Greek allos. It is a different word from the ala- “all” of Alemanni, a coincidence that has often confused amateur etymologists.
  • Sāzzo belongs to the large family of sit words — Latin sedēre, English sit, German sitzen — and meant an inhabitant or settler, someone who has sat down in a place; the same element survives in the German Sasse “settler” and in the dialect verb sitzen “to sit”.
  • The referent. Who were the “foreign settlers”? The most economical reading is the Alemanni who crossed the Rhine and settled on Roman soil from the third century onward, foreigners from the Roman point of view. A rival reading takes “other” to mean “on the other side”, i.e. the far bank, and treats the name as a relational description of the settlement area. The sources do not decide the question.

Attestation and phonetic development

The name is first recorded in Latin as Alisatia or Alsatia in the early Middle Ages, in saints' lives, charters and royal diplomas. Old High German Alisāzzo and its plural underlie the German Elsass; the initial vowel shifted from a to e, the medial -i- was lost, and the ending was reduced, producing Elsāz and finally Elsass. French took the Latin Alsatia, lost the intervocalic -t-, and formed Alsace with the usual -ce outcome; English borrowed the French form in the sixteenth century and has kept it.

Folk etymology and its correction

Because the German name Elsass contains the syllable Els-, it was early reinterpreted as “the seat of the Els”, i.e. of the river Ill, which flows through Strasbourg. The interpretation is charming and repeatedly appears in local guidebooks, but it is false: the Els- of Elsass is the regular German development of Ali-, and the river Ill has a different, Celtic or pre-Celtic name. The error is a textbook case of the way a familiar element is read back into an opaque name.

History and the name

Alsace's history explains why the name carries such weight. The Alemannic settlement of the third and fourth centuries made the region Germanic-speaking; the Frankish conquest and the Treaty of Verdun attached it to the eastern kingdom; the Holy Roman Empire and the Decapole of ten imperial cities gave it a fragmented, urban character; the French annexation of 1648 and the occupation of Strasbourg in 1681 made it a frontier province. After 1871 the German Empire governed it as Reichsland Elsaß-Lothringen, and the name became a slogan of French revanche; after 1918 it returned to France, was annexed by the Third Reich in 1940, and was liberated in 1944–45.

Cultural and English afterlife

The region's wine, its Alemannic dialect, its timber-framed villages and its storks have made “Alsace” a byword for a picturesque, slightly Germanic France. In English the Latin form Alsatia had a strange second life: from the seventeenth century it denoted the debtors' sanctuary of Whitefriars in London, a legal no-man's-land, and hence any den of dubious immunity; Shadwell's play The Squire of Alsatia (1688) popularised the usage. In British English Alsatian also became the standard name of the German Shepherd dog, a piece of wartime renaming that outlasted the war. These extensions are not etymological, but they show how a border province's name can become a metaphor for exemption, and a dog breed's label.

Caution

The ali- + sāzzo analysis is the standard one and is supported by the oldest Latin forms; the precise reference of “foreign” remains uncertain, and the Ill etymology, though popular, is demonstrably wrong.
