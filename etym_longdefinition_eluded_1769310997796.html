eluded — the past tense and past participle of the verb elude: to have escaped or evaded something — a pursuer, a danger, an obligation, or a question — typically in a skilful, dexterous, or cunning way. The escaping agent frustrates the pursuer's attempt to catch, grasp, or pin it down, often with an air of playful or mocking superiority. In Austen's sentence, Mr. Bennet "eluded the skill of them all": he slips out of his family's cleverest verbal traps as if the whole interrogation were a game he has decided to win.

Core senses

  • Physical escape: to slip away from pursuit or capture — "the suspect eluded the police". The chase is not merely outrun but outmanoeuvred: the pursuer's best efforts come to nothing.
  • Intellectual / mental escape: to fail to be grasped, remembered, understood, or achieved — "the answer eluded him", "the meaning of the poem eluded her". The same verb covers dodging a blow and dodging comprehension; the mind is the newest pursuer.
  • Obligation / consequence: to escape what ought to apply — "he eluded his duties", "to elude the law".

Polysemy and secondary meanings

  • Abstract achievement sense: success, fame, or happiness "eludes" someone — the desired thing stays just out of reach; the emphasis is on persistent failure despite effort.
  • Memory sense: a name or fact "eludes" the speaker — present at the edge of recall but refusing to come to mind; a familiar, mildly comic use.
  • Mocking / tricking sense (archaic): older English (16th–18th c.) also used elude for "to mock, ridicule, or deceive", preserving the playful root sense of Latin eludere "to play out of a game".
  • Legal sense: "to elude the law" or "elude capture" is standard in legal and police usage for evading lawful pursuit or obligation without necessarily breaking a rule head-on.
  • Register: formal and slightly literary; plainer everyday equivalents are "escape", "evade", "dodge", "get away from". The -ed form eluded serves both as simple past ("he eluded the skill of them all") and as a participial adjective (rare: "an eluded grasp").
  • The -ed form in narrative: because eluded is the past tense, it appears constantly in storytelling — detectives, soldiers, and lovers all "elude" or are "eluded"; the form is the natural vehicle for describing a pursuit that ends in failure ("the thief eluded every checkpoint", "the killer eluded the police for years").
  • Difficulty nuance: eluding always implies a real contest of wills or wits. If a task or opponent is merely avoided, the word is avoid; if it is genuinely eluded, the thing resisted serious effort — the difficulty is part of the word's meaning.

Historical semantic bridge

Latin eludere (from ex- "out of" + ludere "to play") originally meant "to play out of a game" — in Roman sport and rhetoric, to step aside so that an opponent's thrust strikes empty air, mocking the effort. The physical dodge was the source image; intellectual bafflement followed when the same verb was applied to arguments, laws, and later to memories and answers. The bridge from "play" to "escape": to slip out of a game is at once a trick and a jest. The -ed inflection is simply the regular English past marker, which attaches to the borrowed verb elude exactly as it does to native verbs — proof that the Latinism had been fully naturalised into English grammar.
