The history of chiefest is really the history of chief, one of the great Romance loanwords of medieval English, plus the small and revealing tale of what happened when English speakers decided that a word meaning “first” was not superlative enough. The form chiefest illustrates three big themes in the history of English: the Norman-French reshaping of the vocabulary, the restless tendency of speakers to intensify words that are already absolute, and the survival of a “redundant” form in high literary style long after everyday speech has abandoned it.

The Indo-European and Latin foundation. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *kaput-, “head”. This root is reconstructible from cognates such as Latin caput (“head”), Old English hēafod (“head”), and Sanskrit kapālam (“skull”), and it may itself be related to an even older root for “to seize, grasp”. The basic sense was simply “head” — the part of the body, and by extension the top or chief part of anything. Latin caput (attested from the earliest Latin) carried all the figurative senses that “head” carries in modern languages: the head of a person, the top of an object, the capital of a column, the source of a river, and — crucially — the chief, the head of a household, tribe or people.

From Latin to Old French. As Latin developed into the Romance languages, caput underwent regular sound changes: the p weakened, and in northern French the word became chief (later modern French chef), while southern French kept the more conservative form cap. In Old French, chief meant “head, head of a group, leader, principal part, beginning, end” — a rich cluster of senses inherited from Latin caput. It is from Old French that the word entered English, carrying the full weight of both the physical and the figurative senses.

Entering English (13th century). Following the Norman Conquest, French words flooded into English, and chief is first attested in English around 1300 in the sense “head, ruler, leader, person in authority”, borrowed from Old French chief. Very quickly the adjective developed: “chief” as “principal, main, most important” appears within a few decades (early 14th century). The noun and adjective then developed in tandem — the noun for the leader, the adjective for the leading thing. The adverb chiefly (“mainly”) is also medieval. From the start, chief as an adjective carried the sense of absolute primacy: the chief city, the chief virtue, the chief man.

The formation of the superlative. Precisely because chief was such a strong word, English speakers in the Middle Ages felt no hesitation in inflecting it: chiefer and chiefest are both attested in Middle English, and the superlative chiefest is recorded from the 14th century onward. It appears in Chaucer and throughout medieval religious and courtly writing. By the Renaissance the form was firmly established in the literary language, used naturally wherever writers wanted the very strongest possible superlative.

The Renaissance and Shakespeare. In the 16th–17th centuries chiefest is entirely normal and frequent in elevated prose and verse. Shakespeare uses it repeatedly — “the chiefest head of all his honours”, “chiefest glory”, “chiefest friend” — and the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611) uses it in phrases such as “the chiefest of ten thousand” (Song of Solomon). It is a mark of the word's success that it occurs in exactly the contexts of high praise and solemn emphasis where the modern ear finds it most archaic: religious, heroic and courtly registers. For a Renaissance writer, “chiefest” was simply the superlative of “chief”, with no whiff of redundancy.

Prescriptive reaction and decline (18th–19th centuries). The 18th-century prescriptive tradition, anxious to rationalize English grammar, attacked forms like chiefest, perfectest and most unique as illogical: since chief already means “first”, they argued, nothing can be “chiefest”. Johnson's dictionary (1755) records chiefest, but such criticisms gradually pushed it out of ordinary prose, and by the 19th century it was largely confined to literary, dialectal or deliberately archaic usage. This is the classic fate of the “redundant superlative”: driven from the schoolroom, it survives in literature as a deliberate stylistic gesture, and its very “redundancy” becomes a rhetorical resource.

Tolkien and the modern revival. In the 20th century, chiefest found a natural home in fantasy and historical fiction, where archaism is a stylistic resource rather than a fault. Tolkien's use in The Hobbit — “O Smaug the Chiefest and Greatest of Calamities” — is a perfect example: the doubled superlative (chiefest AND greatest) piles emphasis on emphasis to create the grand, pompous, flattering tone Bilbo needs. Today the word is understood immediately but recognized as archaic-literary; it signals high style and rhetorical magnification, and it remains a vivid demonstration of how English intensification works — and how grammar itself can become a rhetorical tool.
