Trojans is the plural of Trojan, denoting in its oldest and core sense the people of ancient Troy (Troia, Ilium) — the city in the Troad of north-west Anatolia whose siege by the Achaeans is told in the Iliad. Led by King Priam, Prince Hector, Paris, Deiphobus, Helenus, and allies such as Sarpedon of Lycia and Memnon of Ethiopia, the Trojans are the besieged eastern host, noble, pious to Apollo and Aphrodite, and ultimately doomed when the city falls through the Wooden Horse. In Ruskin's source sentence they are the mass slain: Tydides or Idomeneus always killed twenty Trojans to other people's one — the anonymous many against the named few.

Homer's treatment complicates any simple Greek-versus-barbarian reading. The Trojans speak the same heroic language, observe the same sacrifices, and win the poem's deepest pathos (Hector and Andromache in Book 6, Priam begging Achilles in Book 24). A child's reading, however, registers numbers: heroes need victims, and Trojans supply them. Victorian schoolboys thus absorbed two Trojans at once: pitiable noble foes and countable casualties.

  • Ancient ethnonym (head sense): natives of Troy; e.g. the Trojans defended the Scaean Gate. Adjectival form Trojan: Trojan plain, Trojan War, Trojan prince. Always capitalised in this sense.
  • Roman-descent sense: in Virgil and medieval historiography, Trojans are ancestors of Romans (and by fabrication, of Franks and Britons). E.g. Brutus the Trojan founded Britain. The bridge is Virgilian pietas: Aeneas carries his father and gods from burning Troy to Latium, turning defeat into destiny.
  • Proverbial hard-worker sense: work like a Trojan: to labour indefatigably, honestly, and without complaint. First recorded in early 19th-century sporting and school slang, it derives from admiration for Trojan defence against superior Achaean heroes, not from numbers. E.g. she worked like a Trojan to finish the harvest. The semantic bridge is heroic endurance transferred to everyday toil. Ruskin's sentence inverts the perspective: his Achaean kings out-work even Trojans.
  • Deceptive-infiltrator sense: Trojan Horse, Trojan: a person or thing that subverts from within while seeming benign. From the Wooden Horse (Odyssey 8, Aeneid 2): beware Greeks bearing gifts. Modern extensions include Trojan-horse legislation (a bill hiding unpopular clauses), Trojan malware (software posing as useful), and espionage Trojans. Here Trojans names the trick, not the people: the horse the Trojans dragged in. The bridge is metonymy from users to device.
  • Brand, team, and technical senses: Trojan (U.S. condom brand, 1920s) promises strength and reliability; USC Trojans, Troy Trojans (university teams) claim fighting spirit; Trojan Records (Jamaican label), Trojan asteroid (astronomy: asteroids at Jupiter's Lagrange points named for Trojan heroes), troy ounce (unrelated etymologically but often confused). In computing, Trojan is short for Trojan horse program, distinct from virus and worm because it does not self-replicate. Each shows commercial or technical appropriation of ancient virility and cunning.
  • Lower-case and plural notes: capitalised for people; lower-case trojan is common in computing. Plural Trojans in sport and computing can be countable individuals (fans, infections). Pronunciation RP /ˈtrəʊdʒənz/; singular /ˈtrəʊdʒən/.

Mastering Trojans therefore means holding together Bronze-Age ethnonym, Roman ancestor, Victorian work-ethic proverb, and digital threat in one derivation.
