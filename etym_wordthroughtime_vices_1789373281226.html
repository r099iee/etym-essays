- 14th Century (c. 1300s) - Borrowed into Middle English from Anglo-Norman and Old French (*vice*), heavily anchored in Catholic theology to designate mortal and venial sins, corrupt habits, and the spiritual opposites of Christian virtues.
- 15th to 16th Century - Gains physical embodiment on the English theatrical stage as "the Vice" in morality plays; simultaneously extends into technical vernacular to describe physical defects in horses (equine stable vices) and structural flaws in building timber.
- 17th to 18th Century - Secularized through Enlightenment moral philosophy and essayistic literature (Montaigne, Francis Bacon, David Hume); writers analyze human vices as social hypocrisies, natural psychological excesses, and character weaknesses rather than strictly demonic subversions.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Crystallizes in urban sociology, criminal law, and municipal governance; the collective phrase "the vices" comes to denote commercialized urban evils (prostitution, drunkenness, gambling), leading to the creation of municipal "vice squads."
- Mid to Late 20th Century - Reaches profound literary sophistication in Proustian and modern criticism (separating the author's personal vices from the creative self), while colloquial English heavily softens the plural "vices" to describe harmless guilty pleasures, caffeine cravings, and minor indulgences.
- 21st Century - Operates across two distinct registers: a serious legal/ethical register designating commercialized crime and ethical corruption, and a conversational register denoting everyday weaknesses and lifestyle indulgences.
