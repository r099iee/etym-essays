Origin and the shape of the word. English fury descends directly, through Old French and Latin, from the Latin verb furere, "to rage, to rave, to be mad." Latin furere is itself of debated origin: the most commonly repeated theory links it to an Indo-European base meaning "to blow, to swell" (the idea being that a creature in a rage swells up and foams at the mouth), but the link is not universally accepted, and some authorities mark the word's origin as simply unknown. What is certain is that by the classical period Latin had the noun furia, "violent passion, rage, frenzy," and the plural Furiae, the goddesses of vengeance whom the Greeks called Erinyes.

The mythological channel. The mythological use of Furiae is decisive for the history of the word. In Greek tragedy and Latin poetry, the Furies pursue murderers and wrongdoers, sometimes driving them mad; they are figures of supernatural, inescapable wrath. When the Romans rendered Greek religion and literature into Latin, furia and furiosus ("raging, mad") became standard words for the state of being seized and possessed by a divine or quasi-divine frenzy. This is why, two thousand years later, "fury" in English can still slide between "personal anger" and "supernatural vengeance."

Into Old French and Middle English. After the fall of Rome, the popular Latin of Gaul developed the form furie, which entered Old French as furie, "fury, rage." The word crossed the Channel in the early fourteenth century (first attested around 1300–1330) as Middle English furie, meaning "fierce passion, anger, rage." Chaucer and the Middle English poets use it both for the wrath of individuals and for the furious forces of the natural world, such as storms and fires. In the religious literature of the period, fury is closely tied to the deadly sin of wrath and to the torments of the damned, which keeps the word's moral weight heavy from the very beginning.

Renaissance and Shakespeare. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries see fury become intensely psychological. Shakespeare uses the word dozens of times, and in his plays it moves freely between personal rage, the frenzy of battle, and cosmic or natural violence: characters speak of being "in a fury," and storms, seas, and passions all rage furiously in his verse. At the same time the humanist revival of classical learning restored the mythological Furies to full literary life, so that English poetry could once again invoke the avenging goddesses by name. The Renaissance also kept alive the old idea of furor poeticus, the "poetic frenzy," so that a poet seized by inspiration could be said to write in a fury.

Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the age of revolution, fury moves decisively into public life. Newspapers describe mob fury, revolutionary fury, and the fury of war; the word becomes a headline staple for storms, fires, battles, and riots. The Romantic poets, meanwhile, love the word precisely because it bridges the human and the natural: the "fury" of a waterfall or a storm is both a feeling and a fact. By the nineteenth century the abstract sense of sheer violent intensity — fury as a property of any raging thing — is fully established.

The twentieth century and today. In the twentieth century fury is democratised and even lightened. The fixed phrase "fast and furious" (which became famous again through the film franchise beginning in 2001) turns the word into a marker of speed and energy rather than anger. Journalism uses "fury" for everything from "fan fury" at a football result to "taxpayer fury" at a scandal, and the mythological Furies live on in high culture, from Aeschylus's Oresteia to modern fantasy and heavy-metal bands. In the twenty-first century fury is the standard headline noun for righteous, collective anger — climate fury, consumer fury, online fury — while keeping its ancient core: a rage that is too big to contain, and that makes itself heard exactly as Smaug's fury rumbles and roars out of the mountain.

  • Semantic drift summary: divine wrath → personal rage → natural catastrophe → sheer intense speed — one continuous thread of "force that cannot be stopped."
  • Attestation notes: first English uses c. 1300–1330; "fast and furious" is recorded from the late 19th century; the adjective furious always stayed alongside the noun.
