DISUNION is a noun denoting the state of being divided, severed, or lacking internal coherence — a condition of discord, fragmentation, or rupture within something that was formerly one. In William Empson's passage it carries a specifically psychological register: "neurotic disunion" names the private disintegration of the self, the inner warring of impulses and faculties that a neurotic mind experiences as a loss of its own unity. By contrasting "neurotic disunion" with "a fully public theological poem," Empson signals that the ambiguity he studies in his final example is objective, shared, and institutional rather than a symptom of personal mental fracture. The word thus works as a diagnosis rather than a mere description of disagreement; it implies a whole whose parts have fallen out of harmonious relation with one another, whether in a person, a society, or a body of belief.

Polysemy and the full range of senses. The word is built from the Latin negative prefix dis- ("apart, asunder") and union ("a joining into one"), and its whole history is a commentary on the two directions in which that combination can be pulled. Because a "union" is a thing that was once made one, "disunion" always describes a fall from unity rather than a primordial state of separation. Its senses include:
  • General / everyday: lack of agreement; the severing of a bond; discord and division among people, groups, or parts of a whole. This is the broad, neutral core of the word.
  • Political: the fracture of a state, federation, or alliance into rival or independent parts; specifically, in American history, the feared breakup of the federal Union, where "disunion" became the charged name for secession and civil rupture.
  • Ecclesiastical / religious: the separation of a church or religious body into factions or schism; in theological prose, the disunion of the communion of believers.
  • Psychological: the loss of inner coherence of the self — the mind at war with itself, the dissociation of its faculties. This is Empson's "neurotic disunion" and it is the most modern sense.
  • Familial / social: estrangement between persons or parties once bound together, as of a family fallen into disunion.
  • Metaphysical / intellectual: the incoherence or internal contradiction of a doctrine, argument, or system of ideas; the disunity of a philosophical position.

Semantic bridges and modern shifts. The historical bridge runs from the concrete act of unjoining to the abstract state of discord. In Late Latin, disunire meant simply "to sever what has been made one"; Old French desunion carried this into "discord, rupture of alliance." In the eighteenth century the political sense hardened around the American republic, so that in the nineteenth century "disunion" was a near-treasonous word for secession — Webster and Calhoun debated it as an abstract menace, and abolitionists were accused of "disunionism." The twentieth century turned the word inward: psychology borrowed the image of a broken whole to describe the self's loss of coherence, and literary criticism (as in Empson) uses it for mental states that resist a unified reading. Today "disunion" retains its formal, often literary flavour; it is rarer in ordinary speech than "division" or "split," and its political fire has largely faded, but it remains the precise word for a unity that is damaged yet still recognizable — oneness that has lost its oneness.

Register, collocations, and usage notes. The word belongs to formal, literary, historical, and political prose rather than casual conversation. It collocates with verbs and prepositions of rupture: "to threaten disunion," "to breed disunion," "to fall into disunion," "disunion within," "disunion among," "disunion between." Common pairings include "civil disunion," "political disunion," "domestic disunion," "mental disunion," and the historic American phrase "the disunion of the states." The noun stresses the second syllable, /dɪsˈjuːnjən/. It is not a synonym of mere difference: where "disagreement" is a clash of opinion, "disunion" is the state of having come apart; where "discord" is active quarrelling among still-joined parties, "disunion" has already severed them.
