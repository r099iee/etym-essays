Nicene (adjective) — Of, pertaining to, or derived from Nicaea, and by far most commonly applied to the Christian Church's great fourth-century doctrinal settlement: the First Council of Nicaea (325 AD), the creed that council promulgated, and the orthodox Trinitarian theology that creed defined. A "Nicene" formulation is one that upholds the consubstantiality of the Son with the Father against Arianism; "Nicene Christianity" is the catholic, conciliar, creedal Christianity of the Roman and post-Roman world.

In the source context. Gibbon contrasts the refined, literate bishops of the Romanized world — German princes "taking part eagerly in disputes touching the Nicene theology" — with the still-pagan kings of Wessex and Mercia worshipping Thor and Woden. "Nicene" here is a synecdoche for orthodox Christian doctrine in its most intellectually demanding form: the Trinity, homoousios, the philosophical creed of the ecumenical councils, an emblem of the civilisation that the heathen Anglo-Saxons had not yet entered. Gibbon's point is that the German courts of the Continent had become the pupils of bishops and the arena of theological debate, while the rulers of Britain still performed sacrifice in the temples of the old gods.

Historical grounding. The First Council of Nicaea was convened in 325 by the Emperor Constantine in the Bithynian city of Nicaea (modern İznik, Turkey) to settle the Arian controversy that threatened to split the eastern churches. The council condemned Arius, who taught that the Son was a created being not of the same substance as the Father, and issued a creed affirming that the Son is homoousios — "of one substance" — with the Father. The creed's clauses were expanded and ratified at the First Council of Constantinople (381), producing the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, still the most widely shared statement of Christian faith and the doctrinal yardstick of orthodox Christianity for sixteen centuries.

Polysemy and modern senses.
  • Nicene Creed: the fixed noun phrase in which the word most commonly appears today — the creed itself, or the adjective qualifying the creed.
  • Nicene theology / Nicene faith / Nicene orthodoxy: ecclesiastical-historical terminology for the orthodox Trinitarian doctrine of Nicaea as opposed to Arianism, in current use in church history, patristics, and theology.
  • Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers: the standard English title of the great 19th-century patristic collection, A Select Library of Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers of the Christian Church.
  • Nicene Christianity: a term in theology and church history for creedal, conciliar, Trinitarian Christianity.
  • Nicaean (distinct adjective): the alternative Latinate adjective (Latin Nicaeanus) usually reserved for the city itself and its history — the Nicaean Empire of the 13th century, the Nicaean school — while "Nicene" gravitates to the creed and the councils.

Semantic bridge. The word descends from the Greek city name Nīkaia, "city of victory", founded and renamed around 301 BC and linked in Greek to nīkē "victory". The shift from "of the city" to "of the creed made at the city" is a classic metonymy: the place of the council came to name the doctrine itself, exactly as "Chalcedonian" and "Constantinopolitan" would after it, so that a Bithynian town gave its name to one of the cornerstones of Christian orthodoxy.
