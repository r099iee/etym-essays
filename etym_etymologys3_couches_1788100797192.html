Latin ground. The verb at the head of this line is Latin collocāre, "to place, put, set in order, station": com-, "together", + locāre, "to place", from locus, "place". Locus itself is a puzzle with no securely agreed Indo-European ancestor (de Vaan marks its etymology uncertain; a link to a root meaning "set, put" is often suggested but unproven), yet collocāre is squarely attested from the classical period: Caesar stations troops, Cicero "settles" a daughter in marriage (collocāre fīliam), and the everyday grammar of placing things properly hums beneath the word. In the spoken Latin of the western provinces, collocāre lost its middle syllable to the syncope that reshaped countless verbs (*collocāre → *colcāre), and Gallo-Romance then palatalised the velar before the -i- of the infinitive ending, producing Old French couchier, "to lay down, to put to bed": the verb of a nurse laying a child in its cradle, and the courtly verb of going to bed — se coucher is still, today, French for "to go to bed".


Into English. English borrowed couchier through the Anglo-French of the court by the 14th century, as couchen: "to lay down, to place". From the first it spread in every direction a laying-down can take: bedding and repose; weaponry — the 15th–16th-century battlefield sense "to couch a lance" means lowering the spear into the horizontal rest and charging; and the ambush sense of beasts settling low in cover, couching in the grass of a lair. It is precisely this animal sense that Wilde needs: his riverhorse "couches there among the bulrushes", lying low and half-hidden — the posture of rest and concealment combined. Heraldry imported the same participle: a beast drawn lying with head raised is "couchant", one more trace of the French verb in the armoury of English.


Words, beds, and surgery. From "to set words down in order" comes the rhetorical sense fixed in the 16th–17th centuries: to couch a speech or letter "in" a certain style is to phrase and arrange it — today's formal passive idiom, "couched in diplomatic terms". The noun couch, "a bed", appears in Middle English almost as early as the verb and extends to every place of repose — the beast's lair, the poet's couch — before settling, in the 19th century, on the upholstered sofa of the parlour; by the 20th century "couch" was the ordinary American word for the living-room sofa (Britain preferring sofa or settee). Medicine borrowed the verb in the 15th–16th centuries for the old cataract operation, "to couch a cataract" — pushing the opaque lens aside with a needle — a practice as old as Celsus and the Sushruta tradition and refined by medieval Arabic oculists; the verb survives in histories of ophthalmology, and in this very reading passage it sits next to the word "cataract". Psychoanalysis then gave the noun its most famous furniture: Freud's couch, whence "on the couch" = in therapy by the mid-20th century.


Twentieth-century collapse and comedy. The dignified old verb of resting and lurking largely left everyday speech in the 1900s, surviving in idioms and literary narration, while the noun became the centre of a comic empire: the "couch potato" — coined in California in the 1970s and broadcast across 1980s television culture — is the sedentary television-watcher, the ancient posture of repose reduced to an emblem of laziness, and "couch surfing" (21st-century slang) means staying, ungratefully, on friends' sofas. One small irony completes the picture: couch grass, the creeping weed, is no relative at all — it is "quitch", from Old English cwicu, "living, quick", reshaped by folk etymology. The verb form couches, third person singular, now does its quiet work chiefly in literary prose like Wilde's and in the fossil phrase "couched in …".


  • Eras: Latin collocāre → Vulgar Latin *colcāre → Old French couchier → Anglo-French → Middle English couchen → modern English couch / couches.
  • Shifts: syncope of -loc-; palatalisation of /k/ to /tʃ/ before -i-; the -ier infinitive levelled to -en then -∅; the third-person singular -es added to the verb.
  • Culture: courtly bedding, lances, ambush and heraldry, formal rhetoric, cataract couching, the Freudian couch, the couch potato.
