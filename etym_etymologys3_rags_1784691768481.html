The biography of rags begins in Scandinavia, among sheep and rough wool. The Proto-Germanic base *ragga- denoted anything shaggy, rough, or tufted — an uneven, hairy surface of projecting fibres — and Old Norse preserved it in rögg, "a shaggy tuft of coarse wool". When the Scandinavian settlers of the Danelaw brought their dialects into northern England, this word travelled with them, and by the early fourteenth century Middle English ragge meant a torn strip of cloth, a shred, or a tattered garment. The borrowing is typical of the deep Norse layer in English: everyday, concrete words for rough and homely things (compare the sky, the window, the egg, and — significantly for the same root — the rug, a coarse woollen covering taken into English from the same rögg in the sixteenth century). The semantic path runs from texture to damage: the original sense of shagginess, of a rough tufted edge, became the modern sense of a torn, frayed remnant, the rough edge being precisely the shared idea.

  • Sound and shape. The Scandinavian word rögg, with its rounded vowel and geminate -gg-, settled into English as ragge with a short a, the familiar English spelling winning out over the Norse rounded vowel. The plural rags adds the regular English plural -s. The family kept close company: ragged, the adjective, preserves the old rough-edge sense, while rag, the noun, travelled from tuft to shred.
  • Semantic drift: from tuft to poverty. From "torn strip of cloth" the word spread, by the plural rags, to mean ragged clothing and so poverty itself: "in rags" has been the phrase of destitution since the sixteenth century, and the idiom "from rags to riches" completes the arc in the nineteenth. The adjective ragged kept the older textural sense: a ragged edge is a rough, jagged, uneven one, and a ragged line of troops is a straggling, uneven one. The word thus holds both ends of its history — the tuft of wool and the torn garment — inside a single family.
  • Socio-cultural spread. Rags ramified into dozens of idioms and institutions. The rag-and-bone man collected rags for paper-making; ragtime music took its name from the "ragged" or syncopated rhythm; "red rag" is a flag of revolution and, in the proverb "a red rag to a bull", a thing that provokes; "to rag" someone is to tease or berate them, and a "rag" in British slang is a newspaper of the more sensational kind; at British universities a Rag is a charitable fundraising carnival. Each of these reaches back, through metaphor, to the torn scrap of cloth.
  • Literary attestations. The plural rags for ragged clothing is all but proverbial in English literature — the "rags and tatters" of the destitute, the rags of sorrow and of royalty ("the beggar in rags"). In The Hobbit, Tolkien reaches back past the poverty to the texture: the "flying rags" of the night sky are not ragged people but wind-torn strips of cloud, and the transfer shows how deeply the word had soaked into the language — a sky becomes a tatter of linen.

The word's extraordinary productivity is the key to its history. A single Norse word for shaggy wool has given English a noun for torn cloth, a noun for poverty, an adjective for jagged edges, a verb for teasing, a name for a musical style, a word for a newspaper, and an image for wind-torn clouds — every one of them held together by the picture of a rough, frayed, torn surface. Rags, in the passage, is the youngest of these uses and the oldest of them at once: the storm-cloud strips are literally "ragged" in the oldest, textural sense, and their flying tatters are the same image a Norse farmer would have seen in a tuft of coarse wool.
