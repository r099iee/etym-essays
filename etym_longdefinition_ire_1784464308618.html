IRE (n.) — intense anger, wrath, or fury; a formal, elevated, and often literary noun denoting a strong, usually publicly visible fit of anger. IRE is not the everyday word for being annoyed: it names anger at its most concentrated, whether felt by a monarch, a mob, a god, or a dragon, and it is the anger that observers can see gathering and breaking. Primary Sense — Intense anger, wrath, fury
  • Definition: keen and violent anger; wrath; rage regarded as a state or an outburst.
  • Core examples: 'the ire of the king', 'to arouse the ire of the crowd', 'drawing the ire of critics'.
  • Register: formal, literary, journalistic; the noun of headlines and epic verse rather than of quarrels in the street.
Secondary and extended senses
  • Journalistic / rhetorical ire: in modern prose and news writing ire commonly denotes acute, righteous, or formidable public anger ('the proposal drew the ire of residents', 'a verdict that stoked public ire'); it is used because it is compact, emphatic, and slightly old-fashioned enough to command attention.
  • Theological and legal ire: historically the wrath of God, 'divine ire', and the ire of judges and monarchs; in older religious and legal texts ire is a sanction, a force of retribution as much as an emotion.
  • Humoral / physiological ire: in classical and medieval moral psychology (from Latin īra) anger was tied to the body — the boiling of the yellow bile or choler, a sensation of heat and swelling rising in the breast; this bodily reading survives in phrases describing anger as 'hot' or 'blazing'.
  • Derived and related words in English: irate (from Latin īrātus, 'angered'), irascible (from īrāscibilis, 'prone to anger'), irascibility.
Set phrases and collocations
  • to arouse / draw / rouse / raise / incur / vent one's ire
  • the ire of — standard collocation: 'the ire of shareholders', 'the ire of the electorate'
  • in one's ire — 'in his ire he shattered the glass' (literary)
  • righteous ire — anger felt to be justified by injustice
Semantic reasoning and the historical bridge
  • IRE entered English in the 13th century as a Romance loan, not a native Germanic word: Middle English borrowed Old French ire, itself from Latin īra 'anger, wrath, passion'.
  • Because English already possessed the native Germanic anger-family (anger, wrath, fury), ire entered as a high-register, poetic alternative — the anger of lords, saints, and poets — and it has kept that elevation for seven centuries.
  • The Latin word itself is rich: īra was the great classical noun of the passion, anatomized by Seneca in De Ira (c. 41 CE) and tied in humoral theory to the choleric temperament; those associations of heat, swelling, and justified force still colour how ire feels when a modern writer uses it.
Distinguishing ire from its near-synonyms
  • anger — the everyday, general noun for displeasure; ire is its elevated, literary or journalistic counterpart.
  • wrath — the epic, often divine or vengeful form of anger, Germanic in origin; ire is the formal latinate form, colder and more controlled.
  • fury — frenzied, violent, near-maniacal rage; ire can be cold, deliberate indignation.
  • rage — violent, often uncontrolled anger; ire is more dignified and composed.
  • indignation — anger sparked specifically by injustice or unworthiness; ire need not carry the moral judgement.
Usage notes
  • IRE is a noun only; the corresponding verb is the phrase 'to ire' (rare, chiefly poetic) or, normally, 'to anger' / 'to incense'.
  • Countability: usually uncountable, but 'ires' occurs in poetic plural ('the many ires of the gods').
  • In Tolkien's verse on the card, 'the dragon's ire more fierce than fire' uses the word in exactly its traditional epic register — the formal anger of a monster, hot, blazing, and catastrophic, rhyming with 'fire' to reinforce the heat.
