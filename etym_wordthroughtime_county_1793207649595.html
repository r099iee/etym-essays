- Rome — the companion: Latin comes means a fellow-traveller of the emperor, an officer of the court, with no land attached.
- Franks — the warlord: comes becomes count, a great lord holding a comitatus, a territory of his own to rule and defend.
- 1066 — the renaming: the Normans call the English shire a counte, the count’s domain, and the French word settles over the Saxon soil.
- Middle Ages — the shire at work: county courts, sheriffs (shire-reeves), and knights of the shire make the county England’s working unit of justice and tax.
- 1600s — the export: colonists plant counties in Virginia and New England, and the word sails with the charters to cover a continent.
- Today — maps and matches: English counties survive as cricket teams and postal nostalgia, American counties as court-houses and sheriffs — the count’s riding become Everyman’s address.
