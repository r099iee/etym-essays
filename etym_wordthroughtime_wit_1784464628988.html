Old English Faculty Word - Wit descends from the Old English witt ("mind, understanding, consciousness"), the everyday word for the knowing, perceiving faculty — kin to wise, wisdom, and witness.
Middle English Plural Faculties - In Middle English wit and the plural wits cover mental capacity, the bodily senses as instruments of knowing, and practical cleverness; medieval psychology spoke of multiple "wits" as collectible parts of the self.
17th-18th Century Specialization - Early Modern and Augustan English specialize the singular: wit increasingly means verbal sparkle, epigram, comic intelligence — the age of "wit" as social genius — while the plural keeps the older faculty-sense in fixed phrases.
Contemporary Sense - Today wit means verbal cleverness and humor, while the plural wits survives in idioms of composure and clear mind ("keep your wits about you," "collect my wits").
