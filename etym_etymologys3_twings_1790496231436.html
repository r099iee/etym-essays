The history of twings must be told honestly in two layers: the deep, secure history of twig, and the thin, uncertain history of the nasalised spelling twing/twings that Ruskin happens to preserve.

Twig begins in PIE *dwo-, two — or more precisely the extended form *dwig-, that which forks into two. The semantic logic is visible in the object: a twig is where a branch divides, one becoming two. From this notion come Sanskrit dvi- (two), Greek di-, Latin bi- (from *dwi-), and Germanic *twi- by Grimm's law (PIE d becomes Germanic t). Proto-Germanic formed *twigga-, literally the forked one, with the geminate -gg- and the masculine -a- stem typical of tree-part words. Cognates include Old High German zwig (modern German Zweig, branch), Dutch twijg, and Old Norse kvistr (branch, by a different suffix on the same fork idea). Old English twigge (feminine, plural twiggan) already means what it means today: a small shoot, a switch, a divining rod, a sprig for sweeping or binding.

  • Old to Middle English stability: twigge passes through the Norman Conquest almost unchanged in form and sense, because conquerors rarely rename coppice-work. Middle English twigge/twig appears in gardening, fuel, and punishment contexts: birchen twigges for sweepynge, a rodde of twigges for chastisynge. Phonologically the geminate -gg- simplifies and the final -e drops, giving modern /twɪɡ/. Besoms — brooms made by binding birch or heather twigs around an ash or hazel handle — are the great medieval consumer of twigs, and broom-twig becomes a fixed collocation centuries before Ruskin.
  • Where does the N come from? Northern and Scots dialects frequently insert or preserve a nasal before a velar stop: twig beside twing, parallel to pairs like wig/wink habits in some dialects or frog dialectalisms. The process is sporadic, not a regular sound law, and twing never displaced twig in print. The English Dialect Dictionary tradition records scattered twing forms for a slender shoot or a pincer-like fork, consistent with a local pronunciation fossilised by a literate hearer. Ruskin, a Londoner with Scottish roots recalling a Perthshire-tinged household story, is exactly the kind of writer to hear and keep such a form.
  • Ruskin's bundle (Praeterita, 1880s): the anecdote belongs to the pathology of an evangelical household where a lie is a soul-emergency: the grandfather sends forthwith for an entire bundle of new broom twings. Entire and new both sharpen the cruelty — not a handful of worn sweepings but a full, green, shop-bought bundle. Victorian readers would recognise the implement at once: the birch besom sold by every ironmonger, equally a cleaning tool and a birching rod. The passage is now almost the sole literary attestation of the spelling, which is why modern editors sometimes silently normalise it to twigs while textual scholars retain twings as the authentic reading.
  • Confusion neighbourhood: the spelling sits one letter from twine (string, from twine to twist), twins (double children), and twinges (sharp pains, from verb twinge to pluck). None shares its etymology, and all are vastly commoner, so twings survives in learners' memories mainly as a warning: check the vowels and the final cluster before guessing.

In short, twings is twigs wearing a provincial coat. Its deep root forks back to the number two; its surface oddity belongs to 19th-century dialect notation. To learn it is to learn both a PIE fork and a Victorian household — the green bundle hurrying back in the servant's arms.
