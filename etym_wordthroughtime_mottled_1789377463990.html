- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - The root noun *motley* (Old French *mottelé* / *matelot*) designated the multicolored, parti-colored woollen cloth worn by medieval court fools and jesters.
- Late 16th to Early 17th Century (Tudor & Jacobean Eras) - The past-participle adjective *mottled* was formed by zero-derivation/suffixation from *mottle* (a back-formation from *motley*), applying specifically to fabrics, dyed papers, and variegated animal coats.
- 18th Century (Augustan & Enlightenment Eras) - Natural historians and botanists adopt *mottled* to classify the variegated camouflage of frogs, reptiles, birds, and insects; artisan bookbinders popularize *mottled calf* leather for prestigious volumes.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely used in pastoral literature (Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*, Thomas Hardy) and landscape poetry to describe sun-dappled foliage and amphibian coats; simultaneously enters medical terminology to describe blotchy cutaneous circulation.
- 20th to 21st Century - Cemented in clinical emergency medicine as a primary diagnostic indicator of circulatory shock (*mottling score*); remains ubiquitous in geology, textile design, and biological sciences.
