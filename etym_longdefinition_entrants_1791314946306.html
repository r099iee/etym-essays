The plural noun entrants denotes individuals, organizations, or competing entities that formally enter, enrol in, or gain admission to a contest, educational institution, professional body, examination, or commercial market. In the source sentence—drawn from P. G. Wodehouse, where Bertie Wooster reflects that “the beauty of this scheme of mine was that there was no limit to the number of __________”—the term specifically refers to participants in a competitive betting sweepstake (such as the legendary Great Sermon Handicap). The word carries an official, structured connotation: an entrant is not merely someone who enters a room physically, but someone whose name is formally registered, whose candidacy is accepted, or whose presence inaugurates a competitive standing.

Originating as a French participial borrowing, the noun operates across numerous specialised fields of human enterprise, bridging competitive sport, institutional sociology, labor economics, and modern industrial organization theory:

  • Sporting and contest participants (the primary source sense): Encompasses athletes, racehorses, dogs, chess players, or public contestants whose registration has been recorded for a match, tournament, marathon, or lottery: “over ten thousand entrants completed the London Marathon”; “the dog show attracted prize-winning entrants from across Europe.”
  • Academic and institutional matriculants: Refers to incoming students embarking upon a course of study at a school, academy, or university: “new entrants to Oxford undergo formal matriculation”; “primary school entrants showed remarkable adaptability.” Historically in Scottish universities, first-year undergraduate entrants were classified by this exact title.
  • Labor market and professional recruits: Applied to novices entering a trade, craft, legal bar, civil service, or corporate hierarchy: “new entrants to the medical profession face demanding residency hours”; “wage disparities between established staff and young labor-market entrants.”
  • Industrial economics and corporate strategy (Porter's Five Forces): In modern microeconomics, entrants (or new entrants) refers to new firms penetrating an established industry, bringing additional production capacity and competitive pressure. The “threat of new entrants” is a fundamental concept in strategic management, determining market barriers and profitability.
  • Spatial and legal ingressors (archaic / formal): Historically denoted anyone physically crossing a boundary, threshold, or estate boundary, especially in legal disputes over trespass or right of way.
  • Grammatical dynamics: While the singular entrant highlights the individual applicant, the plural entrants almost invariably evokes a field, a cohort, a pool of applicants, or an aggregate competitive roster. It frequently collocates with adjectives like prospective, successful, potential, late, qualified, and new.
  • Semantic bridge: The word derives from the Latin verb for entering (intrare, from intra, “within”). The semantic transition moves from the concrete physical act of stepping inside an enclosed threshold to the metaphorical act of crossing an institutional boundary, gaining enrolment on an official register, or penetrating an economic domain.

Unlike casual synonyms like players or starters, entrants emphasizes the preliminary administrative act of formal submission, eligibility verification, and inclusion within an organized system.
