grin — A broad smile that reveals the teeth; the facial expression produced when the lips are drawn back and parted so that the teeth show, made in amusement, pleasure, embarrassment, nervousness, hostility, or grim endurance. It is both a noun (a grin) and a verb (to grin, to produce such an expression).

In the source text the usher answers the magistrate Yu-cun with a grin. Yu-cun has just asked, in an uneasy laugh, how he is supposed to settle the Xue manslaughter case. The grin is the answer before any words: a low-ranking insider holds knowledge that outranks the newly arrived magistrate's own — he knows where the criminal has gone, who the kidnapper is, and the whole story of the dead Feng Yuan. The expression is therefore not friendliness but a knowing, gloating, faintly insolent baring of teeth. It mocks the formal dignity of 'Your Honour' while the spoken words stay polite; it is a social weapon dressed as a smile, and the word's ancient snarl-residue (see the etymology fields) is precisely why it is the right term in English translation.

Core meaning and boundaries. A grin differs from a smile in being wider, more open-mouthed, and more legible in the teeth; it is visible from a distance and tends to carry surplus meaning — delight, mischief, malice, or strain. A grin can be genuinely happy (a child's wide grin of delight) but is very often knowing or predatory. Register ranges from colloquial to literary; in narrative prose a bare 'grin' without softening modifiers usually leans mischievous, knowing, or predatory, exactly as in the yamen scene.

Polysemy — exhaustive senses in current English:
  • Broad happy smile (primary sense): a wide, tooth-showing smile of pleasure, greeting, or amusement — 'grin from ear to ear'. This is the neutral dictionary core and the sense behind the short definition.
  • Forced or enduring smile: the fixed smile of someone enduring pain, difficulty, or insult; the idiom 'grin and bear it' (early 19th c.) preserves this sense of grim, resilient endurance.
  • Knowing, mischievous, or predatory smile: a baring of teeth signalling superiority, secret knowledge, or predatory intent — the source-text sense. Set-pieces include the 'malicious grin', the 'sardonic grin', and the 'triumphant grin'.
  • Fixed or deathly grin: the expression of a skull or of someone dead or in collapse — 'the grin of a death's-head'; also the nervous, fixed grin of embarrassment or self-consciousness ('a sheepish grin', 'a nervous grin').
  • Cheshire-cat grin: the disembodied, all-encompassing grin of Lewis Carroll's Cheshire Cat (1865), a culturally fixed image of a smile that persists when all else has faded; the phrase itself is older in provincial British use.
  • Animal residue: historically the word named a snarl or the baring of teeth in rage or threat (Old English grennian), and modern usage still licenses grins in dogs and other animals; to say a dog 'grins' is entirely standard.
  • Verb and derived forms: to grin ('he grinned', 'she grinned at the joke'), the participle 'grinning', and the agent noun 'grinner' are all common; transitive-idiomatic 'grin one's approval' is rare but attested.
Collocations and constructions:
  • with a grin / wearing a grin / a wide grin / a broad grin / a toothy grin / a sheepish grin / a knowing grin / a sardonic grin / a triumphant grin
  • grin from ear to ear; grin and bear it; wipe the grin off one's face; split into a grin; grin like a Cheshire cat
  • verb frames: grin at, grin with delight, grin in embarrassment, grin at a challenge
Register, connotation, and historical note. The word is standard English at every level, but it is never fully neutral: even its happy sense stresses openness and visible teeth, and in negative contexts it suggests insincerity, cruelty, or triumph. In Dream of the Red Chamber, the translator's 'with a grin' is a deliberate choice that lets the usher's teeth do the talking — a smile that asserts knowledge and superiority, exactly the expression a savvy insider wears when he has a newly arrived official at his mercy.
