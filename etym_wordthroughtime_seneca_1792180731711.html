- 17th c. (1600s–1680s) — Recorded by Dutch fur traders as Sinnekens (borrowed from a Mahican Algonquian exonym meaning "stony people"); gradually anglicized as Seneca, stabilized by scholars familiar with the Roman philosopher Seneca.
- 18th c. (1700s–1780s) — Solidified in British imperial diplomacy and American revolutionary treaties as the official name for the westernmost Haudenosaunee nation, feared and respected as a formidable military power.
- 19th c. (1790s–1890s) — Adopted extensively across New York State geography (Seneca Lake, Seneca County, Seneca Falls) and immortalized in the 1848 Seneca Falls Convention for women's suffrage; studied by early ethnologists (Lewis Henry Morgan) for their matrilineal clans.
- 20th c. (1900s–1980s) — Reaffirmed in legal and tribal sovereignty battles (e.g., the Kinzua Dam controversy); recognized in academic literature as a premier historical model of female political participation.
- 21st c. — Embraced in sovereign governance as the Seneca Nation of Indians (Onöndowáʼga꞉), actively revitalizing their language and indigenous constitutional traditions while maintaining strong regional economic presence.
