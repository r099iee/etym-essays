FOIBLES descends from a Latin word whose root is a verb of weeping and lamentation. The chain is one of the clearest in Romance etymology, though it involves a striking semantic shift from "lamentable" to "weak." Chain: 1. PIE *bʰleh₁- "to howl, to cry, to lament" (attested in Latin flēre "to weep," Old Norse blóta; the root denotes vocal lamentation; reconstruction per LIV and de Vaan) — the expressive root of weeping. 2. Latin flēre "to weep, to lament, to bewail" (attested from early Latin, e.g. Plautus, 2nd c. BCE) — the simple verb of weeping. 3. Latin flēbilis "worthy of tears, lamentable, mournful" (derived with the suffix -bilis "able to be, deserving of"; attested classical, e.g. Ovid, 1st c. BCE–CE) — originally "to be lamented," later "weak, faint" (the shift from "deserving tears" to "lacking strength" is a Romance development). 4. Vulgar Latin / Gallo-Romance *flēbilis → *fleible → Old French feble, foible "weak, feeble" (attested Old French 9th–12th c.; the diphthong and loss of -l- are regular Gallo-Romance developments) — now meaning "weak" generally. 5. Old French foible (substantivized adjective) "the weak part," used in fencing of the weak half of the sword blade (attested 16th c. French; the noun use is a technical specialization of the adjective). 6. English foible "the weak part of a sword blade" (borrowed from French, attested 1648, e.g. in fencing manuals) — the literal, technical sense. 7. English foible, figurative, "a weak point of character, a minor failing" (attested 1670s; the transfer from blade to character is a metaphor) — the modern sense. 8. Modern English FOIBLES (plural, "minor weaknesses or eccentricities of character"; in ordinary modern use almost always plural) — the end of this chain. Note on debated stages: the derivation of feeble/foible from Latin flēbilis is the standard and accepted etymology (OED, Etym. Online, FEW); the key nuance is the semantic shift within Latin itself, where flēbilis "lamentable" developed the secondary sense "weak," and it is this secondary sense that survives in French and English. The fencing specialization of the noun (foible as the weak half of the blade) is securely attested in seventeenth-century French and English manuals. No stage is invented.
