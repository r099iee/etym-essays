Linear derivation chain for PLAITED (single root word): 1. PIE *pleḱ- "to plait, to twist, to fold, to weave" (reconstructed root, c. 4500-3500 BCE; Pokorny *plek-; Beekes s.v. Greek πλέκω) - one of the great "fabrication" roots of Indo-European. 2. Latin plicāre "to fold, to bend, to turn; to plait" (Classical Latin verb of the first conjugation, attested from c. 2nd c. BCE, e.g. in Plautus) - the Italic specialization of the root toward folding. 3. Late/Vulgar Latin plicātum, the (neuter) past participle of plicāre used substantivally, "a fold, a plait" -> Old French pleit / ploit "a fold, a wrinkle, a plait of cloth" (vernacular noun; attested in Old French texts from the 9th-12th c.). 4. Anglo-French plait "a fold; a braid of hair" -> Middle English pleit / plait "a fold of cloth; a plait or braid of hair" (attested from the 14th c., c. 1387). 5. English verb to plait "to braid, to interweave, to form into a plait; to fold" (16th c.; the verb is back-formed from the noun; the cloth-folding sense is older within English, the hair-braiding sense emerging as the noun's hair sense grew). 6. English plaited, past participle of to plait, "braided, interwoven, folded into plaits" (attested from the 17th c.) - PLAITED. Note on the derivation: Latin plectere "to plait, to weave" is a sister verb of the same PIE root (it gave English plexus, complex, perplex) but is NOT the direct ancestor of plait; the direct line runs through plicāre "to fold." Note also the spelling split of the 19th c.: the cloth sense was respelled as pleat, while plait remained with the hair sense, so the two modern English words descend from the same Old French noun.
