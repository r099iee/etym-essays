THYME carries its whole history in its scent, for the chain begins not with a plant but with smoke. CHAIN: PIE *dʰeu- "to smoke, rise in a cloud, fume, give off vapour" (c. 4th millennium BCE; the root also underlies Sanskrit dhūmá- "smoke", Latin fūmus "smoke", Lithuanian dū́mai "smoke", Old Church Slavonic dymŭ "smoke", Old English dūst "dust" and dox "dark", Old High German tunst "vapour") → Greek θύειν (thýein) "to burn sacrifice, offer incense, fill with smoke, be furious/raging" (attested from Homer, 8th c. BCE; the same verb gives θυμός thymós "breath, spirit, soul, courage") → Greek θύμον (thýmon) "thyme" — literally "the fumigating herb", the plant burned as incense in temples (attested 5th–4th c. BCE; the -o- grade noun built on θύειν) → Latin thymum "thyme" (borrowed from Greek, 1st c. BCE; also thymus, thybra) → Old French thym "thyme" (learned Romance borrowing, 13th c. CE) → Middle English tyme "thyme" (14th c. CE, spelled without h, pronounced /taɪm/, rhyming with time) → English THYME (15th–16th c. CE: the h restored by Latin scholarship while the pronunciation stayed /taɪm/) NOTE ON DEBATE: The Greek stem is securely attested and the plant-name is securely Greek — thýmon is the normal botanical noun from θύειν. The only debatable point is the deeper PIE reconstruction: the widely accepted root is *dʰeu- "to smoke, fume, give off vapour", whose Indo-Iranian, Italic, Balto-Slavic, and Germanic reflexes (smoke, dust, vapour) are straightforward, but some scholars prefer to derive the Greek θύ- words specifically from a zero-grade *dʰu- and treat the exact vocalism as uncertain. The Greek-to-Latin-to-English route itself is uncontroversial, as is the fact that the English word's modern spelling has an historically restored silent h.
