Single linear chain for the single root word beset: 1. PIE *sed- "to sit, sit down" (reconstructed root, c. 4500–2500 BCE) — one of the most basic somatic verbs of the family, with reflexes in Sanskrit sīdati "he sits" (source of Upanishad, "a sitting down near"), Greek hézomai "I sit," Latin sedeō "I sit," Old Irish saidid, Lithuanian sėdėti, Proto-Germanic *sitjaną "to sit." 2. Proto-Germanic *satjaną / *setjaną "to cause to sit, to set, to place" (reconstructed causative of *sitjaną) → Old English settan "to set, place, put" (attested 8th c. CE) — the ancestor of English "set," "seat," "settle." 3. Old English besettan "to set about, surround, occupy, adorn; to set around" — the prefix be- "around, about" + settan (attested in Old English). The oldest sense is literally "to set around," both spatially (to surround, to hem in) and ornamentally (to stud, to set things about a surface). 4. Middle English bisetten / besetten "to surround, besiege, place, put" (attested 12th–15th c.) — the military sense "to lay siege to, to surround with an armed force" hardened here, matching the continental formations Old Saxon bisettian, Old High German bisezzan, German besetzen "to occupy, garrison." 5. English beset (verb; present besets, past and past participle beset) — attested from the 13th century onward. The modern senses — "to surround and assail," "to trouble or threaten persistently," "to hem in" — all descend directly from the Old English "set about." The chain is thus: PIE *sed- (to sit) → Proto-Germanic *satjaną (to cause to sit, to set) → Old English settan (to set, place) → Old English besettan (to set about, surround) → Middle English bisetten (to surround, besiege) → English beset. No stage is guessed; the weak/strong history of the verb (Middle English bisette as a past form collapsing into the modern invariant beset) is the only noted irregularity. The German cognate besetzen "to occupy, fill, staff" is the same formation on the same root and confirms the Germanic antiquity of the "set around" construction.
