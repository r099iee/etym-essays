First element: mock — from French derision to the prefix of counterfeit The first half of mock-politeness is the verb and combining form mock, and its history is a study in how a language borrows a word it cannot quite trace. Unlike the grand Latinate vocabulary that English took from the classical tradition, mock arrived by the humbler route of Old French, and its deepest roots are so obscure that the honest etymology must begin at the documented French word and flag everything earlier as uncertain. 1. Old French mocquer: the documented start The verb appears in Old French in the twelfth century as mocquer (also moquier), meaning 'to deride, jeer, hold up to ridicule.' The word is vernacular and colloquial from the first — a verb of the mouth and the street, the sound of someone jeering — and it passes into literary use without ever losing that mocking flavour. Its pre-French history is unsettled. The most common proposal connects mocquer to a Germanic verb of grumbling or murmuring (Middle Dutch mokken 'to sulk, mumble,' Middle Low German mucken 'to murmur'), on the ground that derision is closely tied to muttered, contemptuous speech; but the forms do not match perfectly, and many authorities simply describe the word as expressive or imitative — coined from the sound of a derisive snort or mutter. No Indo-European reconstruction for the word family is accepted, and a careful chain therefore begins at Old French and states the uncertainty plainly. 2. Into Middle English: mokken and the birth of the English family Middle English borrows the verb in the fifteenth century as mokken or mocken, 'to ridicule, deride.' From the verb grows a full native family: the noun mockery (a derisive act or imitation), the agent noun mocker, and the participial adjective mocking. The word settles into the language at exactly the moment when English is absorbing huge quantities of French vocabulary, and it becomes the standard verb for derision, competing with the older native scorn and the later learned ridicule. Shakespeare uses it richly — to mock is to imitate in derision, to deceive, or to defy contemptuously (in Macbeth, 'to mock the time' is to trifle with or disregard it) — and the word's theatrical, performance-flavoured use is fully established by the sixteenth century. 3. The combining prefix mock-: sham, counterfeit, performed The crucial development for this card is the adjectival and combining use of mock-, meaning 'sham, counterfeit, imitative, not the real thing.' English begins attaching mock- to nouns in the early modern period to mark a performed or fake version of the second element: a mock-battle, a mock-trial, mock-heroic verse (a style that imitates epic grandeur for comic effect), mock-modesty, mock-solemnity. The logic is transparent and powerful: if to mock is to imitate in derision, then a mock-X is an X performed or imitated rather than genuinely possessed. Mock-turtle soup is a famous case — the 'turtle' is actually veal or calf's head prepared to imitate turtle, so the compound names a counterfeit dish; mock-orange is a shrub whose flowers imitate the scent of orange blossom. The prefix therefore creates a whole vocabulary of performed likeness, and it is exactly this 'performed likeness' semantics that mock-politeness exploits. 4. From mock to the performed courtesy Putting the two halves together, mock- + politeness names courtesy as performance: the surface forms of deference (the bow, the title, the tone, the phrase) put on without the goodwill that genuine politeness is supposed to express. The compound is a transparent formation of modern English, of a piece with mock-heroic and mock-modesty, and its force comes entirely from the prefix's established meaning 'sham, performed.' In Thorin's turn to Bilbo — 'He turned with mock-politeness to Bilbo' after calling him the 'burglar-expert' — the gesture performs respect while really teasing and testing: the polite surface is real, the goodwill is not, and the compound captures the double structure perfectly. 5. Modern sociolinguistic afterlife In late twentieth- and twenty-first-century linguistics, mock politeness becomes a technical term in pragmatics and impoliteness theory, where it names the strategic use of over-polite or conventionally polite forms to mock, insult, or distance — the polite register worn as a weapon. The scholarly use is a direct formalization of the everyday compound, and it confirms how far the two elements have travelled: from an untraceable Old French verb of jeering and a Latin verb of polishing to a precise modern term for the weaponized courtesy of social life. Second element: politeness — from polishing metal to refining manners The second half of mock-politeness is the abstract noun politeness, and it carries a far more classical pedigree than mock does. Its story runs from an Indo-European verb of rubbing and beating, through Latin's image of polishing metal and stone, into French's metaphor of refined manners, and finally into an English noun of quality whose very sound still suggests the shine of a surface rubbed smooth. 1. The deep root: *pel- and the physics of polish The noun politeness descends, through Latin polīre 'to polish, make smooth,' from a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *pel-, with the basic meaning 'to beat, drive' — and, by extension, to pound, rub, or strike repeatedly. The semantic bridge is physical and almost primitive: the way you make a surface smooth is to rub, beat, or grind it, and from that repeated friction comes the gloss of a polished object. The root *pel- is one of the most productive in the family, spinning off words for beating, driving, striking, and their results across the Indo-European languages. It should be noted, however, that modern authorities are more cautious than the older handbooks about polīre's precise pedigree: de Vaan's Etymological Dictionary of Latin treats polīre as of uncertain origin, so the *pel- connection, though classic, is best presented as a proposed rather than a proven stage — an honest caveat that the chain records. 2. Classical Rome: from polīre to polītus Latin polīre is well attested from the first century BCE in the sense 'to make smooth and glossy, to polish,' used of metal, stone, and wood. Its past participle polītus 'polished, smooth' is immediately available for metaphor: a polished style, a polished person. The transfer is the heart of the word's whole career — physical smoothness becomes mental and social refinement. In rhetorical and philosophical Latin, polītus describes the cultivated, elegant, civilized individual whose rough edges have been, as it were, rubbed smooth by education and manners. The image is permanent: refinement is polishing, and the polished are the civilized. 3. French poli and the manners metaphor Old French and Middle French inherit the participle as poli, 'polished, smooth,' and develop the social sense with increasing strength — poli comes to mean 'refined, courteous, well-bred.' English borrows the word in the fifteenth century as polite, first with the physical sense 'polished, smooth' and soon with the dominant social sense 'refined in manners, courteous.' The borrowing is part of the Renaissance fashioning of courtly culture, when English, and especially the language of the court and the gentry, deliberately imported French words of refinement. To be polite is to be polished: the rough edges of behaviour smoothed by breeding and consideration. 4. politeness: the abstract noun of quality From the adjective polite English forms the abstract noun politeness by the ordinary suffix -ness, attested from the late sixteenth century. The noun names the quality or condition of being polite — courteous, considerate, well-mannered behaviour — and it quickly becomes a central term of the Enlightenment's civil conversation. In the eighteenth century politeness is a key word of social theory: the 'polite letters' of the Augustan age, the 'polite society' of the drawing room, the whole ideal of conversation as a polished social art. The word carries its origin on its surface: politeness is the social sheen, behaviour polished to a shine. Etiquette manuals of the nineteenth century institutionalized the ideal, and the word spread from the drawing room into the conduct books, courtesy literature, and educational writing that shaped Victorian domestic life. 5. The compound: mock-politeness as performed polish In mock-politeness, the classical noun meets the French derision-verb, and the compound inherits both histories at once. The 'politeness' element supplies the polished, courteous surface — the sheen of refined behaviour — while 'mock-' supplies the falsity, the performance, the imitation. The resulting compound names the special social phenomenon in which the polished surface is genuine but the goodwill behind it is not: courtesy as mask, deference as tease. It is a modern, transparent formation, but every one of its parts carries a long and separate history — a French verb of jeering of obscure origin yoked to a Latin participle of polishing from an Indo-European root of pounding and rubbing. 6. Thorin's gesture and the double edge When Thorin 'turned with mock-politeness to Bilbo' in the planning scene, Tolkien exploits exactly this double structure. The dwarf king performs the courtesies of a host — the turn, the address, the title 'burglar-expert' — while the compound's two roots (French derision, Latin polish) meet in the gesture: manners as a joke and a test. The reader, armed with the word's history, sees the polished surface and the mockery beneath it at the same moment. The card's hyphenated form preserves the compound's transparency, and the two element-chains — one obscure and expressive, one classical and polished — remain distinct even as the word welds them into a single social gesture. 7. Modern continuities In contemporary English, mock-politeness names both a weapon of everyday social exchange and a serious object of linguistic study. The everyday compound is as productive as ever — mock-concern, mock-sympathy, mock-apology — and the scholarly term has given the phenomenon a fixed name in pragmatics. The hyphen keeps the two-element history visible, and every use of the word quietly repeats the ancient equation: the polish of Latin and the derision of Old French, welded into one performed courtesy.
