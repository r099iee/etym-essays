- Old English — the rider's hardware: spora names the prick on the heel, and to spur is the horseman's everyday verb in a kingdom that lives by cavalry.
- Medieval — chivalry gilds it: golden spurs become the very badge of knighthood — to win one's spurs is to earn honour — and chroniclers spur horses across every battlefield page.
- 16th–17th c. — the metaphor mounts: preachers and poets spur dull minds, slow friends, and lagging verses; the heel leaves the stable for the soul.
- 18th–19th c. — industry takes the reins: inventors, prices, and railway manias are spurred; the verb becomes the favourite of economists describing any sharp stimulus.
- 20th c. — motivation's spur: coaches spurring teams, tragedies spurring reforms, hits spurring sequels — the horse quietly retires while the psychology gallops on.
- Today — the trigger-word: spurred headlines every acceleration from house prices to protests, while riders still spur actual horses — the literal and figurative galloping side by side.
