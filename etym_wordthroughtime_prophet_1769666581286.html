- [Medieval religious use] - Entered English via Old French and Latin from Greek prophētēs ("one who speaks for another"); strictly a divinely inspired person who proclaims the will of God — a solemn, reverent, sacred word.
- [16th–18th c. figurative widening] - Broadened beyond Scripture to name any inspired foreteller or interpreter of hidden truths; "prophet" began to describe people who warn of coming events with moral urgency, gaining a portentous, ominous tone.
- [Contemporary sense] - The religious sense remains primary, but a strong figurative register thrives — "prophets of doom," tech visionaries, and cultural critics — so the word now swings between sacred authority and a faintly ironic label for anyone confidently predicting the future.
