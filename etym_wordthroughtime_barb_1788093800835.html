- [c. 1300: "a beard"] - Borrowed from Old French barbe "beard"; a plain, everyday word for facial hair, with no weaponry association in the minds of English speakers.
- [late 16th century: "point of an arrow"] - English adopts the French archery term: a barb is the backward-pointing spike that makes an arrow hold fast; the word moves from the chin to the arrowhead.
- [17th–18th centuries: tackle and anatomy] - Extends to fish-hooks and harpoons; naturalists apply it to the beard-like branches of feathers and the spines of plants — the beard metaphor spreading across the natural world.
- [19th century: steel, wire and words] - "Barbed wire" (1862) arms the American frontier and then the trenches of the First World War; simultaneously "a barb" becomes a stinging verbal criticism — words as arrows.
- [20th–21st centuries: two lives] - In public life "trading barbs" is the standard idiom of political sparring; in slang "barbs" are barbiturate pills; in archery, fishing and ornithology the literal sense never dies — and Tolkien's "barb, shaft and feather" keeps the full ancient arsenal in view.
