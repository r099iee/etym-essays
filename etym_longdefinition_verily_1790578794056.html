verily in the source passage — from Laurence Sterne's Tristram Shandy (1759): “...Had they duly weighed and considered all this, and proceeded accordingly, — I am verily persuaded I should have made a quite different figure in the world from that in which the reader is likely to see me.” — functions as an emphatic modal adverb modifying the participle persuaded, meaning truly, certainly, genuinely, or in absolute truth. Tristram uses the adverb to assert his profound, unwavering conviction regarding how differently his life would have unfolded had his parents exercised proper philosophical mindfulness during his conception. The choice of verily conveys a blend of solemn rhetorical earnestness and self-conscious literary irony, characteristic of Sterne's playful engagement with philosophical and theological prose styles.

Historically, verily is an adverb formed by attaching the native English adverbial suffix -ly (from Old English -līce) to the Anglo-Norman French adjective verrai (Modern French vrai, meaning "true," derived from Latin vērus). It represents a classic Middle English hybrid borrowing where a Romance root replaced native Germanic truth-words while adopting Germanic grammatical machinery. In modern English, verily is classified as an archaic or literary adverb, functioning primarily to express solemn affirmation, indisputable reality, or emphatic certainty.

The historical and lexicographical polysemy of verily encompasses several distinct registers and functions:

  • Epistemic Certainty and Unwavering Conviction (Source Sense): Modifying verbs of mental belief, knowledge, or persuasion (such as verily persuaded, verily believed, verily thought), signifying total intellectual and emotional certainty free from all doubt or reservation. It affirms that the speaker is speaking from deep, unfeigned conviction.
  • Sacred and Prophetic Affirmation (Biblical / Liturgical Sense): Immortally established in English biblical literature through William Tyndale's translation and the King James Bible of 1611, where "Verily, verily, I say unto you..." serves as the solemn formula rendering the Greek double Amēn, amēn (Hebrew 'āmēn, meaning "truly, certainly, let it be established"). In this liturgical register, verily carries the weight of divine truth, prophetic authority, and inviolable decree.
  • Solemn Oath and Testimonial Assertion (Legal / Forensic Register): Historically used in legal affidavits, oaths, and sworn testimonies to certify that the deponent speaks the absolute truth without deceit or equivocation (e.g., "deponent verily believes the contents of the petition to be true").
  • Intensifying Adverb of Factuality (Truly / Indeed): Functioning as a sentence adverb or clausal modifier meaning "in truth," "in reality," or "truly," used to emphasize the reality of an extraordinary fact or assertion (e.g., "he was verily a giant among men").
  • Literary, Antiquarian, and Mock-Heroic Register: In post-eighteenth-century literature, romantic poetry, and historical drama (such as the works of Sir Walter Scott, Lord Tennyson, or Robert Browning), verily is employed deliberately to evoke medieval, biblical, or chivalric atmosphere. In satirical and modern comedic writing, it is often utilized mock-heroically to parody pompous solemnity or archaic gravity.

Stylistically, verily carries a profound resonance of sacred history and classical English prose. While everyday discourse has long since replaced it with modern equivalents like truly, really, certainly, indeed, or in truth, verily remains uniquely evocative in English literature, combining the moral gravity of the Latin root verus with the historic cadence of the King James Bible and early modern masterworks.
