Linear derivation chain for EXPULSION: • Proto-Indo-European *pel- (also reconstructed *pelh-) — 'to drive, push, strike, thrust' — the root of driving and striking motion. (Reconstruction; attested indirectly through its Italic, Hellenic, and other reflexes.) • Latin pellere — 'to drive, push, strike' — Classical Latin verb. (Attested from the 3rd–2nd century BCE onward.) • Latin expellere — ex- 'out' + pellere — 'to drive out, thrust out, expel.' (Attested Classical Latin; used of driving enemies, cattle, and bodily matter.) • Latin expulsus — past participle of expellere — 'driven out, expelled.' • Latin expulsiō (genitive expulsiōnis) — 'a driving out, expulsion' — a noun formed on the participle stem -puls-. (Attested Classical Latin.) • Old French expulsion — 'expulsion, a driving out' — the Romance reflex inherited by Middle English. (12th century.) • Middle English expulsioun — 'expulsion, casting out' — attested from the 14th century, chiefly in medical and theological texts. • English EXPULSION — the modern form, with the full range of physical, medical, institutional, and political senses. (15th century onward, in present-day form.) Note on the debate: the chain is entirely well-attested and uncontroversial. The only point of scholarly discussion lies at the PIE level: the root *pel- (sometimes written *pelh₁-) is reconstructed on the basis of Latin pellere, Greek πάλλω pallō 'to brandish, poise', and a range of other Italic and Hellenic forms, and its precise laryngeal shape (whether *pelh₁- with a following h₁) is debated among specialists, but its existence and its 'drive, strike, push' semantics are secure. No stage of the English descent is in doubt.
