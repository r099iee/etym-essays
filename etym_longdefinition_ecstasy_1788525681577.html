ecstasy (noun; plural ecstasies) — An overwhelming, intense state of sudden emotional transport, transcendent joy, supreme delight, or rapturous elevation that completely absorbs the consciousness; historically and etymologically, a state of profound mental displacement, trance, or mystical absorption in which the soul or mind is perceived to "stand outside of itself," detached from normal sensory perception, bodily limitations, and mundane rationality.

Primary sense — overwhelming joy and emotional transport. In modern general usage (and specifically in Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose), ecstasy designates the peak experience of sublime pleasure, emotional intensity, or aesthetic delight. When the blossoming red rose "trembled all over with ecstasy," the emotion is depicted as a physical and spiritual convulsion of pure, ecstatic vitality born of supreme music and sacrificial love. In this sense, ecstasy exceeds ordinary happiness or contentment by several orders of magnitude: it is all-consuming, electric, and breathtaking.

Sense 2 — mystical and spiritual union. In historical theology, philosophy, and religious mysticism (from Neoplatonism to Christian, Sufi, and Hindu traditions), ecstasy (Greek ἔκστασις, Latin ecstasis) denotes the transcendent state wherein the individual ego, intellect, and physical senses are suspended, allowing the soul to experience direct contemplation or unmediated union with the Divine. Figures such as Plotinus, Saint Teresa of Ávila, and Saint John of the Cross described ecstatic rapture as an out-of-body spiritual elevation accompanied by divine illumination.

Sense 3 — psychiatric and historical medical usage. In older medical and psychiatric treatises (from Galen through the 19th century), ecstasy was classified as a specific trance-like disorder, cataleptic state, or temporary delirium characterized by total immobility, fixed gaze, insensibility to external physical stimuli, and intense internal preoccupation. The semantic bridge to joy arose because patients emerged reporting visions of celestial bliss or unearthly beauty.

  • Pharmacology and club culture — in late 20th-century popular culture and pharmacology, Ecstasy (often capitalized) became the ubiquitous street name for MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine), a synthetic entactogen and empathogen renowned for inducing feelings of intense euphoria, emotional openness, sensory enhancement, and social closeness.
  • Poetry and the Romantic Sublime — in literary theory, ecstasy represents the poet's state of creative frenzy or inspired transport (the Platonic theia mania or "divine madness") where rational control yields to spontaneous poetic genius, as celebrated in John Donne's metaphysical masterpiece The Extasie and in the odes of Percy Bysshe Shelley and John Keats.
  • Erotic and sensory transport — describes the climactic heights of romantic or sensual passion where boundaries between self and other temporarily dissolve in mutual rapture.

Register and semantic evolution. The word has traveled an astonishing trajectory: beginning in Classical Greece as a clinical or terrifying term for "madness, terror, and displacement of mind," it transitioned through medieval mysticism into the supreme aesthetic and emotional noun for radiant bliss and romantic fervor in the English language.
