[ROOT CORE (PIE): *bhel- (to bloom, swell, sprout; leaf)] — branch of FOLIOS
├── Latin: folium (a leaf — of a plant; a sheet or leaf of paper)
│ └── Italian / Late Latin book trade: in folio (in folded-leaf format) ──> folio
│ └── Early Modern English (15th–16th c.): folio (a leaf of paper; a large-format book)
│ └── Modern English: folios (plural) ◄ TARGET
└── Cognates: Greek phýllon (leaf, in chlorophyll, philodendron); the Germanic branch of *bhel- gave Old English blæd (leaf/blade) and English bloom, blossom, blade.
Note: The printing sense is precise: a folio is a sheet folded once to make two leaves (four pages) — the largest and grandest book format. The semantic path "plant leaf → paper leaf → folded-sheet format" is transparent and uncontested.


[ROOT CORE (Latin, pre-Italic): liber (inner bark of a tree ──> writing surface ──> book)] — branch of LIBRAMS
├── Latin: liber (the inner bark of a tree; a written document; a book) — pre-Italic origin debated, possibly from PIE *leubh- (to peel, strip) but no secure PIE etymon is agreed
│ └── Modern literary coinage by Jack Vance (The Dying Earth, 1950): libram (a pseudo-Latin archaism modelled on liber, meaning an ancient book of magic)
│ └── Modern English (in Vancean usage): librams (plural) ◄ TARGET
└── Related genuine Latin words: liber, librī (book), librārius (copyist, bookseller), liberta — and English library, libretto, librarian — all from the same bark/book stem.
Note: libram is NOT a standard dictionary word; it is a deliberate Vancean coinage whose internal derivation (liber + -am) is the author's invention. The prehistory of Latin liber itself is genuinely debated — some scholars derive it from *leubh- "to peel, strip bark," others from *leudh- "to grow," and no consensus exists — so the chain for the real Latin word stops at liber with that debate noted.


[ROOT CORE (PIE): *h₂eǵ- (to drive, lead, move)] — branch of COGENT
├── Latin: agere (to drive, lead, do, act)
│ └── Latin: cōgere (con- "together" + agere "to drive" = to drive together, collect, compel)
│ └── Latin present participle: cōgēns, cōgent- (driving together, compelling)
│ └── English (mid-17th c.): cogent (forceful, compelling — applied to arguments and evidence)
│ └── Modern English: cogent ◄ TARGET
└── Cognates: Latin agere gave English agent, action, act, agenda; cōgere gave coaction, and coercēre ("to enclose, restrain," from cō- + arcēre) gave English coerce; Greek ágein (to lead, in pedagogy, strategy, synagogue); Sanskrit ájati (drives, leads).
Note: The semantic development is transparent and uncontested: an argument that "drives" the parts of the case together into one compelling whole is cogent. The modern restriction to "logically convincing reasoning" is an 18th–19th century narrowing of an originally broader "compelling, forcible."
