tapestries (noun, plural of tapestry) — heavy, richly woven decorative fabric hangings, usually pictorial, hung on walls for insulation, display, and storytelling. In this passage the word appears in Peter's imagination of the hall at Cair Paravel—"put tapestries on the walls, and you get our royal banqueting hall." The specific nuance here is architectural and ceremonial: tapestries are the great woven hangings that clothed the stone walls of a medieval or royal hall, and Peter names them as one of the furnishings that transform a roofless ruin back into the banqueting hall of a king. The word therefore carries the sense of courtly luxury and memory—textiles that both insulated the cold stone and told stories, for real tapestries were pictorial, depicting hunts, battles, and legends in wool and silk. Within the context the tone is that of a boy trying to restore the lost grandeur of his own past; the tapestries are tokens of a vanished civilisation, and the very word, with its old-fashioned, exotic cadence, evokes heraldry, candlelight, and the woven histories of a royal house. 1. The primary craft sense
  • Technique: a true tapestry is a weft-faced weaving, often large enough to cover a wall, in which the coloured weft threads are woven over and under the warps and are worked so densely that they hide the warp entirely; the design is built into the fabric itself, not stitched on afterward. The weaving is executed from a full-scale cartoon, usually on a vertical loom, and may take years to complete.
  • Materials and makers: traditionally of wool with silk, silver, and gold thread for highlights, produced in workshops such as those of Arras, Tournai, Brussels, and the Gobelins in Paris; a wall tapestry is generally distinguished from a carpet by being hung, not laid on a floor.
  • Function: tapestries warmed and insulated cold stone halls, advertised wealth and rank, and told stories—hunts, battles, legends, biblical scenes—so that a hall hung with tapestries was literally a woven chronicle of a royal house. This is exactly the sense in the passage: tapestries are what make a bare hall a royal banqueting hall.
2. Secondary and modern senses
  • Figurative sense: since the 19th century, "tapestry" names any interwoven whole made of many threads—"the tapestry of history," "the rich tapestry of life," "a tapestry of voices"—so that the word stops naming only cloth and comes to mean any complex whole whose parts are woven into one picture.
  • Technical transfers: "tapestry" and "tapestry needle" are used for related textile arts such as needlepoint and Berlin wool-work, which are embroidery rather than true weaving; "tapestry carpet" and "tapestry upholstery" use woven-patterned cloth; in botany and computing the word is occasionally borrowed for anything densely patterned or interwoven (e.g. a "tapestry" of data or ecosystems).
  • Historical name-transfers: the Bayeux Tapestry (c. 1070s) is technically embroidery—stitched wool on linen—yet is universally called a tapestry, showing how the word functions as a general name for a large pictorial narrative textile; "tapestry" also names the decorative hangings of any courtly or ecclesiastical interior.
  • Register: the plural form, as used in the passage, has a ceremonial, slightly archaic ring, fit for heraldry and chronicles; in ordinary modern speech "wall hanging" or "artwork" has partly displaced it, which is why choosing "tapestries" deliberately recreates a royal, old-world atmosphere.
3. Semantic bridge
  • The historical bridge runs from floor to wall to metaphor. The word family began as the woven covering of the floor (Greek τάπης, "carpet"); in the Middle Ages the craft migrated to the wall, and the name followed it, so that a tapestry is a carpet that stood up and learned to tell stories; and in the modern era the word migrated once more, from cloth to any interwoven whole. Every sense preserves the core idea of threads woven together into a dense, decorative, meaningful surface.
