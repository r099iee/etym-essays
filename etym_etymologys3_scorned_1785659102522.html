The cutting root. The biography of scorned begins in the act of cutting. The word descends from the Germanic "mockery" family, and that family is connected, probably though not certainly, to the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)ker-, "to cut, shear," the same root that gives English shear, share, shard, sharp, and scurf. The image behind the connection is striking: to mock a person is to cut their reputation to shreds, to shear away their standing as a beast is sheared of its fleece. The reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *skarną, "mockery, derision," points back to this idea of cutting, and it is from a West Germanic form of that family — Frankish *skern, "mockery, jest, derision" — that the word passed into French. Into French. The Franks who ruled Gaul spoke a Germanic dialect, and their word for mockery, *skern, was borrowed into the Gallo-Romance speech of the ninth and tenth centuries as escarn. Old French escarn (noun) meant "mockery, derision, insult, contempt," and the verb escarnir meant "to mock, jeer at, hold in contempt." This was a word of the medieval court and the battlefield — the vocabulary of troubadours and chroniclers — and it carried the active, mocking contempt of public derision, the laughter and the jeer of the hall. Across the Channel. Anglo-Norman carried escarnir to England, and Middle English took the noun scorne and the verb scornen around the year 1200, in the same era that brought England the chivalric cult of honor in which being scorned was among the worst fates that could befall a knight. From the start the English word did double duty: it meant the active mockery of public derision, and it meant the cold, proud refusal of something judged beneath consideration. It was in the latter sense that the word acquired its moral weight. In the Bible of 1611, the Authorized Version, "scorners" are the proud who mock what is good, and "to scorn" counsel or instruction becomes the mark of the wicked; the word moves steadily from the noise of the jeering crowd to the cold judgment of the righteous man. The passive turn: from verb to badge. The crucial development for this card's word was the solidification of the participle. Scorned, as past tense, describes the act of refusal ("he scorned all offers of compromise"); but as an adjective it describes the state of the person who has been judged worthless — the "scorned woman," the scorned lover, the scorned prophet or statesman whose dignity has been cut away. In revenge tragedy and romance, from the sixteenth century onward, the scorned figure becomes an engine of the plot: the memory of public judgment converts contempt into fury, and the scorned lover is proverbially the most dangerous of creatures. This passive, biographical sense is the one most alive in modern literary English, and it is the sense at work in the card's heroic sentence, where the verb, paradoxically, describes an act of self-respect: to scorn a hollow peace is to refuse what would cheapen one's honor. A false friend to reject. One tempting etymology must be firmly rejected. Because Old French also had escorner, "to cut off the horns" (es- + corne, "horn," from Latin cornu), and because "horn" is the ancient emblem of pride, a folk-etymology has long claimed that scorn descends from the dehorning of a proud beast. The forms are genuinely similar, and the idea is seductive — but the attested history of the words shows that escarn (the mockery word) and escorner (the dehorning word) were different verbs with different sources, and standard dictionaries derive English scorn from the Germanic *skern family, not from cornu. The horn story belongs to the folklore of etymology, not to its scholarship. Sound and later history. In sound, the Middle English scorne lost its final vowel and settled as the modern monosyllable /skɔːn/, with the participle scorned /skɔːnd/. By the nineteenth century the verb had become slightly archaic in everyday speech — the Victorians preferred "despise" and "disdain" for the attitude and "scoff" or "jeer" for the noise — but in narrative, oratory, and poetry the word never lost its force, because no other English word combines quite so tightly the ideas of public mockery, proud refusal, and the injury of being judged worthless. That combination is what the participle scorned still delivers today, and it is why the word remains the property of writers who need to say, in a single syllable, that a man held something beneath him and would not reach for it. Key points. - Germanic origin: Frankish *skern "mockery" → Old French escarn → Middle English scorne (c. 1200). - Probable PIE link: *(s)ker- "to cut" — the mockery is a verbal shearing; the link is likely but debated. - The folk-etymology via Latin cornu "horn" (escorner) is false and should be rejected. - Semantic arc: public jeering → proud refusal → the passive badge of humiliation (the scorned lover). - The participle's modern heroism: "he scorned all offers of compromise" reads as self-respect, not spite.
