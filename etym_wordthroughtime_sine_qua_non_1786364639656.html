- Roman Classical Era (c. 1st c. BCE - 2nd c. CE) - Latin Grammatical Construction: Formed as an ablative phrase in Latin syntactic logic, literally meaning "without which not," appearing in philosophical analysis and formal legal jurisprudence.
- Medieval Scholastic Era (c. 1200 - 1500 CE) - Formal Scholastic Logic & Law: Codified as *condicio sine qua non* in Canon Law, civil jurisprudence, and Scholastic philosophy to distinguish indispensable prerequisite conditions from efficient causes.
- 17th to 18th Century - English Legal and Scientific Adoption: Borrowed directly into Early Modern English legal theory (the "but-for" causation doctrine) and natural philosophy to designate absolute laws of nature and mandatory legal stipulations.
- 19th Century - Victorian Literary & Political Flourishing: Popularized across high-register Victorian literature, essayistic prose (e.g., Thomas Carlyle, John Ruskin), and political theory to designate the core cultural and institutional requirements of civilized life.
- 20th to 21st Century - Universal Intellectual Currency: Maintained across global law, critical theory, medicine, and high journalism as the quintessential Latinism for an irreplaceable, indispensable core requirement.
