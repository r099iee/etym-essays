The word kin is a biography of birth. Its ultimate ancestor is the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *ǵenh₁-, "to produce, beget, give birth," a verb of the most concrete physical meaning — the act of generation itself. From that single verb the whole family pours. In the Indo-Iranian branch, Sanskrit janati (जनति) "begets" (Rigveda, c. 1500–1200 BCE), with janas- "race, people" giving Hindi jan (जन) "person" and jāti (जाति) "caste, community" — the social "birth-group" of Indian society — and Avestan zana- "people, race" appearing in the Persian royal inscriptions. In the Hellenic branch the same root produces genos (γένος) "race, kind, lineage" in Homer, ancestor of modern English gene, genesis, and genealogy. In the Italic branch Latin builds its own empire of the word: gignere "to beget," gens "clan, people" (hence gentle and gentleman through Old French gentil), genus "kind, sort," ingenium "inborn quality" (hence genius), and most strikingly nātio "birth; people" from the older gnātus "born" — the word nation. Everywhere the semantic drift is the same: from the physical act of begetting to the collective so begotten — the race, the kind, the people.

The Germanic line, from which the card's word directly descends, follows the same arc. Proto-Germanic *kunją, "family, kin, kind," becomes Old English cynn, whose range covered "family, race, kind, species" — at once the blood-group and the category. The sound changes are regular: the PIE palatal *ǵ becomes Germanic *k under Grimm's Law, and the reduced zero-grade *ǵn̥- cluster is vocalized and simplified on Germanic soil. From cynn English draws a whole cluster: kindred, kinsman, kinsfolk, kinship, and the legal next of kin. The same root produced Old English cyning "king," etymologically "scion of the kin," the man of noble birth raised to lead the kindred — with cognates German König and Old Norse konungr — and, through the adjective gecynd "innate, natural," the modern English kind, which preserves the ancient idea that one's "kind" is one's born nature. Sibling Germanic languages kept the root for the child itself: German Kind, "the begotten one."

The decisive modern shift is one of narrowing. In Old English, cynn named any category — the kin of animals, the kin of things — but modern English kin has contracted to mean blood relatives only, while the general categorical sense migrated entirely into kind. To be kin, then, is to be "of the same kind" because of a shared origin: the word remains the linguistic record of the foundational bond of the bloodline. The narrowing was accelerated by social history — medieval law and inheritance worked through kin-groups and the payment of weregild within the kindred, which kept the word technical and precise, and later legal English fixed it in phrases like next of kin and kith and kin, where it has survived as a fossil of the old kinship system.

In the literary context of Dream of the Red Chamber, that record does social work. When the narrator says a character "married his own kin, the niece of his Uncle Zheng, strengthening the family's ties," the word measures the marriage against the clan. Endogamous marriage within the kin — cousin marrying cousin — is presented not as scandal but as strategy: the tightening of the family's ties, the consolidation of its property and its power. The etymological depth of kin — the whole "beget" history of birth, race, and nation — underwrites the passage's central claim that the family is a living, self-perpetuating body, and that a wedding inside that body is its most intimate act of self-renewal.

  • PIE *ǵenh₁- "beget" → Sanskrit janati / Greek genos / Latin gens — birth becomes people, race, and nation.
  • PIE *ǵn̥h₁-yo- → Germanic *kunją → Old English cynn — the begotten group becomes the kindred.
  • Old English cynn "any category" → modern kin "blood relatives" — the categorical sense passes to kind.
  • Old English cyning "scion of the kin" → king; gecynd → kind — regal and moral offshoots of the same root.
  • Modern kin in law (next of kin), anthropology (kinship system), and the novel's endogamous marriage.
