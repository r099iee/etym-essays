- Old English — to prosper: spedan means to succeed, and sped is success itself — wealth and luck, not velocity.
- Middle Ages — God speed you: the wish for a prospered journey slowly becomes a wish for a swift one, as success on the road means quick arrival.
- 16th–17th c. — swiftness wins: speed now chiefly means rapidity, and sped is its flying past tense in ballad and Bible.
- 19th c. — railways and telegrams: the sped message, the sped train — Victorian haste gives the old form new iron work.
- 20th c. — the split: motorists get speeded for fines while poets keep sped for arrows, years, and errands.
- Today — literary swiftness: sped survives wherever haste should sound noble — errands, arrows, seasons — while everyday speech says went fast.
