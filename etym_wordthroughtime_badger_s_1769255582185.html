- 15th–16th century coinage — 'badger' appears as the standard English name for the burrowing animal, likely from 'badge': the white-striped face that wears a 'badge'; the older word 'brock' begins to fade into dialect.
- 17th–19th century — the noun is dominated by brutal sport: badger-baiting is a popular pastime, and the word carries associations of the baited, tormented animal and the rough countryside crowd.
- 18th century onward — the verb 'to badger' grows directly out of that baiting: to worry, pester, or hound someone relentlessly, as dogs harry a badger; the word's modern verb life is born.
- Contemporary English — the badger is a protected, much-loved native (Badger of The Wind in the Willows), and the word now sounds cosy and whimsical; the possessive 'badger's' — as in 'a badger's face' — anchors the species exactly while the verb still quietly means 'to pester.'
