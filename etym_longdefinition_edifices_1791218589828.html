Edifices is the plural of edifice: large, imposing, usually permanent buildings, especially ones of stately, monumental or public character. Where building is neutral and house is domestic, edifices signals scale, solidity and dignity — stone temples, government palaces, cathedrals, skyscrapers, or, as in the source passage, five-hundred-foot Iroquois bark longhouses dignified by a classical term.

The word is formal and literary. Everyday speech prefers buildings; journalists, historians and architects choose edifices when they want to stress grandeur, permanence or institutional weight. It is count-noun and almost always concrete in the plural, though the singular readily goes abstract.

  • Core architectural sense: a large and imposing building. Government edifices, public edifices, religious edifices, ancient edifices. Example: the marble edifices of the capital gleamed in the sun. This is the sense in the source: sprawling edifices of bark-covered timber.
  • Historical-monumental nuance: edifices often implies survival and heritage — Roman edifices, Mughal edifices, colonial edifices. The word frames buildings as witnesses of civilisation rather than mere shelter.
  • Singular abstract extension — the edifice of: a complex system conceived as a building: the edifice of democracy, the edifice of modern physics, the edifice of lies, the edifice of marriage. The plural of this metaphor exists — the edifices of power, ideological edifices — but is rarer; writers usually keep the metaphor singular.
  • Ecclesiastical origin sense: in older texts an edifice could specifically mean a church or temple, or the Church as institution — the edifice of God. Modern usage has secularised, but hymns and the King James legacy preserve the echo.
  • Ironic or critical use: crumbling edifices, decaying edifices, soulless concrete edifices — where grandeur curdles into pomposity or failure. To call tower blocks edifices can be praise or mockery depending on adjective and tone.
  • Technical-planning register: municipal codes and heritage listings speak of protected edifices, listed edifices, adjoining edifices, where edifice functions as a precise legal term for any built structure regardless of beauty.

Grammatical and collocational notes: edifices takes adjectives of scale and judgement — grand, imposing, towering, stately, crumbling, derelict — and prepositional complements — edifices of stone, edifices of government. Verbs commonly paired are raise, erect, construct, restore, demolish, dwarf. The source's raised edifices is typical 19th–early-20th-century diction. Number agreement is regular; the singular is edifice, pronounced /ˈedɪfɪs/, plural /ˈedɪfɪsɪz/.

Semantic bridge for polysemy: all senses share the image of a large thing built up course by course. From Roman stone temples to bark longhouses to abstract systems, an edifice is whatever has been edified — built up — whether of marble, timber or ideas. The metaphorical edifice of state works because institutions, like buildings, need foundations, scaffolding, maintenance and can collapse. That single building schema unifies the concrete plural and the abstract singular.
