Die is, in its dominant verbal sense, to cease living: the heart stops, the breath leaves, the organism passes from animate to inanimate. Human beings, animals, plants, and by extension fires, engines, sounds, hopes, and loves all die, and the verb's great power lies in that extension — whatever burns, moves, sounds, or matters can die when its sustaining force fails.

Core meaning in detail: The verb divides into the literal — organisms ceasing to live — and a vast penumbra of figurative dyings: fires die down, applause dies away, batteries die, fashions die out. Two nouns share the spelling: die the gaming cube (singular of dice), a small marked block whose throw decides the game; and die the tool, an engraved stamp or mould that coins medals, cuts threads, or shapes metal — the instrument that gives form by striking.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To cease living (primary verbal sense, from c. 1200): men, beasts, and plants die. The bridge is the word's stark core, borrowed from the Norse, that displaced native swelt and has named mortality ever since.
  • To cease functioning or burning (extended sense): the engine died, the fire is dying, his enthusiasm died. The bridge runs from the body's last breath to any system's last flicker: machines and flames are granted little lives so that they may be granted little deaths.
  • To fade from perception (sensory sense): the sound died away, the light is dying in the west. The bridge is the dying fall: what withdraws gradually is felt to expire rather than merely stop, and poets from Shakespeare to Tennyson have tuned their closes to it.
  • To long painfully, to be dying for or to (hyperbolic sense): dying for a cup of tea, dying to see her. The bridge is desire staged as mortal need: the craving is so fierce that only death is an adequate measure, and comedy lives in the gap between the claim and the survival.
  • The gaming cube (noun, singular of dice, from the 14th c.): to cast a die, the die is cast. The bridge is the throw that gives fortune — from Latin datum, a thing given — so that chance itself is named for the gift of the fall.
  • The coining stamp or mould (noun, technical): the die that strikes sovereigns, the die that cuts a screw-thread. The bridge runs from the same giving root: the stamp gives its image to blank metal, impressing form as the cube impresses fate.

Register and connotation: die the verb is the plain, unflinching word — blunter than pass away, colder than perish, and therefore the choice of soldiers, doctors, and headline writers alike. Its euphemisms multiply precisely because the monosyllable will not soften: fall asleep, pass on, lose, each a veil thrown over the same stone. The nouns are specialist survivors — the gambler's die kept alive by proverb, the engineer's die by the workshop — while the Cobbett spelling in the context reminds us that even the once wore stranger clothes on the page.
