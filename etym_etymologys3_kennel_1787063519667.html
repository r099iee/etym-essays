Kennel is a Latin word for a dog's place that travelled to England through the mouths of medieval French huntsmen. Its history runs straight back to canis, the Latin for 'dog' — the same root that gives canine, canary (from the dog-headed islands), and the poodle's French name — and in its eight hundred years of English life it has moved from the kennels of kings to the boarding-kennel industry of the pet age.

The Indo-European and Latin base: canis. Latin canis, 'dog', descends from the Proto-Indo-European noun *ḱwṓ (stem *ḱun-), the common Indo-European word for 'dog', attested by reflex in virtually every branch of the family — Greek kyōn, Sanskrit śvā, Germanic hund (English hound), and Latin canis. In Latin the dog was canis in all its functions: shepherd's dog, guard dog, hunter's hound, scavenger, pet — and the adjective canīnus gives English canine.

Latin builds a place-word: canīle. From canis, Latin formed a derived noun meaning 'a place where dogs are kept', using the suffix -īle — the very suffix that builds shelters and stalls for animals: equīle, a stable (from equus, horse); bovīle, a cow-byre (from bos, ox); ovīle, a sheepfold (from ovis, sheep). The dog's version was canīle, literally 'dog-place'. Classical authors also used the related canīlia for a dog-hole, and the late Latin world knew the word as the ordinary term for a doghouse or the quarters of a pack of hounds.

Vulgar Latin and Old French: kenil. In the spoken Latin of Gaul, canīle developed into the Romance ancestor that produced Old French chenil (Modern French chenil, still the word for a kennel) and, crucially for English, Old North French kenil — the northern dialect form that the Norman lords and their hunt-servants brought to England. The phonetic journey from canīle to kenil involves the regular Romance weakening of the Latin a toward e, the reduction of the long suffix -īle, and the natural loosening of the Latin accent pattern; the result, kenil, is the direct loan-source of the English word.

Middle English: kenel. Middle English borrowed kenil in the fourteenth century as kenel (also kennel), with the sense 'a dog's house or shelter'. The earliest records are hunting documents and aristocratic household accounts, for the kennel was, in medieval England, primarily the property of the nobility — the place where the packs of hounds that made a lord's hunting possible were kept and bred. A great household maintained its kennel as carefully as its stable, and the word enters English as part of the vocabulary of the chase.

Early modern expansion. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries kennel widened from the lordly hunt to ordinary dog-keeping: any dog-house was a kennel, whether it sheltered a mastiff, a turnspit, or a lady's lapdog. The same period shows the word beginning to be applied contemptuously to human housing — a 'kennel' as a mean, dirty, cramped dwelling — a disparaging transfer that survives in phrases like 'they live in a kennel', meaning in a filthy hovel. The pack sense ('a kennel of hounds') is also recorded, naming the dogs of one kennel collectively.

The modern era: boarding, breeding, and the pet industry. The nineteenth century brought the word's most important modern development: the kennel as a business. With the rise of dog breeding as a respectable Victorian pursuit, dog shows, and the first boarding establishments, 'the kennels' came to mean a commercial or institutional place where dogs were kept, bred, and exhibited — the origin of the modern boarding kennel, rescue kennel, and breeding kennel. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the industry has softened and professionalised: modern kennels offer heated sleeping quarters, runs, grooming, and 'doggy daycare', and the word has travelled from the medieval kennels of a king's hounds to the holiday boarding kennel of the suburban pet owner.

Literary and proverbial use. The kennel is a recurring image of canine fidelity and canine neglect in English literature. The chained dog 'forgotten in a kennel for a week' in The Water-Babies (1863) draws on the reader's instant recognition of the kennel as the dog's whole world — his house, his prison, and, when forgotten, his grave-sentence. Proverbs and idiom keep the word close to the ground, and the American 'in the doghouse' (from the 1930s, meaning in disgrace) modernises the very structure that kennel names.

Summary of the chain:

  • *ḱwṓ (Proto-Indo-European, 'dog')
  • canis (Latin, 'dog')
  • canīle (Latin, 'a dog-place, kennel', from canis + place-suffix -īle)
  • chenil / kenil (Old French / Old North French, 'kennel, dog's shelter')
  • kenel (Middle English, 14th c., 'a dog's house, kennel')
  • kennel (Modern English, 'a dog's shelter; an establishment where dogs are boarded, bred, or kept')

From a Proto-Indo-European word for the dog itself, Latin built a place for the dog, French carried that place-name into English with the Norman huntsmen, and modern English has turned it into the whole industry of caring for the dog. Eight centuries after it arrived, the kennel still stands exactly where it began: over the dog, and around its shelter.
