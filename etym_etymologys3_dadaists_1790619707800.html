Dadaists derives from one of the shortest, strangest art-words ever chosen: dada, French nursery babble for a wooden hobby-horse, elevated in 1916 to name world negation. Its etymology is therefore not deep Indo-European but shallow, imitative and programmatic — child-speech weaponized.

Linguists classify dada as reduplicated infant babble of the mama, papa, tata type: a consonant-vowel syllable doubled for ease. In French domestic speech since at least the early 19th century dada meant hobby-horse (“aller à dada” — to ride horsie) and by extension a pet craze or hobby (“c'est son dada”). Littré and Larousse record it as child language, imitative in origin, with no Latin ancestor beyond onomatopoeia. Romanian da, da (yes-yes), Russian da and West African dada were later invoked playfully by Dadaists themselves to claim universality, but these are folk-etymological jokes, not sources.

  • Legendary coinage (Zurich, 1916): memoirs disagree — Ball says a paper-knife slipped into Larousse opened at dada; Tzara says naming at Café Terrasse; Huelsenbeck says song. All agree on intent: choose the silliest, most meaningless dictionary word to insult Meaning itself. First journal Cabaret Voltaire (June 1916) and Dada 1 (1917) fix spelling capital D.
  • Morphology: French dadaïste and German Dadaist add learned suffix -iste / -ist (Greek -istes) meaning adherent, creating oxymoron: solemn doctrine-suffix on baby-talk stem. English borrows both Dadaist (1919-1920 reviews) and Dada as adjective. Plural Dadaists adds regular -s.
  • Phonetics: French /dada/ with dental /d/ and open /a/ is preserved in RP /ˈdɑːdɑː/ with lengthened back vowels, plural /ˈdɑːdɑːɪsts/ with diphthong /aɪ/ from -ist /ɪst/ plus /s/. American often /ˈdɑːdɑːɪsts/ or /dæˈdɑːɪsts/.
  • Semantic expansion: from proper sect (1916-1924) to style-term for nonsense-collage to general adjective dadaistic (1930s) for any absurd prank. Dictionaries now list lowercased dadaist for imitators.
  • Debate / nuance: no PIE root is posited; to assign one would be false. The word is historically documented as nursery imitation, not inherited. Competing origin stories (Russian yes-yes, Tzara's Romanian) are self-mythology recorded in manifestos and must be read as Dada jokes about etymology itself.

The irony that proved productive: by calling themselves babblers, the Dadaists licensed the typographic behavior described in the source — treating letters as 26 toys, not transparent signs. Ball's sound poem Karawane (1916), Hausmann's poster-poems, Schwitters' Ursonate all perform dada literally: pre-semantic voicing. Media history thus reads Dada etymology as method: return language to mouth-shape and hand-shape before sense.

Attestations: French dada 1820s children's books; Larousse 1866; Zurich Cabaret Voltaire poster Feb 1916; Tzara Manifeste Dada 1918; Berlin Dada-Messe 1920; English Athenaeum review 1919 mocking “Dadaists”; Breton's 1924 break founding Surrealism, fixing Dada as closed historical term.
