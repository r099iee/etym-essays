Proto-Indo-European *mer- "to gleam, glimmer, sparkle" (traditional attribution for the Germanic morning-word; the deeper link beyond Germanic is debated — see note) (c. 4500–3000 BCE) → Proto-Germanic *murganaz / *murgina- "morning, the time of early daylight" (c. 500 BCE – 200 CE) → Old English morgen "morning, sunrise; the day following a given day" (recorded from the 8th century) → Middle English morwen / morwe / morow(e) / morrow "morning; the next day" (12th–15th centuries; loss of internal -g- and vowel reduction) → English morrow "morning; the day after a given day" (15th century onward; also fossilized in tomorrow, good morrow) → MORROW (present-day literary/archaic noun) Note on debate: a widely cited connection links the word to PIE *mer- "to gleam, glimmer," making the morning "the shining time," and German Morgen / Dutch morgen / Old Norse morginn support a common Germanic base; however, many authorities (including OED) stress that no secure Indo-European cognates outside Germanic can be demonstrated, and the exact pre-Germanic source remains uncertain. Some scholars instead connect the root to a "dark/blink" idea of fading night. Both views are stated here; neither is certain.
