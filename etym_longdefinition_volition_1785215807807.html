VOLITION — the power and the act of willing Volition is the exercise or power of the will: the faculty by which a person makes conscious choices, forms intentions, and determines a course of action, or a single deliberate act of choosing and willing. In the reading passage — "a living-romance heroine of the royalist volition and questionable destiny" — the word denotes precisely this sovereign, self-determining agency: Carlyle imagines Harriet Taylor Mill as a woman whose will asserts itself proudly and unyieldingly, a heroine who is not the passive plaything of fate but the agent of her own choices. The tone is heroic and faintly archaic, the vocabulary of philosophy pressed into the service of a romantic portrait. SENSE 1 — The philosophical and psychological sense Volition is above all a technical term of the philosophy of mind, and this is its primary, most precise meaning:
  • It names the act of willing as distinct from the desires, reasons, or intentions that precede it: the executive step in which a want is converted into a decision to act.
  • In classical and scholastic psychology, volition is the "act of the will" (Latin volitio) that follows upon intellect and appetite; the word was coined in the medieval schools precisely to give this act a name.
  • In modern philosophy and cognitive science, "volition" and its relatives (voluntary action, volitional control) remain standard terms for deliberate, willed behaviour as opposed to reflex, habit, or compulsion; it is contrasted with instinct, coercion, and automaticity.
SENSE 2 — The everyday and idiomatic sense Outside the technical vocabulary, volition survives almost entirely in a single fixed phrase:
  • "Of one's own volition" means freely, by one's own choice, without being asked, forced, or compelled: "she left the company of her own volition."
  • In this idiom the word keeps its full formal dignity — the phrase is deliberately weightier than "of her own free will" — and it is the main way ordinary speakers still encounter the noun.
  • Related collocations: "free volition," "conscious volition," "an act of volition," "with full volition," "volitional" (adjective, used in psychology and law), "volitionally" (adverb).
SENSE 3 — The literary, figurative, and Carlylean sense Because the word names deliberate self-determination, it is readily available for heroic and romantic portraiture:
  • "Royalist volition" (Carlyle) suggests a will that is proud, imperious, self-possessed, and aristocratically unbending — the agency of a romance heroine who defies circumstance.
  • The word's inherent elevation lets writers signal that a character acts from inner sovereignty rather than from impulse, pressure, or accident; it is the noun of the self-made destiny.
  • In this use it hovers between the philosophical precision of Sense 1 and the dignity of the "of one's own volition" idiom, carrying both at once.
SENSE 4 — Legal and institutional uses In law and administration the word is used for consent and agency:
  • "Acting of one's own volition" in legal contexts means acting voluntarily, without duress, fraud, or undue influence — a fact that determines the validity of contracts, confessions, and wills.
  • "Volitional" acts are those within the agent's control, as opposed to involuntary or reflexive ones, and the distinction matters in criminal and tort law.
THE SEMANTIC FIELD AND ITS BOUNDARIES
  • Will is the broader, everyday word for the mental power to choose or decide; volition is the formal, philosophical term for the individual act or exercise of that power.
  • Choice emphasizes the selection among alternatives and its outcome; volition emphasizes the inner act of willing itself, not the item chosen.
  • Desire is the passive longing or appetite that precedes the will; volition is the active resolution that turns wanting into decision.
  • Intention names a mental plan or aim that may or may not issue in action; volition is the executive step in which intention is converted into a willed act.
  • Determination stresses firmness and persistence in holding to a course; volition is the immediate act of willing a particular action.
  • Decision names the outcome or the act of settling between alternatives, closer to choice; volition keeps its focus on the willing faculty itself.
ETYMOLOGICAL SHAPE AND WHY IT MEANS WHAT IT MEANS The word is a medieval coinage built from the Latin verb velle "to wish, want, be willing": from its stem vol- (as in volō "I wish") the Scholastic philosophers formed volitio, "the act of willing," adding the abstract suffix -itio. Classical Latin had no such noun — it spoke only of wanting and willing through verbs — and the coinage answered a genuine philosophical need: to name the executive act of the will as a distinct object of study. That scholastic birth is still audible in the word's register: volition is a word with the laboratory dust of medieval logic on it, which is exactly why it can dignify a phrase like "royalist volition" with the weight of two thousand years of reflection on the will.
