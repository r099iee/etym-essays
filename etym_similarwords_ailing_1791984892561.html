• sick: Similar because it is the plainest word for being in poor health; differs because it usually implies a discrete illness or nausea and is direct rather than sympathetic, whereas “ailing” suggests a chronic, lingering condition.
  • ill: Similar because it is a near-exact synonym in British usage; differs because it is more formal and more often predicative (“he is ill”), and it can suggest a serious or diagnosed disease, while “ailing” is milder and typically attributive.
  • unwell: Similar because it is a polite, slightly euphemistic way of saying not healthy; differs because it usually describes a temporary or unspecified indisposition, not the long decline that “ailing” implies.
  • poorly: Similar because it is a gentle British synonym for unwell; differs because it is colloquial, often childish, and almost never used of institutions.
  • indisposed: Similar because it is a formal euphemism for slight illness; differs because it implies a temporary incapacity, often politely invented, and carries no suggestion of chronic weakness.
  • infirm: Similar because it describes weakness brought on by age or long illness; differs because it emphasises physical frailty and loss of strength, and is now chiefly literary or geriatric.
  • sickly: Similar because it describes a habitual susceptibility to illness; differs because it points to a constitutional tendency from childhood, while “ailing” describes the current state of a person or institution.
  • failing: Similar because it can describe declining health or a declining organisation; differs because it implies approaching collapse or death, a stronger and more final judgement than “ailing”.
  • declining: Similar because it is used of weakening institutions and health; differs because it is neutral and statistical, whereas “ailing” adds sympathy and a medical metaphor.
  • struggling: Similar because it is the usual alternative for a weak business or team; differs because it describes effort against difficulty, not illness, and can imply that success is still possible.
  • moribund: Similar because it describes something close to death or collapse; differs because it is far stronger, literary and usually terminal, whereas “ailing” leaves room for recovery.
  • debilitated: Similar because it means weakened by illness; differs because it is a clinical or formal word for loss of strength and is rarely applied to economies.
