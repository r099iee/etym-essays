Exaptation is a term of evolutionary biology, coined in 1982 by the paleontologists Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba in their paper "Exaptation — a Missing Term in the Science of Form," to name a trait whose current function is not the function for which it was originally shaped by natural selection. The word was built to fill a gap: biologists had long observed that features can change jobs — feathers that evolved for insulation coming to serve flight, jawbones whose pieces come to serve hearing — but had no precise word for the reuse itself. Gould and Vrba proposed aptation as the general term for any fit between organism and environment, whether or not it arose by selection for its present role, and then split it: an adaptation is a fit built by selection for its current function, while an exaptation is a fit now used for a function other than the one that shaped it. An exaptation may begin its career either as an adaptation (a feature selected for one role) or as a non-adaptive byproduct (a spandrel); in both cases it is later "co-opted" — drawn out of its old job and fitted to a new one. The term is deliberately non-teleological: it insists that evolution has no foresight, that nothing is designed in advance for a future function, and that functional novelty arises by recycling existing parts.

In the present context the word is used with textbook precision: the passage calls the shift "a mechanism that evolved for one basic purpose (speech processing and threat detection) that got hijacked for another (art, story, and bonding)." The neural machinery in question was not built for art or narrative; it was commandeered. This is the essence of exaptation — function after the fact, the finding of a new job for an old organ. The word must be carefully separated from its neighbours. An adaptation was shaped by selection for its present function; an exaptation was not. A pre-adaptation — the older term Gould and Vrba sought to replace — wrongly implied that a trait was somehow "waiting" for a future role, a teleological hint the new coinage was designed to remove. A spandrel (Gould and Lewontin, 1979) is a non-adaptive structural byproduct, a feature that exists because of the constraints imposed by other selected features; a spandrel becomes an exaptation only when it is later co-opted for a use. Byproduct and side effect describe origin without addressing later reuse; exaptation names precisely the reuse.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings:

  • Evolutionary biology (primary sense, 1982 onward): the co-option of a biological trait for a function other than that for which it evolved — e.g., feathers (insulation before flight), the reptilian jawbones of the mammalian middle ear, the human chin, the panda's "thumb."
  • Evolutionary psychology and cognitive science (extended sense, 1990s onward): used for mental machinery co-opted for new cognitive roles — language, music, art, religion — often in the very phrasing of the present passage.
  • Linguistics (specialized sense, 1980s onward): following Roger Lass and others, exaptation names the recycling of grammatical material that has lost its function, by analogy with the biological coinage.
  • Technology, product design, and business (loose popular sense, 2000s onward): the word is borrowed for any "hacking" or repurposing of an existing tool, platform, or process for a novel use — "exapting" a technology means finding an unintended application for it.
  • Cultural evolution (recent scholarly sense): applied to the reuse of cultural artifacts, institutions, and practices for new social functions.

The semantic bridge between the senses is the single idea of functional recycling without forward-looking design: in every domain, an exaptation is an old part in a new role, and the term's value lies in insisting that the role was found, not planned. The spelling is exaptation (not ex-adaptation); Gould and Vrba deliberately used aptation as the umbrella term so that ex-aptation could literally mean "a fit out of its original context." The register is technical and mildly corrective — the word is typically introduced to overturn a naive adaptationist account — and it has become a standard item of the vocabulary of popular science, always glossed, exactly as it is glossed in the present passage.
