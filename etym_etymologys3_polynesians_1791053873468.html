Polynesians is a modern ethnonym built entirely from ancient Greek parts to label peoples whom Greeks never knew. Its history therefore has two layers: the deep history of its Greek elements poly- and nesos, and the shallow 18th-19th-century history of their combination into a colonial geographic term that became an ethnic self-designation.

First element poly-: PIE *pelh1-u-, an adjectival u-stem meaning many, much, full, yields Sanskrit puru much, many, Avestan pouru, Greek polys much (masc. polys, neut. poly), Latin plus more (for *plous), English full, folk. In Greek polys was ubiquitous: polyglottos many-tongued, polymathes having learned much, Polydeukes, Polynices many-victory. The combining form poly- entered Latin and then modern scientific vocabulary as a productive prefix for many: polygon, polytheism, Polynesia.

  • PIE *pelh1- to fill, full: zero-grade *plh1- + -u- gives many as fullness. Semantic bridge: full → abundant → many.
  • Greek polys / poly: irregular suppletion (polys, pollon, pleistos most) shows antiquity; Pindar, Homer use it for men, ships, counsels.
  • Modern productivity: 17th-18th c. science coins poly- words freely (polyp, polygon, Polynesia) without needing new Greek authority.

Second element -nesia from nesos: Ancient Greek nêsos (Attic nêsos, Ionic nēsos) meant island, especially larger inhabited islands as opposed to mere rocks. Homer already uses nêsoi for the Aegean isles. Unlike poly-, nêsos has no secure Indo-European etymology; Beekes and others list it as probably Pre-Greek substrate, with no convincing cognates in Latin, Sanskrit or Germanic. Some older proposals linked it to *sneh2- to swim/float (that which swims on sea) or to *nes- to return home, but these are now regarded as folk-guesses. Honest etymology therefore starts the second chain at Greek, not PIE, a permitted non-PIE equivalent.

  • Greek nêsos island > nes- + -ia abstract region: analogy with Indonesia (Indian islands), Micronesia (small islands), Melanesia (black islands) shows 18th-19th c. systematic naming of Pacific by size, population and island multitude.
  • French Polynésie: coined 1756 by President Charles de Brosses in Histoire des navigations aux terres australes to denote all South Sea islands with many small isles, initially covering the entire Pacific beyond the East Indies. His -ésie follows Asie, Indonésie pattern.
  • narrowing: 1831-1832 French navigator Dumont d'Urville restricts Polynésie to the eastern triangle (Hawaii-Easter Island-New Zealand) as opposed to Melanesia (west, dark-skinned) and Micronesia (northwest, small islands), introducing racialized tripartition now criticized but institutionally fixed.

English borrowing was rapid in the age of Cook. 1760s-1770s English translations of Brosses and Cook's journals use Polynesia; adjective Polynesian from 1790s; plural Polynesians for inhabitants from early 19th c. Phonologically Greek poly /poly/ > French /pɔ.li.ne.zi/ > English /pɒlɪˈniːʒə/ with stress shift to penult and palatalization -sia to /ʒə/ or /ʃə/, plural adding /nz/: Polynesians /pɒlɪˈniːʒənz/. Orthographic capital P marks proper ethnonym status.

Semantic evolution in English mirrors colonial knowledge:

  • 1756-1770s: purely geographic. Polynesia means many-isled southern seas; Polynesians means inhabitants thereof, undifferentiated savages, noble savages in Rousseauist fantasy.
  • 19th century: racial-anthropological. Comparative philologists (Hale, Humboldt) prove Polynesian languages form a branch of Austronesian/Malayo-Polynesian; anthropologists contrast Polynesian chiefdoms (Hawaii, Tahiti, Tonga, Samoa, Maori) with Melanesian and Micronesian societies for complexity, seafaring, monumental architecture (moai, marae, heiau). Noble-savage admiration coexists with colonial subjugation.
  • 20th century: navigational rehabilitation. Experimental voyages (Hokulea 1976) prove Polynesians settled the triangle by intentional star-navigation, overturning drift-voyage theories; perception shifts from passive primitives to master navigators. Source sentence with thousand-ell nets handled by a hundred men exemplifies this older admiration for large-scale economic-political organization generating the state.
  • Present: self-designation and specificity. Many prefer specific identities — Hawaiians/Kanaka Maoli, Samoans, Tongans, Maori, Tahitians — with Polynesian as pan-ethnic umbrella in diaspora (e.g., Polynesian communities in Auckland, Sydney, Salt Lake City). In New Zealand Pasifika/Polynesian is bureaucratic; in linguistics Polynesian remains precise subgroup (Proto-Polynesian c. 1000 BCE, divided into Tongic and Nuclear Polynesian).

Literary attestations: Cook and Banks journals, Melville Typee and Omoo, Stevenson In the South Seas, Gauguin Noa Noa, Margaret Mead Coming of Age in Samoa, Thor Heyerdahl Kon-Tiki controversy. Each reframes Polynesians from exotic others to cultural agents.

Thus Polynesians traveled from Greek many + island through French кабинет geography to a living identity: outsiders named the multitude of islands, insiders now navigate under that name while asserting distinct island nations within it.
