A waistcoat is a close-fitting, usually sleeveless garment worn over the shirt and beneath a jacket or coat, traditionally the third element of a three-piece man's suit. In British English “waistcoat” is the standard term; in American English the same garment is usually called a vest (while British “vest” means an undershirt — a classic transatlantic trap).

Primary sense — the tailored garment. The waistcoat is buttoned down the front, may be single- or double-breasted, and covers the torso from the shoulders to about the waist or hip. In formal dress it is cut with a low “V” front so that the shirt and tie show, and its back is often of a cheaper or thinner fabric since it is hidden. It fastens over the trouser waistband, and in older men's tailoring the trousers were held up by a belt or braces hidden beneath it. A pocket watch chain traditionally crosses the chest from one waistcoat pocket to another.

History and fashion. The waistcoat's place in Western men's dress was fixed by a famous act of royal fashion: in October 1666 Charles II of England decreed a new national mode of dress — a long coat, breeches, and a sleeved under-garment worn beneath the coat — to replace the elaborate French fashions. This under-garment, called a “waistcoat,” was originally long and had sleeves; over the following century it was cut shorter and the sleeves were dropped. In the 18th century waistcoats were the most luxurious part of a gentleman's dress, embroidered and worked in silk and gold thread; in the 19th century, with the rise of sober Victorian tailoring, they became quiet, subdued components of the three-piece suit, still worn daily by respectable men. In the 20th century the waistcoat retreated to formal, ceremonial, and costume wear, though it has repeatedly resurfaced in fashion.

Extended and non-tailoring senses.

  • Decorative and ceremonial: richly ornamented waistcoats survive in court dress, folk costume, and fancy-dress; also worn by waiters and staff as part of uniform.
  • As an outer or casual garment: in some styles — quilted, padded, or knit “sleeveless jackets” — the word waistcoat is stretched to cover warm or protective garments worn over other clothing (e.g., a padded shooting waistcoat).
  • In children's and costume language: waistcoats appear in school uniform, period drama, and playful fantasy.
  • Literal use in Tolkien: Smaug's boast that he possesses “a waistcoat of fine diamonds” is a comic metaphor — the dragon treats the gem-studded armour of his belly as a gentleman's garment. The word's dignified, slightly fussy associations make the joke work.
  • Rare verbal use: to “waistcoat” someone means to dress them in a waistcoat; virtually obsolete outside costume contexts.

Pronunciation and form. The spelling is waist + coat, but the word has a famously non-transparent traditional pronunciation: alongside the standard modern /weɪs.kəʊt/, an older, naturalised pronunciation /ˈwɛs.kɪt/ (“weskit”) is recorded in British English. The garment covers the area between the waist and the shoulders, hence the name.

Register and nuance. “Waistcoat” is a standard, slightly formal British noun. It contrasts with “vest” (American), “doublet” and “jerkin” (historical predecessors), and “cardigan” / “gilet” (modern sleeved or outer alternatives). In fiction and period writing it is a reliable marker of a character's era and social class.
