- Late 14th c. — moral theology enters English: borrowed from Old French *detraccion* and Latin *detractio*, specifically defined in Middle English religious manuals (Chaucer's *Parson's Tale*) as the deadly sin of pulling down a neighbor's good name through slander or the malicious revealing of secret faults.
- 15th–16th c. — physical and literal senses co-exist: used alongside its moral sense to describe the physical taking away, subtraction, or withdrawal of parts or substances from a whole.
- 17th–18th c. — the courtly and political weapon: detraction becomes the central vice of court life, coffeehouses, and partisan pamphlets; essayists like Addison, Swift, and Bacon write extensively on how envy drives the mediocre to systematic detraction of men of wit.
- 19th c. — Victorian aesthetic and philosophical scrutiny: thinkers like Ruskin analyze detraction as a psychological and cultural malady, noting how critics safely attack living creators whose reputations are not yet armored by history.
- 20th c. — transition to formal and literary register: physical and legal senses fade from common parlance, leaving *detraction* as an elevated, formal term for malicious belittlement and the ungenerous discounting of merit.
- Today — ethical and intellectual critique: preserved in academic, literary, and ethical discourse to identify cynical bad-faith attempts to diminish excellence, innovation, or moral integrity.
