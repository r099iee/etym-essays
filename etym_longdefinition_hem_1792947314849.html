In its source use hem is the verb of encirclement: to hem in, to surround strangers and shut them within a living wall so that no way out remains. The trees, says the rumour, do actually move and can surround strangers and hem them in, closing round like a stitched border round cloth, patient, root-slow, and inescapable once the ring is complete.

Core meaning in detail: The verb grows straight out of the noun hem, the folded and stitched edge of a garment that keeps cloth from fraying and holds its shape. To hem is first to make that border with needle and thread, folding raw edge under and fastening it flat. To hem in is to treat living bodies as the cloth and hostile presences as the stitching: to edge them round, to bind their movement, to finish their freedom as a seamstress finishes a hem. Tolkien, who loved the homely craft word turned to forest dread, makes trees behave as giant needlewomen, hemming travellers as one hems a handkerchief.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To surround and confine, to hem in (primary verb sense in the source, from the 16th c.): hemmed in by trees, by crowds, by regulations. The bridge is the sewn border as enclosure: as stitching runs round the whole cloth edge and leaves no raw gap, so encirclers run round the whole party and leave no way out.
  • To make a hemmed border on cloth (primary craft sense, from Old English): to hem a skirt, a handkerchief, a sail. The bridge is the original noun made verb: to put a hem upon, folding and sewing the edge, the housewifely labour behind every garment before overlockers existed.
  • To utter hems, to clear the throat hesitantly (verb hem, to hem and haw, from the 16th c.): hemming before an awkward question. The bridge is imitative and separate in origin: the throat-noise hem mimics the caught breath, nothing to do with sewing, yet happily colliding in the phrase hemmed in by hems, trapped by hesitations.
  • The stitched edge itself (noun): the hem of a dress, the hem of a wood in older poetic use. The bridge reverses the verb: the result names the labour, the folded inch of cloth that signals finish, modesty, and care, let down in poverty and taken up in plenty.
  • To edge or border a place metaphorically (literary): meadows hemmed with woods, a village hemmed by hills. The bridge carries sewing into landscape: hills and trees stand as the folded border round the field-cloth, nature tailoring its own garments.
  • To restrict by rules or obligations (figurative social): hemmed in by tradition, by debt, by promises. The bridge extends the ring from bodies to lives: invisible stitching of custom and contract binds movement as surely as boughs, and the verb serves headline writers for economies and parliaments alike.

Register and connotation: hem is small, domestic, and quietly menacing in this use. The craft itself is gentle, feminine, and meticulous, the very opposite of forest violence, which is why the image chills: the trees do not smash or chase, they stitch, patiently and without anger, until strangers find the world hemmed. The monosyllable clicks shut like a needle through linen, and the particle in completes the trap: to hem is to edge, to hem in is to imprison.
