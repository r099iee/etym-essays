PIE (reconstructed, before c. 3000 BCE): *pau- "few, little, small" — the root of the word's smallness; collateral reflexes include Greek pauros "few, little" and Latin paucus "few" (ancestor of English paucity and, via Proto-Germanic *fawaz, of English few). Latin (archaic period): pullus "young of an animal — foal, chick, offspring" — from the base sense "small, young"; the semantic bridge from "small" to "young animal" is direct. Late Latin: pullanus "young of an animal" — a derivative in -anus from pullus. Old French (9th–13th c.): poulain, poulin "colt, young horse". Middle French (15th c.): poulenet "little foal" — the diminutive of poulain, recorded in the fifteenth century. Lowland Scots (mid-17th c.): powney, powny "a small horse" — first attested in the 1650s; the word passed into English by way of Scotland, from the French diminutive. English (early 18th c.): pony "a small horse of a small breed" — from Scots, spreading into general English in the eighteenth century. Modern English (18th c. to present): pony "a small horse of any of several small breeds". NOTE ON DEBATE: The exact route of the diminutive from Middle French poulenet to Scots powney is not documented word-for-word; the standard account (poulenet → Scots powney → English pony) is secure in outline and dating (Scots 1650s; English 18th c.), but the precise intermediate pronunciations are inferred, not attested. The deeper root *pau- is shared with paucus and few; the pullus → poulain → poulenet branch is the attested chain for pony.
