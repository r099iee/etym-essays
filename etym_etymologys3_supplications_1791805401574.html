English took the word from Old French in the last decades of the fourteenth century, at a moment when both the devotional and the administrative senses were alive in French. The Old French noun supplicacion descends from Latin supplicātio, a noun of action from supplicāre. The Latin verb is a compound of sub "under" and plicāre "to fold, bend" — the same verb that gives complicate, explicit, duplicate and ply. The prefix assimilates before the labial: sub-plicāre becomes supplicāre, which is why English writes a double p and pronounces a single /p/.

The gesture behind the word

The literal image is of folding oneself down, of bending the body low before a superior. Latin supplex, the adjective and noun meaning "kneeling, suppliant", is built on the same elements. In Roman usage the gesture and the speech act were inseparable: to supplicate was to approach a god or a magistrate in a posture of submission, hands extended, knees bent. That physical origin survives in English collocations such as "on bended knee in supplication" and explains why the word can never quite be neutral.

The Roman supplicatio

  • A supplicatio was a public act of prayer decreed by the Senate, usually of one to several days, during which temples were opened, offerings made and the citizen body attended.
  • It was voted for victories, for the aversion of prodigies and for the recovery of magistrates; Livy and Cicero refer to such decrees repeatedly, and the number of days was a mark of honour.
  • The word therefore had a civic and political dimension from the start: it named an authorised public act, not a private sigh. English supplication preserves some of that public, formal quality.

Christian Latin and the Bible

The Latin fathers used supplicatio freely for prayer, and the noun passed into the liturgy. English translations of the Bible gave the word its most quoted home. At 1 Timothy 2:1 the Authorized Version reads "supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks"; the Greek behind "supplications" is δέησις (deēsis), "entreaty", and the Vulgate renders the series with obsecrationes, orationes, postulationes. The English word thus entered devotional vocabulary as one item in a carefully graded list of prayer-types — a fact still visible in liturgical prose.

Petition and procedure

  • In medieval England a supplication was a written request to the Crown, to Parliament, to a bishop or to a court; the form had fixed formulae and was enrolled in registers.
  • The university supplicat — Latin for "he prays" — is the formal application for a degree, still pronounced in Oxford and Cambridge ceremonies.
  • Canon law knows the libellus supplex, a petition to an ecclesiastical judge; the term survives in the Roman Curia.
  • This institutional life is why the word appears in legal and parliamentary records far more often than in poetry between the fifteenth and eighteenth centuries.

Phonetic and orthographic notes

  • Latin -tiōnem yields French -cion and English -tion, pronounced /ʃən/; the spelling supplicacioun in Middle English reflects the French stage before the Latinising respelling to -tion.
  • The modern pronunciation /ˌsʌp.lɪˈkeɪ.ʃən/ keeps the short first vowel of the closed syllable and reduces the second to /ɪ/; the plural simply adds /z/.
  • The double p is etymological, not decorative: it records the assimilation of sub- to the following p.

Modern life of the word

Today supplication is rare in speech but alive in three niches: liturgy and scripture, historical narrative about the ancien régime, and literary prose that wants a note of abasement. Its plural is the more frequent form, since supplications are typically many and repeated. The word's survival is a good index of register: where a modern writer would say "appeals" or "pleas", an eighteenth-century scene says "supplications", and the difference in tone is exactly the difference between a request and a prayer.
