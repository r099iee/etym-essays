- c. 1300 — out in the open: Middle English abrod means outside the house, in the broad daylight, away from one’s own roof.
- Mid-15th c. — across the sea: with trade and pilgrimage widening horizons, the word stretches to mean in foreign countries.
- 16th–17th c. — rumours walk too: abroad extends to anything at large in the world — stories, spirits, and mischief abroad in the land.
- 18th–19th c. — the Grand Tour and empire: young gentlemen and colonial officers go abroad as a rite of passage; the word gleams with prestige.
- 20th c. — everyone’s word: package holidays, study-abroad schemes, and football played abroad democratise it; it also chills in phrases like enemies abroad.
- Today — foreign soil or open air: living abroad dominates, while rumour abroad and abroad in the fields survive as deliberate archaisms.
