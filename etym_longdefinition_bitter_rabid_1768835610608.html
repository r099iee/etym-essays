bitter — In its primary gustatory sense, bitter is one of the four basic tastes: a sharp, acrid, pungent flavour, the opposite of sweet, detected chiefly at the back of the tongue and typically experienced as unpleasant. But the word’s literary power lies in its figurative reach, and the passage being corrected exploits it fully: the competition among the carvers is said to be “bitter,” meaning it is acrimonious, resentful, angry, filled with sharp and painful feeling. The metaphor is tactile and gustatory at once — a rivalry that “bites,” an experience that stings and gnaws like an acrid substance on the palate. In context the word performs a dark economy: these men’s only passion, once their days of love had guttered, is the production of wooden sculpture, and their rivalry is described with a word that fuses taste, pain, and moral acidity — the competition does not merely excite them, it corrodes them, the way a bitter taste corrodes the tongue.
  • Gustatory primary sense: the sharp, acrid taste (coffee, dark chocolate, aloes, unripe fruit) detected at the back of the tongue; one of the basic tastes, the opposite of sweet.
  • Emotional/figurative sense: acrimonious, resentful, full of sharp and lingering anger — bitter tears, bitter enemies, a bitter rivalry; the taste of pain projected onto feeling.
  • Physical intensity sense: harsh, piercing, relentless sensation or weather — bitter cold, a bitter wind — where the sting of cold is borrowed from the sting of the taste.
  • Specialised and idiomatic senses: “bitter” as a style of brown pale ale (19th c.); “to the bitter end” (to the last, however painful — though the nautical origin from “bitt” the anchor-post is a folk-etymology dispute); “a bitter pill to swallow” (a hard truth); the noun “the bitter” as the opposite of the sweet in life.
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: sharp stresses acute sensation or keenness of mind; acid and acrid are narrowly gustatory or olfactory and connote burning chemicals; pungent is intense and often appetising; sour is a different taste family (fermentation); bitter alone carries the lasting, corrosive, moral charge.
rabid — Rabid means (1) affected with rabies, the acute viral disease of dogs and other animals that causes frenzy, foaming, and biting; and (2), by far the commoner modern sense, fanatical, extreme, wildly intense — a “rabid” supporter, a “rabid” hatred. The word comes from Latin rabidus “mad, raging, furious,” from rabere “to rave, be mad,” the same root that gives rage and rabies. In the passage the competition is “bitter and rabid”: rabid adds to bitter the note of frenzy and contagion — a rivalry that has gone beyond resentment into something mad, foaming, and out of control. Where bitter is the corrosive sting of feeling, rabid is the blazing, irrational heat of it.
  • Medical/veterinary sense: affected with rabies — “a rabid dog,” “rabid fox” — with the classic symptoms of frenzy, hydrophobia, and foaming at the mouth; rabid also describes the disease’s rapid, violent course.
  • Fanatical/figurative sense: extremism, zeal, wild intensity — a rabid fan, rabid nationalism, rabid opposition — where the mind is imagined as bitten and maddened like a rabid animal.
  • Nuance versus near-synonyms: fanatical stresses devotion to a cause; extreme stresses going beyond the usual bounds; fierce and wild stress intensity without the medical “bitten, maddened” image; rabid alone imports the animal frenzy and the sense of infection spreading.
Read together, “bitter and rabid” gives the competition its full emotional anatomy: bitter supplies the lingering, corrosive resentment that gnaws at the palate of the soul, and rabid supplies the frothing, contagious madness — two kinds of extremity fused in a single rivalry that consumes the craftsmen it inflames.
