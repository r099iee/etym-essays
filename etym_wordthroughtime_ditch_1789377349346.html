- Old English (pre-1100) - Attested as *dīc* (palatalized in southern Saxon dialects to /diːtʃ/); denoted both the excavated trench/channel and the earthen bank or mound created by the excavated soil.
- Middle English (12th–15th c.) - Spelled *diche* / *dych*; specialized into an agricultural drainage furrow, land-grant boundary marker, or castle defense channel, while northern doublet *dike* increasingly designated raised embankments.
- 17th Century - Achieved prominent military and political currency when William III of Orange coined the phrase "die in the last ditch" (1672), creating an enduring idiom for unyielding, desperate resistance against overwhelming odds.
- 18th to 19th Century - Commonplace across rural literature; the stagnant water in drainage ditches inspired the simile "dull as ditchwater" (popularized by Charles Dickens); American English began using the verb "to ditch" for derailing trains or dumping refuse.
- Early to Mid-20th Century - Royal Air Force slang adopted "the Ditch" for the English Channel, giving birth to the aviation verb "to ditch" (emergency water landing); American slang popularized "ditching" for skipping school or severing friendships.
- 21st Century - Continues as an essential term in hydrology, civil infrastructure, and ecology, alongside universal colloquial usage for discarding and evasion.
