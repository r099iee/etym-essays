Heretofore is a small architecture of pointing. It is a compound of three deictic elements — here, to, fore — each descending from a separate Proto-Indo-European root, welded together in Middle English into a single temporal adverb, and the whole word is a lesson in how languages convert space into time. Here descends from the PIE demonstrative particle *ki-/*ko-, the finger pointing at what lies near the speaker. In Proto-Germanic it became *hēr, "in this place," and Old English hēr carried it into English unchanged in sense. The same particle appears in Latin cis, "on this side" (whence cisalpine and, in the twentieth century, cisgender), in Greek sēmeron, "today," literally "this day," and in the Baltic in Lithuanian šìs, "this." The Sanskrit hyas, "yesterday," is sometimes loosely cited beside here, but it descends from a separate root, *gʰdʰyés, cognate with Latin heri and Greek khthes, and must not be grafted onto this branch. To descends from the PIE directional particle *de/*do, "toward" — a bare grammatical gesture of motion toward a goal — which became Proto-Germanic *tō and Old English tō, later splitting into to and the emphatic too, "also," literally "to it as well." The same root appears inside Greek compound particles such as hóde, "this one here," where the -de is the same pointing-toward morpheme. Fore descends from the great PIE root *per-, "forward, in front, before," which spread through the whole family: in Sanskrit it produced puras, "in front," purā, "formerly," and pūrva, "foremost, eastern" — still alive in Hindi pūrv/pūrab, "east," and Odia pūrba, so that in the Indic languages the future literally lies before the speaker and the east is the direction of origin; in Greek it gave pró, the prefix of prologue, prophet, and program; in Latin prō, the prefix of proceed and provide; and in Germanic *furai, whence Old English fore and for and English fore, before, and forth. The compound itself is a Middle English coinage. English, with its habit of fusing prepositional particles, assembled hēr + tō + fore, "up to before this," in the fourteenth century, by the same analogical impulse that produced its twin hitherto (hither, "to this place," + to). Both words seal a closed past; both were from the very beginning the property of law and of formal prose. In medieval and early modern statutes, "heretofore" marked the boundary of a legally defined past — all acts "heretofore done," all obligations "heretofore incurred" — a usage that hardened the word into the register it still occupies. By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word had spread through official and literary prose, and in Victorian adventure fiction it became a raised curtain: a sentence beginning "Heretofore..." holds a tableau of stillness, then opens the scene to what follows. That is exactly its work in the passage — the Martian sky has heretofore been silent and stirless; the whole meaning of the word is that this is about to end. In contemporary English the word is rare in speech and confined to legal, contractual, and elevated literary registers. It has no plural, no comparison, no conjugation; it is a fossilized three-part pointing gesture that still works with complete precision. Its semantic history is not a drift so much as a specialization: from a general "before now" it became, above all, the lawyer's word for a closed past, while its twin hitherto retreated further into bookishness. The sound has also drifted with the language: Middle English "heere-to-fore" became modern /hˌiətʊfˌɔː/, the old long vowels shortened and smoothed by the Great Vowel Shift and later reductions. The spelling closed up from here-to-fore to heretofore between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, completing the word's fusion in writing as it had long been fused in sound.
  • 14th c. — compound coined in Middle English; earliest recorded use of a closed temporal past.
  • 15th–17th c. — fixed in legal and statutory language as the boundary marker of a defined past.
  • 18th–19th c. — spreads through official prose and Victorian adventure fiction; the raised-curtain device.
  • Contemporary — rare, formal, legal; a museum piece that still functions with complete precision.
The word is thus three ancient particles, each older than writing, fused in the fourteenth century and still sealing the past for us today — a reminder that our most precise temporal vocabulary is built, brick by brick, out of pointing at space.
