Ceylon is the former English and general European name for the island country in the Indian Ocean now officially called Sri Lanka, used as the country's name under Portuguese, Dutch and British rule and as an independent dominion from 1948 to 1972. In contemporary usage it survives as a historical-geographical term for the island before 1972 and as a brand in Ceylon tea, Ceylon cinnamon and Ceylon sapphire. The word is a proper noun and is always capitalised.

Geographically Ceylon denotes the teardrop-shaped island off the southeastern coast of India, about 65,000 square kilometres, with Colombo as commercial capital, Kandy as cultural capital, and a central highland producing world-famous tea. Historically it denotes three colonial eras, Portuguese Ceilao 1505-1658, Dutch Ceylon 1658-1796, and British Ceylon 1796-1948, followed by the Dominion of Ceylon 1948-1972, before the name was changed to Sri Lanka, meaning resplendent island, in the 1972 republican constitution.

  • Historical country sense: the island state as a colonial and early independent polity, e.g. British Ceylon, independent Ceylon, Parliament of Ceylon. This is the dominant sense in books written before 1972.
  • Geographical island sense: the physical island itself, e.g. the Veddahs of Ceylon, the highlands of Ceylon, used interchangeably with Sri Lanka for natural features.
  • Product-brand sense: Ceylon as a mark of origin and quality, e.g. Ceylon tea for high-grown black tea, Ceylon cinnamon for true cinnamon Cinnamomum verum, Ceylon sapphire for cornflower-blue corundum.
  • Cultural-literary sense: romantic exotic Ceylon of travel writing, all palm groves, elephants, temples and spice gardens, often idealised as Eden or Taprobane.
  • Administrative-legal relic sense: Bank of Ceylon, Ceylon Electricity Board era names and pre-1972 statutes where Ceylon remains the legal term.

Polysemy is diachronic rather than simultaneous. The bridge from ethnic Siṃhala, lion-people, to island name is metonymy from inhabitants to land, common in country naming. The bridge from island to products is origin metonymy, where the place guarantees type and quality, as in Champagne or Darjeeling. The bridge from contemporary name to historical name is political replacement: after 1972 Sri Lanka became the official endonym and Ceylon became the exonym retained for nostalgia and branding. Modern speakers therefore distinguish historical Ceylon from present Sri Lanka in time but not in place.

Domain usages reinforce this. In tea trade, Ceylon is a protected origin term regulated by the Sri Lanka Tea Board. In gemmology, Ceylon sapphire means a specific hue and origin premium. In history and anthropology, Ceylon appears in phrases like colonial Ceylon, Ceylon Tamils and Ceylonese English. The demonym was Ceylonese, now largely replaced by Sri Lankan, though Ceylonese still appears for pre-1972 persons and Burgher communities.

In the source context, like the Veddahs of Ceylon, the word simply anchors an ethnographic example in a geography every early 20th-century reader would recognise. It carries no ideological load beyond locating the Veddas on the familiar imperial map, where Ceylon sat alongside New South Wales, Tasmania and Bushmanland as sites of primitive life in a global stadial history of dwelling.
