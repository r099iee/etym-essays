THE EVOLUTION OF STEWING

The word stewing has an unexpectedly sensual and even scandalous past. Today it evokes the homely image of a pot simmering on a stove and, by extension, a person brooding in silent anger. But the word entered English not from the kitchen but from the bath-house, and for several centuries it carried associations of heat, steam, crowded rooms, and even vice.

1. The Romance Roots: Bathing in Steam

The ultimate ancestor of stew is the Late Latin verb extufare ("to smoke, fume, steam"), a formation related to tufus or the Greek-based vocabulary of vaporous heat. From Latin, the word passed into Old French as estuver — "to stew, to bathe in a heated room." The key point is that in medieval Europe the same establishments that offered hot, steam-filled bathing rooms also cooked food in the same hot, moist conditions. The association between "a hot steamy chamber" and "cooking by steam or simmering" was therefore natural from the start.

2. Entering English: The Stew as Bath and Brothel

In Middle English, the verb stewen meant both "to bathe" and "to simmer in liquid," while the noun stew referred to the heated bath-room itself. By the 16th century, however, the word had taken a notorious turn. The "stews" — the public hot bath-houses of London's Bankside district — became notorious as places of disrepute, and the noun came to mean a brothel. This is why the word carried a taint of vulgarity for generations; the OED records that in polite usage "stew" had to be carefully avoided. Even after the bath-houses declined, the association of "stew" with heat, steam, and dubious establishments lingered in the language.

3. The Culinary Sense Comes to the Fore

Despite the scandalous detour, the culinary sense of stew — to cook slowly in liquid — steadily grew dominant from the 14th century onward. Stewing was an essential technique of pre-modern cooking because it could render even the toughest cuts of meat tender through prolonged moist heat. The dish called "stew" (a thick soup or ragout of meat and vegetables) became a staple of the poor and the hearty food of the working class. The present participle stewing developed as the standard descriptor of this slow, gentle method of cooking.

4. The Figurative Leap: Simmering Emotions

The psychological sense of stewing grew naturally out of the culinary image. If a person is like a pot of food simmering over a low flame — hot, confined, gradually intensifying, and unable to release its heat — then "stewing" is the perfect metaphor for suppressed anger, prolonged worry, or anxious brooding. The figurative use is attested from the 17th and 18th centuries and has flourished ever since. The fixed idiom "to stew in one's own juice" (first recorded in the early modern period) crystallises the metaphor: a person left to face the consequences of their own actions is like food left to simmer in its own juices, unable to escape its own heat.

5. STEWING in Modern Usage and in Tolkien

In modern English, stewing operates on both planes simultaneously. In the kitchen it is a neutral culinary term; in conversation it is a vivid, informal verb of emotional states: "stop stewing about it," "he stewed over the insult all day." The word is used of worry, anger, resentment, and even anticipation. In J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit, the adjectival use in "the stewing depths of the dragon-haunted caverns" extends the metaphor in an original direction: the caverns themselves are rendered as a great suffocating vessel in which the dwarves have been slowly "cooked" — oppressed, confined, and heated by proximity to the dragon. The image fuses the culinary sense (enclosure, heat, liquid darkness) with the psychological sense (suppressed dread), and it works precisely because both strands of the word's history remain alive.

6. Conclusion

The history of stewing is a journey from the steam-baths of medieval France and London, through a notorious association with vice, into the homely kitchen, and finally into the vocabulary of human emotion. Its career shows how a word can shed scandal, embrace utility, and then bloom into metaphor, all while retaining the deep image of heat enclosed and building slowly — whether in a pot or in a heart.
