• Quintus Roscius Gallus: Similar because he bears the same gentilicium and is often meant when later writers say simply “Roscius”; differs because he was a comic actor, not the Umbrian defendant of 80 BCE, and his fame produced the English byword.
  • Sextus Roscius: Similar because it is the full form of the name in the source; differs because the praenomen and the ethnic “of Ameria” disambiguate the man, while bare Roscius can point to either famous bearer.
  • Roscian: Similar because it is the adjective derived from the name; differs because it describes a style or reputation (chiefly of acting) and is a rare literary formation, not a name.
  • thespian: Similar because it matches the byword sense “a great actor”; differs because it is a generic term of rather elevated register and carries no implication of supreme skill.
  • histrio: Similar because it is the Latin word for an actor and belongs to the same theatrical world; differs because it was a professional label, often contemptuous in Cicero's mouth, not an honorific name.
  • cliens: Similar because Roscius was Cicero's client and the word describes that relationship exactly; differs because it names a legal and social bond, not a person, and any Roman could be a cliens.
  • reus: Similar because it is the procedural term for the defendant in a Roman trial; differs because it is a role, not an identity, and it says nothing about the charge or the man.
  • parricida: Similar because it names the crime of which Roscius was accused; differs because it is the accusers' label, repudiated by the defence, and it would be defamatory to apply it to the acquitted man.
