The word FAERIE carries within it the oldest Indo-European idea of speech as destiny. Its root is the Proto-Indo-European verb *bheh2-, "to speak, to say, to declare," the same root that produced Latin fari, "to speak," and the English words fame, fable, and confess. In the Italic branch this verb gave the Romans fatum, literally "that which is spoken" — but spoken by whom? By the gods. Fatum was therefore the divine utterance that fixed a person's destiny, the fixed decree of fate, and from it Latin built fatalis, "fatal," and the classical image of the three Fates, the Parcae, who spin and cut the thread of life. In Vulgar Latin the plural of fatum, the fata, hardened into a singular feminine being: a goddess of destiny, a fate-woman, the supernatural being who presided over what was decreed to happen. From this Late Latin figure every Romance word for "fairy" descends — French fee, Italian fata, Spanish hada, Portuguese fada — and, via Old French fae, the English fay.

The medieval transformation turned a goddess of fate into a lady of enchantment. In Old French, fae was the fairy woman, the woman of fate become a being of magic, and to it was attached the abstract suffix -erie — the suffix that built nouns of state, practice, and collective domain (as in English -ery/-ry: knavery, witchery, sorcery). The result was faerie. Crucially, faerie did not at first mean "a fairy." It meant enchantment itself: the condition of being fey or enchanted, the sorcery that the fairy women practiced, and — by extension — the land or realm where they dwelt. The word was environmental and collective before it was ever personal: faerie was a power and a place, not a person.

Middle English borrowed the word in the fourteenth century and kept this collective, environmental sense. Chaucer, in the Wife of Bath's Tale, writes that in the days of King Arthur "al was this land fulfild of fayerye" — all the land was filled with faerie — meaning the realm and its inhabitants together, a whole enchanted Britain. It is exactly this sense that the word in our passage requires: "the secret realm of faerie" is not a single winged creature but the otherworld itself, the perilous land where courtly sprites hold midnight revels under silver boughs.

The great literary consecration of the word came with Edmund Spenser, whose The Faerie Queene was published in 1590. Spenser's spelling faerie — and his magnificent allegory of holiness, temperance, and chastity moving through the fairy court — fixed the archaic form in the English literary imagination as a register of high, dangerous, moralized enchantment. Milton caught the same music in L'Allegro (1631), writing of "faery elves, / Whose midnight revels, by a forest side / Or fountain some belated peasant sees" — a passage that our context's "midnight revels under silver boughs" seems almost to echo. After Spenser and Milton, faerie belonged to the poets.

The modern era fractured the word in two. By the eighteenth century the collective faerie had been reanalyzed as a singular individual: the "fairy," a small winged creature of children's books, wearing the nursery's tinsel, and the original realm-sense faded from ordinary speech. But it never died. The ballad collectors and the great Victorian folklore revival — Keightley's The Fairy Mythology, and the collections of Croker and Campbell — kept the older, menacing sense alive, and the twentieth-century fantasy tradition restored it to the centre of English. J.R.R. Tolkien, in his essay "On Fairy-Stories" (1939), defined Faerie as "the Perilous Realm," the enchanted land that is beautiful, dangerous, and absolutely real within its own laws — a definition that has governed fantasy literature ever since. It is this revived, archaic, perilous FAERIE that modern writers use when they wish to escape the nursery and recover the mystery, beauty, and danger of the older folklore.

Summary of the eras:
  • PIE (c. 4th-3rd millennium BCE): *bheh2- "to speak, declare" — root of Latin fari "to speak."
  • Classical Latin (1st c. BCE): fatum "what is spoken (by the gods), destiny"; fatalis "fatal."
  • Vulgar Latin (c. 3rd-6th c.): *fata "goddess of destiny, fate-woman" — Romance fee, fata, hada, fada; English fay.
  • Old French (c. 12th c.): fae + -erie > faerie "enchantment, sorcery; the fairy realm."
  • Middle English (14th c.): faerie/faery/fayerye "the realm of fairies"; Chaucer's "fulfild of fayerye."
  • Spenser (1590) and Milton (1631): faerie fixed as an archaic, literary, perilous register.
  • 18th-19th centuries: reanalysis to singular "fairy" — the nursery word; the folklore revival keeps the realm-sense.
  • 20th century: Tolkien's "Perilous Realm"; modern high fantasy revives FAERIE.
