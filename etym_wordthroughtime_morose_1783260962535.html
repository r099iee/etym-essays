- [17th-century loan] - 'Morose' entered English in the 1600s (from Latin morosus, 'peevish, self-willed'), first meaning sour-tempered and ill-humoured, a word of character.
- [Psychological use] - In 18th and 19th century prose it named an habitual, churlish gloom - a temperamental sullenness that colours all judgment - the disposition of a man set against being pleased.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'morose' still denotes sullen, gloomy ill-temper, now applied both to persons and to moods and atmospheres, its suggestion of a peevish refusal to be pleased intact.
