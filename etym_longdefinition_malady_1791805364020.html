malady (/ˈmæl.ə.di/) is a countable noun denoting a disease or illness, especially one that is chronic, lingering, deep-seated or difficult to cure. It is one of the oldest learned synonyms of sickness in English, and it has always kept a certain dignity: a malady is something a person is afflicted with over a stretch of time, not a passing infection that merely interrupts a week.

The source passage uses the word at its gravest and most concrete. Louis XV, hastening across his kingdom in 1744 and suspending the Flanders campaign in order to go to the relief of Alsace, "was arrested at Metz by a malady which threatened to cut short his days": here malady names a dangerous acute fever that halts an army and puts a king's life at risk. The writer chose it over fever, attack or distemper because it keeps the diagnosis unspecified while lending the episode gravity. The syntactic frame is typical of the word: a malady seizes, strikes, prostrates, confines or threatens its victim.

Primary sense: disease of the body

  • A morbid condition of the body, whether acute or chronic: "a pulmonary malady", "a wasting malady", "the malady from which he suffered".
  • Often with a defining modifier naming the seat or kind of illness: a skin malady, a nervous malady, a strange malady of the blood. The construction is slightly old-fashioned; clinical writing now prefers disease or disorder.
  • In older medical English a malady could be either a named disease entity or a loose symptom-complex; humoral pathology spoke of maladies arising from corrupt humours, which is why the word so often appears beside humour, constitution and temperament in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century texts.
  • It does not by itself imply contagion (unlike infection), nor a specific cause (unlike deficiency or injury); it names the sufferer's condition from the inside, so to speak, and leaves aetiology open.

Secondary sense: disorder of the mind

  • By extension from the body to the psyche, a malady may be mental or emotional: "a malady of the soul", "the malady of melancholy". The extension is old; medieval and early modern religious prose routinely treated sin and despair as maladies requiring a physician, with Christ as medicus, so the metaphor of cure was ready-made.
  • Modern usage keeps this sense mainly in literary or rhetorical registers; clinical language would say mental illness or psychiatric disorder.

Figurative sense: a persistent ill in society or institutions

  • Since the eighteenth century the word has been applied to collective ills: "the maladies of the body politic", "the malady of the age", "a social malady". The bridge is the same medical metaphor — a society showing symptoms (unrest, corruption, inequality) is diagnosed and treated — so the malady is understood as structural and chronic rather than incidental.
  • Typical collocations: deep-seated malady, growing malady, incurable malady, the prevailing malady, a malady of long standing.
  • The word is also used, half-humorously, of a habitual obsession — "his little malady" for a harmless mania — a nineteenth-century conversational use that survives faintly.

Register, connotation and range

  • Register: formal and literary, now somewhat archaic in ordinary speech; in medicine it is obsolete except in historical or self-consciously elegant prose.
  • Intensity: stronger and more ominous than ailment, milder and less catastrophic than plague; it implies duration, suffering and often gravity, but makes no claim about contagion.
  • Countability: regularly countable (a malady, maladies), with a mass use in abstract writing ("the spread of malady").
  • Because it is a "soft" word, with no visible Greek or Latin technical root on its surface, it can sound evasive or euphemistic — which is precisely why Victorian novelists reach for it when a character's disease must remain unnamed.
  • It belongs to the same semantic field as disease, illness, sickness, ailment, infirmity, disorder, complaint, affliction and distemper, but occupies the literary, slightly archaic corner of it.

Why the word matters in this reading

The passage turns on a political fact: the king's malady at Metz produced a wave of public anxiety that crystallised into the epithet Bien-aimé. The choice of malady rather than a named fever keeps the focus on the human vulnerability of the sovereign and on his subjects' reaction, not on medical detail. Later writers would do the same with "the royal malady" of George III, where the phrase again works by indirection, naming a condition that could not be discussed plainly.
