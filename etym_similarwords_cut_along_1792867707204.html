• Hurry along: Similar because both mean to move fast down a route; differs because hurry along is plain and neutral, while cut along adds a crisp snap of sudden departure.
  • Hasten along: Similar because both urge swift forward motion; differs because hasten is formal and literary, while cut along is colloquial and brisk, the sergeant rather than the psalmist.
  • Dash along: Similar because both picture rapid energetic travel; differs because dash stresses violent speed and impact, while cut along stresses the smart instant start.
  • Scurry along: Similar because both describe small quick busy movement; differs because scurry suggests short pattering steps, mice and porters, while cut along suits a longer purposeful stride.
  • Hustle along: Similar because both push someone into brisk motion; differs because hustle implies jostling pressure or crowds, while cut along is a clean command to be off.
  • Run along: Similar because both dismiss or direct someone down a path; differs because run along can be gently dismissive, especially to children, while cut along keeps its conspiratorial urgency.
  • Push along: Similar because both keep travel moving forward; differs because push along labours against reluctance or heaviness, while cut along flies on willing legs.
  • Make tracks: Similar because both mean to leave quickly; differs because make tracks is frontier slang stressing visible departure, while cut along is English-lane briskness with a named route.
  • Cut and run: Similar because both share the sharp cut of sudden leaving; differs because cut and run flees trouble or responsibility, while cut along hurries toward a rendezvous.
  • Get along: Similar because both pair motion with along; differs because get along merely proceeds or manages, while cut along injects hurry and dash.
