The history of chattel is the history of counting heads — from Latin head to medieval livestock-wealth to modern movable property and enslaved person as property.

It begins with Proto-Indo-European *kaput- meaning head. Latin inherits it as caput, capitis (head, chief, life, capital city in later use). From caput Latin derives the adjective capitalis (pertaining to the head, capital, chief, punishable by loss of head) and its neuter substantive capitale (property, stock — literally what is counted by heads). Per-head counting of livestock was the ancient census method: Roman capitatio, Germanic head-taxes and medieval heriots all reckon wealth in heads.

  • Late Latin to Old French: Vulgar Latin *capitale (with stress shift and syncope) gives Old Northern French catel, catel and Central Old French chatel (12th c.), meaning movable goods, livestock, money, treasure — anything counted as stock. The Norman c- form (catel) versus Parisian ch- form (chatel) explains the English doublet: England, conquered by Normans, receives both. Chattel and cattle are thus the same etymon borrowed in two dialectal shapes and then semantically split. Old French chatel also yields capital (re-borrowed learnedly later), chapitel, cheptel (livestock lease, surviving as French cheptel — livestock, deadstock).
  • Middle English split (13th–14th c.): Early Middle English chatel, catel (c. 1200, as in Ancrene Wisse, Layamon) means goods, property, livestock, money indiscriminately. By Chaucer (late 14th c.) catel still covers both: the Wife of Bath's catel is wealth; the Nun's Priest's farm catel includes poultry. Gradually spelling and sense diverge: cattle (with -t- doubled, influenced by Norman catel) narrows to bovine livestock and then farm beasts generally; chattel (with Parisian ch- and learned -ttel) narrows to household movables and legal personalty. The 15th–16th c. legal handbooks cement goods and chattels as a fixed doublet.
  • Sound changes: Latin ca- /ka/ palatalises before a? Actually the shift is ca- > Old French cha- /tʃa/ in Central French (palatalisation of k before a), retained in English chattel /tʃætəl/; Norman-Picard retains /k/ giving catel > cattle /kætəl/. Latin -pit- simplifies, intervocalic -t- voices then devoices, final -e silences. English Great Vowel Shift does not greatly affect the short a, which remains /æ/ in RP.
  • Early Modern law and empire: Coke, Blackstone and conveyancers define chattels personal vs. real; inventories of the dead list chattels down to spoons. With Atlantic slavery (17th–19th c.), colonial codes (Barbados Slave Code 1661, Virginia statutes) explicitly rank enslaved Africans as chattel — chattel slaves as opposed to indentured servants with residual rights. Abolitionist literature seizes the word to expose commodification; pro-slavery law clings to it to defend heritability. After abolition the phrase chattel slavery fossilises as the historiographic term for absolute property-slavery versus other forced-labour systems.
  • Modern survivals: General speech keeps chattel mainly in goods and chattels and chattel slavery; lawyers keep chattel mortgage, chattel paper, chattel lease (increasingly replaced by personal property security). French cheptel and English capital, cattle remain living cognates reminding speakers that capitalism once literally meant head-counting. The source sentence — capital, chattel and cattle from capitale from caput (head of cattle) — compresses this millennium of head-counting into one clause.

Literary attestations track the split: Ayenbite of Inwyt (1340) chetel; Chaucer catel; Malory, Paston Letters catel/chatel; Shakespeare cattle for beasts, chattels for goods (Merchant of Venice Jew's chattels); Blackstone Commentaries II (1766) formal definition; 19th-c. abolitionist narratives (chattel as moral indictment). Socio-culturally the word moves from barnyard to courtroom to middle passage — from counting oxen heads to counting human heads as oxen — and its modern power derives from refusing to let that equation be forgotten.
