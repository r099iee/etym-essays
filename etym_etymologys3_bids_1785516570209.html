The verb bid — of which bids is the third-person singular present form — belongs to a small, distinguished class of English strong verbs whose semantic arc traces a complete reversal of social power. In Old English, biddan was the principal verb of humble, earnest request: one bæd (begged, prayed to) God, one bæd a lord for mercy, one bæd a favour of a friend. The word was the normal, unmarked term for "to ask," exactly as bitten still is in modern German. Old English biddan belonged to Class V of the strong verb system, with the vowel alternation i ~ æ ~ Ǣ ~ e (biddan – bæd – bǢdon – beden), structurally parallel to verbs like sittan "sit," licgan "lie," and giefan "give" — all verbs of basic everyday human activity. The past participle (ge)beden yielded the noun (ge)bed "prayer," which in Middle English became bede "prayer"; and in a fascinating semantic metonymy, the plural bedes "prayers" became the word for the small round objects on a rosary, each bede representing one prayer recited — giving Modern English bead. Every time a speaker says "bead" they are unwittingly preserving the Old English word for a prayer. How, then, did a verb of humble asking become a verb of lordly commanding? The answer lies in the pragmatics of medieval social hierarchy — what linguists call the social-meaning layer of vocabulary. In a society structured by rigid ranks, the same speech act, uttering a request, has radically different force depending on the relative status of speaker and addressee. When a peasant "bids" a lord for clemency, it is a plea. When a lord "bids" a vassal to attend him, it is a command, albeit one softened by the polite fiction of a request. Over centuries of feudal and courtly usage, the command sense of bid — always latent in the asymmetry of rank — hardened into the standard meaning, while the humble sense retreated. The development is a classic instance of what historical linguists call social semantic bleaching or politeness-driven drift, and its Germanic cousins show the same root taking different directions: German bitten stayed firmly in the "ask/request/pray" domain, Dutch bidden became "to pray," while English bid climbed the social ladder into authority. A second etymological strand complicates the picture. Old English possessed two distinct strong verbs: biddan "to ask, pray" (class V) and bēodan "to offer, proclaim, announce" (class II, related to the root of English bode and bead, and to the same PIE root *bʰewdʰ- that gives Greek peuthomai "to learn"). These two verbs converged in Middle English into a single verb "bid," and it is the bēodan line that supplies the modern commercial sense: to bid at auction is to offer a price, and the noun bid and the card-game bid "to declare one's tricks" are extensions of that "offer" meaning. The modern word therefore is a blend: its command-and-invite senses descend from the humble "ask/pray" verb, while its offer-and-declare senses descend from the "offer/proclaim" verb. This double ancestry explains why "bid" can mean both "to order" and "to offer money" — two verbs that sound alike after centuries of convergence. By the early modern period the strong forms of the command sense had begun to fossilise. Shakespeare could still write "I bade them good night" with perfect naturalness, and the Authorized Version of the Bible uses bid, bade, and bidden freely for divine and royal commands. But as English settled into its modern grammar, the strong pattern bid/bade/bidden retreated into literary and archaic use, while the weak pattern bid/bid/bid took over in the auction and card-game senses. The present-tense form bids, by contrast, has never lost currency: "he bids," "the Lord bids you" remain fully grammatical and, precisely because the strong past forms have become archaic, the whole verb now carries an elevated, old-fashioned, quasi-Biblical flavour in its command sense. That flavour is exactly what J.R.R. Tolkien — a professional philologist — exploited when he wrote "The Lord of the Eagles bids you to bring your prisoners to the Great Shelf." The Eagles are not merely animals; they are the sentient servants of Manwë, ancient and dignified, and their language must reflect that dignity. Tolkien could have written "commands" or "orders," but those words are blunt and military; he needed a verb whose courtesy and authority were fused, and bid — with its residual memory of "to ask" underneath "to command" — supplied precisely the chivalric, courtly, Beowulf-and-Sir-Gawain tone he wanted. The single word bids thus carries the whole social history of the verb: a prayer that became a command, an offer that became a price, and a fossilised politeness that still softens authority.
