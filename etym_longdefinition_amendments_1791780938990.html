amendments is the plural of amendment, a count noun meaning “a change made to a document, proposal or arrangement in order to correct or improve it”. In the source sentence — “Certain amendments must be made to these descriptions” — the word is used in its plain textual sense: specific corrections or adjustments have to be introduced into a set of descriptions before they can stand. The noun does not imply that the descriptions are worthless, only that they are not yet accurate.

Morphologically the plural is unremarkable: amendment + -s, with the singular from Old French amendement and ultimately Latin emendare “to remove a fault from”. The verb behind it, amend, is the same word that gives the shortened English verb mend: to amend is literally to “de-fault” a text.

Primary sense: a corrective change to a text

  • Textual correction. An amendment may repair a factual error, a clumsy phrase or an ambiguity: “minor amendments to the glossary”. In scholarly editing the near-synonym emendation is preferred for a conjectural correction to a corrupt manuscript.
  • Alteration of a proposal. The plural is standard where a document passes through successive drafts: “the committee accepted three amendments and rejected the fourth”.
  • Improvement rather than replacement. An amendment adjusts what is already there; it is not a redraft, a rewrite or a repeal. This boundary is the word’s defining feature.

Legislative, constitutional and legal senses

  • Parliamentary. In Westminster procedure a bill is examined clause by clause and members table amendments; a friendly amendment is one offered by a supporter to improve the drafting, while a wrecking or killer amendment is designed to make the bill unacceptable. Amendments are moved, debated, carried or defeated.
  • Constitutional. In the United States “the Amendments” are the articles added to the Constitution, above all the first ten, ratified in 1791 and known as the Bill of Rights: the First Amendment (speech, religion, assembly), the Second (arms), the Fourteenth (due process, equal protection). Amendment here is entrenched, requiring supermajorities; the word has become a proper noun by capitalisation.
  • Civil and commercial law. Pleadings may be amended with the court’s permission; contracts are amended by deed or written variation; patents and insurance policies are amended by endorsement. The plural often covers a schedule of changes appended to an agreement.

Technical and transferred senses

  • Soil amendment. In horticulture and agronomy, soil amendments are materials worked into the ground to improve its structure or fertility — compost, lime, gypsum, peat. The phrase is a twentieth-century extension of the “make better” core and is now extremely common in gardening writing.
  • Administrative and technical. Budget amendments, planning amendments, licence amendments: in bureaucracy the word is neutral and procedural, referring to any formal variation of an approved instrument.
  • Religious and moral (historical). From the fourteenth to the seventeenth century amendment of life meant repentance and moral reform — a sense still audible in the Prayer Book and in early modern sermons, but now archaic outside set phrases.

The semantic bridge

Latin menda was a blemish or fault; emendare meant to take that fault out. The Romance noun amendement carried the sense of “correction, reparation” into medieval English, where it covered both the mending of a text and the mending of a soul. When parliaments began to alter bills in writing, the same noun was ready to hand: a correction to a clause became an amendment to a bill. The step from “correction” to “any formal alteration” was small, and by the nineteenth century the word could name changes that were purely additive.

Register and collocations

  • Formal and institutional; neutral in tone, mildly positive in implication (an amendment improves), though in politics it can be a weapon.
  • Verbs that go with it: propose, table, move, draft, adopt, carry, accept, reject, vote on, ratify, introduce, withdraw.
  • Common phrases: constitutional amendment, budget amendment, technical amendment, friendly amendment, amendments to the agreement.
  • Not to be confused with amends (plural only), which means compensation for a wrong — “make amends” — not a textual change.
