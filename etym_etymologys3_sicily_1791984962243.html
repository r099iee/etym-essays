The history of the name Sicily is a history of three peoples and of the languages that displaced one another on the island.

The three peoples of the island

Ancient writers distinguished the Sicani in the west, the Elymians in the north-west around Eryx and Segesta, and the Sicels, or Siculi, in the east. Thucydides opens his account of the Sicilian expedition with an ethnography (VI.2) in which the Sicels are said to have crossed from Italy, displacing the Sicani; whatever the truth of that, the Sicels gave the island the name by which the Greeks knew it. Homer, earlier, calls the island Sikanie, after the other people — evidence that the Greeks first knew Sicily through the west, and that the name changed as their knowledge and their settlements shifted eastwards.

From ethnic name to place name

Greek Sikelía is simply the land of the Sikeloi, formed with the productive suffix -ía that also gives us Italia and Graecia in their Latin shapes. The name is political as much as geographical: to call the island “Sicel-land” was to name it after the people the Greek colonists were displacing. From the eighth century BCE the colonies — Naxos in 734, Syracuse in 733, then Gela, Acragas, Selinus and Himera — made the Greek name current throughout the Mediterranean, and it was the Greek name, not the Carthaginian or the indigenous one, that Rome inherited.

The Roman province

Latin Sicilia is a formal adaptation of the Greek, and it entered official language in 241 BCE when the island became Rome's first province after the defeat of Carthage. The provincial charter known as the lex Rupilia (132 BCE) and Cicero's speeches against Verres (70 BCE) are the great documents of Sicilian administration, and Cicero's Verrines are also the fullest ancient description of the island's cities, temples and tax-farming. For the Roman world Sicily meant grain, Greek art and slave estates; the name carried all three.

Medieval and modern transformations

Arabic Siqilliyya preserved the name through the Islamic centuries, and the Norman conquest of 1085 attached it to a kingdom that soon included the Italian mainland. The resulting anomaly is instructive: the “Kingdom of Sicily” came to be ruled from Naples, and the phrase “the kingdom beyond the lighthouse” distinguished the island from the mainland portion. In 1816 the two were formally merged as the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, an arrangement that lasted until Garibaldi's landing in 1860.

Into English

  • The name reached English through Old French Sicile, and Middle English texts use Sicile and Sicilye interchangeably.
  • The final -y of the modern spelling is an English remodelling, probably by analogy with Italy; the -e forms survive in older poetry and in the Latinising Sicilia.
  • Shakespeare, drawing on Italian sources, sets The Winter's Tale partly in “Sicilia” and uses “Sicilian” as an adjective of courtly polish — the Latin form signals romance and distance.
  • Eighteenth-century travellers and excavators made the island a station of the Grand Tour, and the name acquired the double charge it still has: Greek temples and sulphur mines, learning and poverty.

What the name carries now

Modern Sicily is an autonomous region of Italy with its own parliament in Palermo and its own Romance language, sicilianu, which is not a dialect of Italian but a separate descendant of Latin with Greek, Arabic and Norman French strata. The island name, meanwhile, has become a test case in debates about Mediterranean migration, about the Mezzogiorno, and about the ethics of archaeological collecting. In the reading, none of that is present: Sicily appears only as the province where a young Roman quaestor learned his trade — but the density of the later history is exactly what makes a single place name able to carry so much.
