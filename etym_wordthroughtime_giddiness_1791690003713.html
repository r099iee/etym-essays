- 9th–11th Century: Old English Demonic Madness & Divine Possession - Originated as Old English *gidig* / *gydig* (derived from Proto-Germanic *gud-iga-, 'possessed by a god or divine spirit', from *gudą 'god'), denoting religious frenzy, violent lunacy, delirium, or being divinely insane.
- 12th–14th Century: Middle English Bleaching toward Physical Vertigo - As the pagan concept of divine possession faded under Christianization, *gidi* softened semantically from demonic mania into physical unsteadiness, swimming of the head, and bodily swooning caused by illness, intoxication, or spinning in circles.
- 16th–Early 17th Century: Intellectual Instability & Baconian Skepticism - Augmented by the abstract nominal suffix *-ness* to form *giddiness*, expanding from physical dizziness to signify intellectual fickleness, moral flightiness, and an aversion to settled dogma (crystallized by Francis Bacon in 1625: 'delight in giddiness, and count it a bondage to fix a belief').
- 18th–19th Century: Romantic Exuberance & Youthful High Spirits - Shifted increasingly toward joyful, breathless excitement, romantic flutter, bubbling feminine vivacity, or manic high spirits in domestic literature (Jane Austen, Charlotte Brontë).
- 20th–21st Century: Dual Clinical & Socio-Economic Usage - Functions in modern English across two primary registers: clinically as an informal, everyday term for mild vestibular disorientation, and socio-economically as a critique of manic financial bubbles, irrational market exuberance, and rapid cultural attention churn.
