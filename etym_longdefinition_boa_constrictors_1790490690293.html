boa-constrictors (singular boa-constrictor) are very large, non-venomous, heavy-bodied snakes of tropical America that kill prey by constriction — coiling round it and tightening with each breath until circulation fails. The name properly denotes Boa constrictor, the common boa (2–4 metres), but in Victorian popular use, and in the source simile — pipes like boa-constrictors — it means any huge crushing serpent: boas, pythons, and anacondas alike. The child Ruskin sees leather water-hoses, thick, ribbed, flexible, dripping, and reads them as captive snakes: dark coils lying on Brunswick Square pavement, swelling with water as a boa swells with a meal. The image joins natural history to street machinery, a favourite Ruskinian double vision.

Zoologically, true boa-constrictors belong to family Boidae, genus Boa, species constrictor, with subspecies across Central and South America and the Caribbean. They are viviparous, nocturnal ambush hunters, taking rats, bats, birds, opossums, and small deer. Contrary to legend they do not crush bones nor suffocate by preventing lung expansion alone; modern physiology shows they arrest blood flow, causing rapid cardiac collapse. They are excellent swimmers and climbers, long-lived (25–40 years in captivity), and famously tolerant of handling, which made them circus, menagerie, and Regent's Park Zoological Gardens favourites from the 1830s. A London child of the 1820s could have seen them in travelling shows and in book-plate engravings before ever seeing a hose.

  • Strict taxonomic sense: Boa constrictor Linnaeus 1758: e.g. a red-tailed boa-constrictor, the Trinidad population of boa-constrictors. Tan with dark saddles, iridescent, heat-sensitive labial pits. This is the dictionary head sense, always hyphenated in British use, often closed (boaconstrictor) in older texts.
  • Popular giant-snake sense (source sense): e.g. as thick as a boa-constrictor, pipes like boa-constrictors. Any enormous constricting snake, including Indian pythons and green anacondas. The bridge is size plus method: if it is huge and squeezes, Victorians call it a boa-constrictor regardless of genus.
  • Boa as garment (first-element background): a long feather or fur stole worn round the neck, named for its snake-like coils; e.g. an ostrich-feather boa, a music-hall boa. The bridge is shape: the stole lies round the throat as a boa lies round prey. Understanding this prevents confusion when 19th-century novels mention ladies' boas beside live ones.
  • Constrictor as functional noun (second-element background): any animal or muscle that constricts; e.g. constrictor muscles of the throat, the Venezuelan red-tailed constrictor. In anatomy, constrictor pharyngis squeezes food down; in herpetology, constrictor marks the killing method versus venom.
  • Figurative stranglehold sense: e.g. the boa-constrictor of debt, a boa-constrictor grip on trade, bureaucratic boa-constrictors. Anything that slowly squeezes life, money, or freedom is a boa-constrictor. The bridge is method mapped to institutions: gradual tightening, no single bite, inescapable pressure.
  • Colloquial exaggeration: e.g. to eat like a boa-constrictor (gorging then fasting), to digest like a boa-constrictor. Boas take huge meals at long intervals, fasting weeks after a deer; human gluttony is likened to that rhythm.

The hyphen and plural matter. Victorian printers hyphenated transparent natural-history compounds (boa-constrictor, rattlesnake, hammer-shark) and pluralised the whole (boa-constrictors) rather than the first element. To write boas constrictor would be pedantic Latin; to write boa constrictors open is modern American habit. Ruskin's hyphenated plural is standard 1820s–1880s book English, preserved in the note exactly as the child would have read it in Goldsmith's Animated Nature or watched it chalked on a menagerie van.
