Introduction. Note is a word that began as a mark and became a civilisation. Its ancestor, Latin nota, meant "a mark, sign, distinguishing token" — something stamped or written so that a thing could be recognised. From that single image, English has drawn musical tones and written symbols, lecture notes and love notes, banknotes and footnotes, attention and reputation, and the quiet "note" of character in a voice — the sense Tolkien uses when the rumble of Smaug's snoring "changed its note." Every one of these senses is a mark: something by which a thing is recognised. The history of the word is the history of all the things humans have marked, and all the ways we have used marks to know the world.

Phase 1 — the Indo-European root of knowing (before 500 BCE).

  • The reconstructed PIE root *ǵneh₃- meant "to know." It is one of the great roots of the family, with descendants in nearly every branch: Sanskrit jñā-, Greek gignṓskein "to know" (the source of gnosis, diagnosis, prognosis), Latin gnōscere/nōscere, and — through Germanic — English know and can ("to know how," the ancestor of "can" = be able).
  • The Latin verb nōscere meant "to get to know, learn, recognise," and its past participle nōtus "known, recognised" generated a whole family: notitia "knowledge, fame," notāre "to mark, note," and nota itself — the mark by which something known is identified.

Phase 2 — Latin nota: the mark (1st century BCE onward).

  • Nota in classical Latin meant a mark, sign, or distinguishing token: the brand on an animal, the stamp on a coin, the mark in a book. It could be a secret sign (the notae of shorthand, notae Tironianae — Tiro's shorthand marks for recording speech) or a public disgrace: the nota censoria was the mark of censure the Roman censors set against a citizen's name, stripping him of rank — the ancestor of "a note of shame."
  • From "mark" came the verbs: notāre "to mark, note," adnotāre "to mark down, annotate," and the nouns of marking: notārius "a shorthand writer, recorder" (English notary), notitia "notice, fame" (English notice, notoriety).
  • In the medieval church and scriptorium, nota took its decisive musical step: the written mark that stood for a tone — the ancestor of the modern musical note and of the word notation itself. The mark, which had identified things, now identified sounds.

Phase 3 — Old French and Middle English: the mark arrives (10th–13th centuries).

  • Old French note carried the Latin senses — mark, sign, note — into the Anglo-French of post-Conquest England, and English took the noun note in the 13th century with two senses already prominent: the general "mark, sign, token," and the musical note (a mark of the scale).
  • Chaucer and his contemporaries use note of marks, of music, and of reputation: "a man of note" is a man marked out. The verb noten "to mark, observe" appears alongside; "to note" a thing is to mark it in the mind — the birth of the attention sense.

Phase 4 — the 14th to 16th centuries: records, letters, and promises.

  • By the 14th–15th centuries a note was a brief written record — something marked down for memory — and then a short message: "a note" to a friend. The written mark had become the written communication.
  • The 16th century added the financial sense: a "note of hand" (also bill of exchange) was a written promise to pay — a document marked with a sum and a signature. From this came the banknote: paper money as a marked promise, first issued in significant quantity in the 17th–18th centuries. "A ten-pound note" is literally a written mark that pays.
  • The musical sense matured in the same period: printed notation, note values, the note as the atom of melody — the word became part of the universal language of music.

Phase 5 — the 17th to 19th centuries: scholarship, diplomacy, and the "note" of feeling.

  • Scholarship made the note a fixture of the page: the footnote (17th century onward) is a mark at the foot of the page annotating the text; "a note on X" became the standard title of a short learned communication.
  • Diplomacy adopted the note as a formal written communication between states — still standard usage today ("a diplomatic note," "an exchange of notes").
  • Prose writers of the 18th–19th centuries developed the character-sense: "a note of warning," "a note of sadness in her voice" — the perceptible quality by which a feeling or a place is recognised. This is the sense Tolkien deploys: the snoring's note is the dragon's identifying sound, and its change is the signal of waking.

Phase 6 — the twentieth century and today.

  • In 1937 J.R.R. Tolkien wrote in The Hobbit: "the rumble of his snoring changed its note. Then Bilbo fled." The sentence uses the oldest logic of the word: the note is the mark by which you know a thing, and when the mark changes, what you know has changed — the sleeping dragon is becoming the waking one.
  • Today note is alive in every register at once: musical notes and sticky notes, banknotes and lecture notes, footnotes and thank-you notes, "take note" and "of note," "strike a note" and "compare notes" — and, in the digital age, the note-taking app has made the humble note more ubiquitous than ever.
  • The whole family — notice, notable, noteworthy, notation, notary, notify, notorious — still turns on the same Latin mark: to notice is to mark in the mind, to notify is to make known, a notary records marks of legal fact, and notorious is "too well marked." One mark, five centuries of English, and a civilisation of things worth knowing.

Summary. From PIE *ǵneh₃- "to know," through Latin nōscere "to know" and nota "mark," the medieval music of notation, the 13th-century English noun, and the proliferating marks of record, money, scholarship, diplomacy, and feeling — note has always meant the same thing: the sign by which something is recognised. A banknote is a marked promise; a musical note is a marked sound; a man of note is a marked man; and when Smaug's snoring changed its note, the mark by which Bilbo knew the dragon was asleep had changed too — and so he fled.
