• shrieking: Similar because both name high-pitched piercing cries; differs because shriek implies a human or animal cry of alarm and is more intense and sudden, while shrilling can be any piercing sound, including instruments.
  • screeching: Similar because both are harsh high-pitched sounds; differs because screech implies grating friction (brakes, birds) and discord, while shrill is piercing but not necessarily grating.
  • squealing: Similar because both are high-pitched prolonged sounds; differs because squeal is thinner and more nasal (pigs, tyres, excited children), often with a sliding pitch.
  • squeaking: Similar because both are high-pitched; differs because squeak is short, soft and intermittent (mice, hinges), while shrilling is loud and sustained.
  • whistling: Similar because both are high, piercing, sustained tones; differs because whistle is musical and produced by air through a small aperture, while shrill is harsher and can come from any source.
  • skirling: Similar because both name high-pitched sounds of instruments; differs because skirl is specifically the Scots word for the bagpipe's sound and is a different word origin.
  • keening: Similar because both are high-pitched sustained cries; differs because keen is specifically the Irish/Scottish lament for the dead — ritual and mournful.
  • ululating: Similar because both are high-pitched vocal wails; differs because ululation is a rhythmic, modulated, culturally specific cry (Middle East, Africa), not a general piercing sound.
  • wailing: Similar because both are loud high cries; differs because wail is longer, lower and full of grief, while shrill emphasises pitch and penetration.
  • piercing: Similar because both describe sound that cuts through other sound; differs because piercing is an adjective describing effect, while shrilling is the act or sound itself.
  • strident: Similar because both mean loud, harsh and insistent; differs because strident stresses unpleasant loudness and insistence rather than high pitch.
  • sharp: Similar because both describe a cutting acoustic quality; differs because sharp can describe any brief high note, while shrill implies sustained piercing quality.
  • treble: Similar because both involve high pitch; differs because treble is a neutral musical-register term, while shrill carries judgement of unpleasantness.
  • high-pitched: Similar because both denote high frequency; differs because high-pitched is a neutral descriptive term, while shrill adds piercing intensity and often negative connotation.
  • harsh: Similar because both are unpleasant to the ear; differs because harsh can be low and rasping, while shrill is specifically high and piercing.
