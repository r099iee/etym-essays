- Late 14th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French; utilized in religious and philosophical contexts (Chaucer, Wycliffe) to translate Latin *felicitas*, signifying supreme spiritual bliss, divine beatitude, or the ultimate heavenly good (*summum bonum*).
- 16th to 17th Century (Renaissance & Early Modern English) - Expands from spiritual beatitude to worldly prosperity, civil flourishing, and domestic joy; crucially develops its rhetorical sense (Shakespeare, Bacon) of a happy knack, inspired elegance, or striking aptness of literary phrasing.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - Becomes a cardinal virtue of neoclassical aesthetics and literary criticism (Pope, Johnson); critics praise "felicity of expression" as the hallmark of poetic genius that expresses nature "to advantage dress'd."
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Widely used in novelistic prose (Jane Austen, George Eliot) to characterize domestic harmony, romantic compatibility ("marital felicity"), and refined conversational charm.
- 20th Century - Formalized into linguistic philosophy through J. L. Austin's speech act theory ("felicity conditions"), denoting the contextual criteria necessary for a performative utterance to be legally or pragmatically valid.
- 21st Century - Retains both its classical literary register as a compliment for exquisite prose style and its technical linguistic usage, standing as an enduring mark of communicative grace and human flourishing.
