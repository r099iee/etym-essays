LULL (verb; RP /l'l/). The word means to soothe or quiet down, especially to induce sleep or rest, by gentle, rhythmic, repetitive means. In the opening of Wordsworth's Prelude the verb closes the poet's great question of repose: "and what clear stream / Shall with its murmur lull me into rest?" Here the agent of the soothing is nature herself: the "murmur" of a "clear stream" is personified as a nurse or mother who rocks the weary poet into rest with the low continuous music of flowing water. The verb is chosen for its sound as much as its sense — the soft liquid l-, the murmured back vowel, the doubled final consonant enact in miniature the very sound they describe, a word that croons as it is spoken. The tone is tender, restorative, and deeply hopeful: the poet asks what stream will lull him, trusting that some natural lullaby exists to receive his exhaustion. The primary sense of lull is transitive: to calm, quieten, or send a person (or creature, or even a troubled feeling) into sleep or stillness by gentle means. One lulls a baby with a lullaby, lulls an anxious mind with a steady rhythm, lulls a room into peace with music. The intransitive use ("the wind lulled," "the storm lulled") means the disturbance itself subsides of its own accord, growing quiet — a sense particularly at home in nautical and meteorological writing. And as a noun, lull names the temporary quiet that follows activity: a lull in the storm, a lull in conversation, a lull in the fighting, a lull in trade. The noun is a back-formation from the verb, but it has become so thoroughly naturalized that it now often leads the meaning in the minds of speakers. The word's full semantic range radiates around the image of gentle quietening:
  • To soothe to sleep: to calm a person, especially a child, into sleep by gentle sound or motion — "she lulled the baby to sleep," "the rocking of the boat lulled him." This is the oldest and most central sense, the verb of lullabies.
  • To quieten by rhythm or sound: to make someone drowsy or peaceful through continuous, soft, repetitive stimuli — "the rain lulled her anxious thoughts," "the music lulled the whole room." The agent is typically a sound, a motion, or an atmosphere rather than a person.
  • To calm or suppress (feelings, suspicions, vigilance): to soothe a mental state so that it quiets — "to lull one's fears," and, with a darker twist, "to lull someone into a false sense of security," i.e. to quiet their vigilance precisely in order to betray it. This idiom gives the word its treacherous secondary life.
  • Intransitive (of storms, winds, seas): to subside, grow calm, fall still — "the wind lulled toward evening," "the sea lulled." The disturbance dies down by itself.
  • Noun — temporary quiet: a brief period of stillness or decreased activity between disturbances — "a lull in the storm," "a lull in conversation," "a lull in the fighting." The noun implies calm that may not last, a pause that will be followed by more activity.
  • Noun — deceptive calm: in fixed phrases the noun also names the quiet that conceals danger, the "lull before the storm" when everything is still and menacing.
Semantically the crucial features are gentleness and rhythm. Lull never means to silence abruptly or by force; it always implies a gradual, almost hypnotic easing into quiet, usually by sound or motion. That is what distinguishes it from hush (sudden, commanding), quieten (mere reduction of noise), and sedate (medical drugging). The word also carries a strong association with tenderness and care — the nursery, the cradle, the mother's voice — which is why its transferred uses (a stream lulling a poet, music lulling a room) feel so warm and restorative. Its sound symbolism reinforces the meaning: the prolonged liquid l and the murmured vowel suggest a voice running down to a hush, and etymologists class the word as echoic or imitative for exactly this reason. In register, lull is a word of everyday availability that has also been a favorite of poets for six hundred years. It is neither slang nor archaic: one can say "the noise lulled me to sleep" in ordinary conversation, and yet its soft, crooning quality has always recommended it to verse, where it appears in descriptions of rest, nature, and the passage of consciousness into sleep. The noun is fully current in journalism ("a lull in the talks," "a lull in the market") and in weather reporting ("a temporary lull in the winds"). The word's only real danger is the negative idiom: to lull someone into a false sense of security is to calm them harmfully, and in security, military, and business writing the verb is often used in exactly this suspicious sense. Learning lull means learning both halves — the nurse's croon and the con-artist's calm — and recognizing that the same quieting power can be either kindness or treachery depending on who wields it.
