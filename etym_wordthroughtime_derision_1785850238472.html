- Late 14th c. Middle English derisioun: scornful mockery, laughing someone to scorn — perceived as pointed, often public contempt rather than private dislike
- 15th–16th c.: religious and humanist polemic — holding doctrines or persons "in derision" as a moral-social weapon; association with shame and orthodoxy battles
- 17th–18th c.: satire, comedy, and parliamentary culture — derision as rhetorical theater; "howls of derision" crystallize the image of collective jeering
- 19th c.: novelistic and journalistic expansion — objects of derision include failed inventors, fallen politicians, social climbers; tone of respectable scorn
- 20th c.: still formal-literary; contrasts with slang mockery; used for institutional or crowd contempt
- Contemporary: applies to media pile-ons and public humiliation events while retaining a slightly elevated lexical color — not casual banter but charged, dismissive ridicule
