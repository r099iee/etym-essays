- Old English (c. 8th–11th Century) - Attested as *smoc*, denoting a woman's primary linen undergarment, chemise, or shift worn against the bare skin; viewed as an intimate, essential foundation garment.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century) - Spelled *smok* or *smocke*; heavily associated with female domesticity and intimate modesty; spawned bawdy idioms and colloquial compounds relating to women and marital politics.
- 17th to 18th Century (Early Modern to Georgian Era) - Begins its semantic transition from an intimate interior undergarment to an exterior utilitarian work garment; the compound *smock-frock* emerges across rural England as the standard outer tunic for agricultural laborers and wagoners.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Reaches its cultural zenith in rural and artistic life; worn universally by farm boys, shepherds, and village youth (as in Oscar Wilde's *The Remarkable Rocket*), while also adopted by painters as studio workwear; concurrent emergence of *smocking* as an artistic needlework technique for children's fashion.
- 20th to 21st Century - Largely recedes as everyday agricultural attire, surviving as a protective garment for preschoolers, artists, and medical personnel, and as a timeless decorative gathered style in high-end children's clothing.
