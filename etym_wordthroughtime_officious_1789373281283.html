- Late 16th Century (Renaissance) - Borrowed into English from Latin *officiōsus*; utilized in an exclusively positive and complimentary sense to describe individuals, angels, or servants who are dutiful, obliging, kind, and full of good offices.
- 17th Century (Jacobean & Restoration Eras) - Continues in high literature (Milton, Dryden) as a term of praise for attentive service and eager helpfulness, though the first subtle hints of excessive, fawning servility begin to emerge in satirical plays.
- 18th Century (Augustan Age) - A profound semantic divergence occurs; while Samuel Johnson's dictionary still defines it primarily as "kind, doing good offices," writers increasingly use it pejoratively to censure busybodies whose uninvited assistance becomes an aggressive nuisance.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - The pejorative meaning completely overwhelms the positive sense; *officious* becomes the standard term of contempt for self-important minor bureaucrats, overzealous policemen, and pedantic editors.
- 20th to 21st Century - Securely entrenched in modern English as a sharp condemnation of intrusive meddling, petty bureaucratic tyrants, and gratuitous editorial tampering, with its archaic positive meaning surviving only in historical texts and legal glosses.
