Single root word: TONGS. The chain runs from a well-attested PIE "bite" root to the modern plural noun; the "biting" metaphor is the word's spine. Proto-Indo-European *denk- "to bite, clamp, seize" (reconstructed root; attested reflexes: Sanskrit daśati "he bites," Greek daknein "to bite," both from the zero-grade form *dn̥k-) → Proto-Germanic *tangō "that which bites; gripping instrument" (reconstructed noun; the name of the tool by metaphor — the tongs seize an object the way jaws seize food; also *tangi- "pointed end, prong, fang") → Old English tange / tong / tanga "tongs, pincers, forceps; the smith's gripping instrument" (noun; recorded from c. 9th–10th c.; already used in the plural for the two-limbed tool) → Middle English tonges / tanges / tongis "tongs" (noun; 12th–15th c.; the plural form crystallizes as the standard, a plurale tantum like scissors and shears) → Early Modern English tongs "two-limbed gripping instrument" (noun; singular-construction "pair of tongs" recorded; idiom "hammer and tongs" recorded 1708) → Modern English TONGS (noun; plurale tantum: the two-armed gripping tool of forge, hearth, and kitchen). Note on debate: the PIE *denk- "to bite" etymology is standard and secure for the Germanic family; the zero-grade form *dn̥k- explains the Greek daknein and Sanskrit daśati. No serious alternative etymology has been proposed for tongs; the only nuance to flag is the plural-only grammar, which is a Middle English innovation of form (the tool of two limbs spoken of as a pair) rather than a change of meaning.
