- 16th-century coinage - to waylay is to lay oneself in the way, to lie in wait beside a road or path in order to stop, rob or attack a traveller; the verb names the stock profession of the highwayman
- 17th-18th century - the age of the highwayman fixes waylay as the crime of the open road: concealed agents springing on the unsuspecting, halting journeys by stealth; the word carries menace, cunning and the terror of the king's highway
- 19th century widening - as highway robbery declines, the word widens to any unexpected and importunate stopping: to be waylaid by a creditor, a journalist or an eager acquaintance is to be intercepted without a crime
- Contemporary sense - the defining ideas remain surprise, secrecy and the interruption of onward progress, but the modern use is more often inconvenience than violence — waylaid in a corridor by a colleague eager for conversation
