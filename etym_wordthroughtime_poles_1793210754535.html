- Old English — the stake in the ground: pal names pointed timbers for fencing and palisades, the farm's pointed punctuation.
- Medieval — gardens and ships: poles prop vines and beans ashore while masts, the great sea-poles, carry sail aloft; one word serves field and fleet.
- 14th c. — the learned twin arrives: astronomers borrow Latin polus for the sky's pivot and the earth's ends, and explorers inherit the dream of reaching them.
- 16th–18th c. — the hop centuries: Kent and Surrey coppices feed thousands of acres of hop-poles, and pole-cutting becomes a woodland trade with its own seasons and prices.
- 19th c. — wires on poles: the telegraph and telephone plant avenues of creosoted poles beside every railway and high road, the hop-pole's industrial heir.
- Today — wire, sport, and science: telegraph poles yield to buried cable while ski-poles, vaulting poles, and magnetic poles keep the old length and the old extremes alive.
