The first sense of “ardour” is a physical one: heat, flame, the burning of a fire. Its root is the Proto-Indo-European base *as-, “to burn, to glow, to be dry”, one of the family’s oldest fire-words. The Indo-Iranian branch preserved it in Vedic Sanskrit āsa-, “ashes, cinders” — the grey residue a fire leaves behind — and some scholars connect the same base with the Hittite word for the hearth. Greek developed the “dry” side of the root: azō, “to dry, parch”, and azaleos, “dry, parched”, which gave the name of the azalea, the flower that thrives in dry, well-drained soil, and which in Greek poetry could describe anything scorched and parched by sun or drought. The root thus stood at the meeting-point of two sensations — dryness and burning — that human language has always treated as near neighbours, since what is hot dries, and what is dry burns.
  • Latin splits the root: Latin split the root in two: ārēre, “to be dry”, and ardēre, “to burn, to be on fire”. From ārēre came āridus, “dry, parched”, hence English arid and aridity, and assāre, “to roast”, whose Romance descendants (Portuguese assado) still mean roasted meat.
  • The burning family: from ardēre came a rich family: arsus, “burnt”, which passed through Anglo-French arson into English as arson, the criminal act of burning; ardēns, “burning, blazing”, whence English ardent, the burning lover or supporter; and, above all, the noun ardor, “heat, flame”.
  • Fire as emotion in Latin literature: Latin authors had long used fire as the metaphor of emotion, and they pressed ardor into service for every passion that burns in the mind — love, courage, eagerness, rage. Cicero speaks of the ardor of the spirit, Virgil sets ardor in the breast of warriors, and Ovid makes the lover’s ardor the fire that will not be quenched. This figurative “burning” is the direct ancestor of our word.
  • Through Old French and Middle English: Old French took ardor as ardeur, “heat, passion, zeal”, and Middle English borrowed it around 1400 as “ardour”, at first still meaning “burning heat” but already reaching for the figurative flame. The British spelling ardour keeps the French form; the American ardor restores the Latin.
In English the word’s history is a climb from weather to worship to scholarship. In the fourteenth century ardour meant heat and flame themselves — the temperature of a fire or the blaze of a burning house. By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries the fire-metaphor had extended into the heart: ardour became the burning intensity of love, desire, and devotion, glowing with romantic and religious warmth, so that a lover’s ardour and a martyr’s ardour shared the same flame. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word migrated to learning and enterprise — the ardour of scholars, reformers, and soldiers — elevated and honourable, still fiery but directed at causes and crafts; the passage’s “scholarly and critical ardour” belongs exactly here, where the flame is spent on prosody and the study of metre. Today ardour is formal and bookish, naming a sustained, glowing commitment to a cause or study, and it can carry a gently ironic edge when the devotion is comic or excessive — as when this chapter surveys the “comically idiosyncratic” handbooks whose authors burned with love for their subject even when the results were laboriously obvious. It is a connoisseur’s word, and the passage uses it with the affectionate mockery of a scholar who knows the fire intimately, honours its heat, and smiles at the shapes it takes.
