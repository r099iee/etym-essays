- [Late 16th c. learned entry (c. 1580)] - Borrowed from Latin disiūnctus 'unyoked, separated, set apart', the past participle of disiungere 'to unyoke'; in English it was a formal, bookish adjective meaning simply 'disconnected, not joined' - neutral in tone, with the vivid yoking image still audible inside its prefix dis- ('apart') + root 'join'.
- [Rhetorical & logical technicalisation (17th-19th c.)] - The word hardened into a term of art: in logic a 'disjunct' names one of the alternatives joined by 'or' in a disjunction, so the members of a disjunction are themselves disjunct (separated) alternatives. Public perception narrowed from general description to specialist vocabulary of the grammar school and the logic lecture.
- [Grammatical sense (20th c., Quirk) ] - Modern linguistics gave it a precise new niche: a 'disjunct' is a sentence adverb such as frankly or fortunately that stands outside the clause structure, commenting on the sentence from without - a sense of detached, exterior observation.
- [Modern register] - Today 'disjunct' is overwhelmingly formal and technical; in ordinary speech it is far outnumbered by 'disconnected' and 'separate'. Its most characteristic modern use is in precisely argued prose - 'not disjunct from but intimately bedded in' - where the negation turns the unyoking image into a claim of deep union, so the word now reads as a stylist's signal of careful, contrastive analysis rather than a casual description.
