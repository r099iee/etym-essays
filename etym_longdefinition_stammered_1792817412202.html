Stammered is the past tense and past participle of stammer: to speak with involuntary stoppages — repeated syllables, prolonged sounds, and blocked restarts — so that the words come out tripping, halting, and doubling back on themselves. It is the voice of nerves made audible: fear, embarrassment, awe, or a constitutional impediment jamming the machinery of fluent speech.

In the source sentence — ‘Well, er, yes, I suppose so,’ stammered Bilbo — the verb is a complete portrait of the speaker's state. Bilbo is cornered, flustered, buying time with fillers (well, er, yes) before surrendering to reluctant assent, and the dialogue tag tells the reader exactly how the assent sounded: not crisp and willing but stumbled out, each syllable catching on the one before. No adverb is needed; stammered carries the whole psychology of a hobbit who would rather be anywhere else.

Core meaning in detail: A stammer interrupts speech at the level of motor control: the lips, tongue, or vocal cords repeat a movement (b-b-b-bad), stretch a sound (ssssorry), or lock entirely (a silent block with the mouth open). Clinicians distinguish developmental stammering (childhood onset, often easing with therapy), acquired stammering (after stroke or trauma), and situational disfluency — the ordinary speaker's er and um under pressure, which is what Bilbo displays. The verb covers all three in everyday use, from the medical to the merely flustered.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To speak with halting repetitions (primary sense): he stammered an apology — syllables repeated, sounds prolonged, blocks and restarts. The bridge is the impediment itself: the word names the oldest and most recognised form of broken fluency, familiar in every classroom and courtroom.
  • To utter falteringly from nerves or awe (literary/dialogue sense, Tolkien's use): she stammered her thanks. The bridge runs from pathology to emotion: extreme feeling borrows the mechanics of the impediment, so even fluent speakers stammer when terrified, smitten, or caught out — the body confessing what the mouth would smooth over.
  • To falter or proceed unsteadily — machines and performances (figurative): the engine stammered and died, the broadcast stammered back to life. The bridge is acoustic: a failing motor coughs in repeated spasms exactly as a stammerer repeats syllables, so the speech defect lends its name to every jerky restart.
  • The noun — a stammer (secondary): he spoke with a nervous stammer. The bridge is zero-derivation, the verb's action frozen into a thing: the habit, the condition, the single episode, all available as one compact noun.
  • Stammerer — the person (agent noun): from Demosthenes with his pebbles to modern support groups, the stammerer is among history's most storied figures of perseverance. The bridge is the agent suffix doing its ordinary work, but the cultural freight is extraordinary — the stammerer as the emblem of eloquence fighting its way out.
  • Stutter — the twin verb: in British use stammer and stutter split the same territory almost interchangeably, while American usage prefers stutter. The bridge is doublet history: two Germanic verbs for one human trouble, divided less by meaning than by ocean.

Register and connotation: stammered is tender where snorted is harsh — it asks for sympathy, not judgment. As a dialogue tag it instantly softens a character: the stammering witness is believed, the stammering lover is forgiven, the stammering hobbit is loved. Only mockery reverses the charge, mimicking a stammer being among the oldest cruelties of the playground. Clinicians and advocates now prefer person-first language (person who stammers), but the verb itself remains neutral and indispensable — the single word that says speech itself is struggling.
