RELISH is a noun of two faces and a verb of one: as a noun it means great enjoyment, zest, or keen appreciation for something, and also a piquant or savory condiment eaten with food to add flavour; as a verb it means to enjoy eagerly, to look forward to, or to take keen pleasure in. In the reading passage the noun carries its most abstract and psychological sense: 'To drink heartily, copiously, and with relish in large draughts' — the word names the eager enjoyment with which the drinking is done, the appetite and gusto that make each large draught a pleasure. The sentence stacks three adverbial expressions ('heartily', 'copiously', 'with relish') to build one picture of eager, abundant, pleasurable drinking, and relish supplies the emotional colour: not merely quantity or vigour, but keen, savouring enjoyment.

CORE SENSES

  • Enjoyment / zest (psychological noun): keen enjoyment, gusto, lively appreciation — 'to eat with relish', 'he faced the task with relish', 'she recounted the story with relish'. The word names appetite turned into pleasure.
  • Condiment (culinary noun): a piquant or spicy food accompaniment, typically of chopped pickled vegetables or fruit — 'pickle relish', 'tomato relish', 'a jar of relish'. In this sense it is a countable noun: a relish, two relishes.
  • Distinctive flavour or trace (noun): a pleasing tang, flavour, or seasoning quality, and by extension a characteristic quality or hint — 'a relish of garlic', 'a relish of irony', 'her remarks had a relish of malice'.
  • Enjoy eagerly (verb): to take great pleasure in, to savour, to enjoy keenly — 'I relish a challenge', 'she relished every moment'; also 'to look forward to' — 'he relished the prospect'.
  • 'To give relish to' (transitive verb): to add flavour or interest to something — 'spices give relish to a plain dish'; by extension, to make something more enjoyable or interesting.
  • Hunting sense (archaic): the scent or trail left by hunted game, and by extension anything that excites pursuit or appetite — the original meaning from which all others grew.

POLYSEMY, JARGON, AND METAPHORIC SHIFTS

  • Culinary register: in modern food English, 'relish' is the standard generic term for a piquant condiment of chopped vegetables or fruit, distinct from a sauce (liquid), a chutney (often sweeter and Indian-influenced), and a pickle (whole or larger pieces); the word entered cookery as a countable noun in the 18th–19th centuries and is now ubiquitous on supermarket shelves.
  • Idiomatic phrase: 'with relish' — eagerly, with enjoyment — is the common modern idiom, used of food, tasks, stories, and even irony ('he recounted the story with evident relish').
  • Figurative seasoning: 'a relish of X' — a trace, hint, or seasoning of a quality — is a literary construction transferring the taste sense to abstract qualities (a relish of satire, a relish of scandal).
  • Psychological verb: 'to relish' in modern use is almost always positive and often anticipatory — relishing a coming holiday, relishing revenge, relishing a good meal; the word implies appetite, zest, and savouring rather than mere liking.
  • Historical bridge: the whole family descends from the Old French idea of 'that which is left behind' — the scent a hunted animal leaves on the ground, which the hounds pursue eagerly. From the scent of game the word moved to the aftertaste of food (the flavour 'left behind' in the mouth), then to flavour and seasoning generally, then to the condiment that adds flavour, and finally, in the 16th–17th centuries, to the psychological sense of keen, eager enjoyment — the mental appetite that pursues a pleasure as eagerly as hounds follow a scent.

WHY THE SECONDARY MEANINGS EXIST (HISTORICAL BRIDGE)

Relish is one of those words whose modern split between a condiment and a feeling hides a single ancient image: something left behind that excites pursuit. Old French relais meant 'remainder, that which is left behind', and in the hunting field it meant the scent left by the quarry — the trail the hounds pick up and chase with eager appetite. From 'the scent of the chase' the word naturally moved to 'the taste left behind' — the aftertaste or lingering flavour of food — and from aftertaste to taste and flavour in general, and from flavour to the condiment that adds it. The final step, the psychological one, transfers the hunter's eager pursuit of the scent to the mind's eager pursuit of pleasure: to have relish for something is to be hungry for it, to savour it, to chase its pleasure as keenly as the hound follows the trail. The jar of pickle relish on a sandwich and the actor relishing a role are, etymologically, the same animal — the quarry's scent still driving the chase.
