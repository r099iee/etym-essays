Origin uncertain — no secure PIE root can be reconstructed for amulet. Stage 1: Latin amulētum “a protective charm worn on the body against evil, disease, or the evil eye” (Pliny the Elder, Natural History, 1st c. AD) — an isolate in Latin with no demonstrable cognates in Indo-European; possibly of non-Indo-European (perhaps Etruscan) origin → French amulette (learned Renaissance borrowing, attested 16th c.; Italian parallel amuleto, 16th c.) → English amulet “a charm worn for protection” (introduced in Philemon Holland’s 1603 translation of Pliny’s Natural History) ◄ TARGET Note on the debated etymology: (a) the Latin grammarians’ derivation from amoliri “to ward off” (a(b)- “away from” + moliri “to exert oneself, to heave,” from moles “a mass, a burden”) is supported by the genuinely attested noun amolimentum “a means of averting,” but the vowel grade does not match cleanly (amu- against amo-); (b) the popular 19th-century theory linking the word to Arabic ḥamāla(t) “a strap or pendant worn on the body” (from ḥamala “to carry”) is chronologically impossible — Pliny attests amulētum in the 1st century AD, long before any substantial Arabic influence on Latin — and is now generally abandoned, though it lingers in popular dictionaries.
