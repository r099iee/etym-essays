*wekw- (Proto-Indo-European, "to speak, say, utter"; attested root, o-grade *wokw-) → Latin vōx, vōcis ("voice," the s-stem noun from the same root, inherited into Latin, attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin vocāre ("to call, summon, name," denominative verb from vox, attested from the 3rd–2nd c. BCE) → Latin vocātiō, vocātiōem ("a calling, a summons; in Christian Latin, the divine call," noun formed with -tiō, attested from the 1st c. BCE, used with religious sense in the Vulgate, 4th–5th c. CE) → Old French vocacion ("calling," inherited/borrowed into Gallo-Romance, 12th–13th c.) → Middle English vocacioun / vocation ("a call from God; a summons," borrowed from Old French, attested 1430s) → Modern English vocation ("one's calling, occupation," secularized from the 16th c.) → English VOCATIONS (plural, "professional callings," the plural form used in institutional and career contexts from the 19th c.). Note on debated points: the PIE root *wekw- "to speak" is securely attested (Greek eipón "I said," Sanskrit vákti "speaks," Avestan vač-), and the chain from Latin vocare onward is uncontroversial. The only nuance is that English voice is a later borrowing of Latin vox through Old French (13th–14th c.), a sibling of vocation rather than an intermediate stage; the direct line to vocation runs through vocāre and vocātiō.
