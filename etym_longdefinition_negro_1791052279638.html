Negro (plural Negroes) is a historical English noun and adjective meaning a person of Black African ancestry, especially an African American. Once the standard, respectful, capitalised term (late 19th-mid 20th century), it is now dated and generally avoided because it is associated with segregation and white-imposed labelling; modern preferred terms are Black (person) or African American. It survives in historical quotations, proper names, and mid-century literature, as in the source: The American Negro is making this transition today.

In its heyday, Negro was dignified and progressive. Black newspapers, churches, colleges and civil-rights leaders used it proudly: Negro history, Negro businessman, The Negro Speaks of Rivers (Langston Hughes), Negro Leagues baseball. Capital N was itself a victory — editors who wrote negro lower-case were accused of disrespect. To call a man a Negro in 1940 was polite; to call him black (lower-case) could be insulting. That reversal — now Black is respectful, Negro is not — is key to reading older texts without anachronism.

  • 1. Respectful ethnonym (historical): A Black person. Example: Negro voters, Negro schools (segregated but self-named with pride). Found in Du Bois, Washington, King (I Have a Dream: the Negro is still languished). Must be understood in context, not censored from quotes, but not reused.
  • 2. Legal/administrative category (historical): Negro slave, free Negro, Negro entrance — Jim Crow and slavery-era classification. Here the word is coldly bureaucratic, marking segregation even when technically neutral.
  • 3. Cultural-historical adjective: Negro spirituals, Negro literature, Negro art — now usually renamed Black spirituals, African-American literature, but historic labels persist in library catalogues and organisation names (United Negro College Fund retains name by choice).
  • 4. Lower-case negro (colour): Rare in English for black colour (negro coat); Spanish/Portuguese normal. Do not confuse with capitalised ethnonym.

Connotations today: For readers over 80, Negro may carry memories of dignity and struggle; for younger readers it feels archaic at best, offensive at worst, evoking segregated signs and white paternalism. It is not the slur that its dialect derivative is, but outsiders using it now cause hurt and signal ignorance. Scholars quoting King or Hughes retain it with capital N and often add [historical term]. Institutions retaining it (UNCF) do so for continuity and brand, with explicit discussion.

Why the shift: The 1960s Black Power and Black is Beautiful movements rejected Negro as imposed by enslavers (Spanish for black, not an African self-name), accommodationist (Negro leaders vs militants), and tainted by Jim Crow. Black asserted pride in colour; African American (1988 Jesse Jackson campaign) asserted heritage. Language followed politics. The source sentence, with its detached sociological tone (making this transition), already hints at outsider categorisation that later generations would reject in favour of self-naming.

Usage guidance: Do not call living people Negroes except in direct historical quotation or proper names. In discussion of older texts, gloss: Negro [historical term for Black/African American].
