In the celebrated opening of Macaulay's History of England, recount performs the most solemn office the verb can hold: it announces the historian's entire programme. “I shall recount the errors which, in a few months, alienated a loyal gentry and priesthood from the House of Stuart.” The word means “to relate, to narrate in detail and in order,” and its register here is high, formal, and deliberate — the measured first-person pledge of a writer who is about to guide his readers through the reigns of James II and William III. Crucially, the verb is the Old French narrative word (reconter, “to tell over again”), not the modern English homograph “to count again”: Macaulay is promising a connected, chronological, comprehensive telling of the errors of James's reign, a story ordered like a count — the deep link between numbering and narrating that the word has carried from its Latin birth. The tone is dignified and programmatic: this is the voice of the Whig historian who believes that history, recounted with candour, is a moral and political lesson, and that the “errors” of a few months deserve the full, orderly narration he proceeds to give them.

Sense 1 — To tell or relate in detail; to narrate the facts of an event or story (the sense on this card). This is the primary and historic sense: “The veteran began to recount his experiences during the blockade of the city”; “She was asked to recount the events of that fateful evening to the magistrate.” The verb implies an ordered, connected, complete telling — a story laid out item by item, as in a count — not a casual mention. It is the word of the chronicler and the historian, of the witness and the biographer, always carrying a faint commitment to completeness and sequence.

Sense 2 — The homograph: to count again (19th century onward, especially of votes). A separate verb formed from re- “again” + count: to count ballots a second time in an election, and the noun “a recount.” This sense is a modern English formation, not a descendant of the Old French narrative verb, though the two words now share one spelling and one sound. The mnemonic on this card plays on exactly this doubling: the historian recounts the tale; the vote-counter re-counts the ballots.

Sense 3 — Related nouns and uses.

  • Recount (noun, narrative): a telling or account of events — “his recount of the incident” (a rarer but standard noun).
  • Recount (noun, political): the second counting of ballots in a disputed election — “the candidate demanded a recount.”
  • Recounting (gerund): the act of narrating — “the recounting of the nation's history is itself a political act,” a phrase that captures Macaulay's own project.

Semantic architecture. The two homographs are a textbook case of convergence. The narrative recount descends from Latin computāre “to reckon together” through Old French conter “to count; to tell” and reconter “to tell over again”: in Old French the two ideas — numbering and narrating — were a single word, because to tell a story is to give an orderly account of its items, and the very word account preserves the double meaning to this day. The electoral recount is a much later English coinage that simply re-uses the prefix re- with count. The two words met in the same spelling only in the nineteenth century. Macaulay's usage, firmly narrative and dignified, belongs wholly to the Old French line. Among near-synonyms, relate is the close formal twin but tends toward the personal and spoken; narrate is the cooler, professional term of the theorist; recite implies reproduction from memory, verbatim and in public; tell is the plain, casual, oral word; and describe focuses on sensory detail rather than sequence. Only recount carries both the ordered completeness of the chronicle and the moral weight of the historian's pledge.
