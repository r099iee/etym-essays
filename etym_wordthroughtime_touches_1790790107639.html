- 12th–13th Century - Entered Middle English from Old French *toucher* (from Vulgar Latin *toccāre*, an onomatopoeic verb meaning "to knock, strike, or ring a bell"); initially denoted physical striking, grasping, or bodily contact.
- 14th Century (Chaucerian Era) - Expanded rapidly into emotional and figurative realms: to be "touched" with pity, to make contact in musical playing ("touching the harp"), and intimate physical contact.
- 16th–17th Century (Renaissance & Elizabethan) - Acquired craftsmanship and artistic senses: the touch of a painter's brush, the touchstone for assaying gold, and the distinctive stylistic traits of a master artisan.
- 18th Century (Augustan Era) - The plural noun *touches* became established in literary criticism and rhetoric to signify delicate satirical points, witty strokes, or brief evocative character sketches.
- 19th–20th Century - Proliferated in popular idiom ("finishing touches," "keeping in touch") and critical theory (such as Roland Barthes' fragmentary aesthetics: "facets, aphorisms, touches and shoves"), representing non-linear, episodic literary gestures.
- 21st Century - Revolutionized by capacitive digital technology (touchscreens, multi-touch gestures) while retaining its enduring literary use for delicate artistic nuances and emotional resonances.
