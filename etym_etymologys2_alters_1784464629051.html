PIE *h₂el- 'beyond, other' (reconstructed root of otherness), with the contrastive/comparative suffix *-tero- giving the pronominal stem *h₂el-tero- 'the other (of two)' → Latin alter 'the other (of two), the second; one of two alternatives' (attested from the early 2nd century BCE, e.g., in Plautus); the base *al- also appears in Latin alius 'other' and in English else → Late Latin / Medieval Latin alterāre 'to make other, to change' (post-classical verbalization of alter; common in medieval learned usage as alterare) → Old French alterer 'to change, to transform; also to impair, to falsify' (12th century CE) → Middle English alteren 'to change, modify' (attested c. 1375–1400 CE, e.g., in the Wycliffite Bible and Chaucer) → Modern English alter 'to change, make different' (16th century onwards) → ALTERS — the card's target form: third-person singular present indicative of alter. NOTE on the debate: the standard account (Pokorny *al- 'beyond, other'; OED) derives Latin alter from the PIE 'otherness' base plus the *-tero- contrastive suffix; an alternative phrasing treats the base as the pronominal stem of 'that/other,' which amounts to the same etymology differently stated. Modern English has neutralized the Old French 'impair, falsify' nuance, which survives only weakly in the derived noun alteration. The homophone altar is unrelated (Latin altāre, from altus 'high').
