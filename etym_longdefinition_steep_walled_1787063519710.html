steep-walled is a compound adjective formed from steep ("rising sharply, almost vertical") and walled ("having walls, enclosed by sides"), and it describes a place whose sides rise steeply or almost vertically — a valley, bay, canyon, or hollow shut in by high, sheer faces. In the source text — Tolkien's The Hobbit — the travellers turn "into a little steep-walled bay, grassy-floored, still and quiet": a small natural cove or hollow whose near-vertical rock walls enclose a grassy, sheltered floor, the perfect hidden refuge on the mountain-side.

PRIMARY SENSE — OF TOPOGRAPHY: ENCLOSED BY SHEER SIDES

  • Used of natural places whose sides rise steeply or vertically: "a steep-walled valley", "a steep-walled canyon", "a steep-walled cove", "a steep-walled gorge".
  • The compound stresses the wall-like quality of the enclosing slopes: not merely hilly but walled — abrupt, near-vertical, as if built by a mason in rock.
  • The places so described are usually sheltered and secret: a steep-walled bay is a cove shut in against wind and view, exactly the "still and quiet" refuge of the source text.
  • Common in geology, geography, mountaineering, and descriptive prose; also applied to man-made places such as a steep-walled quarry, reservoir, or defensive ditch (a steep-walled trench).

THE PARTS — STEEP

  • steep (adjective) means "rising or falling sharply; almost perpendicular": steep hills, steep stairs, steep cliffs, a steep drop.
  • Its oldest English sense was actually "high, lofty, towering" (see the etymologies); the modern meaning focuses on the angle of rise rather than the height.
  • Figurative extensions, chiefly British and informal: "a steep price" = an exorbitant one; "that's a bit steep!" = unreasonable, excessive, cheeky — a nineteenth-century slang sense that still lives in conversation.
  • "A steep learning curve" = a task that is hard to learn quickly — the modern image of something that must be climbed at a punishing angle.

THE PARTS — WALLED

  • walled (adjective, past participle of wall) means "having walls; enclosed or surrounded by walls": a walled garden, a walled city, a walled courtyard.
  • In compound use it describes enclosure and protection: to be walled is to be shut in, defended, hidden, or confined — the wall is the boundary that separates inside from outside.
  • When applied to natural places, walled is metaphorical: the rock faces are "walls" as high and sheer as built ones, and a steep-walled bay is nature's walled garden.

SECONDARY / REGISTER SENSES AND COMPOUND BEHAVIOUR

  • The compound is most at home in narrative and geographical prose; it is precise, evocative, and slightly formal, without being technical.
  • Near-synonym compounds exist — sheer-sided, cliff-bound, high-walled, vertical-sided — but steep-walled uniquely combines the angle (steep) with the enclosing function (walled).
  • In the source text the compound is used with extraordinary economy: in two words it gives the bay its shape (steep walls), its shelter (walled), and its naturalness (grassy-floored beside it, still and quiet) — a complete picture of a hidden haven.
  • The hyphen marks the compound as a single adjectival unit; it is not a dictionary-headword with independent secondary senses, but a living descriptive compound built from two fully productive words.

GRAMMAR AND FORMS

  • Composed of steep + hyphen + walled; the -ed of walled is the ordinary adjectival past-participle ending.
  • Comparable compounds: steep-sided, steep-banked, steep-roofed; walled compounds: high-walled, thick-walled, ivy-walled.
  • Because it is formed on demand, the compound has no fixed plural or other inflections; it functions purely as an attributive or predicative adjective ("a steep-walled bay"; "the bay was steep-walled").
  • Both component words are old and native: steep from Old English stēap "high, lofty", walled from Old English weall "wall, rampart" (itself from Latin vallum) — see the etymologies for both lines.

SUMMARY

  • steep-walled = having sides that rise steeply or almost vertically, enclosing a space like walls — said of valleys, bays, canyons, quarries, and similar places.
  • In the source text it pictures a little grassy-floored cove shut in by high sheer rock, still and quiet: a natural refuge described with two precise, old, and vivid words.
