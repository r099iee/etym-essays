The history of bindle carries a single physical idea — things tied together into one portable parcel — from the oldest strata of the Indo-European lexicon to the slang of American hobo camps and, eventually, to the dictionaries and novels of the twentieth century. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *bhendh-, "to bind, tie, join, fasten together," a root whose importance to the earliest speakers is written across the daughter languages: in Vedic Sanskrit it formed badhnāti "he ties" and bandhu "kinsman" — the relative is literally "one bound by blood" — while in Ancient Greek the same root gave pentherós "father-in-law," one bound into the family by marriage. Binding was not just a technology of cords and knots; it was the root metaphor for the bonds of kinship and society themselves.

In the Germanic branch the root took two parallel forms. The strong verb *bindaną, "to bind," descended into Old English bindan (modern English bind), Old High German bintan (modern German binden), and Old Norse binda. Beside it, speakers built a noun from the zero-grade stem — *bundą, "bond, bundle, what is bound together" — and from that noun a diminutive, *bundilaz, "a little bound-together thing." The diminutive is the direct ancestor of our bundle: it gave Old English byndele, Middle Dutch bondel, German Bündel (diminutive of Bund), and Dutch bundel. In Middle English the word appears as bundel from around 1300, and modern English bundle has carried the sense of "a collection of objects wrapped or tied together" continuously ever since, both as noun and verb ("to bundle up").

The transformation of bundle into bindle is a purely American story, played out on the freight trains and back roads of the late nineteenth-century West. From the 1870s onward the expanding railway network carried a vast floating population of migratory labourers — farm workers, harvest hands, construction crews — who lived on the road and owned, often literally, nothing but what they could carry. Their belongings, bedding rolled in a blanket, spare clothes, a pan, tobacco, were tied into a single cloth pack and slung over the shoulder, sometimes carried on a stick. In the slang of these camps and boxcars the standard word bundle was reshaped into bindle under the direct influence of the verb bind — the pack is precisely "the bound thing." The word is first attested in the 1890s, and it arrived complete with its own social grammar.

  • The bindlestiff (c. 1900s): the hobo who carried a bindle was a "bindlestiff," combining the bundle with the older slang stiff ("an itinerant labourer," as in "farm stiff," "railroad stiff"). The bindlestiff was the rank-and-file of the hobo army — a migrant wage-worker, distinct from the professional tramp who avoided work and from the settled labourer.
  • The Great Depression (1930s): when the crash of 1929 threw millions of men onto the road, the bindle became the emblem of an entire generation. In John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men (1937), George and Lennie carry their bindles from ranch to ranch; the binding cloth holds their whole world, and unslinging it is the ritual of arriving home on the road. The bindle-stiff, carrying all his worldly goods on his shoulder, was read by the 1930s imagination with a mixture of pity, grit, and romanticised wanderlust.
  • Drug slang (20th century): in American street slang a "bindle" became a small folded paper packet holding a dose of powdered drugs, especially heroin or cocaine. The sense is transparent: the folded paper is a little bound-up packet, the urban descendant of the hobo's cloth-bound parcel.
  • Computing and technical reuse: in modern technology the word has been borrowed as the name of a small, self-contained, portable bundle of data or software — a "bindle" as a lightweight container that can be carried and unpacked anywhere. The semantic core — a portable bound-together thing — has survived two hundred years of reuse.
  • Sound and spelling: the shift from bundle to bindle is a dialectal vocalic and consonantal reshaping — the stressed vowel follows bind (/baɪn-/) rather than bundle (/bʌn-/), and the -dle ending shows the syllabic consonant development shared with cradle, bridle, and handle. The RP pronunciation /ˈbaɪndəl/ and the spelling bindle have been stable since the word entered print around 1900.

After the mid-twentieth century the hobo culture itself faded with the end of the itinerant harvest economy, and bindle survived chiefly as a literary and historical word — the property of Steinbeck's California, of hobo folklore and museum culture, and of any writer reaching for the texture of the Depression road. But the word never lost its ancient spine. Five thousand years after *bhendh- first meant "to tie," the bindle still means "the bound thing": a life reduced to what one man can tie in a cloth and carry on his shoulder.
