Fowls, the plural of fowl, means in everyday modern use the domestic birds of the farmyard — chickens, hens, ducks, geese, and turkeys kept for eggs or meat — the clucking, chattering population of coop and run whose noises carry across still country air.

In the source sentence the word wears precisely this farmyard sense: far-off sounds seem near and clear in the hush, and among them come fowls chattering in a yard — hens gossiping over grain, a cock asserting himself, the whole domestic chorus of poultry heard across the distance. The plural matters: a single hen clucks, but only fowls chatter, a crowd talking at once.

Core meaning in detail: The modern barnyard fowl is usually the domestic chicken, Gallus gallus domesticus, in its legions of breeds — layers, broilers, bantams — with ducks, geese, and turkeys admitted as honorary fowls whenever the farm or the festival requires. To keep fowls is to keep a small economy of eggs, meat, feathers, and manure, practised from Neolithic villages to suburban allotments. Yet the word remembers a grander past: for most of its thousand years in English, fowl meant any bird whatsoever, from wren to eagle, and only in the last few centuries did it shrink to the coop — a narrowing that dictionaries still record with the label now chiefly domestic.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Domestic poultry, especially chickens (primary modern sense): to keep fowls, a dish of roast fowl. The henhouse is the word's headquarters, and cookbooks use fowl where farmers say chicken, lending roast dinner a dignified archaism. The bridge is economic history: of all birds, the farmyard ones mattered most to most speakers, so the general word settled onto the useful ones.
  • Any bird, wild or tame (archaic and poetic, the King James sense): the fowls of the air, who neither sow nor reap. For centuries this was simply the ordinary word for bird, used of larks, ravens, and eagles without a second thought. The bridge is the original breadth: Old English fugol knew no distinction between sparrow and supper, and the Bible's translators kept that breadth aloft.
  • Wild game birds hunted for sport (sporting): wildfowl, waterfowl — ducks, geese, and waders pursued with dog and gun. The compounds preserve the old general sense inside the new order: the shooter goes fowling, and the quarry is fowl precisely because bird would sound too small for the sport. The bridge carries the ancient word into the marsh, where tradition protects it from the farmyard.
  • The flesh of such birds as food (culinary): boiled fowl and tongue on Victorian menus. Cookery clings to old words — mutton, venison, fowl — because the kitchen honours the animal differently from the field. The bridge runs from coop to table: the living bird becomes the served dish while keeping its name, a promotion in dignity if not in fate.
  • A person likened to a bird, in jest or scorn (colloquial, rare): an old fowl, a funny old fowl for an eccentric. The bridge is the barnyard caricature: humans who cluck, fuss, and strut invite comparison with the hens they resemble, and affection softens the insult into teasing.

Register and connotation: Fowls sounds rural, slightly literary, and warmly old-fashioned — the word of hymns, carols, and country diaries rather than supermarkets, which prefer poultry or chicken. It carries straw, warm eggs, and morning noise; even its grandest biblical use feels domestic, since the fowls of the air nest in the same imagination as the hens of the yard. To say fowls today is to choose flavour over precision, and to summon a world where birds still had souls.
