The plural noun captives denotes persons, soldiers, or animals who have been captured, taken prisoner, confined, or enslaved by an enemy, authority, or predator, and are held in involuntary restraint, subjugation, or imprisonment against their will. In extended, metaphorical, psychological, and literary contexts, it refers to individuals whose minds, hearts, passions, or attention are thoroughly enchanted, enthralled, dominated, or held in thrall by a captivating person, powerful emotion, charismatic ideology, or irresistible beauty.

The term spans a comprehensive array of historical, legal, military, sociological, and psychological domains:

  • Military Prisoners & Victims of War: The primary historical and tactical sense. Across human history, captives of war were soldiers or civilians seized during combat, siege, or raid. Depending on the era and culture, their fate ranged from being held for ransom (as was common among medieval European chivalric nobilities), forced into chattel slavery (as in ancient Rome, Greece, and the Barbary coast), subjected to ritual sacrifice, or exchanged during formal prisoner negotiations.
  • International Humanitarian Law & Human Rights (Detainees & Hostages): In modern international law, criminal jurisprudence, and human rights frameworks, captives encompasses hostages seized by terrorists or criminal cartels, political prisoners held in totalitarian regimes, and lawful prisoners of war (POWs) protected under the third Geneva Convention of 1949. International law establishes stringent standards ensuring that captives must be shielded from physical abuse, torture, forced labor, and humiliating public curiosity.
  • Psychological, Romantic, & Aesthetic Subjugation (Holding One Captive): In romantic poetry, literature, and psychology, to be a captive describes a person whose emotional autonomy or romantic devotion has been completely swept away by another's charm, beauty, or charisma (e.g., "a captive of her radiant smile" or "a captive to his overwhelming jealousy"). It conveys the paradox of willing or helpless emotional surrender.
  • Ecological & Zoological Confinement (Animals in Captivity): In zoology, veterinary science, and wildlife conservation, captives refers to wild animals captured from their native ecosystems and maintained in zoos, aquariums, breeding facilities, or private menageries (giving rise to the standard phrase "bred in captivity").
  • Intellectual & Ideological Captivity (Captive Audience): In sociology, communication studies, and rhetoric, a captive audience describes listeners who are unable to leave or avoid a speech, lecture, or advertisement due to their institutional circumstances (such as students in a lecture hall, passengers on an airplane, or workers at a mandatory corporate meeting).
  • Commercial & Market Monopoly (Captive Markets): In economics and corporate antitrust analysis, a captive market refers to a group of consumers who have no viable alternatives or competitive options and must purchase goods from a single supplier (such as concession stands inside airport security or proprietary hardware replacement parts).

The semantic bridge connecting the physical chains of a prisoner of war to the metaphorical enchantment of a romantic lover lies in the total surrender of autonomy: in both conditions, the subject's freedom of motion, choice, and agency is subordinated to an external, irresistible power that dictates their circumstances.
