PROFUSELY belongs to one of the oldest word-families in Indo-European, the "pouring" family rooted in PIE *gheu- "to pour." Its story stretches from ritual libations on Indo-European altars to the modern English adverb used of sweating, bleeding, apologizing, and thanking. The evolution is a model case of how a physical, transitive verb — to pour a liquid — gradually gave rise to an abstract sense of abundance and finally to a manner-adverb modifying any action that overflows.

The PIE root and its reflexes. The root *gheu- "to pour" appears across the family with remarkable clarity: Greek kheo "I pour," source of English "chyme" and of "catechism," which passed through the idea of "pouring" instruction into the ear; Sanskrit juhoti "he pours an offering into the fire" — the technical verb of Vedic sacrifice; and Latin fundere "to pour," which developed the specific noun fusio "a pouring out," the ancestor of English "fusion," as metal is poured and melted together. The Latin verb shows a nasal infix in the present stem (fund-, from the PIE nasal present *ghu-n-d-) against the perfect fudi and participle fusus; the same alternation underlies the English words "confuse," "diffuse," "infuse," "refuse," and "transfuse," all of which are compounds of fundere with different prefixes, each meaning some kind of pouring.

The Latin compound profundere and profusus. Adding the prefix pro- "forth, forward" to fundere produced profundere "to pour forth, pour out," and from its participle came the adjective profusus "poured forth, lavish, extravagant, immoderate." The Romans already used profusus in a moral sense: profusa largitas was "lavish, unbounded generosity," and profusus in gait or dress meant "slovenly, disorderly" — pouring out of shape. This double quality — abundance on the one hand, disorderly excess on the other — has stayed with the word family for two millennia.

Entry into English. English borrowed profuse in the mid-15th century directly from Latin profusus, meaning "lavish, extravagant, pouring forth without restraint." It could be used of both persons and things: a profuse spender, profuse moisture, profuse foliage. The adverb profusely appears in the 16th century and has been a steady presence in the language ever since, attaching itself most naturally to verbs of outflow and expression.

Semantic drift in modern English. The interesting modern development is the adverb's collocational field. Today profusely is used overwhelmingly in four zones: first, physiological outflow — sweat profusely, bleed profusely; second, emotional and verbal expression — apologize profusely, thank profusely, praise profusely; third, botanical and visual abundance — flowers grow profusely; and fourth, intensifying adjectives — profusely grateful, profusely decorated. In every zone the image is the same: an outflow that cannot be shut off because the source is overwhelmed. The adverb thus functions as a kind of intensity marker for anything that "pours out," and it rarely attaches to purely internal or static states — one is not "profusely happy" or "profusely wealthy" in normal usage, precisely because nothing is pouring.

Sound and morphology. The Latin sequence profusus shows the expected changes into English: the intervocalic f of Latin is kept as /f/ in the English borrowing, because English took profuse from the written Latin form rather than through French; English stress falls on the second syllable (pro-FUSE, pro-FUSE-ly), with the first syllable reduced in fast speech. The -ly adverbial suffix, from Old English -lice, converts the adjective to a manner-adverb, the standard and productive English pattern.

Socio-cultural notes. The adverb's history mirrors the social history of politeness and excess. In the 18th and 19th centuries, to thank someone profusely was the mark of a properly grateful dependant — an overflowing acknowledgement appropriate to relations of patronage, exactly as in the Dream of the Red Chamber passage that gives us the current reading context, where Yu-cun thanks his patron profusely. In the 20th and 21st centuries the word survives vigorously in medicine (profuse bleeding and sweating), in customer service (apologize profusely), and in travel and nature writing (bloom profusely), keeping its ancient core: the image of a liquid, or a feeling, that simply cannot be contained. The adverb has never lost the sheer physicality of its root, and that is why it remains so vivid.
