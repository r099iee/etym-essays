- Early 16th c.: Emerged in Early Modern English from Latin *perforare*, utilized initially in medical, anatomical, and surgical treatises to describe penetrating wounds and boring through bone.
- 17th c. to 18th c.: Broadened into natural philosophy, botany, and mining to describe naturally porous rocks, insect-bored leaves, and tunneling operations.
- 19th c.: Explosively popularized during the Industrial Revolution with the invention of automated perforating machinery for postage stamps (Henry Archer's patent, 1848), railway tickets, and printing sheets.
- 20th c. to Present: Adopted into modernist and post-modernist literary theory (e.g., Roland Barthes analyzing Flaubert) and avant-garde architecture to designate the deliberate puncturing and opening up of monolithic forms and discourses.
