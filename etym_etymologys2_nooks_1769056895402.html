CHAIN — NOOKS.
1. EARLIEST STAGE UNKNOWN: the word has no demonstrable PIE ancestry and no attested Old English ancestor; the OED classifies its origin as obscure. All proposed sources below are speculative.
2. Middle English nok / noke "a corner of a piece of ground, an angle, a triangular scrap of land" (attested c. 1300); the spelling alternation nok ~ noke reflects ordinary early-Middle-English open/closed-syllable variation, not two distinct etyma.
3. English nook "a corner, a recess, a secluded spot" (spelling settled as nook by the 17th c.; the sense broadened from "corner of ground" through "recess" to "hidden, sheltered place").
4. English plural nooks, as in the fixed binomial "nooks and crannies" (cranny from Old French cran "a notch, a crack") and in this passage's "nooks and wild places."
5. Modern English nooks "small corners, recesses, or secluded spots."

NUANCE NOTE (debated origin): several loan hypotheses have been proposed but none is accepted as the true source. (a) Celtic: comparison with Gaelic cnoc "hill, knob" (whence the place-name element "knock") and a cited niuc "a small hill, a corner of land" — phonetically attractive, unproven. (b) Continental Germanic: Middle Low German nōke / Middle Dutch noke "corner, angle" — argued against by chronology, since the English word appears earlier than the continental parallels. (c) Scandinavian: a word for a projecting peak or bend — plausible, unattested in the needed form. (d) A probable but unproven relative is English notch, whose Middle English form oche came from Old French with an n- attached by rebracketing ("an oche" heard as "a no-che"). The OED verdict — origin obscure — has not been displaced by any modern etymology, and no stage has been invented or merged.
