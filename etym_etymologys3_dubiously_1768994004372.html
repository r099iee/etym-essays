Dubiously has one of the most elegant etymologies in English, because it begins not with a word about doubt but with a number: two. Its ancestor is PIE *dwóh₁, the word for "two," one of the most durable items in the entire Indo-European inventory — a root so stable that it survives in Sanskrit dva, Greek dúo, Latin duo, Gothic twai, Lithuanian du, Old Irish dá, and Hittite dān, the last of them written in cuneiform over three and a half thousand years ago. Everywhere it went, the numeral stayed a numeral: two fingers, two hands, two gods. The word's whole future drama, however, lay in what a particular branch did with it.

The Germanic branch did the ordinary thing — it kept counting, producing two, twain, twice, twin, twelve, twenty, between, and twilight, the "double light" of dawn and dusk. The Hellenic branch counted too, and added the image of the two-pronged choice in dilemma, the "double premise." But it was the Italic branch that made the leap into psychology. Latin took the numeral and, with the suffix -bius ("having, being in the state of"), forged dubius — literally "having two (ways), of two minds." To be dubious in Latin was to be internally divided, pulled in two directions at once. From this single coinage Latin minted an entire vocabulary of hesitation: dubium, "a doubt"; dubitāre, "to doubt, to hesitate, to waver"; dubiōsus, "full of doubt"; dubitātiō, "a doubting." The metaphor is architectural: the doubtful mind is a fork in the road, and to doubt is to stand where two roads diverge.

English received the family by two separate doors. Through Old French douter came the native-looking doubt, which lost every trace of "twoness" and became plain uncertainty. Directly from Latin, in the mid-sixteenth century, came dubious, "of doubtful worth, wavering," and then its adverb dubiously, "in a doubtful manner." The two words diverged in flavour: doubt is the everyday English word, dubious the learned one that still glimmers with its Latin origin — the speaker of dubious is, whether they know it or not, declaring themselves to be of two minds, and the adverb paints a man literally split between trusting and distrusting.

The later history refined the adverb's range. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the adjective dubious extended from the doubting mind to the doubted object — "a dubious bargain," "a dubious character" — and dubiously followed into the verdict-sense: "dubiously acquired" means acquired in a way that invites doubt. The word now splits cleanly between a manner — acting with doubt — and a verdict — being of doubtful worth. In narrative prose, the manner-sense dominates and attaches almost always to a gesture: a look, a sniff, a shrug, a raised eyebrow. The adverb performs doubt rather than stating it; it shows a character holding two possibilities in their head at once. That is precisely its work in the passage, where Maspic sniffs the Martian air dubiously — a man of two minds breathing in a world he cannot yet trust, the two roads of the ancient Latin metaphor opening up inside a single hesitation before he speaks.
