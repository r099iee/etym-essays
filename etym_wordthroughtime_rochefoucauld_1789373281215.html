- [1613–1665] - Functions as the ancient feudal surname and title of one of France's most illustrious noble dynasties (Counts and later Dukes of La Rochefoucauld in Angoumois); François VI de La Rochefoucauld rises as a prominent military commander and leader of the aristocratic Fronde rebellion against Cardinal Mazarin - [connotation: high feudal nobility, martial honor, aristocratic rebellion]
- [1665–1700] - Publication of the Réflexions ou sentences et maximes morales transforms the name into an international literary and intellectual sensation; across European salons, "La Rochefoucauld" becomes synonymous with the unmasking of human virtue as covert vanity and self-love (*amour-propre*) - [connotation: dazzling aphoristic brilliance, courtly cynicism, controversial psychological realism]
- [18th Century (The Enlightenment)] - Revered by Voltaire, Jonathan Swift, and Lord Chesterfield as the supreme master of French prose economy and conversational wit; used as a philosophical touchstone for debates on whether genuine altruism can exist in human society - [connotation: stylistic gold standard, philosophical skepticism, Enlightenment salon sophistication]
- [19th Century] - Deeply admired by Friedrich Nietzsche (who considered La Rochefoucauld one of the greatest psychologists in world literature and a decisive influence on his own aphoristic style in Human, All Too Human), while challenged by romantic idealists who decried his cold, mechanistic view of human motives - [connotation: psychological profundity, pre-Freudian insight, uncompromising unmasker of morality]
- [20th Century to Present] - Permanently canonized in global literary and philosophical discourse as the universal eponym for lapidary psychological cynicism and the grandfather of the modern aphorism; invoked whenever a thinker cuts through self-congratulatory rhetoric with devastating, pithy accuracy - [connotation: supreme aphoristic precision, timeless psychological lucidity, aristocratic literary elegance]
