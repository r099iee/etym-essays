- c. 1600 — "flounder" appears in English for clumsy, helpless struggle, likely echoing the older "founder" and "blunder"; from the start it pictures a body thrashing against a resisting medium — water, mud or snow — with splashing and flailing limbs.
- 17th–18th c. — the word fixes as the vivid verb of ungainly panic: uncoordinated, disordered movement with no grace and no method, urgent yet incapable of getting anywhere.
- 19th c. — the sense extends from body to mind and plans: a speaker "flounders" for a word, an inquiry or enterprise "flounders" in confusion — the thrash of limbs becomes intellectual and financial stumbling that stops short of outright collapse.
- Contemporary — "floundered" stays keyed to panic and loss of composure: the terror of a soldier fleeing through waist-deep water is the same helplessness that keeps the word sharp against "founder" (sinking, failing utterly) on one side and "wade" (deliberate crossing) on the other.
