KNITS is the third-person singular present of knit: “to tie or fasten by knots; to make fabric by interlocking loops of yarn; to draw (the brows) together in a frown.” In the scene from The Story of the Stone, Bao-yu is bantering with the newcomer Dai-yu, punning on the literal meaning of her name—“dai”, a mineral used to blacken eyebrows. Citing the Encyclopedia of Men and Objects Ancient and Modern, he remarks that the mineral “dai” may be used instead of eye-black for painting the eyebrows, “and she has this ‘dai’ in her name and she knits her brows together in a little frown. I think it’s a splendid name for her!” The verb knits conjures the act of tying a knot: the two eyebrows are drawn towards each other and “bound” into one gathered line, just as a knitter binds threads into a fabric. The image is at once physical and figurative—a “knot” of habitual melancholy gathering on Dai-yu’s brow, an emblem of the “melancholy cast” that Hawkes elsewhere gives her. Because the word also names the textile craft, Bao-yu’s joke quietly weaves the brow-movement into the fabric of her name, turning grief into ornament. The tone is playful and affectionate, yet the verb also carries an undertone of worry: knitting the brows is the classic sign of distress, and the “little frown” is the permanent badge of Dai-yu’s sorrow—a dark, expressive brow perfectly matching the mineral black of her name. Polysemy and the full range of senses:
  • The textile craft—to form fabric by interlocking loops of yarn with needles: “she knits a sweater for her new grandson”; the dominant modern sense, with knit fabric, knit stitch, knitting, knitter, knitwear.
  • To tie or bind by knots—the older, more general sense: “knit the ends of the rope together”, “knit them with a firm knot”.
  • Of the brows—to draw the eyebrows together in a frown: the sense in the context; a stock literary gesture of concentration, displeasure, or pain. The transitive “knit one’s brows” and intransitive “her brow knitted”.
  • Of bones—to heal and rejoin after a fracture: “the bones have knitted”.
  • Of people or groups—to join closely and firmly: “a tightly knit community”, “close-knit family”, “knit together in friendship”.
  • Figurative union—to unite or consolidate parts: “knit together the strands of the argument”, “a plot tightly knitted”.
  • Technical/textile jargon—knit as opposed to woven fabric; the knit stitch (plain stitch, garter stitch); machine knitting in industry.
The synonym family draws sharp boundaries. Frowns names the whole facial expression—the darkening of the entire face—whereas knits isolates the brows and their drawing together; a frown may be a single fleeting grimace, while knitting suggests a tighter, more lasting gathering. Furrows borrows from ploughing and describes deep, vertical creases ploughed into the brow, often from age or intense thought; it is heavier and more static than knits, which keeps the sense of active drawing-together. Wrinkles is far more general—any crinkling of skin, of cloth, of the nose, of age—and lacks both the localising of the brows and the idea of tying. Gathers is the general word for collecting or assembling and lacks the knot-imagery; contracts is physiological and impersonal; puckers pinches the skin into small folds (lips, cloth) rather than drawing two brows into one line; scowls is an angry darkening of the whole face, without the sorrowful or thoughtful cast of knitting the brows. Register and idiom: knits is a plain, old, Germanic verb; in the brow sense it is literary and slightly formal, at home in the prose of the novel and of poetry. Its double life as a craft word and a gesture word is the source of its wit in Bao-yu’s speech, where the weaving image literally “knits” the movement into the fabric of Dai-yu’s name.
