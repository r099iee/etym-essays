Snapdragons (plural) are herbaceous garden plants of the genus Antirrhinum whose bilabiate (two-lipped) flowers, when pinched at the sides, open and shut like a mouth — hence the folk name that sees a dragon's snout in the blossom. Tall spikes of closely set flowers in many colours make them a staple of cottage and formal beds; the "snap" is tactile play as much as visual metaphor. In Bilbo's firework memory, Old Took's Midsummer's Eve displays "used to go up like great lilies and snapdragons and laburnums of fire": the plant-names become shapes of exploding light — floral silhouettes painted on the twilight sky. The register is nostalgic, festive, full of child-wonder; the garden lexicon of the Shire is projected onto wizard-fireworks. The plural matches a class of blooms (and of firework forms), not a single plant.

Core senses:

  • Plant — Antirrhinum majus and related species; the common snapdragon (the sense in this card).
  • Parlour game snapdragon — a 17th–19th-century Christmas game in which raisins are snatched from a dish of burning brandy; the "snap" is the catch, the "dragon" the flame. Shakespeare alludes to it in Love's Labour's Lost.
  • Firework shapes — in Tolkien, blossoms of fire that resemble the flower; a metaphorical transfer of the garden image into pyrotechnics.
  • Learned synonym — antirrhinum, from Greek "counter-nose" (snout-like), encodes the same facial metaphor in scientific Latin.

Historical bridge & semantic logic: the name is a transparent English compound — snap (a sudden bite or crack) + dragon (the classical beast). The two-lipped corolla, pinched and released, literally "snaps" like a little mouth, and gardeners saw a dragon's open jaws in the blossom; the plant-name is attested from the 16th century. The brandy-game sense grew out of the same imagery — snatching ("snapping") from the flame (the "dragon"). Neither element is originally botanical: the compound works by folk metaphor, and its transparency is the whole point.

Usage notes & idioms: snapdragon is used for the plant, for the blossom, and historically for the brandy-snatching parlor game; in the plural it names a class of blooms or, metaphorically, of firework shapes. Related garden vocabulary keeps the same naming habit — foxglove, toadflax, lion's mouth (an old folk name for Antirrhinum) — all of which read an animal face into the flower. Botanically the snap is produced by the two-lobed upper and lower corolla lips and their springing hinge; children have pinched snapdragon flowers for centuries, which is why the folk name has stayed vivid long after the learned name antirrhinum took over the floras.

Differentiate: a dragon proper is the mythical beast, not the flower; lilies and laburnums in the same sentence are fellow plant-shapes of fire, not botanical companions in a real bed; antirrhinum is the scientific name. Do not read a combat verb "snap" plus free-standing "dragons". Preserve the compound plural as one garden-and-firework word.
