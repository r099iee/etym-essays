Because nine-tenths is a compound fraction, its history must be told twice: once for nine and once for tenth, each an ancient numeral with an unbroken lineage from prehistory, converging only in modern arithmetic prose.

NINE. The cardinal nine descends from PIE *h₁néwn̥, the word for 9, whose very reconstruction is famous: unusually, the words for ‘nine’ and ‘new’ resemble each other across Indo-European (Latin novem beside novus, Greek ennéa beside néos, Sanskrit náva beside návas), prompting the old hypothesis that nine was counted as the ‘new’ number opening a fresh series — though this remains speculation rather than proof. What is certain is the Germanic path: PIE *h₁néwn̥ yields Proto-Germanic *niwun by Grimm’s Law and vowel developments, giving Old English nigon, Middle English nyne, and modern nine with its silent -e lengthening the vowel. Culturally, nine carries archaicEU magic — the nine worlds of Norse myth, the nine Muses, cat’s nine lives, ‘dressed to the nines’ — so that the numeral arrives in modern fractions trailing faint folkloric prestige.

TENTH (TEN + -TH). The ordinal-fraction tenth descends from PIE *déḱm̥, ‘ten’, extended with the ordinal suffix *-tos to *déḱm̥tos, ‘tenth’. The decimal base itself is presumably digital — ten fingers — and its stability across millennia is remarkable: Latin decem, Greek déka, Sanskrit dáśa, Gothic taihun all transparently cognate. In Germanic, *tehun (ten) plus the ordinal formative *-þa- gives *tehunþa-, Old English tēoða, where the -n- of ‘ten’ had been absorbed; Middle English writes tenthe, and early modern speakers, feeling the link to ten, restore the -n- to produce current tenth, pronounced with the cluster /nθ/ the user’s transcription records. The fraction sense — one of ten equal parts — develops from the ordinal by the regular English pattern (third → a third, tenth → a tenth), fully established by late Middle English commercial and mathematical writing.

  • Ancient counting: both numerals are among the most stable words in Indo-European, attested in every branch; fractions built from them inherit this bedrock intelligibility.
  • Ordinal-to-fraction shift: English systematically converts ordinals into partitives (a fifth, a sixth, a tenth), so tenths as ‘equal divisions into ten’ needs no new coinage, only pluralisation.
  • Compounding: nine-tenths follows the transparent pattern ‘cardinal + plural partitive’ (three-fourths, seven-eighths), standardised in print with the hyphen from the eighteenth century as arithmetic textbooks regularised fraction typography.
  • Idiomatic hyperbole: by the nineteenth century nine-tenths escapes mathematics to mean ‘almost all of it’ — ‘nine-tenths of the work is preparation’ — a rhetorical use exploiting decimal precision for persuasive exactness about what is really an estimate.
  • Sound change witnesses: initial t- in ten/tenth versus d- in Latin decem and Greek deka exhibits Grimm’s Law in miniature; the -th ending continues the old ordinal suffix shared with fourth, fifth, sixth.
  • Literary attestation: from Chaucer’s reckonings through Shakespeare’s ‘nine-tenths’ style proportions (‘nine parts in ten’) to Victorian proverbs like ‘possession is nine-tenths of the law’ (more properly ‘nine points of the law’), the fraction has long served rhetoric as the figure of the overwhelming majority — exactly its force in the source sentence, where repudiating nine-tenths of the evidence means discarding nearly everything.

In the given context the phrase is doing that idiomatic work: nine-tenths of what we seek to explain means the vast bulk of the phenomena, and the decimal dress makes the claim feel measured rather than blustering. Two of humanity’s oldest words combine to say, with mock precision, ‘nearly everything’.
