Buoyantly is a manner adverb meaning in a cheerful, light-hearted and resiliently optimistic way - in the manner of something that floats and bobs back up however hard it is pushed down. In the source sentence Bertie urges Tails up, Aunt Dahlia buoyantly: he speaks with forced heartiness, trying to lift her gloom by sheer bounce, a delivery that is itself part of the comedy because his cheerfulness floats free of the financial facts.

The core image is physical: a buoy is an anchored float that rides the waves and returns to the surface. Transferred to mood, buoyantly describes speech, movement and conduct that refuses to sink under bad news, criticism or debt. It combines three nuances at once: lightness (not heavy or solemn), cheerfulness (smiling, hearty, encouraging) and resilience (springing back after a dip). A buoyant market stays high despite selling pressure; a buoyant person stays bright despite setbacks; to act buoyantly is to perform that unsinkability in voice and gesture.

Grammatically it modifies verbs of speaking, moving and proceeding: to urge, say, walk, sail, carry on buoyantly. It answers how, not what, and colours the whole utterance with bounce. Collocations include to reply buoyantly, to insist buoyantly, to stride buoyantly, to carry on buoyantly. Register is standard and slightly literary-comic; it is warmer than optimistically, lighter than confidently, more pictorial than cheerfully, and carries a hint of deliberate effort - buoyancy maintained against gravity.

  • Cheerful optimistic manner (primary, contextual): Speaking or acting with hearty good humour and hope, trying to infect others. Bridge: direct metaphor from floating buoy to floating spirits that will not stay down.
  • Resilient recovery sense: Bouncing back quickly after disappointment, keeping energy high under pressure. Bridge: physics of return-to-surface applied to psychology of recovery after shock.
  • Light, floating physical sense (literal base): Moving as if floating, dancing lightly, sailing with lift. Bridge: older sense of buoyant as tending to float, preserved in descriptions of step and swim.
  • Economic and collective extension: Markets, prices, hopes and morale described as buoyant when they stay high or rebound. Bridge: 19th-c. financial journalism borrowed the same water image for prices that refuse to sink, feeding back into personal use.
  • Potentially naive or forced cheer (pragmatic edge): In Wodehouse buoyantly can imply cheerfulness maintained in defiance of facts, well-meaning but inadequate. Bridge: observer's judgement turns resilience into blindness when optimism ignores real cheques and bills.

In short, buoyantly packs a small physics lesson into a mood: what floats pushes back against what presses down. To learn it is to picture the harbour buoy nodding on the swell and to hear Bertie's voice riding above Aunt Dahlia's gloom, admirable in energy, doubtful in efficacy, and instantly recognizable as the sound of someone determined not to sink with the ship.
