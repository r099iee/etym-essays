=== MULE === (Chain for the first root word, MULE.) Stage 1 — Pre-Italic Mediterranean substrate *mūlo- / *mūslo- "mule" (a loanword of the ancient Mediterranean; attested indirectly through its Latin reflex, 3rd–1st c. BCE). NO PIE ancestry is reconstructible: this is a substrate or loan word that arrived with the practice of hybrid breeding itself, and the handbooks (OED, de Vaan) state bluntly that there is no Indo-European root. Stage 2 — Latin mūlus "male mule" (and mūla "female mule"), attested from Plautus onward, 2nd–1st c. BCE. A probable Greek relative, mýklos "she-ass, wild ass," is noted by some authorities but its relation to Latin mūlus is uncertain and flagged as such. Stage 3 — Vulgar Latin *mūlus continues the word; Old French mul / mule "mule," 10th–12th c. Stage 4 — Anglo-Norman mule → Middle English mule "mule," 13th–14th c. Stage 5 — English mule "hybrid offspring of a donkey and a horse," modern. NOTE: The English word names both sexes (the older male-only mūlus distinction was lost); Hindi खच्चर khaccar "mule" is a separate Persian loan and is NOT related to the Latin word — it is deliberately excluded to prevent a false link. === HARROW === (Chain for the second root word, HARROW.) Stage 1 — North Germanic implement-word: Old Norse herfi "a harrow" (Viking Age, 8th–11th c.); the deeper origin is disputed and the OED marks harrow "origin unknown." A proposed Indo-European derivation from *ker- / *sker- "to cut, scrape, scratch" (which would make the harrow "the scraper") is a hypothesis only and is flagged as not established — the standard handbooks do not accept it. Stage 2 — Middle English harwe "a harrow" (13th c.), borrowed with the tool itself from the northern source; related continental forms Middle Dutch harke, Dutch hark "rake, harrow," Middle Low German harke "rake," regional German Harke "rake." Stage 3 — English harrow (n.) "a toothed frame for breaking up soil," modern, and harrow (v.) "to break and level soil with a harrow." NOTE ON THE FIGURATIVE MERGE: The modern verb "to harrow" in the sense "to wound, lacerate, torment" is NOT a development of the tool-word alone. It results from a Middle English collision with the Old English verb hergian "to harry, ravage, plunder" (→ English harry), which merged with the tool-word so completely that to harrow gained the figurative sense "to wound, torment," fixed forever by the Harrowing of Hell. Two distinct sources, one modern word — a genuine case of homonymic convergence, and it is noted here rather than hidden.
