PIE *(s)mer- “to get a share, allot, earn; to have a portion assigned” (c. 4000 BCE; also the root of Greek meros “part, share” and moîra “fate, lot”) → Proto-Italic *mere- “to get a share, earn” (reconstructed) → Latin merēre “to deserve, earn, obtain as one’s due share” (also “to serve, do military service”; attested from the 2nd c. BCE) → Latin meritus “deserved, earned” (past participle of merēre) → Latin meritum “what one has earned, desert, worth” (also “a service done, a kindness”) → Old French merite “reward, worth, desert” (10th–12th c.) → Middle English merite “spiritual reward earned by good works” (c. 1200, a theological borrowing) → English merit (noun) “earned worth, desert” (13th c.) → English merit (verb) “to deserve, be worthy of” (16th c.) → English merited “deserved, earned” (past participle) → English UNMERITED “not deserved” (un- “not” + merited; modern sense). Debated / nuanced stages: the same PIE root produced the Latin commercial family mercēs “wages, pay, reward” and merx “merchandise”, hence market, merchant, commerce, and mercenary; the equation of the “share” root with the “trade” root is traditional and widely accepted but has been questioned by some scholars. The further link with Latin memor “mindful” and Greek mérinma “care” (keeping one’s portion in mind) is suggestive but disputed. The specific route to English unmerited passed through the religious sense of Middle English merite, so the modern secular sense rests on the medieval theological economy of earned grace.
