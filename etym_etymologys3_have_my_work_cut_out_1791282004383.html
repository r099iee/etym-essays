Have my work cut out for me is a tailor's metaphor fossilised into an idiom for a daunting task. Its essay must therefore track five ordinary words and one extraordinary figurative leap. Four of the words — have, my, work, out — are ancient Germanic inheritances with straightforward sound histories. The fifth — cut — is a medieval borrowing wrapped in a craft image, and the phrase as a whole is an Early Modern innovation.

Have comes from Old English habban, from Proto-Germanic *habjaną to hold, lift, grasp, ultimately from PIE *keh₂p- to grasp. It shifted from physical holding to possession, obligation, and perfect aspect: I have a coat, I have to go, I have finished. My comes from Old English mīn, from Proto-Germanic *mīnaz, the oblique stem of I, with no foreign borrowing. Work comes from Old English weorc, wyrcan, from Proto-Germanic *werkaną and PIE *werg- to do, cognate with Greek ergon and English energy, wright, and workshop. Out comes from Old English ūt, from Proto-Germanic *ūt and PIE *h₂ut-, meaning motion outward and by extension completion.

  • Cut — the key word: Middle English cutten appears suddenly around 1300 with no Old English ancestor, sparking debate. The dominant view derives it from Old Northern French couteau, coutel knife, from Latin culter knife, culdter ploughshare, via the action of cutting cloth with a knife. Alternative proposals link it to Celtic or expressive *cutt- to cut short. Whatever the deeper root, by 1400 cutten means to divide with a sharp edge and soon to shape by cutting.
  • Tailoring image: in Tudor and Stuart tailoring, to have one's work cut out meant literally that the master had cut cloth pieces out ready for the apprentice to sew. The hard skilled decision was done; laborious sewing remained. Early citations use it neutrally: your work is cut out and ready.
  • Idiomatic reversal: by the 1600s the phrase flips from prepared to daunting. To have your work cut out for you now means so much sewing has been laid out that you will struggle to finish. The image of pre-cut piles becomes a measure of burden. Shakespeare-adjacent drama and Restoration comedy use it for troublesome assignments.

Phonetically the phrase shows normal reductions: have weakens to /həv/ or /əv/, my stays /maɪ/, work in RP is /wɜːk/ with non-rhotic length, cut is /kʌt/, out is /aʊt/. Together /hæv maɪ wɜːk kʌt aʊt/ in careful speech, /əv maɪ wɜːk kʌt aʊt/ in rapid speech. No sound change is idiom-specific; the novelty is semantic.

Socio-culturally the idiom survived because apprenticeship was universal experience. Everyone knew the sinking feeling of facing a bench piled with cut pieces. As ready-made clothing replaced bespoke tailoring, the literal memory faded but the figurative sense — to face a difficult, time-consuming task requiring effort — remained, now applied to fixing messes, winning elections, or pacifying families. Wodehouse uses it in its modern daunting sense: I shall have my work cut out to fix things up means sorting this chaos will be hard labour.

Variants confirm the story: to have one's hands full is parallel, to have a hard row to hoe is agricultural equivalent, to have a lot on one's plate is culinary. None uses tailoring, making this idiom unique. Attestations run from 16th-century tailors accounts through 19th-century novels to modern sports commentary — he will have his work cut out tonight — always with for you, before you, or to do marking the task ahead.
