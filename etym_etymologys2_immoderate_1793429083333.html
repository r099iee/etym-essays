PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (reconstructed, c. 4000 BCE)
*med-
‘to take appropriate measures, measure, give counsel’
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*mod-o-
‘measure, limit, boundary, manner’
↓
Classical Latin (1st c. BCE)
modus
‘measure, limit, bound, rhythm, manner’
↓
Classical Latin Denominative Verb (1st c. BCE)
moderārī
‘to set a measure to, regulate, govern, restrain’
↓
Classical Latin Participle (1st c. BCE)
moderātus
‘within bounds, temperate, restrained’
↓
Latin Negative Prefixation (c. 1st c. CE)
in- + moderātus → immoderātus
‘unmeasured, unrestrained, excessive, immense’
↓
Old / Middle French (c. 14th c.)
immoderat / immoderé
‘excessive, unrestrained’
↓
Middle English (late 14th c.)
immoderate
‘exceeding just measure, intemperate’
↓
Modern English (current)
immoderate
‘exceeding reasonable limits, excessive, unrestrained’

Note: The assimilation of the negative prefix in- to im- before the bilabial nasal /m/ is a standard phonological rule of Latin. The semantic root *med- also yielded Latin medērī (‘to heal’, source of medicine, lit. ‘to take appropriate measures for’), meditārī (‘to think on, meditate’), and Germanic *metan (Old English metan, ‘to measure’, source of modern mete).
