Berthing traces to a single obscure noun — berth — plus the regular -ing. The noun's own ancestry is debated, and the debate must be reported, not hidden.

1. BERTH: sea room that became a bed. Berth (also spelled byrth and birth in the 1500s to 1700s) first appears in early-1600s nautical writing — OED's earliest evidence is Richard Hawkins in 1622; Bailey's dictionary glosses it in the 1620s as convenient sea room for ships or sailors. That original sense is pure clearance: enough water to manœuvre without hitting rocks, other ships or the shore. The figurative phrase to give a wide berth (keep well away from) preserves it untouched. The leading etymology, accepted provisionally by Etymonline and the Historical Thesaurus and argued at length in the OED's discussion, derives berth from the verb bear (to carry, to bear off, to stand clear) plus the abstract noun suffix -th as in strength, health, wealth, depth (Proto-Germanic *-itha). The semantic path would be: bearing off (steering clear) to room made by bearing off to allotted room to bunk. A 1627 quotation for bear in the sense bearing off is cited as the smoking gun: berth equals bearing-off room. On this view berth is analysable as bear plus -th and is a piecewise doublet of birth (bearing of offspring), explaining the centuries of shared spellings birth, byrth without making the sailor's berth derive from the mother's birth — a coincidence of suffix, not of sense. Competing proposals are all recorded and all doubted: derivation from an unattested Old English *beorth (protection, shelter, related to barth); borrowing from Icelandic byrdi (ship's board, side); transposition of northern dialect breith (breadth) — for which, the OED notes, historical evidence is entirely wanting. Wiktionary's Late Middle English birth (bearing away, clearance) plus beren (to bear) plus -th restates the bear-theory compactly. The verb to berth follows swiftly: 1660s of ships (to assign anchoring ground to), 1886 of persons intransitively (to occupy a berth). The noun's sense development is tightly dated: sea room (1620s) to anchoring ground allotted (1660s) to place on ship to stow chests and sleep (1706) to any sleeping place ashore (1778, railway berth, troop berth). Each step is an administrative narrowing: from water anyone avoids to water someone assigns to wood someone sleeps on.

  • Bear background: Old English beran (to carry, endure), past baer to Middle English bare and bore (c. 1400), participle borne and born (late 1700s distinction); PIE *bher (to carry). Suffix -th from Proto-Germanic *-itho forming condition nouns.
  • Why debated: no Old English *berth is attested; early spellings collide with birth; sense sea room does not obviously come from shelter or ship's side; breadth-metathesis lacks textual chain. The OED therefore labels the noun of uncertain origin while calling the bear-plus-th account most probable.
  • Literary harbour: Hawkins, Pepys, Cook's voyages (berths allotted), 19th-century emigrant ships (berth lists), railway timetables (sleeping berth), modern marina contracts (berthing fees).

2. -ING and BERTHING. The suffix -ing continues Old English -ing and -ung (Proto-Germanic *-inga), forming action nouns. Berthing as verbal noun (action of berthing a ship; 1660s onward) and as cost-item (berthing charges, berthing master) is regular. In the context it nominalizes the annual marina invoice and the seasonal warping in and out that, with outfitting and maintenance and failing eyesight, ends Vance's ownership of Hinano. Modern ports distinguish berthing (assigning the quay slot) from mooring (the ropes), docking (entering dock), anchoring (lying to anchor) and dry-docking (lifting out) — a precise division of labour that the single 1620s word convenient sea room could never have foreseen. Science fiction extends it without change: to berth a starship is to give it sea room among stars.
