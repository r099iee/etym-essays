coronation (noun) — the formal ceremony of crowning a sovereign, in which a crown is placed upon the monarch’s head and the monarch is invested with the emblems of rule: the orb, the sceptre, the ring, the robe. The word names the hinge of a reign, the day on which costume becomes authority and a claimant becomes a king or queen. In the treasure-chamber passage the children handle “our coronation rings” — the rings worn on the supreme day when they were formally made “Kings and Queens of Narnia.” The nuance is solemn culmination: the coronation is not one event among many but the pivot of their whole royal history, the moment at which the crown, the ring, and the throne passed to them. The very word carries ceremony in its syllables; it is heavier, more official, and more sacred than “crowning,” and it reaches back to a day of trumpets, oaths, robes, and the touch of the crown. The ring is the perfect synecdoche: a small gold circle that sums up the entire public ritual of the state, and that can be held in the hand long after the ceremony is over.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Literal sense (the ceremony): the rite of crowning a monarch — the English coronation at Westminster Abbey, the French coronation at Reims, the Russian crowning of the tsars, the Byzantine crowning of emperors; the word almost always implies the full religious and civic ritual, not just the placing of the crown.
  • Temporal and metonymic sense: “a coronation” can mean the era or reign that a crowning begins — “a reign that started with a coronation,” “from coronation to funeral” — the ceremony standing for the whole kingship.
  • Ecclesiastical sense: a coronation is commonly a religious rite of consecration and anointing, fused with the Mass and the investiture of regalia; the chrism anointing precedes the crowning in the Roman and Byzantine traditions.
  • Musical sense: “Coronation” titles famous works — Mozart’s Coronation Mass (K. 317) and Coronation Concerto (K. 537), Handel’s Coronation Anthems (1727, for George II) — the word marking the occasion a piece was written for.
  • Political metaphor: a “coronation” is a leadership choice that is effectively pre-decided, with no real contest — “the primary became a coronation” — the idea of being crowned rather than winning.
  • Culinary sense: “coronation chicken” — the cold chicken dish in curried mayonnaise sauce created in 1953 for the coronation banquet of Queen Elizabeth II; the word now labels a whole genre of occasion-dishes.
  • Figurative sense (culmination): “the coronation of a career” — the crowning achievement, the summit of a life’s work; the ceremony generalized into any culminating honour.
  • Television and cultural reference: Coronation Street, the British television soap opera (from 1960), is named for the street, in turn named for the coronation of Queen Victoria in 1837 — the word lodged in popular culture.
The semantic history is a long narrowing and then a widening. Etymologically the word is built from the Latin for “crown” (see etymologyS2), and for centuries it meant only the crowning of a sovereign; the transfer into metaphor (any summit, any pre-decided prize) is modern, and the culinary and television senses show how a word of high state ceremony can be domesticated into everyday life. Each secondary sense keeps the core idea of a crowning — an object or moment placed at the top, elevated above the rest, set above the ordinary run of things.

Coronation marks its boundary against several near-synonyms. Crowning is the plain, functional word for putting a crown on a head, and it is used loosely of anything that completes or tops something (“the crowning achievement”); it lacks institutional weight. Enthronement names the specific act of seating the monarch on the throne — one moment within the larger ritual, centred on the chair rather than the head. Investiture is the formal installation of anyone into an office, honour, or order (a knight, a bishop, a president) and deliberately avoids the crown. Inauguration is the secular, civic ceremony that opens an office or building — deliberately republican and uncrowned. Consecration shifts the focus to religious blessing of a person or thing. Anointing names the chrism rite that precedes the crowning. Accession is the legal act of succeeding to the throne, which precedes the ceremony itself. Among these, only coronation is reserved for the placing of a crown on the head of a sovereign in full state ritual.
