The story of debilitating begins not with sickness but with holding. Its deepest reconstructible ancestor is PIE *ghabh-, to give, receive, and hence to hold and have, which yields Latin habere, to have or hold. From habere Latin formed habilis, literally easily held or handled, hence handy, apt, fit, capable — the ancestor of English able, ability, agility via French. Negated and worn down by sound change, habilis becomes debilis: un-handy, un-able, literally unable to be handled or to handle oneself — lame, feeble, infirm. Cicero and contemporaries use debilis for crippled limbs, broken soldiers, and figuratively for weak arguments and enfeebled minds.

From this adjective Latin built a regular causative verb debilitare, to make debilis — to lame, cripple, weaken, disable — attested in classical prose for wounds, disease, and also for luxury or fear weakening resolve. Its past participle debilitatus, weakened, supplied the stem that English humanists borrowed directly in the 1530s. Thomas Elyot and Tudor translators of Galen and rhetoric use debilitate in medical and oratorical senses: bodies debilitated by sickness, stomachs by surfeit, minds by sloth, arguments by concession. The spelling with single l reflects Latin, not French mediation.

  • Phonetic path: PIE *gh (voiced aspirate) regularly becomes Latin h in *ghabh- > habere; short vowels and suffix -ilis produce habilis with stress on the first syllable; prefixation with de- and haplological loss yields debilis (ha- dropped); verb suffix -itare gives debilitare with long a; English keeps Latin spelling but applies the Great Vowel Shift and stress shift, giving modern /dɪˈbɪl.ɪ.teɪt/ and participial /dɪˈbɪl.ɪ.teɪ.tɪŋ/.
  • Semantic drift: holdable → handy/capable → not-capable/lame → to make lame → to weaken generally → to sap strength of minds, economies, and morale. Each step generalises the locus of weakness from hands to legs to whole bodies to abstractions.
  • Socio-cultural context: Tudor medicine was Galenic and humoral; to debilitate meant to unbalance humours through excess bleeding, purging, or luxury. Victorian empire added climatic and moral anxiety: tropical climates, masturbation, novel-reading, and city life were all called debilitating to robust English constitutions. Twentieth-century psychiatry and welfare medicine normalised the psychological sense now dominant.
  • Literary attestations: Elyot's Castell of Helth (1530s) warns against things that debilitate the stomach; Burton's Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) describes sorrow as debilitating spirits; nineteenth-century novels and asylum reports speak of debilitating grief and overstudy; Woolf criticism, as in the source, routinely calls her depressive episodes debilitating — a humane clinical term replacing older moralising words like weak or hysterical.

Why -ing? English formed the present participle debilitating to express active causation: not the state of being weak (debility) nor the process (debilitation) but the property of a cause that produces weakness. By the 1800s debilitating effects, climate, disease are common collocations. The form thus grammaticalises the body-as-machine metaphor of modern medicine: causes act, bodies are acted upon, and the reviewer can label any drain on capacity — from chemotherapy to bureaucracy — with a single Latinate adjective.
