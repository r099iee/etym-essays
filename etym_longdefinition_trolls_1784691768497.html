Trolls are supernatural beings of Scandinavian folklore, the plural form being used of more than one. In the Old Norse sagas and the later folk belief of Sweden, Norway, and Denmark, a troll (Old Norse troll) is a being of the untamed wilderness: at one end of the tradition a huge, ancient, mountain-dwelling giant, at the other a cave-haunting, magical being of either sex (the female troll, the trollkona or troll-wife, is a witch of formidable power). Trolls are habitually hostile or treacherous towards humans, are skilled in magic and shape-shifting, and — in the best-known folk belief — cannot endure daylight, turning to stone or bursting apart when the sun rises. The word has also carried a secondary sense of clumsy, lumpish stupidity, and the noun has long suggested a heavy, dull, uncouth creature.

In The Hobbit, Chapter 2, the three figures round the fire are revealed in a sentence of five words: "But they were trolls." The revelation does two things at once. Dramatically, it converts a snug, almost domestic fireside picture — three large persons toasting mutton — into mortal danger: trolls eat dwarves and hobbits, and their conversation runs on "mutton yesterday, mutton today, and blimey, if it don't look like mutton again tomorrow". Comically, it attaches all the folklore Tolkien's audience knew: these trolls bicker, speak a rough Cockney-flavoured English, carry purses and smell of raw meat, and their fate is sealed by the folkloric law of sunlight, for when dawn breaks they turn to stone and stand in the woods as "stone trolls" for generations.

Polysemy and secondary senses. The word has spread far beyond folklore, and a full account must separate its modern meanings:

  • Scandinavian folklore (primary): the mountain-giant or cave-being of Norse tradition, magical, man-eating, hostile, daylight-hating — the sense in Tolkien.
  • Transferred sense (of a person): from the folklore, a "troll" is a clumsy, stupid, lumpish, or boorish person — a heavy dullard; the transferred human sense is recorded from the 19th century.
  • Internet trolling (modern, dominant): since the 1990s, a "troll" is a person who posts provocative, insulting, or inflammatory messages online purely to upset others and derail discussion; "to troll" is the verb and "trolling" the practice. The origin of this sense is debated — the metaphor may be the monster lurking under the bridge, or the fishing verb "to troll" (to draw a baited line through the water in hope of a strike); both images converge on the notion of angling for reactions.
  • In computing and gaming: "troll" extends to any deliberately disruptive or baiting behaviour — a "troll post", a "troll account"; in fantasy gaming trolls are standard regenerating enemies, so the word's popular image has hardened toward the brute that heals and comes again.
  • Homonymous verb (unrelated): the English verb "to troll" — to sing or roll out a song cheerfully, to fish by trailing a line, to trundle — comes from Old French troller "to hunt in a wandering fashion", a completely different word that now coincides in form with the monster noun and feeds the internet sense through the fishing image.

Historical bridge. The chain of meanings runs from myth to insult to internet: the folkloric being → the figure of clumsy stupidity → the online provocateur who lurks under the bridge of a discussion thread and baits passers-by, exactly as the folktale troll waits under the bridge to eat travellers. Tolkien's trolls sit midway in this history, as the bridge between the Old Norse giant and the modern metaphor — and as the source of one of the most quoted sentences in children's literature, the five words "But they were trolls".
