Core meaning. Fury is an exceptionally intense, violent form of anger, so overwhelming that it takes over a person or a force completely and can barely be contained or reasoned with. In the supplied passage, Smaug's fury is the dragon's raging, destructive wrath: the roar and rumble that pours out of the mountain is literally the sound of his fury, an anger so great that it smashes rocks, shatters walls, and turns a whole mountainside into an avalanche of rubble. The word always implies more than mere annoyance or irritation — it suggests an eruption, a storm, or a blaze of passion that has, at least for a time, escaped all restraint.

Primary sense: violent personal rage. The dominant modern meaning of fury is a state of fierce, often uncontrollable anger. One "flies into a fury," "trembles with fury," or "is shaking with fury." This is anger at its extreme, destructive end of the scale, bordering on madness or frenzy. It is far stronger than anger, annoyance, or irritation, and it describes a person who has temporarily lost rational self-command. Etymologically and historically this personal sense is the stem from which all the other meanings grow, and it remains the sense speakers reach for first.

Sense 2: the raging force of nature. Because rage was so often compared to a storm, a fire, or a wild animal, fury naturally extended to any natural force that "rages": the fury of the sea, the fury of a hurricane, the fury of a forest fire, the fury of the wind. In this sense the word means violent, destructive energy rather than emotion, and it is exactly the sense used of Smaug in the given sentence — his fury is not merely a feeling but a force, something audible and catastrophic that breaks rocks and brings down cliffs.

Sense 3: vehemence and intensity (metaphoric extension). In modern English fury has broadened still further to describe sheer speed or intensity with no anger at all. A news report may speak of a "furious rally" on the stock market, athletes play "at a furious pace," and the fixed phrase "fast and furious" describes non-stop, unrestrained activity, from car chases to parties. Here the semantic bridge is the sheer forcefulness of the emotion: anything that happens with maximum energy and violence of motion can be called furious.

Sense 4: the mythological beings (the Furies). Capitalised, the Furies were the goddesses of vengeance in Greco-Roman religion — the Greek Erinyes, Latin Furiae — spirits who punished wrongdoing, especially murder and crimes within the family, by pursuing the guilty and sometimes driving them mad. This mythological sense gave the English word its permanent undertone of avenging, almost superhuman wrath, and it is still actively used in literary, artistic, and scholarly contexts today.

Sense 5: older and rarer senses. In earlier English, fury also meant "inspiration" or "poetic frenzy" (the Latin furore poeticus — the divine madness that was supposed to seize poets, prophets, and seers). In older medicine, "fury" or "furor" named acute mania or raving madness. Both senses are now archaic or technical, but they explain why fury so often appears beside ideas of the supernatural, the prophetic, and the insane.

Grammar and word-formation. Fury is chiefly a mass (uncountable) noun: "full of fury," "in a fury." It can take a countable sense in "a fury" (a fit of rage) and in the plural "furies" (both the goddesses and, loosely, several outbreaks of rage). The adjective is furious, the adverb furiously, and the noun furiousness shares the same stem. Common idioms include "in a fury," "with a fury," "fly into a fury," "the fury of the storm," and "fast and furious."

Register and style. Fury is a literary and emphatic word. It belongs to narrative prose, poetry, headlines, and fantasy — exactly the register of Tolkien's dragon-passage — and it is avoided in clinical or diplomatic language, where "anger" or "irritation" is preferred. Its power comes from the fact that it always summons the image of something burning, storming, or avenging: even when it simply means "great speed," it does so with maximum dramatic charge.

Collocations worth learning. "blazing / cold / silent fury," "to vent one's fury," "the fury of the battle," "fury erupted," "to do something in a fury." Notice the spread of collocates across all three senses — emotion (blazing fury), natural force (fury of the storm), and abstract intensity (furious pace) — which mirrors the word's history from divine wrath to everyday vehemence.
