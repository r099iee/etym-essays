Proto-Germanic *twikjan- — "to pluck, pinch, snatch with a quick jerky motion"; an expressive echoic base coined in Germanic to imitate the pinch-and-release of plucking; NO SECURE PIE ETYMON (OED: "probably imitative") ↓ Old English twiccian (also twiccan) — "to pluck, pinch, catch hold of suddenly" (attested in Anglo-Saxon prose and glosses, c. 8th–11th c.); noun twicung "a twitching, a plucking" ↓ Middle English twicchen — "to pull sharply, to pluck" (c. 1200) ↓ Modern English twitch (verb) — transitive sense "to pluck, to pull out" (continuing in dialect, e.g. twitching weeds); intransitive sense "to make a sudden slight spasmodic movement" (from c. 1500s) — THIS IS POTTER'S SENSE ↓ English twitched — regular weak past tense and past participle (twitch + -ed); Beatrix Potter, The Tale of Peter Rabbit, 1902: "the tip of her tail twitched as if it were alive" DEBATED / NUANCED: The etymology is considered "probably imitative" by the OED. *twik- is reconstructed from West Germanic reflexes only (cf. Middle Dutch twicken, Middle Low German twicken, German zwicken "to pinch"); Old Norse shows no clear cognate, using kippa "to snatch" instead. The word belongs to the English "tw-" echoic family (twirl, twitter, twiddle, tweak, twang), none of which traces to PIE. Do not attach a PIE root to this imitative base.
