blithering: An intensive adjective meaning "complete, utter, senseless," derived from the verb blither, to talk nonsense; its core sense is "babbling, driveling," and in the phrase "a blithering sandstorm" Maspic transfers the word from foolish people to weather. The storm is thereby painted as a mindless babbler: a chaotic, incoherent force that raves across the desert without sense or purpose, like a lunatic shouting at the sky. The tone is colloquial, contemptuous, and faintly humorous — the sandstorm is not merely bad but stupid, an idiot wind, overwhelming in a way that seems idiotic rather than sublime. Because blithering normally attaches to fools, its application to a storm is a small act of comic personification: nature reduced to a blathering fool, all noise and no intelligence. The word also functions as a pure intensifier (as in "blithering idiot"), so it simultaneously grades the noun upward in extremity and downward in dignity. Primary and secondary senses.
  • Babbling, senselessly talkative (the etymological sense): the participle of blither, "to talk foolishly, to drivel," describing speech that is meaningless and incoherent — a mouth full of noise without thought. This is the oldest sense, and it still lurks beneath the intensive.
  • Complete, utter, hopeless (the intensive sense): in "blithering idiot," popularized by early twentieth-century comic writing, the word stopped describing talk and became a pure intensifier — "utter, complete, hopeless" — the babbling sense collapsing into a single blast of contempt. This is the dominant modern sense.
  • Transferred to things (Peake's usage): because the intensifier can attach to anything idiotic, it transfers to weather, machinery, and situations: a blithering sandstorm, a blithering waste of time — the note of contemptuous, dismissive idiocy applied to the non-human.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. The word descends from the expressive "babbling" family — Old Norse blaðra, "to babble, to mutter," beside Old English blǣdrian, "to blather," and Middle English blathren — and from the same root-world as bladder, the wind-filled bag: the ancient image of talk as a bladder of wind, inflated, hollow, bursting with air. The semantic bridge is compressed in that image: from talk that is all wind (babble) to a person who is all wind (a fool) to an intensifier that calls anything utterly foolish — the movement from noise to insult. In Old English, blǣdre, "bladder," and the blabbering verbs belong to the same expressive field of breath and speech, so that blithering never quite loses the sense of wind and hollowness. Register and grammar. Blithering is a colloquial, contemptuous word, used almost always attributively before a noun — a blithering idiot, blithering nonsense, blithering fool — and its power lies in fusing intensity with stupidity: it does not merely grade the noun upward but dismisses it as idiotically excessive. Against its neighbours: babbling describes actual meaningless speech; driveling implies weak, slobbering foolishness; gibbering suggests frantic, chattering, monkey-like noise; dithering — a false friend from Middle English dideren, "to tremble" — means vacillating indecision and is unrelated; utter, complete, and absolute are neutral intensifiers without the note of foolishness. Only blithering fuses intensity with contemptuous stupidity — precisely the note Maspic strikes about the oncoming storm, which is at once overwhelming and idiotic, sublime in power and ridiculous in mindlessness.
