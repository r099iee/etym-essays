Proto-Indo-European (c. 3000 BCE)
*dek-
"to take, accept, perceive, suitable"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*dokeje-
"to make accept, teach, show"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
docēre
"to teach, instruct, demonstrate"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE)
doctrīna
"teaching, instruction, learning, body of principles"
↓
Late Latin / Medieval Latin (c. 6th–12th c.)
indoctrīnāre
"to teach, instruct, imbue with learning"
↓
Early Modern English (c. 1610s–1620s)
indoctrinate
"to instruct, educate, imbue with knowledge"
↓
Modern English (mid-20th c.–present)
indoctrination
"dogmatic inculcation, ideological conditioning"

Note: The base root *dek- yielded an immense family of English words denoting suitability, dignity, and teaching, including decent, dignity, docile, doctor, and dogma. Indoctrination underwent a classic pejorative semantic drift during the mid-20th century, transforming from an honorable synonym of education into a sinister label for ideological thought reform.
