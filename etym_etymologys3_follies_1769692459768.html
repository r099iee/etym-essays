From windbag to whimsy: the evolution of FOLLIES

The story of follies begins not with ideas but with air. The reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *bhel- meant "to blow, swell, inflate." In the early pastoral and craft societies of the Indo-European homeland the root produced words for anything puffed up with air or round: bladders, bags, balls, and bellows. Its descendants are scattered across the whole family — Germanic gave English ball and bellows; Hellenic gave Greek phallos "swollen object" and phallaina "whale, the swollen sea-creature"; Celtic gave Old Irish bolg "bag, belly"; Indo-Aryan gave Sanskrit bhāla "forehead, the swelling brow." All these preserve the physical image of inflation, which matters because the entire later career of folly hangs on a metaphor of swelling.

The crucial Latin turn. In the Italic branch the root produced Latin follis: a leather bag, a pair of blacksmith's bellows, an inflated ball, a puffed-out cheek, and a money-purse. Two features of Roman life fed the word's future. First, the bellows: a follis was the smith's air-bag, noisy, expansive, and full of wind. Second, the purse: the follis was also a leather money-bag, later even a coin of the late empire (the bronze follis of Diocletian's currency reform, c. 294 CE). The decisive step was colloquial. In the slang of the Roman street, to call a man a follis was to say he was a "windbag" — loud, puffed-up, full of hot air, and empty-headed: a fool. The image is exactly like calling someone "full of hot air" today, and it is the hinge on which the whole semantic history of the word turns.

From Latin to French to English. As Latin dissolved into the Romance vernaculars of Gaul, follis became Old French fol, first an adjective "mad, foolish," then a noun "fool, jester." From fol came the abstract noun folie, "madness, folly, a foolish act." The Norman Conquest carried folie into Middle English by the early 13th century, where it competed with and largely replaced native words for foolishness such as Old English dæftnes and unwisdom. The early Middle English Ancrene Wisse (c. 1225) already uses folie in the moral sense of sinful folly, showing how fully the borrowed word had taken root in devotional and satirical writing alike.

Sound change and the modern shape. The spelling folly reflects the Old French form folie (the final -e was lost in Middle English), and the plural follies shows the ordinary English plural -s attached to the noun. Pronounced /fˈɒliz/ in RP, the word has the short open o of "fossil," entirely native in its phonology though Romance in its ancestry.

Moral and satirical life in English. From the 13th century onward folly flourished in two registers: the moral and the satirical. Theologians used it for sin considered as irrationality — the fool of the Psalms who "hath said in his heart, there is no God" is the archetype. Satirists used it for social vanity: Erasmus's Moriae Encomium (In Praise of Folly, 1511) made folly the speaker and the organizing concept of a vast mock-encomium, and that Renaissance tradition fed directly into the English literature of the 18th century. The Age of Satire — Swift, Pope, Goldsmith — treated "the follies of mankind" as the natural subject of comedy. Jane Austen inherits this exact tradition: in Pride and Prejudice the narrator's world is full of folly, and Elizabeth's indulgent word for it ("follies and nonsense") is precisely the satirist's compromise between anger and amusement.

The material turn: architectural and theatrical follies. In the late 17th and 18th centuries the word took a decisive material twist. Landscape gardeners on the great estates of England and France began erecting sham ruins, fake medieval towers, grottoes, and pagodas purely for ornament — buildings with no purpose but to please the eye. The French called such a pleasure-house a folie (literally "a madness"), and English borrowed the architectural sense directly: a "folly" became a costly, useless structure that only extravagance and whimsy could explain. This is the modern dictionary sense of a garden folly. In the early 20th century the word leapt again, from stone to stage: the lavish revues of Florenz Ziegfeld Jr., which ran from 1907 to 1931, were christened the Ziegfeld Follies, taking the word's older aura of extravagant, purposeless display and turning it into glamorous mass entertainment.

Summary of the semantic drift. The chain runs: a leather bag full of air (Latin follis) → a man full of hot air (Vulgar Latin slang) → madness and foolishness (Old French folie) → the foolish act and the quality of foolishness (English folly) → a foolishly costly building (18th-c. English) → a lavish revue (20th-c. English). Every later sense is a metaphor built on the earlier one, and the whole ladder rests on a two-thousand-year-old joke about windbags.
