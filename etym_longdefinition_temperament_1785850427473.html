TEMPERAMENT is a person's characteristic disposition — the habitual pattern of feeling, thinking, and reacting that colours how they meet the world and is felt as their nature rather than their mood. In the reading passage the word carries its modern core sense: "Her calm, pragmatic temperament made her an exceptional leader during times of sudden crisis" — it is her inborn emotional framework, the stable set of traits (here calmness and pragmatism) that equips her to act consistently under pressure. A temperament is what a person is like underneath their passing moods: the slow-burning or quick-flaring, the sanguine or melancholic, the steady or restless pattern that shows itself again and again across a lifetime.

SENSES AND USES

  • Core modern sense: innate, characteristic disposition or emotional nature — "a fiery temperament", "a calm temperament", "a volatile temperament". The word names the habitual way a person feels and reacts, distinguished from a single mood or a temporary frame of mind.
  • Historical humoral sense (ancient and medieval medicine): in Greco-Roman and medieval medicine, the temperament was the balance of the four bodily humors — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — that determined both health and character; the classical scheme of four temperaments (sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, melancholic) is still invoked in popular characterology.
  • Music theory and acoustics: "temperament" is the tuning system by which the intervals of the scale are slightly adjusted ("tempered") so that an instrument can play in all keys without dissonance — "equal temperament" divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, while "just temperament" uses pure intervals; the term entered music in the 16th–17th centuries and was consecrated by Bach's The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722).
  • Metallurgy and materials science: the "temper" (and historically "temperament") of steel, glass, or metal is the degree of hardness or elasticity produced by controlled heat treatment — the material is "tempered" by heating and cooling to give it its working quality; this is a technical residue of the word's ancient "proportion" sense applied to alloys.
  • "Artistic temperament" (19th century): the volatile, sensitive, excitable nature romantically attributed to artists, musicians, and writers — the phrase names the supposed emotional intensity and unpredictability of the creative personality.
  • Animal behaviour and veterinary science: the "temperament" of a horse, dog, or breed is its characteristic disposition and trainability — "a good temperament", "a nervous temperament"; the word is standard in equestrian and animal-behaviour writing.
  • Psychology (modern technical sense): in personality psychology, "temperament" is the biologically based, early-appearing core of personality — the genetically influenced dimensions (activity, reactivity, emotionality, sociability) visible in infancy; researchers such as Thomas and Chess described infant temperaments as "easy", "difficult", and "slow to warm up".
  • Climatology and geography (archaic): the "temperament" of a region could mean its climate — the tempering of heat and cold — an old sense now confined to historical texts.
  • Register: temperament is a formal, neutral word, at home in psychology, medicine, equestrian writing, and ordinary educated speech; it is heavier and more technical than "nature", more stable in meaning than "temper".

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: Latin temperamentum meant "due mixture, proper proportion", built on temperāre, "to mix in due proportion, moderate, restrain", itself derived from tempus, "time, due measure, right moment". The core image is of mixing ingredients in the right proportion — the way a cook blends, a musician tunes, a smith heats and cools. Ancient Greco-Roman medicine (the Hippocratics and especially Galen) applied this image literally to the body: health and personality depended on the balance of the four humors, and a person's temperament was their humor mixture — the sanguine blend (blood dominant) cheerful, the choleric (yellow bile) hot-tempered, the melancholic (black bile) gloomy, the phlegmatic (phlegm) calm. From this medical sense the "proportion" idea transferred outward: to music, where the temperament is the proportionate adjustment of intervals; to metals, where tempering is the proportionate hardening of steel; and to the mind, where temperament is the proportionate blend of emotional qualities that makes each person's nature what it is. The modern psychological sense — the inborn emotional framework — is the direct heir of the humoral "mixture" of ancient medicine, with the four humors replaced by dimensions of reactivity and emotionality.
