Knaves, the plural of knave, in its dominant modern sense means dishonest, unscrupulous men — rogues, cheats, and schemers who live by trickery. It is a word of moral condemnation with an archaic flavour: nobody is called a knave in a police report, but pamphleteers, novelists, and orators have used it for centuries to brand the corrupt.

In the source passage Cobbett hurls it twice at the loyalist conspirators of 1793 who met at the Crown and Anchor to crush reformers by prosecutions and spies. His knaves are not boy servants but political operators — men who would coax the landowners estates and capital into their own hands while the government looked kindly on. The repetition hammers the charge: these are not statesmen but sharpers, and their patriotism is a mask for plunder.

Core meaning in detail: A knave is a man void of honour: he lies, betrays, informs, overcharges, and intrigues, all the while posing as respectable. The word always takes a masculine noun in traditional use and always implies agency — a knave is not merely mistaken but wilfully crooked. Its force lies in contempt rather than precision: to call a man a knave is to declare him beneath argument, fit only for exposure.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Dishonest unscrupulous man (primary modern sense, from 16th c.): a rogue, cheat, or villain, as in Cobbett's conspirators. The bridge from servant to scoundrel runs through class contempt: low serving men were stereotyped as pilfering and lying, until the name of the station became the name of the vice.
  • A male servant or menial (earliest sense, Old English): cnafa meant a boy, youth, or serving lad with no stain attached. The bridge to later senses is purely social: servants were distrusted by their masters, and distrust fermented over centuries into definition.
  • A man of humble birth or low station (Middle English): a common fellow as opposed to a gentleman. The bridge is the same class ladder: knave marked the bottom rung, and the moral sense grew like moss on the low stone.
  • The Jack at cards (specialised gaming sense, 17th c.): the knave of hearts, of spades — the lowest court card, depicting a servant-soldier. The bridge is rank: the Jack stands below King and Queen exactly as the knave-servant stood below his master, and the card keeps the medieval household frozen in pasteboard.
  • A crafty fellow, sly rogue (milder literary shade): Shakespeare's jesting villains and clever serving-men — Autolycus the snapper-up of unconsidered trifles. The bridge softens villainy into roguish charm: the knave as trickster, wicked but delightful, whom the audience forgives.
  • A false or traitorous political actor (polemical register, Cobbett's use): informers, placemen, and alarmists who trade in prosecutions. The bridge fuses dishonesty with power: the knave stops being a petty cheat and becomes a public danger, which is why the word thrives in ages of political paranoia.

Register and connotation: knaves is deliberately old-fashioned in modern mouths — pulpit, hustings, and historical-novel English. That archaism is its power: it summons a world of stocks and pillories, of honour plainly legible, and applies that world's rough justice to modern rogues. It is almost always plural in polemic, because knavery loves company, and almost always contemptuous, though literature preserves the affectionate knave of comedy. No one calls himself a knave except in confession or jest.
