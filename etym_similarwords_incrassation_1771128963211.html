• Thickening: Similar because both name the process of becoming denser or more viscous; differs because thickening is the plain, everyday word, lacking the clinical gravity and historical humoral associations of incrassation.
  • Coagulation: Similar because both describe a fluid turning semi-solid or solid; differs because coagulation specifically denotes the clotting of blood or proteins, often a sudden response to injury, whereas incrassation is a gradual, pathological densifying.
  • Congealment: Similar because both describe the loss of fluidity; differs because congealment typically results from cooling (as fat sets on a cold plate), while incrassation implies a creeping, unhealthy thickening independent of temperature.
  • Condensation: Similar because both describe a change in consistency or state; differs because condensation usually means gas becoming liquid or volume shrinking, missing the organic, viscous sludginess of incrassation.
  • Inspissation: Similar because both are learned terms for making or becoming thick; differs because inspissation is even rarer and purely technical, often of liquids boiled down, while incrassation carries humoral-medical and figurative weight.
  • Clotting: Similar because both describe a liquid losing its fluid flow; differs because clotting is the ordinary word for blood or milk forming solid masses, without incrassation's slow, systemic, pathological overtones.
  • Gelation: Similar because both describe the formation of a semi-solid state; differs because gelation is a modern technical term of colloid chemistry, whereas incrassation belongs to an older humoral and alchemical register.
  • Solidification: Similar because both mean becoming solid; differs because solidification suggests a complete change to a hard state (as molten metal cooling), while incrassation is a gradual densifying that stops short of hardness.
