PIE *as- “to burn, glow, be dry” (c. 4000 BCE; one of the oldest fire-roots of the family, with reflexes in Anatolian, Indo-Iranian, Greek, and Italic) → Proto-Italic *āz- / *ard- (reconstructed; the branch that produced both “dry” and “burn”) → Latin ārēre “to be dry, parched” (also the source of āridus “dry”, English arid) → Latin ardēre “to burn, be on fire” (also “to be consumed with passion, to burn with love or desire”) → Latin ardor “heat, flame” (accusative ardōrem, “burning heat”; also figurative “passion, zeal”) → Old French ardeur “heat, passion, zeal” (12th c.) → Middle English ardour / ardur “burning heat; passion, zeal” (c. 1400) → English ARDOUR “glowing, consuming enthusiasm; fervour” (modern sense; the American spelling ardor preserves the Latin form). Debated / nuanced stages: the exact relation of Greek azō “to dry, parch” and the Hittite ḫaššā- “hearth, fireplace” to the Latin ārēre/ardēre pair is debated; some scholars set the Greek and Hittite forms under a related but distinct root, and the internal Italic development from *as- to ard-ēre (with the r-formant) is reconstructed rather than directly attested. The figurative sense (“to burn with passion”) is already attested in Classical Latin literature (Cicero, Virgil, Ovid) and was not an English innovation; English first borrowed the word in its physical sense and acquired the figurative sense through the Romance inheritance.
