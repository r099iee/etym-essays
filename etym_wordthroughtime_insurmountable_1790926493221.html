- 16th–17th Century - Formed in Early Modern English from French *insurmontable*, initially describing physical precipices, mountain walls, and defensive fortifications that could not be scaled.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment) - Shifted from physical topography to abstract philosophical, legal, and intellectual barriers that reason or human capacity could not overcome.
- 19th–20th Century - Became a staple of political discourse, social reform essays, and military strategy to describe insurmountable odds, systemic crises, or structural disadvantages.
- 21st Century - Widely used in science, computing, and economics to designate fundamental physical boundaries, algorithmic complexities, and climate challenges.
