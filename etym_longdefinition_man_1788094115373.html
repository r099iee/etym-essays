In the sentence at hand, MAN is a verb: 'themselves to man the great spurs' — that is, to occupy and defend the mountain spurs with armed men, taking up fighting positions. The verb 'to man' means to supply with people (historically, with men) and to take one's place so as to operate, guard, or defend something: to man the walls, man the barricades, man the pumps, man a ship, man the phones, man a stall.

1. The verb senses

  • To garrison / defend: to station people on or in a defensive position — 'man the ramparts', 'man the trenches', 'man the walls'. This is exactly the sense in the context, where the defenders go to occupy the spurs of the Mountain.
  • To operate: to supply the workforce needed to run something — 'man a ship', 'man the pumps', 'man the switchboard', 'man a call centre'.
  • To brace oneself (reflexive, archaic/literary): 'man oneself' = to summon courage; 'man up' (modern colloquial) = to behave with expected toughness; 'manning up' is the 20th-century phrasal extension.

2. The noun senses (the source of the verb)

  • Adult male human being: the primary modern noun sense — 'a man and a woman'; also used generically of humankind ('man is mortal', 'early man') and historically as a gender-neutral term for a person ('no man is an island').
  • Humankind collectively: 'man' = the human species ('the ascent of man', 'man's inhumanity to man'). This generic use, though still current in elevated prose, is now often replaced by 'humankind' or 'humanity' because of its perceived male bias — a 20th-century semantic-political shift.
  • Servant / subordinate: a male servant or employee ('my man'); in military use, 'men' = the rank and file ('officers and men'); a vassal, from the feudal relationship of lord and man.
  • Gaming, interjection, slang: 'man!' as an exclamation of address or emphasis ('man, that was close!'); 'the Man' (slang) = the establishment or authority, or a drug dealer (1960s counterculture); 'my man' = my friend, associate, or boyfriend.
  • Chess and games: 'man' = a playing piece ('a chessman', 'men' on a board).
  • Husband: in dialectal, archaic, and formal usage ('man and wife'), 'man' = husband.

3. Semantic bridges

  • From 'adult male person' to 'supply with people': the verb (c. 1450, originally of ships) arises because a ship or fortification is 'manned' — provided with its crew or garrison of men — and the word then generalises to any operation needing staff ('man the phones').
  • From 'person / human' to 'servant, subordinate, follower': the social hierarchy of early England made 'man' the natural word for a dependent — hence the feudal 'vassal', the 'henchman', the 'man of the house'.
  • From 'male person' to generic 'humankind': in Old English mann was the general word for any human being (the adult female was wīfmann 'woman', literally 'female person'); the narrowing to adult male happened in Middle English (13th–14th c.), leaving the generic sense as a fossil in 'mankind', 'man-eating tiger', 'no man's land'.

4. Register and usage notes

  • The verb is standard in military, nautical, and operational contexts, and is entirely unmarked today (even of mixed or female staff).
  • 'Man' as a generic noun is now contested in formal usage (compare 'chairman' vs 'chairperson', 'man-hours' vs 'person-hours').
  • Fixed phrases: 'man the lifeboats', 'man overboard!', 'to a man' (every one), 'man of letters', 'the man in the street', 'be one's own man'.
