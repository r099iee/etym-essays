- [Classical Greek: c. 5th–3rd c. BCE] - ἔκστασις (ékstasis) - Literally "displacement, removal, dislocation"; used by Hippocrates and Aristotle for mental derangement, terror, amazement, or alienation of mind - [perception: distressing clinical disorder, madness, or staggering shock]
- [Hellenistic & Late Antique Era: c. 2nd–5th c. CE] - ecstasis - Adopted by Neoplatonists (Plotinus) and early Christian theologians to describe the mystical ascent and trance-like communion of the soul with the Divine - [perception: sacred mystical elevation, sublime spiritual contemplation]
- [Late Middle Ages: c. 1380–1500] - extasie - Enters Middle English via Old French (Wycliffe Bible, Chaucer) denoting a swoon, unconscious trance, or prophetic vision - [perception: holy trance or medical swoon, often hovering between life and death]
- [Renaissance & Metaphysical Era: c. 1550–1700] - ecstasy - Poetic usage blossoms; writers like Donne and Shakespeare use it for the madness of love, passionate delight, and spiritual transport - [perception: thrilling emotional and intellectual elevation; romantic passion]
- [Romantic & Victorian Era: c. 1780–1900] - ecstasy - Solidifies as the ultimate aesthetic term for supreme artistic, natural, and romantic rapture (as in Wilde's Nightingale and the Rose) - [perception: exquisite, sublime delight and transcendent beauty]
- [Late 20th–21st Century] - ecstasy - Maintains traditional literary sense of supreme bliss while gaining global notoriety as the street name for MDMA - [perception: dual register: high literary rapture alongside modern electronic dance and pharmacological culture]
