- Old English to Middle English: "slack" (slæc) means loose, lax, careless — born in the world of ropes and rigging, a slack line is one from which all strain has been withdrawn.
- Early Modern period: the adverb "slackly" settles into a mechanical sense — in a loose, limp manner, without tautness or firmness, the body failing to hold itself together.
- 19th-20th centuries: slack spreads figuratively to effort — to slack off, a slacker — while "slackly" itself keeps the rope-and-sail image, describing a jaw that hangs like a slackened line.
- Old English: "glisten" (glisnian) means to glitter or gleam — part of the great Germanic family of light-words sharing the root of gleam, glow, and glitter.
- Middle English onward: the word specializes from general shining to the soft, reflected, sparkling luster of a wet or oily surface — light trembling on moisture.
- Modern: glistened names precisely that shimmer — the sheen of dew, sweat, tears, or freshly wetted skin; distinct from glowing from within or glittering with hard brilliance.
