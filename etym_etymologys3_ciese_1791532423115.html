A loanword older than England. Ciese is a witness to a world before the Anglo-Saxons sailed. The word goes back to Latin cāseus, the everyday Roman word for cheese, and it entered the Germanic languages during the centuries of contact along the Rhine and the North Sea frontier. By the time the Angles, Saxons and Jutes settled Britain, they already called cheese by a Latin name; the word was part of their vocabulary, not a later borrowing from the church or the Norman court.

The Latin background. Latin cāseus is attested in Republican and classical authors and in agricultural writers such as Cato and Columella, who describe the making and pressing of cheese on Roman estates. Its deeper origin is debated; the usual link is to a root *kwat- meaning "to ferment, become sour," which would describe the curdling of milk, but the connection is not certain. What is certain is that the word was productive in Latin and spread with Roman food culture into the provinces and beyond.

The Germanic loan. West Germanic *kāsijaz is the reconstructed form that underlies Old English cēse / cīese, Old Frisian kāse, Old Saxon kāsi, Old High German kāsi and Old Norse kæsir. The uniformity of the loan across the Germanic world shows that it was taken over before the dialects diverged, probably in the first centuries CE. Latin c /k/ was borrowed as Germanic /k/, and the Latin ending was adapted to Germanic inflection.

  • The Latin long ā becomes West Germanic ā, which in Old English fronts to ǣ or, in Anglian, develops differently, giving the variant spellings.
  • The Latin s between vowels remains /z/ in Old English and later becomes /z/ or /s/ depending on the dialect and the word's position.
  • The initial c before a front vowel palatalises in Old English to /tʃ/, the sound still heard in modern cheese.
  • The final -e of cīese is a real Old English vowel and is lost in Middle English, giving chese.

Old English attestations. Old English glossaries regularly gloss Latin caseus with cēse or cīese, and cheese appears in monastic rules, food rents and medical recipes. Ælfric's Colloquy, a Latin textbook for boys, includes cheese among the goods a shepherd can offer, and the word is common enough to have produced compounds and place-names. The two spellings reflect the two great dialect areas: West Saxon cēse and Anglian cīese.

Middle English and the great vowel shift. Middle English chese is the ordinary form, and the long vowel /eː/ was raised to /iː/ in the Great Vowel Shift, giving modern /tʃiːz/. The spelling cheese preserves the Middle English ee. The word's pronunciation is thus a compact record of two sound changes — palatalisation of c and the raising of the long vowel — separated by several centuries.

Cheese in economy and culture. In the medieval and early modern economy cheese was a basic protein, a rent payment, a monastic commodity and a travellers' food. Regional varieties developed with local breeds, soils and techniques: Cheddar in Somerset, Cheshire, Wensleydale, Stilton, Parmesan in Italy, Gruyère in Switzerland, Gouda in the Netherlands. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought industrial production, processed cheese and global trade, and the late twentieth century brought a craft revival and protected designations of origin.

Semantic extensions. The modern word has generated a surprising range of idioms and slang:

  • "Big cheese": an important person, probably from Urdu chīz, "thing," and not from the food.
  • "Cheesy": cheap, sentimental or tasteless; the link to cheese as plain or strong-smelling food is plausible but not documented.
  • "Say cheese": a photographic instruction, chosen for the mouth shape.
  • "Cheesed off": British slang for fed up, of uncertain origin.
  • "Cheese it": nineteenth-century slang for "stop" or "run away."
  • Sports and gaming: a soft shot, a fastball, or a cheap tactic.

Literary and documentary attestations. Cheese appears in Old English glosses and monastic records, in Middle English recipe collections and estates accounts, in Renaissance dietary writing, in Dickensian and Victorian scenes of rural plenty, and in modern food writing. The word is a small index of English social history: a Roman luxury turned medieval staple, an industrial commodity, and now a marker of taste, region and craft.

The word's shape in the card. The card's ciese is the Anglian spelling, and it is a useful reminder that Old English had no single standard orthography. The same word could be written cēse, cīese or ciese depending on the scribe's dialect and training. Modern cheese descends from this cluster of forms, and its history shows how a borrowed food-word can become one of the most familiar and productive items in the language.
