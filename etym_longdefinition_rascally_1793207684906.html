Rascally is the adjective of rascal: behaving like a rascal, or possessing a rascal’s qualities — roguish, mischievous, knavish, and in its older agricultural use, worthless and good-for-nothing. In the source sentence Cobbett rides toward Guildford and finds the land a rascally common covered with poor heath: thin, hungry, scrubby ground where even the heath can barely grow. The common is a rascal in the farmer’s moral sense — idle, unprofitable, refusing honest work — and Cobbett, who judged soil the way a parson judges souls, condemns it with a word usually reserved for scoundrels.

Core meaning in detail: The word carries two temperatures that never quite separate. The warm one is playful: a rascally grin, a rascally boy, a rascally trick — mischief with a wink, the beloved rogue of story-books from Dickens’s Artful Dodger to the cartoon rabbit. The cold one is contemptuous: rascally conduct, a rascally landlord, Cobbett’s rascally common — worthless, shabby, dishonest, the thing or person that lets the side down. Both flow from the noun’s history: a rascal was first the dregs of an army, then any rogue, then — by affectionate softening — the lovable rogue, and the adjective followed the noun through every shade from damnation to doting.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Roguish, mischievously villainous (primary modern sense, from the 16th c.): a rascally trick, a rascally grin, his rascally charm. The bridge is direct: acting as rascals act — cheating at the edges, breaking the small rules — but with enough wit that the victim half forgives it.
  • Worthless, shabby, good-for-nothing (Cobbett’s sense, 16th–19th c.): a rascally common, rascally soil, a rascally inn. The bridge runs from the person to the thing: as a rascal is a man of no worth, so rascally land is ground of no worth — thin, sour, and irredeemable, condemned in the farmer’s court.
  • Dishonest, knavish, unprincipled (stern sense): rascally behaviour, a rascally fraud. The bridge keeps the noun’s criminal edge unsoftened: not playful mischief but genuine roguery, the rascal as villain rather than scamp.
  • Playfully naughty, endearing (affectionate modern shade): you rascally old thing, a rascally twinkle in his eye. The bridge is love’s amnesty: the rogue’s mischief, viewed with fondness, becomes a compliment, and the adjective turns into a caress.
  • Mean, paltry, sorry-looking (transferred to objects): a rascally little pony, a rascally cold supper. The bridge extends worthlessness from land to anything shabby and disappointing — the common’s poverty generalised to every poor thing.

Register and connotation: rascally is genial, old-fashioned, faintly literary English — the vocabulary of story-tellers, columnists, and indignant farmers rather than of courts or contracts. Nobody is charged with rascally conduct in a statute; everybody understands a rascally landlord in a letter to the paper. Cobbett’s agricultural use is now the rarest shade, fossilised in Rural Rides and period novels, while the playful rogue owns the modern word: to call a child rascally today is to praise with one eyebrow raised, half scolding and wholly delighted.
