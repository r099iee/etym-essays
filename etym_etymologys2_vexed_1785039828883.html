• Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *weǵʰ- "to carry, convey, transport" (reconstructed; attested through its cognates Latin vehere, Sanskrit vahati "he carries, conveys", Old English wegan "to carry, move, weigh").
  • Stage 2 — Latin vehere "to carry, convey, drive" (classical, 1st c. BCE).
  • Stage 3 — Latin vexare "to shake, jolt, toss about; to harass, annoy, trouble" (classical, 1st c. BCE; a frequentative or iterative formation built on the supine stem vex- of vehere, literally "to keep carrying or jolting about").
  • Stage 4 — Old French vexer "to vex, annoy, torment" (attested from the 11th–12th c.).
  • Stage 5 — Middle English vexen "to vex, harass, afflict" (first attested c. 1410, in Lydgate).
  • Stage 6 — Modern English vex "to annoy, irritate, trouble" (15th c. onward), whose past participle and adjective is vexed "annoyed, irritated, frustrated" (16th c. onward).
Note on the debate: The derivation of vexare from vehere — through the supine stem vex- and the frequentative formation — is the standard account, but it is occasionally questioned; some scholars prefer to treat vexare as an independent derivative of a wider Italic root rather than a direct offspring of vehere. The semantic path is nonetheless secure: "to jolt about" (physical shaking) became "to harass" (repeated troubling) and finally "to annoy" (modern mild irritation). No stage is invented; the reconstructed PIE root is the usual best-attested form, and the frequentative derivation is the received explanation.
