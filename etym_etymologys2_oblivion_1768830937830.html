PIE *lei- (with s-mobile, *slei-) “slippery, slimy, smooth” (reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE) → Latin linere “to smear, daub, smooth over” (attested c. 2nd c. BCE) — the root in its “smear” branch → Latin oblivisci (deponent verb) “to forget” — traditionally analyzed as ob- “over, against” + *lev-/liv- related to levis “smooth”: literally “to smooth over, to erase, to rub out of the record” (attested c. 2nd c. BCE, e.g. Plautus) [traditional analysis] → Latin oblivio, oblivionis “forgetfulness, the state of being forgotten” (Cicero and the Augustan poets, 1st c. BCE) → Old French oblivion “forgetfulness” (c. 12th c.) → Middle English oblivion “loss of memory, the condition of having forgotten” (14th c.) → English oblivion “state of being completely forgotten; unconsciousness; utter destruction” ◄ TARGET Note on the debated etymology: the derivation of oblivisci from levis “smooth” is the traditional analysis but rests almost entirely on this single word; rival proposals connect the verb to the root *leibh- “to pour” (forgetting as covering or dousing) or to *leu- “to loosen,” and no certain cognates outside Latin have been demonstrated. Modern scholarship therefore regards the Latin formation as uncertain, and the two “smear/smooth” stages marked [traditional analysis] must be read with that caution. The wider root family *lei-/*slei- itself is solid, with descendants including Latin limus “slime,” līma “a file,” and English slime, slide, slip, and lime.
