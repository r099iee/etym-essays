incongruous (adjective) — that which does not “come together” with what surrounds it: out of keeping with the circumstances, the style, the period or the company in which it appears, or inconsistent with itself, its premises, or the claims made for it. The word carries two distinguishable centres of gravity, one logical and one aesthetic, and most uses blend the two: a remark is incongruous when it does not fit the conversation; a building is incongruous when it does not fit the street; a view is incongruous when its parts cannot be reconciled.

Primary sense — out of keeping with surroundings. In Pater’s sentence the word is used with a preposition: an essay “not incongruous with the studies which precede it” — that is, not out of harmony with the preceding chapters. This collocation (incongruous with, less often to) is the standard modern construction: an ornament incongruous with the façade, a modern tower incongruous with the medieval square, behaviour incongruous with the office. The sense is spatial and social at once: the thing belongs to one frame and appears in another, and the frame cries out against the intruder.

  • Secondary sense — logically inconsistent. Two propositions, claims or elements are incongruous when they cannot be thought together without contradiction: a theory incongruous with the evidence; a man whose word is incongruous with his deed. Here the word neighbours incompatible and contradictory, and the semantic bridge to the primary sense is the old geometric metaphor: what does not “run together” (congruere, “to come together, meet, agree”) cannot be laid over itself, like figures that refuse to coincide.
  • Teritary sense — comically mismatched. In modern idiomatic use the word often marks the absurd or the risible: an incongruous detail, an incongruous combination, a speaker in an incongruous outfit. The mismatch provokes laughter or unease precisely because the two elements are each fine alone and absurd together — the semantic ancestor of the modern “mismatch” aesthetic.
  • Register. The word is bookish but common, more formal than “out of place”, less technical than “incompatible”. Its noun incongruity (an incongruity, the incongruity of X) and its family congruent, congruous, congruence, incongruence keep the mathematical sense alive: congruent triangles touch the same root.
  • Historical nuance for this card. Pater’s use is deliberate: he is defending the structural fit of his Winckelmann essay — an eighteenth-century figure inside a book about the Renaissance — and the word is doing the work of the whole argument: the contradiction of dating is only apparent; in spirit the man belongs.

Morphological bridge. The prefix in- negates Latin congruus “agreeing, fit, suitable”, from congruere “to come together, meet, agree” — com- “together” + a verbal base connected with ruere “to rush, fall”: what agrees is what “runs together” and fits; what is incongruous is what cannot run together into a single shape. The English word entered usage in the seventeenth century from the Latin adjective incongruus, and has never lost the double trace of its origin: geometry (coinciding figures) and social proportion (fitting one’s company).
