- 12th to 13th Century - Borrowed into Middle English from Old French *vertu* (derived from Latin *virtus*); predominantly signified miraculous divine power, spiritual efficacy, healing properties of herbs/stones, or heroic martial valor.
- 14th to 15th Century - Solidified in Christian moral theology (Chaucer, Langland) around the theological and cardinal virtues (prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance, faith, hope, charity), denoting disciplined moral excellence.
- 16th Century (Renaissance & Humanism) - Re-interrogated through classical humanism; Niccolò Machiavelli coined political *virtù* (audacity, prowess, decisive energy against Fortune), while English writers adapted it to intellectual and creative capacities.
- 17th to 18th Century (Enlightenment) - Increasingly associated with domestic conduct, social duty, and female chastity or sexual modesty (Richardson, Fielding, Defoe), while natural philosophers retained "virtue" as physical/chemical active power.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) - Became the central moral buzzword of bourgeois respectability, duty, and self-restraint; also crystallized in the proverbial phrase "make a virtue of necessity" and Terence Kilmartin's aphoristic "defect of a virtue."
- 20th to 21st Century - Experienced a philosophical renaissance through "virtue ethics" (Anscombe, MacIntyre, Foot); broadly used in modern English both for moral uprightness and as an objective merit or advantageous property of a system, technology, or argument.
