In Gibbon's account of an earthquake, 'their affrighted imagination enlarged the real extent of a momentary evil.' The word 'affrighted' does exact psychological work: it describes an imagination seized, filled, and possessed by sudden terror, so that fear itself becomes the engine of exaggeration. It is not a momentary start or a quick alarm but a settled state of dread that clouds the faculty of perception — precisely why Gibbon can say that the frightened imagination 'enlarged the real extent' of the disaster. The tone is formal, grave, and distinctly literary: an eighteenth-century historian chooses 'affrighted' over the plainer 'frightened' because the older word carries dignity and weight, and because, as a participial adjective, it describes a condition fully entered into rather than a passing reaction. The fear it names is totalizing: the imagination does not merely feel afraid; it is 'affrighted,' as a field might be filled with a sudden light. CORE SENSE AND DEFINITION:
  • Primary sense: frightened, alarmed; seized or struck with sudden fear or dread; terrified.
  • Force of the word: affrighted is stronger and more complete than 'frightened.' The intensive prefix a- (Old English ā-, from Proto-Germanic *uz-, 'out, utterly, completely') means the fear has been laid upon the whole person — not merely triggered but poured in full. An affrighted mind is one filled, possessed, and overwhelmed by terror.
  • Register: formal, archaic, and literary. The word belongs to elevated prose, poetry, and historical narrative; in modern speech it is rare and sounds deliberately old-fashioned, Gothic, or mock-heroic.
FULL POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES:
  • Participial adjective (the dominant use, as in Gibbon): 'affrighted' modifies persons, and by extension their faculties and imaginations — 'their affrighted imagination,' 'the affrighted crowd,' 'affrighted eyes.' It describes a condition fully entered into, a settled state of terror, rather than a passing reflex.
  • Past-participial use within a passive construction: 'they were affrighted by the sudden noise' — the verb 'to affright' (to frighten violently, to strike with sudden and great fear) in its participle form. This is the construction underlying the adjective.
  • Attributive noun-adjacent use: 'the affrighted horse bolted' — applied to animals that start and panic, as well as to humans; here the word emphasizes the sudden, uncontrollable, total nature of the fear.
  • Noun use of the simplex 'affright' (rare, 16th–19th c.): 'affright' as a noun meaning 'terror, dread, sudden fear' — 'in great affright.' The participial adjective is far commoner than this noun, but the two travel together in the older registers.
  • Intensified and reflexive constructions: 'affrighted at,' 'affrighted by,' 'affrighted with' — the word takes the full range of prepositional complements of fear.
HISTORICAL SEMANTIC BRIDGE (how the senses connect): The word is transparently built: a- (intensive) + fright (the noun of sudden fear) + -ed (past-participial suffix). The bridge from 'frightened' to 'affrighted' is the prefix. Plain 'frightened' suggests a moderate scare, recoverable and momentary; 'affrighted' names a fear laid on with full force and completeness — the person is not merely startled but utterly overcome, so that the state persists and governs the whole imagination. This is exactly the difference Gibbon exploits: their imagination is not momentarily alarmed but affrighted, and because the fear is total it enlarges the extent of the evil it perceives. Every secondary use — attributive, passive, noun, animal — preserves this core of complete, involuntary, overwhelming terror. CONTEXTUAL READING (Gibbon's sentence in full critical detail): The sentence comes from Gibbon's account of a great earthquake and its aftermath. The people of the shaken cities, their minds disordered by the disaster, magnified the damage they had suffered: 'their affrighted imagination enlarged the real extent of a momentary evil.' The word 'affrighted' is chosen with precision. A momentary evil — the earthquake itself, brief in its violence — is enlarged by the imagination that perceives it, and the engine of that enlargement is fear. Because the imagination is 'affrighted' — filled, possessed, and clouded by complete terror — it cannot measure; it multiplies. The adverb-phrase relationship matters: the affrighted imagination does not simply react but actively constructs a larger catastrophe than occurred. Gibbon's sentence is a miniature treatise on the psychology of panic, and the archaic, dignified word 'affrighted' gives the observation its gravity: this is not the mere 'fright' of the crowd but the total possession of the mind by dread, the very condition in which reason abdicates and exaggeration begins. COLLOCATION AND SYNTACTIC BEHAVIOUR:
  • Typical pattern: 'affrighted + noun' (the affrighted crowd, the affrighted horse, their affrighted imagination) — the participial adjective modifying a person or, significantly, a faculty.
  • Predicative pattern: 'to be affrighted' — 'they stood affrighted,' 'she was affrighted at the sight,' 'affrighted by the storm.'
  • Prepositional complements: chiefly 'at,' 'by,' and 'with' — 'affrighted at the apparition,' 'affrighted by the noise.'
  • Degree adverbs: 'greatly affrighted,' 'sorely affrighted,' 'utterly affrighted' — the collocation 'sorely affrighted' is a stock phrase of older prose and verse.
  • The word is chiefly poetic and archaic in modern use, appearing in historical fiction, Gothic and mock-Gothic prose, and verse; 'frightened' and 'terrified' have displaced it in ordinary speech.
RELATION TO THE REST OF THE WORD FAMILY: Affrighted belongs entirely to the Germanic word family of 'fright.' The base noun 'fright' (Old English fyrhtu, 'fear, trembling, dread') descends from the Proto-Germanic root *furht-, 'fear,' which appears across the Germanic world: Old Saxon forhta, Old High German for(a)hta (whence modern German Furcht, 'fear,' with fürchten, 'to fear'), and Gothic faurhtei. English developed the simplex verb fyrhtan, 'to frighten,' and then strengthened it with the intensive prefix ā- (from Proto-Germanic *uz-), giving āfyrhtan, 'to terrify utterly.' This intensified verb produced Middle English afrighten/affrighten and Early Modern English 'affright,' and the past participle 'affrighted' is its direct descendant. A deeper PIE ancestor *preḱ- 'to fear' has been proposed on the strength of Avestan pərəsaiti, but the link is disputed, and the word's attested history is entirely Germanic.
