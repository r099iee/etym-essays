- Old English to Middle English “bound with cordage” - rāp was purely the physical fibrous cord; the verb ropen meant tying, fastening, or securing physical objects and captives.
- 1840s–1850s American frontier “lassoing cattle” - western ranching established the specific verbal use of roping calves, steers, and wild horses with a lariat, fixing the image of capture at a distance.
- 1850s–1880s American criminal slang “swindling marks” - confidence tricksters, gambling dens, and saloon shills adopted “roper-in” and “roped in” to describe luring naive greenhorns into rigged bets.
- Late 19th c.–early 20th c. transatlantic bleaching “social cajolery” - the phrase was adopted into British English literature (popularized by writers like P. G. Wodehouse) as a humorous, harmless idiom for being cajoled into domestic favors or parlor games.
- Mid-20th c.–present “universal everyday idiom” - widely used across all English-speaking countries in corporate, family, and volunteer contexts for any reluctant recruitment, completely detached from both cattle and crime.
