THE EVOLUTION OF STRAITS 1. Two meanings, one root. The English noun straits has two lives: a geographic life (narrow passages of water connecting two larger bodies) and a figurative life (difficulties, distress, "dire straits"). Both lives descend from a single image — being drawn tight, squeezed, narrowed — and the whole family goes back to the Latin adjective strictus "drawn tight, narrow," the participle of the verb stringere "to draw tight, to bind, to compress." The deep etymology reaches a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root variously cited as *streyg- or *streig-, "to be tight, to bind, to press," the same root that lies behind Latin stringere and, in Germanic, the family of English strangle (through Greek strangalē, a thong) and related "tight" vocabulary. The root's core meaning is physical constriction: pressing together, tightening a cord or a grasp. 2. Latin: from "to bind" to "narrow." In Latin, stringere meant "to draw tight, to bind, to compress," and also "to graze, to touch lightly" (the senses of "stroke" and "constrict" coexisting in one verb). Its participle strictus developed the adjectives "tight, narrow, compressed, strict" — the sense "strict" in English (as in strict rules) is itself a Latin inheritance of "drawn tight, unbending." By the classical period strictus was a fully ordinary adjective meaning "narrow, tight," and it is this strictus, not stringere itself, that lies directly behind the French and English words. The semantic chain is: to bind → to draw tight → to be narrow. A "strait" is thus, at the root, a "narrow," the same idea as the English word narrows. 3. Old French: estreit and the narrowing of the word. With the fall of the Roman empire, spoken Latin evolved into the Romance languages, and in Gallo-Romance strictus became Old French estreit (also estroit; modern French étroit "narrow"). The change was regular: the Latin initial cluster st- was simplified or accommodated to French phonotactics with a supporting vowel (estroit), and the internal -ct- cluster became -it- through palatalization and assimilation — the same sound change that gave fait from factum and droit from directum. Old French estreit meant "narrow, tight, strict," and it passed into English after the Norman Conquest (1066), when the language of the English court and law became French. The English word strait is a direct loan from Anglo-Norman/Old French estreit. 4. Middle English: streit, adjective and noun. In Middle English (12th–15th centuries), streit (later strait, under the influence of Latin spelling strictus) meant "narrow, tight, strict, close." It was used of clothing that was tight, of a strict or rigorous life (strait living), and of a narrow space. From the adjective the noun was formed by conversion — the ordinary process by which English nouns "a narrow" yield place-names and geographic terms. By the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Middle English strait (noun) meant "a narrow passage of water," and the plural straits appears in the same period, applied to the great maritime choke-points of the medieval world — the Straits of Gibraltar, the Straits of Messina. The plural was favoured for single channels by the same idiom that says "the narrows": a strait is perceived as a set of constricted passages between two seas. 5. The figurative turn: from narrow water to tight circumstances. The step from geography to distress is a metaphor as old as the word. Already in Middle English, strait could mean "a narrow, awkward position; difficulty," for a person "in a strait" is squeezed into a tight place with no room to manoeuvre — the same image as "in a tight spot" or "between a rock and a hard place." By the early modern period the plural straits had hardened into the fixed idiom "in straits," and by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries "dire straits" and "desperate straits" were standard. The evolution is perfectly parallel to the physical sense: water forced through a narrow channel is "in straits," and so is a person reduced to difficulty. The adjective survived alongside the noun in compounds: strait-laced (tightly laced, hence morally rigid), straitjacket (a garment that binds), and straitened (reduced to poverty, "straitened circumstances") — all built on the same root image of being drawn tight. 6. Geography and geopolitics: the straits of the world. In the age of sail and steam, the straits of the world became the chokepoints of empire: Gibraltar, Malacca, Hormuz, Magellan, Dover, and the Bosphorus. "The Straits" came to be used absolutely for the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles (the Turkish Straits), the strategic waterway of the Eastern Question in nineteenth-century diplomacy. Gibbon's sentence on this card uses "the Straits of Scylla" for the Strait of Messina, the myth-haunted passage of Homer's Scylla and Charybdis — showing the geographic sense at work in its most literary register. In modern hydrography and international law (the law of the sea), straits are defined with great precision: waters between landmasses connecting two larger bodies, through which passage may be regulated — the word is now as much a term of geopolitics as of geography. 7. Popular culture: the plural in the modern ear. In 1977 a British rock band took the name Dire Straits, punning on the idiom "dire straits" (severe difficulty). The band's fame — hits like "Sultans of Swing" and "Money for Nothing" — gave the plural straits a second, entirely modern life in popular consciousness: for millions of listeners the word now means a band as much as a waterway or a predicament. This is a characteristic twentieth-century development: a centuries-old idiom re-entering the language as a proper name, showing how even the most learned vocabulary can be refreshed by popular culture. 8. Sound and spelling across the centuries.
  • PIE *streyg-/*streig-: the root's exact form is reconstructed and debated; the vocalism and the optional -g- extension are not directly attested.
  • Latin stringere → strictus: the participle strictus yields the "narrow" sense; Latin st- was kept.
  • Old French estreit: prothetic e- (estroit), -ct- → -it-; modern French étroit.
  • Middle English streit → strait: English re-spelled the word on the model of Latin strictus, restoring the -ai- spelling.
  • Plural straits: final -ts pronounced /ts/; RP /stɹˈeɪts/.
9. Summary.
  • Meaning: "to bind/tighten" (PIE *streyg-) → "narrow, tight" (Latin strictus) → "narrow waterway" (ME strait) → "straits" the plural geographic term and the figurative "difficulties."
  • Sound: a chain of regular changes from Latin to French to English, plus a learned re-spelling that restored the Latin look of the word.
  • Register: technical in hydrography and law; idiomatic and emphatic in the figurative sense; modern popular culture has made the plural famous as a band name.
  • Core insight: a single image — being drawn tight — generates both the world's shipping choke-points and the tight corners of human misfortune.
The word straits thus illustrates the remarkable economy of etymology: one picture of constriction, drawn once in the prehistory of the language, still names both the narrows of the earth and the narrowness of hard times.
