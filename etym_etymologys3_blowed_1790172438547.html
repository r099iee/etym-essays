blowed as oath belongs to the oldest English verb for moving air: Old English blawan, to blow, breathe, sound wind, from Proto-Germanic *blaen, to blow, breathe, inflate, from Proto-Indo-European *bhel-, to swell, blow up, inflate, the same swelling root that gives bladder, blister, blast, blowzy, bloat. In Old and Middle English the participle varied between strong blowen / blown and weak blowed, as many verbs wavered before standardisation. London printing and eighteenth-century grammars fixed literary usage as blow, blew, blown, leaving blowed to dialects, forecastles, stables, and nurseries — exactly the soil where oaths grow.

The oath itself grows from sea wind. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century sailors swear blow me down, blow me tight, blow me high and dry: may the wind knock me flat if I lie. The image is literal aboard ship, where wind does fell men. Ashore the formula shortens to blow me and passivises to I’m blowed, I’m knocked over by surprise. By 1800 blow is recognised cant for damn in the sense of explosive expulsion of breath: to blow is to damn politely, as bless is to damn ironically. Grose’s Classical Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue (1785–1811) already lists blow me among fashionable expletives. Pronunciation is regular /bləʊd/, rhyming with flowed, preserving the long-o before -ed while standard blown keeps -n.

  • Nineteenth-century triumph: Dickens, Marryat, and music-hall make I’m blowed the respectable working man’s damn. Sam Weller, cabmen, beadles, and Sairey Gamp say I’m blowed if where a gentleman would say damned if and a lady I declare. The phrase is strong enough to carry feeling across the barrack-room and kitchen, clean enough for the parlour and the circulating library. Grammarians note blowed as vulgar for blown while novelists treasure it as character paint.
  • Wodehouse distribution: in Bertie Wooster’s 1920s–1930s London, blowed is subtly classed below dashed. Bertie himself prefers dashed, deuced, what ho; Jeeves never swears; aunts, policemen, bookmakers, and surprised cronies say I’m blowed. Putting Well, I’m blowed as a whole freestanding card — bare astonishment with no object — lets the oath do maximum work: something just seen or heard has knocked the speaker flat, and breath stands in for blasphemy.
  • Modern afterlife: after the Second World War bloody, bugger, fuck push blowed into archaism. It survives as deliberate period pastiche, in C. S. Lewis’ Narnia, Tolkien’s Gaffer, and heritage television, where saying I’m blowed instantly dates a voice to gaslight and empire. Linguistically it is a fossil: a weak participle preserved inside an idiom after the paradigm moved on, like learnt in book-learnt or dreamt in poetry.

To fill this card is thus to gloss not a thing but a breath: blowed names air weaponised as politeness. From PIE swelling to Germanic blowing to sailor’s curse to valet’s surprise, the thread is inflation — lungs, sails, bladders, tempers swollen till they burst into speech. Well, I’m blowed says: this news has taken my breath, and I spend it here, cleanly, before company.
