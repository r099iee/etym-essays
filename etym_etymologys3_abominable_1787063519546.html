The etymological biography of the adjective abominable provides a profound window into the ritualistic mindset of ancient Roman religion, the theological rigor of medieval Christendom, and a celebrated Renaissance linguistic misconception that permanently enriched English literature.

The word originates in Classical Latin as a compound formed from the prepositional prefix ab- ("away from, off") and the nominal stem of ōmen (genitive ōminis, archaic Old Latin osmen, signifying a "prophetic sign, portent, or solemn augury"). In ancient Roman augury and religious practice, when an observer witnessed a sinister, malignant, or catastrophic sign from the gods (such as a monstrous birth, lightning striking a temple, or a vulture shrieking on the left), the observer was required to perform a ritual of deprecation: abōminārī (literally "to avert an ill omen by prayer, to turn away in religious horror, to abhor as ill-boding").

From this deponent verb, Late Latin derived the verbal adjective abōminābilis ("worthy of being deprecated as accursed, utterly detestable, abhorrent"). In the 4th-century Latin Vulgate Bible translated by Saint Jerome, abōminābilis and the corresponding noun abōminātiō were chosen to translate the Hebrew biblical concepts of to'evah (תּוֹעֵבָה, ritual impurity, idolatry, pagan abominations) and piggul (stale sacrificial meat, unclean things). Thus, the word transitioned from pagan Roman divination to Christian moral theology, signifying anything abhorrent to the holiness of God.

Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, the term entered Anglo-Norman and Old French as abominable, and was subsequently borrowed into Middle English during the 14th century. Early attestations appear in Geoffrey Chaucer's The Parson's Tale (c. 1390) and the Wycliffite Bible, where it denoted horrific sins such as blasphemy, heresy, and sodomy.

During the 15th and 16th centuries, a remarkable phenomenon of folk etymology (pseudo-etymology) took hold of European humanistic scholarship. Renaissance scholars and scribes mistakenly believed that the Latin word was derived not from ab-ōmen, but from ab homine (literally "away from man" or "contrary to human nature, inhuman, beastly"). Under this false assumption, scribes deliberately altered the spelling across Middle French and Early Modern English to abhominable (with an intrusive -h-), asserting that an abhominable act was one so monstrous that it severed a creature from the human race.

This pedantic spelling abhominable dominated 16th-century English literature and was used extensively in the 1623 First Folio of William Shakespeare. In fact, Shakespeare famously satirized this very pedantry in Love's Labour's Lost (Act V, Scene 1), where the pedantic schoolmaster Holofernes rails against colloquial pronunciations: "This is abhominable,—which he would call abbominable: it insinuateth me of insanie." By the late 17th century, classical scholarship corrected the error, and the standard spelling was restored to abominable.

In Modern English, abominable expanded into Gothic horror (John Milton's Paradise Lost, Mary Shelley's Frankenstein), dark fantasy (J.R.R. Tolkien's description of Shelob's and Mirkwood spiders' webs), and 20th-century cryptozoology via the "Abominable Snowman."
