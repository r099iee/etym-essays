The verb endue (also frequently spelled indue) is an elevated, literary, and theological term meaning to endow, furnish, or invest someone with spiritual gifts, moral virtues, intellectual faculties, divine grace, or natural qualities. In traditional religious literature and English liturgical history, it represents one of the most sacred and solemn expressions of divine empowerment, describing an inward investiture of celestial power or spiritual authority bestowed from on high.

The verb possesses an intricate network of theological, poetic, archaic physical, and physiological senses, shaped by centuries of historical lexical convergence:

  • Theological and Liturgical Investiture (Divine Endowment): In sacred Christian scripture and Anglican liturgical tradition, endue denotes the bestowal of the Holy Spirit, righteousness, or moral strength upon human believers or consecrated ministers. The definitive biblical attestation occurs in the Authorized King James Bible (1611) in the Gospel of Luke (24:49), where the risen Christ commands his disciples: "And, behold, I send the promise of my Father upon you: but tarry ye in the city of Jerusalem, until ye be endued with power from on high." Similarly, in the Book of Common Prayer (1549/1552/1662), the word is woven into the daily suffrages and litanies of the church: "Endue thy ministers with righteousness; And make thy chosen people joyful," and in prayers for the sovereign: "Most heartily we beseech thee with thy favour to behold our most gracious Sovereign... endue her plenteously with heavenly gifts." In these contexts, endue is distinctly non-material; it signifies an inward equipping of the soul with transcendent moral and spiritual fortitude.
  • Elevated Poetic and Philosophical Endowment: Throughout English Renaissance and Romantic poetry, endue was employed by master poets to describe how nature, providence, or the Creator equipped mankind with reason, speech, emotional sensitivity, or artistic genius. In John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667, Book IV), Milton describes the complementary perfection of Adam and Eve: "For contemplation hee and valour formd, / For softness shee and sweet attractive Grace, / ... Both of one Form, though not alike endued." Here, to be endued means to be constitutionally furnished with specific human faculties and moral dispositions.
  • Archaic Sense of Clothing, Donning, or Embodiment: Drawing on its historical association with Latin induere ("to put on clothing"), endue historically meant to dress, clothe, or array oneself physically in garments, vestments, or armor. In mythological and metaphysical poetry, it was used to describe a spiritual entity, deity, or disembodied soul assuming human flesh or adopting a visible physical form (e.g., "the angel endued a mortal shape to deliver the divine decree").
  • Obsolete Falconry and Physiological Sense (Digestion): In late medieval and early modern English medicine, hunting, and falconry, endue (sometimes written endew) developed a specialized physiological meaning: of a hawk or falcon, to digest food completely, breaking it down in the gorge or crop and passing it into the lower stomach or intestines. By extension, early natural philosophers applied it metaphorically to the human body digesting nourishment or assimilating humors into physical tissue.
  • Semantic Distinction from "Endow" and Modern Register:
    • Endue vs. Endow: Although endue and endow are historically intertwined and often considered near-doublets, they maintain distinct semantic fields in educated modern English. Endow (from Latin dotare, "to provide with a dowry") is used primarily in secular, material, institutional, legal, and financial contexts (such as endowing an academic chair, a hospital, or a trust fund with revenue, or being endowed with genetic athletic ability). Conversely, endue is reserved for sacred, moral, spiritual, or elevated poetic realms, describing the investment of a spirit, virtue, or power.
    • Spelling Variants (Endue vs. Indue): Modern dictionaries treat endue and indue as etymological and orthographic variants. The form with en- reflects the Old French vocalization, whereas in- reflects Latinate classical restoration. In theological texts, endue remains the preferred historical spelling due to the enduring authority of the King James Bible and the Book of Common Prayer.
