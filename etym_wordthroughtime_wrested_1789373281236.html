- Old English (c. 8th–11th Century) - Attested as the weak verb *wrǣstan*, meaning to twist violently, tighten, bind, or tune musical instruments with twisting pegs.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th Century) - Develops the physical sense of wrenching or pulling an object or weapon away from someone's grasp by rotational torque (*wresten*), while retaining musical tuning senses.
- Early Modern English (16th–17th Century) - Enters widespread legal, religious, and political prose; famously immortalized in Tudor and Jacobean biblical translations to describe the willful twisting and contorting of scripture or legal statutes to suit heretical arguments ("wresting the law").
- 18th to 19th Century - Flourishes in romantic historiography and political journalism to characterize heroic or revolutionary struggles where sovereignty, provinces, or constitutional liberties were wrested from tyrants or hostile foreign powers.
- 20th Century to Present - Established as a sophisticated literary and critical verb characterizing the arduous extraction of hard-won concessions, physical survival against harsh landscapes, or the radical reorganization of narrative components out of linear sequence to serve overarching artistic designs.
