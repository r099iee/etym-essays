FROM SWOLLEN CUSHION TO MORALE SUPPORT

Bolster is a member of one of the oldest word-families in the Germanic languages, the great "swell" family that also gave English belly, bellows, billow, bulge, and bellow. The noun bolster is attested in Old English from the eighth century with the sense "a long, padded cushion or pillow," and it descends from the reconstructed Proto-Germanic noun *bulstraz "cushion, bolster." The name is transparently a description of what the thing is: a bolster is a "swollen thing," a stuffed, bulging pad, formed from a root meaning "to swell." The family is large and vigorous across the whole Germanic world — Old Norse bolstr, Old High German bolstar, Middle Dutch bolster — and it is this ancient, homely object-word that would go on to give English one of its most used verbs of support.

In Old English and Middle English the word is purely a noun for a long cushion. It is the bolster that supports a head in a medieval bed, the padded support laid across a bench or chair, and, importantly for later developments, the soft pad used in various trades to protect or cushion a surface. The noun is a very concrete, physical word — you can picture the long stuffed roll lying across the bed-frame at the head of the mattress, or behind a sleeper's back. The verb does not exist in Old English. The transformation of bolster from noun to verb begins in the sixteenth century, when the word starts to be used for the action of propping up or supporting with a bolster, and then, swiftly and profitably, for any act of propping up, supporting, or keeping something from falling.

  • Noun-to-verb conversion: the 1500s see the birth of the verb, and within a generation it is being used figuratively. A fragile cause, a wavering argument, a weak case in law — all can be "bolstered," that is, propped up from beneath so they do not collapse. The conversion is a classic case of functional shift, and it is precisely the pillow image that makes it vivid: the bolster is placed under something to stop it sagging or falling.
  • Figurative expansion: in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "to bolster up" becomes a common phrase for shoring up arguments, reputations, authority, and institutions. The collocation is everywhere in political and religious controversy — pamphleteers "bolster up" their doctrines, politicians "bolster up" their power. The word carries a faint suggestion of artificial, external support: to bolster is to keep something upright from outside, as a cushion props up a heavy head. The playwrights and essayists of the Restoration and Augustan age use it constantly in the language of controversy.
  • Technical senses: while the figurative uses bloom, bolster also multiplies in the workshops and structures of English life. In masonry and joinery a "bolster" is a horizontal timber or steel plate set atop a post or wall to spread the load and support a beam or girder — a literal architectural prop that repeats the cushion's job at the scale of a building. In cutlery the "bolster" is the thick metal junction between a knife blade and its handle, providing weight, balance, and a handguard. In railways a "bolster" is the cross-member on a bogie or carriage that carries the body of the vehicle. In armour the "bolster" is the padding worn under mail; in glassmaking it is a support for a blow-pipe; in tanning it is a blunt knife; in printing and metalwork it is a bed or pad on which work rests. Every technical sense preserves the same idea: a strong thing set under or behind another thing to support it.
  • Modern abstract use: in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries bolster becomes the standard verb for boosting confidence, morale, self-esteem, arguments, and economies. "Bolster confidence," "bolster the economy," "bolster the case for reform," "bolster the troops' morale" are routine in journalism and policy. The modern word has largely lost the old suggestion of artificial padding and now simply means "to strengthen, reinforce, or encourage" — a noun that meant "swollen cushion" is now used to describe everything from self-confidence to national defence.

The evolution of bolster is thus a study in the power of a simple concrete image. An ancient Germanic word for a stuffed pillow, born of a root meaning "to swell," moved from the bed to the workshop, the building site, the armoury, and the knife-maker's bench, and finally into the most abstract reaches of modern language, where we bolster ideas, spirits, markets, and arguments. At every step the image has been the same: something placed beneath to hold something up. The cushion never left the language — it simply grew to support the whole weight of modern abstraction. And because the word retains its physical honesty, it remains one of the few support-verbs in English that a stonemason and a minister of finance can use with equal precision.
