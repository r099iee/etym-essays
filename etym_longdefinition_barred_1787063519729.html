Barred is the past participle of the verb bar, and its meanings divide into three families: (1) fastened or secured with bars — a "barred window" is fitted with iron bars; (2) blocked, obstructed, or formally excluded — "barred from entering," "the road was barred"; (3) marked with bars, i.e. crossed by bands or stripes of colour or light — "a pale sky barred with black." It is this third, pictorial sense that appears in the sentence "The stars were coming out behind him in a pale sky barred with black."

  • Primary sense — fitted with bars: "a barred window," "a barred gate," "a barred cell." The noun "bar" (a rod, pole, or beam) yields the verb "to bar" = to fasten with a bar; the participle describes anything so fastened, with the physical image of rods across an opening.
  • Blocked or obstructed: "to bar the way" — to place a barrier across; "the entrance was barred"; "the road was barred to traffic." From physical blockage the word extends naturally to metaphorical obstruction.
  • Excluded or forbidden: "to be barred from a profession, from a country, from a competition," "he was barred from entering the country." Legal and formal uses: "the defendant is barred from re-litigating the claim"; "time-barred" or "statute-barred" — a claim that can no longer be brought because the statutory period has passed; "the bar" (the legal profession) — a lawyer is "called to the bar," and a "disbarred" lawyer is expelled from the profession.
  • Marked with bars — the striped sense: "a barred sky," "barred plumage," "a barred pattern." In heraldry and natural description, "barred" means crossed with transverse bands or stripes; the barred owl (Strix varia) is named for the bars on its breast feathers; in astronomy the "barred spiral galaxy" (a 20th-century term) is a spiral galaxy whose arms spring from a central bar of stars.
  • Textiles and design: "barred" fabrics, wallpapers, and painted surfaces carry horizontal or vertical bands; "barring" is the process of marking with bars or of fitting with bars.
  • Verb nuances — to bar: to fasten, obstruct, exclude, or forbid; also (in heraldry) to divide a field by bars. The participle "barred" therefore ranges over all these senses according to context, and only context tells whether a "barred" object is secured, forbidden, or striped.
  • Figurative uses: "barred" appears in legal, medical, and institutional idioms — "barred from practice," "barred from the ballot," "time-barred" — always preserving the image of a barrier placed across the way.

Semantic bridge: all senses flow from the noun "bar" (a rod or barrier). A window fitted with rods is "barred"; a gate with a bar across it is "barred" (hence blocked); an excluded person is treated as though a bar had been placed in their way (hence forbidden); and a surface crossed by rods of colour or shadow is "barred" — the stripes themselves are bars of light. In Tolkien's sentence the sky is barred: pale bands are cut by bars of black cloud, so that it is striped like a ruled or barred surface, and the bars of darkness framing the emerging stars carry a quiet sense of confinement and danger befitting the hobbit's fearful venture.
