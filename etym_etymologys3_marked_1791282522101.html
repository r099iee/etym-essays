Marked is the past-participle/adjective of mark, whose lineage runs from Proto-Indo-European through Proto-Germanic and Old English with exceptional continuity of both form and meaning around the idea of boundary, sign, and trace.

PIE origin. The ultimate root is PIE *merg- (also written *merǵ-) "edge, boundary, border, mark." Reflexes abound: Latin margo "margin, edge," Sanskrit maryādā "boundary," Old Irish mruig "borderland," and Germanic *markō. The semantic kernel is division made visible: a line that separates and thereby signifies. Phonologically PIE voiced aspirate *ĝh became Germanic *k by Grimm's Law, and syllabic *r vocalised to ar/ur.

Proto-Germanic. PGmc *markō (feminine noun) meant "boundary, borderland, district" — hence English march (border region, as in the Welsh Marches) and marchioness — and by extension "sign set on a boundary, token, imprint." The verb *markōjaną "to mark, to stamp" and noun *marką "mark, print" derive from the same stem. The form is securely attested across Gothic marka, Old Norse mǫrk, Old High German marka.

  • Old English: OE mearc (noun, feminine) "mark, sign, boundary, target" and verb mearcian "to mark, distinguish, observe." Pronounced roughly /mæark/, with breaking of a to ea before r + consonant. Already both concrete (a stain, a coin called a mark) and abstract (a distinguishing trait).
  • Middle English: ME marke (noun/verb) after Norman Conquest, with vowel levelling and loss of inflections. The boundary sense survives in march while the sign sense becomes dominant for mark. Past participle marked, ymarked is common by the 14th century in Chaucer and Wycliffe: "a marked man," "marked with blood."
  • Early Modern English: 16th–17th c. spelling stabilises as mark/marked; the Great Vowel Shift raises the vowel to modern /mɑːk/. Shakespeare uses participial marked both literally ("marked with tragedy") and figuratively ("a marked proselyte"). The "noticeable, conspicuous" adjectival sense — the one in Wodehouse — is fully established by the 18th century.
  • Modern extensions: 19th–20th c. idioms (marked man, marked cards, marked improvement) and 20th c. linguistic theory (markedness in Prague School phonology, Jakobson/Trubetzkoy) generalise the old boundary/sign opposition into a universal principle: every system has a default (unmarked) and a salient deviation (marked). The waistcoat put on "in a marked manner" is thus the latest descendant of a Bronze-Age border stone: a visible line drawn around behaviour.

Unlike many Germanic words reshaped by French, mark46881078 was never displaced; French marque is itself a Germanic loan back into Romance, proving the root's tenacity. Its spelling, sound, and core sense have therefore moved in a straight, well-documented line for over two thousand years.
