Root 1 — HAY
  • Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *kewh₂- (also *kew-) "to hew, cut, strike, smite" (reconstructed).
  • Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hawwaną "to hew, cut down" (reconstructed; Old English heawan, Modern English hew).
  • Stage 3 — Proto-Germanic *hawją "hay; that which is cut" (reconstructed; literally "the cut thing").
  • Stage 4 — Old English hēg / hīeg "hay, mown grass" (attested 8th c.).
  • Stage 5 — Middle English hey / hai "hay" (attested 13th c.).
  • Stage 6 — Modern English hay "grass cut and dried for fodder" — the first element of haymaking.
Root 2 — MAKE (in -making)
  • Stage 1 — (Ultimate origin uncertain; a traditional but unproven link to PIE *mag- "to knead, shape, fashion" is often proposed — see note).
  • Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *makōną "to make, do, work, build" (reconstructed; attested through Gothic gamaikōn "to do, make", Old Saxon makōn, Old High German mahhōn, Old Norse maka).
  • Stage 3 — Old English macian "to make, construct, form, cause" (attested 9th c.).
  • Stage 4 — Middle English maken / make "to make" (attested 12th c.).
  • Stage 5 — Modern English make "to create, construct, produce" (15th c. onward), with the native gerund making formed within English.
  • Stage 6 — Compound: hay + making — Modern English haymaking "the process of making hay" (attested 16th c., the age of the proverb "make hay while the sun shines").
Note on the debate: The further origin of Proto-Germanic *makōną is genuinely uncertain. The traditional view links it to PIE *mag- "to knead, fashion," on the analogy that early making was the plastic, hand-familiar work of kneading dough or moulding clay (Greek mágis "kneading-trough", mágeiros "cook" are cited as cousins). The theory is possible but unproven; the OED marks the further etymology as uncertain, so that stage is stated here as a debated possibility, not as fact.
