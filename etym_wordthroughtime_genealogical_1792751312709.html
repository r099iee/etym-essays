- [Hellenistic Greek / 3rd c. BCE–1st c. CE] — Coined as genealogikós (adjective to genealogía), describing the mytho-historical enumeration of heroic bloodlines, divine parentage, and royal genealogies (as seen in Hesiodic catalogues and historical chronicles of Polybius and Diodorus Siculus) — [perception: celebrated as the primary ideological tool for legitimizing royal dynasties and divine descent]
- [Late Antiquity & Medieval Era / 4th–14th c.] — Preserved through Latin genealogicus by Christian scholars (e.g., Jerome, Isidore of Seville) primarily to decipher and harmonize the biblical lineages of the Old Testament patriarchs and the Messianic descent of Jesus — [perception: revered as sacred theological scripture and mystical lineage demonstration]
- [Early Modern English / Late 16th–17th c.] — Borrowed into English during the Tudor and Stuart eras, becoming an essential instrument of the College of Arms for aristocrats and rising gentry eager to validate property titles, coat armor, and social precedence — [perception: viewed as a mark of gentility, dynastic pride, and legal title to land]
- [Victorian Era / 19th c.] — Popularized among the middle classes through parish register indexing, antiquarian societies, and literary parody (notably Tolkien's affectionate portrayal of Hobbits obsessed with sprawling family trees and endless cousinhoods) — [perception: understood as an obsessive domestic hobby and a marker of bourgeois respectability]
- [20th–21st c. / Contemporary Digital & Genomic Era] — Revolutionized by computerized census databases and consumer direct-to-consumer DNA sequencing (autosomal, Y-DNA, mtDNA), transforming into one of the most widespread internet-based personal pursuits globally — [perception: embraced as a deeply democratic, scientific quest for personal identity, ethnic roots, and biological connection]
