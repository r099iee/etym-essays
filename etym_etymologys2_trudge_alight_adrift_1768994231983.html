=== TRUDGE === (Chain for the first root word, TRUDGE.) Stage 1 — Early-modern Scots / northern English trud, trudge "to walk slowly and heavily" (first attested c. 1540–50 in Scottish and northern sources). NO PIE root and no Germanic etymology is securely established: the OED marks trudge "origin unknown; perhaps imitative," and the word belongs to the expressive, sound-symbolic stratum — the blunt tr- onset and the heavy -udge rime perform the meaning of feet planted in mud. Stage 2 — English trudge (v.) "to walk slowly and with heavy steps, to plod," and the noun trudge "a weary, heavy walk" (modern). Dialect variants trud, truddle, trudger, trodge are expressive reshapings of the same heavy-walk gesture. NOTE ON DEBATE: Because the word has no reconstructed ancestor, nothing has been merged or skipped — the honest chain is simply "expressive coinage in the 16th c. → modern English," and any deeper source is unknown rather than guessed. === ALIGHT === (Chain for the second root word, ALIGHT, in its shining sense.) Stage 1 — PIE *leuk- "light, brightness; to shine" (reconstructed; one of the great pan-Indo-European light-roots, attested in nearly every branch: Vedic ruc- "to shine," Greek leukós "white," Latin lux/lumen, Lithuanian liepsna "flame"). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *leuht- "light" (reconstructed); Old English lēoht "light" (n.) "illumination, brightness" and the verb līhtan "to light, to kindle" (attested from the 8th c.). Stage 3 — Old English / Middle English ā- + līhtan "to light up, to kindle," and the adjectival formation a- "on" + light "lit" → alight "lit up, ablaze, shining" (Middle English alighten "to kindle, set alight"; the adjective settled in the 16th–17th c.). Stage 4 — English alight (adj.) "brightly lit, ablaze, glowing" (modern; the word of this card). NOTE ON THE HOMONYM: There is a second, unrelated English verb "to alight" meaning "to dismount, descend," from Old English ālīhtan "to descend, to relieve of a burden," built on the DIFFERENT root *legwh- "not heavy." The two alights coincide in spelling and pronunciation but descend from unrelated roots — a famous false-friend pair, kept strictly separate here. === ADRIFT === (Chain for the third root word, ADRIFT.) Stage 1 — PIE *dhreibh- "to drive, push, urge onward" (reconstructed; an extension of *dher- "to press, shake"; a proposed Hellenic reflex, Greek tríbō "to rub," is debated and flagged as such). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *drībanan "to drive, push, hunt, impel" (reconstructed); Old English drīfan "to drive, urge, hunt, chase" (attested from the 8th c.) → English drive. Stage 3 — Proto-Germanic *driftiz "a driving, that which is driven" (derived from *drībanan); Old Norse drífa "to drive, rush," drif "a snowdrift, that which is driven" (Viking Age, 8th–11th c.). Stage 4 — Middle English drift "a driving, impulse, a snow-drift" (14th c.) → English drift (n.) "a slow current or tendency; that which is carried along" (modern). Stage 5 — English adrift (adj./adv.) "floating at random, not anchored, without guidance," formed from a- "in, on, at" + drift (first attested 16th–17th c. in nautical use). NOTE: The chain from "to drive" (active, human force) to "adrift" (passive, directionless) is the semantic heart of the word — the active shepherd of drive becomes, in adrift, a person or thing simply carried; the modern psychological sense ("adrift from family," "a life adrift") is a 19th–20th c. extension of the nautical image and is a meaning-shift, not a new etymon.
