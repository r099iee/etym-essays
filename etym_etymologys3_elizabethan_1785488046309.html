Two Semitic roots, one oath. The word Elizabethan begins as a proper name built from two Hebrew elements. The first is ʾēl (אֵל), the common Semitic word for “god” — attested in Ugaritic as ʾil, in Akkadian as ilu, and still living in Arabic as ʾilāh, “a god”, which with the definite article becomes Allāh, “the God”. The second element is the root šbʿ, “seven”, from which Hebrew built šeḇaʿ, “seven”, and — through the ancient idiom by which making a “seven” was taking a sacred oath — the verb šāḇaʿ, “to swear”, and the noun šeḇûʿā, “oath”. Combining the two gave the theophoric name ʾĒlîšeḇaʿ, “my God is an oath” (or “God has sworn”), a covenant-name of the same type as Daniel (“God is my judge”) and Michael (“who is like God”). It is first found in the Hebrew Bible as the name of the wife of Aaron; in the New Testament the Greek form Elisábet (Ἐλισάβετ) is borne by the mother of John the Baptist.

From Jerusalem to Westminster. The name travelled through the standard channels of the age: the Septuagint’s Greek Elisábet became Latin Elisabeth in the Vulgate, and Old French and Anglo-Norman carried it into English, where it settled as Elizabeth and became one of the great royal names of Christendom. A medieval Old Provençal and Old Spanish shortening produced Isabel, the ancestor of Spanish Isabel and Italian Isabella — so that Isabel and Elizabeth, though they look like different names, are in fact two reflexes of the same Hebrew original. In England the name attached itself to the house of Tudor: it was borne by the daughter of Henry VIII who reigned from 1558 to 1603 as Elizabeth I, the Virgin Queen around whom the greatest flowering of English literature arranged itself.

A name becomes an epoch. The adjective Elizabethan was coined by applying the Latin-derived suffix -an (from -ānus, “belonging to”) to the queen’s name, in the same way as Victorian, Jacobean and Edwardian. It first appears in historical and literary scholarship in the early nineteenth century, when the Romantic rediscovery of the “age of Elizabeth” made the queen’s name a label for a whole culture. Victorian criticism then canonised the period: the word expanded to cover the drama of the Globe, the sonnet sequences, Hooker’s prose, the 1559 Religious Settlement and the defeat of the Armada — an entire golden age compressed into an adjective. Sound changes played only a minor part in the name’s history (chiefly the Hebrew-to-Greek reshaping of consonants and the Latin-to-French vowel weakening); the real engine of change was cultural: a royal proper name, growing sacred through a dynasty, then secularised into a period label as scholarship looked back at a vanished greatness.

  • Literary attestation. The word carries the full weight of Attridge’s 1974 title: Elizabethan here is a technical label for quantitative verse experiments, the precise scholarly sense the adjective had acquired by the twentieth century.
  • Socio-cultural register. Always capitalised, always epochal; in modern speech it is at once scholarly, celebratory and faintly theatrical, whether applied to a play, a sonnet, an oak table — or a cat in a veterinary cone.
