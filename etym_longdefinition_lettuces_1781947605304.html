Lettuces (noun, plural) — the plural of lettuce: a cultivated plant of the daisy family (Asteraceae), Lactuca sativa, grown for its crisp edible leaves, which are typically eaten raw in salads. In the world of Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit, the word names the very first crop the disobedient Peter raids in Mr. McGregor's garden, the initial indulgence of the greedy feast that soon makes him "rather sick." The plural is concrete and child-story plain: not botanical jargon, but countable garden plants lined in a bed, each head a unit of theft and appetite. CORE SENSES
  • Botanical / horticultural sense. A member of the daisy or composite family (Asteraceae), Lactuca sativa, a cool-season annual or semi-annual herb cultivated for its leaves, with a characteristic milky white sap (latex) that exudes when a leaf or stem is cut — the property that gave the plant its Latin name lactuca, "the milky one," from lac, "milk."
  • Culinary sense. The edible leaves of the plant, served fresh in salads, sandwiches, wraps, and burgers; lettuce is the archetypal "salad vegetable," valued for texture, water-content, and coolness rather than for flavour or nutrition — which is why it is often half-jokingly called the least interesting element of a dish.
  • Countable plural sense. Individual plants or heads: "lettuces" pictures rows or heads in a garden bed, each a discrete, countable unit. This is exactly how Potter uses it — a crop Peter can "eat some" of, alongside "some French beans" and "some radishes."
SECONDARY AND MODERN MEANINGS
  • US slang: lettuce = money. From the 1920s–30s, green paper currency was called lettuce (alongside cabbage, dough, kale) because the green of banknotes echoed the plant's leaves; period underworld and jazz slang used "to make the lettuce" for earning cash and "tossed salad" for mixed funds.
  • Homophone pun: "lettuce pray." The sound-identity of lettuce and let us powers one of English's most durable puns — "lettuce pray," "lettuce all be friends" — and is exactly the phonetic play this card's mnemonic exploits ("LET us" sounds like LETTUCES).
  • Shakespearean heritage: "salad days." The era of youthful inexperience takes its name from green salads in Antony and Cleopatra (c. 1606), "My salad days, when I was green in judgment," embedding lettuce-land, greenness, and immaturity in a fixed English idiom.
  • Varietal terminology. The modern lettuce economy is organised by type: cos (also romaine), crisphead (iceberg), butterhead (bib, Boston), looseleaf, and stem or celtuce lettuce — jargon of seed catalogues, horticulture, and the food trade that a child's story deliberately avoids.
WHY THIS WORD IN THIS SCENE Potter's rhythm — "FIRST ... and then ... AND then, feeling rather sick" — builds the whole comic moral of greed on the specificity of the crop. Lettuces completes a triad of kitchen-garden produce (lettuces, French beans, radishes) that anchors the episode in edible material reality; without the lettuces, the sequence of greed and queasiness loses its garden specificity. REGISTER AND COLLOCATIONS Common collocations: a head of lettuce, lettuce leaves, crisp lettuce, butter lettuce, iceberg lettuce, cut-and-come-again lettuce, to grow lettuces. Register is everyday, concrete, and kitchen-garden domestic; the word carries none of the elevation of cuisine terms, which is precisely why it belongs in a children's book about a rabbit.
