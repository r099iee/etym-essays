The biography of talon begins, surprisingly, at the ankle. Its ancestor is Latin talus, the anklebone — specifically the astragalus, the block of bone on which the leg turns — and, by extension, the ankle and heel of the whole foot. Crucially, no secure Indo-European ancestor has ever been found for talus: the great comparative linguists register no convincing PIE etymon, and the most likely explanation is that the word was borrowed from an unknown Mediterranean source into Latin. It has no Greek sibling (Greek tálos means something entirely different — a young man or lad), no Sanskrit sibling, no Germanic sibling; the Germanic languages built their words for heel and ankle from other roots, as English does with heel. The word is, in effect, an orphan in the Indo-European family, and its story is therefore the story of a single Latin word travelling forward rather than a great branching of cognates. Roman culture, however, gave the word a second life: the knucklebones of the ankle, the tali, were cast as dice in gambling, so that the anklebone-name was also a gambler's word, and the astragalus itself entered Latin anatomy and later English anatomy as the talus.

From talus, Late Latin made a derived form, talō, talōnem, meaning the heel, and from this Old French built talon, the ordinary word for the heel of the foot and of the boot. With the Norman Conquest, talon crossed the Channel and entered Middle English around 1300 as taloun, and for a century or more it meant simply 'heel' — the heel of a human foot, the heel of a shoe, the heel of a deck of cards, a sense that French talon still keeps today, naming the stock of cards left in the deck. The great shift happened in the falconer's mews. The falconer, looking at a bird of prey perched on his gauntlet, saw that the hindmost claw — the great spur-like hallux at the back of the foot, the claw that strikes and kills — sat exactly where a human heel would be, and in Old French that claw was already called the talon, the bird's 'heel'. English adopted the transferred sense from falconry, and from the fifteenth century onward the word began to climb up the foot: first the hind claw alone, then all the claws of a raptor, and finally, in literary usage, the talons of dragons, griffins, harpies, and mythic beasts.

The semantic journey is a study in metonymy — the part standing for the whole, the heel standing for the claw, the claw standing for the predator's power:

  • Sound change: Latin talus (short a, light second syllable) produced Vulgar Latin talō and the accusative talōnem; Old French reduced the form to talon, and Anglo-Norman brought it into Middle English as taloun/talon, the modern English pronunciation losing the final -n's nasal colour in the standard accent.
  • Falconry and the transfer: the fifteenth-century hawking manuals record the talon as the raptor's heel-claw; the transfer from body-part to weapon is the hinge of the whole history, and it is why the word always implies grasping and seizing rather than mere scratching.
  • Figurative extension: from the seventeenth century onward the word reaches humans — a tyrant's talons, to fall into the talons of the law — always preserving the sense of being caught and held by an implacable, predatory grip; and from birds of prey it spreads to the mythic beasts of romance and fantasy.
  • Technical uses: in card games the talon is the undealt stock (the 'heel' of the pack, from the French card sense); in botany a petal-claw is a talon; in anatomy the talus remains the anklebone, a direct 17th-century re-borrowing from Latin.
  • Literary register: Tolkien's use in The Hobbit (1937) — the Lord of the Eagles 'seized him in his talons' — gathers the whole history into one image: the anatomical precision of the hallux, the falconer's heel, the menace of the predator, and the sudden grace of rescue.

What is constant across the eight hundred years of the English word is the physics of the grip: a curved, hooked, grasping instrument that seizes and holds. Whether the talon is the anklebone rolled as a die, the heel of a boot, the stock of a card-deck, the great hind claw of an eagle, or the figurative grasp of tyranny, the word never loses its image of a hooked point that locks on and will not let go — which is exactly why being caught in talons remains terrifying even when, as in the rescue of Bilbo, the talons save your life.
