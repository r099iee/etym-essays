The history of bethinking is a history of prefixes, of reflexives, and of how a verb moved from everyday Old English use to the dignified margin of the modern lexicon. The word is a native Germanic formation, and every layer of it can be traced without leaving the English family tree. The prefix. The story begins with the PIE preposition *h₁epi, "at, near, upon," which in Proto-Germanic became *bi, "by, near, about." Old English had be- as a productive prefix attached to verbs and nouns, drawn from the preposition be "by." Its functions were various: it could be transitivizing (making an intransitive verb take an object), intensifying (adding thoroughness or direction), or simply decorative. The prefix gives English a whole family of "be-" verbs whose meanings include a strong inward or thorough dimension — bemoan, bewail, bestir, bespeak, beguile, belabor — and bethink belongs squarely in this family. In Old English, be- could attach to þencan to give beþencan, and the resulting verb meant "to think about, to consider, to take thought of." The prefix simultaneously made the verb transitive (one thinks something over) and directed the thinking at its object with emphasis, a combination that the reflexive construction would later exploit. The base. The base þencan "to think" descends from Proto-Germanic *þankijaną, the causative of *þankōną, "to think of with gratitude," which is why English has the twin pair think and thank, along with the noun thought. The PIE root behind *þank- is reconstructed as *tong- or *(s)tenǵ-, "to think, to feel," but the reconstruction is insecure: the Germanic forms have no certain cognates outside Germanic, and authorities differ on the root shape. What is certain is the inner-Germanic system: the noun *þankaz "thought, gratitude," the verb *þankōną "to think of, to thank," and the causative *þankijaną "to cause to think, to consider." Old English þencan continues the causative, and its forms — þencean, þencan, þohte (past), geþōht (participle) — show the characteristic Germanic weak verb pattern with its well-known vowel alternations, which survive in modern think–thought–thought. Sound change in the base. Old English þencan had a fronted vowel from the earlier *þankij-; the initial fricative þ [θ] became the modern th, and the palatal environment produced the affricate-like cluster we now hear in /ˈθɪŋk/. In the participle bethinking, the same base appears with the productive present-participial suffix -ing, which English developed from the Old English suffix -ung / -ing that originally formed verbal nouns. By Middle English the form was bithinken / bethenken, with the vocalism of thenken, and by the 16th century the spelling settled on bethink. The pronunciation /bˈɛθɪŋkɪŋ/ shows the familiar shortening of the prefix vowel, the loss of the old i-mutation length, and the modern participial ending. The reflexive turn. The decisive development is semantic: from "to think about (something)" the verb came to mean "to think oneself into" a course — i.e., to direct one's own thought. The reflexive construction "to bethink oneself of" and "to bethink oneself that…" is attested from the 14th century, and it is this construction that fixed the verb's modern literary meaning: to call to mind for oneself, to take thought, to resolve. The reflexive pronoun turns the ordinary grammar of thinking into a small drama: the subject is both agent and patient of the thought, which is why bethinking feels like a soliloquy. The biblical translators loved it — "bethink thee," "bethink ye" are imperatives of self-consultation — and it appears throughout the King James Bible, where it is used of remembering duties, reconsidering ways, and taking thought. Literary life and drift. In Middle English bethink was common in devotional and homiletic prose: Ælfric uses beþencan for "to consider, to reflect," and the word continued into the Wycliffite Bible and Chaucer. It remained a normal, if slightly formal, verb through the 16th and 17th centuries, used by Shakespeare, by the Authorized Version, and in legal and homiletic English. The 18th century still accepted it in respectable prose, but as the language developed blunter, more analytic idioms — to think of, to remember to, to remind oneself — the reflexive bethink began to recede. By the 19th century it survived chiefly in historical fiction, biblical quotation, and consciously archaic style. Today the participle bethinking, as in the card, is a fully literary form: it belongs to narrators who want to show a character pausing, weighing, and deciding, with the gravity of an older English that took inner deliberation seriously. Socio-cultural context. The fate of bethink mirrors the fate of reflexive self-address in English generally. Reflexive verbs were far more common in earlier English — one could "bethink oneself," "bestir oneself," "beware oneself" — and their decline tracks the drift toward impersonal and analytic constructions. A word that could render a moment of private calculation with a single verb was too useful to disappear entirely, but it has been pensioned off into the high register, where its archaism is itself meaningful. When a modern narrator writes bethinking himself, he signals not just a thought but a ceremony of thought — the measured self-consultation of a character who will act on what he has called to mind, exactly as the little novice does when he turns from the monastery to the yamen.
