From cognomen to household name

Catilīna began as an ordinary aristocratic surname. Roman names ran in three parts — praenomen, nomen, cognomen — and the Sergii, a patrician clan, used Catilina to mark one branch of the family. The name is first attested in the first century BCE, and its stem resists analysis; the suffix -īna is familiar from other Latin names, but no Latin root yields a convincing sense. The most economical explanation is that the cognomen entered Latin from Etruscan or another Italic language, where the -ina/-ena ending is common in personal names. Claims of a Latin meaning — “little whelp” and similar guesses — should be treated as speculation rather than etymology.

The name in the 60s BCE

What made the name permanent was politics. Catiline's career followed the standard path of a nobleman short of money: military service under Sulla, the praetorship in 68 BCE, a governorship in Africa, then a prosecution and exclusion from the consular race. Defeated by Cicero for 63 and by Decimus Junius Silanus for 62, he turned to conspiracy. The sources — Cicero's speeches, Sallust's monograph, Plutarch's Life of Cicero, Appian and Cassius Dio — were written by his enemies or after his death, and all agree that the name had become a warning before the year was out.

Latin literature and the fixing of the type

  • Cicero, In Catilinam I–IV (63 BCE): delivered in the Senate and the Forum; the First opens with the most famous interrogative in Latin prose; the Fourth argues for the death penalty.
  • Sallust, Bellum Catilinae (c. 42–40 BCE): a monograph with a moral thesis; Catiline is bold, corrupt and doomed; the speech of Caesar and the reply of Cato dramatise the choice between clemency and severity.
  • Plutarch, Life of Cicero and Life of Caesar: Greek biography that supplies the scaffolding later ages used.
  • Appian and Cassius Dio: the conspiracy set inside the larger narrative of the late Republic.

Englishing the name

English did not meet Catiline directly from the Senate house but through the Latin curriculum. Sixteenth-century grammar schools read Sallust and Cicero, and the name came into English with the texts, spelled Catiline after Latin Catilīna. Because it was learned in the classroom, it kept a schoolbook flavour even when it was used as a political insult. Ben Jonson's tragedy Catiline His Conspiracy (1611) put the whole story on the London stage, complete with the debate on the conspirators' punishment; the play failed, but it confirmed the name's theatrical life.

From name to byword

By the seventeenth century “a Catiline” was a stock term in pamphlets for a suspected traitor, and the adjective “Catilinarian” covered both the speeches and the plot. The pattern is familiar in English: a classical proper noun becomes a common noun of abuse, as with “a Nero”, “a Judas” or “a Quisling”. What distinguishes Catiline is the social colouring: the word implies a traitor from the ruling class, someone who uses his own privilege as a weapon. In the eighteenth century the name was pressed into party warfare; in the nineteenth it migrated into schoolbooks and romantic drama, with Crébillon's Catilina (1748), Voltaire's Rome sauvée (1752) and Ibsen's Catilina (1850) recasting the conspirator as a freedom fighter.

Modern usage and scholarship

Twentieth-century historians reopened the question of Catiline's aims. Syme's The Roman Revolution (1939) treated him as one symptom of a decaying oligarchy; later work has asked how much of the conspiracy was Cicero's construction for political advantage. The name itself has settled into three uses: the historical individual, the Catilinarian corpus, and the literary byword for an aristocratic plotter. In journalism it survives as a slightly bookish flourish, usually in phrases such as “a Catiline of the suburbs” or “the Catiline of the party”.

Sound and spelling

  • Latin: Catilīna, with long ī in the second syllable; the cognomen is stressed on the penult.
  • English: /ˈkæt.ɪ.laɪn/, with the Latin ending reduced to -e; the spelling Catiline has been standard since the sixteenth century.
  • Variants: Catilina is retained for the Latin form and in Continental scholarship; Catilinarian is the adjective; the plot is “the Catilinarian conspiracy” or “Catiline's conspiracy”.

What survives, then, is not a family name but a political fable: the aristocrat who, having lost an election, tries to burn the constitution down. The word's long life is a tribute to Cicero's rhetoric and to Sallust's art — and a reminder that the victor's vocabulary is often the one that lasts.
