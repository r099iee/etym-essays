Core meaning in context. In the given passage, lashings are the violent, sweeping blows of Smaug's tail — each swing of the tail lands like a whip-stroke, "breaking rocks to pieces" and "smashing wall and cliff." The word lashing (here in the plural) names repeated strikes, the way a whip or a long, powerful object is laid across a target again and again. The picture is of a huge tail cutting through the air and hammering the mountain with stroke after stroke.

Primary sense: blows and strokes of a whip. The most basic meaning of lashings is "a beating" or "the strokes of a whip": a criminal "received a lashing," a horse "was given a lashing." As a plural noun, lashings simply collects these strokes — so many blows, so many "lashings." This is the sense Tolkien draws on, transferring the idea of whipping strokes to the whiplike blows of the dragon's tail. The singular lashing means one such stroke or, by extension, the whole act of whipping.

Sense 2: the act of violent striking. More abstractly, a lashing is the act of striking with a whip-like motion, or the violent lashing about of a thing — "the lashing of the waves against the rocks," "lashing rain," "the lashing of a flag in the wind." Here the word has left punishment behind and simply means violent, repetitive striking or thrashing. In weather-writing especially, lashing has become almost a technical word for driven rain and crashing seas, and the plural lashings is used for the successive waves of such onslaughts.

Sense 3 (nautical): bindings of rope. From the separate verb to lash, meaning "to bind or fasten tightly with cord or rope," comes the noun lashing meaning the rope or cord itself, or the act of binding: sailors secure cargo and spars with lashings. This sense is centuries old and still fully alive in sailing, rigging, and general cordage vocabulary. Note that this "lashing" is a homonym of the beating one, and the two words are easily confused; in nautical contexts the binding sense is the only one intended.

Sense 4 (British colloquial): abundance. In British and Commonwealth English, lashings in the plural famously means "a great deal, plenty, an abundant supply," almost always of food or drink: "lashings of cream," "lashings of tea," "lashings of jam." This idiom, first recorded in 1835, has a warm, old-fashioned, almost childish ring — it evokes generous helpings and good things in large quantities, and it is closely associated with the cosy world of English tea-tables, school stories, and summer picnics. It is a pure idiom: you cannot say "a lashing of cream," and the singular is not used in this way.

Grammar. Lashings is a plural-only (plurale tantum) noun in its "blows" sense; the singular lashing is a countable noun meaning one stroke or one rope binding. In the idiom "lashings of," the word behaves like a quantity word ("a lot of"): it takes a plural verb and refers to an uncountable mass ("there are lashings of sandwiches," "with lashings of cream"). The related verb is to lash, with the past participle lashed ("lashed to the mast," "lashed by the rain") and the phrasal verb "lash out" (to strike suddenly, or to spend or criticize lavishly).

Register and style. The "blows" sense of lashings is literary, forceful, and slightly archaic — modern speakers usually say "blows," "strokes," or "beatings" — which is exactly why Tolkien uses it: it sounds like epic punishment. The nautical sense is technical and precise. The "abundance" idiom is conversational, nostalgic, and exclusively British-Commonwealth in flavour, which makes the same spelling carry two almost opposite emotional weights: one brutal, one cosy.

How the senses connect. The abundance idiom seems unrelated to whipping until one remembers that lashing is violent, generous motion: quantities that are "lashed" onto a plate, or drink that is "lashed" into a cup, are amounts so generous they seem to be poured with abandon. Many idiom-books derive "lashings of" from the image of copious liquid "lashing about" in a container — an image of plenty in motion — though the exact origin remains uncertain.
