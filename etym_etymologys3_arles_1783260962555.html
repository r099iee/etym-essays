The name Arles is a Celtic compound that has been pronounced on the banks of the Rhône for over two thousand years, and its two elements each open a window onto the deeper Indo-European world. To read the word is to read a history of Celtic settlement, Roman conquest, Provençal speech, and modern art.

The first element: are-. The word begins with the Gaulish preposition *are-, "near, in front of, before", a member of the Celtic family of "before/for" words that also gave Old Irish air- and ar ("for, before") and Welsh ar ("on, at"). Behind the Celtic forms lies a reconstructed Indo-European base, often written *h₂er- in the sense "in front of, near, at", though the deeper analysis is debated and the handbooks differ on how exactly to segment it. In Gaulish the element was extraordinarily productive in place-names: Aremorica, "the land by the sea" (the coast of north-western Gaul), and Arduenna, "the Ardennes", are formed on the same pattern. So the first syllable of Arles is a spatial word — it orients the place against its landscape.

The second element: -late-. The second half of the compound is *lati-, the Celtic word for marsh, swamp, or mud — a base reflected in Old Irish lathach ("mud, puddle") and Welsh llaid ("mud, dirt"). The city stands where the Rhône begins to break into its delta, on ground that was for most of its history marshy and flood-prone, so *Arelate, "the place before the marsh" (or "beside the marsh"), is a perfect environmental description: a settlement that defined itself by the wetland at its feet. The compound illustrates the Celtic habit of naming settlements by topography, exactly as the Britons had done across the Channel.

Romanization and the sound change. The Gaulish name passed into Latin as Arelate (or with the variant Arelas, genitive Arelatis), and Caesar mentions the place in his account of the civil war of 48 BC; shortly after, the colony was planted with veterans of the Sixth Legion, and the city — "Arelate, the little Rome of Gaul" — became a great river-port and the head of a canal to the sea. It was Constantine's occasional residence and the seat of the Praetorian Prefecture of the Gauls in the 5th century. As Latin evolved into the Romance speech of southern France, the final syllables were eroded: Arelate lost its ending and emerged in Old Provençal as Arles. This is the ordinary Gallo-Romance attrition of unstressed final syllables, and it is why the modern name is only a stump of the ancient one.

The name in history and in English. In the Middle Ages the city gave its name to the Kingdom of Arles, the Holy Roman kingdom of Burgundy and Provence (10th–14th centuries), so the toponym doubled as a royal title. English historical and geographical writing of the 16th century onwards naturalized the name as Arles, always pronounced "arlz", and Gibbon's sentence places it precisely among the great Romanized courts of the Continent. In 1888 Vincent van Gogh arrived and painted the sunflower, the night café, and the starry Rhône; within two years his art had made "Arles" a household word in every language of Europe. A name built from Celtic words for "near" and "marsh", worn smooth by Latin and Provençal, thus ended its biography as the label of both a Roman capital and a modern painter's paradise.
