PIE root *yeh₁- (or *ye-), "to throw, hurl, cast" (reconstructed; pre-3000 BCE) → Proto-Italic *jakjō, "to throw" (reconstructed; 2nd millennium BCE) → Latin iacere, "to throw, cast, hurl" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g. Plautus) → Latin dēicere (compound dē- "down, away" + iacere), "to throw down, cast down, fell" (attested 1st c. BCE, e.g. Caesar) → Latin dēiectus, past participle, "cast down, thrown down" (1st c. BCE) → Old French dejecter / desjeter, "to cast down; to dishearten" (11th–13th c., from Latin dēicere) → Middle English dejecten, "to cast down, throw down" (early 15th c.); dejected, "cast down in spirits" (1580s) → dejected + adverbial suffix -ly (Old English -līce) → dejectedly, "in a low-spirited, downcast manner" (adverbial use recorded from the 17th c.) Note on debate: The exact shape of the PIE root is disputed. It is traditionally reconstructed as *yeh₁- "to throw," but the Greek reflex hiēmi "I send, hurl" and the Sanskrit root yā- "to go, travel" are sometimes assigned to the same etymon and sometimes to different roots; the Italic line leading to iacere is secure from Latin onward, and the spelling of the root may appear as *ye-, *yeh₁-, or *h₁yeh₁- in different handbooks. The Latin chain from iacere to dejectedly is well attested at every stage.
