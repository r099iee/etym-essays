Single root word (casement is not a compound). Linear chain from PIE to modern English: - Proto-Indo-European *kap- 'to take, seize, grasp, hold' (reconstructed; c. 4500–3500 BCE; the same root gives Latin capere and English have, heave, heavy through Germanic). - Latin capere 'to take, seize, catch, hold' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). - Latin capsa 'box, chest, case, repository — that which holds things' (attested 1st c. BCE; formed from capere). - Old French casse (also caisse) 'case, box, frame, reliquary, chest' (attested 11th–12th c.; from Latin capsa, with the cluster simplified). - Old French / Anglo-French casse + suffix -ment > casement 'a casing, frame, enclosure around an opening' (reconstructed intermediate; attested in Middle French and Anglo-French by the 14th–15th c.). - Middle English casement 'the frame or case of a window; later a hinged window' (attested mid-15th c.). - English casement 'a window hinged on the side; a window frame' (modern sense established 16th–17th c.). Debate note: the formation casement presupposes a French base meaning 'to enclose in a case' (verb casser 'to enclose') plus the nominal suffix -ment; this intermediate form is reconstructed because the earliest attested English examples already show the specialized window sense. The military word casemate is a separate but parallel formation from the same root. The phonetic simplification of Latin capsa to Romance cassa/casse (loss of the -p- in the -ps- cluster) is regular and not in dispute.
