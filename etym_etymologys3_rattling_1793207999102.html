The past of rattling is refreshingly honest: it has almost none. Where other words drag millennia of roots behind them, rattle was coined from the noise itself, and every century since has simply found new things that go rat-a-tat. Its history is not a chain of inheritance but a lengthening list of applications — dice, tongues, coaches, nerves — each generation hearing its own clatter in the old syllables.

The imitative birth: Some words are arbitrary — nothing in horse sounds like a horse — but rattle belongs with bang, crash, and babble among the words that perform their meaning. A rapid series of dental knocks, r-a-t-t-l, with the tongue tapping the teeth-ridge exactly as a loose shutter taps its frame: the mouth becomes the thing described. Such words are invented independently wherever the sound occurs, which is why Middle Dutch ratelen, Middle Low German ratelen, and Middle English ratelen appear almost simultaneously around the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries — not one borrowing chain but neighbours imitating the same noise, with English very likely catching it from the Dutch of traders and weavers in the wool towns.

  • 14th c. — dice and bones: the earliest English uses name percussive play and chill: dice rattling in the box, teeth rattling with cold, harness rattling on the road. Chaucer's age knows the sound intimately and needs no definition.
  • 15th–16th c. — the scolding tongue: to rattle takes on speech: a rattling tongue, to rattle in the ears, to rattle off prayers or curses. Reformers and schoolmasters use it against empty volubility — words as mere mouth-noise — and the rattlesnake, named by American colonists for the clatter in its tail, exports the image to natural history.
  • 17th c. — the age of the coach: improving roads and springless carriages make fast travel a percussive experience, and rattling becomes the standard adverb of ostentatious speed. Highwaymen, elopements, and stock-jobbers all arrive rattling; the noise advertises the haste, and the haste advertises the money.
  • 18th–19th c. — Cobbett's Guildford road: Spicer's four-horse rattle is the sound of the funds, of new wealth performing itself in harness and out-riders against the old gentry's one-horse gig. Cobbett, the farmer's son with a countryman's ear, hears class in the clatter: retrenchment goes quietly, speculation goes rattling. Meanwhile the railways spread the participle nationwide — trains rattle over points, carriages rattle through tunnels — and slang makes rattling an all-purpose intensifier for hearty approval.
  • 20th c. to today — nerves, engines, witnesses: psychology gives us the rattled nerves of the shaken witness; motoring gives us the rattling exhaust and the rattling good run; broadcasting gives us presenters who rattle through the headlines. The echoic core is untouched: children still rattle boxes to guess their contents, exactly as their medieval ancestors rattled dice.

Sound changes: There was almost nothing to change, because the word was born already fitted to English mouths. The Middle English infinitive ratelen lost its final syllable in the great fifteenth-century shedding of final -en, leaving rattle; the double t marks the short stressed vowel before it, and the participial -ing attaches regularly to make rattling. Pronunciation shifted only with the general vowel system, settling at /ˈræt.lɪŋ/. Imitative words resist irregular change — speakers constantly re-anchor them to the sound — which is why rattle looks nearly the same after six hundred years.

Why imitation endures: Most vocabulary must be learned; echoic vocabulary only needs to be heard. Rattling survives every revolution in transport and media because each new age produces new clatters — the telegraph sounder, the typewriter carriage, the machine gun, the spray can with its mixing ball — and the old imitation stretches to cover them without effort. Cobbett used it to shame a stock-jobber; a mechanic uses it to diagnose an engine; both mean exactly what the syllables say.
