Gnomes is the plural of gnome, a noun for a diminutive legendary being that lives underground and is often associated with mines, buried treasure, and the secret life of the earth. In English the singular is pronounced /nəʊm/ and the plural /nəʊmz/. The word belongs to the vocabulary of folklore, fantasy, and children’s literature, but it also has a long history in Renaissance alchemy and a busy modern life in garden ornaments, cartoons, and computer software.

Primary sense: the folklore creature. A gnome is typically imagined as a small, humanoid, bearded figure, sometimes hunchbacked, who dwells in caves, mines, or beneath the ground. In the standard modern picture, gnomes guard precious metals and gems, work invisibly in mines, and avoid human contact. They are close cousins of dwarfs, elves, goblins, and kobolds, but each of these has a distinct traditional profile.

  • Earth-dweller: the defining trait is habitation beneath the surface, not merely small stature.
  • Treasure-guardian: gnomes are frequently associated with ore, gems, and the secret wealth of mountains.
  • Elemental spirit: in the Paracelsian scheme, gnomes are the spirits of the element earth, as sylphs are of air, undines of water, and salamanders of fire.
  • Diminutive and aged: the conventional iconography gives them short bodies, long white beards, pointed caps, and wise or mischievous faces.

Secondary sense: the garden ornament. Since the nineteenth century, gnome has also meant the painted statue of such a creature placed in a garden. The garden gnome is a distinct cultural object: originally German, later exported across Europe and North America, and now a byword for suburban kitsch. In this sense the plural gnomes can mean a collection of statues rather than a race of beings.

Figurative and idiomatic extensions. The creature’s association with hidden wealth produced the twentieth-century phrase gnomes of Zürich, a derogatory label for Swiss bankers and international financiers, popularized in British politics in the 1960s. The image is of small, shadowy figures manipulating money underground. A different figurative use calls a very small or ugly person a gnome, usually as an insult. In computing, GNOME is the name of a free desktop environment, but this is an acronym (GNU Network Object Model Environment) and not a semantic development of the folklore word.

A separate homograph: gnome as maxim. English also has a rare noun gnome meaning “a short pithy saying, a maxim,” as in the phrase “gnomes and proverbs.” This word comes directly from Greek gnōmē “thought, judgement, opinion,” and is the source of gnomic and gnomon. It is spelled and pronounced identically with the creature word but has a completely different etymology. The plural gnomes can therefore, in principle, mean either creatures or maxims, though context almost always disambiguates.

The semantic bridge. The creature sense develops from Paracelsus’s New Latin gnomus, a coined term for the earth elementals. The word’s probable Greek components mean “earth-dweller,” which directly explains the underground habitation. From “earth-dweller” comes “mine-spirit,” from “mine-spirit” comes “guardian of buried treasure,” and from the treasure-guardian comes the garden statue and the financier insult. The modern cute garden gnome is thus a distant, domesticated descendant of a Renaissance cosmological category.

Register and domains. The word is neutral in folklore and fantasy, affectionate or whimsical in children’s literature, and mildly derogatory when applied to people. In geology and mining history it appears in discussions of mining folklore. In computing, GNOME is a proper name and should be capitalized, unlike the creatures.

Grammar and usage. The plural gnomes is regular. The adjective is gnomish or gnome-like; the collective is usually “a race of gnomes” or “the gnomes.” The word is often capitalized in fantasy settings when it names a people, as in “the Gnomes of the Underdark.” Note that gnome rhymes with home and Rome in standard RP: the vowel is /əʊ/, giving /nəʊm/, not /nuːm/.

Cultural range. Gnomes appear in the folklore of Germany, Scandinavia, Switzerland, and the Low Countries; they are related to the Scandinavian tomte or nisse, the German Bergmännlein (little mountain man), and the English kobold tradition. In modern fantasy they are stock characters: sometimes wise craftsmen, sometimes comic rustics, sometimes sinister underground traders. The range shows how flexibly the word has been adapted to whatever the surrounding story needs.
