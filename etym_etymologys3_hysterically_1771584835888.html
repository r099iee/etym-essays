The biography of hysterically is the biography of a scientific error that became a word of everyday passion. It begins with a humble Proto-Indo-European particle, *ud-, meaning "up, out, away," a grammatical shifter rather than a concrete noun. In the Indo-European languages this particle combined with the contrastive-comparative suffix *-tero- to form *ud-tero-, "the outer one, the further one, the later one," and it is this formation that Greek built into hysteros (ὕστερος), "later, after, second" — the word still alive in the rhetorical figure hysteron proteron, "putting the later before the earlier," and in the physics term hysteresis, "the lagging behind" of an effect. The secure Indo-Iranian cognate is Sanskrit uttara-, "upper, higher, later, further, northern," which lives on in Hindi uttar, "answer, reply; north."

From hysteros came hystera (ὑστέρα), "the womb," understood by the ancient Greeks as "the later one," the organ that comes last or lies outermost. Ancient medicine took this derivation at face value: the Hippocratic physicians believed that the womb was an independent, restless creature that wandered about the body, and that when it rose into the chest it caused hysterikē pnix (ὑστερικὴ πνίξ), "hysterical choking" — a suffocation, spasm, and loss of voice diagnosed almost exclusively in women. Because the womb was the hystera, all these disorders were hysterika, "of the womb," and so hysterikos (ὑστερικός) became the Greek adjective for the whole convulsive, suffocating, emotional pathology of women. Latin borrowed the term as hystericus, and from Latin it entered English in the seventeenth century as hysteric and hysterical, meaning "suffering from a uterine condition," then simply "wildly uncontrolled in emotion." The noun hysteria was coined in modern Latin in 1801 and became the banner diagnosis of the nineteenth century — Charcot's Salpêtrière, Freud and Breuer's Studies on Hysteria — so thoroughly that "hysteria" became a byword for irrational female emotion, a weapon as much as a diagnosis.

The adverb hysterically rode along on all of this, and by the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it had broken free of medicine entirely. Today it ranges from genuine convulsive grief or laughter — Bao-yu sobbing hysterically over his jade — to the jokey hyperbole of "hysterically funny," and it still carries a ghost of the old "female malady" charge, which is precisely why applying it to a weeping young man in The Story of the Stone is quietly pointed: the vocabulary of the womb is visited upon a boy, and the ordered, courtly masculinity of his world is shown to crack under a grief that is older, deeper, and in the old medical terms, "womblike."

In the twentieth century the word loosened further into the everyday intensifier, and this second life is worth noting in its own right. "Hysterically funny," "hysterically loud," "hysterically busy" use the adverb as pure hyperbole — extremely, absurdly, to a degree that tips past control — with no more clinical content than "wildly." Yet even in this weakened form the old charge lingers at the edges: the phrase "hysterically funny" still suggests laughter that has become convulsive, beyond the power of the laugher to stop, and the medical history is never quite erased. The result is a word of unusual double life. In a serious key it describes the genuine breakdown of composure — the sobbing of a young man who has discovered that the thing that defines his existence is unique to him; in a comic key it describes the equally genuine, equally uncontrolled overflow of mirth that we call a fit of laughter. Both keys turn on the same ancient doctrine: emotion so powerful that it seizes the body itself, a convulsion of feeling that, two and a half millennia after the Hippocratic physicians first blamed it on a wandering womb, still goes by the name they gave it.
