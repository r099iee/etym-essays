- c. 1200 — the word is first recorded meaning wise, clever, or ingenious; it is a word of approval for skill and intelligence.
- 13th–14th c. — The sense broadens to elegant, fine, and fashionable in courtly writing, and also to proud or haughty; the word belongs to romance and chivalry.
- Late 14th c. — A decisive turn: quaint begins to mean strange, unusual, or odd, and the older senses start to recede.
- 16th–17th c. — Quaint conceits and quaint devices describe ingenious fancy in poetry and ornament; the word is still linked to cleverness, now of a playful kind.
- Late 18th c. — The modern sense of attractively old-fashioned emerges, helped by antiquarianism, the picturesque, and the fashion for ruins and rustic cottages.
- 19th c. — Travel writing and heritage prose make quaint a standard adjective for villages, customs, and dialects, and the word acquires its faintly condescending tone.
- 20th–21st c. — Quaint is affectionate nostalgia and mild faint praise; critics of tourism point out that calling a living place quaint turns it into a picture.
