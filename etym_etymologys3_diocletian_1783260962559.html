PART ONE — Two Indo-European roots behind a Greek name.

The name Diocletian is built on two ancient Indo-European roots, and its deep history is the history of the Greek religious world. The first element, Dio-, is the stem of the Greek god Zeus, whose own name reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European root *dyew-, "sky, heaven, daylight". That root gave Sanskrit dyaus, Latin Iuppiter (and diēs, "day"), and Greek Zeus — the sky-god whose name literally means "the bright sky". In Greek compounds, the oblique stem Dio- ("of Zeus") appears in a host of personal names: Diogenes, "born of Zeus"; Diodorus, "gift of Zeus"; Dionysius, "of Dionysus"; and Dioklēs, "glory of Zeus".

The second element, -klēs, descends from the PIE root *ḱlew-, "to hear". From "hearing" the root produced the abstract notion of "fame" — literally "that which is heard of one", the report that spreads by word of mouth. Greek kleos, "glory, fame, renown", is the word of Homeric heroism: Achilles chooses kleos aphthiton, "imperishable glory", over a long and obscure life. As the contracted -klēs it forms the second half of the great Greek names of fame — Periklēs "far-famed", Hēraklēs "glory of Hera", Themistoklēs "glory of right" — and Dioklēs, "glory of Zeus", meaning "he whose fame is Zeus's" or "famed by Zeus".

PART TWO — From Diocles to Diocletianus: a name for an emperor.

Dioklēs was a common Greek personal name long before the empire: the Diocles of Syracuse was a celebrated geometer of the 2nd century BCE, and the name was widespread in the Greek-speaking East. The future emperor, born Diocles in Dalmatia around 244 CE of humble Illyrian stock, rose through the army in the third-century age of soldier-emperors. On his proclamation by the troops in November 284, he assumed the full Roman imperial name Caius Aurelius Valerius Diocletianus — a name built, like all imperial names, by heaping the gentile and cognominal elements of the imperial house upon the new personal designation. The final element, Diocletianus, is Diocles plus the Latin adjectival suffix -ianus, meaning "belonging to Diocles": the emperor made his humble Greek-named self the dynastic center of a new order.

The name's career after that is entirely Roman and imperial. In Greek, the emperor was known as Dioklētianos; in Latin, Diocletianus; in the official monuments and coin legends of the Tetrarchy the abbreviated form of his name marched beside those of his colleagues. The pairing on this card, "the splendour of Diocletian and Constantine", was already a topos in the fourth century: Lactantius, the Christian apologist who saw him as a persecutor, and Eusebius, the church historian, fixed the hostile image, while the panegyrics and the imperial monuments broadcast the official image of the restorer of the world.

PART THREE — The shifting reputation of a name.

  • Contemporary official image: the panegyrics of 289–303 and the coinage present Diocletian as the restorer of order, the "founder of peace", the Augustus who renewed the golden age; his joint rule with Maximian was celebrated as the concordia of the two Augusti.
  • Christian memory: from Lactantius's On the Deaths of the Persecutors onward, Diocletian became the archetypal tyrant-persecutor, the emperor who burned scriptures and slaughtered martyrs; the Egyptian church kept the "Era of Diocletian" as a counting of years from his accession, marking the age of martyrdom.
  • Late-antique and medieval world: in the western chronicles he is one of the pagan persecutors (Nero, Decius, Diocletian, Julian), a fixed item in the roll of imperial villains, while his palace at Split, his baths, and his reforms remained standing monuments of late-Roman power.
  • Enlightenment historiography: Gibbon made Diocletian the architect of the "despotism" of the later empire — the ceremonial, bureaucratic, systematically managed state that replaced the accidental autocracy of the earlier Caesars — and it is precisely this image that "the splendour of Diocletian" evokes on this card: the East still displaying the magnificence of an autocracy while its civilization slowly fades.
  • Modern scholarship: historians treat the "Diocletianic" period as the hinge between the Principate and the Dominate, and his price edict and tetrarchic system as classic case-studies in administered economy and collegiate rule.

PART FOUR — A name as a monument.

The personal name Dioklēs, "glory of Zeus", belonged to the pagan religious world whose gods the same emperor's edicts tried to defend by persecution — and whose persecutions in turn gave the Christian church its greatest martyrs. The irony is complete in Gibbon's sentence, where the splendour of the persecutor is exhibited by the court of the Christianizing East, and the name that once meant "glory of the sky-god" became the scholarly byword for bureaucratic autocracy, for the state's last great persecution of Christianity, and — in the standing ruins of Split — for the architecture of imperial retirement. DIOCLETIAN is thus not merely a name but a compressed history: two Indo-European roots (the bright sky, the heard fame), a Greek compound of religious devotion, a Latin imperial suffix, and twenty-one centuries of shifting reputation.
