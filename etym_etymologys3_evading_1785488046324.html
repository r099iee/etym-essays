EVADING belongs to the small, sturdy family of English words built on the Indo-European root *weh₂dʰ- (conventionally written *wadh- in older scholarship), whose concrete sense was "to go, to walk, to wade" — bodily motion, striding over ground, forcing a crossing through water. The root is securely attested in two of the great Indo-European branches. In Italic it produced Latin vādere, "to go, to walk, to march, to advance", a verb of purposeful, often determined motion. In Germanic it produced *wadaną, the ancestor of Old English wadan, our verb to wade — to walk through water or, figuratively, to advance with effort through a difficulty. Evading and wading are therefore cousins, and the oldest image behind "evading a question" is that of wading out of the water and away.

Sound and form. The decisive development happened in Latin. By prefixing the preposition ex-, "out of, away from", to vādere, Latin formed ēvādere, "to go out, to get out, to escape". The prefix regularly appears as ē- before a consonant (the -x- is assimilated and the vowel lengthened), so ēvādere is literally "to walk out of" a place, a battle, or a danger — a trap escaped by walking out the back. The perfect participle ēvāsus, "having escaped", spawned the noun ēvāsiō, "an escaping", which English took in the fifteenth century as evasion, and the adjective ēvāsīvus gave evasive in 1725. The verb itself entered English through Old French évader in the early sixteenth century (c. 1510), becoming Middle English evaden and then modern evade. French had preserved the Latin form almost unchanged, and English at first used the word entirely in the physical sense: "to escape from" a danger, a place, or a pursuer — a neutral and often heroic word.

Semantic drift. The great drift of the word over four centuries has been from physical escape toward intellectual and moral dodging. Already in the seventeenth century writers used evade of questions, duties, and the force of laws: to evade a command, to evade the law, to evade a debt. The physical walk became a mental side-step. Two pressures shaped the shift. First, the collocational field of the verb migrated from concrete objects (a pursuer, a battle, a prison) to abstract ones (a question, an obligation, a difficulty) as English formal prose, legal writing, and journalism expanded in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Second, the word acquired a small moral shadow: because one evades precisely what one cannot, or will not, confront directly, "evade" came to hint at cleverness shading into dishonesty — the guilty person evades the truth, the politician evades the question, the taxpayer evades the law. Modern legal and financial prose fixed this in phrases like tax evasion and evading a draft, where the word is strongly negative. But the moral colouring is context-dependent: the hero evades capture, the hunted evades pursuit, and — as in this passage — a scholar may evade a stylistic convention with perfect legitimacy, quietly wishing it away while continuing to work within it.

The passage at hand. In the sentence from which this card comes, the participle carries the full weight of the word's history. The author of a treatise on English metre has had to refer again and again to a hypothetical poet or reader, and has made that figure masculine throughout — not from conviction, but because he has "found no practicable way of evading the established convention". Evading is here the deliberately careful, rueful word: not rejecting, not ignoring, not transgressing, but sidestepping a convention too deeply embedded to be reformed. The participle form keeps the action open and unassertive — the evasion is a process, an ongoing accommodation, never a finished rebellion. It is precisely the right verb for a scholar who confesses, with courteous irony, that he wished the convention away but could not afford to fight it.

Family and kin. The same Latin verb vādere produced a cluster of English compounds, each preserving the plain verbal core "to go": invādere, "to march in, to assault", gave invade and invasion; pervādere, "to go through, to spread over", gave pervade and pervasive. On the Germanic side, *wadaną lives on in English wade and in Old Norse vaða, German waten, and Dutch waden, all meaning "to wade, to ford". No Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognate is established for this root: it is confined chiefly to Italic and Germanic, and the superficially similar Sanskrit vadh-, "to strike, to slay", belongs to an unrelated root, *gʷhen-. The word remains, then, a Western Indo-European story: a root of walking that became, in English, the standard verb for getting out from under a question, a duty, or a rule — the side-step that this card asks you to recognise in the quiet evasion of an entrenched convention.
