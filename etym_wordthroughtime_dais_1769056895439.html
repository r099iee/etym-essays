- High-table sense — In medieval England the word names the raised platform, and the High Table upon it, at the upper end of a great hall, where the lord and his household sat above the common diners — a working, everyday word of court and castle.
- Platform of honor — The sense widens to any raised seat of authority — the whole block that dignifies a throne, a feast, or a council — still the ordinary word of ceremonial architecture in the period vocabulary.
- The French drift — In French, 'dais' slides from the platform to the canopy above a throne or altar, so the English and French words, once siblings, now point at different objects — English keeps the block, French the covering.
- Contemporary sense — Modern English reserves 'dais' for formal, ecclesiastical, or period settings (a wedding dais, the dais of a guildhall), while daily speech says 'platform' or 'stage'; the word now smells of candle-wax, tapestry, and roast meat.
