ROOT 1 (WATER): Proto-Indo-European *h₂ekʷeh₂- (also written *akʷā-) — "water" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Latin aqua, Gothic ahwa "river, water", Old English ēa "water, river" (in place-names like Avon), Old Norse á, Tocharian āp; NOTE: English water is NOT from this root — it descends from the unrelated *wódr̥) ↓ Latin aqua — "water" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus; aqua was also the Roman legal and engineering term for water supply) ROOT 2 (LEAD): Proto-Indo-European *deuk- — "to lead, draw, pull" (reconstructed root; reflexes include Latin dūcere "to lead", Germanic *teuhaną > Old English tēon "to draw, pull" (whence English tug), Greek δεύω; the Germanic and Latin branches are securely cognate) ↓ Latin dūcere — "to lead, draw, conduct" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE; stem dūc-, supine ductum, past participle ductus "led"; the -duct- root of English conduct, produce, introduce, reduce) ↓ Latin ductus — (1) past participle of dūcere, "led, conducted"; (2) noun, "a leading, guidance; a conduit, pipe" (attested; the engineering sense of a water channel) COMPOUND FORMATION: Latin aquae ductus — "a leading/conveyance of water" (the engineering term, attested in Roman writers on water supply, e.g., Vitruvius, De architectura, 1st c. BCE, and Frontinus, De aquaeductu, c. 100 CE) → contracted compound Latin aquaeductus / aqueductus — "aqueduct" (a water-supply channel; the Romans built a dozen great aqueducts for the city of Rome from 312 BCE onward) ↓ French aqueduc — "aqueduct" (adapted from Latin aquaeductus; attested in French from the 16th century, e.g., in translations of the Roman antiquaries) ↓ English aqueduct — "an artificial channel for conveying water" (first attested in the 16th century, c. 1530s–1540s, in antiquarian and technical writing, borrowed from Latin aquaeductus or via French aqueduc) ↓ English aqueducts — plural (the plural used of Roman waterworks collectively, as in Macaulay's History of England, 1848) DEBATED / NUANCED: English may have taken the word directly from Latin aquaeductus or through French aqueduc; the standard account favors French mediation for 16th-century loans of this class, but direct Latin borrowing is also attested. The spelling -duct- in English reflects the Latin past participle ductus, while the French aqueduc shows loss of the -t- and the terminal -us; English pronunciation /ˈakwɪdʌkt/ blends the two traditions. The PIE root *h₂ekʷeh₂- for aqua is secure; note that English water, however, comes from the different root *wódr̥, so aqueduct's "water" is the Latin branch, not the native English one.
