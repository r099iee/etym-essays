Idomeneus is in Greek mythology the king of Crete and Achaean commander at Troy, son of Deucalion, grandson of Minos, father of Orsilochus, accompanied always by his squire and half-brother Meriones. He leads eighty black ships to Troy (Iliad 2.645–652), is one of the suitors of Helen bound by oath, one of the warriors in the Wooden Horse, and, with Nestor and Odysseus, one of the senior counsellors whose age does not diminish courage.

Homer characterises him as hale, boastful in a good sense, and steadfast: in Book 13 Poseidon inspires him and his aristeia follows — he kills Othryoneus (promised Cassandra), Asius, Alcathous son of Aesyetes, Oenomaus, and others, duels Deiphobus and Aeneas, and rallies the Cretans when the wall falls. Nestor calls him honoured above others in wine and council. After Troy he returns safely (Odyssey 3), though later tradition invents exile to Italy after the sacrifice-vow. The source sentence preserves a child's summary of this martial dominance: Idomeneus always killed twenty Trojans to other people's one.

  • Primary mythic sense: Cretan king at Troy: ruler of Knossos and the hundred cities, representative of Minoan-Mycenaean Crete in the Catalogue of Ships. E.g. Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans. This is the dictionary head sense, always capitalised.
  • Emblem of aged vigour: unlike Achilles' youth or Nestor's non-combatant age, Idomeneus is old yet still a front-line killer. Ancient and Victorian moralists cite him as proof that authority must labour. Ruskin's point — kings do harder work than anybody else — leans heavily on Idomeneus, who, though a king, fights like a common spearman and boasts of it.
  • Later-legend sense: the vow and exile: post-Homeric authors (Apollodorus Epitome 6.10, Virgil, Servius) tell how Idomeneus, caught in a storm, vowed to sacrifice the first living being he met, met his son, sacrificed or exiled himself, and founded Salentine cities. Mozart's Idomeneo (1781) makes this the whole plot: father torn between gods and son. To know Idomeneus fully is to know both Homeric and operatic versions and not to confuse them.
  • Operatic and artistic sense: Idomeneo as title-role tenor/tenor-baritone, ballet and painting subject (e.g. French neoclassical canvases). Here the name signals Enlightenment dilemmas of duty versus humanity, far from Bronze-Age slaughter.
  • No common-noun extension: unlike mentor (from Mentor) or nestor (wise old man), idomeneus lower-cased has no dictionary entry. It never became a type, a product, or slang. Metaphoric use is limited to learned allusion: an Idomeneus among novices would mean an old hand who still outworks the young, but this is rare and context-bound. The semantic bridge therefore stays within proper-name allusion.

To retain Idomeneus is to retain Crete, age, labour, and kingship in one Homeric node, exactly as Ruskin's memory bundles him with Tydides.
