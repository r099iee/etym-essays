Derivation chain for CONQUISTADOR (single root word): 1. Proto-Indo-European *kʷer- "to obtain, acquire, make" (reconstructed root; the classical handbooks give *kʷer- for Latin quaerere, though the precise shape and semantics are debated — see note). 2. Latin quaerō, quaerere "to seek, ask for, procure, obtain" (attested from the 3rd century BCE, e.g. Plautus). 3. Latin conquirō, conquirere "to seek out everywhere, collect, assemble, procure" — con- "together, thoroughly" + quaerere (attested in classical Latin, e.g. Caesar, Livy). 4. Latin conquīsītus "sought out, acquired" — perfect passive participle; the feminine conquīsta "that which has been acquired" (attested in Late Latin as the base of the Romance noun). 5. Old Spanish conquista "conquest" (feminine noun from the Latin participle; attested from the 12th–13th c.) and the denominative verb conquistar "to conquer" (13th c.). 6. Spanish conquistador "conqueror" — conquistar + agent suffix -dor, from Latin -tor/-tōrem "one who does" (attested from the 15th c.; in full flower in the 16th c. of the American conquests). 7. English conquistador — loanword borrowed from Spanish in the 19th century (first attested c. 1830, popularized by W. H. Prescott's histories of the conquests of Mexico and Peru, 1843–1847). Note on the debate: The PIE root behind Latin quaerere is not fully certain. The traditional reconstruction is *kʷer- "to obtain, acquire" (Pokorny), and some sources give *kʷeh₁- or *kʷey- "to make, heap up"; de Vaan treats the pre-Latin source as uncertain (*kʷaes- or *kʷais-). The Romance and English stages, by contrast, are securely attested. The 'conquer' meaning arises in Late Latin from the participle's sense of 'acquired, won,' and only secondarily in Romance.
