Quarriers (singular: quarrier) is the plural of an agent noun meaning "a worker who cuts and extracts stone from a quarry" — a quarryman, a stone-digger, a hewer of building stone. In the reading, the tools "that the miners and quarriers and builders of old had used" are the ancient rock-working implements found in the dwarves' mountain halls: the quarriers are the labourers who won the raw stone from the ground, as distinct from the builders who shaped and laid it.

Senses and domain:

  • Core sense — extractor of stone: a quarrier digs, splits, levers and hauls stone from an open pit or hillside face. The historical craft involved striking, wedging, levering and (from the 17th century onward) blasting; quarriers produce the rough blocks that builders, masons and stonecutters trim and dress. The word names the extraction trade itself, the first link in every chain from hillside to cathedral.
  • The quarrier's craft: pre-industrial stone was won by hand — picks, malls, wedges, and the "plug-and-feather" technique in which holes are drilled along a line and split by driven wedges; blocks were then moved by sledge, shear-legs and crane. "Scabbling" (roughly dressing a block at the face) was part of the quarrier's job, and skilled quarriers knew the grain of freestone, the cleavage of slate and the faults of granite as a sailor knows weather.
  • Distinction from neighbouring trades: a miner works underground in ore, coal or salt seams; a stonemason shapes and lays dressed stone in structures; a stonecutter finishes blocks in a yard; a quarrier is specifically the man at the rock face who wins the raw material. In large enterprises quarriers, masons and builders were organized as separate companies with separate wages, customs and traditions.
  • Modern terminology: in current usage "quarryman" (plural "quarrymen") has largely replaced "quarrier"; "quarrier" now reads as historical, literary or regional. Dialect records from the stone districts show "quarrier" as the ordinary local word into the 20th century.
  • Metaphorical extension of the base verb "to quarry": when one "quarries" data, facts or quotations, one extracts them laboriously from a source — mining a text the way stone is quarried from a hill; the agent noun is naturally extended in playful or technical prose ("quarriers of information").
  • Occupational surname: "Quarrier" (like Mason, Smith, Collier) survives as an English family name from the trade; in Scotland the spelling "Quarrier" is associated with the Victorian philanthropist William Quarrier (1829–1899), founder of the Quarrier's Homes for destitute children.
  • An essential distinction — two unrelated "quarry" words: "quarry" in our sense is the stone-pit (from Latin quadrāre "to make square", because quarries supplied squared building blocks); "quarry" meaning the hunted game ("the hounds pursued their quarry") is an entirely different word from Old French cuiriee, the portion of the slain deer thrown to the hounds, from cuir "hide". The two homonyms are constantly confused; only the stone-pit word gives "quarrier".

Register: "quarriers" is a plain occupational term, but in modern English it is markedly rare and reeks of history: it summons pre-industrial rock faces, parishes of stone-getters, and epic building projects. That antique solidity is exactly why Tolkien uses it of the dwarves of old — a people whose fundamental craft is working stone.
