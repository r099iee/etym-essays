The biography of emulation is a study in how one small Latin word for "competitive copying" travelled from the farms and forum of archaic Italy into the moral philosophy of Rome, the rhetorical schools of the Renaissance, the constitutional histories of the eighteenth century, and finally into the firmware and emulators of the digital age. At every step the core image held steady: a copy made in order to equal — and, if possible, surpass — the model. The Indo-European seed. The chain begins with the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *h2eym- / *aim-, glossed "to copy, to imitate". This root is also traditionally invoked for Latin imago "image, likeness" and imitari "to imitate", and through them English image, imagine, and imitation. The very existence and exact shape of this PIE root is the weakest link in the whole chain: no undisputed cognate survives outside the Italic family, and some linguists prefer to derive aemulus from a "going toward" root. The honest summary is that Latin already possessed, by the second century BCE, a well-developed family of "copying" words, and that they appear to descend from a single prehistoric Italic base even if its external connections are debated. The Italic split. Within Latin, the "copying" family bifurcated along a moral axis. imitari was to reproduce the form of something — value-neutral, like a painter copying nature. aemulari was to copy in order to rival, to reproduce while straining to beat the model; the adjective aemulus named a rival, and Plautus and the comic poets use it for people who are each other's competitors rather than mere echo-copies. The two verbs coexisted because Roman culture drew a real line between mimicry and ambition. Roman moral vocabulary: aemulatio against invidia. The noun aemulatio (Cicero, first century BCE) became a fixture of Roman ethical debate. The rhetoricians (Rhetorica ad Herennium, Quintilian) discussed it as a motive and a figure: to emulate the great orator was to be moved by his excellence, not by hatred. The crucial negative pole was invidia, envy: the envious man wishes to diminish the other, while the emulous man wishes to raise himself. Pliny and the historians of the empire regularly celebrate aemulatio among young aristocrats as the healthy engine of public life. This is precisely the "active emulation of the consuls" that Gibbon would re-import into the Roman constitution sixteen centuries later. Medieval and Renaissance transmission. Latin aemulatio passed into French as emulation and into English as emulacioun around 1400. In Renaissance humanist education the pair "imitation versus emulation" became a central pedagogical question: should a young author copy Cicero, or copy him in order to surpass him? The humanists answered that true imitation is emulation — a copying fired by competition, the only kind worthy of a free mind. The same idea saturated political writing: classical republicans argued that a republic thrives on the emulation of its citizens for public honour. Early modern English and its darker uses. In Elizabethan and Stuart English the word could swing both ways. The Authorized Version of 1611 uses emulations in Galatians 5:20 among the "works of the flesh", meaning jealous rivalry and factious strife — a thoroughly negative sense that is now obsolete. The OED records emulation meaning "jealousy" and "factious contention" into the seventeenth century. Only gradually did the noble, disciplined sense win out in ordinary usage, helped by the rhetorical tradition and by moral essays praising emulation as distinct from envy. Gibbon and the eighteenth century. Gibbon writes at the end of this tradition. In his first chapter, the phrase "the active emulation of the consuls" is not a figure of speech but a piece of causal analysis: because the consulship was held in pairs and for one year only, each consul was forced to compete with his colleague and with all remembered predecessors for the one thing a Roman aristocrat could not buy — gloria, secured by the triumph. Emulation is the psychological motor that converts an office into an engine of expansion. Gibbon inherits the word from the moralists and uses it with full classical weight. The twentieth-century technical sense. In 1953 the MIT Computation Center built an IBM 701 emulator to run Whirlwind programs; the terms emulator and emulation entered computing to describe a program or machine that reproduces the behaviour of another, often older, system. The semantic bridge is exactly the ancient one: the emulator is a copy that must perform as well as the model, matching its functions instruction for instruction. "Game console emulation" today means running the software of a dead machine on a living one — a copy striving, one might say, to equal the original's every capability. Summary. What survives across all these ages is a single structure: imitation plus competition, in which the model is admired and therefore must be matched. From a reconstructed PIE root to a Roman moral ideal, a humanist educational value, a Gibbonian engine of empire, and a computer program — emulation has never meant mere copying. It has always meant copying with the ambition to win.
