Gypsies is the plural of Gypsy (British spelling also Gipsy), an English noun and adjective that for centuries named the Roma people in Britain and much of Europe. The word arose from the mistaken belief that the Roma came from Egypt: Middle English egypcien and gypcian meant "Egyptian", and the shortened Gypsy became the ordinary ethnic label. In modern English, Gypsies is widely regarded as pejorative or at least inaccurate, and careful writers use Roma (singular Rom) or Romani people. The plural form is nonetheless embedded in history, literature, legal statutes, and a number of lexicalized phrases.

Primary sense: the people. Historically, the Gypsies meant the traditionally itinerant Roma communities of Europe, especially those known to English speakers through fairs, fortune-telling, metalwork, music, and horse dealing. The label covered a highly diverse population with different languages, religions, and occupations; it never corresponded to a single tribe. In older documents it often carried a legal and administrative meaning, as in the Tudor statutes against "Egyptians" and later vagrancy laws. Modern usage distinguishes the Roma as a transnational ethnic group from non-Roma Travellers (Irish Travellers, Scottish Travellers, and others), who are sometimes wrongly conflated with them.

Secondary and figurative senses. From the ethnic label developed a set of figurative uses. A gypsy could mean a free-spirited, dark-complexioned, or itinerant person, often with a romantic or slightly disreputable colour; the adjective gypsy meant "unconventional, outdoor, nomadic". These uses are now widely criticized because they recycle stereotypes of criminality, fortune-telling, and rootlessness. Other senses are lexicalized and often capitalised: Gypsy jazz is the style associated with Django Reinhardt and the Quintette du Hot Club de France; gypsy moth is the insect Lymantria dispar, now often called the spongy moth; gypsy cab is a US term for an unlicensed taxi; gypsy skirt, gypsy caravan, and Gypsy Rose Lee belong to fashion, travel, and entertainment. The verb to gyp "to cheat" is derived from Gypsy and is considered offensive; the British phrase to give someone gyp "to cause pain" may be a different word.

Tertiary and technical senses. In entomology, gypsy moth was the standard common name until the Entomological Society of America adopted spongy moth in 2022. In music, Gypsy jazz and gypsy punk are established genre labels, though some Roma musicians prefer Romani jazz or manouche jazz. In law and social policy, Gypsy appears in older British legislation and in the names of some organizations; official terminology now tends to use Gypsy, Roma and Traveller as a combined category, a usage that is itself contested. In literary studies, Gypsies is the title word of poems, novels, and operas, from Pushkin and Merimee to Bizet's Carmen.

Modern usage and semantic bridge. The semantic bridge runs from a false geographical origin ("Egyptians") to an ethnic label, then to a stereotype, then to a term of abuse. The shift is not a simple narrowing or broadening but a change in social evaluation: what began as a neutral, if mistaken, demonym became a heavily charged word. Since the late twentieth century, Roma and Romani activists have campaigned against Gypsy in official and media use. Many style guides now advise Roma or Romani, and Gypsies is retained mainly in historical quotations, organization names, and artistic titles. It is not impossible to use the word neutrally, but it requires context and care.

  • Ethnic sense - the Roma people, especially in older English usage.
  • Legal-historical sense - "Egyptians", a category of itinerants under Tudor and later statutes.
  • Figurative sense - a free-spirited or nomadic person; now stereotyped and contested.
  • Lexicalized compounds - Gypsy jazz, gypsy moth, gypsy cab, gypsy caravan.
  • Modern status - widely considered pejorative; Roma or Romani preferred.
