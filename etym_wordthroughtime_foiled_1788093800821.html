- 14th c. – Middle English "foilen": to trample, crush, defile; in the hunt, to spoil a scent by crossing it — the trail-image is born.
- 1560s–1580s – Semantic pivot: "foil" = to defeat, frustrate, discomfit; hunting logic (lost scent) and fencing (foiling a thrust) merge.
- 17th–18th c. – Standard verb of drama and prose for the frustration of plots; "foiled" settles in as a participial adjective.
- 19th c. – Victorian melodrama fixes "Curses! Foiled again!"; the word becomes deliciously theatrical.
- 20th–21st c. – Headline staple ("foiled robbery", "foiled plot"); still implies active interception by an agent, with a whiff of the stage villain.
