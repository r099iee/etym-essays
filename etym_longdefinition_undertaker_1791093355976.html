An undertaker is a person whose profession is to organise funerals and burials: collecting and preparing the body, supplying the coffin, arranging transport, liaising with clergy and cemeteries, and guiding mourners through paperwork and ceremony. In the source sentence the undertaker signs the burial register alongside the clergyman, the clerk, and the chief mourner, as an official witness and responsible professional.

The core modern sense is occupational and euphemistic. Death-care workers prefer funeral director in American English, but undertaker remains standard in British English and historical texts. Duties include embalming or refrigeration, dressing, viewing arrangements, obituary notices, flowers, vehicles, chapel booking, and compliance with public-health law. The undertaker is thus part craftsman, part event manager, and part grief counsellor, expected to combine discretion with logistical precision.

  • Funeral-professional sense: licensed or traditional manager of interment and cremation. This is the dominant contemporary meaning and the only one most speakers know.
  • Historical entrepreneur sense, now archaic: one who undertakes any project, as in undertakers of a colony, a play, or a building scheme. Found in Shakespeare, Jacobean business tracts, and early American charters. The bridge is to take on responsibility.
  • Contractor and middleman sense: in Irish history, a Protestant undertaker who took on plantation land; in construction, one who contracts to perform work. Both preserve the take-on-duty idea.
  • Figurative and jocular sense: one who undertakes a grim or thankless task, as in the undertaker of doomed reforms. Rare and usually ironic.
  • Compound and attributive uses: undertaker's parlour, undertaker's bill, undertaker-like gloom. Fiction uses the figure for gothic atmosphere.

Grammatically undertaker is a count noun: an undertaker, two undertakers. It collocates with local, family, funeral, and verbs appoint, call, arrange with, and pay. Register is formal-respectful when referring to the profession, literary-archaic when referring to entrepreneurs.

Domain usages include public-health regulation of embalming and burial depth; business history where undertaker and entrepreneur overlap; and literature where Dickensian undertakers like Mr Sowerberry embody solemn commerce and dark humour. The burial-register scene captures the civic gravity: the undertaker is not a peripheral trader but a signatory guaranteeing that interment was properly performed.

Polysemy radiates from the verb undertake, to take on. General undertaking narrowed to funeral undertaking because death is the task most needing a professional taker-on, while business undertaking was captured by entrepreneur and contractor. Euphemism reinforced the split: a plain word for doer became veiled by association with corpses, prompting Americans to coin funeral director, which in turn shows how social attitudes to death reshape vocabulary.
