The personal name Simias belongs to a large and very Greek family of names built on physical epithets turned affectionate. The base is the adjective simos, meaning snub-nosed, with the nose tipped up, and by extension flat or hollow. Unlike heroic names compounded from kleos glory or hippos horse, such names — Simo, Simon, Simos, Simias, Simylos — began as nicknames for a visible trait, then became hereditary and respectable. Plato’s Phaedo already features a Theban Simias, and inscriptions from Rhodes, Athens and Boeotia show the name widely spread by the fourth century, so the Rhodian poet inherited a familiar, friendly-sounding name, roughly “Snubby” made dignified by the patronymic suffix -ias, which often means “son of Simos” or “belonging to the Simos family.”

Phonetically the development is straightforward: Greek Σιμίας with long final -as, iota and eta differentiated, was transliterated into Latin as Simias, the Greek sigma becoming Roman S, the ending preserved. English classicists pronounce it /ˈsɪmiæs/, with short i and ae as ash-like “as,” while modern Greek would say /siˈmias/. Orthographically the capital S is invariable, marking personhood, and the addition “of Rhodes” in English mirrors Greek ho Rhodios, an ethnic that functioned almost as surname in a world without family names, distinguishing this Simias from the Theban philosopher and a dozen homonyms.

Socio-culturally the reference of the name has narrowed and brightened. In antiquity it denoted a living scholar, perhaps a teacher, known for glossaries and months-poems as much as for play. Through late-antique anthologists — Meleager, Philip, Cephalas — who preserved his epigrams and figured pieces among thousands, it survived as a tag attached to three oddities: Axe, Wings, Egg. Renaissance editors printing the Anthologia Palatina kept the attribution, and nineteenth-century German philology, especially the intense work on Hellenistic poetry, restored him to literary history as more than a curiosity. Today classicists invoke Simias not to summon a biography, which remains almost blank, but to mark a media-historical threshold: the moment Greek poetry became visibly bookish, when a Rhodian named Snubby taught verses to draw what they describe, inaugurating a lineage that runs through altars and syrinxes to George Herbert’s altar-wings, Apollinaire’s Eiffel Tower and contemporary concrete poetry on screens.

Sound change is minimal but instructive. The stem sim- keeps short /i/ from Greek sigma-iota through Latin transliteration to English, never diphthongising; the medial -ia- hiatus preserves two syllables where English might otherwise glide them, and the final -as retains Greek nominative masculine singular rather than Latinising to -us, a philological fidelity also seen in Archias, Moschus, Theocritus kept Greek. The accent shifts from Greek pitch on the penult to English stress on the antepenult /ˈsɪmiæs/, a regular anglicisation of classical names.

Semantic drift concerns reference rather than lexical meaning, since proper names denote rather than describe:

  • Nickname to respectable name: from descriptive adjective for a nose-shape to inherited personal name borne by philosophers, poets and ordinary citizens in inscriptions.
  • Living scholar to ethnic tag: from contemporary Rhodian grammarian known for many works to Simias ho Rhodios the author-heading attached to figured poems in anthologies.
  • Author to emblem: from one Hellenistic individual to shorthand for origin of pattern poetry in Renaissance editions, nineteenth-century histories of Greek literature such as Susemihl, and modern surveys of concrete poetry.

Literary attestations anchor each stage: Plato's Phaedo gives a Theban Simias debating immortality with Socrates; the Suda and Athenaeus catalogue the Rhodian's glosses and poems; Meleager's Garland and the Palatine Anthology preserve his epigrams; the Bucolic manuscripts transmit Axe, Wings and Egg with scholia naming Simias as inventor; and modern editors from Brunck to Gow to Acosta-Hughes reaffirm the attribution while debating order and authenticity. Socio-culturally the name thus travelled from Doric Rhodes to Ptolemaic Alexandria to Roman schoolrooms to Byzantine copyists to humanist presses, each community revoicing snub-nosed familiarity as learned ingenuity.
