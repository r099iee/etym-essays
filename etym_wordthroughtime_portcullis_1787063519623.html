- 12th-13th c. - Coined in Old French / Anglo-Norman as porte coleice ("sliding gate"), used across Norman and Angevin fortress architecture throughout France and England.
- Early 14th c. - Borrowed into Middle English (c. 1330) as portcolis / portcullis, appearing in chivalric romances and castle construction rolls (e.g. Caernarfon and Conwy castles).
- Late 15th c. - Adopted by Henry VII as the premier Tudor heraldic badge, linking the Beaufort family crest to English crown sovereignty after the Battle of Bosworth Field.
- 16th-17th c. - Solidified in Early Modern English literature (Shakespeare, Spenser, Milton) as both an architectural feature and an evocative metaphor for impenetrable barriers.
- 18th-19th c. - Formalized as the supreme symbol of the British Parliament; featured prominently in Romantic medievalist literature (Sir Walter Scott) and historical romances.
- 20th-21st c. - Preserved across fantasy literature (Tolkien, Martin), British government branding (Parliament, Customs), and everyday figurative speech for unyielding barriers.
