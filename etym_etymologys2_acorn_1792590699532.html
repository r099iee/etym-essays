Note on folk etymology: The phonetic and orthographic evolution of acorn represents one of the most celebrated cases of folk etymology in the English language. In Proto-Germanic, *akrana- had no historical connection to either the oak tree or cereal grain; it meant simply "produce of the uncultivated field or open pasture" (derived from the pastoral root of acre), denoting wild woodland mast gathered for swine. However, as the ancient connection between æcern and acre faded from speakers' consciousness during the Middle English period, English speakers re-analyzed the unfamiliar word through popular reinterpretation, associating its first syllable with āc ("oak") and its second syllable with corn ("grain, seed"). This natural cognitive assimilation altered both the spelling (producing forms like okecorn and acorn) and the pronunciation, forever anchoring the word to the oak tree.
