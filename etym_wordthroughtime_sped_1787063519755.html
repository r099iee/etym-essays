- [c. 700, Old English] 'spēd' (noun) — success, prosperity, wealth, good fortune; a solemn, honoured word for thriving
- [c. 900, Old English] 'spēdan' (verb) — to prosper, succeed, advance, get on; 'God spede you' was a true blessing of fortune
- [12th–14th c., Middle English] 'spēden' shifts ground — success implies progress, and progress implies swiftness, so the verb starts meaning 'to go quickly, hasten'; the noun begins meaning 'rate of going'
- [c. 1200, Middle English] 'sped' emerges as the strong past tense, formed by analogy with verbs like 'lede' → 'led' and 'mēten' → 'met'
- [16th–17th c.] The modern polarity is set: 'speed' = swiftness of motion; 'Godspeed' freezes the old blessing; 'sped' is standard literary past
- [19th–20th c.] Industrial and motoring age: 'speed' becomes a quantity — speed limits, speeding fines, speeds of engines; 'sped' enters news and technical prose
- [21st c.] 'Sped' remains the fleet, elegant past of motion ('the car sped off'); a separate, offensive schoolyard slang 'sped' (from 'special ed') exists but is unrelated and to be avoided
