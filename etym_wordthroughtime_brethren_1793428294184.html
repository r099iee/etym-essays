- Old English (c. 9th–11th c.) — Umlaut plural: brēþer exists alongside brōþru as the standard plural of brōþor for biological brothers and fellow monks.
- 13th c. — Formation of the double plural: Southern Middle English dialects attach the weak suffix *-en* to brether, giving rise to brethren.
- 16th c. — The Great Semantic Schism: brothers takes over biological family relationships, while the Protestant Reformation and the English Bible enshrine brethren as the sacred form for fellow Christians.
- 17th–18th c. — Guild and professional fraternity: Trade guilds, legal bodies, and medical companies adopt brethren for sworn colleagues; Swift uses it for fellow London surgeons.
- 19th c. — Denominational and fraternal badges: The name is formalized in religious groups (Plymouth Brethren) and fraternal orders (Freemasons, Odd Fellows).
- 20th–21st c. — Solemn and rhetorical register: Retained in religious liturgy, fraternal oaths, civil rights oratory, and literary satire, evoking covenantal ties.
