• Battered: Similar because both denote suffering physical blows and damage; differs because battered implies relentless, repeated, severe impacts that inflict extensive disfigurement, structural fracture, or widespread breakage, whereas bruised focuses on subsurface contusions, discoloration, or localized crushing.
  • Contused: Similar because both refer medically to tissue damage from blunt force; differs because contused is a formal, clinical medical term used in pathological reports, whereas bruised is the ubiquitous, evocative common and literary descriptor.
  • Wounded: Similar because both involve bodily trauma or emotional injury; differs because wounded typically requires an open break in the skin, bleeding, or weapon trauma (or deep psychological agony), while bruised implies an intact skin surface with underlying trauma.
  • Injured: Similar because both describe physical harm; differs because injured is a broad, generic legal and physical term covering any form of damage (sprains, fractures, burns), lacking the specific visual and chromatic qualities of bruised.
  • Crushed: Similar because both involve heavy compressive force; differs because crushed denotes catastrophic deformation, pulverization, or total destruction of internal shape, whereas bruised represents partial, localized subsurface disruption.
  • Lacerated: Similar because both stem from physical trauma; differs because lacerated specifically denotes jagged tearing or ripping of the external flesh, contrasting directly with the unbroken skin of a bruised injury.
  • Dented: Similar because both represent surface depression from impact; differs because dented applies primarily to rigid inorganic materials (metal, plastic) that hold an indentation, whereas bruised applies primarily to living biological tissues or organic matter.
  • Abraded: Similar because both arise from external mechanical friction; differs because abraded denotes the superficial scraping or rubbing away of the epidermis or surface layer, whereas bruised involves deep capillary rupture beneath the skin.
  • Scarred: Similar because both leave evidence of trauma; differs because scarred refers to the permanent fibrous tissue remaining after a wound has healed, whereas bruised refers to the active, temporary, resolving contusion.
  • Chafed: Similar because both involve surface irritation; differs because chafed is caused by continuous light rubbing or friction causing red superficial soreness, rather than blunt force impact rupturing microvessels.
