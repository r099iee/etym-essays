Taxonomy is the science, theory, or system of classifying things — originally living organisms, now any body of items, ideas, or phenomena — into ordered groups or taxa on the basis of shared characteristics, relationships, or principles, and by extension any such ordered scheme itself, often presented as a hierarchy, chart, or tree.

In its core biological sense, taxonomy is the descriptive and naming arm of systematics: the discovery, delimitation, description, naming, and placement of species within nested ranks such as species, genus, family, order, class, phylum, and kingdom (or domain). A taxonomist collects specimens, designates type specimens, publishes diagnoses, and assigns binomial Latin names under the international codes of nomenclature. Here taxonomy is both an activity (doing taxonomy) and its product (a taxonomy of orchids, of beetles, of viruses). Modern biological taxonomy draws on morphology, fossils, behaviour, ecology, and above all DNA sequences, and it stands in constant negotiation with phylogeny: ideally every named group is monophyletic, containing an ancestor and all its descendants.

  • Biological taxonomy (primary sense): the classification of organisms. Example: the taxonomy of Panthera places lions, tigers, leopards, jaguars, and snow leopards in one genus. Includes alpha taxonomy (describing and naming species), beta taxonomy (arranging species into higher ranks), and gamma taxonomy (studying variation within species and speciation).
  • General classification scheme (extended sense): any hierarchical or faceted ordering of a domain. Examples: a taxonomy of prose styles, of wines, of website content, of learning objectives, of financial instruments. In business and information architecture this is the dominant everyday sense: a controlled vocabulary organized parent-to-child so that navigation, search, and reporting are consistent.
  • Principle or theory of classification (abstract sense): the rules by which any such ordering ought to be made — criteria of division, rank, priority, and evidence. De Candolle original sense belongs here: taxonomy as the science of the laws of classification itself, contrasted with mere listing.
  • Knowledge-organization and computing sense: in library science, UX design, and AI, a taxonomy is a curated category tree that powers menus, tags, filters, and ontologies. It shades into thesaurus (which adds synonyms and related-term links), ontology (which adds formal relations and logic), and folksonomy (user-generated tagging). A product taxonomy on an e-commerce site or an intent taxonomy for a chatbot belongs here.
  • Figurative and humorous senses: any mock-systematic catalogue, often with ironic scientific gravity — a taxonomy of excuses, of first dates, of office personalities. The humour depends on borrowing the prestige of biology for trivial material.

Grammatically, taxonomy is a countable noun: one taxonomy, several taxonomies. The plural matters, because rival taxonomies of the same domain routinely compete — lumpers versus splitters, competing textbook charts — and choosing among them is itself an intellectual act. The adjectives are taxonomic (relating to classification: taxonomic rank, taxonomic key) and taxonomical (rarer, same meaning); the practitioner is a taxonomist; the verb is taxonomize (to place in a taxonomy). To call something a taxonomy promises three things: exhaustiveness (everything in the domain finds a place), distinctness (places do not confusingly overlap), and order (places stand in a principled hierarchy or sequence). That promise is precisely why a rhetoric textbook can offer a chart of basic prose types and call it a taxonomy: it claims the modest chart is complete, coherent, and teachable rather than an ad hoc list.

Learners should keep taxonomy distinct from its near neighbours. Classification is the broader act of sorting by any criterion; taxonomy is principled, ideally theory-driven classification. Nomenclature is only the naming rules within a taxonomy. Systematics is the wider science of organismal diversity including evolutionary history; taxonomy is its descriptive core. Typology classifies by ideal types rather than nested ranks and dominates the human sciences. Ontology in philosophy and computing specifies what exists and how entities relate, of which a taxonomy is usually only the hierarchical skeleton. In the source context — a chart of basic types, a taxonomy — the word dignifies an introductory pedagogical device: a small set of labelled boxes into which student writers can sort what they read, presented with the quiet authority of science.
