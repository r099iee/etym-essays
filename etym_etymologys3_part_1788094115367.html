Introduction. The history of part is a history of the word "share" writ large: from a PIE root meaning "to sell, to hand over", through Latin pars "a part, a share", into every corner of English vocabulary, where part and its Latin descendants (partial, particle, particular, participate, party, partner, partition, depart, apartment) form one of the largest word-families in the language. The word entered English twice over: once as a noun from Old French part, and once as a verb from Old French partir "to divide, to share out, to go away" — and both streams flow from the same Latin source.

The Indo-European root. Latin pars, partis "a part, piece, share" is derived by most authorities from the PIE root *per- "to sell, to hand over, to traffic in", the same root that appears in Greek pérnēmi "I sell" and Sanskrit pṛṇāti "he grants, bestows". The semantic development is elegant: what is sold or handed over is a portion of one's goods; hence "a portion, a share". The noun pars is attested throughout Latin literature from Plautus (3rd–2nd c. BCE) onward in exactly this sense: "a part of the whole, a share, a faction, a side". Classical Latin already carried the full polysemy that English would inherit — pars could be a piece of land, a role in a play (partēs "the parts of a drama"), a side in a dispute (in utramque partem "on either side"), and a district or region (in partibus "in the regions of").

From Latin to Old French. The accusative partem passed into Gallo-Romance as the ancestor of Old French part, attested from the ninth century, with the same range of senses: "portion, share, division, side, party". The verb developed in parallel: Latin partīre "to divide, to share out" gave Old French partir, which added to "divide, share" the intransitive sense "to go away, to depart" — already by the twelfth century partir meant "to leave, to set out", a sense that English would borrow wholesale. The Norman Conquest brought both words into Middle English: the noun part is recorded around 1200, and the verb parten shortly after, in the senses "to divide, to separate, to share out".

Middle English growth. Middle English expanded the noun with extraordinary speed. By the thirteenth century part meant a portion of a whole, a share in an undertaking, a side in an argument, a region of the world (in those parts), and a component of a person (mortal parts, "the body"). Chaucer uses the word across this whole range in the late fourteenth century. The verb developed the crucial personal sense: "to part from" — to leave, to take leave of — appears in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, and with it the phrasal "to part with" meaning "to give up, to relinquish", attested by the fifteenth century. The farewell sense was ordinary, everyday English for centuries: people parted from friends, from life, and from their goods.

Early Modern and modern English. The fifteenth and sixteenth centuries added part and parcel (a legal doublet meaning "an integral component"), for the most part, and the theatrical specialization of part as "an actor's role" — Shakespeare's "play thy part". The grammar sense part of speech is classical in origin and settled in English pedagogical usage by the seventeenth century. The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries industrialized the word: spare parts, part-time, to part company, and the American part for the division of the hair. Meanwhile the verb's everyday farewell sense slowly retreated into formal and literary registers: by the twentieth century one said "leave" in ordinary speech, and part carried a weight of ceremony — "we parted as friends", "parting is such sweet sorrow". It is exactly this dignified, archaic colour that Tolkien exploits when Bilbo says he wishes "to part in friendship" from Thorin: the word performs a formal farewell, the closing of a relationship, with all the gravity of its long history.

The family today. Modern English keeps part as one of its highest-frequency nouns, and its Latin family has ramified into party (a group of people sharing a side or purpose), partner, apart ("to the side"), depart, partition, particle, particular, partial, and participate — all radiating from the PIE notion of a handed-over share. The word's trajectory, from marketplace root to global abstraction, is a model case of how a single Indo-European root can saturate an entire modern vocabulary while retaining, in phrases like "to part in friendship", the ancient human act of dividing company.
