English viol — in the plural, viols, the form Tolkien uses — is a Renaissance-era borrowing whose ancestry runs through the Romance languages into Medieval Latin and then stops. Unlike the words for harp or dungeon, it cannot be marched back to a tidy Proto-Indo-European root: the instrument-name vitula surfaces in Medieval Latin with its own origin already obscure. An honest etymological essay must therefore begin not with a PIE asterisk but with a confession of uncertainty, and then build the secure historical chain from the medieval fiddle-name forward.

The obscure Medieval Latin birth

Medieval Latin vitula names a stringed, bowed instrument — a fiddle-type — in the glosses and literary texts of the early Middle Ages. Its origin is debated in the scholarship. The most frequently repeated hypothesis connects it to Latin vitulārī, “to celebrate, exult, leap for joy,” the festive-cult verb (with the minor goddess Vitula, “Joy,” standing behind it): under this reading the instrument was “the festive one,” the thing that makes music for celebration. The alternative is that vitula is a pre-Romance, possibly substrate, instrument-name — an old cultural word of the type that folk-music and dance vocabularies often preserve without a recoverable etymology. Handbooks honestly flag the question as unresolved; no sound PIE equation has won consensus. The responsible treatment is to say so plainly and move on to what is attested.

The Romance journey: Provence, France, Italy

  • Old Provençal / Occitan viola (attested 11th–12th c.): the troubadour world knew the bowed instrument as viola; the Provençal court culture of lyric and music was precisely the milieu in which the word spread.
  • Old French viole (12th c.) and Middle French viole: the word entered French as the standard name of a bowed string instrument, ancestor of the English borrowing.
  • Italian viola and violino: in Italy the same stem produced viola and, by diminutive, violino (“little viola”) — which eventually named the very different, unfretted instrument we call the violin, whose family (violin, viola, violoncello) later overwhelmed the older viol consort in popularity.

The name-family is thus a classic tangle: modern violin, viola, and cello share a stem with viol but name a different instrument type. English sorted the terms late and neatly — viol for the historical fretted consort, violin/viola/cello for the later family.

English: the Tudor-Stuart consort

English borrowed viol around 1500, and by the Elizabethan and Stuart periods the instrument had become the summit of refined amateur and court music. A “consort of viols” — a matched set of treble, tenor, and bass instruments — was the standard chamber ensemble of the English gentry; the viola da gamba, held between the legs, was the gentleman's instrument par excellence. Composers like William Byrd, John Dowland, Orlando Gibbons, Henry Lawes, and Matthew Locke wrote for viol consorts. The word appears throughout the period's musical writing, inventories, and household records. It was during this age that the plural viols became a normal inventory form: a set, not a single instrument.

The violin's triumph and the word's retreat

Through the 18th century the louder, more brilliant violin family displaced the viol consort in concert life; the viol became an antiquarian curiosity, and the word viol retreated from living musical vocabulary into histories and instrument catalogues. By the 19th century it was, to most ears, a word for an obsolete instrument — an old word with an archaic, ceremonial ring. This historical eclipse is exactly why, two centuries later, Tolkien could use it for deliberate archaism.

The 20th-century revival

The early-music movement of the 20th century (Arnold Dolmetsch and his heirs) rebuilt viols, revived consort playing, and returned the word to living use: today viols are played worldwide in consorts and continuo groups. The word's connotative weight, however, is still the accumulated history: courtly, consort-based, pre-violin, ceremonial — the sound-world of an older, quieter music.

In The Hobbit

When Dwalin and Balin bring back “viols as big as themselves” beside Thorin's cloth-wrapped harp, Tolkien is doing two things at once. He is staging a comic scale-joke — bass instruments as big as their players, hauled into a hobbit-hole — and he is choosing deliberately archaic instrument-names (viols, harp, fiddles) to make the dwarves' music feel ancient, noble, and folk-epic rather than modern orchestral. The plural form matches the text: a whole consort's worth of huge instruments arriving at Bag End, sounding the depths that will soon open the “Far over the Misty Mountains” song. The word viols carries four centuries of court-music prestige into that small, crowded room.
