CHAIN 1 — the root odd: *uzdaz — Proto-Germanic, 'point, pointed thing,' a North-West Germanic isogloss whose deeper Indo-European ancestry is NOT securely established (no reliable PIE reconstruction exists for it). → oddi — Old Norse, 'point; triangle; the third or odd man' — the 'point' transferred to the projecting vertex of a triangle, and then to the extra third party whose casting vote decides a tied dispute. → odde — Middle English, borrowed c. 1300, 'odd, not even, leaving a remainder' (the arithmetic sense, of a number that leaves a remainder of one when divided by two). → odd — Early Modern English, extended senses: 'surplus, extra, unmatched' (the odd glove, an odd job) and, from the 16th c., 'strange, singular, peculiar.' → oddment — formed 1780 by suffixation, 'an odd article; a leftover; an extra.' → oddments — the plural, recorded from the late 18th c., 'odds and ends; miscellaneous remnants,' the form in 'He found oddments in his pockets.' CHAIN 2 — the suffix -ment: -mentum — Latin, a suffix forming nouns of instrument, medium, or result (ornamentum, fundamentum). → -ment — Old French, the same suffix in the vernacular. → -ment — Middle English, borrowed with Anglo-French loanwords from the 13th c. and soon productive on native English bases (enjoyment, atonement, bewilderment). → odd + -ment — oddment (1780), 'a thin/odd thing' — literally 'an odd thing,' a noun of result naming the leftover that results from sorting or using a larger supply. → oddments — the plural. NOTE ON DEBATE: the *uzdaz root is confined to North-West Germanic (Old English ord, Old Saxon ord, Old High German ort, Old Norse oddr), with no secure Indo-European relatives, so the first chain stops at Germanic rather than PIE; the 'strange' and 'surplus' senses of odd are English-only developments of the borrowed word, not features of the Scandinavian original.
