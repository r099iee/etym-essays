The history of grumbled begins in the mouth, or rather in the ear: it is one of those words that English calls echoic or onomatopoeic, in which the sound of the word is the sound of the thing. The base is the syllable grum-, the low, dull, rolling noise that thunder makes on a far horizon and that an empty stomach makes in a quiet room. Medieval Europe built this syllable into verbs in its own way: Middle Dutch had grommen, "to growl, grumble, grunt", with the frequentative grommelen; Middle French had grommeler, "to murmur, mutter", another frequentative that repeats the small sound over and over; German produced grummeln on the same pattern. These are not ordinary loans from one language to another but parallel births of the same echo: Germanic and Romance speakers, hearing the same rumble in the throat, independently turned the noise into the name of the noise. English adopted grumble around 1580, in the age of Shakespeare, and the earliest recorded senses are of sound rather than of complaint: thunder grumbles, the belly grumbles, a cart grumbles over stones. Only gradually did the word's centre of gravity move from the noise to the mood, from "to rumble low" to "to express discontent in a low voice", a transfer so natural that the two senses have never separated, and a grumble remains at once a sound and a state of mind. Key stages and shifts:
  • The echoic base grum-: the low, rolling, repetitive rumble, independently formed across the Germanic and Romance dialects (Middle Dutch grommen, Middle French grommeler, German grummeln).
  • c. 1580: English adopts grumble, first of sounds — thunder, the belly, a cart over stones.
  • 17th century: the sound sense shades into the mood sense; grumble comes to mean "express discontent in a low voice", and the grumbler enters the language.
  • 18th century: the word enters politics — the Grumbletonians (1713), disaffected Whigs who grumbled in Queen Anne's Parliament — and the grump family grows from the same stock.
  • 19th century: "grumbling appendix" appears as medical slang; modern English still hears both the thunder and the complaint.
The word in literature and society: the seventeenth century gave English the grumbler, the person whose habitual discontent finds a low, rumbling voice. The Grumbletonians of Queen Anne's reign carried the word into politics, where the malcontent grumbling in Parliament was a stock figure of the age. The grump family — grumpish (1690s), grump (1720) and grumpy (1778) — grew from the same grumbling stock, turning the sound into a temperament. The nineteenth-century "grumbling appendix" shows how far the word could travel: a chronically troublesome organ that rumbles without words. In the context sentence from The Hobbit, the word works at full strength. "To think it will soon be June!" grumbled Bilbo, as he splashed along behind the others in a very muddy track. It is the end of May, the weather is wet, the track is mud, and Bilbo, a hobbit who loves his armchair, his fire and his pantry, splashes along and grumbles that June will soon be here. The grumble is the authentic noise of the displaced homebody: low, rueful, not quite a complaint against anyone, addressed to no one in particular. It is comic rather than bitter, and Tolkien lets us hear the small rumble of discomfort that will, by the end of the book, have hardened into the resolve of a dragon-killer. The verb's rumbling quality quietly suits a story that moves toward thunder, storms and the wildness of the Misty Mountains: the same sound that rumbles in Bilbo's throat rumbles in the weather of the world, and grumbled binds the mood of the man to the mood of the story.
