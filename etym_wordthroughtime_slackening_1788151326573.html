- [16th c.] - Verb slacken coined from slack + -en; “slackening” appears as participle and gerund for loosening ropes and sails; register nautical and practical.
- [17th–18th c.] - Extends to pace, effort, discipline: “a slackening of diligence” names a moral or practical easing; sermons and conduct books give the word a mildly censorious tone.
- [19th c.] - Industrial and journalistic prose use the noun for the slowing of trade, demand, or growth; the word gains an economic register.
- [20th c.] - Weather reports and economics adopt “slackening” as a standard analytical term for easing winds, output, and demand; tone becomes neutral and technical.
- [21st c.] - The word is now as natural in macroeconomics (“slackening demand”) as in navigation (“the slackening of the tide”), always picturing a taut line letting go its strain.
