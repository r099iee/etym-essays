SNUFFLING is the present participle of the verb SNUFFLE, and in the passage from The Hobbit it denotes the act of drawing air audibly and repeatedly through the nose while searching for a scent. Its tone is deliberately animalistic and predatory: the Wargs snuffle as they methodically "smell out" every tree that conceals a victim; the wet, laborious, inhalatory sound implied by the word turns their search into an almost tactile menace, so that the reader hears the hunters working through the night, tree by tree, with the patience of hounds that have all night and know they will succeed. Unlike a single sniff, snuffling is iterative and prolonged, suggesting both a keen, dog-like olfactory sense and a grim, unhurried certainty that no hiding place will escape them; the noise is intimate and close, and it is precisely this closeness that heightens Bilbo's helplessness as the pack closes in on his tree. The present participle -ing makes the action continuous and ongoing — they are snuffling even as we read — and the word's damp, nasal music (snuf-fl-ing) is itself the sound it names, a rare onomatopoeic exactness that lets Tolkien's prose do its work through the reader's ear rather than the page.

Snuffle is a word of many small faces, and SNUFFLING inherits them all:
  • Sense 1 — noisy nasal breathing through congestion (the everyday core): to breathe in a noisy, wet, congested way through the nose, especially because of a cold; "a snuffling child", "the snuffles" as a homely name for the common cold. This is the oldest attested register of the word in English.
  • Sense 2 — animal scenting and pursuit (the sense in the card): to smell about, to nose out a scent, drawing air repeatedly through the nose while searching for a trail; the word of hounds, wolves, and bears working a scent — the Wargs go snuffling about each tree until they have "smelt out" every hidden victim.
  • Sense 3 — nasal, affected, or whining speech (social side-sense): to speak in a nasal, affected, or plaintive tone, "a snuffling voice"; in the 18th–19th centuries the word could mock preachers and pious talk, so snuffling carries a faintly comic or contemptuous edge in this register.
  • Sense 4 — the noun, a snuffle: a single act or sound of snuffling — a wet nasal inhalation, or the repeated congested breathing of "the snuffles"; the noun often appears in the plural, "snuffles", for a head cold.
  • Sense 5 — snooping and nosing about (transferred figurative sense): to snuffle for information is to nose about secretively, the word of the dog extended to human curiosity and intrusion; it overlaps with the older sense of "snuffing" out something hidden.
  • Sense 6 — of inanimate things (rare transferred sense): a damp wind or a quenched candle can be said to snuffle, the sound of the word attaching itself to any wet, choking, repeated noise.

The historical bridge is built on sound. SNUFFLE belongs to the great Germanic nasal word-family — sniff, snuff, snout, snot, snivel, snore, snort — all built on an imitative base that transcribes the wet, congested drawing of air through the nose. The verb is a frequentative: to snuff (to inhale through the nostrils) plus the -le suffix of repeated or continued action (as in waddle, crackle, wrestle) yields snuffle, a word that no longer describes a single inhalation but a whole series of small, wet, laborious ones. The same pattern appears independently across the Germanic languages — Dutch snuffelen "to snoop, sniff", German schnüffeln "to sniff, snoop" — so snuffling is at once a native English formation and a member of a Europe-wide family of onomatopoeia.

In register, snuffling is strongly physical and onomatopoeic, and in narrative prose it is rarely neutral: it carries a predatory or congested undertone, faintly comic when applied to a stuffy human nose, faintly menacing when applied to a hunting beast. It contrasts with the short, quiet, voluntary sniffing, the explosive snorting, and the sleep-bound snoring. In grammar it is a regular verb (snuffle, snuffled, snuffling), and the present-participle form SNUFFLING is the word at its most continuous — the search that will not end until every tree has been smelt out.
