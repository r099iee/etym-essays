Chorus means, at its simplest, many voices sounding together as one, and the group that makes them, and the passage they sing together. It names both the singers and the song: a band performing in unison, and the refrain designed for unison. From this musical core it extends to any simultaneous collective utterance and to theatrical ensembles.

In classical Athenian drama the chorus was a masked band of citizens who sang and danced between scenes, narrating, moralizing, and reacting on behalf of the community. In modern theatre and musical comedy the chorus is the supporting ensemble behind the principals: singers and dancers who fill crowd scenes, echo numbers, and provide spectacle. In pop music the chorus is the hooky repeated section after verses that the audience remembers and sings back.

The source sentence uses the theatrical sense dismissively: to look like "one of the chorus of male guests" is to look like an interchangeable background gentleman, handsome and well-dressed but indistinct, one of many tuxedoed figures arranged for effect in a country-house act rather than a leading man with character.

  • Group singing together / choir (musical ensemble): a church chorus, a school chorus, a male-voice chorus. The semantic bridge from Greek dancing ground is metonymy: place > performers in the place > sound they make. Here chorus overlaps with choir but often implies larger, less formal, or secular forces.
  • Refrain or repeated section of a song (musical form): verse-chorus structure, to join in on the chorus. This develops because refrains are precisely the parts assigned to the whole band plus audience; the singers' name transfers to their passage.
  • Classical dramatic device - masked commentators in tragedy/comedy: the chorus in Oedipus, in Lysistrata. This preserves the ancient function: collective wisdom interrupting heroic action with song.
  • Theatrical ensemble behind principals (chorus line, chorus girls/boys): the dancing chorus, to join the chorus. In musical comedy this is often synchronized, decorative, and deliberately uniform; hence the insult of being merely chorus rather than a star.
  • Any simultaneous unanimous utterance (figurative): a chorus of approval, protest, laughter, groans. The bridge is acoustic: as singers blend, so complaints or praise blend into a single perceived voice. This sense needs no music at all.
  • Biological / acoustic - dawn chorus and similar: the dawn chorus of birds singing together at first light. Ornithologists borrowed the musical term for the overwhelming simultaneity of territorial song.
  • Backing support more generally: to act in chorus with others, to speak in chorus. To do something in chorus is to do it jointly and audibly, emphasizing agreement or drill.

Connotation depends on context: musical chorus is warm and participatory (everyone invited to sing), classical chorus is grave and communal (the city speaking), theatrical chorus can be glamorous or, as here, diminishing (background rather than lead), and figurative chorus can be approving (a chorus of praise) or oppressive (a chorus of condemnation). What unites them is plurality fused into unity.

In short, chorus means a group sounding or acting together, the refrain they share, and by extension any unanimous outburst.
