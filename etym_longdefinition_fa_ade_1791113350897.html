Façade, also spelled facade without the cedilla, denotes in its literal sense the principal front of a building, the exterior face designed to be seen, and in its far more common figurative sense, an outward appearance maintained to conceal a less pleasant reality.

Architecturally, a façade is the composed elevation facing a street, square or courtyard: its materials, windows, doors, orders, cornices and ornament are orchestrated for effect, while side and rear walls may be plain. Architects speak of stone façade, glass façade, curtain-wall façade, south façade and façade retention where only the historic front is preserved. The term implies design intentionality and public address; every building has walls, but only its show-face is its façade.

  • Literal architectural sense: the front exterior. This is neutral and technical, used in planning, conservation and construction. It can be praised or criticized for honesty, proportion or pastiche, but it simply describes orientation and treatment.
  • Figurative deceptive sense: a false, superficial or misleading appearance. Collocations include a façade of respectability, unity, calm, confidence, legality and verbs like maintain, preserve, keep up, drop, see through, penetrate a façade. Here the connotation is pejorative: pretence, hypocrisy, Potemkin polish. That is the sense activated in the source sentence, where cultural monuments are the city's handsome front masking rear squalor.
  • Social-psychological sense: the persona or front a person presents in interaction, close to Goffman's sociological presentation of self. To say his bravado is just a façade means the inner state differs sharply from outer display.
  • Technical extended senses: in computing, a façade pattern provides a simplified interface hiding complex subsystems; in linguistics and set design, a façade can be a flat front with nothing behind. Both preserve the logic of front-hiding-depth.

Polysemy developed by transparent metaphor: buildings have honest backs and dressed fronts, so people and institutions do too. The bridge was 19th-century urban criticism contrasting grand boulevards with slums, exactly the cathedral-capitol-museum versus shambles opposition here. Once façade meant show-front in stone, it easily meant show-front in manners.

Usage notes: pronunciation stresses the second syllable, /fəˈsɑːd/, with silent e; the cedilla is optional in English but preferred in careful style. Register is slightly literary in the figurative sense; front or show would be plainer. Adjectives routinely paired are grand, imposing, elegant, respectable, false, thin, mere. The word almost always implies a contrast between seen and hidden, making it ideal for critiques of civic boosterism, personal hypocrisy or corporate polish.

In short, façade unites architecture and ethics: what faces the street and what faces the world, and what both may be hiding.
