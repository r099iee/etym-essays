Part I — the history of “passion”.

1. The Indo-European root of suffering. The chain begins with the Indo-European root *peh₁-, “to suffer, hurt”, best known through its Greek reflex πῆμα (pēma), “suffering, calamity, misery” — the word Homer uses for the griefs his heroes endure. Reconstruction of the root is routine in the handbooks, though its exact shape (*peh₁- vs. extended dental forms) is debated; the Latin side is not in doubt. Latin inherits the verb patior, patī, “to suffer, undergo, endure, be affected by”, attested from the third century BCE — the verb behind patiens “patient” and patibilis “capable of suffering”.

2. Latin passio: from philosophy to theology. From patior Latin formed the noun passiō, passiōnis, “suffering”, literally “a being-affected”. In the first century BCE Cicero used passio as a calque for the Greek philosophical term πάθος (pathos), “emotion, feeling, experience” — so in classical philosophical Latin the word already names the emotions themselves. Christian Latin then worked a decisive narrowing and deepening: passio became the technical name of Christ’s suffering and crucifixion — Passio Christi — in the Vulgate and the entire patristic tradition (Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine). Medieval liturgy, sermons and passion-plays made the Passion the heart of western Christian piety; within Christianity the word is not any suffering but THE suffering, the redemptive agony of Good Friday.

3. Old French and Middle English: two senses at once. Old French takes passiun, passion with both loads — the liturgical “Passion of Christ” and the general sense “suffering, affliction, strong emotion”. Middle English inherits both in the thirteenth century: the Ancrene Wisse (c. 1225) speaks of the Passion of Christ, and Chaucer’s poetry uses “passioun” of intense feelings of every kind. The decisive widening comes in the sixteenth century: “passion” acquires its modern emotional spectrum — ardent love and desire (Shakespeare’s lovers are full of passion), zeal and enthusiasm (“a passion for music”), and the older “anger”, still heard in “to fly into a passion”. By 1600 the devotional sense and the amorous sense coexist in one word, and the stage is set for the compound’s later ambiguity.

Part II — the history of “flower”.

4. An Indo-European blooming: from *bʰleh₃- to blossom. The second root is *bʰleh₃-, “to bloom, blossom, flourish”, whose Germanic reflex blōwan gives English “blow” (archaic “to bloom”) and “bloom” itself. Its Italic reflex is flōs, flōris “flower, blossom” and “the finest part of anything” (the “flower” of an army, of youth, of wheat). The Latin word passed into Old French as flor (nominative) and flour (oblique), and Middle English borrowed it in the thirteenth century as flour / flour-es, “blossom” — and, following the Latin “finest part” sense, “the flower of wheat”, i.e. fine meal. For four centuries “flour” and “flower” were one word: Chaucer’s flour is at once blossom and finest meal. Modern English split the pair: “flower” (blossom) kept the etymological spelling restored toward Latin flōr- in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, while “flour” (meal) retained the old oblique spelling; the two are still homophones in most accents, including RP, where both are /ˈflaʊ.ə/.

5. The seventeenth-century union: naming the flower of the Passion. The compound “passion-flower” is a calque of flos passionis, and its making belongs to the age of Catholic missionary botany. In the Americas, Spanish missionaries found a flower of startling structure — a corona of filaments radiating round a central column — and read it as a natural emblem of the Crucifixion: ten petal-parts for the ten faithful apostles, the corona for the crown of thorns, three stigmas for the three nails, five anthers for the five wounds, tendrils for the scourges, the round fruit for the world redeemed. The Italian scholar Jacomo Bosio published the full emblematic account in his treatise on the Cross (1610), and the reading spread through Catholic Europe, entering English about the 1640s as “passion-flower”. Linnaeus gave the genus its formal Latin name, Passiflora, in the Species Plantarum (1753) — the devotional name, secularised into systematics.

6. Drift in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In the Victorian era the passion-flower was a conservatory marvel and an emblem of faith in “the language of flowers”; the fruit, long known in Spanish as granadilla, was marketed in English as “passion fruit” from the late nineteenth century — named for the flower’s Passion symbolism, not for any culinary ardour. Pharmacognosy took up Passiflora incarnata as a mild sedative at the turn of the twentieth century, and “passionflower” entered the supplement trade in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries as an anxiolytic. Meanwhile popular etymology performed its quiet reversal: as “passion” in ordinary speech came to mean intense love and desire, the compound was re-read as “the flower of romantic passion” — a folk re-derivation that flatters the flower’s beauty and contradicts its religious origin. The name today thus carries three strata at once: the Greek philosophical “emotion”, the Christian “suffering of Christ”, and the modern romantic “desire” — an entire history of the word “passion” folded into a single botanical compound.
