The origin of shrivel is genuinely obscure — a rare case in which even the family line is uncertain — but the scholarly consensus points to Scandinavia. The word appears suddenly in English around the middle of the sixteenth century, first as shrivell, and its nearest relatives are Scandinavian dialect words: Old Swedish skryvla, "to wrinkle, shrivel", modern Swedish dialectal skryvla, "to wrinkle, crease, crumple", and similar Norwegian forms meaning "to curl up, shrivel". These North Germanic words suggest a formation built on a base meaning "to shrink or draw together with folds", applied to skin, fruit, and leaves that pucker and harden as moisture leaves them. Because no cognates are securely attested outside North Germanic, no Proto-Indo-European ancestor can be reconstructed, and the word must be classed as of unknown or isolate origin within the modern vocabulary: the raw material of Scandinavian dialect passed into literary English without a PIE pedigree. This is not a failure of scholarship but a fact of the record — many homely, bodily words entered English from dialects and dialects from Scandinavia, and some carry no older etymology at all. Its entry into English was almost certainly through everyday speech rather than the written word. The word smells of the farmyard, the kitchen, and the winter hearth — of apples stored too long and shrivelling in a dark loft, of leaves under an autumn sun, of a hand left too near a fire. Early attestations already show the full modern sense of contraction into harsh, rigid folds under heat or dryness, and the register was from the first naturalistic and bodily. English speakers fitted the borrowing to a native pattern: verbs ending in -el or -le are often frequentative or diminutive — compare wrestle, twinkle, sparkle, crumple — and shrivel may have taken the suffix early, giving the crumpling action its shape and its sound. The word itself seems to enact its meaning: the sibilant opening (shr-), the hard medial -v-, and the snapping final -l mimic the pinching and folding of drying skin, a sound-symbolic quality that may be one reason the word survived so vigorously. Once established, the verb specialized quickly. From the start it meant physical contraction by heat, dryness, or age, and its subjects were overwhelmingly plant and flesh: shrivelled leaves, shrivelled roots, shrivelled fruit, shrivelled skin. The Reformation and post-Reformation centuries, with their heightened rhetoric of damnation and decay, found the word useful: sermons and religious prose described souls shrivelling in sin, and the verb acquired a moral, admonitory colouring that it has never entirely lost. In the same period, and throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, naturalists used it of plants and bodies with increasing precision, so that the word moved between the pulpit, the kitchen, the garden, and the anatomical treatise. The figurative extension from plants to persons and powers is the word's most important later development. From the eighteenth century onward, hopes, courage, spirits, and ambitions could shrivel — the inner life pictured as a moist and tender thing that dries and folds under adversity or disgrace. "My courage shrivelled", "his reputation shrivelled", "the dream shrivelled to nothing": the verb became the standard name for a quiet, desiccating kind of failure, distinct from the sudden crash of a collapse and the slow fading of a withering. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries this figurative sense settled into journalism and fiction, where shrivelling economies, shrivelling careers, and shrivelling men became commonplace, always with the underlying image of moisture and fullness being lost. Socio-linguistically, shrivel is a word of the body and the ground, and its register reflects that. It is standard English, but it is never formal or clinical; the medical word for the same process is dehydration or desiccation, and the polite synonym is shrink. Shrivel keeps its folk roots: it is the word a farmer uses of a blighted crop, a cook of a withered apple, a writer of a face lined by the sun. Its phonetic texture — the drawn-out sibilant, the abrupt final consonant — makes it a natural rhyme for scorch, and the two words have been paired in English ever since, most memorably in the goblins' chant from The Hobbit, where "Shrivel and scorch!" commands a double destruction: the surface seared black, the substance crumpled and dead. The history of the word is thus a history of the homely made sinister — a kitchen-and-garden verb, born in Scandinavian dialect, that has served the vocabulary of age, ruin, and apocalyptic fire without ever losing its smell of drying leaves and old apples.
