- Latin tremulus — Neutral descriptive "shaking, quivering"; physical and fearful applications in classical usage.
- Pre-English Romance — French trémuleux and trembler keep the family alive in vernacular Europe; English first naturalizes tremble.
- Early 17th-century English — Tremulous enters as a learned adjective for bodily and physical vibration; still close to medical/natural-philosophical tone.
- 18th–19th centuries — Perception shifts strongly literary: the word becomes a favorite for emotional voices, delicate light, and sentimental or gothic fragility. Public of readers associates it with refined prose feeling.
- 20th–21st centuries — Remains literary-elevated; slightly old-fashioned in speech, fully current on the page. Can feel mannered if overused. No taboo. The exact form tremulous still signals "fine-grained emotional/physical quiver," not violent shaking or slang intensity (unlike tremendous).
