Evolution essay for FLUE A word whose beginning is lost. The biography of flue is unusual because its beginning is genuinely unknown. The word surfaces in English records around 1580 already in its modern sense — "the smoke-passage of a chimney" — with no Old English ancestor and no secure continental relative. This absence of a documented ancestry has given the word a dual personality: every history of it must present two candidate lineages, and no dictionary can pronounce one the winner. The uncertainty is not a failure of scholarship but a fact about the word's origin, and it is worth stating plainly rather than papered over. The Romance candidate: smoke as flowing water. The first lineage points to Old French flue, a noun meaning "a flowing, a stream, a current," built on the verb fluer, "to flow," which is the regular development of Latin fluere, "to flow, run, stream." Latin fluere itself descends from the Indo-European root *bʰlew-, "to flow, swell, run over," the ancestor of a remarkable family in English: fluid, fluent, flux, fluctuation, influx, and refluence. Under this account, a flue is etymologically "the place where smoke flows" — a sixteenth-century borrowing drawn from the French vocabulary of running water and applied by analogy to the running of smoke. The account is attractive because it matches the very thing a flue does: smoke and hot gases flow up it, exactly as water flows in a stream. It also fits the sixteenth-century linguistic fashion, in which English technical vocabulary borrowed freely from French. The Germanic candidate: the tube and the horn. The second lineage points to an English word already in the language. Middle English flewe, recorded from about 1400, meant "the mouthpiece of a hunting horn" or, more generally, "a pipe, a tube." If this is the true source, flue began as the name of a narrow hollow passage — the bore of a horn — and was extended to the tubular passage of a chimney. No secure Indo-European ancestry is claimed for flewe; it may be onomatopoeic, imitating the sound of blowing, which would make it one of the oldest and humblest kinds of word, a name made by the noise of the thing. The two candidates may well have reinforced one another — the Romance "flow" explaining the chimney sense, the native "tube" the pipe sense — and the honest verdict of the dictionaries is that the etymology is uncertain. The word's dual biography is, in miniature, the story of English itself: a native channel-word and a French current-word, converging on one small shaft of masonry. The social-technical context: the age of the English chimney. What is certain is the world in which flue became necessary. The sixteenth century was the great age of the enclosed English chimney. In medieval halls, smoke escaped through a louver in the roof or through a simple opening; the fully enclosed chimney with a flue — a vertical shaft, lined to carry smoke safely through walls and floors — became standard in Elizabethan and Jacobean building, and the new architecture demanded a word for the new part. Flue enters the record precisely when the enclosed fireplace was transforming English domestic life, and it entered carrying the whole vocabulary of the hearth: hearth, grate, fender, andiron, damper, and flue. The word was born technical and has never left the company of builders and engineers. The soot era. By the eighteenth century the word had become indispensable to the trade of the chimney sweep — the narrow, soot-blackened shaft up which the climbing boys were sent, the funnel of creosote that caught fire in the great urban chimney-fires. In Victorian literature the flue acquired its darkest colouring: Dickens's sweeps climb soot-blackened flues; Gothic houses moan with drafts in their flues; the townhouse hides a flue behind every fireplace, so that the word carries the sense of the mechanical sublime — a narrow, unvisited, essential channel, out of sight and doing essential work. This sooty resonance is the word's emotional inheritance, and it is what makes the clinical noun flue capable of Gothic shuddering in fiction. Extensions: music, machinery, and the modern engineering term. The same image of a channel has carried the word into other trades. In organ-building, a "flue pipe" is the flute-class pipe voiced by a windway — the "flue" — that directs a sheet of air against a lip, so that the organ's most characteristic tone is produced by a flue; here the word names both the pipe and its narrow wind-channel, exactly the conduit-sense of the old flewe. In modern industry the word has been extended from hearths to boilers, furnaces, and power stations: the boiler flue and furnace flue are everyday engineering terms, and "flue gas" — the exhaust stream of combustion, rich in carbon dioxide — has become a term of environmental science, treated by flue-gas desulphurisation and, in the climate age, by flue-gas carbon capture. The word has travelled, in four centuries, from the mouthpiece of a hunting horn and the stream of Old French to the inside of a power station, always naming the same thing: a narrow channel for passage. Register and confusions today. Modern flue is a fully standard, engineering-grade noun — no longer colloquial, purely functional. Its everyday home is the householder's annual inspection ("a blocked flue can cause deadly carbon monoxide to seep back into the living space"), its technical home is heating engineering and power generation, and its literary home is the sooty resonance of the Victorian house. Two confusions shadow it: with flu (influenza), a pure spelling accident of homophones; and with the unrelated English word flue meaning "soft down or fuzz" (from Old French flou, "soft, fluffy"), a distinct etymon that coincidentally shares the spelling. The word's isolation in English — with German Rauchfang, Dutch schoorsteen, and Hindi dhūmranāl all unrelated — is itself part of its character: flue is a late, self-made English formation, built in the sixteenth-century building boom, whose ancestry, like the chimney it names, hides a darkness that no one has fully explored.
