1. Hebrew origins: a throne-name of Judah. The name Joachim begins as the Hebrew throne-name Yəhōyāqīm, "Yahweh raises up" — a compound of the divine name YHWH and the hiphil of the verb qūm, "to rise, stand". Its most famous early bearer was the king of Judah (609–598 BCE) installed by Pharaoh Necho and later crushed by Nebuchadnezzar; Jeremiah’s curses and the Books of Kings make his reign a study in compromised politics. The very formation of the name — theophoric, claiming divine support — was a pattern of royal naming throughout Judah.

2. Into Greek and Latin: the name becomes two saints. The Septuagint rend(ers) the name as Iōakeim, and the Vulgate Latin of Jerome gives Ioachim. With late antique and medieval Christianity the name multiplies its referents: the Gospel of Matthew names Joachim (Greek Iōakeim) among the ancestors of Joseph in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:18), and apocryphal tradition (the Protevangelium of James) makes Saint Joachim the father of the Virgin Mary. Through the Mediterranean cult of the Virgin’s parents, the name entered the mainstream of Christian onomastics, borne by fathers, monks, and kings; by the high Middle Ages Joachim was a standard European given name of respectable, pious sound.

  • 12th century: Joachim of Fiore (d. 1202), the Calabrian abbot, predicts an Age of the Spirit; his three ages of Father, Son, and Spirit colour apocalyptic thought for centuries, and his name becomes shorthand for prophetic history.
  • 16th century: the name crosses into French literary history with Joachim du Bellay (c. 1522–1560), whose Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549), written with the Pléiade circle of Ronsard, argues that French can equal the classics — a founding act of French Renaissance literature.
  • 18th–19th centuries: Gioacchino Rossini (1792–1868) carries the Italian form into music; Napoleon’s marshal Joachim Murat into military fame.

3. Pater’s Joachim: the poetry of exile and decline. In Walter Pater’s The Renaissance (1873), Joachim du Bellay appears as the perfect illustration of the Renaissance "putting forth in France an aftermath" — a late, exquisite growth. Pater dwells on du Bellay’s sonnets of Rome (Les Antiquitez de Rome, 1558) and the longing of Les Regrets: the poet lynching after home. For Pater, du Bellay’s "refined and comely decadence" — sweetness and melancholy in exact balance — makes him the herald of the aesthetic movement’s own tastes. The poem Heureux qui, comme Ulysse, celebrating the village of home against the grandeur of Rome, has remained the best-loved French sonnet of its age.

4. Modern standing. As a name, Joachim has settled into dignified rarity in English, with the cognates Joaquín, Gioacchino, Joaquim, and Akim living full lives elsewhere. In literary history it stands, above all, for du Bellay — the poet of the Pléiade, of the French language’s self-assertion, and of the melancholy sweetness Pater made the signature of a Renaissance in its autumn.
