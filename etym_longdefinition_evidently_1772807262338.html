evidently — adverb meaning 'in a way that is clearly seen or understood; obviously, plainly; from the evidence available'. In the source text, the plaintiff reports that Xue Pan, the Nanking boss, 'evidently thought that by money and influence he could get away with anything'. The adverb does not report a hidden private thought recovered by the speaker; it presents Xue's assumption of impunity as something so clear from his conduct that any observer could read it. The tone is damning rather than neutral: the word marks arrogant entitlement as open, almost performative, and so tightens the charge that this boss acted without fear of law or consequence.

Polysemy — shades of meaning in current English:
  • Manner sense — 'plainly, clearly': in an evident manner, in a way that is plain to sight or understanding ('he was evidently angry'). Here the adverb grades how the state shows itself.
  • Sentence-adverb / inferential sense — 'to all appearances, from the evidence': positioned at the start or before the verb to introduce a conclusion drawn from what is manifest ('Evidently, the plan had failed'; 'she evidently knew the truth'). This is the dominant modern use and the source-text sense.
  • Softening nuance — 'so it seems, apparently': in informal and spoken English, evidently can shade toward a hedged claim, nearly equivalent to 'apparently', when the speaker is presenting an inference rather than a certainty; formal usage resists this weakening and keeps 'evidently' stronger than 'apparently'.
  • Logical/discourse use: in academic and argumentative prose, 'evidently' flags a proposition taken as demonstrated or obvious from the preceding evidence ('Evidently, P implies Q'), functioning as a reasoning marker.
  • Legal-adjacent use: through its link to 'evidence', the adverb can carry a quasi-judicial flavour of proof on the record, appropriate to testimony, reports, and forensic narrative — the register of the plaintiff's account.
Boundaries. Obviously is the everyday twin and often interchangeable, but it can sound more conversational and less judicial; apparently introduces a weaker, more provisional judgement — 'so it seems' — and can hedge against error, whereas evidently claims the conclusion is grounded in what is plainly shown; manifestly is more formal and legalistic, stressing demonstration; clearly is broader and less tied to the metaphor of vision; patently often collocates with absurdity or wrongness.

Collocations and register. 'evidently thought/believed/intended', 'was evidently + adjective', sentence-initial 'Evidently, ...'. Register ranges from careful narrative prose to courtroom-flavoured English; it is fully standard, neither slangy nor archaic. In this translation it functions as a quiet moral spotlight: Xue's sense that wealth cancels crime is not conjectural but written on his behaviour for all to see. The word does more than 'obviously' would here because it points to the evidence — the money, the influence, the murdered man — that makes the boss's confidence legible.
