- [Celtic & Gallo-Roman Era (c. 5th c. BCE–8th c. CE)] — Celtic *trugos denoted a pitiable, wretched soul; absorbed into Gallo-Romance for destitute beggars — [perception: regarded initially with Christian pity, then with growing societal suspicion of vagrancy]
- [Medieval French & English Period (c. 1200–1500)] — *truant* designated an able-bodied beggar, rogue, or shiftless vagabond who refused honest labor — [perception: severely condemned in church sermons and royal statutes as a parasitic knave]
- [Tudor & Elizabethan Grammar School Era (c. 1550–1650)] — specialized to mean a schoolboy who absents himself without leave ("playing truant") — [perception: viewed by schoolmasters as a rebellious juvenile delinquent; celebrated in popular drama as a mischievous scamp]
- [Romantic Era (c. 1790–1850)] — elevated by Romantic poets (Wordsworth) into an emblem of spirited rebellion against suffocating urban/pedagogical confinement — [perception: romanticized as a noble runaway spirit seeking pure communion with sublime nature]
- [Victorian Compulsory Education Age (c. 1870–1950)] — legally codified under national compulsory schooling acts; hunted down by municipal "truancy officers" — [perception: viewed strictly as a civil infraction and social welfare crisis requiring state intervention]
- [Modern & Contemporary Era (20th–21st c.)] — analyzed through pedagogical psychology and juvenile jurisprudence as unexcused absenteeism — [perception: treated in education as an indicator of systemic disengagement, while retaining its poetic aura of adventurous escape]
