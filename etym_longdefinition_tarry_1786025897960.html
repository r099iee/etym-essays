TARRY is an English verb meaning to delay, to linger, to stay longer than intended, or to remain in expectation of something. It entered the language in the fourteenth century from Anglo-Norman, and for several centuries it was an entirely ordinary word for waiting and delaying; in modern English it has receded into a deliberately literary, archaic, or poetic register, the kind of word a novelist or a preacher reaches for to add weight and age. Its two core notions are intimately connected: to tarry is to be slow, and to be slow is usually to stay somewhere, so the word oscillates between the temporal sense (delaying, procrastinating, being tardy) and the spatial sense (lingering, loitering, staying behind). Because the note records the base verb, it can be conjugated normally - I tarry, he tarries, we tarried, tarrying - and it is used both transitively in older English ("to tarry a person" meaning to delay or detain them) and intransitively in all periods ("do not tarry", "we cannot tarry any longer").

Primary senses and core usage.

  • Sense 1 - To remain, stay, or lodge in a place. The spatial sense: to stay longer than expected, to linger in a place before continuing a journey, to remain behind while others move on. "He decided to tarry in the village for a few more days". This is close to "stay" but with the overtone that the stay is prolonged or deliberate, and usually with the suggestion that one ought to be moving on.
  • Sense 2 - To delay or to be slow in acting. The temporal sense: to put off action, to be tardy, to hesitate before doing what must be done. "We must not tarry if we are to reach the summit before dark". Here the word carries a mild urgency - a warning that delay is dangerous - and in the imperative ("tarry not") it becomes a command to act at once.
  • Sense 3 - To wait, to remain in expectation. The waiting sense, which merges the two above: to stay in a place while expecting someone or something to arrive, happen, or be revealed. This is the sense of the KJV and of devotional prose: "tarry ye here, and watch", "tarry until I come". It is expectation with patience, the willingness to stay until the awaited thing appears.
  • Sense 4 - Archaic transitive sense: to delay or detain someone. In Middle and Early Modern English, tarry could take a direct object: "do not tarry me" = do not hold me back, do not make me late. This sense has died out in modern usage but survives in the fossilized idiom "tarry not" and in historical writing.

Secondary, figurative, and idiomatic meanings.

  • The vexation ghost-sense. The Anglo-Norman source of the word also meant "to irritate, to provoke", and in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries English tarien briefly carried that hostile sense ("to vex, to weary, to anger"). This sense faded by the sixteenth century, but it explains why tarry sometimes appears in old texts where "torment" would be expected - a genuine historical doublet that modern dictionaries still flag.
  • Religious and devotional register. Through the King James Bible, the Book of Common Prayer, and hymns, tarry acquired a devotional colouring: it is the verb of patient waiting upon God. "Tarry here, I pray thee" (2 Kings 2), "wait and tarry" - such phrases gave the word a solemn, reverent resonance that survives today whenever a writer wants a biblical echo.
  • Proverbial and idiomatic use. "Time and tide tarry for no man" is the great proverbial attestation: time and tide wait for no one. The proverb, recorded from the fifteenth century in the form "tyde and tyme is no man..." variants, has kept the word alive in quotation and has made tarry the natural verb for patient expectation that proves vain.
  • Literary and poetic reuse. Because it is now archaic, modern writers use tarry for period flavour, for the cadence of the Bible, or for the simple dignity of slowness. It appears in fantasy and historical fiction precisely because it is no longer everyday speech; its datedness has become a stylistic resource.

Register, tone, and collocation. Tarry is formal, literary, faintly archaic, and uninflected for humour or dignity. It collocates with not ("tarry not"), with behind, with awhile, with expectation, and in biblical echoes with wait. It has no modern jargon senses, but it thrives in historical fiction, sermons, period drama, and poetry, where its slowness is exactly the point. Its antonym is roughly hasten or depart: to tarry is to refuse speed, to insist on staying a little longer.

Historical semantic bridge. The word's journey is from the ordinary to the archaic. In the fourteenth century it was the everyday verb for staying and delaying, and Middle English used it without any special colouring. Through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it remained common, and the Bible fixed its waiting sense in the national memory. By the nineteenth century it had retreated from conversation into literature, and today its archaism is deliberate. The card's sentence - "but we can __________ no longer" - uses the word in its temporal sense with the classic collocation of urgency: the time for waiting is over, and delay itself has become the danger.
