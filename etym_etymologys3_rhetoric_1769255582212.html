The story of rhetoric is the story of how a plain verb for "saying" became the name of the most self-conscious art in the western tradition. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *wer-, "to speak, say" — one of the family's basic words for verbal utterance, witnessed even in the oldest documented branch, Hittite weriye- "to call." The root was remarkably productive across the whole Indo-European world. In the Germanic branch it gave Proto-Germanic *wurdą and thus our everyday word: Gothic waurd, Old English word, German Wort, Old Norse orð. In Italic it produced Latin verbum, whence English verb, verbal, verbatim, and proverb (a "set word" set forth). In Indo-Iranian it yielded Sanskrit vratá, the solemn vow or religious utterance, which survives in Hindi as vrat (व्रत), a vow or fast. Each of these sister branches preserves a slightly different nuance of the root — the everyday word, the grammatical word, the sacred word — but none of them carried the root toward our English noun.

That honour belongs to the Hellenic branch, and to a phonological accident. In Greek the root *wer- produced the verb eírein "to say," whose related forms appeared with an initial rh- (the Greek aspiration on a sonorant). From the root-noun meaning "that which is said" came rhēma "word, utterance" and then rhētōr "public speaker, orator." In the fifth and fourth centuries BCE — the age of the Sophists, of Isocrates, and above all of Aristotle, whose Rhetoric systematised the art — the Greeks coined rhētorikḗ tekhnē, "the orator's craft," from which Latin took rhētorica. The word then passed through Old French rethorique into Middle English in the fourteenth century as rethorike, settling into the modern form rhetoric.

The semantic journey is a study in specialisation. At its root the word simply meant "say"; by the classical age it meant "the systematic art of saying well." The Sophists of the fifth century BCE taught the art of winning arguments and making the weaker case appear the stronger, and were attacked for it by Plato — and the accusation they attracted is the seed of every later suspicion of rhetoric. Isocrates made rhetoric the education of the statesman, the training that fits a man to address the city. Aristotle answered Plato in his Rhetoric (c. 335–323 BCE) by grounding persuasion in reason: the three appeals of ethos, pathos, and logos, and the analysis of argument, arrangement, and style. Through Cicero and Quintilian the art entered Rome and, with it, the western curriculum for two thousand years.

In the medieval West, rhetoric became one of the three arts of the trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric), a fixed school discipline — the inheritance a tutor like Doctor Cornelius would naturally include in a prince's education. In the Renaissance, the Ciceronian revival made rhetoric the master art of public life: the ideal training of the courtier and the statesman, so that "rhetoric" carried pure prestige. It is exactly this medieval-Renaissance register that C. S. Lewis draws on when he lists "Rhetoric" among Caspian's princely subjects in Prince Caspian — the art of persuasive public speech that a future king must command.

The pejorative shift, however, was already latent. Because the art was a teachable technique separable from truth, "mere rhetoric" could be branded as words without substance; from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the phrase hardened, and "rhetoric" took on its second, everyday life as a term of suspicion — "empty rhetoric," "just political rhetoric," "all rhetoric and no substance." The word thus became two-register: on one side the proud classical discipline and the craft of great public speech; on the other, the dismissive label for language that persuades by effect rather than by worth. Both lives descend from the same root — the plain, ancient verb for "say" — and both are alive in the language today.
