- 1600s Early Modern English emergence - used primarily in medical, physical, and botanical contexts to describe feeble bodily motion, faint pulse, or drooping plants; clinical and literal
- 1700s Augustan and Georgian aesthetic shift - adopted in polite literature and satire to depict aristocratic indolence, fashionable ennui, and unhurried salon posture; socio-cultural and stylised
- 1800s Romantic and Victorian literary apex - widely celebrated in poetry and classical novels (Tolstoy, Byron, Wilde) to convey dreamy atmospheric tranquility, aristocratic nonchalance, or world-weary sophistication; poetic and evocative
- 1900s-present Modern prose refinement - settled into a rich literary adverb for graceful slowness, warm ambient stillness, and unhurried, poised detachment; formal and aesthetic
