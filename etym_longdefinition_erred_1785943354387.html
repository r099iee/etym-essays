ERRED: The past tense and past participle of err — made a mistake, committed an error, or strayed from the correct, right, true, or proper course, whether in judgment, reasoning, conduct, or direction. Sense in this context. The card's sentence — "I have __________, my lord." — is a confession in the plainest possible form. "Erred" here means "I made a mistake, I was wrong," and the short, dignified verb, delivered with the formal "my lord," carries the weight of acknowledged failure: a subject or servant admits that he has strayed from right conduct or sound judgment and places himself at the mercy of his lord's judgment. The brevity of the sentence is the point — "I have erred" is the classic formula of admission, at once humble and dignified, entirely free of excuse-making. The verb's deeper meaning, "to wander or stray from the path," hovers behind the confession: to err is not merely to be factually wrong but to have departed from the right road, which is why the admission carries a moral colouring alongside the purely factual one. Primary sense — to be mistaken. In modern English, to err is to make a mistake, to be wrong, to hold or act on an incorrect belief or judgment: "the court erred in its ruling," "I erred in my calculations," "I erred on the side of caution." This is the sense of the card, and it is the everyday one — though the verb "err" is itself formal and slightly bookish, reserved for contexts that want dignity or precision, while the noun "error" is fully ordinary. The word "erred" is simply the regular past-tense form of this verb, created by the standard English weak inflection -ed. Polysemy and secondary meanings.
  • The literal sense — to wander or stray (the historical base): in its original Latin and Old French career, errāre / errer meant "to wander, to rove, to stray from a path." This sense survives in modern English almost only in the fossilized phrase "knight errant" — a knight who wanders in search of adventure — and in the adjective "errant" (a wandering or straying person or thing). When we say someone "erred," the ghost of this physical wandering is still felt: to err is to have left the straight road.
  • Moral error — to sin or stray from virtue: "to err" has long carried the religious and moral sense of straying from the path of right conduct; the proverb "To err is human, to forgive divine" (Pope, An Essay on Criticism, 1711) keeps this sense alive, where erring includes sin and moral failure, not merely factual mistakes.
  • Legal and judicial use: courts and tribunals are said to "err" in their rulings; "the court erred" is the standard phrase in appellate judgments when a lower court is held to have made an error of law; "clear error" and "erroneous" are terms of art in legal review.
  • "Err on the side of..." — the cautious idiom: "to err on the side of caution" (or generosity, safety, prudence) means deliberately to incline toward the safer or more generous choice when uncertain; this is a purely modern, fixed collocation in which "err" has shed almost all negative force.
  • Error of fact vs. error of judgment: in careful prose a distinction is drawn between factual error (being wrong about the facts) and error of judgment (being wrong in one's decision despite the facts); both are covered by "erred," and the word can be specified by prepositional phrases — "erred in judgment," "erred in fact," "erred in the application of the law."
The historical bridge. All these senses descend from Latin errāre "to wander, to stray," which itself continues the Indo-European root *ers- "to be in motion, to wander." The semantic development is a universal metaphor: the mind, like a traveller, can leave the true path. Physical wandering through a forest ("knight errant") became, in Latin already, moral and intellectual straying — "to wander from the truth," "to go astray in judgment." English inherited the full range: the literal sense decayed into the fossil of "errant," while the metaphorical sense of "to be mistaken" became the living word. The past form "erred" is thus the everyday surface of an ancient metaphor: every error is, at bottom, a wandering from the road. Collocations and register. Erred collocates with "in" + abstract noun ("erred in judgment," "erred in reasoning," "erred in the application of the law"), with "on the side of" (caution, safety, mercy, generosity), and with subjects such as court, judge, government, historian, critic, and (in confession) "I." Its register is formal and dignified; in ordinary speech most people say "made a mistake" or "got it wrong." The word is the past tense and participle of "err," from which English also has "error," "erroneous," "errant," "erratic," and, through Latin, "aberrant." Its antonyms are "was right," "succeeded," "did well," and, in the moral register, "kept the straight path.
