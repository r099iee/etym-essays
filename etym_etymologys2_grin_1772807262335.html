PIE *gher- 'to growl, snarl, gnash' (reconstructed; imitative 'growl-gnash' family; status debated — see note below) → Proto-Germanic *grenō- / *granjan 'to bare the teeth, grimace, howl, snarl' (reconstructed; cf. Old Norse grenja 'to howl', Old High German grennan 'to mutter, roar', German greinen 'to whine, fret', Dutch grienen 'to weep, whine') → Old English grennian 'to show the teeth in pain, anger, or threat; to snarl' (attested in late Old English glosses and homiletic prose, 9th–11th c.) → Middle English grinnen / grennen 'to grin, grimace, mock, show the teeth' (12th–14th c.) → Modern English grin (verb) 'to bare the teeth in pleasure, amusement, or grim endurance' (smiling sense established 15th–16th c.; noun 'a grin' from the 15th c.) → grin (modern noun and verb).

Note on the debate: the PIE-level reconstruction is not certain. Most handbooks treat the Germanic forms as a small onomatopoeic/imitative family of 'growl-grimace' type verbs rather than a securely attested PIE etymon; *gher- (Pokorny) is plausible but not guaranteed. English grind and grit belong to a separate root (*ghrendh- 'to grind, gnash'), and although the two families have influenced each other semantically, they are not the same etymon.
