Thumping is the present participle and participial adjective of the verb thump, used here attributively: pounding with heavy, dull, rhythmic force — or, in a secondary informal sense, remarkably large or emphatic (“a thumping majority”). In the dwarves’ teasing washing-up song — “Pound them up with a thumping pole” — the word paints a crude, heavy implement (a pestle, club, or battering stick) and supplies the song’s percussive comic menace. Bilbo is meant to hear his china smashed to powder; the dwarves’ actual magical deftness undercuts the threat, so the menace is all in the sound of the words. The tone is boisterous, onomatopoeic, mock-brutal: sound-effect diction in verse.

Polysemy — all senses and uses.

  • Literal acoustic-physical sense: striking with heavy, dull, repeated blows; making or accompanied by a thump (“a thumping at the door,” “a thumping pulse”).
  • Physical-action sense: describing an implement or agent that beats heavily (“a thumping pole,” “a thumping mallet,” “a thumping fist”).
  • Intensifying colloquial sense (from the 19th century): remarkably large, emphatic, decisive — “a thumping majority,” “a thumping win,” “a thumping great lie.”
  • Adverbial intensifier: “thumping good” as conversational emphasis (“a thumping good story”).
  • Physiological sense: of a heart or headache — beating heavily, painfully (“a thumping heart,” “a thumping headache”).

Historical and semantic bridges. Thump is an onomatopoeic English formation that surfaces in the early sixteenth century (c. 1530s): its consonant frame and short vowel imitate the dull, solid noise of a heavy blow. The participle thumping is available as an adjective by the mid-sixteenth century, meaning “that thumps, striking heavily.” The intensifying sense — “whopping, very large” — develops in informal English in the 19th century by a common path: heavy physical impact becomes a metaphor for enormous magnitude (the same path as hefty, weighty, and a whopper, all “weight” metaphors). The bridge is weight plus a muffled bang: what lands heavily is big.

Register, collocations, and modern life.

  • Register: stubbornly native English, colloquial, almost never elevated or Latinate — which is exactly why the dwarves can sing it in a kitchen song.
  • Collocations (literal): a thumping heart, a thumping headache, a thumping pulse, thumping music or bass, thumping hail or waves, a thumping blow with fist, mallet, club, or pole.
  • Collocations (intensifying): a thumping majority, a thumping win, a thumping great lie, thumping good — more British than American, carrying an amused, boastful energy.
  • Modern survival: both registers thrive; the intensifying sense is informal and often journalistic.
  • Jargon status: none technical; the word is a native sound-symbolic item of the living language.

Synonym boundaries. Pounding is near-synonymous for repeated heavy blows but stresses physical force rather than the dull sound; thudding stresses the impact noise but not the rhythmic force or the intensifying sense; bashing and smashing are more violent and less rhythmic; heavy and great cover only the secondary “very large” sense. Thumping uniquely fuses impact-noise, physical force, and later intensifying slang — perfect for a song that pretends to pulverize crocks with a pole. Notice the syllable itself: -ump- is a phonestheme of blunt, heavy impact, shared with bump, jump, dump, hump, plump, and stump.
