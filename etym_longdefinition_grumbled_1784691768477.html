Grumbled is the past tense and past participle of the verb grumble, and as such it denotes (1) the making of a low, continuous, rumbling sound and (2) the expression of discontent in a low, muffled, rumbling voice. It also carries the derived noun use: a grumble is the sound or the muttered complaint. The word sits on the border between speech and noise, serving for human voices and for unhuman rumblings alike, and it has been at home in both regions for more than four centuries. Senses and usages:
  • Human complaint. To grumble is to complain or express discontent in a low, rumbling, muffled voice — a half-suppressed rumble of displeasure, often habitual. The grumbler finds something to complain about in nearly everything and voices it in a sound like a small storm, frequently addressed to no one in particular. "Grumbled" as a verb of saying introduces quoted complaints in this low tone: "To think it will soon be June!" grumbled Bilbo.
  • Sound of nature and machinery. Thunder grumbles; an empty stomach grumbles; a heavy cart grumbles over stones; engines and guns grumble. The sound sense is the historically earlier one and remains fully alive: the deep, continuous, rumbling noise that carries no words.
  • The noun. A grumble is a low rumbling sound or a muttered complaint — "the grumble of distant thunder", "he has many grumbles". The plural grumbles collects a list of complaints.
  • Participle and adjective. A grumbling stomach, grumbling thunder, a grumbling workforce; the participial form names both the sound and the state of discontent, and as a habitual adjective it can describe a person's settled temper.
  • Historical and technical extensions. The Grumbletonians (1713) were disaffected Whig politicians of Queen Anne's reign, carrying the word into politics; "grumbling appendix" (19th century) was medical slang for a chronically troublesome appendix; and from the same grumbling stock grew grump, grumpish and grumpy.
Historical semantic bridge: the word is echoic or onomatopoeic, built on the syllable grum-, the low, dull, rolling rumble that thunder makes on a far horizon and that a hungry stomach makes in a quiet room. English adopted grumble around 1580, at first for sounds alone (thunder grumbles, the belly grumbles), and only gradually did the centre of gravity shift from the noise to the mood, from "to rumble low" to "to express discontent in a low voice". The two senses have never separated, and a grumble remains at once a sound and a state of mind: the deep, round, repetitive low-voiced form of protest, the noise of a small storm passing through a mood. In the context sentence, Bilbo's grumble is comic rather than bitter, the authentic noise of the displaced homebody, low and rueful, addressed to no one. Grumbled should be distinguished from its close relatives. To complain is to state a grievance in words, openly and to someone; to mutter or murmur is to speak in a low, indistinct voice, not necessarily in complaint; to grouse, a nineteenth-century word echoing the bird's call, is to grumble in a habitually sour way; to gripe is to complain peevishly; to whine is to complain in a thin, drawn-out voice; to growl is to make a low guttural sound of anger or threat; to rumble is to make the low rolling sound itself. Grumble differs from all of these in combining the rumbling sound with the discontented mood: it is the thunder of displeasure.
