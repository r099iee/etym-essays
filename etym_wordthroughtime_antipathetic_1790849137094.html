- c. 1600–1650: Formed in Early Modern English from *antipathy* (via Greek *antipatheia*), initially used in natural philosophy and alchemy to describe occult, mutual physical repulsions between natural substances (e.g., the antipathetic relation between certain plants, minerals, or animals).
- c. 1650–1750: Shifted increasingly into psychological, moral, and interpersonal discourse to denote instinctive, constitutional dislike or visceral repugnance between individuals.
- c. 1750–1900: Solidified in Victorian literature, philosophical treatises, and essays as an elevated, formal descriptor for diametrically opposed aesthetic principles, cultural tastes, and ethical dogmas.
- c. 1900–Present: Retained in scholarly criticism, literary theory (e.g., Roland Barthes's textual analyses), and intellectual prose to characterize contradictory aesthetic codes, hostile political factions, or mutually irreconcilable conceptual frameworks.
