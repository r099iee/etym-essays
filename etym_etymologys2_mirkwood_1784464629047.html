The compound MIRKWOOD is a modern literary toponym assembled from two independent native Germanic elements on the model of the Old Norse legendary forest-name Myrkviðr. It is NOT a single inherited Indo-European compound; each element has its own chain, and the place-name is Tolkien's coinage. Two separate chains follow. CHAIN 1 — MIRK / MURK ("dark, gloom"), the first element: → Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *mer- "to flicker, shimmer; dim, darken" (reconstructed; proposed root for the Germanic dark-words). [DEBATED: reconstructions and glosses vary by handbook; some scholars prefer a root meaning 'dim, dusky' without a settled shape.] → Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *merkwaz "dark, gloomy" (reconstructed, late 2nd–1st millennium BCE; base of the North and West Germanic dark-words). → Stage 3 — Old Norse myrkr "darkness, dark" (attested Viking Age; the crucial northern parallel for the toponym), beside Old English mirce / myrce "dark, murky" (sparse attestation, early medieval). → Stage 4 — Middle English mirke / merke / murk "dark, darkness" (12th–15th centuries CE). → Stage 5 — Modern English murk / mirk "gloom, darkness" (noun and adjective; mirk is the archaic-poetic spelling kept alive by northern poets and by this toponym). CHAIN 2 — WOOD ("forest"), the second element: → Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *widhu- "tree, wood" (reconstructed; attested by Old Irish fid 'tree, wood', Welsh gwydd, and the Germanic set). → Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *widuz "wood, forest, timber" (reconstructed, late 2nd–1st millennium BCE). → Stage 3 — Old English wudu / widu "wood, forest, timber" (attested from the 8th century CE). → Stage 4 — Middle English wode / wood "wood, forest" (12th–15th centuries CE). → Stage 5 — Modern English wood "forest, timber" (from the 15th–16th centuries CE onward). NAME ROUTING — the compound: → Old Norse Myrkviðr "Dark Forest" (myrkr 'dark' + viðr 'wood'), the legendary dark woodland of Old Norse heroic poetry (attested in Eddic and legendary saga literature). → Modern English MIRKWOOD — Tolkien's Englishing of that northern idea (The Hobbit, 1937), built transparently from mirk + wood. NOTE on the compound: the two element chains are historically secure; the full place-name is a 20th-century literary assembly on a Norse model, not an inherited lexeme. The connection of the Germanic dark-words to PIE *mer- 'to flicker' is proposed and debated, not certain.
