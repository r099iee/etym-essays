A coffin-nail is literally a large nail used to fasten shut a wooden coffin, and by celebrated extension a slang term for a cigarette, joking that each smoke hammers another nail into the smoker's coffin. In the Dickensian source sentence it appears in its literal hardware sense pushed to a comic superlative: the deadest piece of ironmongery, dead because coffins house the dead and nails are cold iron.

The literal sense belongs to undertakers, joiners, and ironmongers. Before mass-produced caskets with screws and hinges, coffins were nailed shut with stout hand-forged nails, often black japanned. To call something a coffin-nail was to name unglamorous funerary hardware. Dickens uses it to establish Marley's deadness by escalating metaphors: Marley is as dead as a door-nail, and a coffin-nail is deader still, because it belongs to the coffin itself rather than a mere door.

The slang sense, attested from the later nineteenth century, transfers the image to tobacco. A cigarette is white, stick-like, and nail-shaped, and smoking was increasingly linked to early death, so wags called cigarettes coffin nails. From there the phrase broadened to any self-destructive habit: drink, drugs, overwork, or worry can each be called another nail in one's coffin. Dictionaries record both the countable cigarette sense and the proverbial phrase.

  • Literal hardware: a stout nail for closing a coffin lid; e.g. the undertaker clinched the coffin-nails.
  • Tobacco slang (countable): a cigarette, especially viewed as unhealthy; e.g. he stepped out for a coffin-nail.
  • Figurative moralism: any act hastening ruin or death; e.g. every binge was another coffin-nail.
  • Intensifier of deadness (Dickensian): the deadest ironmongery, deader than a door-nail, used to assert absolute lifelessness.
  • Shape metaphor: anything long, thin, white and nail-like associated with death, occasionally applied to thin cigars or cheroots.
  • Proverbial frame: survives in the idiom a nail in one's coffin, where coffin-nail compresses the whole proverb into one compound.

Register is informal and often jocular-gothic. One would not write coffin-nail in a medical report for cigarette, nor in a hardware catalogue today, but in fiction, memoir, and anti-smoking rhetoric it is vivid. The hyphen varies: coffin-nail, coffin nail, coffinnail. American and British use agree, though the cigarette sense was especially common in early twentieth-century American slang alongside coffin-varnish for whisky.

Do not confuse with hobnail, door-nail, or tenpenny nail, which name different hardware without the funerary charge. The bridge from hardware to tobacco is visual resemblance plus gallows humour: the smoker is his own carpenter, each puff a hammer blow. That is why public-health posters loved it: a packet of cigarettes pictured as coffin nails makes the abstract risk concrete, and why Dickens loved it a generation earlier: nothing is deader than what belongs to a coffin.
