The present participle STRAYING is built on the Middle English verb straien, recorded from about 1300 with the precise, practical sense of an animal "wandering away from an enclosure or herd" and, for persons, "wandering from the path of justice or rectitude." Behind that modest beginning lies a chain of meaning reaching all the way back to one of the oldest Indo-European roots for spreading and scattering. The ultimate source is Proto-Indo-European *sterh₃-, "to spread out, extend, scatter, strew," a root of astonishing fecundity: it produced Sanskrit stṛṇāti "to scatter, strew," Greek strōnnýnai "to spread," and, in the Germanic languages, the verb that became English strew. But the branch that concerns the stray takes the root through Latin. Latin sternere meant "to spread out, lay flat, pave," and from its past participle strata came the feminine noun that meant a paved road — the same word that, in the phrase via strata, "a paved way," gives English street (through Old English strǣt). When the road-word crossed into Old French it became estree, "a route, a highway," and from estree the French built a verb, estraier, meaning literally "to be out on the highway" — and therefore to roam, to run loose, to wander about the roads. The word was first applied, characteristically, to a horse without a master, the beast that slips its tether and takes to the road. This is the exact image that gave English the stray: an animal that has left its lawful place. The English verb arrived as an aphetic shortening, the initial e- of estraier being dropped, giving straien by about 1300. Almost at once the word developed its double career. On one side it was a practical, pastoral, and legal term: a stray was a beast that had wandered from its herd or lawful enclosure, and in medieval law the estray — an ownerless animal that had strayed and reverted to the crown or the lord of the manor — became a recognized institution, with its own courts and rules of pound and pasturage. The companion word astray, originally the phrase o strai, "on stray," recorded in the 14th century of lost cattle, preserved the same picture: a beast "on stray" was out of bounds. On the other side the word rose from the pasture to the pulpit and the courtroom. From the same Middle English period "to stray" meant to wander from the path of justice, truth, or God; to be "led astray" was to be seduced into sin or error, and the moral reading of the word became one of the commonplaces of medieval religious prose. The physical and the moral senses thus grew up side by side, each feeding the other: the straying sheep of the parable and the straying heretic of the sermon were the same wandering animal in two costumes. A rival etymology, however, has always shadowed the story. The Oxford English Dictionary also records a derivation of the French estraier from Vulgar Latin *estragare, a contraction of *extravagare — representing Latin extra vagari, "to wander outside" — the family that produced extravagant, vague, and vagabond. On this account the stray is not so much a "street-roamer" as an "outside-wanderer," one who goes beyond the bounds. The two theories need not be enemies; both capture the medieval image of the stray beast, and scholars have long suspected that both routes — the paved road and the outside — fed and reinforced each other in the popular imagination. In modern English the word has softened and widened. The legal sting has faded, and straying now describes a stray bullet, a stray thought, a stray current in an electronic circuit, or the gentle drift of the mind away from its focus. The moral sense survives chiefly as the euphemism for marital infidelity and in the formal "straying from the truth," while the old pastoral and legal world survives in the noun a stray and the adjective astray. In the Elves' song in Tolkien's The Hobbit, "Your ponies are straying!" plays on the oldest layer of all — the masterless beast slipping away from the herd and the road after the long descent from the Misty Mountains — and on the gentler moral echo beneath it, for the weary dwarves are straying too, in danger of losing their mounts, their road, and their dignity at once. A word born on the paved roads of the Roman Empire and the scattering of seeds has ended, after two thousand years, as a teasing Elvish warning in an invented valley: still a beast out of bounds, and still — at the same time — a soul off the right road.
