- [Homeric epic, c. 8th century BCE] - [a name from the fist-measure pygmē: the imagined dwarf folk of the far south, famous chiefly as the cranes’ doomed battlefield — wonder, not science]
- [Classical Greece] - [geographers and naturalists (Herodotus, Aristotle, Ctesias) argue about where they live; the word slips from myth into an ethnographic hypothesis]
- [Roman Empire] - [Pliny catalogues the three-span men in his Natural History; the pygmies are standard baggage of the marvellous East and Africa]
- [Middle Ages] - [encyclopaedias and travellers’ books (Isidore, Mandeville) keep the pygmies as a curiosity of India — a small, half-real race at the edge of the map]
- [Renaissance] - [Milton’s ‘small infantry warred on by cranes’ turns the pygmies into a proverb of littleness; the word enriches literature, not travel]
- [19th-century exploration] - [Stanley and Schweinfurth meet real short-statured Central Africans; the ancient word snaps into a living ethnonym — ‘Pygmies’ with a capital letter in Victorian anthropology]
- [20th century] - [fieldwork refines the names (Mbuti, Baka, Aka); the plural ‘pygmies’ also multiplies in zoology and insult — pygmy hippos, pygmy whales, ‘intellectual pygmies’]
- [Today] - [the word is double-edged: a contested but widely used ethnonym, a taxonomic prefix for the smallest of any kind, and a still-live slur against small people — editors and scientists increasingly reach for precise names]
