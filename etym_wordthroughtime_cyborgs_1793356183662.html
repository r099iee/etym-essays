- 1960 — Coined in aerospace engineering: Manfred Clynes and Nathan Kline introduce cyborg in the journal Astronautics to denote a human physiologically modified with exogenous cybernetic devices to survive the hostile environment of outer space without artificial pressurization.
- 1960s–1970s — Clinical adoption and popular imagination: The word migrates into medical circles to describe early cardiac pacemaker recipients and amputees using experimental myoelectric prosthetics, while early television (The Six Million Dollar Man) popularizes the concept as a heroic military super-soldier.
- 1980s — Dystopian cyberpunk and feminist theory: Popular culture casts cyborgs as chilling cinematic monsters (The Terminator) or alienation icons (Neuromancer, RoboCop); simultaneously, Donna Haraway reclaims the term in 1985 as an empowering socialist-feminist metaphor subverting essentialist gender binaries.
- 1990s–2000s — The cognitive turn: Philosophers of mind like Andy Clark and David Chalmers coin the ‘Extended Mind Thesis’ and write of ‘natural-born cyborgs’, shifting the word from surgical implants to cognitive scaffolding (notebooks, computers, Internet search engines).
- Today — Everyday technological symbiosis: The term describes twenty-first-century networked humanity, capturing our inescapable psychological and functional dependence on mobile computing, algorithmic feeds, and cloud-based intelligence.
