The idiom HAVE TO DO is built from three independent root words, HAVE, TO, and DO, each with its own chain, followed by the coalescence of the phrase. Chain 1 — HAVE: 1. Proto-Indo-European *keh₂p- (also *kap-), "to seize, grasp, take hold of," the verbal root of taking; its Latin reflex is capere "to seize" (English capture, capable), and its Greek reflex kṓpē, "handle." 2. Proto-Germanic *habjaną, "to have, to hold" (attested in Gothic haban, Old Norse hafa, Old Saxon hebbian, Old High German habēn), a formation on the PIE root with the sense "to keep what one has taken." 3. Old English habban, "to have, hold, possess" (attested from the earliest texts, c. 8th c.). 4. Middle English haven, "to have, possess" (12th–15th c.). 5. Modern English have (16th c. onward), the verb of possession, holding, and (auxiliary) perfect aspect; the first element of HAVE TO DO. Chain 2 — TO: 1. Proto-Indo-European *do (also *de), "to, toward," a prepositional/adverbial stem of direction (Pokorny *do-), perhaps related to the demonstrative *de. 2. Proto-Germanic *tō, "to, toward," the directional preposition. 3. Old English tō, "to, toward, at, for" (c. 8th c.), used both as preposition and as the marker of the inflected infinitive (tō dōnne, "for the doing"). 4. Middle English to, "to" (12th–15th c.). 5. Modern English to, preposition and infinitive marker; the linking element of HAVE TO DO. Chain 3 — DO: 1. Proto-Indo-European *dʰeh₁- (also *dhē-), "to put, place, set, make," one of the most productive roots in the family; Latin faciō "I make" (English fact, factory), Greek títhēmi "I put" (English thesis), Sanskrit dadhāti "he places." 2. Proto-Germanic *dōną, "to do, act, put" (attested in Old Saxon dōn, Old High German tuon, Dutch doen, Old English dōn), built on the PIE root with a long ō. 3. Old English dōn, "to do, make, act, perform" (c. 8th c.). 4. Middle English don, "to do" (12th–15th c.). 5. Modern English do; the head verb of HAVE TO DO. Phrase chain — coalescence: 1. Old English "habban tō dōnne," "to have [something] for the doing, to have business to perform" (attested c. 9th–11th c.), where tō dōnne is the inflected infinitive (dative gerund) meaning "for doing." 2. Middle English "haven to don," "to have to do, to have business to transact" (12th–15th c.); by the 14th c. "haven to don (with)" means "to have dealings or concern with." 3. Early Modern English "have to do (with)," "to have business, dealings, or relation with" (16th c. onward), fully idiomatic. 4. Modern English HAVE TO DO (as in "have to do with"), a fixed relational idiom, distinct from the obligative "have to" (= "must").
