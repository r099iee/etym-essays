- [c. 1300: the Norman kitchen word] - After the Conquest, Middle English takes the word from Anglo-French as the table-name for the flesh of the pig; it arrives beside “beef” and “mutton” as a culinary word, so that from its first recorded use it names food on the platter, not the animal in the sty.
- [Middle English: the animal/meat split hardens] - The famous two-register division settles in: native Germanic “pig,” “swine,” and “hog” keep the living animal and the farmer’s world, while French-derived “pork” denotes the dressed meat of the dinner table — a word whose Norman pedigree lent it the quiet prestige of the conquerors’ cuisine.
- [Later centuries: plain culinary staple] - By early modern times the social sheen wears off: “pork” is simply the ordinary mass noun for pig-flesh and the head of dish-compounds like “pork pie” and “pork chop,” its aristocratic origin no longer felt.
- [Mid-19th century (American): political graft] - A startling figurative leap: “pork” enters American political slang for government funds and favours ladled out to win votes, from the image of the household “pork barrel” passed around; by the 20th century “pork-barrel spending” is a fixed term of reproach.
- [Contemporary sense] - Two lives in one word: the everyday culinary sense of pig-meat (carrying dietary taboos in some traditions) and the metaphorical political sense of patronage — the same “pork” that fills a picnic pork pie also lines the campaign slush fund.
