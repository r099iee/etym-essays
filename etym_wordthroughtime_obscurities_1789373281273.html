- Late 14th to 15th Century - Enters Middle English via Old French from Latin; initially designates physical gloom, the dark shadows of night, or spiritual blindness and moral ignorance.
- 16th to 17th Century - Broadens in Renaissance humanism, biblical commentary, and classical translation; begins to denote difficult, opaque passages, cryptic allegories, and hermeneutic cruces in ancient texts.
- 18th Century (Enlightenment & Neoclassicism) - The plural noun *obscurities* becomes a primary target of rationalist critique (Locke, Pope, Johnson); clarity of expression is crowned the supreme virtue, and "obscurities in style" are condemned as lazy pedantry or charlatanism.
- 19th Century - Bifurcates into sociological anonymity (virtuous lives languishing in historical obscurity) and a Romantic/Symbolist defense of poetic mystery, where deliberate obscurities are cultivated to evoke the sublime and ineffable.
- 20th to 21st Century - Codified as a technical term in genetic criticism and editorial theory, designating the unavoidable textual ambiguities, illegibilities, and unfinished draft cruces left in colossal modern masterworks (Proust, Joyce, Kafka).
