bark in its botanical sense — the sense used in the source passage — is the protective outer skin of woody plants, covering trunk, branches, and roots. It comprises all tissues outside the vascular cambium: the inner bark or phloem that transports sugars, and the outer bark or rhytidome of dead corky cells that waterproofs, insulates, and defends against insects, fungi, fire, and mechanical injury. Visually it ranges from smooth silver beech, to peeling birch, to deeply furrowed oak, to fibrous cedar that can be stripped and woven.

Beyond botany, bark is famously polysemous with two wholly separate homonyms that learners must not confuse: the abrupt explosive cry of a dog, and a small sailing ship. Only the tree-skin sense belongs to the bamboo passage, where bark and fibres are woven into cord and clothing, recalling Neolithic and indigenous technologies of bast, bark-cloth, and withies.

  • Core botanical sense — tree skin: hard covering of plants; includes inner living phloem and outer dead periderm; functions are protection, gas exchange via lenticels, and storage; harvested as cork, cinnamon, quinine, tannin, and fibre.
  • Material sense — bark as raw stuff: stripped bark used for weaving, roofing, canoes, baskets, cordage, paper, dye, and medicine; e.g. birch-bark manuscripts, Polynesian tapa cloth beaten from mulberry bark, Ojibwe birch canoes; bridge from plant anatomy to human craft.
  • Verb sense — to strip bark: to bark a tree is to remove its covering; transferred to grazing skin — to bark one's shin or knee by scraping it raw; also 19th-century American to bark a squirrel by shooting the bark beneath it to stun without spoiling meat.
  • Homonym — dog's cry (verb and noun): Old English beorcan to utter an abrupt explosive cry; noun beorc the sound itself; idioms to bark up the wrong tree, barking orders, one's bark is worse than one's bite; origin is imitative, unrelated to tree bark, converged by accident.
  • Homonym — small ship (noun, also spelled barque): any small vessel, later a three-masted ship square-rigged on fore and main; from French barque, Late Latin barca; unrelated to tree, though poetic fancy links wooden ships to tree skin.
  • Figurative extensions of tree bark: bark-like roughness — bark-textured paint, bark cloth pattern; dermatological metaphor — bark-like psoriasis plaques; geological — bark of the earth for crust.
  • Idiomatic and technical compounds: bark beetle, bark-bound trees, bark-bed for tanning, bark-peeling, bark-cloth, barking iron; forestry distinguishes bark thickness, bark pocket, bark grafting.
  • Perceptual nuance: rough versus smooth bark signals age and species; stripping bark kills by girdling because phloem transport is severed — hence to bark is also to wound fatally.

The bridge from anatomy to craft explains the passage: bark is not waste but textile. Retting, beating, and twisting separate bast fibres from cork, yielding cordage and cloth of a hundred kinds. Tannins in oak bark tan leather; cork oak gives stoppers; cinnamon is dried inner bark; birch bark is waterproof writing and roofing. Secondary dog-cry and ship senses share spelling only; their histories are separate, and context disambiguates instantly — fibres are woven from tree bark, not from yapping or boats.
