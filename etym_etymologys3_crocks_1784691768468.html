Crocks belongs to the oldest Germanic stratum of the English vocabulary, and its biography is a story of domestic pottery, hearth-hooks, and the slow comic deflation of a household word. The word-cluster begins with a Proto-Germanic base *krukkō- / *kruk-jō-, a reduplicated, expressive formation of the kind that abounds in words for round, hollow vessels. The doubled consonant and the rounded back vowel give the word its pottery texture even in sound: a short, heavy, clunking syllable suited to the object it names. This is the famous "expressive" or "phonesthetic" stratum of the lexicon — words for round things built by repetition and ablaut — and it is precisely because the base is expressive that its deeper ancestry resists reconstruction. No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon is attested for *krukk-. Some scholars have floated a link to a PIE *greg- / *kreg-, "to gather, press together", comparing Latin grex, "a flock, herd", on the idea that a pot is a clay mass gathered or coiled into shape; the proposal is ingenious but speculative, and the standard dictionaries — the OED among them — prudently mark the origin unknown. Honest etymology here stops at the Germanic border: the word is native Germanic, not a loanword, but its pre-Germanic past is beyond the reach of the record. From the Germanic base Old English produced crocc (with the variant crocca), "an earthen pot or pitcher", attested in the glossaries and charters of the Anglo-Saxon period. The geminate -cc- reflects the reduplicated *-kk- of the base, and the rounded vowel the *-u- of *krukk-. By the Middle English period the form had settled as crokke or crocke, and the word's semantic territory widened: it could mean an earthen cooking pot, a storage jar, a pitcher, and — by a striking transfer — the sooty iron hook, the crook, on which pots hung above the hearth. The transfer is pure metonymy: the thing that held the pot took the pot's name, and both the vessel and its iron hanging-rod were called crokke in the same households. The cognates across the North Sea testify to the same domestic world. Old Norse krukka gives Icelandic krukka "pot" and Swedish kruka; Danish has krukke; Middle Dutch crocke becomes modern Dutch kruik "jug, pitcher"; and Middle High German kruche yields modern German Krug "jug". English, however, kept the earthenware sense the longest and the most tenaciously, and by the early modern period crock designated the coarse pottery of kitchens, dairies, and cellars — the homely ware in which butter was beaten, pickles brined, and dripping stored. The modern plural crocks is the form the word takes in exactly Tolkien's domestic context. When Bilbo wakes after the Unexpected Party to find "piles of unwashed crocks in the kitchen", the word does the work of three adjectives: coarse, heavy, dirty. In the early twentieth century the singular also grew a slang life. The colloquial "old crock" — a broken-down horse or a person or machine past its usefulness — transfers the rough physicality of the worn vessel to living creatures. American slang of the twentieth century went further: "a crock" (short for the crude "a crock of shit") means a false or nonsensical tale, keeping the same idea of something hollow, heavy, and full of worthless content. From the 1970s the Crock-Pot, the electric slow cooker named for its glazed ceramic insert, carried the old earthenware tradition into the modern kitchen. The whole history is one of downward drift — from a precious storage vessel valuable enough to be itemised in a will, to the dirtiest pile in the sink, to a pack of lies. Every step is bridged by the same heavy, hollow, rough clay thing, and every step is why crocks, in the plural, still smells faintly of kitchen smoke and stale washing-up.
