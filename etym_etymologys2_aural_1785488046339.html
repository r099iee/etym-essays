Derivation chain for AURAL: 1. Proto-Indo-European *h₂ows- / *h₂ous- "ear" (with the oblique stem *h₂us-és- used in case forms) — one of the most stable body-words of the family, attested across nearly every branch of Indo-European. 2. Italic *aus-i- "ear" → Latin auris "ear" (attested from the earliest Latin texts, 3rd c. BCE onward; e.g., Plautus, Ennius). The Latin noun reshaped the PIE root with the stem-forming element *-i-. 3. Late Latin aurālis "of the ear" — a secondary adjective formed on auris with the productive suffix -ālis (the same pattern as ōrālis "of the mouth," vīsuālis "of sight," later borrowed into English as oral, visual). This adjective is a Late-antique formation of the period when Latin was building a technical, especially grammatical and medical, vocabulary. 4. English AURAL "relating to the ear or the sense of hearing" — borrowed from the Latinate adjective in the mid-19th century, first attested c. 1846, in the great age of English scientific vocabulary-building. Note on debated points: - The Roman grammarians (e.g., Varro) derived Latin audīre "to hear" directly from auris, but this is now rejected; audīre is traced instead to the PIE extended root *h₂ewis- "manifest, perceptible," a near-homonym but a distinct formation. The verbs and nouns built on audīre (audio, auditory, audition, auditor, audit, obey via oboedīre) therefore belong to a different line and are not ancestors of aural. - The Greek reflex οὖς (oûs) "ear," genitive ὠτός (ōtós), gives the medical stem ōt- (otology, otitis, parotid) — a parallel branch of the same PIE root, not the ancestor of English aural. - The second English word aural, "of an aura," from Latin aura "breeze, emanation," is a distinct etymon coinciding in spelling.
