Braces (plural) in British English are the paired straps worn over the shoulders to support trousers — what American English usually calls suspenders. The word pictures two supporting bands that "brace" the garment from above, leaving the hands free and the waist lightly belted or unbelted. In Bilbo's parlour the hobbit "stuck one thumb behind his braces" while declaring that adventures make you late for dinner: the gesture is pure domestic self-possession — thumbs hooked into the straps, chest a little puffed, smoke-rings still forming — respectability embodied in clothing hardware. The register is cozy, provincial, faintly comic; the braces mark Baggins as a settled householder rather than a road-worn adventurer. Tolkien's English is British: the card must keep braces and not import the American synonym.

Core senses:

  • Trouser suspenders (BrE) — the paired shoulder straps that hold up trousers; always plural, "a pair of braces" (the sense in this card).
  • Dental braces — orthodontic appliances (bands, brackets, wires) that brace the teeth into correct alignment.
  • Structural supports — a brace is any prop, strut, or strengthening timber in buildings, scaffolding, or machinery ("wall braces", "knee-braces").
  • Pair sense (count noun brace) — from hunting: "a brace of pheasants" (two birds), "a brace of pistols" (two).
  • Typographical braces — the curly brackets { } used to group items together.
  • Musical brace — the vertical line and bracket joining two staves of a piano score.
  • Verb to brace — to make firm or steady: "brace the beam", "brace yourself for the impact".
  • Carpenter's brace — a cranked drilling tool ("brace and bit"), an old technical specialization.

Historical bridge & semantic logic: every sense hangs on the notion of holding or supporting — the old word for "a pair of arms" (Latin bracchia) fed a noun for an embrace, a pair, and then anything that clasps two things together or holds a thing firm. The clothing sense is a later specialization of "supports": by the early 19th century the paired straps that hold trousers up were being called braces in Britain (where suspenders in British English can mean stocking-supporters). Dental braces and wall-braces are modern technical extensions of the same support-verb.

Usage notes & idioms: braces is effectively a plural-only (pluralia tantum) noun in this sense — you never say "a brace" for one trouser-strap in modern garment usage; the singular brace is reserved for the count-noun meanings (a brace of birds, a structural brace). In tailoring, braces are worn either crossed at the back or straight and parallel, held by buttons or metal clips. The British word retains a slightly old-fashioned, respectable flavor, which Tolkien exploits deliberately: the term anchors Bilbo in a settled, pipe-and-armchair world of waistcoats and smoking-jackets, the very opposite of a road-worn adventurer's gear.

Differentiate: suspenders is the American term for the same straps (and in Britain means stocking-supporters); a belt circles the waist rather than hanging from the shoulders; a strap is the general band; garters support stockings, not trousers. Preserve the plural — a pair of straps is the natural unit of the garment.
