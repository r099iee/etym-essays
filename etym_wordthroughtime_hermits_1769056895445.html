- The desert-dweller — English takes 'hermit' from Greek via Old French meaning one who lives alone in the waste as a religious discipline — the figure of the Egyptian desert fathers, for whom solitude was a profession.
- From desert to wood — As monasticism spreads through the forests and mountains of Europe, the name broadens from the desert-dweller to any solitary who chooses a wild place — a cave, a cell on a crag, a hut in a valley — wherever there is no desert to go to.
- The romance of the wise solitary — In chivalric romance and saint's legend the hermit becomes the storybook figure who shelters and counsels the wandering knight; isolation is read as training rather than punishment.
- Contemporary sense — The religious force fades and 'hermit' becomes a playful general word for chosen retreat from society (the hermit-crab, 'to live like a hermit,' 'gone all hermit'), yet it never loses its flavour of disciplined solitude.
