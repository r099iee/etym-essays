CHAIN 1 — ROOT WORD "SAND": PIE root *bhes- — "to rub, grind, crush" (traditional reconstruction: sand is "that which is ground down," eroded rock); Greek ψάμμος psammos "sand" and Latin sabulum "sand, gravel" preserve the rubbing idea in the classical languages ↓ Proto-Germanic *sandam — "sand, gravel" (cf. Old Norse sandr, Old Frisian sand/sond, Old High German sant → German Sand, Dutch zand) ↓ Old English sand — "sand; also the strand, the shore" (attested c. 8th–11th c.; the word for both the granular substance and the sandy shoreline) ↓ Middle English sand — "sand" (same form; also in compounds) ↓ Modern English sand (noun) — "fine granular rock material"; verb to sand (to smooth or abrade); compounds sandcastle, sandstone, sandstorm, quicksand, sandpaper ↓ English compound sand-bank — "a bank of sand" (with the noun bank, see CHAIN 2) — THE CARD WORD DEBATED / NUANCED: Some modern reconstructions instead derive *sandam from PIE *sámh₂dʰos (perhaps an old derivative of the "press/pour out" root *sem-); the traditional *bhes- "rub, grind" lineage followed by the OED and Etymonline remains the standard teaching path for English sand. Note also that French sable "sand" is from Latin sabulum — the same *bhes- idea via a separate Romance route — and is unrelated to English sable "the fur," which is Slavic. CHAIN 2 — ROOT WORD "BANK": PIE root *bheg- — "to bend, curve" (a bank is a raised, curving ridge of earth); cognates include Sanskrit bhuj "to bend" and English bow (v.), bough ↓ Proto-Germanic *bank- / *bankô — "a raised ridge, a shelf, a bench-like rise" (the "bend" of a surface into a ridge or bench) ↓ Old Norse bakki — "river-bank, slope, ridge, high ground" (Norse settlement of England, 9th–11th c.; the primary donor of the English word) ↓ Middle English banke — "river-bank, slope, shore" (borrowed c. 1200 during the Scandinavian settlement) ↓ Modern English bank (noun) — "raised slope/ridge of earth" (river-bank, earth-bank, sea-bank); the financial bank arrived by a separate Romance route (via French banque, Italian banca "bench, counter" — the moneychanger's bench) from the same Germanic *bank- "bench" family ↓ English compound sand-bank — "a bank of sand" (with the noun sand, see CHAIN 1) — THE CARD WORD NOTE: the native Old English cognate benc "bench" is from the same base, but the ridge sense entered English from Norse, not from native Old English. The financial bank is historically separate from the earth-bank in its route of borrowing, though both draw on the same "raised flat form" family; no stage in the earth-bank lineage is debated.
