- c. 1400–1500s: Emerged in late Middle English as guschen / gousen (likely from Old Norse geysa / echoic origin), describing the violent, sudden eruption of liquids such as water or blood from a wound or vessel.
- 1600s–1750s: Extended into descriptive poetry and drama to portray violent physical currents, storm floods, and emotional outbursts.
- 1820s–1890s: Developed its distinct figurative sense in Victorian social commentary, describing excessively sentimental, hyperbolic, or cloying speech and writing (leading to the term gushy).
- 1900s–1950s: Adopted by the petroleum industry with the coinage of gusher for high-pressure oil strikes, symbolizing spectacular sudden wealth.
- 1960s–Present: Re-conceptualized in literary theory and avant-garde poetics (e.g., Roland Barthes) to describe unpunctuated streams of experimental prose, while continuing in everyday English to describe both fluid bursts and effusive enthusiasm.
