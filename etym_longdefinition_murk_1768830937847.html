murk: Thick, heavy darkness; a gloom so dense that it seems to have substance and body — not the mere absence of light but a palpable, obscuring atmosphere that fills the air like smoke or shadowed water. In the present context — a visionary landscape in which the speaker looks toward the horizon and "thought to see a black curtain rising high into the murk" — the word is used with precise dramatic effect: the murk is the upper region into which the black curtain ascends, a darkness that towers and encompasses, encircling the land in all directions. The curtain and the murk together form a total enclosure: the murk is not a hole in the scene but the very fabric of the sky, the medium of the vision's oppressive grandeur. The tone is apocalyptic and sublime — this is the vocabulary of the numinous, of being sealed inside a world whose edges are darkness. Primary sense: Dense, heavy darkness or gloom; a thick obscuring haze.
  • Of weather and atmosphere: murk of fog, murk of smoke, the murk of a polluted sky — darkness or gloom thickened by particles in the air.
  • Of lightlessness: the murk of deep water, the murk of a cave, the murk of night — darkness with weight, in which objects are not merely dim but swallowed.
  • Of moral or emotional gloom (figurative): "the murk of despair," "the murk of ignorance" — a state of obscurity or spiritual darkness in which nothing can be clearly seen.
Secondary and figurative senses:
  • Obscurity and confusion: mental murk — a fogged, unclear state of mind; in journalism, "the murk of secrecy" means the obscuring of facts.
  • Slang and gaming (murked): in modern slang, "murked" means killed, defeated, or thoroughly outplayed; the violent slang (also spelled "merked") appears in hip-hop lyrics and online gaming commentary.
  • Gothic and fantasy register: in Gothic literature and fantasy, murk names the darkness of haunted and enchanted places; Tolkien's Mirkwood revived the older spelling mirk for a forest whose darkness has the weight of legend.
Modern technical and jargon senses:
  • In nautical and meteorological description: "murk" is used of the dense, obscuring haze of fog banks at sea, where visibility drops to nothing.
  • In environmental and industrial writing: the murk of smog — a darkness thickened by pollution, combining the senses of haze and gloom.
  • In gaming slang: "murked" (to be eliminated) transfers the sense of being swallowed in darkness to being erased from the game.
Semantic bridges and historical development: The word's core image is dying light. Its ancestor, the Proto-Indo-European root *mer-, meant both "to flicker, to glimmer" and "to die, to waste away": light that flickers is light that is dying, and the ancient mind ran the two together. The darkening sky was the daily death of the day, so the "darkness" sense and the "death" sense grew from the same root — the family includes murder, mortal, and marasmus as well as murk. The English noun was reinforced in the sixteenth century by a Scandinavian borrowing (Old Norse myrkr, "darkness"), and the merged word kept the quality that distinguishes it from all its synonyms: density. Darkness is neutral and total; gloom is darkness with a melancholy mood attached; dusk and twilight are transitional, dim but not opaque; dimness is a mere reduction of light; haze and mist are watery, light-scattering veils; murk alone is the darkness that has become a substance — close to smog in texture and to night in finality, but stranger than either, because it is a darkness you could almost reach into. Its etymological sibling murky ("dark, gloomy, obscure," often said of water or weather) shares the same density, and both words in this passage contribute to the sense that the sky itself is a solid, rising curtain of blackness.
