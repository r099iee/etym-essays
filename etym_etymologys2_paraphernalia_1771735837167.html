*bher- (Proto-Indo-European, "to carry, bear, bring"; secure root, also the source of Latin ferō "I carry," Greek phérō, English bear) → Greek phérnē ("dowry, that which is brought/born in marriage," attested 6th–4th c. BCE, from the root with a feminine noun suffix) → Greek parápherna (neuter plural, "goods a bride brings beside her dowry," formed with pará "beside" + phérnē "dowry," attested in Hellenistic legal texts, 3rd–1st c. BCE) → post-classical Latin parapherna ("the wife's goods beyond the dowry," loanword from Greek, attested in Roman legal writing, 3rd c. CE) → medieval Latin paraphernalia ("paraphernal goods," neuter plural of the adjective paraphernalis, formed in the legal Latin of the early Middle Ages) → English PARAPHERNALIA ("a wife's personal property beyond her dowry," borrowed from medieval Latin legal terminology, attested from the 17th century; broadened to "miscellaneous belongings or equipment" in the 18th century). Note on debated points: the root *bher- "to carry" is securely established, and the Greek form phérnē "dowry" is well attested. The main nuance is that the modern broad sense "equipment, apparatus" is a later English semantic development of the 18th century, not a classical meaning; the strict etymological sense "beside the dowry" underlies the modern word only historically.
