The history of Oldenburg runs on two intertwined tracks: a thousand-year German place-name and dynasty, and a sixty-year American art career that made that name globally famous. Both must be told.

Linguistically Oldenburg is transparent Germanic: old + burg. Proto-Germanic *aldaz old, grown-up + *burgz fortified place, hillfort, town, from PIE *bhergh- high, hill, to protect. English old, borough, bury (Canterbury), burgh, bourgeois and German alt, Burg, Bürger are cognates. The name type is common: Oldenburg = the old fortified settlement as opposed to a newer foundation nearby.

  • PIE *bhergh- “high, hill, to fortify” (reconstructed): words for hills, forts, protection across Indo-European; gives Germanic burg, Slavic grad (Belgrade), Irish brí hill.
  • Proto-Germanic *alda- + *burgi- (first centuries CE): compound type *aldaburg- meaning old stronghold. Parallels: *Hamburg (forest fortress), *Magdeburg (mighty fortress).
  • Old Saxon / Old High German ald + burg (8th–11th c.): ald, alt old + burg castle, walled town, refuge. Charlemagne's Saxon wars fortify the region; many burgs founded.
  • Medieval Aldenburg / Oldenburg (1108 onwards): first documentary mention 1108: Count Egilmar I de Aldenburch. Form fluctuates Aldenburg, Oldenburg in Low German charters. The counts build a marshland lordship between Weser and Ems. 1180 city rights; 1345 Bremen law. The name now denotes both castle-town and comital house.
  • Early modern Grand Duchy (1774–1918): Oldenburg becomes a duchy, then grand duchy under Holstein-Gottorp Oldenburgs. The House of Oldenburg branches rule Denmark (1448 Christian I onwards), Norway, Sweden briefly, Schleswig-Holstein, Russia (Holstein-Gottorp-Romanov), Greece (1863 George I), and via Glücksburg the present British, Danish, Norwegian, and Spanish monarchs. The toponym thus becomes one of Europe's most widespread dynastic labels.
  • Modern city (1946–present Lower Saxony): 170,000 inhabitants, university city, administrative centre. German pronunciation stabilizes as /ˈɔldənbʊrk/. Emigration carries surname Oldenburg to America, Sweden, Russia.

Biographical track: Claes Oldenburg born 28 January 1929 Stockholm, son of Swedish diplomat Gösta Oldenburg, nephew of sculptor Sven Hoffert. Family moves to Chicago 1936, where Claes grows up, studies at Yale (1946–1950) and Art Institute of Chicago. Moves New York 1956, works in Cooper Union library, falls in with Kaprow happenings, Judson Dance, Reuben Gallery. 1961 The Store on East 2nd Street sells plaster food and clothes as art-commodity. 1962 Green Gallery shows first soft sculptures sewn by wife Patty Mucha: floppy Floor Burger, collapsing Ice Cream Cone. Letters follow: stuffed alphabet, Soft Alphabet (soft letters that droop like bodies). 1963 moves to Los Angeles briefly, makes Bedroom Ensemble. From late 1960s proposes Colossal Monuments — giant clothespins, lipsticks, teddy bears for cities — mostly rejected as absurd, then from 1976 with second wife Coosje van Bruggen (1942–2009) realizes dozens worldwide. Retrospective MoMA 1969, Guggenheim, National Gallery. Dies 18 July 2022 New York.

Art-historical significance explains your quotation. Around 1960–1965 both Oldenburg and Jasper Johns (b. 1930, alphabet and flag paintings 1954 onwards) independently turn letters, numbers, targets, and maps into painting-sculpture. Johns stencils A–Z in encaustic grids; Oldenburg sews them as soft bodies. Both treat information — alphabets, signs — as depicted things with texture, weight, and style, not transparent carriers. The book cites them as ancestors of an information aesthetics: prose style, typography, and data visualization are not pesky ornaments but the stuff itself.

Reception history: dismissed early as joke Dada, then canonized as core Pop alongside Warhol. 1970s minimalists attacked monument proposals as sell-out; 1980s–2000s public art embraced them as humane landmarks. Soft letters now read as prophecy of emoji, inflatable typography, and meme letters — language gone floppy and physical. The surname, once an obscure north German town, is now for most English speakers first an artist, second a horse breed, third a city — a nice case of personal fame eclipsing toponymy, like Ford, Disney, Kafka.
