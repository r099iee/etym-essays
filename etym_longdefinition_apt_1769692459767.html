APT (adjective): (1) appropriate or suitable in the circumstances; (2) having a natural tendency or inclination to do something; (3) quick to learn, clever, ready. Pronounced /apt/. Adverb aptly; noun aptness. From Latin aptus "fitted, bound, suited". Sense 1 - Appropriate, suitable, fitting (of things). An apt description, an apt comparison, an apt quotation, an apt reply: the thing is well fitted to its circumstance. "The description was very apt." This is the meaning inherited directly from Latin aptus "fitted, adapted" - the object is the perfect key to the lock. This sense judges the fit between expression and situation. Sense 2 - Natural tendency or inclination (of persons; the Austen sense). apt + infinitive means "having a habitual tendency, likely, prone": "children are apt to misbehave", "he is apt to forget", "she is apt to exaggerate". In Pride and Prejudice (1813, Chapter 4) Elizabeth tells Jane: "you are a great deal too apt, you know, to like people in general" - Jane's disposition so inclines her to think well of everyone that she cannot see faults. Here apt marks a characteristic, almost reflexive bias of character - the exact fit of Jane's temperament to goodwill. Sense 3 - Quick to learn, clever, ready (of persons and their minds). "An apt pupil", "an apt learner", "an apt student of politics": apt here means naturally fitted to learn, gifted, quick, teachable - a mind well-adapted to the subject. The related noun aptitude means natural talent or fitness; the adjective in this sense is close to talented and astute but retains the idea of fit. Sense 4 - Fitted, prepared (archaic literal). In older English and in theological writing, apt could mean literally "fitted, fastened, prepared, made ready": "apt for battle", "apt to the occasion". This literal sense (from aptus, past participle of apere "to fasten") underlies all the figurative senses: what is apt is what is fastened on, joined, bound, and therefore appropriate. Sense 5 - Technical and specialist uses.
  • Linguistics: "apt" appears in descriptions of collocation and semantic fit (an apt collocation); in pedagogy, "aptitude test" means a test of natural learning ability, distinguished from achievement.
  • Education: aptitude (aptness) is measured by aptitude tests and cognitive subtests; "aptitude" is contrasted with "achievement".
  • Philosophy (virtue epistemology): "aptness" names a belief that succeeds because of the knower's competence (Ernest Sosa's concept of the apt belief).
  • Law: "apt and sufficient cause" in older legal usage; "apt means" in discussions of causation.
  • Computing (informal): the Linux package manager APT (Advanced Package Tool) is an acronym, but its name was chosen partly as a pun on "appropriate".
  • Rhetoric: an "apt" figure of speech is one fitting its subject.
Sense 6 - Polysemy logic and the semantic bridge. All senses are one image: fitting. From the literal past participle "fastened on, joined", apt became "fitted to a purpose" (suitable), then "fitted by nature to act" (inclined, prone), then "fitted by nature to learn" (clever, quick). The bridge between "suitable" and "tending to" is natural disposition: a person apt to anger is a person whose temperament fits anger; an apt metaphor is a metaphor fitted to the thing. Grammar. apt + infinitive (apt to forget); apt + for (apt for the purpose); predicative and attributive. Not to be confused with adapted (changed to fit) or adept (skilled, from Latin adeptus "attained"). Register and nuance. Apt is neutral, versatile, mildly formal. In the "suitable" sense it is always approving (an apt remark); in the "tending to" sense it is neutral to mildly negative when the tendency is undesirable (apt to spill, apt to complain); in the "clever" sense it is approving. Common collocations. an apt description/comparison/pupil; apt to forget/mislead/please; apt and pertinent; with apt brevity; aptness of phrase.
