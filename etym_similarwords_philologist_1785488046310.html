• Linguist: Similar because both denote scholars of language; differs because a linguist studies language descriptively and scientifically and need not care about literary texts, while a philologist reads texts as cultural and historical documents.
  • Grammarian: Similar because both work with the rules and structure of language; differs because a grammarian specialises narrowly and often prescriptively in grammar, without the historical and literary breadth of philology.
  • Etymologist: Similar because both are scholarly students of language; differs because the etymologist focuses on word origins alone, while philology embraces texts, culture and literature as a single pursuit.
  • Historical linguist: Similar because both study language change over time; differs because the historical linguist works within the modern science of linguistics, while the philologist bridges that science with literary scholarship.
  • Textual critic: Similar because both work with ancient and medieval texts; differs because textual criticism is the narrower craft of establishing a text’s readings, whereas philology is the whole study of language through literature.
  • Lexicographer: Similar because both are language professionals; differs because the lexicographer’s craft is writing and editing dictionaries, while the philologist studies language and literature at large without necessarily compiling dictionaries.
  • Polyglot: Similar in suggesting deep knowledge of languages; differs because a polyglot simply speaks many languages, while a philologist studies language’s history and texts and need not be a many-tongued speaker.
