- 14th century – 'manifest' enters English (via French, from Latin manifestus, "caught in the act, seized by the hand") meaning plainly revealed to the eye or mind, self-evident — a forensic, near-judicial word, the vocabulary of evidence held up for inspection.
- 16th–17th centuries – the word branches outward: a noun 'manifest' (a ship's public list of cargo, a statement of what lies open to inspection) and a verb 'to manifest' (to make evident, to show forth), extending the idea of open display from the law court into commerce, religion, and natural philosophy.
- 19th century – 'manifest' enters the public imagination through Manifest Destiny (1845), the doctrine that American continental expansion was an openly apparent, self-justifying national purpose; the word now carries an ideological, almost prophetic ring.
- 20th–21st centuries – the adjective stays formal ('manifest injustice', 'manifest failure'), while the verb is repurposed by self-help and New Age culture: 'manifesting' your desires means willing them into visible reality. The public perception has drifted from 'evidence seized by the hand' to 'reality summoned by intention.'

- vats: 16th century – from Old English fæt, a vessel or container; large open-topped tubs of wood or metal for holding liquids in brewing, dyeing, tanning, and fermentation — squarely an industrial working word.
- 19th–21st centuries – the sense of industrial processing is retained and extended into metaphor as 'a vat of' (a great quantity of something), so the word now connotes mass production and large, churning scale.
