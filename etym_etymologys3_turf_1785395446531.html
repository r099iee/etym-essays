Turf is one of the few everyday English words whose form has come down virtually unchanged for more than a millennium: Old English turf, plural tyrf, is the direct ancestor of modern turf, and its shape has not altered in over a thousand years. Behind it lies the Proto-Germanic noun *turbaz, "a clod of earth knitted by roots, a sod, a block of peat", with full sibling cognates in Old Norse torf, Old Frisian turf, Middle Dutch turf, and — via Middle Low German and Old High German zurba — modern German Torf, which has narrowed to mean "peat" above all. The Germanic word is referred to the Proto-Indo-European root *derbh-, "to wind, twist, bind together", the same root that yields Sanskrit darbhá-, a tuft of kuśa grass gathered and bound for Vedic ritual, and, inside Germanic itself, Old High German zerban "to wind, spin". The underlying image is of binding and intertwining: turf is not earth alone but earth and root matted into a single fabric, and it is precisely that interwoven strength which let ancient farmers cut it with a spade and use the blocks for walls, roofs, hearths, and, dried from bog-land, for fuel — the peat of modern turf-bogs and of the German Torf. Both Germanic *turbaz and Sanskrit darbhá- are built on the zero-grade of the root, *dr̥bʰ-, the Germanic form adding metathesis to *turb-; Old High German zerban preserves the full grade *derbʰ-. The equation is probable but debated: some scholars add Greek δρέπω "to pluck, gather" to the family, others doubt it, and the deeper PIE status of the root rests on the single image of binding, which is sound but not beyond dispute. Old English used turf for "sod, earthen surface, soil" in the general sense, and for a thousand years the word stayed close to that physical ground. In the treeless, boggy North Atlantic world turf was not merely a word but a building material and a fuel: turf walls and turf roofs rose on the moors and in Iceland and Ireland, and turf cut from the bog and dried became the peat burned on the hearth — a practice so central that "to cut turf" and "the turf-bank" are part of the economic vocabulary of the Gaelic and Norse Atlantic world. Middle English kept the word for the ground and its cut blocks, and the pronunciation and spelling drifted little: turf, torf, and turfe are variant spellings of the same word before the modern form fixed in the early modern period. The plural tyrf of Old English survives as the collective sense of the word in dialect. The extended senses arrived late, and all of them derive from the notion of a piece of ground claimed and possessed. In the eighteenth century "the turf" named the world of horse-racing — the grassy course over which the sport was run became by metonymy the sport itself, giving rise to the turf, the turf-writer, and the turf calendar, and giving the word a fashionable, sporting social register wholly separate from its rural origins. In 1928 the formal term turf accountant appeared for the bookmaker, a euphemism that still governs betting-office names in Britain. The twentieth century pushed the metaphor further: American street slang used "turf" for the territory a gang claimed, and from that concrete possession came turf war (c. 1950) and the general idiom of home turf, one's own turf, and turf rights — an abstract, jurisdictional sense built directly on the ancient ownership of a piece of root-bound ground. The same word that named a ninth-century sod now names the invisible boundary of a gang or a bureaucracy. A second modern strand is technological. When artificial grass was developed in the mid-twentieth century, the first product, AstroTurf (1966), kept the word turf in its name, and "turf" now denotes synthetic playing surfaces as freely as natural ones — field turf, turf pitches, turf engineering. The word thus spans the full arc from a hand-cut block of bog earth to a factory-woven plastic carpet, while the verb to turf (as in "turf out", to eject someone, from flinging them out onto the grass) preserves the older material sense in idiom. In Tolkien's passage turf appears in its oldest and purest sense — the close, rabbit-cropped, root-knitted living surface of the high country — and the word's history makes the moment heavier: this is the ancient substance of the hillside, the same turf that for a millennium of English speech has meant the ground that binds itself together and can be lifted, burned, raced on, and fought over, vanishing here into bare scree with the rest of the remembered landscape.
