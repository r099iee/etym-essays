- [Greek, 5th c. BCE] 'aúra' — a breeze, breath of air; a soft, elemental word for wind
- [Latin] 'aura' — air, breeze, a subtle influence or breath; the word hovers between weather and spirit
- [Old French, 12th c.] 'essorer' — to fly up, to rise into the air; a word of hawking and falconry, of letting the bird mount on the wind
- [Middle English, 14th c.] 'soren' — to fly aloft, to hover at great height; falconry language enters English and at once becomes poetic
- [16th–19th c.] Figurative ascent: 'soaring' ambition, soaring thoughts, soaring spirits and towers — abstract elevation blooms alongside literal flight
- [20th c.] The age of flight and finance: aircraft soar; then prices, stocks, and ratings soar; 'soared' becomes a headline verb
- [21st c.] Triple life: literal aerial majesty (eagles, dragons, jets), steep quantitative climb (markets, temperatures), and lyrical emotional lift (spirits, melodies) all coexist under one word
