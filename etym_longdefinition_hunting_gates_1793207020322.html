Hunting-gates, the plural of hunting-gate, are field gates in hunting country: the stout barred gates set across bridleways, farm tracks, and the gaps between covers, through which the hunt — hounds, huntsman, whippers-in, and the mounted field — must pass. In Cobbett's sentence they complete a catalogue of country that defeats the gig: bye-lanes too narrow for wheels, fields with no road at all, bridle-ways barred to carriages, and hunting-gates built for horses to jump or for hunt servants to open, not for a tall-wheeled trap to bowl through. The compound belongs to the world of organized fox-hunting at its Georgian height, when half the gates a cross-country rider met were maintained, used, and jumped by the hunt.

Core meaning in detail: A hunting-gate is typically a five-barred wooden field gate — oak or chestnut, iron-hung on a stone or timber post, fastened with a drop bolt or a hunting latch that a mounted rider can work without dismounting. Some are purpose-built hunt gates with a rider's catch; others are ordinary farm gates that custom opens for the hunt and courtesy shuts behind it. The hunting field meets them in three ways: the huntsman or a whipper-in gallops ahead to fling them open and stands gate-keeper while hounds pour through; bold riders jump the locked ones, taking the five bars at a flying stride; and cautious followers queue, dismount, or find the gap. Gate-jumping is among the oldest tests of the hunter and rider alike, and the hunting-gate is its fixed furniture — lower and more inviting than a stone wall, higher and more honest than a hedge-gap. Cobbett, no friend of the hunt's landed world, lists the gates as sheer obstruction: things that stop wheels and prove, again, that this is horse-and-foot country.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Field gates used or jumped by the hunt (primary sense in context): the five-barred gates of hunting country described above. The bridge is compositional: hunting (the chase, the organized sport) plus gate (barred movable barrier) gives the gate as the hunt meets it — obstacle, thoroughfare, and test in one.
  • A gate as the barrier of managed game country generally: keepers' gates, cover gates, park gates controlling deer, pheasants, and vermin — the whole iron-and-oak furniture by which sporting estates regulate movement. The bridge generalizes from the fox-hunt to the estate: where game is managed, gates manage the managers.
  • Gate as movable barrier (the underlying noun's primary sense, Old English geat): any hinged or sliding frame that opens and shuts an entrance — garden gates, churchyard gates, railway gates. The bridge is the mechanism itself: a frame that bars and unbars, the oldest sense of the word, from which the hunting compound inherits its oak and iron.
  • Gate as entrance or gateway (extended sense): the gate of heaven, the gates of the city — the opening rather than the barrier. The bridge is metonymy: the frame names the passage it guards, so a hunting-gate can mean in context not the bars but the gap, the ride, the way through the hedge that the gate marks.
  • Gate as attendance or receipts (modern sporting sense): a good gate at a match — the takings counted through the turnstile. The bridge runs from race-meetings and hunt-point-to-points, where crowds pay at the gate: the barrier becomes the money it admits. Distant from Cobbett, but the same oak in modern dress.
  • Gate as starting barrier or flood barrier (technical senses): starting gates on a racecourse, sluice gates and flood gates on rivers, logic gates in circuits. The bridge in each is the gate's essential act — holding back and letting through — applied to horses, water, and electrons in turn.
  • -gate as scandal suffix (separate modern development, from Watergate, 1972): partygate, donated-gate, every public uproar with a hyphen. This is not the same lineage at all — it clips a hotel name, not a field barrier — and careful lexicography fences it off; it is included here only so no reader mistakes the hunting-gate's honest oak for journalism's borrowed hyphen.

Register and connotation: Hunting-gate is a shire word — Surtees, Trollope, the hunting diaries — smelling of saddle soap, wet oak, and November mud. For the hunting world it is hospitable: gates opened, held, and jumped are the small courtesies and courages of the day. For Cobbett's radical reader it is the opposite: one more piece of squirearchical furniture blocking the free passage of the poor man's England, the latch lifted for red coats and shut against everyone else. Modern feeling has complicated both views — the 2004 hunting ban emptied the gates of hounds while leaving them full of riders, walkers, and bridleway disputes — but the object stands unchanged: five bars of oak between one field and the next, daring somebody to jump it.
