Elbe (proper noun) — one of the great rivers of Central Europe, rising in the Giant Mountains (Krkonoše) of the northern Czech Republic, flowing roughly north-west through Bohemia and eastern Germany, and emptying into the North Sea near Cuxhaven; the Czech name is Labe, the German name Elbe. On this card the word appears in Edward Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, where the followers of Ida and Cerdic (the Anglo-Saxon settlers of Britain) brought "all the superstitions of the Elbe" — that is, of the pagan Germanic world east of the Romanized Rhine, for which the Elbe is the great emblematic river.

Sense 1 — The river itself (physical geography). The Elbe is roughly 1,094 km (680 miles) long, flowing through the Czech cities of Hradec Králové and Ústí nad Labem, crossing the German border, passing Dresden, Magdeburg, and Hamburg, and ending in a wide estuary on the North Sea. It is one of the four great rivers of Central Europe (with the Danube, Rhine, and Oder/Vistula) and the great east-west waterway of Germany, navigable far inland, connecting Bohemia to North Sea and Baltic trade. Its basin drains a significant part of the Czech Republic and eastern Germany. In geography the name appears in fixed compounds: the Elbe Valley, the Elbe river basin, the Elbe Sandstone Mountains (Saxon Switzerland) through which the river cuts the dramatic Bastei gorges, and the Elbe–Lübeck Canal and Elbe–Havel Canal that tie it to the wider European canal network.

Sense 2 — Classical antiquity: the frontier of the Roman world. To the Romans the river was Albis, and it carried enormous geopolitical weight. Augustus's stepson Drusus campaigned to its banks in 12–9 BCE and died before he could cross it; later Roman strategists, including Tiberius, dreamed of the Rhine–Elbe line as the permanent boundary of the empire. The dream failed: the disaster of the Teutoburg Forest (9 CE) ended Roman ambitions east of the Rhine, and the Elbe remained forever beyond the frontier. In this classical register "the Elbe" is shorthand for the eastern limit of Roman civilization — the river the eagles never crossed, the symbol of the unconquered Germanic world. This is the sense Gibbon activates: the "superstitions of the Elbe" are the pagan beliefs of the free Germany that Rome never subdued.

Sense 3 — Medieval and early modern history. In the Middle Ages the Elbe became the axis of eastern expansion. Its middle course was the heartland of the Ottonian and Salian empire, with Magdeburg rising on its banks as the imperial frontier city facing the Slavic lands; east of the river lay the territories of the Elbe Slavs (Obotrites, Sorbs), converted and colonized in the great eastward movement of the Ostsiedlung. Hamburg grew where the Alster meets the Elbe, becoming the gateway of the Hanseatic League's trade with the North Sea and the Baltic. The river thus carries a long political history as a divide between German and Slavic Europe, a division whose memory persisted into modern geopolitics, where the Iron Curtain ran along the Elbe at several points.

Sense 4 — World War II: the meeting of East and West. In the modern historical register "the Elbe" means above all Elbe Day, 25 April 1945, when Soviet and American troops met near Torgau on the middle Elbe — the symbolic handshake that split Nazi Germany in two and marked the approaching end of the war in Europe. The meeting made the river a durable image of East-West cooperation at the moment of victory, fixed in war memorials (the "Spirit of the Elbe" monument at Torgau) and in the iconography of the Allied victory.

Sense 5 — Symbolic and figurative register. Because of this history, "the Elbe" functions in literary and political prose as a synecdoche — the river stands for the pagan, unconquered Germanic world in Gibbon's usage; for the East-West divide of the Cold War in twentieth-century writing; and for the Rhine's eastern rival in the geography of European civilization. It is also a common element in place names (Ústí nad Labem, "mouth of the Elbe", in Czech; Děčín on the Elbe), and it supplies the standard navigational and flood-management term Elbe water level in Hamburg.

Distinguishing note — Labe and Albis. The modern Czech name Labe is not a different river; it is the same name in its Slavic form, adapted from the Germanic name of the river by regular Slavic sound change, and the two forms remain in official use in the two countries through which the river flows. The word's core referent — the great river itself — is stable, while its resonance shifts with the historical frame in which it is invoked.
