• Gallia: the Latin name of the same territory, identical in reference to historical "Gaul"; differs because it is the ancient source form used in classical scholarship and inscriptions, not a living English word.
  • Gallic: the adjective meaning "of Gaul" and, by transfer, "characteristically French"; differs because it is adjectival and has acquired a separate cultural sense ("Gallic humour") that the noun lacks.
  • Gaulish: the name of the Celtic language of Gaul; differs because it is strictly linguistic and does not denote the country or its people as a whole.
  • Celt: a speaker of a Celtic language, the wider ethnolinguistic category to which the Gauls belong; differs because Celts also include the Britons, Irish, Celtiberians and Galatians, so "Celt" is broader and not Roman-defined.
  • Celtic: the adjective covering the whole language family and its cultures; differs by its breadth, whereas "Gaul" is tied to one Roman-defined region.
  • Gallo-Roman: the mixed provincial culture of Gaul under Roman rule; differs because it names the hybrid period and civilisation, not the pre-Roman territory.
  • Frank: the Germanic people who took over Roman Gaul and gave their name to France; differs because the Franks were a Germanic ruling group, not the Celtic Gauls, and "Frank" carries its own semantic history ("free, candid").
  • Frenchman: a modern national of France; differs because it is a contemporary civic-national category, while "Gaul" is an ancient ethnic-geographic one used of modernity only as a joke.
  • Belgae: the northern tribal confederation Caesar distinguished within Gaul; differs because it is a sub-group name, not the whole.
  • Aquitania: the south-western third of Caesar's Gaul; differs by being a Roman regional division within Gaul.
  • Galatia: the Celtic settlement area in central Anatolia; differs because it names a distant colony of Celtic speakers, not the European homeland.
  • Wales: a country whose name descends from the same Germanic *walhaz root as "Gaul"; differs because it denotes a different territory and people, though the two words are etymological cousins.
