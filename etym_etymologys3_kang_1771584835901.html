A root of fire and drying The biography of kang begins in the Sino-Tibetan root *kaŋ / *kam, "to dry, to dry by heat" — a root of fire and smoke whose relatives run through the family: Written Tibetan skam "to dry, to become dry" and skem "to dry up, to make dry" preserve the same ancient shape, and related Tibeto-Burman forms meaning "dry" recur across the Lolo-Burmese and Kuki-Chin branches. The intellectual point is decisive for the word's character: unlike the furniture words of the Indo-European languages, which are named for posture — Latin lectus "bed", from "that on which one lies", and English bed, from a Germanic base possibly rooted in PIE *bhedh- "to dig", a resting place dug out — the kang is named for heat. A kang is not primarily "something one lies on"; it is "something that is dried by fire", the object being defined by the technology that makes it habitable. That single fact sets the whole word family on a different road from its European counterparts. The character as diagram In Chinese the word is written 炕, a character that is itself a small diagram of its meaning: the left component 火 is the radical for fire, and the right component 亢 (kàng) supplies the sound, with a hint of "high, uplifted". The character means "to dry by fire, to toast" — the verb is the primary, older sense, still alive in modern Chinese, where grain, cloth, and food may be kàng'd over a fire — and only secondarily, as a northern specialization, the heated platform-bed. The semantic development is a beautiful instance of a culture naming its furniture by its function: a platform that could be warmed by flues beneath it became, in the cold north of China, "the dried-and-heated bed", and the word that had meant "to dry by fire" came to name the great brick bed itself. Sound and form
  • Old Chinese *kʰˤaŋ-s (Baxter–Sagart) or *khāŋ (Schuessler) gives Middle Chinese *khang in the departing tone, and modern Mandarin kàng.
  • The English loanword was first spelled khang or k'ang in nineteenth-century travel and missionary literature, then fixed as kang by modern pinyin romanization.
  • The homophonous character 亢 kàng "high, extreme" is a different word, unrelated to the heating sense, and is used in 炕 only as a phonetic — a reminder that the Chinese writing system keeps words apart that sound alike.
Semantic drift and the object itself The kang the word names is one of the triumphs of vernacular architecture: a raised platform of brick and clay, hollow inside, with a flue network connected to the kitchen stove or a firebox, so that the smoke and heat of a single fire pass beneath the whole sleeping surface and out through a chimney. In the bitter winters of the northern plain and Manchuria it is the domestic heart of the home: at night the family sleeps across it in a row, each in his own quilt; by day it is a sitting place, a dining table, a workbench, a stage for visiting, gambling, and gossip. The poorer the household, the more the whole of life happens on the kang, which is why Chinese fiction is full of people "on the kang" for almost every activity. A single fire heats the platform, and the platform heats the family. Cultural and literary life The word entered English in the nineteenth century through travel and missionary accounts, which glossed it as "kang bed-stove", a doubled translation testifying to the fact that English had no single word for a bed that is also a stove. Modern pinyin fixes the spelling as kang, and the loanword has settled in as a precise, unapologetically Chinese word, used mostly with the definite article — the kang, on the kang. In The Story of the Stone, the "large kang underneath the window, covered with a scarlet Kashmir rug" — the rug itself a luxury import from the far Himalayas — is the point where northern Chinese technology and the international trade of the Jia household meet: the oldest kind of heat, built into brick, dressed in the finest wool of Kashmir, waiting for the family to climb up and be warm. For the English-reading world the word remains a small window onto a way of living that has no exact European name.
