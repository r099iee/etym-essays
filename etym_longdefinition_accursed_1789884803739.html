ACCURSED is an adjective meaning under a curse — doomed, damned, or hatefully detestable. “That accursed goose,” “the accursed day,” “this accursed traffic” all brand a person, animal, thing, or time as bringing evil, deserving hatred, or blighted by ill fortune. It blends supernatural malediction (may evil befall it) with everyday execration (I hate it), which is why it suits both fairy-tale witches and stuck jars, bewitched geese and miserable Mondays.

The primary sense is passive-participial: having been cursed, lying under imprecation or excommunication. From that theological darkness grows a weakened everyday sense, noted from the 1590s, in which accursed simply means damnable, abominable, wretched — worthy of a curse whether or not any priest or witch uttered one. Tone does the work: solemn in scripture, comic-exasperated in tales (“that accursed goose must be bewitched”).

  • Theological-passive sense — lying under a curse: “The accursed race,” “accursed be he who moves my bones.” Here the word reports a spiritual state: excommunicated, anathematised, consigned to evil fate. The bridge to everyday use is the transfer of divine verdict to human annoyance — what God damns, we damn in miniature.
  • Bewitched and ill-fated sense: “That accursed goose must be bewitched!” In folk narrative accursed explains uncanny obstruction: pans that stick, geese that will not cook, voyages that fail. It attributes malign agency — spell, ghost, devil — to stubborn misfortune, halfway between diagnosis and insult.
  • Weakened execrative sense — hateful, damnable, wretched: “This accursed cold,” “the accursed printer.” From the 1590s this became the commonest use: not literally cursed but cursed at. The semantic bridge is performative: to keep calling a thing accursed is to curse it afresh, so the adjective absorbs the speaker’s fury.
  • Literary intensifier for misery and obstruction: Poets and storytellers deploy accursed where very bad would fall flat. “Accursed hunger,” “accursed doubt,” “accursed spot” elevate suffering to cosmic scale. The word’s double c and hiss give it phonetic venom that damned shares and bad lacks.
  • Variant accurst and pronunciation: The clipped form accurst (“accurst luck”) preserves the older participle in -t, pronounced /əˈkɜːst/, beside fuller /əˈkɜːsɪd/. Both spellings mean the same; accurst feels terser, older, more poetic. The unetymological second c (15th c., imitating Latin acc-) is a Renaissance spelling affectation with no root in the curse-word itself.
  • Polysemy bridge — why curse covers prayer, church ban, spell, and swear-word: Old English curs meant both “prayer that evil befall” and “excommunication,” probably extended from Latin cursus “set of daily prayers” to “set of imprecations” in the Great Curse read quarterly in churches. From church ban the word spread to folk spell, then to angry speech (“cursing and swearing”), then to the blighted thing itself. Accursed sits at the end of that chain: the thing the curse landed on.
  • Register and boundaries: Accursed is stronger and more archaic than annoying or wretched, more literary than damned, less doctrinal than anathematised, and more hateful than ill-fated. It damns where unlucky pities and bewitched diagnoses. In modern speech it is often half-jocular, lending mock-epic weight to trivial tormentors like geese, gadgets, and traffic.

In short, ACCURSED means curse-struck and therefore hateful — whether by priest, witch, or exasperated speaker. Its secondary lives as comic insult, poetic intensifier, and fate-word all darken outward from the old church curse.
