stocky: (adjective) Short, broad, thick-set, and solidly built — a compact frame in which mass is packed into a small space, so that the body reads as strong, dense, firmly planted, and weight-bearing rather than tall, slender, or graceful. The word is used chiefly of people and animals, and the mental image behind it is the low, solid stump of a felled tree: something short but immovable, built to carry weight. In the context passage. The sentence "Like most Dwarfs he was very stocky and deep-chested" comes from C. S. Lewis's Narnian novel "Prince Caspian". Here stocky is doing quiet but essential characterisation: Lewis is establishing the Dwarf (the bound prisoner Trumpkin) as a physical type before he ever stands up. The detail that he is about three feet high and deep-chested works with stocky to build the picture of a body that is low and wide, dense and powerful — mass compressed into a small frame, rooted like a thick trunk. The nuance is important because a stocky body implies surprising heaviness and difficulty to move: in the plot this Dwarf must be tied with cords, tied to a stone, and handled as cargo, and his physical density makes that handling concrete. The tone is neutral-to-favourable: unlike squat or dumpy, stocky carries no contempt. It suggests a sturdy, well-proportioned compactness, even a faint respect, as if the body were built for weight-bearing and endurance. Polysemy and modern senses. Although stocky is overwhelmingly an adjective of physical build, it has a small but real spread of secondary and extended uses:
  • Of animals (veterinary and breeding jargon): a stocky pony, a stocky terrier, a stocky bull — animals bred for density, strength, and staying power rather than speed or elegance; the term is standard in descriptions of working and draft breeds.
  • Of plants and objects (botany and design): a stocky stem, stocky columns, stocky furniture — things that are thick relative to their height, built low and strong.
  • Of body parts: a stocky build, stocky legs, a stocky neck — the adjective freely modifies the whole frame or individual limbs.
  • Sports and martial jargon: rugby forwards, boxers, wrestlers, powerlifters, and weightlifters are routinely described as stocky; the word names the compact powerhouse physique prized in strength sports, where a low centre of gravity is an advantage.
  • Occasional figurative use: in literary criticism one meets "stocky prose" — dense, compressed, weighty writing with no spare flesh, transferring the physical image to style.
  • Derived and inflected forms: stockiness (noun), stockily (adverb), stockier / stockiest (comparative and superlative); the set is small but productive in casual speech.
The historical semantic bridge. The adjective is built on the noun stock, "a tree-trunk or stump cut close to the ground", plus the adjective-forming suffix -y ("having the nature of"). The bridge is purely visual: a tree-stock is short, broad, solid, and planted; a stocky person has the same geometry. Because the stump is a standing base — the rooted foundation of a tree — the transfer also borrows the stump's associations of stability and weight-bearing capacity. This is why stocky never carries the contempt of squat (excessively low and ungainly) or dumpy (softly and unpleasantly thick): it takes its dignity from the trunk. Collocations and register. Stocky collocates with frame, build, figure, body, man, woman, legs, shoulders, neck, pony, terrier, and most nouns of living creatures. Its register is standard and broadly informal-to-neutral — at home in sports writing, veterinary description, everyday speech, and narrative prose alike. In fantasy and adventure fiction it has become the default epithet for Dwarves and other short, strong peoples, where it does the double work of describing a build and signalling toughness and endurance. Synonym boundaries. The semantic neighbours differ chiefly in bulk, proportion, and tone:
  • thickset: the closest partner, almost interchangeable, though it leans toward the heaviness of the frame while stocky stresses the whole compact figure.
  • stout: bulk and portliness, soft weight — a stout person may be large without being compact; stocky is firm, dense muscle.
  • squat: shortness and breadth with a contemptuous edge of being excessively low or ungainly; stocky keeps better proportion and a positive tone.
  • burly: sheer size — a big, strong man; stocky says small but strong, the opposite of scale.
  • sturdy: robustness of build or of objects generally, without the specific short-and-broad geometry of stocky.
  • heavyset / heavily built: plain physical density without the connotation of proportion or dignity that stocky keeps.
