• Psychosis: Similar because both are major historical categories of mental disorder; differs fundamentally because psychosis involves a severe loss of contact with external reality (characterized by delusions, hallucinations, and severe thought disorganization), whereas an individual with a neurosis remains fully aware of reality and recognizes their symptoms as distressing.
  • Anxiety: Similar because anxiety is often the primary emotional symptom of a neurosis; differs because anxiety is a specific emotional state or symptom of apprehension, whereas neurosis is the overarching psychological condition, conflict structure, or personality pattern.
  • Obsession: Similar because obsessions are common manifestations of neurotic disorders; differs because an obsession is an intrusive, persistent thought or impulse, whereas neurosis is the broader diagnostic and psychodynamic framework.
  • Phobia: Similar because phobias are categorized as neurotic anxiety manifestations; differs because a phobia is an irrational, intense fear targeted at a specific object or situation (e.g., heights, spiders), rather than a generalized psychological conflict.
  • Hysteria: Similar because hysteria was historically considered the archetypal form of psychoneurosis in Freudian theory; differs because hysteria specifically refers to emotional excitability converted into physical bodily symptoms (conversion disorder) or dramatic emotional outbursts.
  • Melancholia: Similar because both involve chronic emotional suffering; differs because melancholia refers specifically to deep, profound, visceral depression and despair, historically tied to humoral black bile.
