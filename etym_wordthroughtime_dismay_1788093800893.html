- c. 1300: the verb dismaien enters Middle English from Old French — “to alarm, terrify, dishearten” — a strong word of battle and dread; the noun dismai follows immediately as “terror, consternation, rout”.
- 14th–15th c.: stock emotion of chivalric romance — knights and armies “in dismay” before giants, sieges and bad news; the word of broken courage in war and quest.
- 16th–17th c.: peak strength — the Authorised Version’s “fear not, neither be dismayed” (dozens of times), Shakespeare’s and Milton’s dismayed hosts; to dismay = to rout, to unman utterly.
- 18th–19th c.: gradual softening from terror toward “disappointment plus loss of confidence”; the formula “to my dismay” becomes polite adult disappointment (“much to my dismay, the post had not come”); the historical novel revives the strong battle-sense for colour.
- 20th–21st c.: everyday formal word — journalistic, polite, slightly bookish; the complexity of alarm-plus-dejection remains, but the antique power lives only in literature and in the fixed phrase “in dismay”.
