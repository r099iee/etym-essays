carbuncles (noun, plural) — deep red gemstones, most commonly garnets cut en cabochon: polished into a smooth, rounded, unfaceted dome that in strong light appears to glow with an internal fire. The name means, literally, “little coals,” from the Latin carbo, “a coal.” In the treasure-chamber passage the word appears in the great catalogue of the hoard of Cair Paravel: “diamonds, rubies, carbuncles, emeralds, topazes, and amethysts.” The nuance is burning, blood-deep colour: unlike the hard brilliant fire of cut diamonds, the carbuncle is a smouldering, coal-like glow, and its very name links the hoard to heat and life. Set among rubies (bright, faceted, noble red) and emeralds (cool green), the carbuncle supplies the treasure’s darkest, richest fire — a stone that seems to hold an ember inside it, appropriate to a chamber that is “bitterly cold” and deathly still yet piled with glowing wealth. The word is deliberately old-fashioned here, a period-flavoured choice that makes the list feel medieval and heraldic rather than modern.

Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Gem sense (primary, historical): a deep-red precious stone, most commonly a garnet, polished without facets; in older usage the name covered any red stone that glowed like a coal, including the ruby and the spinel.
  • Mineralogical sense: in strict mineralogy, the “carbuncle” is a red garnet (pyrope, almandine, or rhodolite) cut as a cabochon dome; the word survives mainly in historical and heraldic contexts.
  • Medical sense: a carbuncle is also a deep, painful cluster of interconnected boils, an abscess that burns like a coal; this sense is current in medicine and descends from the same Latin word.
  • Heraldic sense: in heraldry, a “carbuncle” (escarboucle) is a heraldic charge: a wheel-like figure of eight radiating spokes, representing a gem; the word appears in the blazons of medieval arms.
  • Zoological sense: “carbuncle” names the scarlet-coloured wart or growth on the head of a turkey and similar fowl — a small red swelling, again the “burning lump.”
  • Architectural and satirical sense: “the monstrous carbuncle” — Prince Charles’s phrase (1984) for a proposed extension to the National Gallery on Trafalgar Square; “carbuncle” became a term of architectural abuse for jarring, ugly structures on the face of a loved city.
  • Chemical connection: the element carbon takes its name from the same Latin carbo; the word-family thus links a jewel, a boil, and a chemical element to the single image of a burning coal.
The historical bridges are all built on the image of the ember. Because a rounded, unfaceted red garnet glows in light like a live coal, the Romans named it a “little coal” (carbunculus), and the medical abscess was named for the same burning sensation; the heraldic charge, the turkey’s wart, and the architectural insult all continue the “glowing lump” idea. The modern decline of the gem sense is a mineralogical one — jewellers adopted “ruby” and “garnet,” leaving “carbuncle” to antiquarian catalogues and fantasy prose — while the medical sense has stayed in ordinary professional use, still burning like a coal.

Carbuncle must be set against the other red stones. Ruby is the modern jeweller’s word for transparent red corundum of the highest value, typically brilliant-cut; a ruby is bright, hard, and faceted, while a carbuncle is a smouldering, unfaceted glow. Garnet is the mineral family (pyrope, almandine, spessartine) to which most carbuncles actually belong; “carbuncle” is the old shape-and-glow term, so the two are related as tradition is related to mineralogy. Spinel (“balas ruby”) is a distinct red stone that medieval gem-lists often confused with the ruby. Bloodstone (heliotrope) is a dark green stone flecked with red — a different effect entirely. Carnelian is a red-brown chalcedony, translucent and modest. And the same word’s medical twin — the burning boil — reminds us that the stone’s name was always about fire rather than faceting.
