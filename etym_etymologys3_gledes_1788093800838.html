The PIE root of shining. Glede's story begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *ǵʰel- (in systems with laryngeals, *ǵʰelh₁-) "to shine, glow; yellow, green", one of the most productive roots in the whole family. Greek draws from it khlōrós "green-yellow" (whence chlorine and chlorophyll); the Balto-Slavic and Germanic worlds drew from it their words for yellow and gold — Old English geolu "yellow", gold — and the Germanic gleam-and-glitter family: glimmer, glitter, gleam, plus the glow words treated here. The semantic core is warm visible light — the light of burning matter and of the sun — and the root's Germanic children cluster around exactly that: to shine warmly, to glow.

Proto-Germanic: glow and its ember. From *ǵʰel- the Germanic dialects built the strong verb *glōaną "to glow, shine", which appears in Old English as glōwan (our glow), with cognates in Old Saxon glōian, Old High German gluoen, Old Norse glóa. From the same root, but with the root vowel shifted to the long ō-grade, Germanic formed the feminine noun *glōdiz "a glowing ember, a live coal" — literally "a glowing thing". In Old English this noun is glēd (feminine ō-stem, genitive glēde, plural glēda); its siblings are Old Norse glóð, Old Saxon glōd, Old High German gluot, and modern German Glut "glowing heat, embers". The relationship of glede to glow is therefore not derivation but ablaut: sister formations from one root, one meaning "the act of glowing", the other "the thing that glows" — the sibling pair persisting recognisably into modern English.

Old English: glēd in the Anglo-Saxon world. In Old English glēd is an ordinary, everyday word — the emphatically named ember of every hearth. The Anglo-Saxon hall ran on gledes: fire was kept overnight as gledes raked together, rekindled from gledes in the morning, and carried from house to house as gledes in a pot; the forge, the funeral pyre and the beacon all worked in gledes. The word appears across the record: in glosses (Latin prūna and carbo glossed with glēd), in prose — recipes, penitentials, medical charms — and in poetry, where hell-fire and heroism alike burn in gledes. Christian imagination of the fiery pit made the word one of both comfort and terror: the same glede warmed the peasant's hands and blazed in the everlasting fire of judgement.

Middle English: glede in the medieval household. Middle English continues the word as glede and gleed (also gleede), "a live coal, a glowing ember, a firebrand", attested throughout the period in every register: in religious prose, including Wycliffite texts, where the coals of divine judgement are rendered with gledes; in the great romances and lyrics, where the ember is the type of the flame that may be quenched or rekindled; and in practical and medical texts, where a glede is used to cauterise, to light, to warm. The medieval household kept the word as its ordinary ember-word, and the standing image of the single glede kept alive through the night carried the fire's promise into proverb and lyric alike — the ember as hope itself.

The great retreat: 15th–17th centuries. In the later medieval and early modern period, standard southern English gradually preferred other words: ember — itself native (Old English ǣmerge, reshaped under the influence of the Norse cognate eimyrja "glowing ash") — and coal, which spread as the blanket word for fuel and burnt remnant alike. This was not a French invasion but a native synonym competition: three good English words for the ember shook down to two, and glede lost the standard registers, retreating northward and into dialect. In northern English — especially Yorkshire — gleed never died: regional dictionaries and dialect surveys record it as a living word for "a live coal" into the 20th century, still used in the exact domestic functions the Old English glosses describe a millennium earlier.

The 19th–20th centuries: poetic revival and fantasy diction. The 19th-century revival of archaic diction — historical romance, Victorian medievalism, dialect literature — brought glede back as a deliberate literary archaism: rust-coloured, old-fashioned, burnished, a word for an ember that glows with history. The 20th century's fantasy and heroic fiction inherited it wholesale; the defining modern attestation is Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937): "His last throes splintered it to sparks and gledes", where the archaic plural glows in context with perfect precision — the wreckage of the dragon's death still burning. For modern readers gledes are encountered chiefly in such epic prose, where the word's obscurity is itself the point: it sounds old because it is old, a genuine medieval ember carried into the modern fire.

Distinguish the homonym. Unrelated: glede "the kite (bird of prey)", from Old English glida, from glīdan "to glide" — the bird that glides. The ember glows (glēd), the bird glides (glida); the homophony is a lexicographical trap as old as the dictionaries that first had to separate the two entries, and a reminder that identical spellings can hide opposite histories.

Summary of the drift. Gledes flows from the PIE shining-root *ǵʰel- through Germanic *glōaną "to glow" and its ablaut sister *glōdiz "ember" into Old English glēd — an everyday Anglo-Saxon word of hearth and hell — through a millennium of Middle English household use, a steady retreat to northern dialect and literary archaism after the synonym competition of the late medieval period, and a final 20th-century afterlife in fantasy diction, where Tolkien's "sparks and gledes" keeps the oldest English word for a live coal glowing in the modern language.
