shelving: Sloping or inclining gradually, in the manner of a series of ledges or shelves; of a shore or seabed, descending stepwise toward the water.

Primary sense and the passage

In the passage the word is geological: the Martian shore rises "high up" in a series of gentle, step-like inclines, and the shelving of the ground is the fossil record of waters that have slowly receded, leaving the riverbed or lakebed terraced behind them. The participle makes the landscape itself a staircase: the shore does not fall steeply; it shelves upward, in flat stages, each a ledge left by an older waterline. The word thus tells the deep history of the scene — a dry basin, a vanished sea — without ever saying so directly. It also carries a faint nautical flavor: a "shelving shore" is one a boat can approach, a beach that slopes gradually into the water and shoals gently.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings

  • (1) placing on storage shelves: to shelve books; shelving as the noun for the whole apparatus of shelves (book-shelving, shelving units).
  • (2) postponing or canceling: to shelve a project, shelving the bill in committee — a metaphor of laying a plan aside on a shelf, recorded from the 17th century.
  • (3) sloping underwater seabed / shelving coast: the geological and nautical sense, of shores and banks that descend in ledges.
  • (4) in engineering and signal processing (transferred): a shelving filter, in audio, boosts or cuts a range of frequencies, so called because the response curve resembles a shelf, stepping up or down — a strikingly apt modern echo of the stepped-shore meaning.
The historical bridge is the word "shelf" itself, which from Old English scylfe named both a household board and a ledge of rock; the verb to shelve then split into the domestic "place on shelves" and the geological "slope in ledges," and the postponing sense grew from the domestic one by metaphor.

Differentiating terms

  • sloping: a uniform, featureless incline; shelving suggests flat stages, a slope built ledge by ledge.
  • inclined: geometric and technical, the language of planes.
  • declivitous / precipitous: steep descent — the opposite sense.
  • terraced: artificial, regular steps cut by labor or agriculture; shelving is the natural step-wise incline of rock.
  • stepped: generic; shelving alone names the homely image of the household shelf applied to the land.
The word's odd beauty is that the homely household shelf and the grand geological shelving shore are the same root: the land is furnished with shelves as a wall might be, each one a flat shelf cut out of the steep by the slow withdrawal of water.

Grammar and word family

Shelving is a present participle: it works both as a descriptive adjective (a shelving shore) and as a verbal noun (the shelving of the seabed). Its verb shelve belongs to the small class of English verbs derived from nouns whose final consonant shifts when the noun is plural or the verb is formed — shelf/shelves/shelve, as in leaf/leaves and wolf/wolves — a fossil of the Old English rule by which a word-final fricative became voiced between vowels. The noun shelving (a set of shelves, the activity of placing things on shelves) is a separate, domestic derivation recorded from the seventeenth century. The word family includes the adjective shelved (placed on shelves; postponed; sloping in ledges), and the transferred technical term shelving filter in audio engineering.

Register and use

In the passage shelving is pure, precise description in the high register of adventure prose — a single participle that lets the reader see a staircase cut into rock. In ordinary modern speech the word is rarely used of land; the domestic sense (putting things on shelves, the shelving in a cupboard) and the bureaucratic sense (shelving a plan) have nearly displaced it. Yet whenever a sailor, geologist, or coastal surveyor speaks of a shelving shore or a shelving bottom, the ancient double life of the word — board and ledge, cupboard and cliff — springs fully awake, and the land once more appears furnished, shelf by shelf, by the slow withdrawal of water.
