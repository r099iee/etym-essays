- 1520s — the beast named: badger (earlier bager) enters English for the grey burrowing mammal, likely for the white badge blazing its head.
- 16th–18th c. — the pit: badger-baiting becomes a prime spectacle — terriers sent down the sett to drag the beast out — and to badger is born in the crowd's roar.
- 1790s — the verb escapes: to badger first means to bait and torment a person as the dogs baited the beast; schoolyards and barracks adopt it.
- 19th c. — the cruelty dies, the word lives: baiting is outlawed (1835), but badgering thrives as the standard term for nagging pressure — the pit closed, the metaphor open.
- 20th c. — professionals badger: journalists badger ministers, lawyers badger witnesses, salesmen badger households — relentlessness becomes a job skill.
- Today — the siege mechanised: call centres, canvassers, and notifications badger by algorithm, while Tolkien's ring reminds every reader that the oldest badgering is personal — one voice, one question, never dropped.
