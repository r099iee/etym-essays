Single linear derivation chain for HEAP (one inherited root):
1. PIE *keup- (variant *kewp-) — "to bend, arch, swell; a vaulted mass" (reconstructed; per Pokorny; attested in Baltic and Germanic; also the base of Latin cūpa "tub, cask", whose connection is debated — see note).
2. Proto-Germanic *haupaz — "heap, pile; crowd" (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE – 200 CE; the initial *k- shifted to *h- by Grimm's Law and *eu became *au).
3. Old English hēap — "heap, pile; troop, band, multitude" (attested from the 8th c., e.g. in Beowulf, where a war-band is a hēap).
4. Middle English heep / hepe — "pile, heap" (13th–15th c.; the "troop, band" sense dies out during this period).
5. Modern English heap — "an untidy pile; a large quantity" (from the 16th c. onward; "heaps of" quantity idioms from the 17th c.; the computing "heap" senses from the 20th c.).
Notes on debated / nuanced stages: (a) The reconstruction of the PIE root is not universally agreed. Pokorny posits *keu- "to bend, arch" with a *-p- extension; de Vaan treats Latin cūpa "cask" as of uncertain connection and cautions that the various "heap/cup" words across the family may be independent formations. The Germanic *haupaz and the Baltic Lithuanian kaupas "heap" (attested) are the securely cognate pair. (b) The Old English "troop, band" sense is well attested; it died out by Middle English and survives only by coincidence in the modern social metaphor "the bottom of the heap."
