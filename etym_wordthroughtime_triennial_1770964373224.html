- 17th c.: enters English as a Latin-derived adjective (tri- "three" + annus "year"), meaning recurring every three years or lasting three years; a formal, learned coinage used in ecclesiastical and institutional schedules.
- 18th–19th c.: becomes a bureaucratic anchor of public life — triennial elections, triennial parliaments, triennial examinations — its weight signalling rarity, structure, and long-anticipated institutional rhythm.
- 20th c.: extends into organizational and cultural domains (triennial conferences, the Venice Biennale's cousins, triennial art shows), retaining a formal, official flavour.
- Contemporary: still a specialized, formal adjective of scheduling; in casual speech people say "every three years", leaving "triennial" to institutional, academic, and official registers.
