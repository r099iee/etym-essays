Stage 1 — PIE root *dʰǵʰem- "earth, ground" (reconstructed, c. 4500-3000 BCE): attested in Greek khthṓn "earth" (whence English chthonic), Sanskrit kṣam "earth", Hittite tēkan "earth", Lithuanian žemė, and Latin humus "ground, soil"; the same root appears in Germanic as the word for "earthling, human" (*guman-, whence English bridegroom and the archaic guma "man"). Stage 2 — Latin humus "ground, soil, earth" (Classical Latin): from PIE *dʰǵʰem- via regular Italic sound changes (initial *dʰǵʰ- > Latin h). The family built on it includes humilis "low, humble" (hence English humble, humility), humāre "to bury" (hence inhume, exhume), and humēre "to be moist, damp" — the earth being characteristically damp. Stage 3 — Latin humēre "to be moist, wet, damp" (Classical Latin): a denominal verb from humus, literally "to be of the earth", hence "to be damp", because the ground is damp. Stage 4 — Latin hūmor "moisture, liquid, fluid" (Classical Latin, attested from Plautus, 2nd-1st c. BCE; also spelled umor in many manuscripts): a noun derived from humēre, naming any bodily or natural fluid. Stage 5 — Old French humour "moisture; (humoral medicine) one of the four bodily fluids; temperament" (12th-14th c.): inherited from Latin hūmor. Stage 6 — Middle English humour "a bodily fluid; one of the four humours; state of mind" (14th c., attested c. 1340-1387 in Chaucer): borrowed from Old French with the full medical range. Stage 7 — English humour "temperament, mood" (16th c.) → "a disposition that finds amusement; the comic quality" (17th-18th c., via Ben Jonson's comedies of humours and the emerging "sense of humour") → Modern English HUMOUR (the word in its British spelling). Note on the debate: an older popular etymology connected hūmor to a PIE root meaning "to be wet" (linking it to Sanskrit ukṣati "to wet, sprinkle"); the standard handbooks reject this, because the Latin word is transparently derived from humus "ground" through humēre "to be damp". The honest chain therefore runs "earth" → "ground" → "dampness" → "fluid" → "bodily fluid" → "temperament" → "mood" → "comic quality".
