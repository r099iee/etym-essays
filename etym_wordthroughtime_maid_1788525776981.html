- Old English (c. 8th–11th c.) - Existed in the uncontracted form mæġden / mæġeþ (feminine diminutive of mæġ / magu, 'child, youth'), denoting an unmarried girl, virgin, or maiden of noble or free status - Youthful female purity and kinship.
- Middle English (c. 12th–15th c.) - Contracted phonologically from mayden into mayde / maid; used broadly in Arthurian romance, courtly love poetry, and religious literature (the 'Blessed Maid' for the Virgin Mary) to signify an unmarried virgin woman - Chivalric, spiritual, and romantic veneration.
- 16th–17th Century (Early Modern English) - Developed a specialized socio-economic meaning denoting a female domestic servant or attendant (short for 'maidservant', 'chambermaid', or 'milkmaid') alongside its poetic sense of young virgin - Domestic labor and social stratification.
- 18th–19th Century (Victorian Era) - Solidified simultaneously as an indispensable professional title for domestic household staff in Victorian class hierarchy, and as an exalted, romantic archetype in aesthetic literature and poetry (e.g. Oscar Wilde's 'the birth of passion in the soul of a man and a maid') - Domestic service vs. high romantic poetry.
- 20th–21st Century - Preserved in professional hospitality and housekeeping services ('hotel maid', 'maid service') and retained in idioms ('maid of honor', 'old maid') and literary/historical contexts - Specialized occupational, idiomatic, and literary descriptor.
