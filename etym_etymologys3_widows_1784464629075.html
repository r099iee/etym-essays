Widow is one of the oldest social words in the English vocabulary — an inherited status-term whose Indo-European pedigree goes back to the second or third millennium BCE and whose reflexes cover nearly the whole family. It is also a rare case of a word whose meaning has stayed remarkably stable for thousands of years: a woman left solitary by her husband's death.

Stage one: Proto-Indo-European. The reconstructed PIE form is *h₁widʰéwh₂ (a feminine noun built on the root *h₁widʰ-, "to be empty, to separate, to be bereft"). The ancient grammarians of PIE used the very word as a textbook example of an archaic feminine formation with the suffix *-éwh₂. The root idea — "emptied", "set apart" — describes the widow as the woman separated from her husband by death. The category was so old that every branch of the family inherited it independently rather than borrowing it.

  • Indo-Iranian — Sanskrit vidhávā "widow" (Rigveda); Avestan viðavā.
  • Italic — Latin vidua "widow" (also "bereft, deprived").
  • Slavic — Old Church Slavonic vъdova; Russian vdová; Polish wdowa.
  • Baltic — Lithuanian vidova (older); Latvian atraitne (replaced).
  • Germanic — Gothic widuwō (in the Gothic Bible); Old Norse ekkja (a replacement); Old High German wituwa (German Witwe); Old English widuwe / widewe.

Stage two: Germanic and Old English. Proto-Germanic inherited the word as *widuwōn (or *widu(w)ǭ), and the West Germanic branches preserved it consistently. Old English has widuwe, widewe, and wuduwe — spelling variants of the same word, all meaning "a woman whose husband is dead". The word appears throughout Old English legal and religious texts, where the widow is a protected class: Anglo-Saxon law-codes and the Church both legislated for the widow's rights, her portion, and her remarriage, so the word is embedded in the social history of early English institutions from the start.

Stage three: Middle English. Middle English levels the variants toward widwe and then widow (with spellings widou, widdow, etc.). The word's grammatical peculiarity — an -s genitive plural widows' — feeds the fixed collocations of ballad and tale: "the widow's son", "widows' sons", the stock figure of the fatherless boy. The male counterpart widower is a later Middle English formation (14th century), built by analogy on the pattern of agent-nouns, which shows that the base word was felt to be inherently feminine.

Stage four: modern English — stability and metaphor. The literal sense has not drifted: a widow is still a woman whose husband has died and who has not remarried, with all the legal and social consequences — inheritance, mourning, remarriage customs. What grew around it is a web of idiom and metaphor:

  • Widow's cruse — the jar of oil that never fails (2 Kings 4), a type of inexhaustible provision.
  • Widow's mite — the poor widow's two small coins (Mark 12:41–44), a type of generous self-giving.
  • Grass widow — historically a wife whose husband is away; later, a separated or divorced woman.
  • Widow's peak — the V-shaped hairline, so called from the peaked mourning-caps of widows.
  • Typographical widow — a single paragraph line stranded at the top of a page, metaphorically "left alone"; paired with its opposite, the orphan (a line stranded at the bottom).

Sound change. The phonetic path runs *widuwōn → widewe / wuduwe → widwe → widow /ˈwɪdəʊ/ (RP) — a classic example of the loss of medial unstressed syllables (syncope) that reshaped English disyllables between Old and Middle English. The spelling widow is fixed by the 16th century.

The Tolkien context. "The unexpected luck of widows' sons" is not demography; it is genre recognition. In the fairy-tale and ballad tradition, the widow's son is the paradigmatic underdog — the boy without a father's protection or inheritance who nonetheless wins fortune through luck, courage, or hidden noble birth. Gandalf is remembered as the teller of the oldest plot-shapes — dragons, goblins, giants, rescued princesses, and the fortune of the widow's boy — and the plural genitive widows' is exactly the text's form, the base of which the card preserves.
