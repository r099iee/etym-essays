stubbed is the simple past tense and past participle of the transitive verb to stub, meaning to strike or knock one's toe (or foot) painfully against something hard and fixed that projects from a surface — a stump, a stone, a doorstep, the leg of a chair. The verb presupposes a fixed, unyielding obstacle: the toe comes abruptly up against something that does not give, so the collision produces a sudden jolt of sharp, localized pain, momentary but intense. In the literary context — “It was not too difficult for the hobbit, except when, in spite of all care, he stubbed his poor toes again, several times, on nasty jagged stones in the floor” — the verb names the small, sharp mishap of a wanderer feeling his way in the dark: his toes, unprotected, come up against the jagged edges of the stone floor. The nuance is of a sudden jolt of localized pain; the object struck is dead and does not give. The word also carries a homely, sympathetic tone: “his poor toes” makes the accident comic-pathetic rather than heroic — a comfortable, domestic kind of suffering every reader recognizes from his own stubbed toes. Because the verb's noun base, a stub, is something blunt, short, and leftover, there is also a faint suggestion of the toe's clumsy collision with a leftover fragment of the world, a projection that is itself stub-like. The register is ordinary, colloquial, and tactile, in deliberate contrast to the grander verbs for blows (strike, smite): Tolkien uses it to keep us firmly inside Bilbo's small, physical, unheroic point of view, stumbling through the dark with tender, funny awkwardness.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings — the word family is remarkably productive:

  • The verb's core sense (19th c.) — to strike the toe against a fixed projection. “He stubbed his toe against the bedpost.” This is the sense used in the passage; it is transitive and takes the body part as object.
  • The noun stub — a short, blunt, projecting remnant: the tree-stump (the oldest sense, from Old English), a pencil stub, a ticket stub, the stub of a candle or cigarette, a cheque stub or counterfoil. The semantic core is “what is left over after the rest has been used, cut off, or torn away”.
  • To stub up / stub (land) (15th c.) — to clear ground of stumps and roots, hard agricultural labour. “They stubbed the field for planting.”
  • To stub out (20th c.) — to extinguish a cigarette or cigar by pressing its burning tip against a surface. The image is of crushing a burning remnant.
  • Stubbed (adjective) — short and blunt, like a stub: a stubbed tail, a stubbed cigar, a stubbed feather. It emphasises the leftover, truncated shape.
  • Technical and modern jargon — in computing, a stub is a placeholder routine or a minimal stand-in for code not yet written; in version control, a stub is an empty placeholder branch or file; in accounting and finance, a stub is the detached counterfoil of a cheque or receipt and, in corporate finance, the residual value left after a buyout. All descend from the “short leftover remnant” sense.
  • Idiomatic use — “to stub one's toe” on a plan or deal is a minor setback or hitch; the physical mishap becomes a metaphor for a small, awkward obstacle in a larger undertaking.


Among near-synonyms, each shades differently:

  • knock: similar in striking against something hard; differs because knocking is generic contact, often deliberate or aimed at a door, with no special link to toes or feet.
  • bang: similar in a painful impact against a fixed object; differs because a bang is the heavier blow of a broader body part or object, not the sharp, localized jolt of a toe.
  • strike: similar in dealing a blow; differs because striking is deliberate and forceful, whereas stubbing is accidental and reserved for a toe against a fixed projection.
  • trip: similar in a foot-catching mishap on uneven ground; differs because tripping involves losing balance and falling, whereas stubbing merely strikes the toe and leaves you standing.
  • collide: similar in coming forcefully together; differs because a collision requires two moving bodies, while the obstacle in stubbing is fixed and still.
  • bash: similar in a hard accidental impact; differs because bashing implies greater force and a broader area, often of the shin or head rather than the toe.
  • bark: similar in injuring a limb on a hard object; differs because “to bark one's shin” scrapes the skin, while stubbing is a blunt, bruising impact, not a scrape.


In register, stub (verb) is informal, colloquial, and homely — the archetypal minor domestic accident. It is not used of serious injury: a stubbed toe is painful but never heroic, and the word's blunt, stumpy character makes it the perfect vehicle for the comic-pathetic sympathy Tolkien cultivates in the passage.
