The loam story begins in mud — honest, sucking, shape-holding mud. Its deepest traceable ancestor is a PIE root meaning slimy, sticky, to smear (*ley- and its scholarly variants), the kind of root gloss that tells you exactly what prehistoric fingers felt: the slipperiness of wet clay between thumb and forefinger. From it Germanic builds *laimaz, clay-earth, which gives German Lehm as well as English loam — a shared inheritance of peoples who daubed wattle walls, threw pots, and knew good brick-earth from bad at a glance. Old English lam covers the whole sticky spectrum: mud, clay, mortar, the potter's wedge, the dauber's batch. There is no praise in it yet; it is simply the stuff that is neither stone nor sand, the earth's middle register.

Medieval London teaches England the commercial value of that middle register. As the city rebuilds in brick from the fourteenth century onward — brickearth dug from the Thames terraces, tiles fired in Kilburn and Hackney kilns — lome becomes an industrial material with pits, prices, and carriers. The great brickearth belt north and west of London, laid down by Ice Age winds and Thames floods, employs thousands of diggers and moulders; whole Middlesex parishes that Cobbett later walks are honeycombed with old loam-pits turned to ponds and osier-beds. Meanwhile husbandry writers begin the long sorting of earths that will end in soil science: Fitzherbert's Boke of Husbandry (1523) distinguishes clays, sands, and gravels by crop; Tusser versifies the teaching; and by the seventeenth century the improving farmer knows that the best wheat land is neither stark clay nor blowing sand but the mixture — the loam, though the formal definition still lies centuries ahead.

The Agricultural Revolution (roughly 1700–1850) makes loam a star. Jethro Tull's hoeing husbandry, Townshend's Norfolk four-course, Bakewell's stock, and Young's tours all preach that texture is destiny: drain the clays, marl the sands, manure everything, and the loam will pay. Arthur Young's county surveys map loams parish by parish as national assets; the Board of Agriculture's reports price land by its loam. Cobbett writes inside this discourse and against its complacency: his Rural Rides soil-readings — black loam here, spewy gravel there, chalk downs yonder — are audits of improvement, distinguishing land that feeds men from land that feeds fundholders. In the source sentence the black loam over Thames gravel is genuinely first-rate market-garden earth (the riverside belt that fed London's vegetables), and his fury is that such land supports tea-garden boxes and gin-drinking poverty instead of independent husbandmen. Soil, for Cobbett, is moral evidence, and loam is exhibit A.

From brickearth to texture triangle:

  • The foundry's debt: bell-founders and cannon-founders adopt loam moulding — sand, clay, horse-dung, and water packed around a pattern — because loam takes and holds an impression through white heat. Whitechapel's bells, Big Ben among their kin, are loam's children; the vocabulary of the brickfield (mould, pattern, casting) transfers to bronze and iron.
  • Victorian chemistry: Liebig, Lawes, and Gilbert at Rothamsted analyse what loam always knew — nitrogen, phosphate, potash — and artificial manures begin to supplement the dung that made black loam black; the colour remains the farmer's quick assay long after the laboratory offers slower, surer ones.
  • The texture triangle: twentieth-century soil surveys formalise loam as the centre of the USDA triangle — roughly 40-40-20 sand-silt-clay with plain loam at the heart and sandy, silty, and clay loams around it. The diagram hangs in every agricultural college: the bullseye every soil is judged against.
  • Garden-centre loam: meanwhile John Innes composts (1930s) bottle loam for pots — sterilised medium loam, peat, and sand in numbered recipes — carrying the field ideal onto the potting bench; every bag of John Innes No. 3 is a sack of standardised loam.
  • Poetry's keepsake: while science measures, literature hallows — native loam, English loam, the loam of first gardens — and the word keeps the affection no texture percentage can state: loam is the earth that loves the spade back.

So from smear-root to seedbed, from daubed hut to bell-mould to Rothamsted plot, loam has travelled from the stickiest to the most beloved of earths — and Cobbett's black band over Thames gravel remains the perfect emblem: wealth underfoot, wasted above, the good earth testifying against the bad economy. Dig it, smell it, squeeze it: the crumb that holds and breaks is ten thousand years of fingers learning the same lesson — this, and not sand or clay alone, is the ground that feeds.
