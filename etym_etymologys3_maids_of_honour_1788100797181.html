Part I — the word MAID. The English noun maid is a shortened form of maiden, which descends from Old English mægden ("maiden, virgin, girl"), itself a diminutive built on mæg(e)þ ("maiden, virgin; girl of marriageable age"). Behind the Old English forms stands a Proto-Germanic stem *magad- / *magadin- meaning "young woman, virgin", and behind that a reconstructed PIE adjective or noun *maghu-, "young, unmarried (person)", which older reference works connected — now rejected — with Sanskrit maghavan- "bountiful"; modern authorities accept the wider set of cognates only with reservations, since the evidence is thin. In Old English the word sat between two poles: it meant "virgin, unmarried woman" on one side and "female servant, handmaid" on the other — the Virgin Mary is repeatedly called a mægden in Old English religious verse, while gospel glosses use the same word for the serving girl at Peter's trial. These two poles survived into Middle English maide, where the apocopated shorter form gradually displaced the longer maiden in everyday speech, though maiden remained as a literary and legal word. From the fourteenth century onward maid carried decisive social meaning: an unmarried woman, especially a young one, whose honour and reputation were tightly regulated; a maid who lost her virginity famously "lost her name". It also named female domestic servants, so that by Shakespeare's day a maid could be both a virgin and a servant — the milkmaid, the serving-maid, Maid Marian — a double life that made the court title "maid of honour" instantly legible: a young noblewoman serving, but serving with honour, in the Queen's household, her virtue part of her office.


Part II — the word HONOUR. English honour comes from Middle English honour, taken in the thirteenth century from Anglo-French and Old French honor, which in turn descends from Latin honōs (accusative honōrem) — "esteem, repute, public distinction, office, reward". Latin honōs is famous among etymologists as a word with no secure Indo-European ancestry: de Vaan's comparative dictionary marks it as lacking a convincing etymology, and older attempts to link it to roots meaning "to load" or "to bear" have not won acceptance; the Romans themselves felt it as a quasi-religious abstraction, and it was deified as the goddess Honour (Honos), who had temples in the Republic. Old French reshaped the form with the -our suffix that marks so many Romance loanwords in English, and the fifteenth-century spelling wars fixed honour in Britain. The Renaissance re-Latinisation touched the spelling (-or appears in some Elizabethan prints), but Noah Webster's American dictionary of 1828 canonically fixed honor for the United States while Britain retained honour — a spelling split still visible today in every headline comparing the "maid of honor" and the "maid of honour".


Part III — the compound, from Tudor court to modern wedding. The phrase "maid of honour" is attested in English from the early sixteenth century, exactly when the Tudor court fixed the office — Henry VII's and Henry VIII's household records list maids of honour by name — and the plural "maids of honour" runs throughout Elizabethan and Jacobean court records and drama, where the young women of the royal closet are stock figures of satire and romance alike. The hyphenated spelling maids-of-honour is a modern typographic regularisation of attributive use. The phrase has astonishingly outlived the institution: the last great ceremonial cohort of British maids of honour walked in Queen Elizabeth II's coronation procession of 1953, yet the title now flourishes in a new global institution — the American wedding — where hundreds of thousands of "maids of honour" are named every year. Its third life, as the Yorkshire curd tart, shows how a court phrase can fossilise in popular memory as a recipe legend. The full semantic arc thus runs from feudal service to royal glamour to bridal festivity to pastry folklore — a single compound word carrying six centuries of English social history in its folds.
