- Late 14th to 15th c. – Boundless duration, eternity, continuous existence - Entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman; primarily used in theological and scholastic contexts regarding divine permanence and the immortal soul.
- 16th to 17th c. – Land tenure & feudal entailments - Adopted into English property law to describe perpetual settlements; became notorious as the "dead hand" (*mortmain*) of deceased landowners seeking to bind future generations.
- Late 17th c. – Common Law restriction ("Rule Against Perpetuities") - Formalized into a legal boundary (Lord Nottingham, 1682) prohibiting indefinite dead-hand restraints on the sale or alienation of real estate.
- 18th to 19th c. – Public endowments & perpetual financial annuities - Applied to philanthropic trust funds, British government Consol debt ("perpetual annuities"), and perpetual care agreements for municipal cemetery plots.
- 20th c. to Present – Quantitative corporate finance & colloquial metaphor - Standardized in financial accounting for perpetual cash flow models ($PV = C / r$); colloquially used to express endless, wearying duration ("waiting in perpetuity").
