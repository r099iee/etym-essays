Proto-Indo-European *h₃reǵ- (also cited as *reg- / *rēǵ-) "to make straight, direct, guide, rule" (Pokorny *rēǵ- "to make straight, direct, rule"; the physical sense is of drawing a straight line, setting a course straight ahead) → [Proto-Italic] *regō "to keep straight, guide" → [Latin] regere "to keep straight, to guide, to rule; to direct in a straight line" → [Latin] rēgnum "kingship, kingdom, rule" (from rēx, rēgis "king," the one who draws the straight line) → [Latin] rēgnāre "to be king, to reign, to exercise royal rule" (denominative verb from rēgnum) → [Old French] reignier / regner "to reign" (10th–13th c.) → [Middle English] reignen, regnen "to reign" (13th c., attested c. 1225) → [English] reign (verb) "to hold royal office" and (noun) "a period of rule" → [English] reigning — the present participle used adjectivally: "reigning monarch," "reigning dynasty" (and, by extension, "reigning champion") NOTE ON SIBLINGS: the same PIE root *h₃reǵ- independently produced Sanskrit rā́jan "king" (Hindi राजा rājā), Celtic *rīg- "king" (Gaulish Vercingetorix "great king of warriors"), and Germanic *rehtaz "straight, right, just" (English right, German recht). These are cognates, not ancestors of reigning. The English word rein (a horse's strap) is UNRELATED — it descends from Latin retinēre "to hold back."
