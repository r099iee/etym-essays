INSOLENCE is a noun denoting openly rude, contemptuous, or disrespectful behaviour or speech, especially behaviour that flouts the deference owed to authority, rank, or social hierarchy. It is not mere bad manners: insolence is pointed and assertive—an active, provocative breach of expected submission. The insolent person speaks or acts as if the other party's authority did not matter, often with a tone of sarcasm, sneering, or brazen self-assurance. At its core the word names the offence of presumptuous impudence directed upward, from the socially inferior to the superior, the younger to the elder, the servant to the master, the soldier to the officer, the subject to the king.

In modern everyday use insolence covers cheekiness, backtalk, and insulting arrogance: the student's insolence toward a teacher, the employee's insolence toward a manager, the customer's insolence toward staff. The word sits at the intersection of etiquette and power: it becomes relevant only where a hierarchy is acknowledged, and its charge is that the speaker has overstepped the bounds of their station. Because it presupposes hierarchy, insolence is a favourite word of courts, armies, and institutions, and in literature it shades into hubris—the overbearing pride that invites downfall.

Primary and secondary senses

  • Behavioural / interpersonal sense: rude, disrespectful, or audacious speech or conduct that flouts manners and offends others (a child's insolence, a stranger's insolence).
  • Hierarchical / political sense: open defiance, insubordination, or brazen contempt shown toward a lord, ruler, officer, judge, or elder; historically grounds for punishment as a threat to order.
  • Judicial / legal sense: contempt of court—insolence toward a judge or tribunal that can be sanctioned as a procedural offence.
  • Literary / tragic sense: overbearing pride and arrogance inviting retribution; in classical and Renaissance tragedy insolence is the social face of hubris (Greek hybris), the presumptuous self-elevation that the gods or fate punish.
  • Militaristic sense: insubordination—speaking or behaving with contempt toward superior officers.
  • Modern egalitarian sense: with the flattening of hierarchies, insolence has broadened to describe any sneering disregard for manners or courtesy, even among equals.

Historical semantic bridge

The modern reading of insolence as "contemptuous defiance" grew out of an older Latin meaning, "unaccustomedness, unusualness." In Latin, insolens meant literally "not accustomed" (in- "not" + solens, participle of solere "to be accustomed"), hence "strange, unusual, excessive." Because departing from custom—especially the ancestral customs of the Roman elite, the mos maiorum—was itself a social offence, "unusual behaviour" shifted into "presumptuous, immoderate behaviour," and finally into the familiar sense of arrogant disrespect. The English word arrived via Old French and has kept this aristocratic, moralising tone ever since: in medieval theology insolence was a branch of the deadly sin of Pride, and in the Tudor and Stuart courts it was a political crime of those who forgot their degree.

Register and grammar: insolence is a mass noun (an abstract quality) but is freely countable in the plural as "instances of insolent speech or acts" ("a string of insolences"). It supports a large derivational family—insolent (adjective), insolently (adverb), insolency (rare variant)—and is the preferred term when the writer wishes to stress defiance of authority rather than mere rudeness. In the source context ("with your __________, witch"), the word is hurled as an accusation by a superior against a subordinate who has defied them—exactly the hierarchical sense: the defiance itself, not merely its rudeness, is the crime.
