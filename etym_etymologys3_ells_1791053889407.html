The history of ells is the history of the body as the first ruler. The singular ell belongs to a very old Indo-European family of forearm-words. Its deepest reconstructible ancestor is usually given as PIE *el-, meaning forearm or elbow, which also underlies elbow (el + bow, the bend of the forearm) and cognates such as Latin ulna (elbow, forearm, later a measure), Greek ōlenē (elbow, forearm), and Sanskrit aratni in the wider cubit system. Whether *el- itself continues an extended form *h₁el- with laryngeal is debated among Indo-Europeanists; Beekes and de Vaan treat the Greek and Latin forms cautiously, and Etymonline gives the Germanic branch without committing to a single laryngeal reconstruction. The note of debate is therefore explicit: the forearm sense is secure, the exact laryngeal shape is not.

From that root, Proto-Germanic formed *alinō, feminine, meaning forearm and by extension the length from elbow to fingertip. This is directly continued by Old Norse öln, Old High German elina, German Elle, Dutch el, and Old English eln. In Old English eln already shows the characteristic double life of body-measures:

  • Anatomical sense: the forearm itself, as in compounds and glosses for Latin ulna and cubitus.
  • Metrological sense: a standardised cubit, the length of the forearm used by builders, weavers, and traders.
  • Legal sense in embryo: a unit the king or town could define, inspect, and punish for falsifying.

Phonetically the development is regular. PIE *e remained a in Proto-Germanic before the following vowel shift to *alinō; Old English fronted and raised to eln with syllabic nasal loss; Middle English vocalised the final nasal to elle, spelled elle, elne, eln in manuscripts; early Modern English shortened the vowel and dropped the final schwa to give ell /ɛl/. The plural was originally strong (elna, eln) but was levelled to regular -s after the Norman Conquest, hence modern ells.

Socially, the ell became one of the great cloth measures of medieval Europe. England standardised it at 45 inches (1.143 m) for woollen cloth, Scotland at 37.2 inches (the Scottish ell), Flanders and France at different lengths (the French aune). Statutes from Edward I to Elizabeth I fix the ell, appoint measurers, and fine sellers of short ells. Chaucer, Langland, and town guild records use elle casually, showing it was everyday commercial vocabulary. Shakespeare has Falstaff and cloth-merchants speak in ells and yards; the King James Bible translates Hebrew ammah (cubit) sometimes with cubit rather than ell, but popular sermons used ell to make scripture concrete.

Key attestations and shifts include:

  • Old English laws and glosses (c. 900–1100): eln glosses cubitus; building and boundary charters measure in elna.
  • Middle English commercial revolution (c. 1200–1400): elle becomes primarily a cloth noun; the Ell-wand (measuring rod) appears as a civic emblem.
  • Early modern standardisation (c. 1500–1700): English ell fixed at 45 inches; Scottish ell separately fixed; proverb Give him an inch and he will take an ell spreads across Germanic languages.
  • Metrication and obsolescence (c. 1800–1900): Britain legalises the yard (36 inches) and later the metre; ell survives only in historical novels, museums, and idiom.
  • Modern survival: plural ells occurs almost only in historical description, as in the Polynesian-net passage, in translations of medieval statutes, and in the idiom.

Semantically, ells therefore never meant merely length. It encoded the forearm, the honesty of trade, the power of the state to standardise, and finally nostalgia for a pre-metric world. Its plural form in the source sentence is rhetorical: a thousand ells evokes an immense, communally handled net, about 1,140 metres if English ells, whose handling required a hundred men and thus political organisation. The modern reader perceives it as archaic and literary, a deliberate archaism that signals deep time.
