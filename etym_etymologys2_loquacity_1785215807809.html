Linear derivation chain for LOQUACITY: 1. PIE *tolkw- ("to speak, talk") — reconstructed; the root is weak and disputed because the family is attested almost solely in Italic/Latin (some scholars decline to accept this reconstruction). 2. Latin loquī ("to speak, talk, say") — deponent verb; attested forms loquor, locūtus; from the classical period (2nd c. BCE onward). The primary source of the whole English loqu- family. 3. Latin loquāx, loquācis ("talkative, garrulous, chattering") — derived from loquī + the suffix -āx ("inclined to, given to"); classical usage (e.g., Horace, Ovid). 4. Latin loquācitās, loquācitātis ("talkativeness, garrulity") — abstract noun built on loquāx with the suffix -tās (the ancestor of English -ity); attested in Classical Latin prose. 5. Middle French loquacité ("talkativeness") — learned formation from Latin loquācitātem, made in the medieval/early-modern French learned vocabulary. 6. Early Modern English loquacity ("talkativeness, garrulity") — borrowed c. early 17th century, at the height of Renaissance Latin-English lexical enrichment; the adjective loquacious is attested in English from the 1660s. 7. Modern English LOQUACITY — the established abstract noun, now chiefly literary and formal. Note on debate: the PIE reconstruction *tolkw- rests on slender evidence; because no secure cognates are attested outside Italic, some authorities treat the word as of uncertain or exclusively Latin origin. The English word is a learned borrowing at every stage — there was no continuous vernacular transmission from Latin through French into everyday English speech.
