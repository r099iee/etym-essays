The word arrest embodies a profound historical lineage that traces back through medieval French jurisprudence, Vulgar Latin administrative enforcement, and classical Latin verbal composition to the ancient Proto-Indo-European concept of standing firm.

Indo-European Foundations and Latin Compounding:

  • The ultimate root of arrest is the prolific Proto-Indo-European root *steh₂-, signifying "to stand, be firm, place, or establish." This foundational root gave rise to an extraordinary family of English words including stand, state, statue, station, and constant.
  • In Proto-Italic, this root developed into the Latin primary verb stāre ("to stand"). By combining stāre with the prefix re- ("back, behind"), Latin created restāre, which meant "to remain behind, stay in place, linger, or hold one’s ground."
  • During the Late Latin and Vulgar Latin periods (c. 5th–7th century CE), administrative and colloquial speech combined restāre with the directional preposition ad- ("to, toward"), forming *arrestāre. Crucially, this compounding transformed an intransitive posture ("to stand still") into a causative, transitive physical action: "to cause to stand still, to check the motion of, to detain."

Old French, Anglo-Norman Feudalism, and Legal Codification:

  • Following the collapse of the Western Roman Empire, the verb passed into Gallo-Romance and emerged in Old French by the 12th century as arester (modern French arrêter), bearing the primary meanings of "to bring to a halt, to stop walking, to cease an action," and crucially, "to take into judicial custody."
  • Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, Anglo-Norman French became the exclusive language of the English royal courts, common law, and feudal administration. Royal bailiffs and sheriffs were issued writs to arrester individuals accused of felonies, treason, or breach of the King's peace. The noun arrest entered Anglo-French legal parlance to designate both the physical seizure of the body and the formal judgment or decree of a supreme tribunal (as in the French legal term arrêt).

Middle English Attestation and Literary Diffusion:

  • The term was absorbed into Middle English during the late 14th century as arresten. Geoffrey Chaucer utilized the word in The Canterbury Tales (c. 1387), employing it both in its technical legal sense and in vivid physical descriptions of halting horses, holding back travelers, and arresting movement.
  • During the 16th and 17th centuries, the word underwent significant aesthetic and philosophical broadening in Elizabethan and Jacobean literature. William Shakespeare frequently employed arrest in both literal judicial contexts and rich dramatic metaphors—most famously personifying mortality in Hamlet: "Had I but time—as this fell sergeant, Death, / Is strict in his arrest—O, I could tell you—" (Act V, Scene 2).

Scientific Specialization and Modern Nuance:

  • The Enlightenment and the 19th-century medical revolution formalized arrest as a standard clinical descriptor for the sudden failure of physiological systems. Medical treatises began categorizing cardiac arrest and respiratory arrest, distinguishing these acute functional stoppages from gradual degenerative decline.
  • In modern literature, the word retains an exquisite psychological depth. When C. K. Scott Moncrieff translated Marcel Proust’s Du côté de chez Swann, he selected arrest to describe the mythical, miraculous gesture of lifting one's arm to halt the sun in the heavens (echoing the biblical narrative of Joshua at Gibeon), perfectly capturing the word’s capacity to convey an absolute, suspended stillness across both cosmic and psychological landscapes.
