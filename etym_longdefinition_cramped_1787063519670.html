CRAMPED is the past participle of the verb cramp, and as an adjective it describes the state of being painfully restricted in space, movement, or room — or, of a limb or muscle, contracted, stiff, and aching. In the Tolkien passage above, the drenched dwarves are described as "soaked and bruised and cramped": after their ordeal in the barrels on the river, their limbs are stiff and painfully contracted, so that they cannot move freely or even fully realise that they have been rescued. This bodily sense — muscles tight and immobile from cold, wet, and prolonged confinement — is one of the word’s primary physical meanings.

  • Physical sense 1 (space): of a room, dwelling, vehicle, or area, so small or so crowded that there is insufficient room for comfort or free movement; e.g. "a cramped little kitchen", "cramped conditions on the boat".
  • Physical sense 2 (body): of a person, limb, or muscle, affected by cramp — involuntarily contracted, stiffened, aching; or of a person confined so tightly that movement is impossible; e.g. "cramped legs", "I was cramped in the back seat for hours".
  • Physical sense 3 (writing): of handwriting or lettering, so small and crowded as to be difficult to read; the letters are pressed together in a tight, uncomfortable way; e.g. "a cramped, spidery hand".
  • Figurative sense (freedom): of people, lives, or minds, restricted or held back — stunted, confined by rules, poverty, or circumstances; a "cramped outlook" is one narrowed and limited; the sense carries the idea of being boxed in.
  • Figurative sense (progress): of activity or development, impeded or restrained; "cramped quarters" for growth; the idiom "to cramp one’s style", meaning to prevent someone from acting freely or at their best, is the living survivor of this sense.
  • Technical sense (machinery and building): in mechanics and construction, "cramped" can describe something held rigidly by a cramp or clamp (an iron bracket), i.e. bound fast and immovable.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. All the senses radiate from the noun cramp, which English took from Old French crampe in the 14th century. The French word meant both a painful muscular contraction and a bent iron hook or clamp — and both meanings descend from a Germanic root meaning "bent, crooked, pressed together" (compare German Krampf, "cramp, spasm"; Krampe, "staple, clamp"; Dutch kram). The body sense and the space sense are one image: a cramp bends, tightens, and locks; a cramped room presses and confines the body in exactly the same way. The figurative sense of restricted freedom is a still later extension of the same idea of being squeezed and held in place.

Register and nuances. Cramped describes the uncomfortable result of insufficient space: it is stronger than confined (which is neutral) and more specific than small (which may be cosy). It carries a persistent note of discomfort and constraint. In the bodily sense it overlaps with stiff, cramped being more active and painful. The idiom "to cramp one’s style" (attested from the early 20th century) shows how the space metaphor was applied to behaviour: to be cramped in one’s style is to be prevented from acting naturally and freely. In literature of adventure and ordeal — like The Hobbit — the bodily sense is used for physical helplessness: the dwarves are so cramped that they cannot even feel relief properly.

  • Synonyms in the space sense: confined, constricted, restricted, crowded, tight, narrow, congested, squeezed, poky.
  • Synonyms in the bodily sense: stiff, contracted, knotted, rigid, aching.
  • Synonyms in the figurative sense: stunted, limited, straitened, hamstrung, inhibited, hindered.
  • Antonyms: spacious, roomy, airy, generous, free, unconfined, relaxed, commodious.

Word-family relations. Cramp (noun and verb), cramped (adjective), cramping (participle), cramp as the name of the bent iron tool or clamp, the derived crampon (a climbing spike, via French crampon, "grappling-iron"), and the verb cram, "to stuff tightly" (from Old English crammian), which belongs to the same broad Germanic family of pressing and squeezing. A spelling trap: "cramped" has only one m.
