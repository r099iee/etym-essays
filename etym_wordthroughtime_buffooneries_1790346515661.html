- 16th–17th c. - Borrowed from Italian commedia dell'arte and French theatrical traditions to describe the physical slapstick, puffed-cheek routines, and clowning of stage jesters.
- 18th c. - Shifted into an aesthetic and moral pejorative among Enlightenment writers, strictly contrasted with refined wit, elegant irony, and classical decorum.
- 19th c. - Extensively applied in cultural and political critique (notably by Ruskin and Carlyle) to denounce journalistic mockery, sensationalist press reviews, and the degradation of public taste.
- 20th–21st c. - Preserved in intellectual and literary discourse to condemn absurd, irresponsible, or undignified spectacles that degrade serious institutions or discourse.
