- 17th–18th c. "culinary and broth foundation" - The noun *soup* naturalizes into English from French *soupe*; early adjectival uses of *soupy* emerge sporadically for broths and muddy farmland.
- 19th c. "Victorian weather and physical sludges" - The adjective *soupy* and derived noun *soupiness* gain widespread currency in Britain to describe thawing snow, muddy macadam roads, and especially the notorious London 'pea-souper' coal fogs.
- Early 20th c. "comedic romantic mush" - British humorists (led by P. G. Wodehouse) immortalize *soupiness* as the comical antithesis of youthful bachelor poise, branding all gushing romantic poetry and dewy-eyed sentiment with culinary mockery.
- Mid-20th c. "critical and acoustic adoption" - Music critics, audio engineers, and film reviewers adopt *soupiness* as technical jargon for muddy frequency overlap in sound mixes, over-orchestrated string arrangements, and blurry cinematography.
- Late 20th c.–Present "bipartite survival" - Flourishes today as both an informal technical term for acoustic/visual murk and a sparkling literary critique of saccharine melodrama.
