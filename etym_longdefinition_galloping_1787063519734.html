Galloping is the present participle and participial adjective of gallop. In its literal sense it describes a horse — or a rider, or by extension any four-legged animal — moving at the gallop, the fastest natural gait, in which the horse advances in a four-beat bounding rhythm with a moment when all four feet leave the ground. As an adjective it describes the gait itself (“a galloping horse”), the speed (“at a galloping pace”), or, figuratively, anything that advances, spreads, or changes so rapidly that it is hard to control.

In the source context — “like the noise of a large pot __________ on the fire” — the word is applied playfully to a boiling pot: its lid rattles and its contents surge so eagerly that the pot seems to be running, bounding, galloping across the flames. The comparison transfers the horse's urgent, thundering, hard-to-restrain motion to a kitchen pot, and the participle does the work of suggesting speed, noise, and barely contained energy all at once.

Primary senses

  • Of a horse or rider — moving at full gallop: “a galloping stallion”, “galloping across the plain”. The fastest gait, four-beat, with a phase of suspension.
  • Of hooves or sound — thundering: “the galloping sound of hooves”, a “galloping rhythm” in music (a rapid, repetitive, leaping beat).
  • Of speed — rapid, rushing: “at a galloping rate”, “galloping progress”. Anything that moves with the urgency of a horse at full stretch.

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Medicine — galloping consumption: from the 19th century, “galloping consumption” was the vivid name for tuberculosis that advanced with terrible speed, wasting the patient within weeks. The phrase fixed “galloping” as the adjective for any fast, unstoppable, destructive progress.
  • Economics — galloping inflation: from the 20th century, “galloping inflation” describes prices rising at an accelerating, runaway rate; the image is of a horse out of control.
  • Of disease generally: “a galloping infection”, “galloping decay” — rapid and hard to arrest.
  • Of trends, technology, and events: “galloping technology”, “galloping change”, “galloping rumours” — development too fast to absorb or control.
  • Military and equestrian vocabulary: galloping is the standard descriptor of cavalry movement; “galloping artillery” was horse-drawn artillery, and the word still colours ceremonial and sporting use (racehorses galloping to the finish).
  • Of a person's heart: “her heart was galloping” — beating fast and out of rhythm, like a runaway horse; a close cousin of throbbing and pounding.

Semantic boundaries and register

Galloping is distinguished from the other gaits by speed and lack of restraint. A trot is a brisk two-beat gait, a canter an easy three-beat lope, a gallop the flat-out four-beat run with a suspension phase. Figuratively, galloping therefore implies not merely quickness but acceleration, urgency, and near-loss of control — the horse has been given its head. It is used of dramatic, unstoppable processes (disease, inflation, rumour, technological change), and it keeps, even in metaphor, the thunder and dust of the original image.

Etymology in brief

Gallop enters English in the 15th century from Old French galoper, generally derived from the Old Frankish (Germanic) compound *wala hlaupan, literally “to run well”. The participial galloping is attested from the early 15th century. The PIE-level ancestry is debated; see the etymology fields for the chain and the full essay.
