- Enclosure — Old English hemman 'to enclose, check, restrain': hemming cattle into a fold, hemming a fugitive; the participle 'hemmed in/about' is a physical picture of a ring drawn tight.
- Garment border — the same verb edges cloth: 'to hem' a garment (sewing a border that encloses the raw edge); the noun 'hem', the border itself, is solid by the 14th century; the two senses are felt as one.
- Metaphor thickens — in 16th–17th-century prose and verse things are 'hem'd with woods', 'hem'd about with waves'; by the 18th century 'hemmed in' is a standard metaphor for restraint by poverty, rules or circumstance.
- Specialisation — the 19th century industrialises the sewing sense (hemming machines, hemming stitches) while the spatial sense retreats into the fixed phrase; everyday English confines the verb mainly to cloth and to 'hemmed in'.
- Today — 'hemmed in' is overwhelmingly figurative (hemmed in by debt, by mountains, by goblins and wolves), the sewing sense is universal in clothing, and 'hem and haw' stands apart as an unrelated imitative expression.
