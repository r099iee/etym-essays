The verb exemplifies encapsulates a fascinating linguistic evolution that spans over two thousand years, branching from an archaic Proto-Indo-European verbal root meaning "to take," developing through Roman retail and rhetorical practice, hardening into a rigid medieval scribal formula, expanding into humanist moral philosophy, and culminating in modern semiotic aesthetics.

1. Indo-European and Proto-Italic roots: the mechanics of taking. The evolutionary origin of the word begins with the Proto-Indo-European verbal root *h₁em- (or *em-), which signified "to take, obtain, gain, or distribute." This versatile root produced widespread descendants across the Indo-European family, including Old Church Slavonic jęti ("to take"), Russian vzyat', Lithuanian imti ("to take"), and Old Irish em-. In Proto-Italic, the root yielded the primary verb *em-ō ("I take").

In the archaic Latin of the Roman Republic, this verb appeared as emere. Interestingly, while its primitive meaning was "to take" (a meaning retained in compound verbs like sūmere < *sub-emere, "to take from below/assume," and redimere, "to buy back/redeem"), the everyday marketplace of Rome shifted the primary meaning of emere to "to buy" or "purchase"—that is, "to take possession of goods in exchange for money."

2. Classical Latin: the birth of *eximere* and *exemplum*. When the prepositional prefix ex- ("out of, forth from", tracing to PIE *h₁eǵʰs) was attached to emere, it formed the compound verb eximere, which meant "to take out, extract, remove, set aside, or except." In Roman agriculture, mining, and trade, when a merchant or inspector surveyed a large batch of grain, amphorae of wine, or a herd of cattle, they would physically "take out" one unit from the mass to test its quality and demonstrate the character of the whole lot.

From the past participle stem of eximere (exemptus), Latin derived the neuter instrumental noun exemplum. Etymologically, an exemplum was literally "that which is taken out from a group as a representative sample." In classical Roman literature and rhetoric, exemplum rapidly expanded beyond material samples into intellectual, civic, and moral realms:

  • In Roman jurisprudence: exemplum signified a legal precedent, a court transcript, or a model form of contract established by praetorian edicts.
  • In Roman rhetoric and historiography: Popularized by Cicero, Livy, and Seneca, the exemplum became the premier rhetorical tool of Roman pedagogy. Orators illustrated moral arguments by citing historical exempla—short, dramatic biographical anecdotes detailing the bravery of Horatius Cocles, the filial devotion of Cloelia, or the stern justice of Brutus. In the 1st century CE, Valerius Maximus compiled his famous nine-volume anthology Factorum ac dictorum memorabilium libri IX ("Memorable Deeds and Sayings"), organized systematically as a treasury of moral exempla for statesmen and students.

3. Medieval Latin chancellery practice: the birth of *exemplificare*. During the 12th and 13th centuries, as feudal monarchies, ecclesiastical courts, and monastic chanceries developed complex bureaucratic administrations, scribes faced an urgent practical problem: how to produce authentic duplicates of royal charters, papal bulls, and land grants that would carry identical legal authority in distant courts without risking the loss of the fragile original parchment.

To describe this statutory process, medieval jurists coined the compound verb exemplificāre, uniting exemplum with the causative verbal combining suffix -ficāre (derived from facere, "to make, do", from PIE *dʰeh₁-, "to put, place, make"). Literally meaning "to make an exemplum," exemplificāre meant to transcribe an original charter word-for-word, compare it in open court with the original, and affix the Great Seal of the king, bishop, or chancellor. The resulting certified document was known in Latin as an exemplificātiō.

4. Anglo-Norman transmission and Middle English expansion. Following the Norman Conquest of 1066, the term entered Anglo-Norman French as exemplifier and was adopted into English legal and literary writing in the late 14th century (c. 1380–1400) as exemplifien. In the English Courts of Chancery and Common Pleas, an "exemplification under seal" was an unassailable legal proof. Concurrently, literary authors such as John Gower and Geoffrey Chaucer began using the verb in moral and didactic narratives to mean "to demonstrate by an illustrative parable or historical instance."

5. Renaissance humanism to modern analytical philosophy. During the 16th and 17th centuries, English Renaissance authors, including William Shakespeare and Francis Bacon, secularized and widened the verb. To "exemplify" no longer required a formal legal seal or a clerical sermon; a monarch's noble conduct could exemplify majestic governance, or a general's valor could exemplify military honor. By the 19th century, the verb was a cornerstone of scientific, aesthetic, and sociological prose.

In the 20th century, the term achieved renewed theoretical prominence through analytic aesthetics. In 1968, philosopher Nelson Goodman established in Languages of Art that exemplification is a fundamental mode of non-verbal symbolization: an object or artwork exemplifies a feature when it both instantiates and refers to that feature. Today, the third-person form exemplifies stands at the intersection of ethics, aesthetics, and linguistics, identifying individuals, actions, and artifacts that serve as luminous benchmarks of human excellence.
