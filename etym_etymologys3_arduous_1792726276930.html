1. Indo-European Etymological Bedrock of Elevation. The adjective arduous descends from the Proto-Indo-European verbal and adjectival root *h₃erdʰ- (traditionally reconstructed in earlier scholarship as *eredh- or *er-), signifying "to grow upright, rise upward, be tall or high." This root is celebrated among historical linguists for producing terms that connect vertical spatial elevation with rectitude, nobility, and imposing physical grandeur across diverse Indo-European branches:

  • In Celtic, the unextended root produced Gaulish arduo- ("high"), which named the rugged, heavily wooded mountainous plateau known to the Romans as Arduenna Silva (the modern Ardennes Forest in Belgium and France, which also inspired the literary Forest of Arden). In Goidelic, it produced Old Irish ard ("high, elevated, lofty, noble").
  • In Hellenic, with regular phonetic developments, it yielded Ancient Greek orthós (ὀρθός, "straight, upright, standing erect, correct", from which English derives orthodoxy, orthogonal, and orthography).
  • In Indo-Iranian, it produced Sanskrit ūrdhvá- ("erect, rising upright, elevated, high").
  • In Italic, the root developed into Proto-Italic *ardwo-, which yielded Classical Latin arduus.

2. Classical Latin: The Steepness of Mountains and the Heights of Virtue. In Classical Latin, arduus was primarily a topographical and architectural term. It described sheer rock cliffs, towering mountain peaks, precipitous ravines, and fortified battlements:

"Ardua montium" — The steep heights of the mountains.
"Arduus ad solem" — Towering upward toward the sun (Virgil, Aeneid II.475).
Because ascending a steep vertical incline requires intense muscular effort, breathlessness, and sustained physical struggle compared to horizontal walking, Roman authors seamlessly mapped the physical steepness of arduus onto moral and intellectual realms. In the Latin philosophical tradition (Cicero, Seneca, Livy, Horace), tasks that required exceptional fortitude, virtue, or civic courage were routinely described as ardua. Horace famously encapsulated the stoic endurance of life's hardships in his Odes (II.3): "Aequam memento rebus in arduis servare mentem" ("Remember to maintain a calm and balanced mind in arduous circumstances"). Thus, in Roman culture, arduus became the ultimate descriptor for the steep, narrow path of virtue (ad astra per aspera).

3. Renaissance Humanism and Early Modern English Borrowing. Unlike many words of Romance origin that entered English via Norman French following 1066, arduous did not pass through Old French. Instead, it was borrowed directly from Classical Latin during the high Renaissance of the 16th century (attested in the 1530s–1570s). Humanist scholars and translators, seeking to elevate the expressive capacity of the English vernacular to match the rhetorical grandeur of Cicero and Virgil, adopted Latin arduus and anglicized it with the productive adjectival suffix -ous (derived from Latin -ōsus, denoting fullness or abundance). Initially, English writers preserved the dual Latin usage, employing arduous both for literal precipitous terrain (steep hills and cliffs) and for challenging intellectual or political enterprises.

4. Miltonic Epic and the Grand Style. In the 17th century, John Milton cemented arduous as an indispensable instrument of the English grand style. In Paradise Lost (1667), Milton deployed the word to depict both the monumental physical struggle of the fallen angels traversing cosmic chaos and the daunting spiritual ascent toward righteousness:

"The arduous quest..." (Book II)
"Through the wild Abyss... With arduous toil..."
Milton's use of arduous established a profound literary convention: the word was indelibly associated with epic undertakings, cosmic striving, and the tragic or glorious expenditure of heroic will against overwhelming resistance.

5. Wordsworthian Introspection and the Modern Vocation. In Book I of William Wordsworth's The Prelude (1805/1850), the word arduous is placed at a critical autobiographical crux:

"When, as becomes a man who would prepare
For such an arduous work, I through myself
Make rigorous inquisition, the report
Is often cheering..."
Wordsworth applies arduous not to an external mountain expedition, nor to a classical military campaign, but to the composition of a monumental philosophical poem exploring the human mind. The "arduous work" is the poet's life work—a massive inward voyage of memory, aesthetic creation, and spiritual self-discovery. The word carries all the accumulated weight of its Latin heritage: the steepness of the climb, the necessity of rigorous stamina, and the high summit of aesthetic achievement. In the 20th and 21st centuries, while retaining this romantic artistic gravitas, arduous has become a premier standard term in journalism, diplomacy, and science, describing everything from grueling polar expeditions to arduous peace negotiations, forever testifying to humanity's determination to climb steep summits.
