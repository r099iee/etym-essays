- Late 16th to Early 17th Century - Enters English directly from Latin *inveterātus*; initially utilized in Galenic medical treatises to describe chronic, deep-seated physical diseases ("inveterate ulcers") and in Protestant theological tracts describing deeply rooted original sin.
- 17th to 18th Century - Expands into political, legal, and moral philosophy; applied by John Locke, Edmund Burke, and Jonathan Swift to describe lifelong prejudices, entrenched political factions, and bitter sectarian hostilities ("inveterate hatred").
- 19th Century - Flourishes in Victorian literature and essayistic prose (William Hazlitt, Charles Dickens, George Eliot); used to characterize deeply ingrained psychological eccentricities, incurable bachelors, inveterate gamblers, and hardened social habits.
- 20th Century - Enters literary criticism, intellectual history, and academic scholarship; employed by critics like Vladimir Nabokov to describe cemented canonical rankings, fixed literary pairings, and reflexive critical dogmas.
- 21st Century - Retains elevated intellectual status in geopolitical analysis (describing intractable sectarian conflicts), sociopolitical critique (entrenched systemic biases), and literary prose.
