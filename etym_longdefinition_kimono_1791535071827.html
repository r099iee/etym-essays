kimono (noun)

1. Primary Cultural Sense: Traditional Japanese National Costume
The traditional national garment of Japan, consisting of a full-length, straight-seamed, T-shaped wrapped robe with wide, rectangular sleeves and a continuous flat collar. Cut from a single standardized bolt of woven fabric (a tanmono, roughly 38 cm wide and 12.5 meters long) into straight geometric rectangles without darts or curved tailoring, the garment wraps around the body from left over right (the reverse, right over left, being reserved strictly for dressing corpses at funerals) and is secured snugly at the waist by a wide, elaborately tied sash known as an obi. Worn by people of all ages and genders, the formal silk kimono is characterized by intricate textile artistry—including yūzen paste-resist dyeing, shibori tie-dyeing, gold leaf application, and hand embroidery—with distinct patterns, sleeve lengths, and silhouettes signaling the wearer's age, marital status, season, and the formality of the occasion (ranging from the unmarried woman's swinging-sleeved furisode to the married woman's understated tomesode and formal black kurotomesode).

2. Polysemy, Western Fashion Senses, and Domain Usages

  • Western Loungewear and Dressing Gown: A loose, fluid, lightweight robe, dressing gown, or housecoat modeled after the Japanese silhouette, typically made from printed silk, satin, viscose, or lightweight cotton. Worn casually inside the home over sleepwear or poolside as an elegant cover-up, it typically features a simple self-tie fabric belt rather than a structured stiff obi.
  • Sleeve Pattern and Tailoring Term ('Kimono Sleeve'): In Western garment construction, pattern-making, and haute couture, a kimono sleeve is a specific type of sleeve cut in one continuous, seamless piece with the bodice (front and back torso panels), eliminating the traditional curved armhole seam (armscye) and shoulder seam. This creates a soft, flowing, unconstructed drape under the arm that slopes gently from the neckline, offering supreme comfort and dramatic visual fluidity.
  • Contemporary Outerwear and Streetwear Cardigan: In modern global fashion, a casual, open-front, hip- or knee-length light jacket, cardigan, or duster coat inspired by the upper half of the kimono or the Japanese haori. Often featuring floral, botanical, or bohemian prints, it is worn open and unfastened over jeans, t-shirts, or summer dresses as a breezy outer layer.
  • Martial Arts Uniform (Colloquial Usage): In European languages (such as French, Spanish, Russian, and Italian) and colloquially in English, the white heavy-cotton uniform worn in martial arts like judo, karate, and Brazilian jiu-jitsu is frequently referred to as a kimono, although the precise Japanese term for the training suit is keikogi or dōgi.
  • Corporate and Financial Slang ('Open the Kimono'): An American corporate, legal, and investment-banking idiom originating in the 1980s meaning to disclose fully one's proprietary internal books, secret technology, strategic plans, or intellectual property to an outside partner or prospective acquirer during thorough due diligence. Although widespread in late-twentieth-century Wall Street and Silicon Valley parlance, the phrase is increasingly discouraged in modern corporate governance guidelines due to its exoticizing and gendered undertones.

3. Semantic Bridges and Evolutionary Logic
The semantic evolution of kimono is an extraordinary study in cultural specialization and global transmission. Originally in medieval Japan, the word kimono was not a specialized cultural label, but the everyday native compound for clothing in general—literally meaning 'thing to wear' (kiru 'to wear' + mono 'thing'). When everyday Japanese clothing consolidated during the Edo period around the specific straight-seamed robe known as the kosode ('small sleeves'), people simply referred to it as their everyday kimono. Following the Meiji Restoration of 1868, as Western-style tailored trousers and suits (yōfuku, 'Western clothes') flooded Japan, the term kimono was retained as a retronym (alongside wafuku, 'Japanese clothes') to differentiate native dress from Western apparel. Simultaneously, Western visitors and collectors during the late nineteenth-century Japonisme craze were entranced by these exquisite robes, borrowing the word kimono into English and French to denote the specific national dress of Japan. Western fashion designers then abstracted its straight lines and sleeve cut into modern loungewear and pattern-making, cementing its double identity as both sacred national art and universal wardrobe archetype.
