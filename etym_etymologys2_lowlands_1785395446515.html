Compound root word: LOWLANDS = LOW + LAND. The two elements have independent histories, and each is given its own linear chain; the compound itself is an English formation. Chains run for each root in isolation, followed by the compound result. CHAIN 1 — LOW (from Proto-Indo-European *legʰ- "to lie down"): Proto-Indo-European *legʰ- "to lie (down), lie flat" (reconstructed base; ~4500–3000 BCE; the root of English lie, lay, lair, and of the Greek lékhos and Latin lectus "bed") → Proto-Germanic *lēgaz "lying flat, low" (reconstructed adjective, o-grade of *legʰ-; ~1st millennium BCE) → Old Norse lágr "low, short, humble" (attested in the Viking-Age Scandinavian records; the same adjective survives in Danish lav and Swedish låg "low") → Middle English lowe "low" (borrowed in the 12th century from Old Norse; Old English had no native adjective of the root, which is why the word is a Scandinavian loan in English) → Modern English low "low" (with Dutch laag "low" as the Continental West Germanic sister) → low, used as the first element of the compound lowland. CHAIN 2 — LAND (from Proto-Indo-European *lendʰ- "open land, heath, unenclosed ground"): Proto-Indo-European *lendʰ- "open land, heath, unfenced ground" (reconstructed base; ~4500–3000 BCE; the reconstruction is itself debated, and the OED notes that Germanic *landą has no certainly attested cognates outside Germanic; proposed Celtic and Balto-Slavic equations — Old Irish lann "land, enclosure," Polish ląd "dry land," Czech lada "fallow land" — are plausible but not universally accepted) → Proto-Germanic *landą "land, ground, country, territory" (reconstructed noun; ~1st millennium BCE; remarkably stable in sense across all branches) → Old English land "land, ground, country, realm" (native from the earliest records; the same word as in England, the land of the Angles) → Middle English land "land, country" (12th–15th c.) → Modern English land "land, country" (with Germanic sisters Old Norse land, Gothic land, Old High German lant, Dutch land, German Land) → land, used as the second element of the compound lowland. COMPOUND RESULT: low + land → lowland (noun and adjective) "region of low, level country" (formed in English, 17th–18th c., chiefly Scots, where the Lowlands named central and southern Scotland against the Highlands) → Modern English LOWLANDS (the regular plural of the noun, and the proper-name plural for the Scottish Lowlands). Note on debate: the deeper origins of both elements are genuinely nuanced. For low, the Proto-Germanic *lēgaz is the standard reconstruction from *legʰ-, but the adjective's life in English depends on the Old Norse loan — English had no native cognate adjective, so the word enters Middle English from Scandinavian, not from any Old English form. For land, the PIE base *lendʰ- is reconstructed with uncertainty: some scholars derive the Germanic *landą from the same root as the Celtic and Balto-Slavic land-words, but the OED cautions that no certain Indo-European cognates are established, and the root may be as old as the earliest Germanic. The compound itself is not borrowed: lowland is an English formation, coined in the early modern period and taken up most firmly in Scotland.
