The historical evolution of the word intonations represents a profound semantic journey from ancient cosmic storm imagery to Christian liturgical ceremony, classical music theory, and ultimately to the nuanced psychology of human interpersonal speech.

1. Deep Indo-European & Classical Roots

At its deepest etymological bedrock, the root of intonation descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)ten-, an onomatopoeic verbal root meaning "to thunder, groan, roar, or resound deeply." This ancient root generated a vast family of words across the Indo-European daughter languages associated with reverberating noise and celestial shock:

  • In Proto-Germanic, it yielded *þunraz (thunder), which gave Old English þunor (modern thunder), as well as the Old Norse deity name Þórr (Thor), and modern English astonish and stun (literally "to be struck by thunder").
  • In Sanskrit, it produced stanati ("it thunders, roars").
  • In Ancient Greek, it yielded stenein ("to groan, lament loudly").
  • In Proto-Italic, the initial s-mobile was lost or shifted, yielding the verb *tonāje-, which entered Classical Latin as tonare ("to thunder, roar, resound").

From Latin tonare, Classical Latin formed the noun tonus (itself borrowed from Greek tonos, literally "a stretching, tension, cord, musical pitch," from PIE *ten-, "to stretch"). In the post-classical era, the prefix in- (meaning "into, upon, or in accordance with") was compounded with tonare to create the Late Latin verb intonare. Originally, intonare meant "to thunder forth, resound loudly, or recite with thunderous resonance." However, under the growing influence of church music, it underwent a crucial semantic shift to mean "to sing according to a specific musical tone or pitch."

2. The Medieval Ecclesiastical Incipit

During the development of Gregorian chant in the Medieval Catholic Church (c. 9th to 13th centuries), the Latin noun intonatio was coined as a precise technical term in sacred psalmody. In monophonic liturgical singing, the intonatio was the initial phrase, melodic formula, or opening motive (incipit) sung by a solo priest, cantor, or choirmaster:

  • Its liturgical purpose was to establish the fundamental reciting pitch (tenor), the key, and the specific ecclesiastical mode (such as Dorian, Phrygian, or Mixolydian) for the choir before the entire monastic congregation joined the chant.
  • Without a clear, authoritative intonatio, a choir of dozens of singers risked drifting in pitch or singing out of harmonic unison.
  • Thus, in medieval Latinity, intonatio was not yet casual speech modulation; it was a sacred, strictly governed acoustic tuning act.

3. Renaissance Choral Practice & French Transmission

As polyphony flourished throughout the Renaissance and early modern Europe, intonatio passed into Middle French as intonation (recorded in the late 14th and 15th centuries) and Italian as intonazione. In this period, its meaning broadened significantly within music theory:

  • It came to denote not merely the opening chant formula, but the ongoing accuracy, correctness, and purity of musical pitch produced by a singer's voice or an instrumentalist's strings and wind pipes.
  • Musicians began speaking of "good intonation" (pure, in-tune frequency adherence) versus "false or defective intonation" (singing sharp or flat).
  • The term entered the English language in the early 17th century (circa 1610–1620) as a direct scholarly and musical loanword from French and Late Latin, retaining this precise musical-acoustic definition.

4. Rhetorical Transposition to Spoken Speech

During the Enlightenment and the 18th century, the rise of elocutionary manuals and rhetorical science prompted British and European linguists to apply the analytical tools of musical notation to natural human speech. Theorists such as John Walker (author of The Melody of Speaking Delineated, 1787) and Joshua Steele (Prosodia Rationalis, 1779) recognized that spoken language possesses an inherent, continuous musical contour:

  • The term intonation was formally borrowed from musicology into linguistics to designate the shifting pitch melodies, rises, falls, and sustained registers of the spoken human voice.
  • Writers and philosophers observed that whereas the lexical words convey propositional content, the accompanying intonations convey the speaker's emotional attitude, communicative intent, interrogation, doubt, sarcasm, or social authority.

5. Aristocratic Sociolinguistics in 19th-Century Literature

In 19th-century European literature—most brilliantly exemplified by Leo Tolstoy in War and Peace and Anna Karenina, as well as Jane Austen and Henry James—the plural form intonations became an indispensable tool for psychological and social characterization:

  • In courtly, aristocratic societies (such as the Francophone Russian aristocracy of St. Petersburg in 1805), social power was rarely exercised through overt vulgar shouting. Instead, authority, condescension, breeding, and courtly pedigree were signaled through slow, measured, carefully modulated intonations.
  • Tolstoy specifically highlights how Prince Vasily Kuragin speaks with "those slow, patronising intonations peculiar to a man of importance who has grown old in court society," demonstrating how pitch contour serves as a subtle weapon of diplomatic supremacy and aristocratic aloofness.

6. Modern Phonetics & Cognitive Pragmatics

In 20th and 21st-century phonology, intonation is classified as a foundational pillar of prosody alongside stress and rhythm. Modern acoustic phonetics uses pitch trackers to map fundamental frequency (F0) curves, identifying universal and language-specific intonational contours:

  • Terminal Falling Intonation: Signals completion, assertion, factual certainty, and authoritative finality in declarative sentences.
  • Rising Intonation: Signals yes/no questions, conversational incompletion, uncertainty, or deference to the listener.
  • Fall-Rise Contours: Convey nuanced pragmatic subtexts such as hesitation, polite contradiction, reservation, or biting irony.
  • Sociolinguistic Shifts: Contemporary linguists study phenomena like High Rising Terminal (upspeak) and vocal fry, analyzing how generational cohorts reshape intonational melodies to express empathy, collaboration, or in-group solidarity.
