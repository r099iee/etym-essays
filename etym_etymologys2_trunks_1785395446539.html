TRUNKS descends from Latin truncus, of uncertain deeper pedigree. No secure Proto-Indo-European etymon is established: the Latin adjective truncus "maimed, cut short, lopped" probably comes from a root meaning "to cut, lop, strike", but the dictionaries (including de Vaan) mark the pedigree as uncertain. Stage 1 — Latin truncus (adjective, attested from the early Latin period) "maimed, cut short, lopped"; (noun) "the lopped tree-stem, a stump; a trunk", the tree-trunk conceived as that which has been cut off. Deeper origin: de Vaan marks the PIE pedigree as unclear; Greek attempts (e.g. trýchō "to wear out") are speculative, and the OED keeps the origin open. Stage 2 — Latin truncāre "to lop, cut short, mutilate": source of English truncate, truncated (via Latin truncātus). Stage 3 — Old French tronc (12th century) "tree-trunk; torso; alms-chest": the Romance continuation of Latin truncus, which also gave Italian tronco, Spanish tronco, and Portuguese tronco "trunk, stem, log". Stage 4 — Middle English trunke (14th century) "tree-stem, body, box": borrowed from Old French tronc into English. Stage 5 — English trunk (15th–20th centuries): tree-trunk (primary sense); human torso (15th century); large storage box or travelling chest (16th century, from the hollowed tree-trunk); elephant's proboscis (17th century); main body of a pillar or carriage (17th century); trunk road, trunk line, trunk call (19th century); trunk-hose and, by 20th-century extension, swimming trunks. Stage 6 — English trunks (plural — the exact form of this card): both many tree-stems and the plural-only garment. DEBATED AND NUANCED POINTS: The Latin origin is secure, but the deeper pedigree is not — de Vaan marks the etymology of truncus as uncertain, and no Proto-Indo-European root can be confidently reconstructed. Related forms — Old French tronçon "a piece cut off" (source of English truncheon), and trench via Old French trenchier "to cut" — belong to the same family of "cutting", but the exact shape of the pre-Latin root is unknown. No Sanskrit or Germanic cognate of truncus is attested; the tree honestly stops at Latin.
