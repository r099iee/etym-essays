- c.400 BCE–1500s: medical definition — aphorismos means a pithy definition that marks off a disease or treatment; Hippocrates Aphorisms are clinical rules; perception is technical, authoritative, physicianly.
- 1528–1700s: general moral maxim — English borrows aphorism for any concise principle, especially in medicine, law, and divinity; Bacon and Harvey write aphoristically; perception broadens from clinic to study, still serious and learned.
- 1700s–1800s: salon wit and moralist art — La Rochefoucauld, Chamfort, Lichtenberg, Blake make the aphorism a vehicle of worldly irony and Enlightenment critique; perception becomes elegant, skeptical, Parisian and Weimar pewter.
- 1880s–1950s: philosophical fragment — Nietzsche, Kafka, Wittgenstein use numbered aphorisms to shatter systems; perception is avant-garde, prophetic, anti-academic; the fragment itself is philosophy.
- Contemporary: quotable content — from desk calendars to social feeds, aphorism means any shareable one-liner; perception splits between admiration for craft and suspicion of TED-talk banality; the form thrives because brevity suits the screen.
