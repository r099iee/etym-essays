- 15th Century (Late Middle English / Anglo-Norman Legal Register) — Borrowed into English law courts from Anglo-Norman French acrue (past participle of accroistre, 'to increase'), strictly denoting feudal rights, landed reversions, or property accessions falling to an heir through natural succession or legal attachment.
- 16th–17th Century (Early Modern Commercial & Financial Era) — Extended from landed property to commercial finance and usury law, specifically designating the continuous, temporal accumulation of monetary interest, dividends, and yields upon principal capital.
- 18th–19th Century (Enlightenment Moral & Political Philosophy) — Broadened beyond monetary yield into moral, spiritual, and philosophical discourse; abstract virtues, societal benefits, cultural reputations, and spiritual blessings were described as 'accruing' to individuals as the natural fruit of righteous conduct.
- 20th Century (Accounting & Corporate Formalization) — Formally codified into the international language of GAAP and corporate law through 'accrual accounting,' designating revenues earned and expenses incurred regardless of when cash is exchanged, alongside continued literary usage for cumulative benefits.
- 21st Century (Contemporary Era) — Widely utilized across finance, legal proceedings (statutes of limitations), workplace benefits (accruing paid time off), gamified loyalty programs (accruing points/miles), and religious literature (spiritual merit accruing from devotion).
