adage is a short, Latinate noun for a piece of inherited wisdom, and almost all of its interest lies in the drift from respect to contempt. It entered English in the fourteenth century as a neutral word for a proverb or a maxim — a saying handed on because it seemed to contain something durable. Through the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries it could be applied to a sentence of Scripture held to be beyond dispute, or to the motto or legend on a coat of arms, and the word still carried genuine authority. During the seventeenth century, in an age of classical education and sharpening irony, the sense inverted: an adage became a saying that was trite, stale and worn out — something a learned man repeated without having examined whether it was true. That dismissive sense is now the word’s default, and adage is usually a slightly superior word for platitude. In the quoted line of dialogue it is used in exactly this way, of a proverb invoked with a shrug: the poor cat in the proverb is a stock figure of misery, and the speaker is using the proverb to say nothing in particular.

1. Primary and historical senses, oldest to newest.

  • A saying, a proverb, a maxim (14th–15th c.). The original and still central sense, a translation of Latin adagium. Neutral, and often admiring: a saying in which the wisdom of generations has been compressed.
  • A saying of authority; a text received as a rule (15th–16th c.). The word was used of a sentence of Scripture or of a doctrinal formula regarded as decisive, so that the adage of the church had the weight of a citation rather than of a proverb.
  • A motto, legend or device on a coat of arms (16th–17th c.). Here it names the particular function rather than the truth of a saying: the words a family puts over its arms.
  • A jest or witty saying (16th c.). A direct inheritance of the classical Latin sense, in which an adagium could be a remark made in sport as easily as a moral.
  • A trite, stale, worn-out saying; a platitude (17th c. onwards). The reversal, and now the usual sense. An old adage is nearly always a way of saying a cliché.
  • A maxim, an aphorism, a saying current among a nation, trade or school (18th–19th c.). A neutral, faintly dusty register, especially in the eighteenth century’s essayists, who spoke of the adages of antiquity and the adage of the Romans.
  • A slogan, a motto, a piece of received wisdom in a particular field (20th c.). A mild revival in the language of business, sport and politics, where the adage is the line everyone knows and nobody examines.

2. Register, connotation and the two halves of modern usage.Adage is used in two ways that are only just related, and the choice between them is usually deliberate. The neutral or faintly respectful use calls a saying an adage when the point is its age and its authority: there is an adage about the value of small things that this town lives by. The dismissive use calls it an adage when the point is that it is stale and hollow: he retreated behind the usual adages about self-reliance. The same sentence can carry both readings, which is why the word is so useful in argument. Its register is literary and slightly bookish in both; it is not a word for casual speech, and a speaker who uses it is choosing to sound dry, learned, or sardonic.

3. Secondary and figurative uses today.

  • A platitude, a truism, a circular piece of reasoning. The dominant modern sense. That’s an adage, not an argument is a common and useful objection in debate, and the word has become a label for reasoning that cannot be wrong because it cannot be wrong.
  • A national, professional or generational saying.The American adage that …, the old shopkeeper’s adage about …: the word is often used to attribute a piece of received wisdom to a group, which flatters the group and dismisses the saying at the same time.
  • A brand or corporate motto. In modern business writing an adage is the sort of line that appears on a badge or a T-shirt: memorable, unoriginal, and intended to be unarguable.
  • A title or a heading in a rhetorical sense. By extension, the word is occasionally used of any formulaic opening that is doing the work of a proposition — the adage at the head of an essay, the preamble that is really a summary in disguise.
  • Irony and understatement. Because it is a formal, slightly pompous word, it is now a comic instrument: applying it to something embarrassingly obvious is a way of drawing attention to the obviousness while pretending to be thoughtful.
  • The crossword and dictionary sense. In puzzle and quiz usage adage survives as a clean three-syllable word for a proverb, which is part of why it is still familiar to speakers who would not use it in conversation.

4. Grammar, collocation and idiom. The noun is countable, normally singular, and takes an in the singular and adages in the plural: an adage, the old adages, a familiar adage. The commonest collocations are adjectival: old, ancient, familiar, well-worn, hoary, trite, current, popular. The standard frames are the noun after an old or a familiar, the phrase it is an adage that …, and the partitive as the adage has it. Unlike proverb, adage almost never introduces a directly quoted saying, though as the adage goes and quoted forms in parentheses do occur. The word is a countable noun only; there is no mass-noun use in standard English. In the quoted context, the poor cat i’th‛ adage shows the word functioning as a container for a stock comparison — the proverbial cat whose situation is proverbial, an example of the very staleness the word now implies.
