EVOLUTION OF PRECIPICE: FROM 'HEAD BEFORE' TO THE BRINK OF CATASTROPHE Precipice is a word whose whole personality is contained in its formation. It descends from the Latin compound praeceps, built from the preposition prae, 'before, in front of', and the noun caput, 'head'. The compound means literally 'head before' - a person or thing with the head forward and foremost. Latin used it of a running or falling body: to go praeceps is to go head-first, and from that physical picture the word spread to moral and mental life, so that praeceps also meant 'rash, hasty, violent', because a person who rushes head-first into action is precisely the person who has not looked before they leap. This double life - physical headlong motion and moral rashness - is the seed of everything the English word has become. The two roots behind the compound The first element, prae, belongs to one of the oldest and most securely reconstructed pieces of the Indo-European inheritance. Its ancestor is the preposition *per(i), 'before, in front of; through, across', whose reflexes appear across the family - Sanskrit pari, Greek peri, Latin per - and which also underlies English words such as per, through (via a Germanic route), and the prefix pre- (from Latin prae again). The second element, caput, 'head', descends from the reconstructed PIE root *kaput- or *kauput-, the source of a great family of head-words: Gothic haubi-th, Old English heafod (modern head), German Haupt, and Latin caput itself, the root of captain, capital, capital punishment, and per capita. The exact vocalism of the PIE form is debated, but its reality is not in doubt. What is striking is how deeply the image of the head-first fall reached into the Latin word: praeceps is not a description of a cliff at all, but of a falling body, and the cliff is only the place that makes the body fall that way. From falling body to steep place (classical Latin) In classical Latin the noun praecipitium was formed from the stem of praeceps with the suffix -ium. It meant literally 'the place of headlong falling' - the steep place, the precipice - and Latin writers used it, as English writers would later, both for physical cliffs and for moral falls: to be drawn over the praecipitium of disgrace. The family around it grew richly. The verb praecipitare meant 'to throw headlong', and from it come English precipitate (to throw headlong; to act hastily), precipitation (a headlong fall; condensed moisture falling from the sky), and the astronomical and chemical senses of precipitation, all keeping the ancient image of something tumbling down. The adjective praeceps itself gave English the doublet precipice (via the noun) and the later formations precipitous, precipitously, and precipitancy. The borrowing into English (c. 1590 and after) English took the word in two steps. The adjective precipitate entered the language in the sixteenth century from Latin, and the noun precipice was borrowed around 1590 from Middle French precipice, which itself was a learned re-borrowing of Latin praecipitium in the twelfth century. From its first appearance in English, precipice was a grave and dramatic word: it named a steep place where one falls headlong, and the falling head inside it was felt from the start. During the seventeenth century the figurative sense grew alongside the literal: to stand 'on the precipice of' ruin, war, or disaster came to describe any situation balanced on the brink of catastrophe, and the word became a favourite of the literature of the sublime - the Romantic vocabulary of epic heights and abysses, of mountain ranges and vertiginous gorges. The modern word In modern English the figurative sense may now lead: the word is as likely to describe a bank's balance sheet as a mountain wall - a company on the precipice of bankruptcy, a nation on the precipice of war, a species on the precipice of extinction. The word remains formal, literary, and epic; ordinary speech prefers cliff or drop and reserves precipice for writing that wants gravity and scale. Yet the literal sense has never died, and in Tolkien's The Hobbit the two lives of the word meet in a single sentence. The travellers are left on a mountain spur from which there is no path down except by flying and no path off except by jumping over a precipice: the noun is at once the literal sheer drop around the spur and the brink of disaster on which the whole company now stands. A word formed two thousand years ago from the image of a falling head does its work in the space of a sentence, turning a piece of mountain geography into the shape of catastrophe itself.
