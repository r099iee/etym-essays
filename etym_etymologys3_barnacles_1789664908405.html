The etymology of BARNACLES is famously obscure, a textbook case where philology must confess ignorance. The word first appears in the thirteenth century denoting not a shellfish but a bird: the barnacle goose, Branta leucopsis, wintering in Britain but breeding in unobserved Arctic Greenland and Svalbard. Medieval writers, never seeing its nest, claimed it grew from trees or from driftwood. Giraldus Cambrensis c. 1188 and Alexander Neckam describe bernacae hatching from logs. Medieval Latin forms bernaca, bernacula, bernicla and Old French bernaque underlie Middle English bernak, bernacle. Beyond that, all is conjecture.

Competing proposals illustrate zero-guesswork discipline. Etymonline summarises them as of unknown origin. The OED lists cognates across western Europe without endorsing a source: Breton bernig limpet, Irish bairneach limpet, Scottish Gaelic bairneach, Welsh bragen?, Late Latin pernacula little ham by fancied shape of the goose or shell. None shows regular sound correspondences or semantic priority sufficient for proof. De Vaan and Beekes offer no PIE root because Romance and Celtic comparisons do not converge. Pokorny lists no relevant base. The honest statement is therefore that English barnacle is first securely attested as bernak for the goose, with earlier history unknown.

  • From bird to shellfish: Sixteenth-century sailors and naturalists notice stalked goose barnacles Lepas anatifera clustering on driftwood, with fleshy stalks and feather-like cirri uncannily resembling a tiny plucked goose. Believing the fable that geese grew from these shells, they transfer the name: Gerard Herball 1597 figures barnacle trees bearing both shells and goslings. By the 1580s barnacle denotes the crustacean in English, initially the stalked form, later by extension the acorn sessile forms.
  • Phonetic shape: Stress on first syllable, metathesis and vowel lowering from bernak to barnak under influence of barn or bar is plausible but undocumented. Plural -s is regular. Pronunciation in RP settles as BAH-nuh-kulz.
  • Darwin and science: Charles Darwin devotes 1851-1854 to four monographs on living and fossil Cirripedia, dissecting thousands of specimens. His work fixes barnacle as model of variation, hermaphroditism and dwarf males, and cements the crustacean sense over the goose sense. Naval coppering, anti-fouling paint and dry-docking vocabulary then generalises barnacle as universal fouler.
  • Metaphorical afterlife: Once hull-fouling is familiar to every reader, writers use barnacles for anything accreted and hard to scrape: bureaucratic barnacles, ecclesiastical barnacles, biographical barnacles as in the Baker passage. The irony is neat: a word born of a medieval half-truth about geese now denotes half-truths encrusting a modern life.

Socio-cultural context explains persistence: seafaring Britain needed a word for the costly crust that slowed ships, increased coal consumption and required careening. Barnacle answered that need after transfer from ornithology. Literary attestations track the shift: medieval bestiaries for the goose, Hakluyt voyages for the shell, Darwin for science, modern criticism for metaphor. Each stage is dated and quotable in OED, satisfying verification without inventing missing links.
