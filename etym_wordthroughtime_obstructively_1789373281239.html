- Early 17th Century - Enters Early Modern English derived from the adjective *obstructive*; predominantly utilized in medical and physical treatises to describe bodily humors, thick phlegm, or physical debris that obstructively plug veins, arteries, or conduits.
- Late 17th to 18th Century - Expands into civil, legal, and spatial registers; applied to physical barricades, blocked highways, and contentious legal filings that act obstructively against property rights, public transit, or judicial proceedings.
- 19th Century - Achieves widespread political and journalistic notoriety during the Victorian era to describe systematic parliamentary filibustering—especially the tactical obstructionism of the Irish Parliamentary Party under Parnell—cementing its association with deliberate procedural paralysis.
- 20th Century - Broadens into psychology, labor relations (work-to-rule strikes), and literary/aesthetic criticism; modernist critics utilize the term to describe avant-garde techniques, jarring formal digressions, or didactic interpolations that disrupt smooth narrative immersion.
- 21st Century - Flourishes in legal jurisprudence (obstruction of justice, behaving obstructively during depositions), corporate governance, and narrative theory, denoting both adversarial malice and productive formal friction in contemporary art.
