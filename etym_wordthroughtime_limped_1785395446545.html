- Old English 'limpan' - 'to happen, to befall' (hit limped, 'it happens'): a neutral word about how things turn out, with no walking sense at all.
- Middle English 'limpen' - 'to happen' gains a negative shading, 'to fail, to fall short' - the seed of the modern word.
- 1560s - 'limp' appears as the walking verb: a leg that comes up short with every step, an uneven, halting gait favouring one sound leg; motion that continues but cannot hide its cost.
- 1706 - a separate adjective 'limp', 'flaccid, without stiffness', resurfaces the root's ancient 'hang slack' meaning after a thousand years.
- 19th century - the noun 'limp' ('walk with a limp', 'a slight limp') names the uneven gait itself.
- Modern English - plain, physical, unsentimental: a word of walks and wounds, not of hospitals, combining physical damage with dogged onward effort.
