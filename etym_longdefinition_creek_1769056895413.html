creek has two settled modern senses, one British and one American. In British English (and in C. S. Lewis's Narnia), a creek is a narrow, sheltered inlet of the sea — a natural indentation of the coast into which the sea reaches, small enough to feel friendly: "they ran the boat up into a little creek and moored her to a tree." The nuance is one of protected, intimate scale: the creek is the antithesis of the storm-driven deep, a natural harbour hidden from open water, and the adjective "little" plus the image of mooring to a tree give the word a homely, almost domestic feel — the sea's edge becomes a garden nook. This is the British sense of the word, and Lewis, writing as an Englishman, uses it as a working seaman's term for a snug place of refuge. In American English, by contrast, a creek (dialectal crick) is a small fresh-water stream or tributary — the niche that brook and rivulet fill in Britain. This famous Atlantic split means the same word names two different kinds of water on the two sides of the ocean.

Secondary senses and derivatives:

  • The idiom "up the creek (without a paddle)" (from c. the 1930s) — "stranded, in trouble, in a difficulty," from the image of a boat stuck on a small stream with no way of moving it.
  • The ethnonym Creek — the Creek (Muscogee) people of the American Southeast took their English name from the riverside and creek-side settlements along which they lived.
  • Technical and geographical compounds — "tidal creek," "creek mouth," "creek bank," "run a boat into a creek" — used in coastal navigation and landscape description.
  • Dialectal crick — the American pronunciation/spelling variant, also familiar from the idiom "crick in the neck," though that "crick" (a cramp) is a separate word from a different source.

Synonym boundaries. inlet is the general term for any indentation of the shoreline admitting water. cove is a small, rounded, sheltered bay with a curving shore — a basin rather than a channel. bay is larger and more open. estuary is specifically the tidal mouth of a river where fresh and salt water meet. harbour implies a place of shelter for shipping, often improved by man-made works. channel is a navigable passage, usually wider and more functional. brook and rivulet are the British words for the small fresh-water streams that Americans call creeks. The distinctive feature of a creek, in the coastal sense Lewis uses, is that it is a waterway defined by the shore — a bend or nook in the land that lets the sea in; and in the American sense, a small flowing stream. Both senses preserve the ancient "bend, hook, nook" image that lies at the heart of the word's history.

The historical bridge between the two senses is worth spelling out, because it is one of the most instructive sense-shifts in the language. In its oldest attested English meaning (Middle English creke, c. 13th century) the word named a fissure or narrow opening in the coast — the "bend" in the land where the sea creeps in. British English kept that coastal meaning. When English colonists reached North America they first used creek for the same tidal inlets they had known at home, then extended it to the fresh-water streams that ran down into those inlets, and finally — as settlement pressed inland — to any small river or tributary. The metaphor that makes this natural is precisely the ancient one: a stream, like an inlet, is a winding, bending thread of water finding its way through the land. So the same word now denotes two related but distinct kinds of waterway — a tidal arm of the sea (Britain) and a small fresh-water stream (America) — and both are still governed by the root image of a "bend or nook."

Register and collocation. The word is everyday and mildly literary. It appears in the collocations "a little creek," "creek mouth," "tidal creek," "creek bank," and "run a boat into a creek," and in the idiom "up the creek (without a paddle)" — meaning stranded or in trouble, from the picture of a boat stuck on a small stream with no way of moving it. In Narnia it is a working seaman's word for a snug place of refuge: the adjective "little" and the tree-mooring give the scene a homely, protected, almost domestic feel, the antithesis of the storm-driven deep. For the learner, the essential fact is the Atlantic split: in British English a creek is salt tidal water you can moor a boat in; in American English it is a small fresh-water stream you might wade in.
