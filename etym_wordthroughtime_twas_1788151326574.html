- [16th c.] - ’Twas is an ordinary spoken contraction of “it was,” ubiquitous in plays, letters, and verse; it carries no special dignity beyond informality.
- [17th c.] - Shakespearean stage English and the prose of the age make contracted forms familiar to every speaker; ’twas is still everyday, alternating freely with “it was.”
- [18th c.] - Grammarians begin treating contractions as informal; ’twas retreats from formal prose while thriving in poetry and song, where its single metrical beat is a gift.
- [19th c.] - Wordsworth uses it in elevated blank verse; Moore’s 1823 “’Twas the night before Christmas” fixes it in popular memory; in speech it survives as rural and dialectal.
- [20th–21st c.] - ’Twas is a fossilised archaism: the instant signal of ballads, Christmas poetry, and mock-heroic pastiche — no longer said, always quoted.
