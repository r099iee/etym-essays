- Late 16th c. — Adjective inherent entered English via Scholastic theology and legal treatises to describe divine attributes or permanent constitutional rights adhering in a sovereign or entity.
- Mid-17th c. — Adverbial form inherently first documented in English philosophical and scientific treatises (Hobbes, Boyle, Locke) to delineate essential primary qualities from secondary, accidental traits.
- 18th c. — Established as a foundational term in Enlightenment political philosophy and revolutionary declarations regarding the "inherently unalienable" rights of mankind.
- 19th c. — Broadened into jurisprudence, maritime insurance, and patent law, establishing statutory doctrines like "inherent vice" and inherent judicial authority.
- 20th–21st c. — Indispensable analytical qualifier in cognitive linguistics (Chomskyan generative grammar), systems engineering ("inherently safe" design), and sociology.
