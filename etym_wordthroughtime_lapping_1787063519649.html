- Old English lapian — "to drink with the tongue" — a concrete animal verb (dogs, cats, calves), neutral and domestic; no poetic weight at all.
- 14th c. — a new sense appears: "to make the sound of liquid" — probably imitative; the sound of a dog lapping becomes the sound of water hitting a shore — lapping waves are born.
- 16th–17th c. — lapping enters poetry: Shakespeare and the lyric poets use "lapping waves" for gentle, soothing shorelines — the word gains a romantic, pastoral register.
- 18th–19th c. — "lapping" water becomes a stock Romantic image of peace and solitude (lakes, shores, boat rides) — the antithesis of crashing, raging waves.
- 19th–20th c. — figurative extension: "lapping up" praise, compliments, information = absorbing greedily — the animal drinking sense becomes a metaphor for eager consumption.
- 20th–21st c. — "lapping" also enters racing jargon (to lap = to complete a circuit, from a different root, OE læppa "fold") and engineering (lap = polish with a rotating disk) — homographs that have fused in perception with the splash-word, though historically separate.
