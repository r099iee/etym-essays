• Spite: Similar because both involve the desire to hurt or frustrate another; differs because spite is typically petty, trivial, and impulsive (motivated by minor personal pique, like scratching a car), whereas malice implies a deeper, more dangerous, and systemic will to inflict genuine ruin.
  • Malevolence: Similar because both denote an active wish that evil befall another; differs because malevolence is an abstract, philosophical disposition or state of mind (wishing ill), whereas malice is more dynamic, actionable, and tied directly to intentional conduct in law.
  • Rancor: Similar because both describe deep-seated, bitter animosity; differs because rancor stresses the corrosive, sour emotional feeling of long-held resentment festering inside the aggrieved person, while malice emphasizes the aggressive outward intent to strike back and harm.
  • Malignity: Similar because both refer to evil intent; differs because malignity denotes a permanent, congenital wickedness of character (a pathological evil nature), whereas malice can arise from specific rivalries or legal circumstances.
  • Envy: Similar because envy frequently acts as the primary fuel for malice (as Johnson notes); differs because envy is the internal pain and covetous sorrow caused by another's excellence or good fortune, whereas malice is the proactive desire to destroy that excellence or harm that person.
  • Ill will: Similar because both describe hostile feelings; differs because ill will is a milder, everyday colloquial phrase denoting general unfriendliness or coldness, lacking the severe criminal and moral culpability of malice.
  • Vindictiveness: Similar because both aim to inflict suffering on another; differs because vindictiveness is obsessed specifically with revenge and retaliation for a perceived slight, whereas malice can be unprovoked, wanton, or driven purely by competitive jealousy.
  • Animus: Similar because both describe active hostility; differs because animus (from Latin for mind/spirit) often implies an underlying intellectual bias, institutional prejudice, or animosity motivating an official decision.
  • Venom: Similar because both describe toxic, harmful intent; differs because venom is a visceral, organic metaphor highlighting the caustic, bitter, and stinging expression of hatred in speech or writing.
