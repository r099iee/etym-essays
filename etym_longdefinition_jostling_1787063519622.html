Jostling is the present participle, verbal noun, and participial adjective of the verb jostle (historically justle), designating the dynamic, irregular, and often rough physical act of pushing, elbowing, shoving, or colliding against people or objects in a confined space, as well as the aggressive figurative competition among rivals striving for supremacy, priority, or limited resources.

Kinematically and socially, jostling occupies a middle tier of physical friction: it is more chaotic and persistent than a single accidental bump, yet less overtly hostile than a deliberate punch or violent assault. It describes the turbulent fluid dynamics of crowded human assemblies—such as packed market squares, subway platforms, festival crowds, and riotous mobs—where individual trajectories clash and individuals must constantly maneuver, nudge, and absorb minor impacts to make headway. When applied to inanimate objects (such as barrels tumbling down a subterranean tunnel), it conveys erratic, multidirectional collisions against enclosing boundaries and neighboring bodies.

The semantic versatility and polysemic dimensions of jostling span several concrete, social, and abstract domains:

  • Primary Physical & Spatial Motion (Crowd Friction): Denotes the rough, continuous physical contact that occurs when navigating through dense throngs. Pedestrians jostle one another on narrow sidewalks; commuters jostle for entrance into train doors; and soldiers jostle in tight formations during chaotic retreats.
  • Inanimate & Hydrodynamic Turbulence: Describes unguided physical objects crashing, rubbing, and knocking against one another and their environment while being carried by gravity, currents, or mechanical conveyances. In Tolkien's river-gate passage, the barrels are depicted jostling against the walls of the tunnel, capturing their unguided, turbulent clatter in the dark subterranean watercourse.
  • Figurative, Political, & Commercial Rivalry: Extensively used in socio-political commentary to describe fierce, maneuvering competition where competitors elbow one another out of the way to secure power, favor, or market share (e.g., "ministers jostling for the prime minister's ear" or "tech giants jostling for dominance in artificial intelligence").
  • Cognitive & Psychological Tumult: Metaphorically applied to internal mental states where disparate ideas, competing loyalties, painful memories, or contradictory impulses crowd and agitate the consciousness (e.g., "a dozen frantic thoughts jostling in her mind").
  • Equestrian & Chivalric Ancestry (The "Jousting" Bridge): Historically derived from the frequentative form of joust, the word originally carried direct chivalric connotations of horsemen tilting, clashing armor, and charging lance-to-lance before generalizing into broader domestic and crowd-based shoving.

Historical Semantic Bridge & Polysemy: The evolution of jostling is a classic example of frequentative morphological elaboration and semantic democratization. In medieval Anglo-Norman chivalric culture, jouster (from Late Latin iuxtāre, "to come together, approach side-by-side") was an elite aristocratic sport where armored knights galloped toward each other in ritual combat. In 14th-century Middle English, speakers attached the iterative/frequentative suffix -le to create jostle, transforming the singular, noble shock of a knightly tilt into the repetitive, disorderly shoving of common crowds in bustling medieval urban markets.
