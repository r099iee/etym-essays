MENIAL is a single-root word; its chain is linear: 1. Proto-Indo-European *men- 'to stay, to remain, to stand still' (c. 4000–3500 BCE) — source of Greek ménō 'I stay, I remain', Latin manēre 'to stay', and Armenian mnam 'to remain'. 2. Latin manēre 'to stay, remain' (attested from the 3rd c. BCE). 3. Latin mansio, mansiōnis 'a staying, a sojourn, a dwelling, a house' (attested from the 2nd c. BCE; in the imperial administration, a staging post on the great roads). 4. Vulgar Latin *mansionāta 'household' — a reconstructed (not directly attested) derived feminine noun meaning a family establishment, inferred from its descendants. 5. Old French maisniee / mesniee 'household, retinue, body of servants' (attested from c. the 11th c.). 6. Anglo-Norman meignal, menial 'pertaining to the household, domestic' (attested from the 13th–14th c.). 7. Middle English meynial, menial 'of the household; a domestic servant' (attested from the 14th c.; Chaucer uses meyny for a lord's following). 8. Modern English MENIAL — adjective and noun, with the sense narrowed from 'of the household' to 'low, unskilled, servile' (fully established by the 16th–18th centuries). Note on spelling and ambiguity: the Middle English servant sense also appears as meiny, meinye, menye; the modern spelling menial standardizes the Anglo-Norman form. The separate, unrelated Middle English use influenced by 'mean' exists but does not belong to this chain.
