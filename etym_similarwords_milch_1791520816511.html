• Milk: the exact English cognate, descended from the same Proto-Germanic noun; similar because it denotes the same mammary secretion and shares the same range of dairy compounds; differs because English has specialised milk as both noun and verb (“to milk”), whereas German uses melken for the verb and reserves Milch for the noun.
  • Molke: the watery residue left after curdling and straining milk; similar because it is a dairy liquid obtained from Milch and often sold as a health drink; differs because it is specifically whey, translucent and sour, not the whole white secretion.
  • Sahne: the fatty layer that rises on milk; similar because it is a dairy product separated from Milch and used in cooking; differs because it is the rich cream, not the whole liquid, and is called Rahm in southern and Austrian usage.
  • Rahm: the southern German and Austrian word for cream; similar because it names the same fat-rich dairy product as Sahne; differs because it is regionally marked and also appears in idioms such as den Rahm abschöpfen, “to skim off the cream”, which Milch does not form.
  • Buttermilch: the liquid left after churning butter; similar because it is a milk-based drink and carries Milch in its name; differs because it is fermented, slightly sour and low in fat, a by-product rather than fresh milk.
  • Magermilch: skimmed milk; similar because it is literally milk with the fat removed; differs because it names a specific fat content and is a technical dairying term, whereas Milch alone is unmarked.
  • Kuhmilch: cow's milk; similar because it is the most common everyday kind of Milch; differs because it specifies the animal of origin and is used where the default cannot be assumed, as in infant feeding or allergy labelling.
  • Muttermilch: mother's milk, human breast milk; similar because it is the original, etymologically primary sense of the word; differs because it is physiologically specific and emotionally charged, and it never appears in the dairy or commercial compounds.
  • Milchling: a mushroom of the genus Lactarius that exudes milky latex when cut; similar because it is named for the milky fluid it produces; differs because it denotes a fungus, not a food liquid, and belongs to botanical rather than domestic vocabulary.
  • Emulsion: a technical term for a suspension of one liquid in another, such as milk or lotion; similar because it describes the physical structure of Milch and of Sonnenmilch; differs because it is a scientific category covering paint, medicine and cosmetics, with no dairy or nurturing associations.
  • Milchglas: opal or milk glass; similar because it borrows the whiteness and opacity of Milch; differs because it denotes a manufactured material, and the compound is lexicalised rather than freely formed.
  • Milchstraße: the Milky Way; similar because it is the most famous compound of Milch and depends on the same white-band image; differs because it is an astronomical proper name, a calque of Latin via lactea, and has no connection with lactation.
