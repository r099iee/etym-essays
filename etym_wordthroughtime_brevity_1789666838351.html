- Classical Latin virtue — brevitas as rhetorical ideal in Cicero and Quintilian alongside clarity; perceived as disciplined, manly, and republican against Asiatic copiousness.
- Medieval ambivalence — brevity admired in maxims and collects but distrusted as obscurity in theology and law; perception oscillates between wisdom and insufficiency.
- Renaissance and Early Modern canonization — Hamlet's brevity is the soul of wit (1603) fixes the proverb; plain-style science (Royal Society) mandates brevity as honesty; perception is prestige-positive.
- 19th–20th century professionalization — legal, military, and journalistic brevity as efficiency; telegrams and headlines make shortness economic necessity.
- Contemporary Baker moment — Baker was never afraid of brevity: short declarative sentences (Nothing happened) revalued as courage, attention, and witness rather than poverty; brevity now signals respect for the reader and for silence.
