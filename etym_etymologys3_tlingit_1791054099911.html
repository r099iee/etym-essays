Tlingit is not an Indo-European word at all. It is the English spelling of the Indigenous self-name Lingít (pronounced with a voiceless alveolar lateral fricative [ɬ], often written Tl- in English), meaning human being or people in the Tlingit language, a branch of the Na-Dene family spoken in southeast Alaska and adjacent British Columbia and Yukon. The deeper prehistory of Lingít lies within Na-Dene historical linguistics, related distantly to Eyak and the Athabaskan languages. Proto-Na-Dene reconstructions (Sapir, Krauss, Leer) propose roots for person and people, but the exact Proto-Na-Dene antecedent of Lingít is not settled with the certainty of Indo-European sound laws, so responsible lexicography begins the verifiable chain at attested Tlingit Lingít rather than inventing a proto-form.

Contact history explains the English form. Russian fur traders and Orthodox missionaries, the first Europeans to describe the people in the late 18th century, generally called them Koloshi (from Russian kolos, awl, referring to labrets) — an exonym the Tlingit never used for themselves. British, American, and German ethnographers of the 19th century — notably in the reports of the Harriman Alaska Expedition, Franz Boas, and John Swanton — adopted the self-name in spellings such as Thlinket, Tlinkit, Tlingit. The initial Tl- attempts to render the lateral fricative /ɬ/ plus stop cluster unfamiliar to English ears. By the early 20th century Tlingit had standardised in anthropology, government treaties, and maps, displacing Thlinket and Kolosh in English.

Phonetics therefore show adaptation, not regular sound change:

  • Tlingit /ɬɪŋkɪ́t/ (with high tone on second syllable): lateral fricative + velar nasal + glottalised stress, tonal.
  • English /ˈtlɪŋɡɪt/ or /ˈklɪŋkɪt/: lateral replaced by consonant cluster tl- or kl-, tone lost, stress shifted to first syllable, final -t de-glottalised. Received Pronunciation typically says TLING-git with a clear /t/ + /l/ onset.
  • Spelling stabilisation: 19th-century variation Thlinket → Tlinkit → Tlingit; 20th-century Alaska Native brotherhoods and the Central Council affirm Tlingit in English, Lingít in Tlingit orthography.

Socio-cultural context is essential. The Tlingit are a matrilineal, clan-organised (Raven/Crow and Eagle/Wolf moieties), potlatch-holding Northwest Coast culture famed for totem poles, Chilkat weaving, cedar canoes, and salmon stewardship. Early travel literature, including the source passage about a seal-mimicking fisherman, often framed them as primitive war or cunning savagery — language modern scholarship rejects as colonial stereotyping. Twentieth-century land claims (notably the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act 1971), language revitalisation, and repatriation have restored Tlingit as a name of sovereignty: Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska, Sealaska Corporation, Tlingit language immersion schools.

Literary attestations trace this revaluation:

  • 1790s–1860s Russian-American period: Koloshi in Russian logs; Lingít recorded in word-lists by Veniaminov.
  • 1880s–1910s American ethnography: Swanton's Tlingit Myths and Texts (1909) establishes Tlingit as scholarly standard.
  • 1930s–1960s popular synthesis: Will Durant and popular histories use Tlingit fisherman as an illustration of primitive ingenuity, as in the present context.
  • 1970s–present: Capitalised Tlingit as people, adjective (Tlingit art), and language (to speak Tlingit); plural either Tlingit or Tlingits, with Tlingit preferred by the community.

In modern English Tlingit functions as proper noun (the Tlingit), adjective (Tlingit fisherman, Tlingit totem), and language name. It carries no Indo-European etymology, no PIE root, and must not be forced into one. Its evolution is a story of transcription, exonym displacement, and Indigenous self-assertion.
