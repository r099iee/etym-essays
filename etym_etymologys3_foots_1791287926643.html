The verb foots in "foots the bill" is the third-person singular of foot, and its story begins not with paying but with the human foot itself. The ultimate ancestor is PIE *ped- "foot", one of the most stable roots in Indo-European, reflected in Latin pes, pedis, Greek pous, podos, and Sanskrit pad. By Grimm's Law, PIE *p became Germanic *f, giving Proto-Germanic *fots, then Old English fot, with plural fet by i-mutation, the ancestor of modern foot/feet. From the body-part, Germanic and later English derived a rich spatial metaphor: the foot as the lowest part, base, or bottom of anything — foot of a hill, foot of a ladder, foot of a page.

In medieval accounting this metaphor became technical. Wardrobe accounts of Edward IV (c. 1480) speak of "the foote of the deliveree of stuff", meaning the sum or total written at the bottom of a column of figures. By the late 15th century this noun produced a verb: to foot an account meant to add up a column and enter the total at the foot. Acts of the Lords of Council (1491) record a count "footed". For two centuries "foot up" simply meant "add up", as in "debts foot up to £50,000" (The Times, 1867) or "losses footed up to £5000" (1893). Paying was not yet in the picture; footing was reckoning.

  • Body to base: from anatomical foot to the bottom of any object, document, or hill — a universal metaphor preserved in footnote, foothill, foot of the stairs.
  • Base to total: in manuscript ledgers, the total sits at the foot of the column; "foot" becomes bookkeeping jargon for the sum itself (1480 onward).
  • Total to action: noun becomes verb — to foot = to cast up figures and write the sum below (late 15th c.).
  • Reckoning to paying: the clerk who foots the account is typically settling it; by 1819 Estwick Evans writes of his dogs "leaving their master to foot their bills" — the first clear "pay" sense, American frontier context.
  • Fixed idiom: by mid-Victorian times "foot the bill" is a set colloquial phrase, with a nuance of paying for others or paying something large or unwelcome.

Estwick Evans's 1819 Pedestrious Tour is the OED's first citation for the pay sense, and later authorities (Etymonline dating it 1848, American Heritage dating the idiom early 1800s) agree on a 19th-century American crystallization. The semantic bridge is transparent: adding up precedes paying; the man who totals the bill takes responsibility for it. Wodehouse's use — "Uncle Tom foots the Boudoir bills" — preserves the classic implication: a wealthy backer routinely covering a publication's losses, half-expected, half-resented. Modern usage keeps that rueful colour: governments, taxpayers, or parents "foot the bill", rarely with joy. Phonologically the word is conservative: OE fot lengthened and diphthongised to modern /fʊt/, with voiceless plural /fiːt/ and regular third-person foots /fʊts/. The spelling double-o reflects Middle English lengthening. No serious etymological debate attaches; the chain PIE *ped- through Germanic is textbook, and the bookkeeping development is richly documented in account books, dictionaries, and newspapers.
