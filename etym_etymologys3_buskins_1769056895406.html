A wander-word. Buskin is a wander-word: a noun whose history is a series of borrowings across Europe, and whose ultimate source has never been recovered. The OED calls its origin "obscure," and the word belongs to a small, romantic class of European dress-words whose true starting-point has been lost. The likeliest reconstructed chain runs from a Middle Dutch form such as broseken — a diminutive meaning "a little soft boot" — into French as brosequin or brousequin, and from Old French into English in the mid-sixteenth century. English records the word from 1560 in the shapes buskin, busskin, or busken, and it clearly arrived as a trade- and dress-word, naming the soft leather half-boot worn by travellers and hunters. The same obscure donor apparently fed Italian borzacchino, Spanish borceguí, Portuguese borzeguim, and Provençal brocequin; the presence of near-identical forms in several Romance languages argues for a single, widely traded source-word whose exact origin — possibly eastern Mediterranean or Near Eastern — is now unknowable. Medieval Latin glosses such as broschini in thirteenth-century inventories show the same loan-fabric circulating among the clerics and merchants who first wrote the word down. No Indo-European, Semitic, or Turkic ancestor can be securely assigned; the word simply entered history as a foreign boot.

The path of the loan. The borrowing-path is a textbook case of medieval trade and travel. A soft, comfortable boot that laced above the ankle was practical footgear for hunters, pilgrims, and couriers crossing the rough roads of late-medieval Europe, and the fashion for such boots travelled with the people who wore them. Each language took the word from its neighbours and adapted the sound to its own mouth — Dutch broseken became French brosequin, and French brosequin became English buskin — while Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, and Provençal each kept their own near-twin. The instability of the word's spelling in sixteenth-century English (buskin, busskin, busken) is the normal signature of a recent loan still settling in. Had the word kept only this life, it would be a minor, half-forgotten item of the historical wardrobe, the ancestor of no modern word.

The decisive turn: tragedy. The decisive turn in the word's history came not from etymology but from the theatre. In classical Athens the actors of tragedy wore the kóthornos, a high boot of soft leather reaching above the calf; it was the stock footgear of the tragic stage, just as the low sock marked comedy. When Renaissance Europe revived classical drama, English playwrights and critics needed a name for this tragic boot, and "buskin" — already in the language as a soft boot — was pressed into service. From the 1590s "the buskin" became a standing metonymy for tragedy itself: Milton speaks of the "buskin'd stage," Dryden of the buskin of tragedy, and the pair "sock and buskin" (comedy and tragedy) passed into the common vocabulary of criticism. "To assume the buskin" meant to write or act tragedy; the adjective "buskined" meant both "wearing buskins" and "tragic." The word thus acquired a double life: a plain dress-word for a practical boot, and a lofty literary symbol for the highest and gravest genre of the stage. This theatrical metonymy is the reason the word survived at all — without it, buskin would have died out of ordinary speech as "boot" took over; with it, the word entered the permanent vocabulary of English literary criticism.

Semantic drift and register. The semantic history of buskin is therefore a story of elevation against a background of decline. In its first English century the concrete boot-sense was dominant and practical; in its second the theatrical sense rose to dominate learned usage; and through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "sock and buskin" hardened into a fixed phrase while the everyday boot-sense quietly receded. By the nineteenth century the word was bookish, and in the twentieth and twenty-first it has become rare and deliberately archaic — a word that in ordinary speech has been replaced by "boot" or "ankle-boot," but that in prose carries a fairy-tale, Renaissance-stage glamour. For the learned ear it also carries the memory of Athens, because the theatrical sense keeps alive the kóthornos of the Greek tragic stage, the very boot worn by Aeschylus and Sophocles' actors. In C. S. Lewis's Prince Caspian the word does its full historical work at once: the warm, soft, quiet boot is the old dress-word, and the tragic stage-echo is the fairy-tale, elevated glamour the narrative wants. A single word spans a medieval trade-route, a Renaissance revival, and a prince's midnight escape — an etymology folded into a plot.
