- Old English — shelter for soldiers: herebeorg means lodgings or quarters, literally army-shelter, a roof for men on the move, with no sea in sight.
- Early Middle English — any roof in a storm: herberwe widens to lodgings, inns, and refuge generally — travellers take harbour for the night on dry land.
- Late Middle English — the sea moves in: by the 14th c. the shelter of ships joins the shelter of travellers, and harbour towns from Bristol to Bergen live by the word.
- Early modern — refuge of the soul: preachers and poets make harbours of heaven, marriage, and philosophy — any calm water in a stormy life.
- 18th–19th c. — engineering tames the coast: breakwaters, piers, and dredged channels turn open roadsteads into true harbours, and the word fills with granite, tariffs, and pilotage.
- Today — shelter everywhere: warships and yachts, data-harbour laws and safe-harbour clauses, harboured grudges and harboured refugees — one old army lodging stretched from lodging house to law court to the Grey Havens.
