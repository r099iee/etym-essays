- c. 1300: the verb bar appears in English — “to fasten (a door, gate) with bars” — a physical, everyday action of security.
- 14th c.: extends to “to obstruct, block the way” — roads, rivers, passes barred — still physical, now also figurative of fortune and plans.
- 15th–16th c.: legalisation — people and claims “barred” from rights, offices and courts; “the Bar” becomes the legal profession (barristers); heraldic treatises regulate the “barred” (bar-wise) shield.
- 17th–19th c.: the statute-barred claim in law; natural history adopts “barred” for striped plumage and markings (the Barred Owl named in the 19th century); music borrows the French barre for the bar-line.
- 20th–21st c.: informal British “he’s barred” = banned from a pub or club; “barred from” is ubiquitous in journalism (immigration, doping, sport); social media “barred” = suspended; astronomy finds barred spiral galaxies — while the oldest physical sense (a door barred) remains as vivid as ever.
