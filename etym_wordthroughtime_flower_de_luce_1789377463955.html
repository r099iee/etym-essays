- 12th to 13th Century (Old French Origins) - Established in feudal France as *fleur de lys* ("flower of the lily"), adopted as the sacred armorial emblem and royal badge of the Capetian monarchy.
- 14th Century (Late Middle English) - Borrowed into English following the Hundred Years' War as *flour-de-lis*, *flour-de-lice*, and *flower-de-luce*; featured in Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales*; incorporated into the Royal Arms of England by Edward III (1340).
- 15th to 16th Century (Tudor & Elizabethan Era) - The spelling *flower-de-luce* becomes the universal English name for all iris species in gardens and literature; celebrated by William Shakespeare in *The Winter's Tale* and *Henry V*.
- 17th to 18th Century - John Gerard (1597) and John Parkinson describe countless varieties of "Flower-de-luces" in their landmark herbals; remains standard in English horticultural prose before Linnaeus popularizes the genus name *Iris* (1753).
- 19th Century (Victorian Romanticism) - Cherished as a deliberately archaic, nostalgic pastoral term by Romantic and Victorian authors (Longfellow's 1867 poetry volume *Flower-de-Luce*, Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*).
- 20th to 21st Century - Largely supplanted in everyday conversational speech by *iris*, surviving as an esteemed poetic archaism and as a classic term in heraldic and medievalist scholarship.
