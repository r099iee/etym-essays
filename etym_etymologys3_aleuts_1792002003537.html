The etymological emergence of the ethnonym Aleuts is one of the most historically illuminating case studies of colonial exonymy, maritime contact linguistics, and cartographic institutionalization in the North Pacific. Unlike endonyms (autonyms) created by Indigenous communities to define their own selfhood—such as the native self-designation Unangax̂ (singular) or Unangan / Unangas (plural), which translates literally as "people of the sea" or "people of the coastal surf"—the word Aleut was an external descriptor coined and propagated by Russian fur-trade explorers during the mid-eighteenth century.

The precise linguistic origin of the Russian root aleut (алеут) has generated considerable philological debate among Russian, Chukotkan, and American linguists, leading to two principal hypotheses:

  • The Chukotko-Kamchatkan Hypothesis (The Standard Scholarly Derivation): Advanced by prominent historical linguists including Roman Jakobson and accepted by the Alaska Native Language Center, this derivation posits that Russian Siberian promyshlenniki (fur-trade adventurers) borrowed the term from neighboring Paleo-Asiatic peoples on the Kamchatka Peninsula. In the Koryak, Aliutor, and Chukchi languages, the word for "island" is reconstructed as alyat or aləw-, with alí-ut or aliut denoting "islanders" or "those who dwell upon the islands." When Vitus Bering and Aleksei Chirikov made landfall on the westernmost Aleutian islands (the Near Islands, including Attu and Agattu) during the Second Kamchatka Expedition in 1741, Russian traders questioned local Kamchatkan and Koryak guides regarding the identities of the distant maritime people. The guides referred to them generically as alí-ut ("the island people"), which the Russian ear transcribed phonetically as aleut (алеут).
  • The Unangan Endogenous Hypothesis: An alternative theory, noted by early ethnographers and missionary linguists such as Ivan Veniaminov (Saint Innocent of Alaska), suggests that the root may have been a garbled Russian mishearing of an indigenous Unangam Tunuu word, specifically allíthuh or allíthuk, meaning "a community," "an assembly of people," or "a gathering/host." Under this view, when Russian sailors asked the islanders who they were, the inhabitants may have responded with a word indicating their village assembly or collective kinship group, which the Russians phoneticized as aleut.
  • The Aliutor Toponymic Hypothesis: A third, related proposition suggests that Russian Cossacks, already familiar with the Aliutor people (inhabitants of the Olutorsky Gulf on the Kamchatka coast), erroneously transferred their name across the Bering Sea to designate newly encountered islanders who displayed superficially similar maritime subsistence practices and clothing.

Regardless of whether the ultimate source was Chukotkan or Unangan, the historical trajectory of the word was decisively driven by the commercial machinery of the imperial Russian fur trade. Following the voyage of the Sv. Petr (St. Peter) and Sv. Pavel (St. Paul), Russian trading consortia flooded into the Aleutian chain to harvest the exceptionally dense, valuable pelts of the northern sea otter (Enhydra lutris). Throughout the 1750s and 1760s, promyshlenniki records utilized aleuty (алеуты) exclusively for the inhabitants of the western Near Islands. However, as trading fleets pushed progressively eastward along the Rat, Andreanof, and Fox Islands, Russian administrators extended Aleuty as an all-encompassing administrative label across the entire archipelago.

The term entered West European languages in the final quarter of the eighteenth century. In 1778, Captain James Cook sailed into the North Pacific on his third circumnavigation, encountering Russian outposts on Unalaska Island. Cook's widely published official journals, released in London in 1784, introduced the terms Aleutian Islands and Aleutians to the English-speaking world, securing their place on maritime navigation charts. German and French translations quickly followed, standardizing Aleuten and Aléoutes across continental European geography.

During the nineteenth century, the Russian-American Company (chartered in 1799) created a profound linguistic and cultural complication by applying the term Aleut to the Pacific Yupik (Sugpiaq) inhabitants of Kodiak Island and Prince William Sound, who spoke an Eskimoan language entirely distinct from Unangam Tunuu. The Kodiak Islanders adopted this exonym into their own grammar as Alutiiq, creating a permanent dual usage that persists in modern ethnology. Following the 1867 Treaty of Cession, American administrators adopted the spelling Aleuts, which became codified in federal treaties, scientific reports, and legal statutes, culminating in modern self-determination movements where the ancestral autonym Unangan is increasingly reaffirmed.
