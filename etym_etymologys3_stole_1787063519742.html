I. The verb: Germanic theft and stealth

The verb steal belongs to the oldest stratum of Germanic vocabulary. Its history begins in the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root *stel- / *ster-, glossed "to steal, to rob"; the exact vocalism and shape of the PIE root are debated, and some scholars prefer a connection to a root meaning "to place, to set" (PIE *stel-) on the theory that stealing was originally "carrying away secretly". Whatever the precise root, the outcome in Proto-Germanic was a strong verb *stelan-, with the classic ablaut pattern of the Germanic strong verb classes (present, past singular, past plural, participle).

In Old English the verb is stelan, a class IV strong verb. Its attested forms include the present stelan ("to steal"), the past singular stæl, the past plural stælon, and the past participle stolen. Old English texts use it both for the crime of theft (with strong legal and moral weight, as in the law codes and penitentials) and - remarkably early - for furtive motion: writers could say that someone "stole" (i.e., slipped quietly) toward a place. The intransitive motion sense is thus not a modern invention but a continuation of Old English practice.

The Middle English period (roughly 1100-1500) brought dialectal variety: forms such as stele, stal(e), and stole appear in texts. The past tense gradually stabilised on a form with a long mid vowel, written stole, whose /oː/ reflects the influence of the past plural and past participle stems (compare the parallel development of "bore" from "bear"). This documented levelling - the ō-forms winning over the older æ-forms - is why the modern past tense is "stole" rather than *"stale".

By Early Modern English the conjugation "steal, stole, stolen" was fixed. Shakespeare uses the past tense richly: "I stole into a neighbour's house", "he hath stole away", and the famous "Thou dost ... steal men's hearts". The King James Bible (1611) preserves both senses - "lest he ... steal" (Exodus) and the motion sense in "he stole away" (1 Samuel). Across the 17th-19th centuries the figurative extensions accumulated: "steal a kiss", "steal a glance", "steal a march", and the idiom "stole the show" (popularised in the early 20th century, originally of theatrical performances).

Modern English shows the full flowering of the figurative system. In sport, "to steal a base" (attested from the late 19th century, as baseball organised) and the "stolen base" statistic; in entertainment, "to steal the show" (someone who outshines the stars); in romance, "to steal a heart"; in business and journalism, "to steal a lead" or "steal a march on rivals". The crime sense remains primary in legal and moral discourse, but the word's cultural centre of gravity has shifted toward the audacious, the clever, and the charming.

II. The noun: from Greek stolē to the stole

The noun stole is an entirely separate word that happens to share the same spelling. Its story begins in ancient Greek with the verb stéllō ("to make ready, to equip, to array, to send"), from which is formed the noun stolē, meaning "a garment, robe, equipment, or array". The Greeks wore the stolē as a long flowing garment.

Latin borrowed the word as stola, a long, sleeveless outer robe reaching to the feet, worn by respectable Roman matrons as a badge of married status - the stola was, in effect, a social uniform, and legislation even attempted to police who might wear it. From secular Roman dress the word passed into Christian use: in Late Latin and the early Church it came to denote the liturgical vestment - the stola worn by a priest at mass, a long scarf-like band symbolising authority and, in the Eastern tradition, the orarion.

English received the word through two channels. Old English, in contact with the Roman Church, borrowed it early (Old English stole for the vestment, recorded in Anglo-Saxon glosses). Middle and Modern English continue the ecclesiastical sense; from the 19th-20th centuries fashion extended the term to the secular "evening stole", a long scarf of fur or silk worn by women over the shoulders. So the modern noun has two faces - liturgical and fashionable - both ultimately from Greek stolē "array, garment".

III. Convergence

The past-tense verb stole (Germanic, from PIE) and the noun stole (Greek via Latin) converged on identical spelling and pronunciation entirely by accident. Dictionary entries therefore run them as two separate headwords. In the Tolkien context, the word is used as the verb in its ancient intransitive sense - a quiet, creeping motion - while the "mounds of treasure" he creeps toward keep the crime sense in the reader's ear.
