- 14th c. - Borrowed from Middle Dutch / Middle Low German (*hobbelen*, to rock, toss, or sway) into Middle English as a verb (*hobelen*), meaning to rock back and forth, sway unsteadily, or walk with an uneven, limping gait.
- 15th–16th c. - Acquired a specific equestrian and pastoral meaning: to fasten the legs of a horse, camel, or cow with straps (*hobbles*) to prevent them from straying while grazing.
- 17th–18th c. - The nominal form *hobble* developed to designate both the awkward, limping gait itself and the physical restraining ropes placed on animals.
- 19th c. - Extended metaphorically into economics, politics, and social commentary meaning 'to deliberately restrict, handicap, or impede an institution, enterprise, or competitor.'
- 20th–21st c. - Frequently featured in narrative literature (depicting exhausted or wounded wanderers walking with a 'hobble and a wobble'), medical descriptions of lameness, and geopolitical analysis of crippling sanctions.
