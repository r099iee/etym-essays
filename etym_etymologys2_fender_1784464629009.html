PIE *gʷhen- "to strike, slay, smite" (c. 4000–3500 BCE; the labiovelar *gʷʰ- regularly yields Latin f-, as *gʷʰer- "warm" → Latin formus) → Latin -fendere (bound verb "to strike, thrust, keep off", attested only inside compounds such as defendere, offendere, infendere) + de- "away, off" → Latin defendere "to ward off, protect, repel" (classical prose; legal and military senses, 1st c. BCE) → Old French defendre "to defend, resist" (9th–13th c.) → Middle English defenden "to defend" (13th c.) → English defender / defendour "one who defends" (agent noun, late 13th c.) → aphetic shortening fender "protector, guard, barrier" (late 13th c.) — the initial unstressed syllable drops, exactly as cute < acute and spite < despite · nautical fender "pad hung to keep a hull off docks and wharves" (15th c.) · fireplace fender "low metal guard before a hearth" (1680s) · automotive fender "guard over a motor-car wheel" (US, 20th c.); railway buffer beam → back-formation verb fend "to ward off" (c. 1300), as in to fend for oneself => fender "low metal guard before a fireplace" (the sense fixed by the context). DEBATE / NUANCE: The PIE root *gʷhen- for Latin fend- is standard and secure; the bare verb -fendere is attested only in prefixed compounds, so its isolated meaning is reconstructed from those forms. The Germanic branch of the same root (*bana- "slayer") gives English bane (as in wolfsbane, baneful) — a cousin, not an ancestor, of fender.
