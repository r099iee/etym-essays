• Feed (a fire): Similar in adding fuel to a fire; differs because feed emphasizes the supplying of fuel and says nothing about stirring or arranging, while stoke includes the poking, stirring, and banking of the coals as well as the feeding.
  • Fuel: Similar in adding material to a fire; differs because fuel as a verb is chiefly abstract and modern ("to fuel debate"), and is not the everyday verb for tending a fire, while stoke is the concrete, physical word for the act at the grate.
  • Poke: Similar in thrusting at a fire; differs because poke names a single jab of a stick and says nothing about adding fuel or managing heat — poking is momentary, stoking is sustained.
  • Stir (up): Similar in agitating; differs because stir suggests mixing or redistributing the contents of a vessel and loses the contact of a pointed instrument with the fuel — one stirs porridge but stokes a fire; as "stir up" it is the mild generic for inciting, weaker than stoke's deliberate feeding.
  • Tend: Similar in looking after something; differs because tend is the general verb for caring for or looking after anything, while stoke is the specific technical verb for tending a fire or furnace — to stoke is to tend in the way fires require.
  • Bank up: Similar in managing a fire; differs because bank up means to heap ash or fuel over a fire to make it burn slowly overnight, a specific conserving action, while stoke is the general feeding-and-stirring of a fire to keep it lively.
  • Replenish: Similar in refilling; differs because replenish is the formal, general word for filling up again, while stoke is concrete and fire-specific, carrying the physical image of the poker and the grate.
  • Encourage / incite / fan (figurative): Similar in the modern sense of intensifying an emotion; differs because encourage is gentle, incite suggests provoking to action, and fan pictures a breeze on a flame — while stoke is the strongest and most deliberate: it pictures someone actively feeding fuel to a fire of public feeling, the standard verb of political journalism for intensifying anger, anxiety, or controversy.
  • Kitchen: Similar in being a food-preparation room; differs because the kitchen is the main cooking room, where the cook works, while the scullery is the smaller back room for washing dishes and pots — the scullery is the wet, rough workroom attached to the kitchen.
  • Pantry: Similar in being a small service room off the kitchen; differs because the pantry (from pain, "bread") stores food — bread, provisions, tableware — while the scullery is for washing up and rough work; one stores in the pantry, one washes in the scullery.
  • Larder: Similar in being a service room; differs because the larder stores meat and perishable food (from lard), a cool storeroom, while the scullery is the washing room, not a storage room.
  • Utility room: Similar in being a workroom for household chores; differs because the utility room is the modern name for a laundry and general-work room in a contemporary house, while scullery is the historical name of the dish-washing back kitchen of the large house.
  • Wash-house / washroom: Similar in being a room for wet work; differs because a wash-house is for laundry, while the scullery is for washing dishes and pots; scullery is also the more specifically historical, servant-world word.
  • Buttery: Similar in being a service room of the old household; differs because the buttery (from butte, "bottle," later "butt") was the store-room for wine, ale, and provisions kept by the butler, while the scullery was the room for washing the dishes and rough kitchen work.
  • Office: Similar in the archaic sense of a room for service work; differs because office in the old household meant any service room or department (the scullery was "the office of the scullion"), a sense now obsolete outside such phrases, while scullery names the dish-washing room specifically.
