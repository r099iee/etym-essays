Greensward means, in its essential modern sense, an expanse of smooth, grass-covered ground: turf or lawn regarded as a continuous green surface, the kind of close-cropped grass one pictures in parks, gardens, village greens, and the manicured landscapes of English literature.

In the source sentence the word marks a place in a landscape: at the south end of the greensward there was an opening, so the greensward here is a defined open lawn or grassy clearing with edges and an entrance, a green room within a larger terrain, and the sentence treats it as a named feature of the ground rather than as mere vegetation.

Core meaning in detail: A greensward is grass plus the soil-bound mat beneath it, apprehended as a surface to walk on, rest on, or look across. The word stresses smoothness, evenness, and verdure: a greensward is not a rough meadow of tussocks and weeds but turf at its most inviting, the green carpet of the earth. Gardeners would call the same thing a lawn; poets reach for greensward precisely because it carries centuries of pastoral music that the plain word lawn cannot supply.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • An expanse of grassy turf (primary modern sense): a lawn, a village green, the grass of a park or churchyard; the default sense in both everyday and literary use, as in the source sentence.
  • Turf as material, the grassy surface layer (technical and poetic): the living mat of grass roots and soil, the skin of the ground itself; this preserves the force of the second element sward, which originally meant skin or rind before it narrowed to mean turf. The semantic bridge is the skin metaphor: soil wears grass the way a body wears skin.
  • A lawn in sport and ceremony (specialised): the close-mown greens of bowls, cricket outfields, and croquet lawns; the bridge is functional, since these games demand exactly the smooth unbroken turf the word evokes, and commentators borrow the poetic term to dignify the playing surface.
  • Pastoral countryside in the abstract (literary): the green world of shepherds, elves, and summer afternoons, as in Shakespeare and the Romantics; the bridge runs from the literal lawn outward to everything the lawn stands for, freshness, peace, and natural plenty.
  • A burial green or churchyard turf (solemn register): the grass over graves, the green mantle of rest; the bridge is metonymic, since churchyards are kept as smooth turf, and elegiac verse from Gray onward has fused green grass with quiet remembrance.
  • Figurative freshness and flourishing (rare, metaphorical): to speak of the greensward of youth or of hope is to borrow the turf as an emblem of vigorous new growth; the bridge is the green-equals-growth symbolism carried by the first element green itself.

Register and connotation: Greensward is bookish and gently archaic in everyday speech, where lawn does the plain work, but it remains fully current in nature writing, fantasy, and landscape description. Its connotation is wholly benign: coolness, softness, openness, and order. Where sward alone can sound stark, the prefixed green- fills the word with colour and life, so that even a single mention lays a carpet of grass before the reader's eye.
