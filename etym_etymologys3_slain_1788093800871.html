A verb of blows. The story of slain begins with a blow, not a death. The Germanic verb at its core — East Gothic slahan, Old Norse slá, Old High German slahan, modern German schlagen — all mean "to strike, hit"; the PIE root reconstructed behind them, *slak-, has the same sense, and its probable extra-Germanic relative, Welsh llach "blow, stroke", confirms that the oldest meaning was physical impact. Only within pre-history did the Germanic verb specialize: to strike, in Germanic heroic culture, was so often to strike to kill that "strike" and "kill" fused in a single word. English inherited both meanings in Old English slēan: glossaries render Latin percutere "to strike" and occidere "to kill" alike with it, and the derived noun slecg, "a sledge, heavy hammer" (the ancestor of sledgehammer), shows the impact-sense fossilised beside the death-sense.

Old English — the strong verb of heroic death. Old English slēan was a class VI strong verb — slēan, slōg, slōgon, (ge)slægen — and it is one of the most frequent verbs of violence in the earliest literature. The elegies, the battle poetry and the prose of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle use it for enemies struck down, saints martyred and beasts killed; its participle, in the form (ge)slægen, already means "killed" as often as "struck". The Beowulf poet's world is full of things done "mid sweorde" — with the sword — and to be slain in that world is to receive a blow that ends speech, the formulaic dignity of the fallen warrior.

Middle English — the ordinary word for killing. In Middle English, slēn (and its participle slayn, slain, slawe) was simply the normal word for killing — chronicles, romances and sermons all slay at every level, from dragons to dinner-fowl. It was during this period that "the slain" hardened into the collective noun for the dead of a battle, a usage that would survive for centuries in dispatches and memorials. The participle's vowel pattern also settled: the old slægen gave way to slain, re-formed on the stem of the infinitive with the -n of the participle, producing the distinctive modern alternation slay – slew – slain.

1611 — scripture takes the word. The King James Bible fixed slay, slew, slain as the English of sacred violence: "Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him" (Genesis 4:8); "a Lamb as it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6, and again 13:8, the Lamb "slain from the foundation of the world"); "thy slain men are not slain with the sword" (Isaiah 22:2). For three centuries every English-speaking reader met the word on almost every page of the Old Testament, so that "slain" became the unmistakable register of doom, sacrifice and battle — at once more solemn than "killed" and more direct than any euphemism.

17th–19th centuries — the newspaper of war. The epic word then went to work. Sermons, epic poetry and, from the 18th century, military dispatches standardized "slain and wounded" for the casualties of a battle; Parliamentary and newspaper usage made "the slain" the dignified collective for the dead of Waterloo, Sebastopol and Gettysburg. The American Civil War and the world wars kept the word current in exactly this collective, memorial sense — "names of the slain", "slain in action" — so that modern English still expects "slain" in solemn journalistic contexts ("the slain officer"), a usage one hundred and sixty years old.

The 20th century — holy collapse and slang rebirth. Two surprising developments followed. In charismatic Christianity from the mid-20th century, believers overcome by the Holy Spirit fall prostrate in worship, said to be "slain in the Spirit" — the violence of the word softened into ecstatic surrender. And in secular slang, "slay" took on a life of its own: early-20th-century American English said "you slay me" for "you overwhelm me with laughter" (the old striking sense turned comic), and from the Black and Latino ballroom and drag cultures of the 1980s–2000s — documented in the film Paris Is Burning (1990) — "slay" emerged as the verb of dazzling performance, spreading worldwide in the 2010s as a pop exclamation ("slay!", "slay queen"). The participle "slain" itself never followed its stem into slang; it remains scriptural, epic and grave.

Today. "Slain" occupies a narrow but strong band: biblical quotation, fantasy and heroic diction (dragons are slain; the Worm of Dread in the source passage is slain), journalism's solemn register for murder victims and fallen officers, and — through Revelation — the sacrificial language of the Lamb. It is the most final of the kill-words, the word of the blow that leaves nothing to add.

  • Key attestations: OE glosses of percutere/occidere; OE slecg "sledge-hammer"; Beowulf-era heroic diction; Genesis 4:8, Revelation 5:6 and 13:8, Isaiah 22:2 (KJV 1611); 19th-c. dispatches "slain and wounded"; "you slay me" (early 20th c.); "slain in the Spirit" (mid-20th c.); ballroom "slay" (1980s–2010s).
  • Phonetic notes: class VI ablaut slēan / slōg / slægen; ME participle re-formed as slain on the infinitive stem; the -ai- of slain preserves the vowel of slay.
  • Register today: literary, scriptural, solemn-journalistic; the stem's slang senses have not contaminated the participle.
