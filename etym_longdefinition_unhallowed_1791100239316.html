Unhallowed means not hallowed — not consecrated, blessed, or made holy — and by extension profane, impure, or wicked. Hallowed ground is a churchyard solemnly dedicated by a bishop; unhallowed ground is everything outside it, where in former times suicides, executed criminals, unbaptized infants, and excommunicates were buried without rites. Unhallowed hands are hands unworthy to touch sacred things, whether from sin, low birth, or mere modesty. In the Dickens source the speaker calls his own hands unhallowed in mock humility before the ancestral simile, as if meddling with proverbial wisdom were sacrilege against the nation.

The primary sense is ecclesiastical and topographical. Canon law distinguished consecrated cemeteries from unhallowed pits; Shakespeare's priests deny Ophelia full rites because her death is doubtful, leaving her in unhallowed limbo. The secondary sense is moral-gothic. Unhallowed rites, hours, and deeds happen at midnight, without God's sanction: witches' sabbaths, murders, and curses are unhallowed. The tertiary sense is rhetorical self-abasement, as here: calling oneself unhallowed to flatter what one refuses to change.

  • Unconsecrated place: ground never dedicated; e.g. they were laid in unhallowed earth beyond the wall.
  • Unblessed object or time: lacking sanction; e.g. unhallowed hours when ghosts walk.
  • Profane person or limb: impure or unworthy; e.g. keep thy unhallowed hands from the altar.
  • Wicked act: godless and cursed; e.g. that unhallowed murder cries to heaven.
  • Mock-modest formula: self-deprecating piety; e.g. my unhallowed hands shall not disturb it.
  • Gothic atmosphere: horror diction for dread; e.g. unhallowed rites in the ruined abbey.

Register is archaic, solemn, and literary. No modern council writes unhallowed on a burial form; it says unconsecrated section. But poets, horror writers, and historical novelists keep unhallowed for its shudder. It collocates with ground, grave, hands, rites, deed, and hour, and with verbs like bury, touch, disturb, and profane. The negative prefix un- is transparent, but the base hallow survives almost only in the Lord's Prayer, Hallowe'en, and this negation, which preserves its aura.

The bridge from topography to morality is consecration theology: what the Church has not blessed is exposed to evil, and what is exposed becomes evil's instrument. Thus soil outside the wall and a murderer's hand share one adjective. Dickens exploits both levels: the speaker pretends the old saying is a relic and himself a sinner unfit to handle relics, while really declaring he will change nothing. To call reform unhallowed is to canonise inertia.

Modern horror and fantasy keep the adjective in service precisely because consecration law is forgotten: readers feel the shudder without knowing the canon. Black-metal bands, video games, and gothic novels scatter unhallowed crypts and unhallowed rituals to signal ancient evil sealed off from grace. Liturgists meanwhile prefer unconsecrated or unblessed in rubrics, leaving unhallowed to poets. The Dickensian speaker stands between these worlds, half believer and half humbug, using a dying legal-moral term to baptise his own refusal to reform as reverence.
