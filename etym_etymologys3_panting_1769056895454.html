The biography of pant is really the biography of a mislaid word — an ancient Greek term for "appearance" that wandered west and ended up describing the breath of a runner. It begins with the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰeh₂-, "to shine, to gleam, to appear," which in Ancient Greek produced phaínō, "to bring to light, to make appear." From phaínō the Greeks coined phantasía, "the making visible; an apparition; an impression on the mind," together with its siblings phántasma, "a ghost, a phantom," and phainómenon, "that which appears." Latin borrowed the whole cluster as phantasia and phantasma, and English later re-imported these learnedly as fantasy (through Old French), fancy (a contraction), phantom, phantasmagoria, phenomenal, emphasis, and diaphanous — the brilliant, airy, seeing-words that are pant's grand cousins. The decisive shift happened in late Latin and Old French, where the notion of "seeing visions" slid toward the physical state that produces them. A person breathless, fevered, or fainting is proverbially "oppressed with visions," and so Vulgar Latin coined *phentasiāre — literally "to be troubled by apparitions" — which Old French wore down into pantaisier, "to be out of breath, to be distressed, to pant," beside the adjective pantois, "gasping." Anglo-Norman carried the verb into Middle English as panten in the fourteenth century, and by the time the modern spelling pant settled, the Greek "appearance" had been completely forgotten: a panting person is no longer seeing visions but simply heaving for air. The luminous beginning of the word — light, showing, appearance — became invisible inside the gasping present participle. A rival account, defended in parts of Romance scholarship, reads the word from the belly rather than the vision. Latin pantex meant "paunch, belly," and some scholars derive the verb from a *pantiāre, "to breathe hard from the belly" — the diaphragmatic heaving of the exhausted. The OED's traditional route through phantasía remains the majority view, but the belly etymology keeps its modern defenders, and the true history may never be settled beyond dispute; what is certain is that by the time panten appears in English records it already means rapid, distressed breathing. In English the word then followed a curve of progressive demystification. The earliest senses are bound up with nightmare: to pant is to gasp under a suffocating vision, and the participial noun still carries a faint supernatural chill in Middle English romances. By the 16th and 17th centuries the supernatural colour had faded and the word narrowed to the ordinary breathing of exertion — the short, rapid, audible breaths of running, heat, and hard work. It was then that the figurative construction took root: "to pant for" or "pant after" something means to yearn or long for it, and the biblical register made it grand — "my soul panteth after thee" — so that the word carries both a plain physical sense and an elevated devotional one. The present participle panting became the workhorse form: the panting dog, the panting runner, the panting messenger. Modern English is entirely physiological and plain. The participle marks arrival, effort, and the honest, unheroic cost of the body's doings — the runner at the finish, the child mid-shock in an enchanted wood. Alongside it the word has colonised specialist corners: in zoology the pant-hoot names the loud, rhythmical call of chimpanzees; in engineering a panting action is the rhythmic flexing of a ship's hull under heavy seas; in medicine panting is a recognised sign of certain respiratory and anxiety states; and in dogs panting is a vital cooling mechanism, the animal's substitute for sweating. Every one of these senses still carries the same core — rapid, shallow, audible breath — and every one of them is a descendant of a Greek word for light.
