Twittered is the simple past tense and past participle of the verb to twitter, and its primary, literal sense is that of a bird uttering a rapid, light, tremulous succession of thin chirping notes. In the sentence from Oscar Wilde's “The Happy Prince” (1888) — “It is a ridiculous attachment,” twittered the other Swallows — the word does exactly this work: the Swallows do not sing, they do not cry out; they produce a volley of quick, chattering, faintly derisive notes that sound, to the human ear, like dismissive gossip. The past-tense form is a perfectly regular weak formation (twitter + -ed), pronounced /ˈtwɪtəd/, and it is the form one meets constantly in nineteenth-century nature writing, in Edwardian children's books, and in the mock-heroic prose of the fairy tale.

The verb itself is first recorded in Middle English as twitteren in the late fourteenth century (OED), meaning precisely “to chirp, to utter a succession of small sharp notes”, and it is what linguists call an onomatopoeic, or echoic, formation: English re-creates the birds' sound with a reduplicated twit-twit base, to which the frequentative suffix -er (as in chatter, flicker, flutter, stutter) is attached to express repetition. Comparable echo-verbs exist across the Germanic languages — German zwitschern, Danish kvidre, Swedish kvittra — and this cross-linguistic similarity strongly suggests that each language formed its own word from the same acoustic model rather than inheriting a single ancestral root. It is therefore a word with no reconstructible Indo-European ancestry: its “root” is a sound, not a lexeme.

  • Sense 1 — the vocalisation of birds (primary sense): to utter a quick succession of light, thin, tremulous notes; typified by the swallow, the martin and the finch, and distinguished from sustained song (warbling, trilling). This is the core sense of twittered in the Wilde passage, where the Swallows' sound is rendered as sociable, noisy mockery.
  • Sense 2 — rapid human chatter: from the later seventeenth century the verb extends from birds to people: to talk quickly, excitedly and at length about trivial matters. The semantic bridge is acoustic — rapid light syllables of human speech mimic the birds' staccato note-streams — and in older prose the subject is characteristically a woman or a gossiping group, with an amused, slightly patronizing undertone.
  • Sense 3 — tremulous agitation: through the noun twitter (“a twitter” = a state of nervous excitement; “all in a twitter”), the verb acquires the notion of quivering with excitement or fluster, as if the person vibrated like a bird's wing. This sense, current from the eighteenth century, transfers the sound image to a bodily one: agitation as a flutter.
  • Sense 4 — the social-media platform (2006–2023 and after): when the microblogging service Twitter was launched in 2006, co-founder Jack Dorsey described the post as “a short burst of inconsequential information” — a “chirp” — deliberately recruiting the word's associations of brevity, lightness and rapid-fire succession. The verb to twitter came to mean “to post short messages”, and twittered appeared in past-tense narration about the platform (“she twittered her thoughts to forty thousand followers”). After the platform's rebranding to X in 2023 the platform sense receded into historical usage, but it dominated global media language for nearly two decades.
  • Derivative landscape: the family includes tweet (a single message, formed on the bird's single note), tweeter, twittering, and journalistic compounds such as twittersphere and twitterstorm; the playful coinage Twitterature (c. 2007) denotes literature composed in tweet form.

Grammatical and phonetic note: twittered is a regular dental-past form. The base twitter ends in an unstressed -er syllable, and the past-tense suffix surfaces as the voiced apical stop /d/, giving the two-syllable /ˈtwɪtəd/ with first-syllable stress; the double t in the spelling reflects the reduplicative echoic base. Spelling has been stable since the Early Modern period.

Register and flavour: in modern English the bird sense remains the dictionary-default meaning; the human-chatter sense is now slightly old-fashioned or literary; the platform sense is historical after 2023; and the agitation sense survives chiefly in the fixed phrase “in a twitter”. The word is a rare specimen of a sound-imitating verb that travelled from hedgerow to boardroom — from the swallow's nest to the heart of global communication technology — without ever changing its spelling or its staccato rhythm.
