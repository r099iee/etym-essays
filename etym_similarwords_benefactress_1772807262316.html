• Benefactor: Similar because it is the exact counterpart meaning “one who gives benefit”; differs because benefactor is the unmarked, gender-neutral form that is today’s expected word, while benefactress specifies a woman and carries a slightly dated, ceremonial, literary tone.
  • Patron / patroness: Similar because both name a supporter of a person, cause, or institution; differs because patron emphasizes continuing sponsorship and cultural or institutional standing (patron of the arts, of a charity), while benefactress stresses the conferral of benefit—often a single decisive kindness—rather than ongoing support.
  • Donor: Similar because both involve giving money or goods; differs because donor is bureaucratic, legal, and gift-focused (of blood, organs, funds), lacking the personal moral debt and the benevolent character implied by benefactress.
  • Philanthropist: Similar because both confer good, often with money; differs because philanthropist implies public-scale, systematic, usually large giving aimed at society at large, whereas benefactress can be a private, personal subsidy to a single individual.
  • Sponsor: Similar because both support a person or activity; differs because sponsor is commercial or institutional (of events, athletes, visas) and implies a formal arrangement, not the charitable or moral relation of benefactress.
  • Supporter: Similar because both indicate help; differs because supporter is general and may be moral or emotional without money, whereas benefactress implies material, effective conferral of good.
  • Saviour / rescuer: Similar because both denote someone who delivers another from harm; differs because saviour and rescuer stress deliverance from danger rather than moneyed or institutional kindness, and carry religious or heroic overtones alien to benefactress.
  • Patroness: Similar as the -ess feminine of patron; differs because patroness emphasizes an ongoing patronage relationship (institutional, continuing), while benefactress emphasizes the good done, which may be a single remembered act.
  • Well-wisher: Similar because both wish or do good to another; differs because a well-wisher need give nothing and bear no obligation, while a benefactress’s good is concrete and creates a debt of gratitude.
  • Maecenas (literary trope): Similar as a byword for a wealthy patron of the arts; differs because Maecenas is a proper-name trope, masculine, classical, and denotes connoisseur patronage, not feminine benefaction.
