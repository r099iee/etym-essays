- Ancient Rome — strewed flat: Latin prosternere means to throw down flat, to overthrow — soldiers, altars, and enemies levelled with the ground.
- 14th–15th c. — the worshipper’s word: English borrows prostrate for the penitent laid face-down before God; monastery floors know the posture daily.
- 16th c. — courts and conquests: ambassadors prostrate themselves before eastern thrones in travellers’ tales; defeated armies lie prostrate on lost fields.
- 17th c. — the sickroom: physicians adopt the word for bodies felled by fever and grief — prostrate with ague, prostrate with melancholy; collapse becomes clinical.
- 18th c. — botany borrows it: Linnaean classifiers name trailing stems prostrate, the worshipper’s posture lent peacefully to thyme and cranberry.
- 19th c. — empires and novels: prostrate nations fill Victorian histories; heroines are prostrate with emotion on fainting couches across three-volume novels.
- Today — awe, illness, and gardens: pilgrims still prostrate themselves at shrines, patients are prostrate with flu, and gardeners plant prostrate junipers — one posture, three worlds, from the altar to the sickbed to the rockery.
