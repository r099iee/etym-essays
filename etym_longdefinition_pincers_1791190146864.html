pincers most commonly denotes a hand tool consisting of two pivoted limbs that close on a workpiece to grip, squeeze, pull, or cut it, always used as a plurale tantum — a pair of pincers — because the action requires two opposing jaws working together. In its primary workshop sense it is a cold-forged steel instrument with handles at one end and short, bevelled jaws at the other, used especially by carpenters, blacksmiths, farriers, surgeons, jewellers, and electricians for extracting nails, holding hot metal, bending wire, or gripping small objects that fingers cannot safely hold.

In zoology, pincers denotes the paired grasping claws of lobsters, crabs, scorpions, earwigs, stag beetles, and other arthropods, technically called chelae or chelipeds, used for defence, feeding, mating display, and burrowing. By extension, military and figurative language uses pincers for any double-flanking action that squeezes from two sides, as in a pincer movement, pincer attack, or economic pincer of rising costs and falling demand.

  • Tool sense — carpenter's / blacksmith's pincers: two hinged arms with pincing jaws; used to pull nails, cut wire, hold hot iron, twist and grip; distinguished from pliers which have flat serrated jaws for turning, and from tongs which are longer and for holding rather than pulling; end-nippers and side-cutters are specialised subtypes.
  • Surgical / precision sense — forceps and tweezers: small pincers for sterile gripping of tissue, bone fragments, dressings, or watch parts; differs in fineness, sterilisation, and lack of cutting force; often called forceps in medicine to mark register.
  • Zoological sense — chelae: the enlarged first pair of legs in decapod crustaceans ending in a movable finger against a fixed finger; crusher and cutter claws in lobsters are asymmetric; scorpion pedipalps and earwig cerci are also called pincers; the semantic bridge is visual and functional resemblance to the tool.
  • Military figurative sense — pincer movement: simultaneous attack on both flanks to encircle an enemy, also called double envelopment; e.g. Cannae, Stalingrad; the bridge is the image of two jaws closing; differs because no physical tool is present, only manoeuvre geometry.
  • Figurative pressure sense: to be caught in a pincer between two opposing forces, such as inflation and unemployment, duty and desire; evokes squeezing pain and loss of escape; differs in abstractness but retains bilateral pressure.
  • Botanical / everyday object sense: pincer-like structures in plants such as the traps of Venus flytrap, or sugar pincers, ice pincers, and eyebrow pincers for serving or grooming; small spring-loaded variants.
  • Grammatical note — plurale tantum: like scissors, tongs, tweezers, and shears, pincers is almost always plural even for one instrument; singular pincer usually means one jaw, one claw, or attributive use as in pincer attack; plural verb agreement is normal: the pincers are sharp.
  • Domain-specific nuances — dentistry, farriery, glassblowing, electronics: dental pincers for extracting teeth; farrier's pincers for trimming hoof wall; glass pincers for shaping molten glass; electronics end-nippers for leads; each retains grip-and-leverage core with material-specific jaw geometry.
  • Literary and historical nuance: torture pincers, red-hot pincers in martyrdom accounts, witchcraft lore where the Devil's pincers nip; connotes cruel precision; modern horror retains this gothic charge.

Polysemy is unified by the image-schema of TWO PARTS CLOSING TO SEIZE. From concrete gripping, English extended it by metaphor to animal anatomy through resemblance, then to tactics through function, then to abstract pressure through feeling. No sense means a single blade, a container, or a piercing point alone; all require opposed closure. In the source context — making tongs, pincers and vices from branches — the word sits in a primitive-technology list where wood is shaped into gripping instruments, emphasising human ingenuity in extending the hand's pinch with leverage.
