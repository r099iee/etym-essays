The Latin root and the gathering sense. The verb coil descends from Latin colligere "to gather together, collect", a compound of the prefix com- "together" and legere "to pick, gather" — the same legere that yields collect, select, lecture, and legion. This Latin verb was inherited into Old French as coillir (later cueillir), meaning "to gather, pick, harvest, collect", senses that survive in modern French cueillir "to pick (fruit, flowers)".

The nautical bridge (16th century). English borrowed the word in the 1560s, but in a surprisingly specific environment: the ship's deck. When a sailor gathers a rope up into a neat circular heap so it will run out freely, the rope is said to be 'coiled' — the sense of 'gathering together' was transferred from fruit and goods to the folding of line into concentric rings. The noun is recorded first (1560s) meaning "a coiled mass of rope, a twisted thing", and the verb followed about two decades later (1580s) meaning "to wind or twist into rings". From that moment the word's centre of gravity shifted permanently from "gathering" to "spirality".

A false friend: Shakespeare's 'mortal coil'. Hamlet's famous "this mortal coil" (1602) means the bustle, turmoil, and noisy disturbance of earthly life — but that coil is almost certainly a different word, an obscure English noun meaning "noise, commotion, fuss" of unknown (possibly French or onomatopoeic) origin. Etymologists explicitly warn against merging the two: the turmoiling coil is dead outside the Shakespearean phrase, while the rope-coil line is the one that lived. The phrase nevertheless shows how slippery early-modern vocabulary could be.

Expansion through the 17th–19th centuries. Once the spiral sense was fixed, coil expanded quickly: serpents coil, hair coils, springs coil, smoke coils. Natural-history and travel writing made "coiling" a stock verb for snakes, and the participle form found a permanent home in adventure prose. The 19th century added precise technical senses — the electromagnetic induction coil (Faraday, 1830s), the coil spring, the coiled wire of telegraphy and telephony — so that by 1900 the word belonged simultaneously to the deck, the drawing room, the serpent pit, and the physics laboratory.

Modern figurative and scientific uses. The 20th century drew the spiral into metaphor: coiled energy, coiling dread, a coiled spring of tension — the spiral as stored power about to release, precisely the image Tolkien uses when Smaug coils his length before his thunderous rush. Modern science made the word structural: the coiled-coil protein motif, the double helix described as a pair of coiling strands, solenoids, and spiral galaxies are all described with the vocabulary of coiling. In everyday modern English coiling is fully neutral — hose, cable, smoke, hair, snakes, springs — and the participle remains the default for any winding in progress.

The inflected form. Coiling is a straightforward present participle (verb + -ing), attested from the 1580s onward. Its continuous aspect is essential to its literary power: where coiled states a finished spiral, coiling keeps the motion alive, so narrators use it to build slow, tightening, serpentine suspense before a sudden release — the exact rhythm of the dragon's emergence in The Hobbit.
