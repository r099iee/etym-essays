1. The Norse loan and its mystery. The verb kindle, the base of rekindle, entered Middle English around 1200 from Old Norse kynda “to set on fire, to kindle” — a Viking-Age loanword that displaced native expressions for lighting a fire. The origin of Norse kynda itself is debated: the Oxford English Dictionary marks kindle as “of uncertain origin”, and the most plausible connection is to the Old Norse noun kyndill “candle, torch”, which is itself a loan from Latin candēla “candle”, from candēre “to shine, be white-hot, glow”, from the Indo-European root *kand- “to glow, shine” (seen also in candidate, literally “white-robed”, and incandescent). If that connection is right, the humble English verb “to kindle” carries a hidden chain of descent: a PIE root meaning “to glow” passed into Latin, travelled north as a candle-word, and was turned into a fire-verb in Old Norse before crossing the North Sea to England. The chain is probable rather than certain, and honesty requires flagging the uncertainty — but no better etymology exists.

2. Middle English and the ember culture. In medieval England, where fire was daily labour — struck from flint, fed with tinder, kept alive through the night in the hearth — “kindle” was a workaday word, and its use in homiletic and devotional prose is concrete. The word's emotional colouring begins with the Bible and the sermon: “kindle” is used for the wrath of God (the “kindled anger” of the Psalms), because flame is the natural image of anger in the Judeo-Christian tradition. This biblical strand is the bridge that later made “rekindled fury” possible.

3. The coinage of rekindle (1590s). English began forming re- compounds freely in the 16th century, and rekindle is first recorded in the 1590s. The prefix re- is Latin (from PIE *wre-, an extended form of *wer- “to turn”: “again, back”), and by the 1590s it was fully productive in English. The early uses are literal — relighting fires and lamps — but the figurative extension follows almost immediately, because the Reformation and the wars of religion gave the age an intense vocabulary of inflamed passions: sermons speak of “rekindled” heresies, seditions and zeals.

4. The 17th–18th centuries: political and moral heat. In the 17th century rekindle becomes a standard verb of political and religious polemic: wars are rekindled, feuds rekindled, “the flames of rebellion” rekindled. The metaphor system is entirely consistent: anger, war and heresy are fires that can be quenched or rekindled. By the 18th century the word is equally at home in poetry (the rekindled flame of love) and in the debates of the Enlightenment.

5. The 19th century: Romantic tenderness. The Romantic movement fixed the word's most enduring sense — the revival of feeling. “Rekindled love”, “rekindled hope”, “rekindled friendship” fill the novels and verse of the period, and the word acquires its modern softness: it no longer primarily means “to light a fire” but “to bring warmth back to something that had gone cold”. The ember metaphor is the key: embers hold heat invisibly, so a rekindled feeling is one that was never quite dead.

6. The 20th–21st centuries: journalism and self-help. In the 20th century rekindle becomes a headline verb — disputes rekindled, rivalries rekindled, peace processes rekindled — and in the 21st it migrates into the vast literature of relationships and self-improvement: “how to rekindle the spark”, “rekindled passion for work”. The literal sense has almost vanished from ordinary speech, surviving in poetry and in accounts of hearth and campfire. The word's whole career is thus a textbook semantic drift from concrete to abstract, from the hearth to the heart, with the fire image fading to a metaphor that most users no longer consciously see.

7. The word in this card's context. Tolkien's sentence — “the hatred of their race for the dwarves had been rekindled to fury” — uses the word at its full historical depth: an ancient enmity (the goblins' hatred of the dwarves) treated as a fire that never truly went out, flaring again into full fury after the killing of the Great Goblin. Every layer of the word's 800-year history — Norse fire-lighting, biblical wrath, political polemic, Romantic feeling — is compressed into the single participle.
