• carcass: Similar because it is the very same word; differs only orthographically — carcass (French-shaped -ss) is the global default spelling, while carcase is the traditional British variant, still standard in the meat trade.
  • corpse: Similar because it denotes a dead body; differs because corpse is specifically human and relatively neutral or clinical, whereas carcase/carcass is typically animal or contemptuous of humans.
  • cadaver: Similar because it means a dead body; differs because it is the Latinate medical/forensic term used of human remains in mortuary and dissection contexts — never of animals or of rotting flesh generally.
  • carrion: Similar because both concern dead, decaying animal flesh; differs because carrion names the rotting flesh itself as substance or food for scavengers, not the whole body.
  • remains: Similar because it denotes a dead body or its fragments; differs because it is a polite, euphemistic term of neutral register covering bones, ashes, and fragments, with none of carcase's grossness.
  • body: Similar because it can denote a dead body; differs because it is fully neutral and universal — no decay, contempt, or animal implication, and no technical restriction.
  • hulk: Similar because it denotes a stripped-down frame, especially of a ship; differs because it is purely structural and nautical — no flesh, decay, or organic charge at all.
  • skeleton: Similar because it is the bony remnant or structural framework; differs because it is only bone, neutral and technical, and lacks any sense of flesh or putrefaction.
  • shell: Similar because it is the empty framework left behind; differs because it is fully abstracted and structural, usable of arguments and eggs with no corpse connotation.
  • offal: Similar because it denotes animal body parts after slaughter; differs because it refers specifically to the discarded inner organs and entrails, not to the whole dressed body.
  • stiff: Similar because it is slang for a dead body; differs because it is jocular underworld slang for a human corpse, never applied to animals, and far more informal than carcase.
  • mummy: Similar because it is a preserved dead body; differs because it is embalmed and preserved, the exact opposite of a decaying carcase, and historically Egyptian in association.
