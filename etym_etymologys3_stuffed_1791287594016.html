Stuffed is the past participle of to stuff, and its story begins not in a kitchen but in a Roman shipyard with coarse hemp fibre. Latin stuppa meant tow — the broken, tangled fibres of flax or hemp left after combing — used for caulking ships, stopping cracks, and packing. From this humble noun Late Latin derives the verb stuppare, to stop up with tow, to plug. The semantic kernel is already full: to push soft material into a cavity until it is tight.

Through Gallo-Romance this becomes Old French estofe, estoffe meaning material, cloth, provisions — anything one equips oneself with — and the verb estofer, estoffer meaning to fill up, to furnish, to equip, to cram. A knight is estoffé when well equipped; a cushion is estoffée when well filled. When Anglo-Norman carries the word to England after 1066, Middle English stoffen (c. 1330) means to fill with stuff, to cram, to provision a fortress or a belly. The noun stuff in English retains the wonderfully vague sense of matter in general, while the verb keeps the forceful sense of pushing in.

Sound change is modest because the word is borrowed late: Latin st- survives intact in French est- (with prosthetic e before sp-, st-) and is then simplified back to st- in English when the prosthetic vowel drops: stuppa > estope > stuff. The double ff marks the short vowel /ʌ/ in stuffed /stʌft/, and the -ed ending continues Old English -od / -ed, devoiced to /t/ after /f/ by regular assimilation, hence stuffed pronounced with /t/.

Semantic expansion in English is exuberant:
  • Culinary filling: to stuff a turkey, stuffed peppers, stuffed crust. Direct continuation of to fill a cavity with tasty material, now the dominant domestic sense.
  • Cramming and overfilling: a stuffed suitcase, stuffed full, stuffed with money (rich), to stuff ones face (eat greedily), to get stuffed (rude dismissal, as if one were waste to be crammed away). Force and excess become the point.
  • Taxidermy and toys: a stuffed moose, stuffed animals, stuffed head on the wall. Here stuffing preserves the outward shape while replacing the living inside with tow, straw, or polyester — an exact return to Latin stuppa as packing material. Wodehouses stuffed moose belongs here, evoking both the hunting-lodge trophy and the nursery toy, glassy-eyed and solemn.
  • Defeat and contempt: to stuff the opposition (beat hollow, originally in rugby and elections), to stuff up (botch, Australian), stuffed shirt (pompous person, stiff as if padded). Stuffing becomes stuffiness: full of oneself, inflexible, lifeless.
Each branch keeps the idea of an interior forcibly filled until the exterior is taut, whether with sage-and-onion, money, straw, or self-importance.

Culturally, stuffed tracks abundance and preservation. Medieval stuffing fed armies; Victorian stuffing displayed empire in natural-history museums and trophy rooms; modern stuffing comforts children with teddies and mocks pomposity with stuffed shirt. Wodehouse exploits both museum and nursery associations: a man preserving the quiet stolidity of a stuffed moose is at once as grand as a baronial hall trophy and as absurd as a toy that cannot blink. The participle stuffed thus carries a quiet insult — lifeless, mounted, incapable of response — even while sounding affectionately comic.
