Every history of detest begins with counting. The speakers of Proto-Indo-European had a single ancestral numeral for “three,” reconstructed as *tréyes, with a zero-grade *tri- reserved for compounds — a shape preserved almost untouched in Sanskrit tráyas, Greek treîs, Latin trēs, and Old English þrīe. Numbers mattered in Roman law for a very practical reason: a contract, a sale, or a will was only as solid as the men who stood by to confirm it, and the ideal confirming party was a disinterested bystander who had nothing to gain. Out of this habit Latin forged the word for “witness”: testis, analysed by modern scholars as *tris-sto-, “one who stands as the third” — the third person present at a transaction between two principals, the outsider who could later be summoned to swear to what he had seen and heard. The act of bearing witness, testārī, became the solemn engine of Roman courts and Roman religion alike, and from it poured the whole family that still lives in English: testimony, testator, testament, attest, contest, and protest.

The decisive turn comes with the prefix. Latin dē- marks motion “down from, away from, against,” so dētestārī means literally “to call witnesses against” — to denounce a person or a thing formally, calling upon the gods or the law as witnesses to one's execration. In classical Latin this was at once a legal and a ritual act: a citizen could dētestārī someone by formally renouncing them in a public declaration, and Roman authors use the word for “to curse” and “to abominate.” Christian Latin inherited the word with its curse still attached, and in the Vulgate and the Church Fathers dētestārī came to mean the spontaneous recoil of the righteous from sin. When the word crossed into Old and Middle French as détester in the twelfth century it already carried both layers — the formal denunciation and the personal abhorrence — and English borrowed it around 1530, in the early Tudor period, as a learned verb.

The semantic journey of the last five hundred years is a quiet one. In English, detest dropped almost all of its courtroom furniture: no one who says “I detest hypocrisy” is literally summoning witnesses, and the religious curse has faded to a memory. What survived is the core judgemental structure — the idea that the dislike is deliberate, reasoned, and pronounced against someone — and it is precisely that latent formality that Jane Austen exploits when Elizabeth “began to detest the very sight of him.” The participle form the sentence uses, and the emphatic collocation “the very sight,” make the aversion feel like a settled ruling rather than a passing mood. Meanwhile the sister words kept the older public senses: protest still means to declare publicly (and gave us Protestant, the confessors who protested), attest still means to certify formally, contest still means to dispute as if in a lawsuit, and testament still names both a will and a sacred covenant. The word test, by contrast, belongs to a different Latin pot (testum, an assayer's vessel) and must not be confused with the witness family.

Modern usage completes the picture. Everyday English now prefers hate, and detest has retreated into the emphatic, the formal, and the literary — which is exactly where Austen's sentence lives. The collocations detestable cruelty, held in detestation, and the theatrical I detest the very sight of keep the word's ancient witness-box dignity alive. To say that a person detests something is still, however faintly, to say that they have judged it and found it wanting — a judgement with a two-thousand-year-old legal pedigree behind it.
