DEPLORE (verb, transitive) carries two principal modern senses that must be carefully distinguished. In its dominant contemporary use it means to feel and express strong disapproval of something — to condemn an act, situation, or development as wrong, regrettable, or shameful. In its older, still-recognisable literary sense it means to lament or mourn deeply over a loss, misfortune, or sorrowful event, to bewail something with grief. Both senses share the same underlying emotional gesture: the speaker's distress poured out toward an object that is felt to be a wrong or a sorrow. The word is thus a verb of public feeling — it is rarely a merely private, internal reaction; to deplore something is ordinarily to say so, in speech or writing, and the utterance carries the weight of judgment as much as of feeling.

In the source context — the biographer's sentence, "however much we may deplore the exaggeration of Mill's references to his wife, we recognise that, for whatever reason, the pair lived an ideally happy life" — the word is used in its blended modern sense, where grief and censure are combined. Deploring the exaggeration is both regretting it as an unworthy lapse and mildly censuring it as a fault; the concessive frame ("however much we may deplore") admits the disapproval and then sets it aside so that the sentence can proceed to the more important recognition of happiness. The tone is formal, critical, and charitable at once: the voice of a biographer who wishes to register a fault without allowing it to diminish the love it records. It is worth noting how precise the collocation is — one deplores an exaggeration, an excess, a lapse, not a fact one merely dislikes; the object of deploring is always something judged to be blameworthy or sad.

Full polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Strong disapproval / censure (modern dominant sense): to regard and describe something as wrong, regrettable, or unacceptable; e.g. "governments deplore acts of violence," "he deplored the decline of standards." This is the sense of editorials, official statements, diplomatic resolutions, and formal criticism.
  • Lamentation / mourning (older literary sense): to mourn, bewail, or express deep grief over a loss or misfortune; e.g. "she deplored the death of her friend." Retained in formal, poetic, and historical registers; the primary sense before c. 1800.
  • Blended grief-and-censure (transitional sense): to regret deeply while expressing strong disapproval, the sense the source sentence uses; sorrow and condemnation fused in a single measured act of judgment.
  • Rare transitive sense (18th c. usage): to weep over something, literally to shed tears for it; chiefly archaic and attested in sentimental literature.

Derived forms: the adjective deplorable ("deserving strong censure; lamentably bad," from Latin dēplōrābilis), the adverb deplorably, the noun deplorer ("one who deplores"), and the rare noun deploration ("the act of bewailing"). Note the internal path of the adjective: "worthy of tears" first, then "pitiable," then "shamefully bad" — the same drift from weeping to censure that governs the verb.

Register and collocations: the word is formal and written, typical of journalism, political and diplomatic language, sermons, and literary criticism; it is uncommon in relaxed speech, where "hate," "disapprove of," or "can't stand" usually replace it. Fixed collocations include "deeply deplore," "much deplored," "deplore the fact that," "deplore violence," "deplore the loss," "deplore the state of affairs." Because it fuses feeling and judgment, it is often chosen precisely where a writer wants both emotion and authority: to deplore is to be saddened and to sit in judgment at once.
