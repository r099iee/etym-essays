• wagon: Similar because it is the identical lexical item; differs because wagon with a single g is the standard modern spelling in North American English and widespread in contemporary British English, whereas waggon is the traditional British and historical spelling favored in 18th- and 19th-century literature and military history.
  • cart: Similar because both are wheeled vehicles for transporting goods and agricultural produce; differs because a cart typically has two wheels and is smaller, lighter, and more maneuverable, whereas a waggon possesses four wheels and is engineered for heavy, bulky long-distance freight.
  • dray: Similar because both haul heavy industrial freight; differs because a dray is specifically a low, heavy, sideless wagon designed for transporting heavy kegs, barrels, or brewery casks, often lacking high side-rails.
  • wain: Similar because it is the native Anglo-Saxon etymological cognate for a heavy harvest vehicle; differs because wain is an archaic, poetic term surviving largely in romantic verse and celestial astronomy (Charles's Wain), whereas waggon was borrowed in the 16th century for practical commercial freight.
  • lorry: Similar because both carry freight across overland routes; differs because a lorry in British English is a modern motorized commercial truck powered by an internal combustion engine, whereas waggon primarily denotes horse-drawn or railway freight stock.
  • carriage: Similar because both are four-wheeled horse-drawn conveyances; differs because a carriage is designed exclusively for transporting human passengers in comfort and style, featuring soft springing, enclosed cabins, and elegant upholstery, whereas a waggon is strictly utilitarian and designed for cargo.
  • van: Similar because both transport merchandise and goods; differs because a van is an enclosed box vehicle (whether horse-drawn or motorized) designed to protect valuable goods from weather and theft, whereas a traditional waggon often features open slat sides or canvas hoop tilts.
  • tumbril: Similar because both are rustic freight conveyances; differs because a tumbril is a two-wheeled farm dung-cart whose body tilts backwards to dump its load, famous during the French Revolution for carrying condemned aristocrats to the guillotine.
  • truck: Similar because both denote freight vehicles; differs because truck in modern English refers almost exclusively to large motorized freight haulers, or in railway mechanics to the wheel-bearing bogie assembly beneath a railcar.
  • barrow: Similar because both move freight; differs because a barrow (such as a wheelbarrow or handbarrow) is a small one- or two-wheeled conveyance pushed or pulled by human muscle rather than draft animals.
