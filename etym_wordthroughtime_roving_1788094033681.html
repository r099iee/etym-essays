- (late 1400s) - A "rover" is a pirate: the word is borrowed from Middle Dutch rover "robber" — the sea-robber's roaming is his defining habit.
- (1530s) - English back-forms the verb rove "to wander" from rover; roving first means simply "wandering, roaming about" — of people, armies, and animals.
- (1500s–1600s) - Archery gives roving a second flavour: "roving" shots are aimed at random marks; shooting at random and wandering at random blur together in the word's feel.
- (1600s–1700s) - Military and naval use: "a roving commission" lets an officer move and act at his own discretion; roving patrols range freely.
- (1800s) - Romantic colour: roving becomes the poetic word for free, wandering life; newspapers invent "the roving reporter," a journalist who travels for stories.
- (1900s–) - The idiom "a roving eye" crystallises: a gaze that wanders becomes a moralised joke about flirtation and unfaithfulness — the dominant everyday use today.
- (1937) - Tolkien's "Smaug's roving eye" in The Hobbit fuses all the layers: the free-ranging, predatory, searching gaze of the dragon.
- (today) - Literal wandering (of eyes, travellers, cameras) and the flirtation idiom live side by side; roving still sounds freer and keener than plain "wandering".
