- 1550s–1650s: Borrowed from Latin as an elevated moral and philosophical term for natural disposition or physical hanging-forward - Used in humanist treatises.
- 1650s–1800s: Standard literary and theological term for innate human passions and fateful personal drives (as in Defoe and Hume) - Often carried moral gravity.
- 1930s–Present: Technical adoption in economics (Keynesian marginal propensity to consume) and modern law (propensity evidence) - Retained high academic and formal prestige.
- Contemporary: Widely used across cognitive science, everyday intellectual prose, and analytical writing to describe behavioral tendencies.
