Scandinavian inheritance. The verb cower is one of the many words English owes to the Viking settlements of the Danelaw. Its attested ancestor is the Old Norse verb kúra, meaning "to lie quiet, doze, sit still" — the passive, huddled posture of a resting or waiting creature. The crucial and surprising fact is that the Old Norse word carried no fear: a kúra-ing animal was simply lying low and still, perhaps drowsing, and the same root appears in Scandinavian words for sitting hunched in thought or resting quietly.

Entering English. English took the word over in the 13th century (Middle English curen, couren, attested around 1300), probably directly from Old Norse in the north and east of England, and possibly reinforced by the related Middle Low German kūren "to lie in wait, lurk" brought by continental traders. The first English senses are entirely practical and neutral: "to crouch, stoop", and above all "to lie in wait" — the poised crouch of a hunter waiting in cover for game. In this early use there is nothing fearful about the posture; it is a skilled, deliberate position, the stance of an ambush.

The shift to fear. The great semantic event in the word's history took place in the 16th century. English writers began to use "cower" of crouching in dread — the shrinking of a beaten man, a terrified animal, or a servant before his master — and this fearful sense rapidly became the dominant one. The transition is easy to understand: the same low, drawn-in posture that a hunter uses to hide from prey is used by the prey to hide from the hunter, and once English speakers attached terror to the crouch, the word changed colour for good. By the end of the 17th century the old "lie in wait" sense was effectively dead, and "cower" had become a word about fear.

Literary career. From the 18th century onward "cower" became a favourite of novelists and poets whenever they needed to show panic or oppression: frightened dogs cowering under furniture, beaten servants cowering in doorways, conquered peoples cowering before tyrants. It is the stock word for fear made visible in the body, and its strong visual quality — the word draws a picture — kept it in constant literary service. It also developed the figurative use in which whole groups or institutions "cower" before power, a use that journalism still exploits today.

Related and confusable words. Two neighbours deserve note. The noun coward, "a timid person", looks temptingly like a cousin but is a separate word: it descends from Old French couard and ultimately from Latin cauda "tail" — the idea of a frightened animal turning its tail. The verb cow ("to cow someone into silence", "to be cowed"), meaning "to intimidate", is also distinct, coming from Old Norse kuga "to subdue, oppress". The three words — cower, coward, cow — are the Norse and French branches of the same mental territory: fear, submission, and the low posture that goes with them.

Phonology and form. The word has been phonologically stable since its borrowing: Middle English curen/couren became early modern cower, pronounced with the standard "cow" diphthong, and it has not changed since. The inflected form cowered, the past tense and participle, is regular, and the word has never developed irregular forms. Its stability is a sign of how ordinary and fully nativized it became — within two centuries of the Norman Conquest it was an everyday English verb, and it has never left ordinary speech.

The modern word. Today "cower" and its inflected forms still mean crouching fear and submission, exactly as they have since the 16th century. It is used of dogs and children, armies and nations, in fact and metaphor, and it remains one of English's most precise verbs for panic made physical — which is why Tolkien could use it to undo the dwarves' boasting in a single image: confident claims one moment, cowered down in fright the next.
