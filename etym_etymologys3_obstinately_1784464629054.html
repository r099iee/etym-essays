Obstinately is the manner adverb of the adjective obstinate, and its biography is the biography of a 'standing firm' image that Indo-European inherited, Latin organized, and English borrowed to name the stubbornness of mind and will. The word never loses the physical picture planted at its root — a person standing fixed against pressure — even as the picture moves from the body to the character. 1. The Indo-European root: *steh₂- and the universal act of standing The chain opens with one of the most securely reconstructed roots in all of Indo-European: *steh₂-, 'to stand, set, be firm.' Its attestation is almost embarrassing in its richness: Sanskrit tiṣṭhati 'stands,' Avestan, Greek histēmi 'I set up, stand,' and Latin stāre 'to stand' all continue it, and English itself keeps it in stand, stay, stead, and state. The root's ancientness and spread mean that the very idea of standing — and of firmness — was a primary resource of the ancestral language, the basis for a huge family of 'set, fixed, standing' words. It is no accident that words for persistence, position, and stability across the family all draw on this one root. 2. Latin: from standing to standing against Latin organizes the root into stāre 'to stand' and a swarm of prefixed derivatives. The relevant branch adds the prefix ob-, 'against, in front, in the way,' producing obstāre 'to stand before or against, to oppose, to obstruct' — a word of physical and military blockage, of standing in someone's way. From this 'stand against' material Latin forms obstināre, 'to persist, to set oneself resolutely,' and its participle obstinātus, 'resolved, stubborn.' The precise morphology is debated in the handbooks: the classical account sees ob- plus a 'stand' verb *stināre (with the nasal infix seen also in dēstināre, source of English destiny), while some scholars prefer to derive obstināre directly from obstāre. What matters for meaning is that both analyses give the same image: to set oneself firmly against, to plant oneself in a fixed stance. The word's stubbornness is thus literally a stance, a standing-ground, and the noun obstinacy is a way of standing that refuses to shift. 3. The medieval route into English English borrows obstinate in the later fourteenth century, in the wave of Latin and French loan-words that enriched Middle English. It arrives through two channels at once — directly from Latin obstinātus and through Old French obstiné — and it enters the language as a moral and religious word. In medieval and early modern usage, obstinacy is a grave matter: sermons and moral treatises denounce the obstinate sinner, the obstinate heretic, the obstinate rebel who refuses the counsel of the church or the state. The word's register from the start is therefore formal, Latinate, and morally charged: obstinate is not merely 'stubborn' but 'stubborn against reason, authority, or grace.' This double edge — firmness shading into fault — is the word's permanent signature. 4. The adverb: obstinately as a manner of speech and action From the adjective English forms the manner adverb obstinately by the ordinary suffix -ly, attested from the late fifteenth or sixteenth century. As an adverb it does what all -ly manner adverbs do: it reports how an action is performed, colouring a verb with the quality of the adjective. To speak obstinately is to speak in a fixed, unyielding way; to hold obstinately to a belief is to hold it against persuasion and evidence. In modern prose the adverb is at home in narrative description and psychological commentary, where it paints a character who will not be moved — and, because the adjective carries the hint of fault, the adverb usually suggests that the firmness is excessive, mulish, or comic as much as admirable. The nineteenth-century novel uses it of stubborn peasants, wilful children, and implacable rivals alike; the word never quite loses the moral edge it gained in the pulpit. 5. The double valence: fault and virtue A crucial nuance of the whole family is its double valence. Alongside the pejorative 'obstinate in error' stands the admiring use: an obstinate courage, an obstinate refusal to surrender, an obstinate faith. The word's etymology — standing firm — is morally neutral, and context decides whether the fixed stance is praiseworthy resolve or blameworthy mulishness. This is exactly why the near-synonym resolutely exists: resolute is the same 'fixedness' seen as a virtue, while obstinate tips toward the fault. A writer chooses obstinately when they want the reader to feel the friction of the fixedness — the refusal to be talked off the point. 6. Bilbo's business manner In the passage from The Hobbit, Bilbo 'said he obstinately, putting on his business manner' when he demands to know 'all plain and clear' about the gold, the dragon, the risks, and the terms. Tolkien chooses the Latinate adverb deliberately. The word paints hobbit tenacity wearing the borrowed costume of a businessman — the same mulish clarity the Baggins would use on a borrower — inside a room full of song, prophecy, and dwarves. The obstinacy is both laughable and sensible: a respectable hobbit refusing to be dazzled, standing firm (the root image!) against the romance of the quest. The adverb is thus character portrait and narrative irony at once: the fixed stance that will, against all odds, carry this particular hobbit all the way to the Mountain. 7. The family and its boundaries The adjective, noun, and adverb are all in continuous English use since the 14th–16th centuries: obstinate, obstinacy, obstinately. The family stays tightly packed — there is no meaning drift into unrelated domains — and the word's Latinate formality keeps it slightly apart from the native stubborn. English has not let obstinately wander into jargon; it remains a general word of character and behaviour. Its scientific and clinical uses (an obstinate fever, an obstinate problem) merely apply the same 'refusing to yield' image to diseases and difficulties, proving once more that the whole family is one image, repeated across the centuries: a person, or a thing, that stands fixed and will not be moved.
