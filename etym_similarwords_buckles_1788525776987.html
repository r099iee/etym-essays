• clasps: Similar because both designate mechanical hardware devices designed to hold two separate edges or overlapping ends together; differs because a clasp often relies on interlocking hooks, spring catches, or decorative hinged latches without an adjustable tongue-and-strap frame, whereas a buckle typically incorporates an adjustable strap passed through a rigid frame and secured by a prong.

  • fasteners: Similar as a generic category of hardware used to secure apparel, baggage, and structural materials; differs because fastener is a broad, neutral umbrella term encompassing buttons, zips, snaps, velcro, rivets, and bolts, lacking the specific historical, sartorial, and structural connotations of buckles.

  • hasps: Similar because both consist of hinged metal hardware fittings used for securing objects; differs because a hasp consists of a hinged flap that folds over a projecting staple to receive a padlock or peg, primarily used for securing doors, gates, and heavy trunks rather than clothing or flexible straps.

  • catches: Similar because both operate as fastening mechanisms that restrain movement; differs because a catch is usually an internal or surface-mounted latching device (such as a door catch or jewelry spring catch) that arrests motion upon contact, rather than an external strap-tensioning frame.

  • collapses: Similar when compared to the verb sense of buckles denoting sudden physical or organizational failure; differs because collapse implies a total inward falling down, disintegration, or destruction into rubble, whereas buckles specifically emphasizes the initial outward or lateral bending, bowing, and warping caused by axial compressive stress.

  • warps: Similar in describing progressive geometric distortion under physical strain; differs because warp typically implies gradual twisting, curling, or torsion (often caused by moisture or uneven heating in timber and plastics), whereas buckles designates a sudden, dramatic structural yielding under compressive load.

  • yields: Similar in describing capitulation, surrender, or structural deformation under applied load; differs because yield is a formal, broad term denoting either mechanical deformation beyond the elastic limit or general psychological concession, lacking the vivid physical imagery of bending and bulging inherent in buckles.

  • succumbs: Similar in the figurative sense of giving way before an irresistible force, disease, or temptation; differs because succumb carries a higher, tragic, or fatal literary register, highlighting terminal defeat or death rather than sudden structural crumpling.
