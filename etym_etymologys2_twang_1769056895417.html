No Proto-Indo-European root can be reconstructed for twang — the word is a purely imitative (onomatopoeic) coinage of English, not an inherited word. The chain is therefore English-internal: - Imitative coinage: twang (noun) "a sharp, ringing sound of a taut string released" — first recorded 1553, in Tudor English; coined to imitate the plucked-string sound → twang (verb) "to pluck a string so that it twangs; to make such a sound" — 16th century, alongside the noun → twanged / twanging (inflected forms) — 16th century → semantic extension: "a thin, nasal tone of voice" — 17th century (a nasal twang) → semantic extension: "a regional accent" — 18th–19th centuries (a Southern / Australian twang) → twangy (adjective) "having a twang" — 20th century (of sound, voice, guitar) → the base form twang (the noun and verb on this card) Note (coinage analysis and cross-linguistic status): The internal shape of twang is frequently analysed as the combination of the tw- onset of tension-and-pull words (twitch, twist, twine) with the resonant -ang tail of impact words (bang, clang) — a blend that fits the taut-string sound. Because the word is onomatopoeic, it has no cognates in Sanskrit, Hindi, French, Italian, or German: every language re-creates its own imitation of the plucked string (e.g. German zwängen? — no, the true imitative parallels are independent), and no PIE, Indo-Aryan, Romance, or Germanic branch can be drawn. The word is a deliberate creation, and its history is a history of sound-symbolism, not of inheritance.
