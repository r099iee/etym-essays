Proto-Germanic *knuttaz “knot, lump” (reconstructed; no secure PIE ancestry established—see note) → Proto-Germanic *knuttjaną / *knuttijōną “to tie into a knot, to knit” (denominative verb built on the noun; reconstructed) → Old English cnyttan “to tie, knot, knit” (attested glossing Latin nectere, ligare; 10th c. onwards) → Middle English knitten “to tie, to knit” (12th–15th c.) → English knit “to tie; to make fabric by interlocking loops; to draw together (the brows)” (modern) → English KNITS (third-person singular present, as on this card) DEBATED / NUANCED STAGES: (1) No secure PIE ancestry is established for the kn- family; etymonline and others tentatively suggest PIE *gen- “to pinch, compress, make tight” (also claimed as the ancestor of knuckle and knock), but the kn- onset and geminate -tt- have the look of a reduplicative, sound-symbolic formation, so the honest verdict is “no secure PIE ancestry”. (2) ANTI-MERGE GUARD: do not merge with PIE *mezg- “to plait, knit”, a genuinely different root that produced the Balto-Slavic and Indic knit/plait words (Lithuanian megzti “to knit”, mazgas “knot”; Sanskrit muñja- “reed-grass for plaiting”); English knit does NOT descend from *mezg-. (3) Related but also distinct: PIE *ned- “to bind together” gave Latin nōdus “knot”, nectere “to bind”, and Old English nett “net” (whence English net), not knit.
