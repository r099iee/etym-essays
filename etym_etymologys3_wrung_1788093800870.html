A root of turning. The history of wrung is the history of an idea — twisting — told by a single Germanic family. Behind the English word lies the PIE root *wer-, "to turn, bend", one of the most productive roots in the language's prehistory, extended here with a nasal to *wreng-, "to turn, twist". The same root, in different extensions, gave English wrist, writhe, wrest, wry and wrong — the last being a remarkable lexical trophy: Old Norse vrangr, "crooked, twisted", travelled into English as wrong, so that the moral vocabulary of error and the physics of twisting are cousins. The Germanic continuation *wringan- "to twist, press, squeeze" is visible across the family: German ringen "to wrestle" (literally, to twist oneself against another), Dutch wringen "to wring", and English wring itself.

Old and Middle English — the strong verb of moisture. Old English wringan (class III strong verb: wringan, wrang, wrungon, wrungen) is attested from the ninth century in the sense "to press, squeeze, twist", used of pressing liquids, twisting bonds, and forcing things out of shape; glossaries render Latin exprimere "to squeeze out" and torquere "to twist" with it. Middle English wringen sharpened the moisture sense — to wring clothes, sponges and, in medical recipe texts, to wring out the offending humours — while the strong past forms (wrang, wrungen) levelled, by the early modern period, to the single form wrung for both past tense and participle, exactly the pattern of sing – sang – sung.

The 15th–16th centuries — hands and grief. The decisive emotional development came with the fixed phrase "to wring one's hands", recorded from the fifteenth century in devotional and dramatic texts: the gesture of a person who twists their hands together in helpless grief, despair or anxiety. The phrase made the verb the property of tragedy — miracle plays, mystery cycles and early-modern drama filled their stages with characters wringing their hands while awaiting doom — and it remains in daily use after five hundred years. The KJV of 1611 fixed the physical register in a proverb: "Surely the churning of milk bringeth forth butter, and the wringing of the nose bringeth forth blood: so the forcible stirring of wrath bringeth forth strife" (Proverbs 30:33) — the verb's ancient squeezing sense given scriptural form.

The 16th–17th centuries — extraction as pressure. The figurative sense of extorting by pressure — to wring money, a confession, a promise from someone — is documented from the Elizabethan period onward. Its logic is exactly the logic of the cloth: what is hidden inside a reluctant person, like water in a towel, can be forced out by twisting. The image proved so strong that it survives in modern headlines and courtroom diction — evidence "wrung" from a prisoner — and in the colloquial threat "I'll wring your neck".

The 19th century — laundry, and wringing wet. The industrial century mechanized the image. The clothes-wringer — two rollers that squeezed water from washed fabric — entered homes from the 1860s, making "wring" a household-technology word; the intensifier "wringing wet" ("soaked so thoroughly that the water could be wrung out") became a standard colloquialism; and "wringing one's hands" began its slow descent from tragic gesture toward editorial cliché. At the same time literature discovered "heart-wringing" — that which twists the heart with pity or grief — and the emotional sense reached its peak in Victorian melodrama and Victorian journalism alike.

The 20th–21st centuries — from grief to mockery. The twentieth century completed a remarkable perception shift: "hand-wringing" became a noun, and a contemptuous one. Where wringing the hands had once been the sincerest possible gesture of grief, modern editorials use "hand-wringing" for anxious, ineffectual fretting — the politician who wrings his hands instead of acting. The gesture's sincerity decayed into theatre. Meanwhile "wrung" remains fully alive in the literal senses: wrung towels, wrung necks, wrung-out bodies after exertion, and — in poetry — music wrung from harp-strings, as in the source passage, where melody is squeezed out of twisted wire by the same ancient motion of turning and pressing that gave the word its first meaning a thousand years ago.

  • Key attestations: OE glosses of exprimere/torquere; Proverbs 30:33 (KJV 1611); 15th-c. "wring one's hands"; Elizabethan extortion uses; 19th-c. laundry wringer and "wringing wet"; 20th-c. "hand-wringing" as mockery.
  • Phonetic notes: PIE *wreng- > Germanic *wringan- > OE wringan; class III strong verbs level wrang/wrungen to wrung by the 16th century; the participle lost its final -en (wrungen) in the same levelling, unlike the -en of draw/drawn.
  • Register today: literal senses everyday, extraction senses formal-literary, "hand-wringing" journalistic and derisive; the word has never lost the violence of the twist.
