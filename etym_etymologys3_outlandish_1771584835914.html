The word "outlandish" is a native English compound that has spent a thousand years travelling from geography to insult. It was built in Old English from three pieces — "ut" ("out"), "land", and the adjective suffix "-isc" — and its meaning has drifted from the neutral "foreign" to the mocking "barbarous, bizarre", a drift that maps the social history of the English-speaking peoples and their relation to the world beyond the border. The Old English foundation.
  • The pieces: Old English "ut" (also "ute"), "out", from Proto-Germanic *ūt, from the PIE root *ud-, "up, out, outward" (the root of "utter", "utterly", and related to Sanskrit "ud", "up, out"). "Land" from Proto-Germanic *landą, from the PIE root *lendh-, "open land, heath, uncultivated ground" (the root of German "Land", Dutch "land", and Old Norse "land"). And the adjective suffix "-isc", from Proto-Germanic *-iskaz, from PIE *-isko-, which forms adjectives meaning "of, pertaining to, of the nature of" (the same suffix as in "English", "Danish", "heathenish").
  • The compound: Old English "utlendisc" (spelled variously "utlendisc", "utlendesc"), attested from the 9th–10th century in glosses and homilies (Ælfric and the Old English glossaries), meaning "foreign, of another country, of the out-lands". At this stage it is a neutral geographical word: an "utlendisc" man is simply a man from another land.
From foreign to barbarous: Middle English.
  • The meaning begins to darken in Middle English. "Outlandish" (spellings "outlandish", "outlandissh", "owtlandish") still means "foreign", but the social ground has shifted: in a world where the foreigner is the pagan, the Viking, the enemy beyond the border, "foreign" carries distrust. The word therefore slides from "foreign" toward "foreign and therefore uncouth": by the 14th century "outlandish" can mean "strange, uncouth, of foreign and barbarous manner".
  • The 15th–16th centuries complete the slide. By the Tudor period "outlandish" means "barbarous, unpolished, bizarre" as its primary sense, with "foreign" reduced to a secondary, archaic meaning. The word now names not merely what comes from another country but what seems to come from beyond civilisation altogether — the "out-land" of the border country, the marches, the pagan north.
Sound and form.
  • "Outlandish" shows the normal development of Old English "ūt" to Middle English "out" (with the characteristic rounding of "ū" before "t") and of "lendisc" to "landish"; the modern form "outlandish" has been stable since the 16th century. The word's rhythm is heavy and expressive — "out-LAND-ish" — which suits its mocking charge. It also produced "outlander" (a foreigner, a person from the out-lands) and "outlandishness" (the noun).
Semantic drift in the modern language.
  • In the 17th–18th centuries "outlandish" is used for anything grotesquely foreign in dress, speech, or manners — the vocabulary of travellers, moralists, and satirists for what seemed exotic and absurd. The 19th century extends it to ideas and behaviour: "outlandish" now means wildly unconventional, bizarre, absurdly out of proportion, and no longer requires literal foreignness. A man's clothes, a woman's ideas, a scheme, a price can all be "outlandish" — the word has detached from geography and become a pure pejorative of strangeness, while still carrying the memory of its geography like a faint watermark.
  • The modern word is thus fully pejorative or at least mockingly amazed: to call an act "outlandish" is to place it beyond the pale of civilised custom, as if it belonged to another land. In Dream of the Red Chamber the verse-portrait uses it with exactly this force: Bao-yu's "acts outlandish and his nature queer" are the judgement of conventional society on a boy who, morally, belongs elsewhere.
Literary and cultural attestations.
  • The Old English word appears in Ælfric and the glosses; Middle English romances and statutes use it for foreigners and foreign goods; the Elizabethans and Jacobeans (including Shakespeare, who uses "outlandish" for foreign and barbarous things) fix its pejorative force; the 18th- and 19th-century novelists use it for comic grotesquery; and modern journalism keeps it for the wildly unconventional — outlandish claims, outlandish outfits, outlandish theories. Its thousand-year drift — from neutral geography to mocking judgement — is a casebook of how a society's word for "foreign" hardens into its word for "absurd".
Summary.
  • From PIE *ud- and *lendh-, through the Old English compound "utlendisc" ("of the out-lands"), through Middle English's slide from "foreign" to "uncouth", to the modern pejorative "bizarre" — "outlandish" is English history in one word, the story of the border drawn between the in-lands of custom and the out-lands of strangeness.
