- Old English — falling upon: befallan means to fall upon, to happen to, used of lots, fates, and accidents descending on men.
- Middle English — lots and providence: the lot befell Matthias, duties befall ranks; the participle still carries fairness and order as easily as calamity.
- Early modern — chronicles and scripture: it befell in those days becomes the storyteller's overture from Tyndale to Shakespeare, lending events the authority of heaven.
- 18th–19th c. — the novel's fate-word: historians and novelists reserve befallen for tragedies, fortunes, and turns of plot, while happen takes over ordinary life.
- Early 20th c. — archaism with honour: the verb retreats from speech into headlines, obituaries, and liturgy, kept alive precisely by its solemnity.
- 1954 — Tolkien crowns it: adventure befalls Bilbo, and the archaic participle becomes the hinge of the greatest modern fantasy — fate arriving disguised as a wizard's knock.
- Today — disaster's participle: befallen survives in news reports of calamity and in fantasy's echo of them, the word we reach for when happened feels too small for the blow.
