- [15th-century loan] - Entering English in the 1400s (from Latin imprudentia), 'imprudence' named a want of foresight - the failure of practical wisdom, contrasted with prudence, one of the four cardinal virtues.
- [Moral weight] - In moral and theological writing of the 16th and 18th centuries it was a species of vice, not mere error - a defect of wisdom itself, carrying genuine ethical gravity.
- [Political diagnosis] - In 19th-century historical prose (as in Macaulay) it became the verdict word for failures of statecraft - ministers' imprudence breaking empires - grave, analytical, retrospective.
- [Contemporary sense] - Today 'imprudence' is a formal word for rashness or lack of caution in conduct and finance, milder in everyday use but still implying a blameworthy want of foresight.
