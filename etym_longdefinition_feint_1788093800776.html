Feint (noun, occasionally verb) denotes a deliberately false attack or motion: in combat, sport, and war it is the movement made to look like a real strike in order to make the opponent react - to parry, flinch, or commit - so that the true attack can land elsewhere. Figuratively it names any pretence designed to mislead: a feint of friendship, a feint of retreat, a feint of indifference.

1. The combat sense.

  • Fencing and swordplay. In the fencing traditions (French, Italian, and their English pupils) the feint is one of the foundational actions of the art: an attack made at one line in order to draw the opponent's parry, after which the fencer disengages and hits in another line. Seventeenth-century French fencing academies systematised the feinte alongside the attack and the parry, and the word entered English precisely in this technical fencing and military register.
  • Boxing and martial arts. In boxing a feint is a jab-like movement that does not land but forces a reaction - lowering a guard, stepping back - that the true punch exploits. Almost every striking art has the concept (the fint of sabre, the body feints of kickboxing), and in modern games such as football and rugby, "feints" (step-overs, dummy passes) deceive defenders bodily rather than with weapons.
  • Warfare. In military doctrine a feint is a secondary attack or demonstration designed to make the enemy commit reserves, shift his defence, or reveal his intentions; the feint is delivered where the enemy can see it so that the main attack can be delivered where he cannot. The sentence of this card is a textbook miniature of the tactic: "A few brave men were strung before them to make a feint of resistance" - a thin line staged to look like a defence, so that the orc host commits itself to that front while the rest withdraws.

2. The figurative sense: shapely deception.

  • From combat the word extends to any calculated deception: to "make a feint of" leaving, sleeping, agreeing, or searching is to perform an appearance with intent to mislead. The register is cooler and more deliberate than "trick": a feint is planned, spatial, and tactical; it implies that the deceiver controls the geometry of the situation.
  • Narrative and rhetoric use the word constantly - the feint of sentries, feints of courtship, feints in negotiation - where it brings a whiff of the duelling-ground into social life.

3. Grammar and spelling tangles.

  • Feint is primarily a noun; the verb "to feint" (back-formed, meaning "to make a feint") is old in fencing and now general in sport. The related English adjective is feigned, while the noun itself preserves the ghost of the French past participle feint ("feigned, pretended").
  • The feint/faint trap. Both words descend from the past participle of Old French feindre "to feign". Old French feint also meant "weak, spiritless" (from the notion of feigning or shirking duty), and that strand gave English faint ("lacking strength"); the noun feint (1680s) gives the deception sense. The two words are therefore historical doublets - near-homophones that split apart - and pupils' confusion of "faint" and "feint" in writing is entirely rational.
  • Feint-ruled paper. The old spelling survives commercially in "feint ruling": the faint, light lines of ledgers and notebooks are "feint lines", from an older spelling of "faint". A reader who sees "feint lines" should not imagine deceptive lines - though the pun is irresistible.

4. Related arts and modern life.

  • Military history's grandest feints include diversionary landings, sham bridge-building, and the whole machinery of deception operations - the Normandy campaign of 1944 (Operation Fortitude) is often described as a feint of continental scale, fixing German reserves on a landing that never came.
  • In business, politics, and games the noun keeps its tactical sharpness: "the announcement was a feint to distract from the real acquisition"; "his charity was a feint". The word never lost the duelist's precision: a feint has a target's eye in mind.

5. Semantic bridge in one line.

  • Feint is "shaped" (Latin fingere, "to shape, form, imagine"): the feint is an appearance shaped into the form of an attack - a made thing, a fiction of menace, whose whole purpose is to be believed for one decisive instant.
