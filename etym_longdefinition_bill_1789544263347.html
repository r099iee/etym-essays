Bill is an extraordinarily polysemic English noun (and verb) encompassing anatomical, financial, legislative, legal, typographical, and mechanical definitions. In its core zoological sense (as featured in the Sherlock Holmes canon regarding the goose in "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle"), it designates the horny projecting mouthparts or beak of a bird.

The extensive domains and functional meanings of the word include:

  • Anatomical & Zoological Sense: The beak or rostrum of a bird, comprising the upper and lower mandibles covered by a tough, keratinized sheath (the rhamphotheca). Used especially of waterfowl (geese, ducks), waders, and passerines. Also applied analogously to the snout or beak-like jaws of other animals (such as the platypus, turtles, or cephalopods).
  • Commercial & Accounting Sense: An itemized statement of charges or fees rendered to a customer for goods supplied or services performed; an invoice. By extension, the aggregate amount owed. In banking and international trade, a bill of exchange or bill of lading is a formal negotiable instrument commanding payment or certifying the receipt of cargo.
  • Legislative & Parliamentary Sense: A formal draft of a proposed statute presented to a legislative body (such as Parliament or Congress) for deliberation, debate, amendment, and potential enactment into law upon royal assent or executive signature.
  • Monetary Sense (Paper Currency): A piece of paper money issued by a central bank or treasury (e.g., a ten-dollar bill, a one-hundred-dollar bill), predominantly in North American English.
  • Legal Sense (Pleadings and Indictments): A formal written complaint, petition, or accusation submitted to a court of equity or grand jury (e.g., a bill of indictment, or a bill of complaint). When a grand jury finds sufficient evidence, it endorses a "true bill."
  • Publicity, Theatrical & Advertising Sense: A printed public notice, poster, or playbill announcing an entertainment, theatrical performance, or public gathering. Hence the idiom "top billing" (the leading position on a promotional poster) and "billboard" (a large outdoor advertising hoarding).
  • Historical Weaponry (Billhook/Polearm): A medieval cutting weapon consisting of a curved hooked blade attached to a long wooden pole, wielded by infantrymen (billmen) to hook and unhorse cavalry.
  • Nautical & Mechanical Senses: The extremity or pointed tip of the fluke of an anchor; also the peak of a cap (the visor).
  • Verbal Senses: To present an itemized invoice to someone; to announce or promote someone on a program (e.g., "billed as the greatest show on earth"); and in ornithology, to touch bills together affectionately (hence "billing and cooing").

Historical Semantic Bridges: The divergence of *bill* into two massive semantic streams represents one of the most famous homophonic convergences in the English language. Stream 1 (avian beak, billhook, anchor tip) descends from Proto-Germanic *bilją (cleaving instrument), where the bird’s sharp beak was viewed metaphorically as a natural cutting axe or pickaxe. Stream 2 (document, invoice, law, currency, poster) entered Middle English via Anglo-Norman *bille*, a diminutive of Medieval Latin *bulla* (a leaden seal attached to papal edicts and charters). Over the centuries, the term for the seal (*bulla*) transferred metonymically to the parchment document itself, then to legal petitions, financial accounts, parliamentary drafts, and finally paper money.
