Plume begins with Latin pluma, a soft small feather or down, as distinct from penna, a large wing-feather or quill. Latin pluma already had figurative extensions: the first down on a youth cheek, a featherbed, and by metonymy plumage as a whole. Its deeper origin is honestly obscure. Older handbooks cite PIE *pel- to cover, with feather and skin, or *pleuk- to fly, with feather, fly, and pluck, comparing English feather, fleece, fly, but de Vaan stresses there are no secure extra-Italic cognates and the m-formation is isolated, so the responsible statement is that pluma is of unknown deeper etymology with a debated PIE link noted but not asserted. The chain below therefore starts securely at Latin.

What is certain is the Romance descent. Old French plume kept feather and, crucially, quill-pen, since pens were goose feathers, so plume became writing itself: gens de plume for writers, plume as pen and style. Borrowed into Middle English around 1300 as plume for an ornamental feather, knightly plume and princely ostrich feathers, it already carried aristocratic display. The verb follows naturally from behaviour: birds plume, preen and dress, their feathers; humans, metaphorically, plume themselves when they dress up, show off, or congratulate themselves, as a bird puffs its plumage in courtship and threat.

  • Latin pluma noun: soft feather and down; domestic pillow and bed, bodily cheek-down; neutral descriptive.
  • Old French plume: feather plus quill-pen; metonymy to authorship; heraldic and equestrian ornament and panache.
  • Middle English plume noun: prestige feather on helm, hat, horse; sign of rank, hence plume as honour in cap.
  • Early Modern plume verb: to dress feathers in falconry, then reflexive to plume oneself: to pride and boast, Shakespearean and Baconian, often ironic.
  • Modern reflexive idiom: to plume oneself on or upon something; slightly mocking tone, as in the source where human distinctions deflate to degree.
  • Modern scientific noun: smoke plume, ash plume, mantle plume; feather lightness and spread as visual model.

Phonetics show French borrowing intact: silent final e, long vowel in English, versus French rounded front vowel. The noun-to-verb conversion by zero-derivation is typically English and early modern. Culturally the plume moved from battlefield to writing desk to psychology: Franklin tool-using animal quip and its gloss that we plume ourselves on reason, speech, or tools, puffing up like pigeons, captures Enlightenment deflation of human exceptionalism. Literary peaks include Milton plumed hosts, Pope plumed troops, Darwin plumed birds where sexual selection explains why birds literally plume themselves, closing the loop between human vanity and animal display that the source passage exploits. Heraldic rolls, millinery catalogues, and regimental dress manuals preserve the feather as rank made visible, while geology textbooks extend it downward into the earth.
