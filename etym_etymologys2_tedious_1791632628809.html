PIE (c. 4000 BCE)
*teh₂- / *teh₁- (uncertain)
"to melt, waste away, be disgusted"
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 800 BCE)
*taideo-
"to feel disgust, loathing, weariness"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE)
taedere (impersonal taedet)
"to disgust, weary, fill with loathing"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 2nd c. BCE)
taedium
"weariness, disgust, loathing, boredom"
↓
Late Latin (c. 4th c. CE)
taediosus
"wearisome, disgusting, irksome"
↓
Anglo-Norman / Old French (14th c.)
tedious / tedieus
"irksome, wearisome, causing disgust"
↓
Middle English (c. 1380)
tedious / tediose
"tiresome, painful, wearisome"
↓
Modern English (modern)
tedious
"tiresome, wearisome, monotonous"

Note: Derives via Anglo-Norman from Late Latin taediosus, built from the noun taedium ("disgust, weariness, boredom") and the impersonal verb taedere ("to feel disgust or loathing"). The Proto-Italic reconstructed base *taideo- has no certain Indo-European cognates outside of Italic, though philologists tentatively compare PIE *teh₂- ("to melt, melt away"), reflecting the feeling of wasting away in exhausted boredom.
