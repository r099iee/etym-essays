The word drank is a fossil of the oldest grammatical machinery of the Germanic languages — the ablaut system, in which the vowel of a verb stem changes to mark tense. Its entire history is the history of one English verb, drink, and of the vowel that shifted inside it. Understanding drank therefore means understanding a strong verb of the third class over two and a half thousand years.

The Indo-European background

Behind the Germanic family stands a reconstructed Proto-Indo-European root, usually given as *dʰreg- (sometimes *dʰrebʰ- or *dʰreĝ-), meaning "to drink". The reconstruction is genuinely debated: the only secure descendants are in Germanic (English drink, German trinken, Dutch drinken, Old Norse drekka), and scholars differ over whether the Italic and Celtic forms belong to the same root. What is certain is that the Germanic root *drinkaną existed by about the middle of the first millennium BCE, conjugated as a strong verb of the third class — the class of sing, ring, and begin, whose past is formed by shifting the stem vowel rather than adding -ed.

The Proto-Germanic ablaut pattern

In Proto-Germanic, the present stem *drinkaną had the vowel /i/. Its past tense stem was formed by ablaut: the past singular used the vowel /a/, giving *drank (pronounced with a short a), and the past plural and participle used /u/, giving *drunkan and its participle. This is the exact three-step pattern inherited into English: drink (present) – drank (past singular) – drunk (past participle). Crucially, the form drank itself, with its distinctive a-vowel, is not an English invention — it is the direct descendant of the Proto-Germanic preterite stem, transmitted without interruption for roughly 2,500 years.

Old English

In Old English the verb appears as drincan (infinitive) with the past singular dranc and past plural druncon; the past participle was (ge)druncen. The -a- preterite is attested throughout the Anglo-Saxon corpus, from laws and homilies to the great poems. Because Anglo-Saxon feasting was central to social life — the mead-hall, the ceremonial cup passed among warriors — the word appears constantly in heroic and religious contexts. The Beowulf-poet describes warriors drinking in the hall; the translators render the Eucharist and the wedding feast at Cana with the same verb. Already in Old English, then, drincan / dranc covered both the everyday act and the ceremonial toast.

Middle English

In Middle English the vowel of the past tense was pulled two ways: the traditional form drank (with a) and a northern, later standard dronk (with o), while the participle was dronken or drunken. Chaucer and his contemporaries use both. The co-existence of drank and dronk sets up the great grammatical debate of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: which form should be the past tense, and which the participle? In the everyday language of London and the court, "drunk" increasingly did service for the past tense as well ("I drunk"), creating the very confusion that later grammarians would clean up.

The grammatical revolution of the 17th–18th centuries

The rise of standard English grammars — from John Wallis (1653) to Robert Lowth (1762) and Lindley Murray (1795) — fixed the rule that survives today: drank is the simple past, drunk is the past participle used with have/be. The grammarians rationalised the pattern by analogy with sing–sang–sung and ring–rang–rung, and the school tradition enforced it. This was prescriptivism at its most successful: the modern educated speaker distinguishes the pair automatically, and "I have drank" is now heard as a solecism, though it survives in regional and working-class speech, where it is not felt as an error.

Semantic drift: from thirst to intoxication

While the form was being standardised, the word's social meaning was darkening. Alcohol had always been part of drinking, but the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with cheap gin and the temperance movement, loaded drink with moral weight. "He drank" became a soft way to say that a man was a habitual drunkard; "he drank himself to death" entered the obituary. The participle drunk hardened into the adjective for intoxication, while drank stayed the neutral past — and this asymmetry persists. Today the same sentence "they drank a lot" swings between harmless and alarming depending on context.

Modern English

  • Standard rule: drank for the simple past ("I drank coffee"), drunk for the participle ("I have drunk") and for the adjective of intoxication ("he was drunk").
  • Colloquial and regional British speech still uses "drank" where the standard expects the participle ("I've drank"), a relic of the pre-prescriptive pattern.
  • The ceremonial sense survives in "drinking to" healths and toasts, exactly as Tolkien uses it — a direct line to the mead-hall cup of Old English poetry.

What is remarkable about drank is that its phonetic form has survived almost unchanged from Proto-Germanic while its social register has been continuously rewritten — from plain preterite, to feasting word, to grammatical battlefield, to modern marker of both thirst and intoxication.
