guttered — linear derivation chain: 1. PIE *ǵʰeu- “to pour” (reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE) — the base proposed for Latin “pour” words (cf. Latin fundō “to pour,” from the full grade *ǵʰew-) and, via the “drop” sense, for the Latin drop-word gutta (the exact linkage is debated; see note). 2. Latin gutta “a drop” (attested in classical Latin) — either from the “pour” root or of uncertain origin; the source of the Romance words for “drop” and for the channel that collects drops. 3. Old French gote, goute “a drop” (from Latin gutta) → Old French gotiere, gouttière “water-channel, roof-gutter, pipe” (12th–14th c.). 4. Middle English goter, guter, gutere “channel for carrying off rainwater, drain” (borrowed from Old French gotiere, attested 13th–14th c.); modern spelling gutter fixed in the 16th c. 5. English gutter (noun) → verb gutter “to form channels, to flow in channels” (attested 16th c.); of a candle, “to burn with the wax running down the sides in channels, to flicker and sink” (attested 18th c.). 6. English guttered (past tense and participle of gutter; “a guttering candle” attested 18th–19th c.). Debate note: the ultimate origin of Latin gutta “a drop” is uncertain. The connection to the PIE “pour” root *ǵʰeu- is plausible and widely repeated but not certain; other scholars treat gutta as an isolated Latin formation. The English candle-verb, by contrast, is securely attested as a 17th–18th-century development of the roof-channel noun, the channel of melting wax on the candle’s side being felt as a tiny gutter. muddle — linear derivation chain: 1. Origin of Middle Dutch modde “mud, mire” is uncertain; it belongs to a Low German and Dutch family of “mud” words with no securely established Indo-European root (some connect it with a Germanic base *mut- “mud, swamp,” of unknown provenance; the ultimate etymology is unknown). 2. Middle Dutch moddelen “to make muddy, to dabble in mud” (attested 15th–16th c.) — denominative verb from modde “mud.” 3. English muddle (verb) “to make muddy, to stir up as mud, to befoul” — borrowed from the Dutch verb or formed natively on the English noun mud (attested late 16th c., e.g. “muddling the water”); by 1690s the sense has extended to “to bungle, mismanage, confuse.” 4. English muddle (verb) “to confuse the mind, to befuddle” (attested 18th–19th c.; “muddled with drink” 18th c.). 5. English muddle (noun) “a state of confusion or disorder” (attested 19th c., c. 1818). 6. English muddle (noun and verb; modern form unchanged). Debate note: the etymology is genuinely uncertain at the root — the Dutch word modde has no agreed Indo-European source, so the chain begins with the attested Dutch form and explicitly flags the ultimate origin as unknown. The English verb and noun are, however, fully attested from the late 16th and early 19th centuries respectively, and the derivation from “to make muddy” to “to confuse” is transparent.
