unreason is a compound of the negative prefix un- and the noun reason; each element is traced in isolation, then joined. ELEMENT 1 — the privative prefix un-: PIE *n̥- "not" (the zero-grade privative prefix, reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; also the source of Greek a-/an- "un-", Latin in- "un-") → Proto-Germanic *un- "not, un-" (reconstructed) → Old English un- "not, lacking" (attested, pre-9th c. CE; still the productive negative prefix of English) → English un- "not, lacking, opposite of" (the prefix in unreason) ELEMENT 2 — reason: PIE *reh₁- / *rē- "to count, reckon, calculate, think, arrange in order" (reconstructed root, c. 4000 BCE; also source of Greek arithmos "number" → arithmetic, and Germanic *rēdaną "to counsel, interpret" → English read) → Latin rērī "to think, calculate, reckon" (attested 1st c. BCE; from the perfect participle ratus, meaning "fixed, settled") → Latin ratiō "reckoning, account, calculation; reason, judgment, procedure" (attested 1st c. BCE) → Late Latin ratiōnem (accusative of ratiō) "reason, account" (c. 3rd–5th c. CE) → Old French raison / reson "reason, speech, cause, account" (attested 9th–12th c. CE) → Middle English resoun / reson "reason" (attested 13th c. CE, via Anglo-Norman) → English reason "the power of rational thought; a cause or justification" (modern form) COMPOUND STEP: English un- + reason → Middle English unreson / unresoun "lack of reason; a wrong, an injustice; an unreasonable act" (attested 14th c. CE) → English unreason "irrationality; the absence or breakdown of rational order" ◄ TARGET (present-day rootWord) Debate / nuance note: Middle English unreson is attested from the 14th century, but its exact route is debated. It may be a native formation (Old English un- + Middle English resoun), or a borrowing of Anglo-Norman unresun / Old French unreson, in which the prefix is likewise the negative un-. Both routes converge on the same modern form, so the etymology is secure in substance though the precise path is ambiguous. The personified, capitalized Unreason of modern literary and philosophical prose is a 19th–20th-century rhetorical development built on the older noun, not a new etymology.
