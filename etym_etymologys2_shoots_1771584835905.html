Chain for the single root of SHOOTS (one root: the verb shoot). Variable stages = 7. 1. PIE *skeud- “to throw, shoot, hurl, impel” — a root of sudden outward propulsion; reconstructed from Sanskrit chodati “he impels, urges, drives”, Slavic *kydati “to throw”, and the Germanic family (c. 4500–3000 BCE). 2. → Proto-Germanic *skeutaną “to shoot, project, push out” — a strong verb (class II), the immediate source of the Germanic forms. 3. → Old English scēotan “to shoot, dart, rush; to push, thrust” (strong verb, 8th–11th c.) — used of shooting arrows, of water rushing out, of things projecting suddenly; the derived noun scēot meant “a shot, a missile”. 4. → Middle English shoten / schoten “to shoot; to sprout” (c. 1200) — by c. 1300 the verb acquired the sense “to grow rapidly, to sprout”. 5. → English shoot (v.) “to discharge a missile; to grow rapidly, to sprout” (14th–15th c.) — the growing sense is attested from c. 1300. 6. → English shoot (n.) “a young branch or stem growing from a bud, seed, or root” (c. 1500) — a nominal use of the verb's sprouting sense; the stem that has been “shot out”. 7. → English shoots (plural of the noun; the card's form) — Final node: English SHOOTS (rootWord), the botanical plural, 16th century to present. Debated / nuanced stages: (1) The PIE reconstruction *skeud- is secure in outline but the full root shape is debated (some handbooks write *skewd-); the Sanskrit reflex chud- from *skeud- is generally accepted. (2) The date of the botanical noun is not precise: the sense “young branch” is placed around c. 1500 in the OED, though the underlying image (a shoot is a stem shot out) is already active in Old English verb usage. (3) Sister formations from the same root — Old English scyttan “to bolt, shut” (whence English shut), English shout, sheet, and scud — are related but separate branches; they are not stages of this chain.
