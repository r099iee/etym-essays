- The Old English verb dwellan meant to mislead, hinder, or wander astray — a word of errancy and delay, with no hint of home.
- In Middle English it lingered as "to tarry, remain, abide," and gradually settled on the idea of staying put in a place.
- By the 14th century dwell meant to live in a place, and dwelling hardened into the noun for a place of residence, wearing a formal, almost legal neutrality.
- In modern usage that neutrality lets dwelling serve primitives and beasts too — "cave dwellings," "rock dwellings," "dwellings of the dead" — so in literature it has come to mean a home drained of warmth, an architectural lair that houses intelligence without sheltering it.
