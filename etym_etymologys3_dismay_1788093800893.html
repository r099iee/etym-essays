A Germanic heart inside a French-made word. Dismay is one of the most surprising etymologies in English: a word that looks and behaves entirely French is, at its core, Germanic. The chain runs: Proto-Germanic *maganą “to be able, to have power” — the very root of everyday English may and might (Old English magan “can, be able”; miht “might, power”) — was borrowed, in the form *magan, into the Romance speech of northern Gaul. The Franks, whose language had already given western Romance a large stock of military and social words (war, ward, guard, frank itself), contributed this word for “power, ability”. The speakers of late Gallo-Roman Latin built on it a verb, *exmagare — ex- “out, away from” + *magare “to be powerful” — meaning “to deprive of strength, to un-power, to disable”. The reconstruction is standard in Romance etymology (it is the accepted ancestor of French esmaier and of the Italian and Iberian cognates treated below), and it preserves exactly the semantics that surface in English: to dismay is literally to take the might out of someone. This is why the feeling is so specific — being dismayed is being suddenly, shockingly de-powered, not merely saddened.

Old French: esmaier and desmaier. From *exmagare, Old French made esmaier “to trouble, alarm, frighten” (attested from the 11th century), with the noun esmai “alarm, consternation” — the ancestor of modern French émoi, still familiar in the phrase en émoi, “in a state of agitation”. The Old French intensity was later reinforced with the prefix des-: desmaier “to alarm utterly”. The two verbs coexisted, and English inherited the reinforced form. The same Romance coinage spread across the family: Old Occitan esmaiar, Italian smagare “to lose strength, grow weak”, and — most strikingly — Spanish desmayar and Portuguese desmaiar “to faint”, with Spanish desmayo “a fainting fit; dismay”. In the Iberian languages the “deprived of power” sense hardened into the physical collapse of fainting; in French and English it remained mental — the collapse of courage. The divergence is a small masterpiece of semantic branching: one inherited meaning, “to un-power”, taking the body in Spain and Portugal and the spirit in France and England.

Middle English: a strong word enters. English borrowed dismaien around 1300, at the height of the Anglo-Norman period, when the lexicon was absorbing the vocabulary of French courtly culture — chivalry, romance and war. The Middle English verb meant “to alarm, frighten, terrify; to fill with dread”, and the noun dismai (back-formed almost immediately, c. 1300) meant “terror, consternation, rout”. In the Middle English romances — Arthour and Merlin, Guy of Warwick, the alliterative Morte Arthure — dismay is what befalls knights before giants, pagan hosts before Christian knights, and castles at the news of siege; it is a strong, almost physical word of battle. The form history is ordinary: OF desmaier → ME dismaien (spellings dismeien, desmayen, dysmayn), settling as dis-may by the 15th century — at which point English speakers re-analysed the prefix as the common dis- “apart, away”, reinforcing the perceived sense of being robbed of something.

16th–17th centuries: the age of its greatest strength. The Authorised Version of 1611 uses the verb and participle prolifically — “fear not, neither be dismayed” rings through Deuteronomy, Joshua, Chronicles, Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel, one of the Bible’s great consolations for armies and cities. Shakespeare works the word hard across the history plays — dismayed councils, dismayed hosts, counsels of dismay — and Milton uses it of the fallen angels and of the war in heaven, where “dismay” names the breaking of celestial courage. In this period to dismay can still mean “to rout, to break utterly”; the adjective describes a near-panic helplessness. The word’s intensity reached its historical peak in the 17th century: it stood alongside terror and dread, a notch below despair.

18th–19th centuries: the great softening. Over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the word’s voltage steadily declined. The causes are social-linguistic: as warfare professionalised and religious terror receded from ordinary life, the vocabulary of panic lost its daily urgency, while polite society needed a word for “alarming disappointment” that was stronger than annoyance and weaker than horror. The reaction formula “to my dismay” became the standard adult way of saying “contrary to my hopes, significantly upsetting me”. The 19th-century historical novelists — Scott first among them — deliberately revived the strong battle-sense for colour in siege and battle scenes: “they hastened back in dismay” is exactly this register, and Tolkien’s use of it in The Hobbit (the source sentence of this card) is a conscious archaism of the word’s medieval strength. Meanwhile the noun accumulated its modern collocations: in dismay, with dismay, to one’s dismay, dismay at something.

20th–21st centuries: stabilised and ever-present. Modern English retains the full verb–noun–adjective system — dismay, dismayed, dismaying, dismayingly — with the classical collocations (“to my dismay”, “much to my dismay”, “in dismay”, “dismayed to learn”, “dismayed that”) current in journalism, fiction, diplomacy and everyday prose. The register is formal-but-common: appropriate to a letter or a newspaper, out of place in street slang. The present meaning — “sudden alarm joined with discouragement” — is a softened inheritance of the medieval “terror, rout”, which in turn was a Gallo-Roman “to deprive of strength”. And the word’s full history remains encoded in its spelling: dis + may, still quietly saying “un-might” to anyone who knows where may and might come from.
