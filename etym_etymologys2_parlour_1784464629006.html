PARLOUR descends through French from a Greek noun built on a very old verb of throwing; the single linear chain is as follows. PIE *gʷelH- 'to throw, to strike' (reconstructed; the root standardly cited for Greek bállein) → Greek βάλλειν bállein 'to throw' (aorist stem βαλ- bal-) → Greek παραβολή parabolḗ 'a throwing beside, a comparison, an illustration' (παρά para- 'beside' + βολή bolē 'a throw'; classical Greek, 5th–4th c. BCE) → Latin parabola 'a comparison; a parable; in Late Latin, speech, a word' (borrowed from Greek; the 'speech' sense in Christian Latin, 3rd–4th c. CE) → Vulgar Latin *parabolare 'to speak' → *parlare 'to speak' (by shortening, proto-Romance) → Old French parler 'to speak' → Old French parloir (also parleur) 'a monastery room where speaking is allowed' → Anglo-Norman parlo(u)r → Middle English parlour 'a speaking room; a room for receiving guests' (c. 1200) → English parlour (British) / parlor (American) → PARLOUR. Note on the debate: the PIE shape behind Greek bállein is reconstructed as *gʷelH- (or *gʷelh₁-) 'to throw, strike', but the root's precise laryngeal and cognate set remain debated among Indo-Europeanists; the stage from Greek parabolḗ onward, by contrast, is solidly attested at every step. The Latin parabola split early: the 'comparison/parable' sense gave the English words parable and parabola (the curve), while the Christian-Latin 'speech, word' sense is the ancestor of parlour, parley, parlance, and French parole.
