tendons is the plural of tendon: the tough, inelastic cords of dense fibrous connective tissue that attach muscle to bone and transmit contractile force to produce movement. The Achilles tendon joining calf to heel, the patellar tendon below the kneecap, and the flexor tendons of fingers are familiar examples. In the source passage, the tendons of animals are split and drawn into threads fine enough to sew with — a precise ethnographic observation, since cleaned, dried tendon fibres separate into lustrous, immensely strong filaments ideal for embroidery and garment-sewing before steel needles and synthetic thread.

Anatomically, a tendon is ~85% collagen type I in parallel bundles with tenocytes, crimped to allow slight give, sheathed in paratenon or synovial sheath where it glides. It is whitish, low in blood supply (hence slow healing), and strong in tension but vulnerable to sudden overload and chronic friction. Ligaments join bone to bone; aponeuroses are flat tendon-sheets; fascia wraps; tendon is the rope.

Core senses and collocations:

  • Named tendons: Achilles / calcaneal tendon, patellar tendon, biceps and triceps tendons, rotator-cuff tendons, flexor and extensor tendons of hand and foot, peroneal tendons.
  • Injury language: strained, torn, ruptured, inflamed tendons; tendinitis / tendinosis / tendinopathy; tendon repair, graft, transfer; to rest, ice, and rehab tendons.
  • Animal-material sense: as in source, animal tendons as thread, cord, and glue-stock; tendon-threads dyed with berry juice for quillwork and embroidery; back-sinew sewn parkas.
  • Culinary sense: beef tendon (ngau gan) in Vietnamese pho and Chinese hotpot — simmered to gelatinous tenderness, prized for texture; crunchy tendon cartilage in Japanese oden.
  • Figurative extension (rarer than sinew): tendons of the organization for supporting cables or lines, but sinews remains the idiomatic choice for strength metaphors.

Further technical and everyday nuances:

  • Medical specialization: orthopaedics distinguishes tendinous vs muscular pain; physiotherapy prescribes eccentric loading for tendons; imaging (ultrasound, MRI) visualizes frayed tendons.
  • Sports discourse: to have strong tendons is to be durable; to have weak tendons is to be injury-prone; warming up protects tendons because cold collagen is brittle.
  • Craft continuity: traditional bowyers still back bows with leg tendons; taxidermists, bookbinders (tendon-glue), and Indigenous artists maintain tendon-thread sewing as heritage practice.
  • Number and countability: plural tendons stresses multiplicity — the body has ~4,000 named tendon slips; a carcass yields dozens of usable strands; injuries often affect pairs.

The bridge from anatomy to thread is material identity: the same collagen rope that moves a limb, once cleaned and split, becomes sewing cord that joins hides. Pre-industrial peoples saw no boundary between body and tool — art began where nature left off — and tendons names both the living connector and the harvested filament. To speak of tendons drawn into threads is to recall a world where strength was literally spun from animals into clothing.
