Tullia is not a word with a lexical meaning but a Roman woman’s name, and its history is the history of Roman naming. A Roman man carried three names — praenomen, nomen and cognomen — but a woman normally carried only the feminine form of her father’s nomen. Tullius therefore produced Tullia, exactly as Julius produced Julia, Cornelius Cornelia and Claudius Claudia. The suffix is the ordinary feminine -ia, and there is no separate etymology for the woman’s name: it is the clan name with a feminine ending.

The nomen Tullius

The nomen itself is usually explained as a derivative of the old praenomen Tullus, formed with the patronymic or gentilic -ius, so that Tullius would mean something like “of Tullus” or “descended from Tullus”. The praenomen is genuinely old Latin: Tullus Hostilius is the legendary third king of Rome, and the name belongs to the archaic stratum of Roman personal names. Beyond that point the record becomes uncertain. Roman antiquarians and many modern handbooks describe Tullius as Etruscan in origin, which is plausible given how many Roman family names came from Etruria, but no Etruscan source form has been securely identified, and other scholars treat the name as simply Italic. Two Tullias in Roman memory

The name carried a heavy literary charge before Cicero’s daughter was born. Livy tells of the two daughters of King Servius Tullius, both called Tullia, one gentle and one fierce. By the working of chance or design the fierce Tullia was married to the mild Arruns, and the gentle Tullia to the ambitious Lucius Tarquinius. Tullia Minor and Lucius murdered their spouses and married each other; then they overthrew Servius, and Tullia drove her chariot over her father’s body, so that the street was afterwards called the Vicus Sceleratus, the accursed street. Livy’s narrative made her a moral emblem of filial impiety, and Boccaccio later included her among his famous women. Any Roman hearing the name Tullia would therefore hear this story as well as the living woman.

The historical Tullia

  • Born around 79 BCE, she was the elder child of Cicero and Terentia and grew up during her father’s rise to the consulship.
  • Her three marriages — to Piso Frugi, to Furius Crassipes and to Dolabella — trace the political weather of the late Republic and show a woman with preferences of her own.
  • Cicero’s letters call her Tulliola and dwell on her health, her pregnancies and her unhappiness; they are the fullest surviving representation of an elite Roman daughter by her own father.
  • Her death in February 45 BCE, after the birth and loss of a son, provoked the Consolatio, a lost work admired in antiquity, and a plan for a shrine that suggests grief bordering on the religious.

English afterlife

English readers met Tullia through translations of Cicero’s letters and through Lives of Cicero. Conyers Middleton’s History of the Life of M. Tullius Cicero (1741) gave her a prominent place in the narrative, and eighteenth- and nineteenth-century biographies made her death one of the emotional climaxes of the story. Modern scholarship has shifted the emphasis: Susan Treggiari’s study of Terentia, Tullia and Publilia (2007) treats her as a social actor rather than a passive object of paternal grief. The name itself remains what it was in Rome — a clan name in feminine form — and its interest for lexicographers lies in that transparency: to explain Tullia is to explain the Roman system of gentile names, not to recover a lost root.

Caution

No writing by Tullia survives, and her voice is known only through her father’s letters and through later biography. Statements about her feelings, her merit or her reciprocity towards Cicero are inferences, and the biographer’s aside in the source is a literary judgement rather than evidence.
