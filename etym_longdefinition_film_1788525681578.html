film (noun and verb; plural films) — A thin, delicate membrane, pellicle, skin, or coating of organic or inorganic substance spread across a surface or suspended within a medium; in physiological and literary contexts (as in Oscar Wilde's The Nightingale and the Rose), a misty opacity, dimness, haze, or veil of moisture and failing vitality that clouds the eyes during illness, exhaustion, fainting, or impending death; in modern science and technology, a thin chemical, polymeric, or emulsion layer, as well as the flexible celluloid strip used in photography and the motion picture medium itself.

Primary sense — delicate membrane, pellicle, or coating. In its original Germanic physical sense, a film is any exceptionally thin layer of substance. This includes the delicate membrane lining the inside of an eggshell, the thin pellicle that forms on the surface of cooling boiled milk or tea, an iridescent sheen of oil on water, or an applied protective coat of varnish or wax. The essential semantic characteristic is minimal thickness combined with continuous superficial coverage.

Sense 2 — ocular opacity and the veil of dying (The context in Wilde). In pathology, physiology, and dramatic literature, a film denotes a cloudy, translucent obstruction or veil covering the cornea, pupil, or lens of the eye. This may be caused by a pathological condition (such as a cataract, corneal ulcer, or pterygium) or by the physiological collapse of ocular circulation during fainting, agony, or death. In Oscar Wilde's tale, as the nightingale exhausts her vital life-force pressing her breast against the thorn, "her little wings began to beat, and a film came over her eyes"—a classic literary trope depicting the fading of sensory sight as life ebbs away.

Sense 3 — photography, cinematography, and the motion picture art. In the late 19th century, the word underwent a massive technological expansion. It first designated the thin light-sensitive photographic emulsion of silver halides suspended in gelatin, and subsequently the flexible strip of transparent cellulose nitrate or acetate coated with that emulsion. By metonymy and semantic broadening, film became the universal name for motion pictures, the film industry, and the entire aesthetic medium of cinema (e.g., "film theory," "art film," "documentary film").

  • Materials science and physics — thin-film technology refers to micro- and nano-scale layers of materials (from atomic monolayers to several micrometers thick) deposited on substrates to fabricate semiconductors, solar cells, optical anti-reflective coatings, and liquid crystal displays.
  • Fluid mechanics and engineering — film lubrication or fluid film describes a thin hydrodynamic layer of oil, grease, or gas that completely separates two solid moving surfaces to minimize friction and wear in bearings and pistons.
  • Meteorology and optics — describes atmospheric hazes, thin veils of cirrus clouds, or condensation on window panes ("a film of frost").
  • Verbal usage — as a transitive/intransitive verb, to film means either to cover with a thin coating or misty veil ("her eyes filmed with tears") or to record scenes on a motion-picture camera or digital sensor.

Register and semantic scope. Film exhibits one of the most successful semantic trajectories in English: an ancient, humble Old English anatomical word for an animal membrane that effortlessly expanded into high-tech nanotechnology, medical diagnosis, and the defining global art form of the twentieth century.
