MOUNDS is the plural of mound, a noun whose central meanings are (1) a raised mass of earth, sand, stones, or similar material - a small hill, bank, or heap - and (2) any large pile or heap of accumulated matter, whether natural or artificial. In the supplied context - "the nearest edge of the mounds of treasure" - the word refers to the huge heaps of gold and gems upon which the dragon lies: literal piles that are also, as the whole novel makes clear, burial-mound-like accumulations of ancient plunder.

Primary senses

  • A raised bank or hillock of earth: a natural or artificial elevation of ground, smaller than a mountain, often rounded. "The children rolled down the grassy mound."
  • A heap or pile of any loose material: earth, sand, snow, stones, and by extension treasure, coins, food, paperwork. "A mound of dirty laundry covered the chair."
  • A defensive earthwork or rampart: the historical sense - an artificial bank thrown up as a fortification or as the foundation of a castle keep (the "motte" in motte-and-bailey castles).
  • A burial mound: a tumulus, barrow, or cairn heaped over a grave - the funerary monument of prehistoric and ancient peoples; also the great "Mound Builders" cultures of the Americas.

Secondary and specialised senses

  • Baseball: the "pitcher's mound" - the raised circular area of earth in the middle of the diamond from which the pitcher throws (attested 1906).
  • Geology and geography: any rounded elevated landform, e.g. glacial mounds, morainic mounds, and mound springs (natural artesian mounds).
  • Biology: "mound-building" ants, termites, and birds (malleefowl build incubation mounds); a mound of nest material or fungus.
  • Archaeology: "tell" or "mound" sites - accumulations of collapsed mud-brick towns rising as artificial hills (the Middle East's "tells").
  • Landscaping and horticulture: a raised planting bed or decorative earthwork.
  • Anatomy (rare, descriptive): the "mound of Venus" (mons pubis) - a rounded prominence of the body.
  • Figurative quantity: "mounds of work", "mounds of money", "a mound of evidence" - informal hyperbole meaning a very large amount.

Semantic reasoning and historical bridges

The word's history runs from fortification to landscape to pure quantity. The earliest recorded sense (mid-16th century) is the military earthwork - a mound thrown up to defend or to support a fort. From "defensive bank" the word broadened to any raised bank or small hill of earth (natural landscape), and from "small hill" it broadened further to "heap, pile" of any material, because a pile of earth is exactly what a mound is made of. Once the word meant "pile", it could be applied to piles of anything at all - treasure most memorably - and finally, in informal modern speech, "mounds of X" is simply an emphatic way of saying "a lot of X".

Register and nuance

  • Landscape sense: neutral, descriptive, at home in geography, literature, and everyday speech.
  • Archaeological and funerary sense: solemn, ancient, evocative (barrows, tumuli, Mound Builders).
  • Heap and quantity sense: neutral to informal, capable of grandeur (mounds of treasure) or banality (mounds of laundry).
  • Baseball sense: fully technical and conventional.

In Tolkien, the "mounds of treasure" simultaneously evokes the pile, the burial mound (the dragon sits on the plundered hoard of vanished kingdoms), and the legendary heaps of Germanic gold - the same word used in Beowulf criticism of the dragon's hoard.
