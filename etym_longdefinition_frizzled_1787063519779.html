FRIZZLED is the past participle and participial adjective of the verb frizzle, meaning "to burn or scorch something until its surface shrivels, curls, and tightens into small crisp coils," or, of hair, "to make it curl into a tight, tangled, wiry mass by heat, moisture, or chemical treatment." In the passage from The Hobbit, Bilbo's hair has been singed and frizzled right down to the skin by dragon-fire: the heat has scorched the surface of his skin and curled his hair so completely that it lies crisp and burned flat. The word therefore fuses three simultaneous ideas: intense heat, surface damage, and a distinctive curling, crisped texture.

1. Primary sense — singeing and scorching. The oldest and most persistent core sense is scorching: to frizzle something is to burn its surface so violently that the affected layer tightens, blisters, shrivels, and curls. Applied to hair, wool, or fur, it describes strands shrivelling into tight, crisp, singed coils — precisely the image in Tolkien's sentence, where dragon-fire has left Bilbo's hair burnt to the skin. In this sense the word overlaps with singe but adds the curling, crisping result: a singed sleeve merely smells burned, whereas frizzled hair is actually curled and crusted by the heat.

2. Culinary sense — crisp frying. Since the early nineteenth century the verb has carried a well-established cooking sense: to frizzle (and the passive adjective frizzled) means "to fry something, especially bacon or thin slices of meat, over high heat until its edges curl and become crisp and brown." A pan of frizzled bacon spits, curls, browns, and turns crunchy. Here the heat-damage image of the older sense is converted into a deliberately sought-after texture: the very shrivelling-and-curling motion that ruins hair is what makes bacon deliciously crisp.

3. Hair-styling sense. Parallel to the cooking sense, and even older, runs a hair-dressing sense: frizzle (a frequentative of friz) means "to curl the hair into tight, small, wiry ringlets," and by extension frizzled describes hair that is tightly curled, typically by heated curling irons or by dampness. In this use the emphasis is on the physical result — tight crimped coils — rather than on damage. The modern adjective frizzy and noun frizz grow out of this same family.

4. Secondary and metaphoric senses.

  • Scorched or shrivelled surface (general): anything from a singed sleeve to a burned electrical wire can be called frizzled — the common thread is surface scorching with curling or shrivelling.
  • Exhaustion or burnout (informal, rare): colloquial extensions such as "I'm frizzled" borrow the image of being dried up and withered by heat to mean utterly tired or cooked by the sun, close in spirit to roasted or done in.
  • Withered vegetation: plants burned by drought or frost may be said to be frizzled, their leaves curling and crisping at the edges — the same scorch-and-curl template transferred to botany.
  • Figurative singeing (literary, playful): because singeing is superficial rather than fatal, frizzled can imply a minor but humiliating burning — a character whose dignity has been scorched, not consumed.

5. Register and tone. In register, frizzled is informal, vividly onomatopoeic, and faintly comic: the crisp fizz of frying and the hiss of singeing are built into the sound of the word. Unlike clinical scorched or charred, it carries texture and motion — you can hear and see the curling. This is why Tolkien chose it in a moment of gentle humour: the terrible dragon-attack ends with dwarves doctoring singed skin and the comic image of hair growing back over singed and frizzled skin.

6. The semantic bridge. The thread uniting every sense is the physical template of heat applied to a surface → shrivelling + curling + crisping. The hair-curling sense supplies the curling, the singeing sense supplies the scorching, and the cooking sense unites both in a deliberate, appetising outcome. Once that template is grasped, every use — burnt hair, crisp bacon, shrivelled leaves, a sun-baked walker — is the same picture painted with different palettes.
