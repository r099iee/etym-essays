Hebrew ʾĒlîšeḇaʿ (אֱלִישֶׁבַע) “my God is an oath”, a theophoric Old Testament name borne by Aaron’s wife (Exodus 6:23), built on ʾēl “God” + šeḇaʿ “oath” (the same word means “seven”; the oath was sworn on the sacred number — this duality is itself debated) → Greek Elisábet (Ἐλισάβετ), the Septuagint and New Testament form (mother of John the Baptist, Luke 1:5), with the Hebrew ayin and shin reshaped for Greek phonology → Latin Elisabeth / Elisabetha, the Vulgate form → Old French / Anglo-Norman Elisabeth → Middle English Elizabeth (personal name, attested 12th c.) → English Elizabeth → Elizabeth I (1533–1603, r. 1558–1603), the Virgin Queen → ELIZABETH + -an (adjectival suffix from Latin -ānus “belonging to”) → Elizabethan “of the age of Elizabeth I” (coined by historians and men of letters in the early 19th century, recorded c. 1818). Debate note: the second element is analysed either as “oath” (šāḇaʿ “to swear”, via the idiom of swearing a “seven”) or as the numeral seven directly; modern scholarship favours the oath reading but records the uncertainty.
