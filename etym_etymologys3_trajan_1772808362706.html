The name TRAJAN reaches English not through a chain of sound-changes but through a chain of political memory and onomastic convention. Its linguistic biography begins in uncertainty: Traianus is a Latin cognomen, a family name of the Roman onomastic system, and its ultimate source is not securely recoverable. Several possibilities are discussed by scholars. The name-form Traius (or Traia) resembles a gentilic name of Italic type, and some researchers have suggested connections with Osco-Umbrian or with the local name-stock of the Italian peninsula; others point to the possibility of Etruscan influence on Roman personal names generally. Because the family that bore the name is firmly associated with Italica, a municipium in Hispania Baetica founded in the third century BC by Italic settlers, it is also conceivable that local Iberian or mixed colonial naming habits shaped the form. No reconstruction to a Proto-Indo-European root with a determinate meaning ("to endure," "three," and similar glosses have all been proposed and all remain unsubstantiated) is accepted by modern scholarship. The honest summary is: the pre-Latin origin is opaque. What is fully attested is the morphology. Latin formed relational names with the suffix -ianus: from a base name or place name it creates an adjective meaning "of, belonging to, descended from." Thus Hadrianus means "of Hadria," and Traianus means "of the Traii" or "of the Traia family." The emperor's full style, Marcus Ulpius Traianus, combines the praenomen Marcus, the nomen Ulpius (from the gens Ulpia, of Umbrian origin — Ulpius itself is an Italic gentilic), and the cognomen Traianus, which distinguished this particular branch of the Ulpii. This tripartite structure is standard Roman nomenclature: the praenomen names the individual, the nomen the gens or clan, and the cognomen the family branch within it. The historical man behind the name was born at Italica in AD 53. His father, also Marcus Ulpius Traianus, had been a distinguished senator and governor, serving as proconsul of Asia and perhaps as a commander under Vespasian and Titus. The younger Trajan rose through the senatorial career — praetor, legionary commander in Spain, consul — until his standing as a proven general made him the natural choice of the childless emperor Nerva, who adopted him in AD 97. On Nerva's death the following year Trajan became emperor, the first ruler of the empire to be born outside Italy, in the provinces of Spain. His adoption formalised a transfer of power based on merit and military capability rather than blood, and it inaugurated the sequence of five "adoptive" emperors whose reigns Gibbon celebrates as the happiest period of the empire's history. Once enthroned, Trajan's name attached itself to an extraordinary range of monuments and institutions. The Column of Trajan in Rome, completed in AD 113, preserves in its spiral reliefs the narrative of the Dacian Wars, and the inscription cut in Roman square capitals at its base has become the canonical model of the Roman alphabet. The Forum of Trajan, with its adjacent Basilica Ulpia, Trajan's Market, the Trajanic harbour at Portus, and the Via Traiana in southern Italy all carry his name, so that in archaeology and art history "Trajanic" denotes a specific building style and administrative epoch. The Roman Senate's acclamation of him as Optimus Princeps, "best of princes," remained the only official award of that title in Roman history, and subsequent emperors from Constantine onward were praised as the equals of Trajan, making his name a byword for the ideal ruler in Roman political rhetoric. The name then travelled through languages. Romance and European forms — Italian and Spanish Trajano, Romanian Traian, French and German Trajan — preserve the Latin stem in locally adapted spelling and pronunciation. The Romanian form deserves special attention: because the emperor conquered Dacia, the modern Romanian nation treats Traian as a native heroic given name, a banner of Roman (Latin) identity in a country whose language descends from Latin, and it is used widely as a male personal name and in place-names and institutions. English, following the classical and historical tradition, settled on Trajan, with the stress on the first syllable, /tɹˈadʒən/. In the twentieth century the name made a final, remarkable leap out of history into typography. In 1994 the type designer Carol Twombly released the digital typeface Trajan for Adobe, a capital-letter font based directly on the lettering of the Column of Trajan inscription. The font has become ubiquitous in film posters, book jackets, and display design, so that today a graphic designer who says "set it in Trajan" is using the emperor's name to mean a specific style of Roman capitals. This is a pure case of onomastic-to-technical shift: the proper name of a second-century emperor now designates a font. Gibbon's use of the name, however, stays firmly in the historical register: for him Trajan is the second of the five virtuous rulers, a pillar of the argument that personal character, exercised through public administration, produced the fourscore years of happiness. The etymology of the word thus resolves into two strands — an opaque Latin cognomen of debated origin, and a brilliant, well-documented afterlife in which the name of a Spanish-born soldier-emperor became the label of monuments, a dynasty of good rulers, a national identity in Romania, and finally a font of the Roman alphabet.
