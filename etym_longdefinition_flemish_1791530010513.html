Flemish is the English adjective and noun associated with Flanders (Dutch Vlaanderen), the low-lying coastal territory of the southern Low Countries, and with the West Germanic speech and culture of its inhabitants. As an adjective it means "of, belonging to, or characteristic of Flanders, the Flemings, or their language"; as a noun it names that language and, by extension, the Dutch-speaking population of northern Belgium. The word is at once geographical, linguistic, ethnic and art-historical, and its precise force depends on which of those frames a speaker or writer activates.

Primary sense: the language. In linguistic classification Flemish is not a separate language from Dutch but the southern, Belgian portion of the Low Franconian dialect continuum. It comprises West Flemish (spoken around Bruges, Ypres and Kortrijk), East Flemish (Ghent and the Scheldt valley), and the Brabantian dialects of Antwerp, Brussels and Leuven, with Limburgish varieties shading into the south-east. Standard Dutch (Algemeen Nederlands) remains the official written and educational norm in Belgium, so that "Flemish" usually denotes either the spoken dialects or the distinctive Belgian variety of standard Dutch, often called Belgisch-Nederlands or "Belgian Dutch" in English. Characteristic markers include the uvular /r/, strong final devoicing, the pronouns gij and ge, the diminutive suffix -ke, a stock of archaisms (schoon "beautiful", beenhouwer "butcher"), and a layer of French loanwords (camion, chauffage, facteur). The informal intermediate register known as tussentaal ("in-between language") is a well-studied feature of contemporary Flemish speech.

The people and the region. The Flemings (Vlamingen) are the Dutch-speaking inhabitants of the historical County of Flanders and, more broadly, of the Flemish Region and the bilingual Brussels-Capital Region. The county, first recorded in the ninth century, grew rich in the Middle Ages on the cloth trade of Ghent, Bruges and Ypres, passed through Burgundian, Spanish and Austrian rule, and in 1830 became part of the new Kingdom of Belgium. Because French was long the language of the Belgian elite and administration, the nineteenth-century Flemish Movement campaigned for Dutch in courts, schools and public life; its victories include the 1898 Equality Law, the Dutch-language University of Ghent (1930), the fixing of the Language Border in 1962-63, and the federalisation of Belgium into Communities and Regions from 1970 onwards. "Flemish" therefore also carries a modern institutional and political sense: the Flemish Community and Flemish Region, the Flemish Parliament and Government, and, more contentiously, Flemish-nationalist politics.

Art-historical and cultural sense. In the history of art "Flemish" labels the painting of the southern Netherlands: the fifteenth-century Flemish Primitives (Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden, Hans Memling, Gerard David), the peasant scenes of Pieter Bruegel the Elder, and the Baroque of Rubens, Van Dyck and Jordaens. Auction houses, museums and survey courses still use "Flemish" for this tradition, although the older Netherlandish schools straddle modern Belgium and the Netherlands. The adjective extends to Flemish lace, Flemish linen and wool, Flemish furniture, Flemish gardens, Flemish beer and the beef-and-beer stew known in French as carbonnade flamande.

Technical and specialist senses.

  • Flemish bond: a brickwork pattern in which each course alternates headers (short ends) and stretchers (long sides), prized for strength and decorative contrast; common in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English brickwork.
  • Flemish eye: a rope splice forming a loop, traditionally used in rigging.
  • Flemish coil (or Flemish flake): a rope laid in a flat, decorative spiral on deck.
  • Flemish horse: a short footrope at the outer end of a square-rigged yard, on which a sailor stands.
  • Flemish giant: a large breed of domestic rabbit.
  • Flemish twist: a style of bowstring made from twisted waxed linen or synthetic strands.
  • Flemish Cap: a submarine bank in the North Atlantic east of Newfoundland, famous in fisheries disputes.

Modern, figurative and contested uses. In contemporary English "Flemish" is mostly a neutral descriptor, but it can be politically charged: "Flemish-nationalist", "Flemish separatist" and "Flemish identity" carry debating weight in Belgian politics, while "Flemish" in Brussels can signal language-community affiliation. Advertising and tourism have turned it into a brand ("Flemish Masters", "Flemish style"), and in EU regional statistics it names a NUTS region. The word rarely appears in figurative idioms; its force comes from the concrete contrast with "Walloon" and "Dutch".

Semantic bridges. The path from marshland to nation runs through material culture: the flooded coastal pagi gave the name, the medieval cloth towns gave it wealth and visibility, the county gave it political shape, the language gave it identity, and the painters gave it prestige. Each later sense is a metonymic extension of the earlier one, so that a single adjective can point to a delta, a county, a dialect, a community, a government and a school of painting.
