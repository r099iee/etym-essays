divested — stripped or relieved of clothing, property, possessions, rights, office, or authority; past tense and past participle of "divest." In the literary passage this card draws on — "Only now is the child finally divested of all that he has been" — the word is a small engine of total negation. It is a formal, almost legal verb, and its formality does a specific kind of work: the child is not "stripped" (which would be violent, physical, and sudden) but "divested," a word that suggests a systematic, even ceremonial removal, item by item, of everything that once clothed him — name, origin, family, history, expectation. The adverb "finally" completes the picture: the divesting is an achieved state, the end of a long unwinding, and it is presented with an eerie neutrality as the child's true inheritance. Behind the sentence stands the ancient metaphor that equates the self with its clothing — identity as a garment to be put on and taken off — and "divested" activates that metaphor in full: the boy stands in the world as a body from which every covering has been removed, naked, new, and unclaimed. The word is genuinely polysemous, and its senses descend from a single Latin image of taking clothes off:
  • To unclothe, to strip off garments (original literal sense): to divest someone of their clothes, or reflexively to divest oneself — to undress, to shed one's covering. This is the sense from which all the others radiate, and the reflexive form ("he divested himself of his coat") kept the literal meaning alive longest.
  • To dispossess of property or estate (legal sense): to divest someone of land, goods, or inheritance; to strip a person of what they own by law or by force. The noun divestiture is the legal term for the act of depriving or being deprived of property or rights.
  • To deprive of office, rank, or authority: to divest a monarch of power, an officer of command, a trustee of responsibility; the removal is formal and usually systematic rather than violent.
  • To deprive of rights or powers (civil sense): to divest a class of people of citizenship, suffrage, or legal standing — the word appears in constitutional and civil-rights prose for the stripping of entitlements.
  • To relinquish deliberately (reflexive, figurative): to divest oneself of an identity, a belief, a memory, or a role — to shed, item by item, everything that has constituted one's public or inner self. This is the sense the passage uses: the child is divested of "all that he has been."
  • To sell off assets (modern corporate/financial jargon): since the mid-20th century, "to divest" means to sell or otherwise dispose of a business, holding, or subsidiary; "divestment" and "divestiture" are standard terms in corporate finance, and "to divest" also names the ethical strategy of withdrawing investments from companies in industries such as fossil fuels, tobacco, or arms — the money is "stripped" from the enterprise, in a direct financial extension of the clothing image.
Its synonyms mark out different intensities of loss. To strip is rapid, forcible, and physical — one strips a bed, a thief strips a house, a man is stripped of his rank in humiliation; to deprive is to deny someone something they need or are owed, and it is morally loaded (deprived of liberty, of sight); to dispossess is legal and proprietary, of land and holdings; to denude is technical and mostly used of land or surfaces (a hill denuded of trees); to unclothe is simple and physical, without consequence; to disrobe is ceremonial, of formal garments; to bereave is to lose someone or something through death or severance, carrying grief. "Divest" is set apart from all of them by its combination of formality and totality: it is the systematic, item-by-item removal of everything that covers a person or an entity, performed with the gravity of law or ritual rather than the violence of theft — which is exactly why the passage can use it of a child stripped of an entire identity and still sound hushed and neutral rather than cruel.
