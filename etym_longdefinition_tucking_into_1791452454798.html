Core sense: eating with appetite. Tucking into is the present participle of tuck into, a British phrasal verb meaning to begin eating something eagerly and with visible enjoyment. It belongs to informal speech and carries a warm, slightly comic approval: a person tucking into a plate of food is hungry, unpretentious and happy. The construction is transitive, so one tucks into a meal, a pie, a plate of chips or, in the Wodehouse passage quoted on this card, a patch of lake weed.

Why a duck can tuck into weeds. The image on the card is deliberately absurd. A duck is not usually described as dining, but it is certainly eating, and the phrase lends the bird a faintly human, relish-filled appetite. The humour depends on the register clash: tuck into is a hearty, homely, human idiom, and applying it to a duck grubbing in the shallows makes the bird seem like a greedy guest at a good table. That is exactly the kind of quiet joke at which Wodehouse excelled.

The family of tuck phrasal verbs. The phrase is one member of a small, vigorous cluster:

  • Tuck in: to begin eating, or to eat heartily; also, separately, to settle someone into bed by tucking the covers around them.
  • Tuck into: to attack a particular dish or meal with appetite.
  • Tuck away: to eat a great deal, often secretly or steadily; also to store something in a safe place.
  • Tuck up: to arrange the bedclothes around someone; also, informally, to exhaust or to ruin.
  • Tuck under: to push something beneath something else, as one tucks a napkin under a chin.
  • Tuck in behind: to move closely behind another vehicle or runner, sheltering in its slipstream.

The wider polysemy of tuck. The verb on its own is remarkably productive, and its senses radiate from a core idea of pushing, folding or drawing something into a confined space.

  • To fold or gather fabric: a seamstress tucks a pleat, and a tuck is the flattened fold itself, a tailoring term in use since the fourteenth century.
  • To thrust or push in: to tuck a letter into a pocket, a shirt into trousers, a child into bed.
  • To cover snugly: to tuck someone up against the cold, the sense that gives us the warm domestic image of being "tucked in".
  • To draw the limbs close to the body: a diver tucks, a gymnast performs a tuck, a skydiver goes into a tuck. The body becomes the confined space.
  • To hide or store away: a village tucked away in a valley, savings tucked away for a rainy day.
  • To consume food (noun): British school slang for sweets, cakes and treats, hence tuck shop and tuck box, a boarding-school institution.
  • Cosmetic surgery: a tummy tuck is an abdominoplasty, the skin and fat of the abdomen drawn tight, a twentieth-century extension of the tailoring sense.
  • Closeness or difficulty: nip and tuck describes a contest so close that the rivals are tucked in alongside each other.

The eating sense and its logic. How did a verb for folding cloth and thrusting things into pockets come to mean eating? The bridge is the image of putting food into the body, briskly and with purpose. To tuck into a meal is to stow it away, to pack it in, and the preposition into does the crucial work of direction. The idiom belongs with a whole English family of eating verbs built on insertion or disposal: put away, get stuck into, polish off, stow, pack away. All of them treat food as something to be dealt with rather than savoured, and all of them sound hearty rather than refined.

Register and regional distribution. Tuck into is marked as British and as informal. It is at home in conversation, in comic fiction, in cookery writing with a cosy tone, and in journalism that wants to sound human. It is not the language of menus, medical reports or formal banquets. Its American counterparts are dig into, dig in, go to town on and chow down on, though tuck into is widely understood in North America as a Britishism and is sometimes used self-consciously for flavour.

Grammar and aspect. The phrase is used in the progressive precisely because it names an activity in progress: one is tucking into a meal while one is eating it. The simple past tucked into reports a completed episode of hearty eating, and the imperative tuck in! is a cheerful invitation to start. The participle on this card is therefore the natural form for a narrative description of an ongoing meal, which is exactly how Wodehouse uses it.

The semantic bridge. The unifying idea is insertion into a containing space: cloth into a fold, a letter into a pocket, a child into bed, limbs into a compact shape, and finally food into a hungry body. The preposition into makes the direction explicit, and the appetite sense is simply the most bodily member of the family. The word's warmth comes from that physicality: it treats eating as a robust, practical, slightly greedy act of stowing things away.
