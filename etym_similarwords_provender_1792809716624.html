• Fodder: Similar because both name bulk feed for animals; differs because fodder is specifically coarse animal feed (hay, straw) and figuratively means expendable material (cannon fodder), while provender is the wider laid-in store, fit for men as well as beasts.
  • Forage: Similar because both feed horses and armies on campaign; differs because forage stresses food found or stripped in the field (foraging parties, hunter-gatherers), while provender stresses food fetched and stored beforehand — finding versus providing.
  • Provisions: Similar because both are stores laid in against future need; differs because provisions is the plain modern word (ship’s provisions, forthcoming provisions), while provender is its archaic country cousin, smelling of stables and campaigns.
  • Rations: Similar because the oldest provender was literally a measured daily allowance; differs because rations stresses strict measured shares in scarcity (wartime rations), while provender suggests hearty sufficiency — the full manger, not the weighed ounce.
  • Feed: Similar because winter provender is exactly animal feed bought in bulk; differs because feed is the blunt farm word (chicken feed, cattle feed), while provender dresses the same hay in doublet and hose.
  • Fare: Similar because country provender and country fare both mean plain hearty eating; differs because fare stresses what is served at table (passengers’ fare, festival fare), while provender stresses what is stored beforehand — the barn behind the banquet.
  • Victuals (vittles): Similar because both are archaic hearty words for food laid in; differs because victuals belongs to ships, sieges, and Shakespeare (salt victuals), while provender belongs to stables, farms, and the Shire.
  • Sustenance: Similar because both name what keeps bodies (and minds) alive; differs because sustenance is abstract and solemn (bare sustenance, spiritual sustenance), while provender is concrete and jolly — sacks you can hoist, not concepts you contemplate.
  • Prebend: Similar because it is provender’s own twin, from the same Latin allowance; differs because a prebend is a cathedral clergyman’s stipend and estate, paid in coin and dignity, while provender stayed in the stable-yard, paid in oats.
