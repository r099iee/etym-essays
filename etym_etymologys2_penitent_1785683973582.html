PENITENT descends from the Latin verb paenitēre, "to regret, repent," whose own deeper ancestry is genuinely uncertain. The certain, linear Roman-to-English chain is given first; the contested PIE hypotheses are appended as a clearly marked note. CHAIN 1 — the certain Latin-to-English line: 1) Latin paenitēre "to cause regret; to regret, repent" (attested from the classical period; a verb of uncertain origin — no universally accepted PIE etymon; the two rival hypotheses are discussed in the note below). 2) Latin paenitēns, paenitent-em "regretting, repenting" (present participle of paenitēre; attested) — classical Latin. 3) Late Latin paenitentia "repentance, penance" (abstract noun from paenitēns; attested in Christian Latin, where the church gave the word its sacramental sense). 4) Old French penitent "repenting; a person doing penance" (borrowed from the Latin participle, transmitted through the vocabulary of the medieval church) — c. 10th–12th c. 5) Middle English penitent "penitent, repentant" (borrowed from Old French) — c. 1340. 6) Modern English penitent — PENITENT (adjective "feeling sorrow for sin and resolving to amend"; noun "a person performing penance"). NOTE ON DEBATE — the uncertain PIE ancestry of paenitēre: no single origin is established. Two rival hypotheses exist. - Hypothesis A (the poena link): Latin paenitēre is sometimes connected with Latin poena "punishment, penalty," which descends from Greek poinē "recompense, blood-money, penalty," from PIE *kʷoinā- "payment, recompense, price" (the root also of English fine and fee through Latin and Germanic channels). The underlying picture would be the regret of a debtor who must pay, or of an offender who owes satisfaction — "repentance" as the sorrow of one who owes recompense. The formal connection is unproven and is treated with caution by modern authorities. - Hypothesis B (the paene link): a rival account derives paenitēre from Latin paene, "scarcely, almost" (of uncertain origin), the sense being "to feel one has 'almost not' done something, to come short, to repent" — the regret of one who falls just short. This too is unproven. The honest verdict is that paenitēre stands as a Latin root of uncertain PIE ancestry: whatever its ultimate source, its living history begins in Rome, and the chain from Latin paenitēns to English penitent is secure. The related Latin poena does NOT demonstrably lie behind penitent, and the two words must not be silently merged.
