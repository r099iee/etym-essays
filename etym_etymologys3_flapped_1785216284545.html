Flapped belongs to a large, thoroughly Germanic, sound-symbolic family whose members share the consonantal frame f-l- and imitate a quick, flat, loose motion: flap, flip, flab, flabby, flipper, fluster. There is no Proto-Indo-European ancestor to reconstruct; scholars describe the base *flapp- as onomatopoeic or 'expressive,' a word coined, as it were, from the noise of a flat thing striking a flat thing. The same base surfaces independently in the neighbouring Germanic dialects — Middle Dutch flappen 'to strike,' Middle Low German flappen — a distribution that shows the word was native to Germanic from an early stage even though it cannot be traced to PIE. The family's shared phonetics are the clue to its origin: the initial fl- and the short plosive in -pp- mime a brief, smacking, flat impact, and the same template reappears across the whole group whatever the vowel (flip, flap, flop).

English enters the written record in the mid-fourteenth century. Middle English flappen meant 'to strike or slap,' a transitive verb of blunt physical force, and its earliest nominal use, 'a blow or slap,' follows within a generation; the word carried a rough, smacking, onomatopoeic energy. The great semantic shift came in the sixteenth century, when the verb moved from the effect (the blow) to the instrument (the wing): to flap came to mean 'to beat the wings up and down,' and then, soon after, to flap one's arms. The violence drained out of the word; it became loose, repetitious, purposeless — the labour of a bird beating air, and of a person beating the air in imitation of a bird.

From the wing, the modern cluster of senses unfolded.

  • The broad piece: a flap is a broad flat thing attached at one edge and free at the other — a pocket flap, a tent flap, an earflap — first recorded in the sixteenth century. The idea is the same wing: a plane that flexes at a pivot.
  • The loose flutter: the intransitive sense 'to flutter or swing loosely in the wind' appears in the eighteenth century, of sails, flags, and loose clothing; the word now names aimless repeated motion rather than force, and any trace of purpose drains away.
  • The flapper: a flapper (eighteenth century) was a young bird learning to fly, or a broad flat object; in the 1920s the word became the defining slang term for a fashionable, restless modern young woman — the noun for a thing that flaps, transferred to a generation that flapped its arms and danced its way through the Jazz Age.
  • Technical transfers: in aeronautics an aircraft flap is the hinged wing surface that alters lift; in surgery a skin flap is living tissue moved to repair a wound; in printing a flap is a loose cover leaf. Every transfer keeps the broad-flat-piece-at-a-pivot image alive in professional registers.
  • The panic: in the twentieth century, informal British English turned the physical flapping inward: to 'flap' or to be 'in a flap' is to be in a state of agitated panic — 'don't flap!' — the loose, helpless beating of a wing become mental fluster, the same loss of control now emotional rather than physical.

The context sentence uses the past tense Flapped in the wing-sense but transfers it back to a human hand: 'He flapped his hand in the dark.' The historical trajectory — from a hard blow, to a wing-beat, to a loose, helpless, repeated sweep, to nervous agitation — is what makes the sentence work. The hand does not strike anything; it beats the empty air in short flat sweeps, like a bird's wing against nothing, all effort and no result. The word has travelled from force to futility, and in the dark tunnel every stage of that journey is present at once: a blow that never lands, a wing that finds no air to press, a panic that cannot find its way out. That is the deep reason flapped, and not waved or groped, is the perfect verb for a hand searching blindly where there is nothing to find.
