• Mele: Similar because both constitute the foundational core of traditional Hawaiian performing arts and oral literature; differs because mele specifically denotes the poetic vocal chant or sung text, whereas hula is the physical dance, movement, and bodily gesture that visually interprets the mele.
  • Ori Tahiti: Similar because both are indigenous East Polynesian dance traditions sharing common ancestral roots, percussive accompaniment, and narrative hand gestures; differs because Ori Tahiti (Tahitian dance) emphasizes rapid, vigorous, percussive pelvic revolutions (tamau and faʻarapu) driven by thundering toʻere drums, whereas traditional hula emphasizes rooted footwork, torso poise, and subtle, literal gestural translation of chanted poetry.
  • Siva: Similar because both are indigenous Polynesian narrative dance forms that honor ancestral heritage, chiefs, and social history; differs because Siva (the traditional dance of Samoa) features distinct fluid wrist rotations, gentle walking paces, and strict etiquette tied to the faʻa Samoa (Samoan social hierarchy), lacking the explicit Hawaiian sacred dedication to forest and volcanic deities like Laka and Pele.
  • Haka: Similar because both are indigenous Polynesian embodied movement traditions transmitting ancestral power and cultural identity through intense physical presence; differs because the Māori haka is predominantly a vigorous, postural challenge, war cry, or communal social proclamation marked by heavy foot-stamping, eye-rolling, tongue protrusion, and chest-slapping, whereas hula centers on melodic narrative storytelling and expressive lyrical gestures.
  • Choreography: Similar because both denote structured, intentional sequences of human body movement designed for expressive or aesthetic performance; differs because choreography is a broad, universal Western concept referring to dance composition across all styles and genres, entirely detached from the sacred liturgy, genealogical records, and linguistic chanting inseparable from authentic hula.
