The evolution of will reflects one of the most significant semantic and grammatical trajectories in the Germanic language family, bridging ancient Indo-European concepts of human desire with profound philosophical debates, Anglo-Saxon legal institutions, and English modal syntax.

1. Proto-Indo-European Roots and Cognate Network:
The word derives from the Proto-Indo-European root *welh₁- (or *wel-), signifying "to choose, want, wish, desire, or please." This fecund root generated a vast network of cognates across Indo-European branches:

  • Latin: *velle* ("to wish, want"), *voluntas* ("will, free will, choice"), and *voluptas* ("pleasure"), which entered English through French borrowings such as *volition*, *voluntary*, and *benevolent*.
  • Sanskrit: *vṛṇīte* ("chooses, prefers") and *vara* ("wish, choice, boon").
  • Greek: *elpis* ("hope, expectation") and *boulesthai* ("to want, will").
  • Germanic: Gothic *wiljan*, Old Norse *vilja*, Old High German *willio* / *wollan*, and modern German *Wille* and *wollen*.

2. Old English Dual Inheritance: Noun and Athematic Verb:
In Anglo-Saxon England, the root developed along two parallel grammatical tracks:

  • The Weak Noun willa: Denoted personal wish, desire, pleasure, joy, or purpose. In religious texts like the Old English translation of the Lord's Prayer, *Geweorðe þīn willa* translated *Fiat voluntas tua* ("Thy will be done").
  • The Optative Verb willan: Conjugated as an irregular, athematic preterite-present/optative verb expressing subjective desire, volition, and willingness (e.g., *Ic wille* = "I wish / I desire"). It did not serve as a neutral future marker; future events were expressed using the present tense or with *sculan* ("shall", originally expressing debt or moral obligation).

3. Middle English Grammaticalization and Legal Development:
During the Middle English period (1150–1500), two transformative developments occurred:

  • Auxiliary Future Marking: The verb *willen* underwent grammaticalization, shedding its strict requirement of active personal desire to become a generalized marker of future intention and prediction, gradually supplanting *shall* across various dialects.
  • The Legal 'Last Will': In medieval English probate law, *wille* acquired a precise legal definition as a written document governing the posthumous demise of real estate (land), while the Latinate *testament* governed personal goods (chattels). By the 16th century, English legal practice combined them into the standard doublet *last will and testament*.

4. Philosophical Conceptualization and Modern Usage:
During the Enlightenment and 19th-century philosophy, *will* was elevated into a fundamental metaphysical concept:

  • Filial Defiance in Literature: In Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* (1719), Crusoe's admission that his maritime wanderlust led him "strongly against the will, nay, the commands of my father" represents the classical early modern clash between individual self-will and patriarchal moral duty.
  • 19th-Century Philosophy: Arthur Schopenhauer positioned *Wille* as the blind, striving metaphysical essence of the universe (*Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung*, 1819), which Friedrich Nietzsche reframed into the *Wille zur Macht* ("Will to Power").
  • Contemporary Science: In 21st-century cognitive psychology and neuroscience, *will* is analyzed through executive function, ego depletion, self-control, and the neurological mechanisms of decision-making.
