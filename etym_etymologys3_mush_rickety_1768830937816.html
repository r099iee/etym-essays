Part I — THE EVOLUTION OF MUSH The history of mush is the story of a plain American breakfast food that grew into a universal metaphor for shapelessness — and then for shapeless sentiment. A dialectal variant of "mash," it crossed the Atlantic with the settlers of North America, became the frontier's daily porridge, and was finally repurposed by critics and romantics alike as a word for anything soft enough to lose its form. 1. Old English origins: mashing. The deep ancestor is Old English māscan, "to crush, pound, or mix into a soft pulp," a verb of the pre-Conquest vocabulary of the kitchen and the brewery. The related noun māsc named the pulpy mixture of malt and hot water from which beer is made — the mash-tun is still the brewer's term for the vessel in which grain is steeped. This dual life (kitchen food and brewhouse mixture) shaped the word's future: mash always meant a pulpy mass produced by crushing or steeping. The ultimate origin of the Germanic root is uncertain — no secure Indo-European ancestor is agreed upon, though a connection with PIE *mag- "to knead" (Greek mássein, "to knead," whence English "mass," "masticate") is sometimes proposed. The word may simply be imitative of the sound and motion of crushing.
  • Sound shift: Old English māscan passed into Middle English as mashen/masken, the vowel shortening and the cluster simplifying, to give the modern verb and noun mash.
  • Semantic constant: through every stage the core is the same — pulpy matter created by crushing, pressing, or steeping.
2. The American adventure: from mash to mush. In the 17th century the word mush appears in English as a dialectal variant of mash — a form in which the -sh had become voiced in some regional speech. The form was carried to colonial North America, where it attached itself to the one grain the New World produced in abundance: maize. Cornmeal boiled in salted water or milk became mush, the frontier's daily porridge, and "fried mush" (chilled slices fried in fat) survives today in Southern and Appalachian cooking. Some scholars see a second, reinforcing source in the German Mus ("soft food, porridge") brought by Pennsylvania Dutch settlers — a plausible but unproven confluence. From 1670 onward, American usage made mush the ordinary name of the cornmeal porridge. 3. The semantic widening: all shapelessness. By the 19th century mush had outgrown the breakfast bowl. It came to name any soft, pulpy, shapeless mass — snow mush, leaf mush, "a mush of" anything that had surrendered its structure to wetness and decay. The adjective mushy followed, meaning soft and yielding to the touch. This widening is the word's central drift: from a specific food to a general state of matter. A thing that is mushy has lost the internal tension that holds a shape; it yields, flows, stains, and cannot stand. 4. The figurative collapse: mush as sentiment. In the 20th century the word made its last great transfer — from the texture of matter to the texture of feeling. "Mush" came to mean mawkish, cloying sentimentality, and "mushy" to describe romance that is soft, sweet, and undemanding. The bridge is exact: as mushy matter is soft and shapeless to the hand, so mushy feeling is soft and formless to the mind — emotion without structure, without edge. "Mush" now runs the full semantic gamut from the frontier breakfast to the sentimental novel, a single word spanning food, physics, and feeling. Part II — THE EVOLUTION OF RICKETY The history of rickety is a story of a childhood disease becoming a universal adjective for structural weakness. From the ancient Greek word for the spine, through the medical Latin of Renaissance Europe, to the English disease-name and finally the everyday adjective of wobbly chairs, the word has travelled from the bones of sick children to every shaky frame in the language. 1. Greek beginnings: the spine. The chain begins with Ancient Greek rháchis, "spine, backbone, ridge" — a word of the body's central axis, attested from the classical period. Greek medicine, always fond of -itis disease names (appendicitis, tonsillitis), formed rhachîtis for a disease of the spine. By the time of Galen (2nd century CE), rhachîtis named a condition of spinal and bone disease in children — the disease we now call rickets. The Greek term passed into medieval and Renaissance learned Latin as rhachitis / rachitis, the scientific name of the disease. 2. The English problem: rickets. When the disease-name entered English in the 17th century (c. 1630s), it took the form rickets — and here the history becomes genuinely difficult. The expected English adaptation of rachitis would be something like "rachitis" or "raketis." Instead the word appears as rickets, with the distinctly English rick- spelling. Two explanations compete: (1) the medical word was contaminated by native English words — "wricket" (to wriggle), or the "rick" of "rickets" being influenced by words for twisting or stiffness; or (2) the disease had an independent popular name in English from which "rickets" grew, to which the learned rhachitis was only later attached. The OED remains cautious; the exact pathway is genuinely uncertain. What is certain is the outcome: by the late 17th century "rickets" was the standard English name of the childhood disease of softened, bowed bones, caused (as we now know) by vitamin D deficiency.
  • Medical meaning: in the 18th century "rickets" and "rickety" belonged to medicine and to the pathos of the sickly child — the bowed legs, the enlarged joints, the tottering frame of the rachitic child were a familiar sight in the slums of industrializing Europe.
  • Moral coloring: the rickety child was pitied, and the word carried a faint overtone of weakness and helplessness from the first.
3. The great generalization: from bones to buildings. In the 19th century the adjective rickety escaped the sickroom. It came to describe anything weak, loose-jointed, and liable to collapse: a rickety old wooden chair, a rickety staircase, a rickety cart, a rickety shed. The transfer is vivid and physical: the rickety object wobbles like a child with bowed legs; its joints are loose and its frame unsound; it stands only by luck and will give way at the first real pressure. This is the sense in Vance's sentence — the creature's rickety frame is a body whose structure can barely hold itself together, as fragile as a wobbly chair. 4. The figurative reach. In modern English rickety extends by metaphor to anything unsound: a rickety argument, a rickety economy, a rickety organization — structures that are about to collapse under strain. The word has completed the arc from Greek spine, to medieval disease, to Victorian chair, to modern metaphor: from the bones of the body to the bones of any structure, and finally to the unsoundness of institutions themselves. It is a textbook case of a medical term generalizing into everyday structural language.
