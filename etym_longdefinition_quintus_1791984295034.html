Quintus is a Latin masculine praenomen — the personal name that stood first in a Roman citizen's three names — and it means “fifth”. It is not a common noun in English and has no plural; it survives as a proper name, a historical label and a schoolroom Latin word. In the source passage it denotes Quintus Tullius Cicero (c. 102 – 43 BCE), the younger brother of Marcus Tullius Cicero, who accompanied him on the study tour of Greece and Asia Minor in 79–77 BCE described in the surrounding text.

Roman names were conservative and formulaic. A freeborn male citizen normally had a praenomen, a nomen (the clan name, here Tullius) and a cognomen (the branch name, here Cicero), and the praenomen was drawn from a short inherited list: Gaius, Lucius, Marcus, Publius, Quintus, Sextus, Titus, Gnaeus, Aulus, Decimus and a handful more. In inscriptions the name is abbreviated Q., and a Roman reading a milestone or a dedication would have recognised the letter instantly.

The ordinal praenomina

  • Meaning “fifth” — Quintus is the ordinal of quīnque “five”, exactly as septimus is the ordinal of septem. The name belongs to a group of numerical praenomina — Primus, Secundus, Tertius, Quartus, Quintus, Sextus, Decimus — which once recorded the order of birth.
  • The meaning had worn off — by the late Republic the ordinals were inherited labels, not birth certificates. A Quintus need not have been a fifth son, any more than a Sextus was a sixth; families repeated favourite praenomina for generations, and the “number” was often purely traditional.
  • Feminine and derivative forms — Quinta is the feminine, and the diminutive Quintinus produced the later saint's name Quentin. The adjective quīntus also underlies a whole family of English words: quint, quintet, quintuple, quintile, quintessence.
  • Calendar and camp — Quīntīlis was the fifth month of the old Roman year, later renamed July for Julius Caesar; quīntāna was a street in the Roman camp and survives in the English “quintain”, a jousting target.

The bearer: Quintus Tullius Cicero

Quintus was about four years younger than his brother Marcus, and the two were educated together in Rome and later in Greece and Asia. He married Pomponia, the sister of Cicero's closest friend Titus Pomponius Atticus, a match that was meant to bind the families and instead produced a famously sour marriage. He was praetor in 62 BCE, the year of Cicero's consulship and the Catilinarian crisis, and then governed the province of Asia for three years (61–59 BCE); his brother's letters to him during that governorship are a running commentary on provincial extortion, tax-collecting and the temptations of absolute power.

  • Soldier — from 54 to 52 BCE Quintus served as a legate under Julius Caesar in Gaul. He held a winter camp in the territory of the Nervii against a siege and is mentioned by Caesar in the Bellum Gallicum; the episode is one of the few places where a Cicero other than Marcus appears in a first-hand military narrative.
  • Writer — he composed tragedies (Cicero reports that he dashed off four in sixteen days) and a short handbook on electioneering, the Commentariolum Petitionis, addressed to Marcus for the consular canvass of 64 BCE. Its authorship is disputed: some scholars assign it to a later rhetorician, but it is transmitted with Cicero's works and is a vivid picture of Roman electioneering — names, favours, crowds, flattery.
  • Brother — the surviving correspondence includes three books of letters from Marcus to Quintus and a number from Quintus to Marcus. They show affection, irritation, shared literary jokes and the strain of a family alliance that had gone wrong.
  • Death — in the proscriptions of 43 BCE, after Caesar's assassination, Quintus and his son were hunted down and killed. The sources differ in detail; the stark fact is that the brother of the man who had denounced Antony died at the hands of Antony's regime.

Why the name matters here

The source is a life of Cicero, and “his brother Quintus” is the standard way of identifying the companion of the Eastern journey. The tour of 79–77 BCE was undertaken partly for Cicero's health — the “ailing throat and lungs” of the next clause are his — and partly for the rhetorical training that would make him the leading advocate of Rome. Quintus went with him, studied with him, and remained his closest, most difficult ally for the rest of his life.

Register and modern use

The word is a proper name, capitalised and invariant. In English it is chiefly a classical or literary given name, rare but recognisable; it appears in historical fiction (Quintus Arrius in Ben-Hur), in Latin textbooks (the Cambridge Latin Course's Quintus Caecilius Iucundus) and in scholarly references to the Tullii Cicerones. Its ordinal meaning is invisible to most modern readers and has to be explained.
