uppermost in the source passage — from Laurence Sterne's Tristram Shandy: “...and, for aught they knew to the contrary, even the fortunes of his whole house might take their turn from the humours and dispositions which were then uppermost; — Had they duly weighed and considered all this, and proceeded accordingly, — I am verily persuaded I should have made a quite different figure in the world...” — functions as a predicate adjective meaning prevailing, predominant, or exercising supreme and ascendant influence. Sterne here draws directly upon the medical and psychological doctrine of the four bodily humours (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) and temporary mental dispositions, where whichever humour or passion was floating to the surface and riding "uppermost" at the moment of conception would permanently imprint the child's temperament, physical health, and destiny.

In general modern English, uppermost (and its occasional adverbial use) describes that which occupies the highest physical position, the highest rank of authority, or the primary place in one's conscious mind and attention. It is a compound formed historically from the comparative adjective upper combined with the superlative suffix -most (an etymological double superlative). Unlike purely spatial terms such as top or highest, uppermost frequently conveys dynamic psychological prominence, competitive supremacy, or structural layering.

The polysemy of uppermost unfolds across several distinct literal and figurative applications:

  • Psychological and Cognitive Preoccupation (Primary Modern Sense): Highest in one's conscious thoughts, priorities, or emotional focus; occupying the foremost place in the mind. Common collocations include uppermost in one's mind, uppermost in one's thoughts, uppermost in public concern. For example: "The safety of the trapped miners was uppermost in the rescue team's minds." Here, mental attention is conceived as a stratified container where the most pressing concern floats to the top surface.
  • Physical Spatial Elevation and Layering (Literal Sense): Situated in the highest physical position or situated at the very top surface of a layered structure. Examples include the uppermost branches of the oak, the uppermost deck of the ship, the uppermost strata of sedimentary rock, the uppermost shelf of the bookcase. It implies a series of lower tiers or layers beneath it.
  • Historical Humoral and Physiological Ascendancy (Source Sense): In early modern medicine and humoral pathology, describing the particular bodily fluid, passion, or mental disposition that rises to predominance in the organism, suppressing competing humours and governing physiological and psychological behavior.
  • Political, Social, and Competitive Dominance: Holding supreme power, ruling authority, or emerging victorious in a violent struggle or political rivalry. Used in phrases like "to come out uppermost" or "to remain uppermost in the factional conflict." This sense emphasizes the competitive ascent over rivals, emerging at the summit of power.
  • Geological and Structural Stratification: In geology and archaeology, designating the latest, topmost sedimentary horizon or cultural stratum (e.g., "the uppermost geological layer dating to the Holocene epoch"), which by the law of superposition represents the most recent deposit.
  • Metaphorical and Idiomatic Applications: In ethical decision-making, describing the principle or duty that takes absolute precedence over all competing obligations (e.g., "duty to the public must remain uppermost").

Stylistically, uppermost is a refined, standard literary and formal term. It avoids the abruptness of monosyllabic top while offering greater spatial and psychological precision than general adjectives like important or highest. Its versatility in bridging physical altitude and internal cognitive priority makes it an enduring staple of English descriptive prose.
