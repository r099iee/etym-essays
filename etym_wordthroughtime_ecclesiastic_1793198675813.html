- Classical Athens — the citizen assembly: ekklesia is the sovereign body of summoned citizens voting on war and peace, purely civic, nothing churchly yet.
- Septuagint and New Testament — the summoned people of God: translators and apostles take the civic word for the congregation of the faithful, and the assembly becomes the Church.
- Patristic and medieval centuries — the institution: ecclesiasticus now means of Holy Church — her clergy, courts, and councils — set against the lay and secular powers of pope against emperor.
- Reformation — the contested word: reformers wield ecclesiastic abuses against Rome while defenders exalt ecclesiastic authority, so the adjective learns both pride and polemic.
- Seventeenth-century Russia — the monopoly of the letter: Church Slavonic and ecclesiastic purposes own all writing, leaving the fairy tale to grow wild and oral outside the cloister wall.
- Enlightenment and after — the anticlerical edge: philosophers speak of ecclesiastic privilege and censorship, and the word picks up the chill of the critic alongside the warmth of the believer.
- Today — the historian's adjective: neutral, bookish, precise — ecclesiastic courts, ecclesiastic patronage — with the noun kept for period flavour among bishops and synods.
