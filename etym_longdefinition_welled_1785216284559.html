welled is the simple past tense and the past participle of the intransitive verb to well, in modern English almost always with the particle up. It means to rise to the surface of its own accord from a hidden depth: water welling out of a spring in the earth, tears welling in the eyes, and — by the extension that gives the word its literary power — feelings welling up in the heart. The root sense is of something rising unbidden, under its own gentle pressure, from below; nothing forces it, nothing pushes it from above, it simply comes up because there is more of it than the container can hold. That is the exact nuance Tolkien requires.

In the literary context — “A sudden understanding, a pity mixed with horror, welled up in Bilbo's heart: a glimpse of endless unmarked days without light or hope of betterment, hard stone, cold fish, sneaking and whispering” — the verb describes an emotion that rises involuntarily, like groundwater seeping up through the soil: not a deliberate act but a flooding of the heart from beneath, sudden and yet gradual in its swelling. The nuance is of something elemental, unstoppable, and pure: the pity wells up, filling Bilbo's whole chest before he can think or act. The tone is quiet, tender, and awed — Tolkien, a professional philologist, chooses the most natural, water-simple word available, so that the reader feels the emotion as a physical upwelling rather than an intellectual conclusion. Unlike gushing or surging, welling has no violence and no force beyond its own steady pressure; unlike trickling, it is not feeble but abundant; unlike flooding, it does not overwhelm destructively but fills generously. It is the language of springs, fountains, and overflowing vessels, and in this sentence it makes the sudden understanding itself rise like living water from Bilbo's depths.

Polysemy & Secondary Meanings — the verb has a small but important family of senses, historically layered:

  • Rise of water from the earth (the literal core, Old English onwards) — water wells up from a spring, a fountain, or an underground source; the noun well itself names the spring, the shaft sunk to reach it, and, by extension, the oil well and the borehole. This is the sense on which all the others rest, and it is still fully alive: “Water welled up from the chalk.”
  • Tears and bodily fluids (16th c. onwards) — tears well in the eyes (recorded from the sixteenth century), blood wells from a wound; the image is of liquid rising to the rim and gathering there under steady internal pressure. “Tears welled in her eyes.”
  • Emotional and mental upwelling (18th–19th c. onwards) — pity, gratitude, understanding, anger, and memory well up in the heart or mind. This is the metaphorical transfer that dominates modern literary use: feelings are imagined as a hidden reservoir of water inside a person, rising on its own when a seal gives way. “A feeling of deep gratitude welled in his heart.”
  • Nautical and technical senses — water wells in the bilges or hull of a ship (the well being the compartment where it collects); in engineering, liquid wells up through a borehole. These are extensions of the spring-sense to contained or artificial channels.
  • Transitive and reflexive extensions — chiefly poetic or rare, to well something up is to cause it to rise, as when “the music welled up through the hall”; the transitive use “to well tears” is marginal but attested.
  • Idiomatic and phrasal use — well up is nearly the only phrasal form; well over describes liquid rising until it spills, and the noun in compounds like wellspring extends the image to a person, an idea, or an institution that is the source of life-giving abundance.


Among near-synonyms, each shades differently:

  • gush: similar in liquid rising or pouring out abundantly; differs because gushing is a violent, spurting outflow under pressure, whereas welling is a steady, gentle upwelling with no force.
  • surge: similar in a powerful upward rise; differs because a surge is a great wave-like heave of force, while welling rises softly of its own accord.
  • flood: similar in abundant outpouring; differs because flooding overwhelms and swamps its container, while welling fills generously without destructive excess.
  • brim: similar in liquid approaching a rim; differs because brimming only reaches the edge and stays level, whereas welling actively rises from below and can overflow.
  • seep: similar in slow movement through a substance; differs because seeping is thin, slow passage through porous matter, lacking welling's abundance and fullness.
  • trickle: similar in gentle flowing; differs because a trickle is feeble and meagre, whereas welling is steady and full-bodied.
  • emerge: similar in coming into view or existence; differs because emergence is neutral and intellectual, with none of welling's liquid, involuntary, elemental pressure.


In register, well up is quiet and literary but not archaic; it sits comfortably in fiction, biography, and elevated journalism, and is equally at home in speech of tears rising unbidden. It is the deliberate opposite of the violent water-verbs, and its gentleness is precisely what lets Tolkien dignify the pity without melodrama.
