AUGUSTUS: AN EVOLUTION ESSAY FROM AN INDO-EUROPEAN ROOT TO A RELIGIOUS ADJECTIVE The story of "Augustus" begins with PIE *h2ewg-, the root meaning "to increase, to grow, to make flourish". In Latin this root produced the verb "augeo, augere" ("to increase"), and a large family of words for authority: "auctor" ("one who promotes or originates", hence "author"), "auctoritas" ("influence, authority"), and "augur" ("a priest who interprets the will of the gods"). The adjective "augustus" is built on this root with the suffix -tus, and its core meaning is "endowed with increase", i.e. "blessed, made to flourish, consecrated". Already in the second and first centuries BC Latin used "augustus" of sacred objects and places - things hallowed by divine favour. This religious valence is the key to everything that follows: the word meant, not "political leader", but "holy, majestic, increased by heaven". THE POLITICAL CRISIS AND THE COINAGE OF A TITLE When Octavian defeated Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC, he faced a delicate constitutional problem. He was master of the Roman world but had no desire to be called "rex" (king), a word hateful to republican ears, and he was equally wary of "dictator", the title that had helped destroy Julius Caesar. The solution, engineered in the Senate settlement of January 27 BC on the motion of Lucius Munatius Plancus, was to give him a name rather than a job. The Senate formally conferred the cognomen "Augustus", drawing on the sacred adjective: Octavian would be "the Venerable One", "the Consecrated One", a man whose pre-eminence was presented as a divine augmentation rather than a political office. The word was so designed that it could not be translated as any offensive republican term, and it bound religion to politics: Augustus's authority was "auctoritas", his person "augustus", his power a gift of the gods. The title succeeded brilliantly; the emperor was now simultaneously princeps ("first citizen"), Caesar (heir of the dynasty), and Augustus (sacred, majestic, divinely increased). THE TITLE BECOMES AN INSTITUTION From 27 BC onward "Augustus" was not a personal nickname but a hereditary, dynastic, and institutional title. Every subsequent Roman emperor bore it, often jointly with a "Caesar" (the junior or designated successor); empresses took the feminine "Augusta". The title spread across the empire: in the Greek East it was rendered "Sebastos" ("venerable"), and the month Sextilis was renamed Augustus in the emperor's honour (our "August"). The cult of the living emperor, encouraged by the Augustan settlement, gave the word a religious charge throughout the Roman world, and even after the fall of the western empire the Byzantine emperors continued to be styled with forms of the name for centuries. THE WORD MOVES INTO ENGLISH English encountered "Augustus" through Latin historical literature. In Tudor and Stuart translations of Livy, Suetonius, Tacitus, and Dio, "Augustus" entered as a proper name for the first emperor, and the sixteenth-century histories of Rome - including the Roman material in Shakespeare's Antony and Cleopatra and Cymbeline - fixed it in English historical vocabulary. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word had also generated a cluster of derivatives: the adjective "Augustan" for the emperor's age and its literature, and the common adjective "august" for anything majestic and awe-inspiring. When Edward Gibbon wrote The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire in 1776, "Augustus" was a familiar name with two centuries of English usage behind it, and his opening sentence could rely on the reader's recognising both the man and the meaning of the title: the figure who ended the Republic's "rapid succession of triumphs" and instituted "a spirit of moderation". THE AUGUSTAN AGE AND METAPHORIC EXTENSIONS The word's most important cultural extension is "Augustan". Because the reign of Augustus was the golden age of Latin literature - Vergil's Aeneid, Horace's Odes, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Livy's History - "Augustan" came to mean "characteristic of a great literary golden age". English critics of the early eighteenth century, celebrating the age of Pope and Swift, called it the "Augustan Age" of English letters, and the term remains in use for any period of classical polish and cultural flowering. From this, "Augustus" and "Augustan" take on a metaphorical life in political and cultural commentary: an "Augustus" is any ruler or reformer who consolidates, moderates, and ends destructive expansion - precisely the role Gibbon assigns to the historical Augustus in the source passage. The word has thus travelled from a PIE root of growth and increase, through a Roman religious title, to a name for the first emperor, a label for a literary golden age, and a general metaphor for wise consolidation. PRONUNCIATION, FORM, AND MODERN USE In English the name is pronounced with stress on the second syllable, matching the Latin "Au-GUS-tus", while the derived adjective "august" moves the stress to the first syllable. As a given name "Augustus" has remained in continuous use in the English-speaking world (often shortened to "Gus" or "Augie"), and it is a standard stock name for dignified or pompous fictional characters.
  • PIE *h2ewg- "to increase" underlies the whole Latin family (augeo, augur, auctor).
  • 2nd-1st c. BCE: Latin adjective "augustus" "venerable, consecrated" is attested.
  • 27 BC: the Senate confers the name "Augustus" on Octavian.
  • 1st-4th c.: the title becomes the senior imperial rank; "Augusta" for empresses; Greek "Sebastos".
  • Month Sextilis is renamed Augustus ("August").
  • 16th c.: the name enters English through translations of Roman historians.
  • Early 18th c.: "Augustan Age" names the golden age of English letters (Pope, Swift).
  • Modern: "august" (adjective) and "Augustus" (given name) continue the word's life.
CONCLUSION "Augustus" condenses nearly two and a half thousand years into a single name: an Indo-European root about growth and increase; a Latin religious adjective for the divinely blessed; a political title invented to disguise absolute power as sacred majesty; an imperial institution lasting half a millennium; an English literary-critical term for golden ages; and a living given name and metaphor today. Its evolution shows how a word can move from the vocabulary of farmers and priests to the summit of world power, and from a Roman Senate motion to the prose of Gibbon and the modern imagination.
