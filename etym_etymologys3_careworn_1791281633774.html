Careworn is a transparent Modern English compound, but each half reaches deep into Germanic prehistory, and the combination itself tells a 19th-century literary story about how worry was imagined as friction.

First element care: Old English caru, cearu meant sorrow, anxiety, grief, and also charge or oversight. It descends from Proto-Germanic *karo, sorrow, lament, itself usually referred to PIE *gar-, to cry out, call, lament. The semantic bridge is vocal: lamentation as audible sorrow becomes inner sorrow. Cognates include Old High German kara, lament, and Old Norse kor, complaint. In Middle English care widens to cover worry, attention, and love: to take care, to care for. By early modern times it names both the burden (cares of the world) and the tenderness (parental care), a duality still felt in careworn, where care is both love that burdens and worry that erodes.

  • Cry to sorrow: PIE cry-word becomes Germanic noun for lament, then general sorrow.
  • Sorrow to responsibility: that which grieves you is what you must look after, hence care as charge.
  • Burden to love: in modern English care splits into worry and affection, both present in the careworn mother who loves and frets.

Second element worn: past participle of wear, Old English werian, to wear clothes, carry, erode by use, from Proto-Germanic *wazjan, to clothe, from PIE *wes-, to clothe, dress. Latin vestis, clothing, and Sanskrit vastra share the root. Old English already shows both senses: to wear a cloak and to wear down by friction. Middle English worn therefore means both dressed and eroded. The participle worn in compounds like time-worn, careworn, and footsore carries the eroded sense.

  • Clothe to erode: clothes that are worn long become thin; the verb generalizes to any abrasion by time or use.
  • Physical to moral: faces, like garments, can be worn thin by invisible friction, here by anxiety.

The compound care-worn itself is first attested in the early 19th century, around the 1820s-1830s, in poetry and novels of the Romantic and early Victorian periods. It belongs to a family of evocative participial compounds coined then: care-laden, sorrow-worn, time-worn, world-weary. Its success owes to industrial and urban hardship made visible, Evangelical moralizing that read character on faces, and Romantic taste for melancholy physiognomy. Dickens, the Brontes, Eliot, and Hardy use careworn as shorthand for respectable suffering.

Phonetically nothing is hidden: care /kea/ plus worn /wo:n/ fuse with stress on the first element, the rhotic linking them in RP /keawon/. The spelling with -worn rather than -worn-out preserves poetic compression. No French or Latin overlay intervenes; this is pure Germanic stock, which is why it feels plain, homely, and sincere compared to Latinate anxious or haggard from French.

Modern usage has thinned but not vanished. Newspapers still write of careworn communities after disasters, and novelists of careworn faces at funerals. The Wodehouse sentence plays on its solemnity: to call a bather careworn is mock-elevated, as if bath toys could cure years of grief. The joke preserves the word by proving its gravity can still be borrowed for bathos.
