- Old English slǣpeþ — in use as the plain, everyday third-person present of a very common verb, unmarked in homilies, chronicles and verse.
- Middle English slepeth — the standard southern literate form; northern writers increasingly use slēpes, but London and the scriptoria keep -eth.
- 15th–16th century — the -es/-s ending conquers speech; sleepeth begins to sound conservative, scriptural, bookish.
- Early Modern English — the King James Bible crowns it: 'Our friend Lazarus sleepeth' (John 11:11); poets value its extra syllable for metre; sleepeth becomes the sound of holy gravity.
- 17th–18th century — the form vanishes from spoken English; it survives in scripture, hymns, epitaphs and the pulpit.
- 19th–21st century — deliberate archaism: Victorian novelists, fantasists (Tolkien and his heirs) and parodists all reach for sleepeth to sound hallowed, antique or comically solemn; today it reads as sacred, remote, faintly magical.
