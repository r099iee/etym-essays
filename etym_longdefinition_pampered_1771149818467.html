pampered is an adjective (and the past participle of the verb to pamper) meaning "overindulged, treated with excessive attention, comfort, and kindness; spoiled; mollycoddled." The word describes a person or creature whose every desire has been gratified and whose hardships have been removed, to the point of weakness, softness, or entitlement. Its historical core is physical: the verb to pamper originally meant "to cram with rich food, to overfeed," and the modern sense is a generalization of that image of stuff-fed indulgence from the stomach to the whole person.

In the supplied context. The passage contrasts "a modest man, quite without the arrogance of the pampered aristocrat." Here pampered does heavy moral work: it characterizes an aristocrat whose lifelong removal from difficulty and satiety of every comfort have produced arrogance — the confidence and contempt that come from never having had to strive, want, or endure. The word condenses a theory of character: comfort without challenge breeds pride; modesty is the virtue of the un-indulged.

Range of senses and uses.

  • Overindulged in comforts — a pampered pet, a pampered child: provided with luxuries, softness, rich food, and constant attention.
  • Spoiled by excessive kindness — emphasis on the resulting character: wilfulness, expectation, ingratitude, softness.
  • Physically eased of all hardship — pampered skin, a pampered lifestyle: the sense of being groomed, massaged, fed, and cared for like a prized object.
  • Self-indulgent, of behavior — "a pampered existence," "pamper yourself with a spa day": today also a positive or marketing sense, promising luxurious self-care.
  • Noun and verbal derivatives — pampering (the act), pamperer (one who indulges). The brand name Pampers (diapers) deliberately evokes the baby-like, soft, "cared-for" image.

Register and nuance. Pampered is mildly to strongly judgmental when applied to people, carrying the implication that comfort has weakened or corrupted them; but it is affectionate or neutral when applied to pets or when used in self-care marketing. It is softer than "spoiled" (which implies ruin or moral damage), less protective than "coddled" (which stresses sheltering from difficulty), and more focused on material comfort than "indulged" (which stresses gratifying desires or whims). The word keeps a faint culinary memory of its original meaning: pampering is, at root, feeding someone beyond need.

Usage notes. The adjective typically precedes a noun (pampered child, pampered aristocrat) or follows a linking verb (the dog was pampered). The underlying verb pamper is transitive and may be reflexive ("pamper yourself"). The word is commonly intensified by "thoroughly," "utterly," or "grossly," and it is often paired with words for wealth, rank, luxury, and idleness.

Metaphoric shift and semantic bridge. The central bridge of the word is from feeding to indulgence in general. In its earliest English record, to pamper was to stuff someone with rich food — a physical, almost agricultural act aimed at the body. Over the 16th and 17th centuries the image generalized: what can be crammed with food can also be crammed with comforts, attention, and luxuries, until the "stuffing" becomes psychological and the person, like an overfed animal, grows soft, sluggish, and unfit for hardship. This is why pampered so often carries a moral aftertaste: it does not merely describe luxury but implies that luxury has done something to the character, removing the tempering that struggle provides. The reading context depends on exactly this bridge — the pampered aristocrat is arrogant precisely because his whole life has been one of crammed comfort, with nothing sharpening him.

Distinctions worth remembering. Pampered is about the ongoing lavish treatment and its softening effect, rather than about a single act of kindness. A host may pamper a guest for an evening, but a person is only called pampered when the treatment has been habitual enough to leave a mark. The word thus hovers between description and judgment: it states what has been done to someone while quietly suggesting what it has made of them.
