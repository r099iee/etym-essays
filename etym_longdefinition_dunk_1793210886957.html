Dunk is, in its oldest English sense, a homely verb of the breakfast table: to dip something briefly into liquid before eating it — to dunk a biscuit in tea, a doughnut in coffee, a crust in gravy. The action is quick, partial, and companionable: the morsel goes in, soaks for a heartbeat, and comes out to the mouth, flavoured and softened.

In the source sentence the word is used in its looser colloquial sense of reckoning or expecting — that is one more than I dunk they will get — a rustic or dialectal colouring of think, or a wry understatement meaning to count on or suppose. Cobbett's Sussex countrymen spoke a broad dialect in which vowels shifted and strangers misheard, and the printed word preserves that country tang: a farmer's guarded guess about what some party will get, stated sideways with a shrug. Whatever the precise shade, the core of the verb — a quick decisive dip into something — colours even this odd use: to dunk is to commit, to plunge in with the answer.

Core meaning in detail: Food dunking works by capillary timing: the crumb must absorb enough liquid to soften without collapsing into the cup. Whole rituals attend it — the British biscuit engineered to survive three seconds in tea, the American doughnut dunked in diner coffee, the communion of office workers around a shared packet. From this table act the word leapt, in twentieth-century America, to the basketball court: to dunk a ball is to leap and thrust it down through the hoop from above, the shot as decisive as a fist through tissue. The dunk shot, first flourished in the 1930s and banned in college play from 1967 to 1976 precisely because giants like Alcindor made it unstoppable, remains the game's purest theatre of dominance.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • To dip food briefly in drink or sauce (earliest in English, 1919 in print, older in Pennsylvania German speech): dunking biscuits, doughnuts, and soldiers of toast. The bridge from the German original is the immigrant table: Pennsylvania Germans said dunke for dipping bread, and America listened.
  • To plunge or submerge suddenly (generalised): to dunk a lobster in boiling water, to dunk a baptismal candidate, to dunk a biscuit-maker's machinery test piece. The bridge widens the table dip to any quick immersion — the food sense generalised to bodies, objects, and rites.
  • To score by thrusting the ball down through the basket (basketball, from 1930s): the slam dunk, the alley-oop dunk, the putback dunk. The bridge is visual and violent: the ball is dipped into the net from above, plunged through the hoop as a biscuit is plunged into a mug — only with a ten-foot leap and a roaring crowd.
  • To defeat decisively, to humiliate (slang, from basketball): to dunk on an opponent in debate, in an exam, in a rap battle. The bridge carries the court's dominance into every contest: just as the dunker towers over the defender, so the debater slams the argument home beyond reach.
  • A doughnut or biscuit made for dunking (noun, mid-20th c.): coffee and dunks at the church social. The bridge turns the act into the object — the thing dunked lends its name to the ritual, as a smoke names the cigarette by the act.
  • To guess, suppose, or reckon in dialect speech (rare, as in Cobbett's line): more than I dunk they will get. The bridge, if it is one, runs through the countryman's clipped vowel — think flattened toward dunk — or through a joking understatement, dipping the claim before serving it.

Register and connotation: dunk is warm and informal everywhere except the physics lab — the vocabulary of kitchens, playgrounds, and sports pages. Food dunking carries affection and mild comedy; basketball dunking carries swagger and supremacy. Even the slang dunk on keeps a grin inside its cruelty, since the dunk is the showman's shot, performed as much for the gasp as for the score.
