- 17th century (deaden, from dead + -en): "to deprive of feeling, make numb" — a limb deadened by cold, sensation killed off
- 18th–19th century extension: "to deprive of force or intensity" — to deaden pain, to deaden an argument, vitality withdrawn by a small, controlled act of killing
- Later acoustic specialization: "to deaden sound" — a room deadened by rugs and hangings, resonance extinguished at the point of contact
- Modern: general "make less sharp or resonant," plus the figurative deadened senses, deadened emotions — the vocabulary of numbness and muffled feeling

- 14th century (russet, from Old French rousset): the reddish-brown of coarse homespun wool, the undyed cloth of the English peasantry
- Tudor and Elizabethan era: russet as the social colour — humble, honest, unpolished dress worn close to the soil, a marker of plain country folk
- 16th–17th century: colour-noun extension — autumn leaves, iron rust, russet apples; a general warm red-brown
- Modern: a literary, earthy red-brown of great warmth; the old social weight of peasant honesty is now only a historical echo
