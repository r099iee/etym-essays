- [13th–14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Old French guise to signify customary manner, habitual fashion, or practice (e.g., "after his guise"); viewed as a neutral or commendable description of personal style, traditional etiquette, or cultural practice — [perception: standard medieval term for manner, fashion, or way of behaving]
- [15th–16th c. Late Middle to Early Modern English] — Shifted from abstract "manner" to outward visual appearance, particular clothing, costume, or masquerade dress worn at courtly pageants and theatrical revels — [perception: associated with theatrical role-playing, courtly attire, and festive spectacle]
- [17th–18th c. Early Modern to Enlightenment English] — Deepened into a negative moral connotation denoting a deceptive exterior, false front, or hypocritical cloak assumed to mislead observers and conceal malicious intent — [perception: viewed with moral suspicion as artful dissimulation and hypocritical duplicity]
- [19th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Solidified in formal prose, political journalism, and literary criticism as an analytical term for ideological masking, covert operations, or commercial rebranding (predominantly in the idiom "under the guise of") — [perception: sophisticated marker of concealed motives, institutional pretense, or transformed identities]
