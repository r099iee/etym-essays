In the sentence "A strange race of fishermen performed the ghastly __________," the noun office is being used in its oldest English sense, the one that survives in the Office for the Dead: not a place of clerical work, but a prescribed duty, a ceremonial function, a solemn charge. The juxtaposition of the decorous, almost liturgical noun with the adjective ghastly creates the whole eerie effect of the sentence: a task that in ordinary life would be a bureaucratic routine is here revealed as a supernatural rite. These fishermen are not workers but ministrants; their ferrying of the dead is an involuntary liturgy, an obligation laid on them by fate rather than by any contract. The word therefore carries a somber, mythic, and faintly ironic weight — the formality of office dignifies the horror even as it makes it more chilling, suggesting that the grim crossing of the dead is conducted with the impersonality and regularity of an institution.

The full polysemy of office. English office has grown a remarkable fan of senses, all radiating from the Latin original's double meaning of 'duty' and 'post'.

  • 1. A duty, service, task or ceremonial function (the card's sense). The oldest English sense, surviving chiefly in religious and formal phrases: the Divine Office, the Office for the Dead, the office of the priesthood, the last offices (the rites of burial performed for the dead).
  • 2. A position or post of authority or employment. To hold office, to take office, the office of president, the office of consul or king — the formal, often appointed or hereditary charge within an institution.
  • 3. The room, building or premises where business is done (the modern primary sense). The doctor's office, office hours, the head office. This is the sense the word has in the great majority of contemporary utterances.
  • 4. The institution or its employees. The whole office knows, the office has decided — office as a collective, abstract organisation with a life of its own.
  • 5. A government department or agency. The Home Office, the Patent Office, the Office for National Statistics.
  • 6. Ecclesiastical liturgy. The set of daily services of the Church (the Divine Office), and by extension a single service or rite — the Mass, a burial office, the Office for the Dead that lies behind this card's sentence.
  • 7. Obsolete and rare — a favour, kindness, service done for another. Latin officium meant 'service, kindness' as well as 'duty'; to do someone an office (a good turn) is now archaic.
  • 8. The plural offices as duties or rites. 'The last offices' — the rites of burial — preserves the plural ceremonial sense intact in modern English.

Historical bridge. The whole fan can be told as one movement: Latin officium meant 'the doing of a service' — a duty, a favour, a ceremonial function. Christian Latin took it up for the liturgy (the 'office' of prayer, the Divine Office). Medieval political language took it up for a post of authority (the 'office' of an official). The Renaissance administrative world took the post and gave it a room — first the room where duties are done (the spatial sense already appears in Old French), then the bureaucracy and the company. The room is thus the youngest sense, and the priest's rite the oldest: which is exactly why the word works so eerily for the 'ghastly office' of the boatmen, a liturgical duty performed as an institution.

Boundaries. A task is a piece of imposed work with no ceremonial colour; a duty is a moral or legal obligation felt inwardly; a job is paid employment, flatly secular; a function is what a thing normally does, mechanical and neutral; a role is a part one plays, exchangeable and theatrical; a post or position is the slot one occupies. Office, by contrast, denotes a formal, often hereditary or appointed charge within an institution, and when applied to a rite it takes on a liturgical colour: the office is what the officiant does, the rite is what is done.

Derivatives. Officer (one who holds an office), official (adjective and noun), officiate (to perform an office or ceremony), officious (over-eager to serve, meddling), officeholder, office politics, office hours. The card's own mnemonic is right to insist that the priest's Divine Office is the root meaning and the desk much later: sense 1 is the historical head of the whole family.
