- [Old English: the physical stab] - Prician means to prick, sting, or pierce with a sharp point; the word is bound to the body and to pain.
- [Middle English to 16th century: the goad] - Transferred senses grow outward: to spur or incite a horse is to prick it forward, and a 'prick of conscience' is an inner goading — the sharp point turned moral.
- [1590s: the ear idiom is born] - 'To prick up one's ears' is first attested, describing dogs, horses, and hares raising their ears to listen; the animal is imagined setting its ears upright like a needle-point.
- [17th-18th century: idiom goes public] - The phrase migrates from hunting-lodge language into general English; any sudden, sharpened attention can now be described as 'pricked'.
- [19th century onwards: the reflex wins] - 'Pricked up his ears' becomes the everyday heart of the word, an instinctive, animal-quick alertness; the literal 'prick' of piercing survives in physical contexts, while the vulgar slang noun 'prick' shadows the word's coarse register.
- [Contemporary sense] - Almost always the reflexive gesture of sudden attention — ears pricking up at an unexpected sound — with the original tang of a tiny, acute stab still felt in the image.
