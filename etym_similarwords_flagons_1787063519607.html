• Pitchers: Similar because both are large upright vessels with a spout and handle used for holding and pouring liquids; differs because pitchers are general domestic containers (often handle-less or open-topped without lids) used for water, milk, or juice, whereas flagons are specifically historical, often lidded, and associated with wine, ale, or liturgical decanting.
  • Decanters: Similar because both serve to aerate, hold, and pour wine at the table; differs because decanters are almost exclusively clear glass or crystal vessels designed for formal table aesthetics and sediment separation, whereas flagons are traditionally metal (pewter, silver) or stoneware vessels featuring sturdy handles and hinged lids.
  • Tankards: Similar because both are traditional drinking/serving vessels made of pewter, wood, or ceramic with single handles and hinged lids; differs because tankards are individual drinking vessels for direct personal consumption, whereas flagons are significantly larger serving decanters used to fill multiple drinking cups.
  • Flasks: Similar because both derive from the same historical root and store potable liquids; differs because flasks are small, highly portable, flat or pocket-sized containers designed for personal travel, whereas flagons are stationary, heavy, and capacious table decanters.
  • Jugs: Similar because both are sturdy containers for liquids with narrow necks and handles; differs because jugs represent everyday utilitarian pottery or plastic without historical, ceremonial, or festive connotations, whereas flagons possess distinct liturgical and banquet heritage.
  • Chalices: Similar because both play prominent roles in historical banquets and Christian liturgical rites; differs because a chalice is a footed goblet or cup intended for direct drinking, whereas a flagon is the larger pouring pitcher that fills the chalice.
