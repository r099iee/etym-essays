Nestling as used here is the present participle (and participial adjective) of the verb nestle, meaning to settle, press, or lie closely and cozily against or within something sheltered for warmth, comfort, protection, or affection. In the phrase "nestling snugly abaft the shoulder blades," it describes something tucked closely and comfortably just behind the shoulder blades, resting in the hollow or shelter they create.

At its core, to nestle is to make a small nest of one's body and surroundings: to sink slightly into softness, to fit into a hollow, to seek contact that feels safe. It implies gentle, unforced closeness rather than gripping or clinging. A cat nestles into a blanket, a child nestles against a parent, a cottage nestles among trees. The action is quiet, sustained, and contented.

Grammatically, nestling can be a progressive verb form (is nestling), a participial adjective (a nestling head), or, in an entirely separate but related noun sense, a baby bird (a nestling still in the nest). Only context tells which is meant; here it is unambiguously verbal: the act of snuggling into place.

  • To settle snugly against someone or something for warmth or affection: the primary modern sense. Example: to nestle against a partner, to nestle a baby in one's arms. The semantic bridge from birds is direct: as a bird sinks into the lined cup of a nest, a person sinks into bedding, clothing, or an embrace.
  • To lie half-buried or embedded cozily within something soft: to nestle into pillows, into a duvet, into deep grass. Here the emphasis is on being partially enveloped and supported on several sides.
  • To house, shelter, or tuck something closely into a protected place (transitive): to nestle the baby into the carrycot, to nestle jewels into tissue paper. The subject creates security for the object.
  • Of places or buildings: to sit sheltered within surrounding landscape: a farm nestling in a valley, a village nestling among hills. This is a metaphorical extension where topography replaces bedding; the settlement is imagined as a small warm thing held by larger enfolding landforms.
  • Noun sense - a young bird not yet able to leave the nest: robins, sparrows, or eaglets still featherless or downy and dependent on parents. This preserves the oldest diminutive formation nest + -ling (little nest creature) and is technically distinct from the participle, though they coincide in spelling.
  • Figurative / emotional sense - to settle into comfort, routine, or intimacy: to nestle into a new home, into a habit, into friendship. The physical softness becomes psychological security.

Literary nuance matters: nestling almost always carries tenderness, vulnerability, and willing closeness. Unlike cowering (fearful), huddling (often cold or frightened), or lounging (idle sprawl), nestling suggests trust. To describe something as nestling abaft the shoulder blades is to make it sound small, welcome, and safely carried, not heavy or intrusive. The accompanying adverb snugly redoubles this coziness.

In short, nestling means fitting oneself or being fitted closely into a warm, sheltered closeness, whether bodily, domestic, or landscape.
