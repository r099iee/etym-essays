Derivation chain for SPINDLY (root word one of three): Stage 1 — PIE *(s)pen- "to draw, stretch, spin" (reconstructed; pre-4th millennium BCE) — the root of drawing out, stretching, and spinning thread. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *spinnaną "to draw out and twist thread, to spin" (reconstructed; before c. 500 CE); from it *spinilō "the spindle, the instrument of spinning." Stage 3 — Old English spinnan "to spin" and spinel "the spindle, the tapering rod for holding thread" (attested 9th–11th centuries). Stage 4 — Middle English spindel / spindle "the spindle" (12th–15th centuries). Stage 5 — English spindle (noun) + the adjectival suffix -y → spindly "long and thin like a spindle, weak and fragile" (coined 17th century) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain *(s)pen- → *spinnaną → spinel → spindle → spindly is secure. The related Latin root pendēre ("to hang, be stretched out") shares the same PIE source and appears in English as pendant, pendulum, depend, append, and suspend, but is a separate Latin branch, not part of the English spindle lineage.
Derivation chain for RHEUMY (root word two of three): Stage 1 — PIE *sreu- "to flow, stream" (reconstructed; pre-4th millennium BCE) — the root of flowing water and streaming motion. Stage 2 — Ancient Greek rhein "to flow"; rheuma "a flow, a stream, a bodily discharge or fluxion" (attested from the classical period). Stage 3 — Latin rheuma "a flow, a catarrhal discharge" (attested in medical Latin) — a direct Greek loan in the medical vocabulary. Stage 4 — Old French reume / rheume "a cold, catarrh, watery discharge" (attested from the 12th century) — borrowed from Latin. Stage 5 — Middle English reume / rheum "a watery discharge of the eyes or nose; catarrh" (entered 14th century) + the suffix -y → rheumy "watery, discharging thin moisture, especially of the eyes" (modern English) ◄ TARGET. Note: The chain is secure. The same PIE root *sreu- produced Sanskrit srávati ("flows," whence Hindi srot, "stream") and Proto-Germanic *straumaz ("stream"), both independent collateral branches of the same ancient root.
Derivation chain for FLABBY (root word three of three): Stage 1 — No securely reconstructable PIE root exists. The word belongs to the expressive, imitative family of flap / flop / flab, whose Germanic ancestors are partly sound-symbolic; the OED and standard etymologies treat the origin as obscure or imitative. Stage 2 — English flab "soft, loose, hanging flesh" (attested late 17th century), a back-formation or dialectal member of the flap family. Stage 3 — English flab + the adjectival suffix -y → flabby "soft, loose, lacking firmness" (coined 1690s) ◄ TARGET. Note on the debate: The etymology is genuinely uncertain beyond the imitative family. Related forms include flap (the motion), flop, and the dialectal flabber, and some authorities compare Dutch flab and German Flabbe; no regular PIE derivation is agreed upon, so the earliest stage is stated explicitly as unknown rather than invented.
