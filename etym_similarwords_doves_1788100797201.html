• Pigeons: Similar because pigeons and doves belong to the same family (Columbidae) and share body, gait, nesting and cooing; differs because in everyday English “pigeon” names the larger, grey, city-adapted birds and the domesticated meat-and-racing breeds, while “dove” names the smaller, softer, often white or tenderly tinted bird of parks and poetry — though ornithologists use the two words interchangeably.
  • Turtle-doves: Similar because the turtle dove is the classic small dove (genus Streptopelia) with the softest purring call; differs because “turtledove” pins the word to particular species and to the biblical-Chaucerian emblem of faithful love, while “doves” remains the general bird-plural.
  • Mourning doves: Similar because the mourning dove (Zenaida macroura) is North America’s common wild dove, famous for its sorrowful cooing; differs because it names one species, whereas “doves” covers the whole family — and the mourning dove’s name tilts the tone from gentle to melancholic.
  • Rock doves: Similar because the rock dove (Columba livia) is a true dove — the ancestral wild stock of every domestic pigeon; differs because “rock dove” is the technical ornithological tag for a species most speakers call a pigeon, so it lacks “dove”’s poetic and political aura.
  • Squabs: Similar because a squab is a young pigeon or dove, and doves have been eaten for millennia; differs because “squab” views the bird as table meat rather than living symbol, carrying a butcher-shop register instead of the dovecote’s.
  • Pacifists: Similar because since 1962 a political “dove” is precisely one who counsels peace, i.e. a pacifist-leaning figure; differs because “pacifist” is the formal word for a principled opponent of violence — a creed — whereas “dove” is a folk-bird metaphor for anyone in a war debate who urges restraint.
  • Lambs: Similar because “lamb” is likewise an animal metaphor for a gentle, innocent, vulnerable person; differs because the lamb stresses sacrificial meekness and timidity, while “dove” stresses softness, purity and a non-warlike stance — and, in the plural, a whole political camp.
  • Angels: Similar because both words are used of a sweetly kind, peaceable person (“she is an angel” / “she is a dove”); differs because “angel” draws on heavenly messengers, goodness and protection and is more hyperbolic, while “dove” keeps the bird-image of white plumage and soft voice, plus the political second life.
