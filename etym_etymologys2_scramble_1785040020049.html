PIE origin — *sker- "to cut, scrape, scratch" (reconstructed), with extension *skerbh- / *skrebh- "to scrape, scratch." - PIE *sker- "to cut, scrape, scratch" (reconstructed) → extended root *skerbh- / *skrebh- "to scrape, scratch" (reconstructed) → Proto-Germanic *skrapōjan "to scrape, erase" (reconstructed) → Old English scrapian "to scrape" (attested) → Middle English scrapen → English scrape "to rub or scratch off a surface" (attested) → nasalized dialect form scramp "to pull or scrape together with the hands" (dialectal, attested) → variant scramb (dialectal) → dialectal scramble / scrammel "to rake together with the hands" (with frequentative suffix -le; dialectal) → English scramble "to make one's way by clambering, struggling or wriggling along; to strive with others jostling for a share" (attested 1580s) → **SCRAMBLE** (Modern English). Notes and debates: - The OED treats the origin as ultimately obscure: scramble is traced as a nasalized form of scrapen (scrape) via the dialect forms scramp → scramb, with the frequentative suffix -le; an alternative view makes it a nasalized form of scrabble (attested 1530s, itself from Dutch schrabben / schrabbelen "to scrape, scratch"), which may have influenced the shape of scramble. Both routes agree on the scraping/scratching family. - The PIE root *sker- is well attested across Indo-European (Greek keirō "to cut," Latin caro "flesh," etc.), so the deep ancestry is secure even though the English word's immediate dialectal path is imperfectly documented. - The frequentative suffix -le (as in scrabble, wrestle, gobble) marks repeated, energetic little actions — the repeated raking motion that produced both the climbing and the jostling senses. - Senses attested after the first appearance: scrambled eggs (the verb sense of stirring/mixing together, attested 1822; the dish, 1843), scramble "to render a signal unintelligible" (1927), and the aviation/military sense of launching aircraft in haste (1940s).
