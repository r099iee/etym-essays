creaked is the simple past and past-participle form of the verb creak — to make, or to give out, a harsh, high-pitched, grating or squeaking sound, typically produced when rigid, dry, or stressed material moves against itself. In the passage — "the woods roared and creaked all round him" — the verb captures the exact acoustic signature of massive timber under extreme strain. Where the forest as a whole roars (a diffuse, low, continuous sound), a creak is a localized, higher, dry, staccato squeal: wood fibres rubbing and snapping against one another, trunks bending past their elastic limit, branches grinding at their sockets. A creak is the noise of resistance just before failure, and C. S. Lewis places it deliberately just before the catastrophe — "There came a crash. A tree fell right across the road just behind him." The creaking is thus the audible prelude to breaking, and the reader is made to feel the tension in the wood and to share the rider's dread.

Primary concrete sense (past tense): the word names the sound made by dry, hard, stressed objects, almost always of wood, metal, or bone:

  • Hinges, doors, and gates: a rusty hinge creaks when it turns; a door creaks on its frame; an old gate creaks in the wind.
  • Floorboards, stairs, and furniture: old boards creak underfoot — the classic stage-whisper of a house's age; a chair creaks under weight.
  • Wood and rigging: ships' timbers and masts creak under strain at sea; trees creak in a gale — the exact use in our text.
  • Human and animal bodies: knees, joints, and knuckles creak with age or stiffness.

Secondary sense — slow, laboured movement: by extension, creak describes motion that is slow, stiff, or working with difficulty, as when an old mechanism "creaks into action" or a tired traveller "creaks out of the saddle." The sound stands for the effort.

Tertiary sense — barely succeeding: in British usage creak through, creak past, or creak by means to pass a test, clear a hurdle, or survive a crisis only just, with the narrowest possible margin — "he creaked through the exam." The image is of a straining, groaning passage under maximum load.

Metaphoric and figurative extensions:

  • Structural and systemic: a "creaking" institution, economy, administration, or system is one that functions only under strain — held together despite age, inefficiency, or over-extension, threatening but not yet collapsing.
  • Proverb: "the creaking gate hangs longest" — the thing that complains most audibly often proves the most durable; a figure of deceptive weakness hiding real toughness.
  • Adjective creaky: old, stiff, rickety, worn, or barely functional — creaky knees, a creaky plot, a creaky start-up, a creaky legacy system — where the sound becomes the emblem of decrepitude and endurance.

Modern technical jargon: in structural engineering and tribology (the science of friction, wear, and lubrication), "creak" and "creaking" denote the stick–slip frictional noise emitted by joints, bearings, floor systems, and timber structures; it is studied as a form of vibration and acoustic emission, distinct from crackle, rattle, and squeal. The word also appears in the maintenance vocabulary of wooden boats and timber frames ("the vessel's frame creaked"), and in fiction, games, and sound design it is a stock cue of horror — the creaking stair and the groaning house are the fixed sound-signals of dread and approaching catastrophe.

Sound symbolism and register: creak is an onomatopoeic English verb. The initial /kr-/ cluster plus the raised front vowel produces a dry, tense, high-frequency scrape in the mouth of the speaker, and the word is among English's most vivid sensory verbs — one hears it. Register is plain and concrete in the literal sense but immediately atmospheric in narrative: it is a word of physical honesty, the opposite of music or romance, and it belongs equally to the carpenter's yard and the ghost story.

Historical semantic bridge: the word entered English around 1300 meaning the harsh, grating cry of a bird (crows, rooks), and only in the 15th century transferred to inanimate objects such as doors, hinges, and timber. The bridge is purely acoustic: a high, dry, intermittent, grating note sounds alike whether produced by a bird's throat or a strained timber, so the verb simply widened its subject. From the 18th century the figurative extension to ageing, strain, and barely-managed effort followed, because what creaks is felt to be under stress — near its breaking point yet still enduring — and that tension has kept the word alive across all its senses to the present day.
