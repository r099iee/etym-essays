ERRED: The past tense and past participle of the verb err; made a mistake, committed an error, or strayed from the correct moral, logical, or physical path. PRIMARY SENSE: To have erred is to have gone wrong — in reasoning, in judgement, in conduct, or in aim. The word is the dignified, formal way of saying that someone made a mistake: an accountant has erred in a sum, a judge has erred in law, a navigator has erred in course. Unlike blunder, error carries no suggestion of clumsiness or stupidity; it states calmly and objectively that a deviation from what is true, right, or intended has occurred. In this it belongs to the formal and literary register, the verb of courts, philosophy, and careful prose. SENSES AND CONTEXTS:
  • Intellectual and logical: erred in his calculations, erred in her reasoning — the neutral, technical sense of being wrong about facts or arguments.
  • Moral and religious: erred from the path of duty, erred as sinners — straying from moral or divine law, a sense close to transgression (echoed in the proverb "to err is human, to forgive divine").
  • Legal and ethical idiom: "erred on the side of caution" — deliberately chose the safer option rather than risk a catastrophic mistake, i.e. a defensible deviation in a prudent direction.
  • Navigational and physical: "the vessel erred from its bearing" — strayed physically from a course or line, the literal wandering sense.
  • Archaic and literary: to have wandered or travelled — the same root via the medieval sense of errer "to travel," preserved in the phrase knight errant (a knight journeying in search of adventures).
SEMANTIC BRIDGE: The whole family rests on Latin errāre, "to wander, to stray, to go astray." The meaning is transparently spatial at first: the wanderer leaves the road, the traveller loses the path, and from that literal straying comes the figurative straying of a mind from truth, a life from duty, or a calculation from accuracy. Old French had two verbs spelled errer — one from Latin errāre "to wander" and one from Latin iter "a journey" — and their collision in medieval French reinforced the wandering sense and produced the errant knight. English inherited the single sense "to make a mistake" alongside the fading physical sense, and in modern usage erred is almost wholly intellectual and moral: the mistake has left the forest and the highway behind and now lives entirely in the mind. REGISTER AND THE PROVERB: Erred belongs to the formal register — the verb of legal opinions, scholarly corrections, and measured apologies — and its brevity gives it weight: a single word admits fault without melodrama. The proverb "to err is human, to forgive divine," from Alexander Pope's An Essay on Criticism (1711), is the word's best-known appearance and fixed the sense of fallible human judgement at the centre of English culture; the line adapts the Latin saying "errare humanum est," showing how directly the English verb inherits the Roman moral vocabulary. In ordinary speech the word is now rare and slightly grand: one slips up, makes a mistake, or goofs, but one errs in print, in court, and in the careful statements of those unwilling to be casual about fault. Its derivatives — error, erroneous, errant, erratic, aberration, errata — form a small family that keeps the old "wandering" image alive in law (error of law), publishing (errata slips), and science (erratic boulders, aberrations in the data). Together they show a word that began as walking in the woods and ended as the formal name for the failure of reason itself.
