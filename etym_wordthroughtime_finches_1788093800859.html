- (Old English, 8th-c. glossaries) finc - the plain vernacular name of the chaffinch and its kin: one of the oldest recorded English bird-names, probably echoic of the bird's “fink” call.
- (Middle English) finch - homely and useful: the cage-bird of cottagers, the quarry of merlins, a rustic sound-word in fields and threshing floors.
- (16th–18th c.) Natural history fixes “finches” as a class of small seed-eating songbirds; goldfinch, greenfinch, bullfinch, chaffinch become the familiar English set, each name carrying its own observation.
- (19th c.) Linnaean taxonomy gives them the family Fringillidae; colonial ornithology exports the name worldwide — house finches, purple finches, Darwin's specimens mislabelled in the Beagle's boxes.
- (1936) “Darwin's finches” is coined for the Galápagos birds Darwin had collected a century earlier - the word becomes an icon of evolution, taken far from any English hedgerow.
- (Modern) “Finches” stays warm and ordinary in birdwatching, exact in ecology textbooks, and globally famous in biology classes: a name that began as a village echo of a bird's call now signifies adaptive radiation itself.
