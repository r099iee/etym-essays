DERIVATION CHAIN FOR "SUMPTUOUSNESS":
Stage 1 — PIE root: *h₁em- / *em- "to take, to distribute" (reconstructed); the idea of taking hold; buying as "taking possession."
Stage 2 — Latin: emō, emere "to take, to buy" (classical, 1st century BCE); in early Roman usage buying was "taking possession" of something.
Stage 3 — Latin: sumere (sub- "up from under, into one's hand" + emere) "to take up, to take upon oneself, to lay out money, to spend."
Stage 4 — Latin: sumptus "expense, cost, outlay" (the past participle of sumere used as a noun; literally "what has been taken up and used").
Stage 5 — Latin: sumptuōsus "costly, expensive, lavish" (classical adjective, from sumptus + -ōsus "full of").
Stage 6 — Late Latin: sumptuōsitās "expensiveness, costliness" (abstract noun from sumptuōsus + -tās).
Stage 7 — Old French: sumptuosité / sumptueux "lavishness" / "lavish" (borrowed from the Latin forms).
Stage 8 — Middle English: sumptuous (late 15th century) "costly, magnificent, lavish," borrowed from Old French sumptueux.
Stage 9 — English: sumptuous + -ness (native Germanic suffix) — sumptuousness (16th century) "costliness, lavishness."
Stage 10 — English: SUMPTUOUSNESS.

NOTES ON COGNATES AND CORRECTIONS (explicit):
The root *h₁em- is securely attested in Latin emere, Lithuanian im̃ti "to take," Old Church Slavonic jęti "to take" and Old Prussian īmt; it is sometimes compared with Hittite ēpzi "seizes," but that equation is debated and is best left unasserted. The earlier condensed tree wrongly placed Sanskrit yamati "he holds, restrains" under this root — Sanskrit yam- belongs to the separate PIE root *yem- "to hold back, restrain" — and it also placed Germanic *niman "to take" here, which modern handbooks assign to the separate root *nem- "to take, to allot" (cf. Greek némein "to distribute"). Neither is a cognate, and both have been removed. The spelling "sumptuous" (with -pt-) reflects the Latin participial stem sumpt-, while French later dropped the -p-, giving somptueux; English kept the Latin spelling.
