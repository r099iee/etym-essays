Provisions (plural noun) are supplies of food and drink gathered and stored in advance — the stores that sustain a journey, a siege, a voyage or a campaign. The singular provision is more abstract: the act of providing or supplying something, preparation made beforehand, or a stipulation in a legal document. In the reading context the word is used in its most concrete plural sense: the three boats leaving Lake-town are 'laden with rowers, dwarves, Mr. Baggins, and many provisions' — the food stores for the expedition to the Lonely Mountain.

Primary senses.

  • Plural concrete — food stores: provisions are victuals and drink laid in for a journey, expedition, military campaign or siege — 'the ship's provisions', 'winter provisions', 'to run out of provisions'. This is the sense in the source sentence and the one with the longest narrative pedigree.
  • Singular abstract — the act of providing: 'the provision of healthcare', 'provision of services' — supplying or making available what is needed. The related idiom make provision for means to plan and prepare in advance ('make provision for old age').
  • Legal and institutional — a clause: a stipulation in a law, treaty, contract or will — 'under the provisions of the Act', 'a provision in the contract'. Here 'provision' is something provided for in advance in a document.
  • Ecclesiastical (historical): in medieval canon law, a 'provision' was the Pope's appointment of a cleric to a benefice — 'papal provisions' — a practice that provoked the English Statute of Provisors (1351). The sense is archaic but historically important.
  • Financial and accounting: in modern accounting, 'provisions' are sums set aside from profits to cover expected future liabilities — 'a provision for bad debts', 'provision for depreciation'.
  • Derivatives: provisional (arranged for the present, pending confirmation — 'a provisional booking'), proviso (a condition attached to an agreement, from the medieval Latin formula proviso quod, 'it being provided that'), and provider (one who supplies).

Semantic bridges. Every sense flows from the Latin verb prōvidēre, 'to foresee, to provide for' — literally 'to see ahead' (prō- 'before' + vidēre 'to see'). To provide is to see a future need and meet it in advance. The earliest English sense (14th century) was abstract: provisioun meant 'foresight, prudence'. From 'seeing ahead' the word moved to 'that which is seen to and supplied' — a store — and the plural hardened into the specific word for food for a journey. The legal sense ('a clause') is 'something provided for in advance in a document'; the accounting sense is the same foresight logic applied to money. The theological word providence is a doublet from the same root: God's eternal seeing-ahead.

Register and collocations. Provisions collocates with food, stores, supplies, arms, ships, boats, wagons, army, expedition, journey, voyage, siege, winter and campaign. In modern daily speech the concrete plural sounds slightly old-fashioned — 'supplies' and 'groceries' dominate — which gives provisions a literary, adventurous flavour perfectly suited to the expedition narrative in which it appears.
