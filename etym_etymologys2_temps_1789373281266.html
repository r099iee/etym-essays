PIE (Proto-Indo-European) (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*temp-
"to stretch, pull, extend, span"
Note: Competing philological hypothesis connects Latin tempus to PIE *temh₁- ('to cut'), suggesting time as a carved section or carved interval. Leading modern authorities (de Vaan, Ernout-Meillet) favor *temp- ('to stretch/extend'), yielding 'extent, duration'.
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000–500 BCE)
*tempos- (stem *tempes-)
"extent, stretch of duration, measure, section"
↓
Classical Latin (c. 3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE)
tempus (stem tempor-, nominative/accusative neuter)
"portion of time, season, opportunity, moment, temple of the head"
↓
Late / Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–8th c. CE)
tempus / *tempos
"time, meteorological weather, atmospheric season"
Note: Neuter s-stem merged into masculine nominal declensions; semantic expansion to encompass daily atmospheric weather took hold across Western Romance.
↓
Old French (c. 11th–13th c.)
tens / temps
"time, weather, season, grammatical tense"
Note: Early Old French spelled the word tens reflecting phonetic loss of /p/ before /s/; Renaissance humanists restored the etymological Latin 'p'.
↓
Middle / Early Modern French (c. 14th–17th c.)
temps
"time, weather, metric beat, fencing interval, dance step"
