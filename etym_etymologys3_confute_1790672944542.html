confute descends directly and without intermediaries from Latin confutare, a compound of intensive con- together, fully, forcefully and a second element -futare whose deeper origin is debated among Latinists. Cicero, Livy, and Quintilian all use confutare in two linked senses: to check or repress (to confute rashness, to confute audacity) and, by transfer to intellectual combat, to put down by argument, to refute. The past participle confutatus supplied the stem for English borrowing. The word entered English in the 16th century as part of the humanist flood of Latin verbs in -ute: confute, refute, dispute, impute, compute.

  • The futare problem: Ernout-Meillet and de Vaan both flag futare as obscure. The traditional guess connects it to beating or striking — as if to confute were to beat down together — perhaps via a lost verb for pouring boiling water or beating, cognate with fustis cudgel and fundere to pour. Others connect refutare and confutare to futis water-vessel, as if to pour cold water on an argument. No Proto-Indo-European root commands consensus, and dictionaries responsibly mark the element as of unknown origin. What is certain is the Latin sense, not its prehistory.
  • Refute versus confute: Latin possessed both refutare (re- + futare, to drive back, repel) and confutare (con- + futare, to check utterly, suppress). English borrowed both in the 1500s and has kept them as near-doublets ever since. Early writers use them interchangeably: Tyndale, Cranmer, and Hooker confute Papists, Anabaptists, and atheists where later polemicists would refute them.
  • First English attestations: Confute appears by the 1520s–1540s in Reformation controversy: to confute erroneous opinions, to be confuted by Scripture. Shakespeare uses it in this full-blooded sense — to confute the proud — where checking and refuting merge. Seventeenth-century logic textbooks list confutation as a formal part of a disputation, alongside confirmation: first you confirm your thesis, then you confute your adversary's.

Phonetically the borrowing is regular: Latin confutare /konfuːˈtaːre/ loses its infinitive ending to give stem confut-, anglicised as /kənˈfjuːt/ with the characteristic yod of -fu- after a labial, reduction of the first vowel to /kən-/, and stress on the second syllable. The given RP /kənˈfjuːt/ is standard on both sides of the Atlantic. Spelling has been invariant; the noun confutation preserves the Latin stem intact.

Semantically English narrowed what Latin left broad. Latin confutare could repress anything — hopes, plans, insolence — while English confute almost exclusively refutes propositions and persons. The checking sense died by 1700, leaving a purely intellectual verb. At the same time the verb grew rarer and more elevated: refute and disprove took over everyday argument, while confute retreated to theology, philosophy, and deliberate archaism. To use it today is to claim a classical standard of proof — not merely to disagree but to finish.

The author's quip — terms difficult to confute in the abstract — turns that standard back on jargon. A term that cannot be confuted even in theory fails Karl Popper's test avant la lettre: it is not irrefutable because true, but because empty. Circular definitions and off-putting nomenclature protect themselves from confutation by saying nothing definite enough to be wrong. The sentence thus uses confute in its strictest sense — decisive intellectual overthrow — to expose words that evade even the possibility of overthrow, and are therefore marvelously unhelpful to anyone who must actually write.
