coveted:
- Entering under the commandment (13th c.) - Middle English coveten, from Old French covoitier, carries into English the full weight of the Decalogue - 'thou shalt not covet' - and the word is born already a sin, the desire judged by law
- The pulpit word (medieval to early modern) - covetousness ranks among the seven capital sins, and covet sits in sermon and moral-treatise language as the longing for what belongs to another, its very sound humming with condemnation
- Intense but secularizing (18th-19th c.) - the word keeps its ardour but loosens its sin - to covet a prize, a position, or an honour is a fierce, simmering ambition, the moral edge dulled yet the heat unchanged
- The coveted object (20th-21st c.) - the adjective coveted becomes a staple of prizes, roles, and markets - the coveted award, the coveted spot - desire still burning bright, but the old pulpit thunder now a faint undertone

battlements:
- The fighting wall-top (14th c.) - from Old French bataillement, the notched parapet of castle walls, built for battle and named for it - merlons and crenels, the archer's skyline
- Romantic ruin (19th c.) - the Gothic revival turns battlements into the picturesque signature of the castle, and the defense becomes the ornament
- Castle shorthand (contemporary) - the word still names a real defensive feature but now chiefly evokes the medieval - the notched wall along which moonlight walkers pace
