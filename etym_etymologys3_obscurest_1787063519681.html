An Indo-European veil. The story of obscurest begins with the Indo-European root *(s)keu-, “to cover, conceal, hide”, one of the great “veiling” roots of the family. It produced Greek words for hiding and skin, Germanic words for shadow and shade (English sky, shy, shade), and, in Italic, the adjective obscūrus: Latin joined the prefix ob- (“over, toward”) to the “cover” base, so that the literal value of obscūrus was “covered over”, hence “dark, dim, out of the light”. Already in Latin the word spanned the physical and the mental: an obscūra night, an obscūrum speech (unclear), an obscūrus writer (little known). The whole modern polysemy is already present in the Roman adjective.

Through French to English. English received the word via Old French obscur around 1400, and the earliest English uses keep the visual sense: “dark, dim, indistinct”. In the 16th century the word climbed from the eye to the mind and to society: an obscure meaning is one the understanding cannot light up; an obscure person is one the world’s eye has not fallen upon. The superlative obscurest arises naturally in this period — English simply affixes the native -est to the borrowed adjective — and for two centuries it is unremarkable, the ordinary strongest form.

The rise of most obscure. English adjectives borrowed from French and Latin often waver between native -est and analytic most. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, most obscure won in plain prose; obscurest retreated into verse, elevated oratory, and consciously archaic narration. It became, in effect, a register marker — the superlative that tells you the speaker is reaching for the tone of an older, grander world.

Three obscurities in one word. The modern word still carries its three-layered heritage, and the superlative intensifies all three:

  • Visual obscurity — darkness, dimness, concealment: “the obscurest corners of the wood”.
  • Intellectual obscurity — difficulty, abstruseness, opacity of meaning: “the obscurest passages of the treatise”.
  • Social obscurity — anonymity, neglect, lack of fame: “the obscurest of the old poets”.

Tolkien’s archaic superlative. In the card’s context — “no songs had alluded to him even in the obscurest way” — Tolkien selects obscurest precisely because of its antique colour. The prose of The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings deliberately mixes modern narration with the register of ancient legend, and a superlative in -est is part of that texture, alongside words like upholden and yore in the neighbouring verse. The choice is not idle: obscurest makes the faintness of the allusion doubly faint — the narrator reaches for the strongest, most archaic word to say that there was no trace at all.

Sound and form. Pronounced /əbˈskjʊərɪst/, with the stress on the second syllable, the word keeps its French-derived cluster sc and the dark, “covered” feel of its Latin ancestor. It remains a live, if choice, word — poets, journalists, and storytellers still reach for obscurest when they want the strongest, most dignified form of the superlative.
