The evolution of plump: a North Sea sound-symbolic word with two faces 1. No Indo-European root: an expressive Germanic origin The most important fact about plump is that it has no Indo-European root. It belongs to the sound-symbolic, imitative stratum of the North Sea Germanic vocabulary — the family of words whose very shape mimics the sound and feel of a heavy, soft mass striking something or sinking into water. The initial pl- cluster, the short -u-, and the closing -mp combine to produce a dull, rounded, splashy acoustic image: plump. Related words across the Germanic coastal dialects tell the same story: Middle Dutch plompen and Middle Low German plumpen mean 'to plunge, to fall flat, to plump into water'; Dutch pof and German plumps and plumpen belong to the same expressive company. This is the class of vocabulary that grammarians call 'imitative' or 'onomatopoeic', and it resists the ordinary sound-law reconstruction that links most English words to PIE ancestors — not because the words are new, but because they were coined again and again in different times and places from the same imitation of sound. 2. The drop-verb: from a sound to an action The sense used on the card is the oldest English branch. In the later medieval and early modern period English, in close commercial contact with the Low Countries, took up plump first as an adverb of manner — with a sudden heavy impact, bluntly, straight down — and then built from it a verb: to fall plump, and by extension to set something down plump, to deposit it as if it were a falling mass rather than a carefully handed gift. The semantic core is kinetic and acoustic all at once: weight plus suddenness plus blunt contact. A thing plumped down is not placed; it is dropped. The verb is first recorded in the sixteenth century, and it has kept its ungraceful character ever since. Unlike the Latin-derived serving verbs of the same register, plump never sounds elegant: its history is splash, thud, and blunt motion. 3. The figurative extension: 'to plump for' From the physical decisive drop the verb developed a purely figurative use. If you come down heavily on one side of a question, you 'come down' for one answer; if you let your vote drop, you 'plump' it on a candidate. By the nineteenth century the idiom 'to plump for' had settled into standard English, meaning to choose or vote decisively and without hesitation for one person or option. The history is transparent: the physical thud of a heavy object hitting the ground became the decisiveness of a commitment. In political usage it was even more specific — to give one's whole vote to a single candidate rather than splitting it, a practice that produced the word 'plumper'. The drop-verb thus carried its whole kinetic force into the language of choice. 4. The separate branch: the adjective 'plump' Alongside the drop-verb, English took up a second word of the same spelling: the adjective plump, meaning full, rounded, well-fleshed. Its source is again the Low Countries — Middle Dutch plomp, 'blunt, thick, lumpy, dull' — where the same expressive family produced the image of something thick, soft, and heavy. In English, from the sixteenth century, the word shifted in value: what was blunt and heavy in Dutch became, in English, pleasantly full and rounded, soft and well-filled rather than coarse. The adjective applies warmly to fruit, cushions, cheeks, and figures, and it has generally kept an affectionate tone: a plump baby, plump grapes, plump pillows. Dictionaries usually treat this adjective as a separate word from the drop-verb — they are homonyms of different derivations — yet both draw on the same deep well of Germanic sound symbolism, the one capturing a heavy soft mass in motion, the other a heavy soft mass at rest. 5. Sound symbolism and the 'plump up' verb The two branches touch in the verb 'to plump up', to make something soft full again — plumping up pillows or cushions. Here the adjective's fullness and the verb's action combine: one fluffs a cushion back to roundness with a series of soft pats, exactly the motion the pl- and -ump of the word imitate. The adjective 'plump' and the 'plump up' verb belong to the same rounded, soft image, while the drop-verb and 'plump for' belong to the heavy, blunt, decisive one — two faces of one expressive family that English has kept as separate words. 6. The word in The Hobbit In Tolkien's sentence, 'Bilbo plumped down the beer and the cake in front of them, when loud came a ring at the bell again', the verb performs its whole history. Bilbo's hospitality has become physical labour under pressure: the refreshments hit the table with the same blunt, unceremonious energy as his fraying patience. The word's sound symbolism does the comic work — beer and cake deposited as a heavy, irritated fact — and the past form plumped freezes that one ungraceful gesture just as another ring at the bell undoes any hope of rest. It is a masterstroke of choosing exactly the verb whose etymology (weight, suddenness, thud) matches the scene.
