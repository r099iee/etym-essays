PIE *dek- "to take, receive, accept" (c. 4500–3000 BCE) — the root of receiving and accepting, extended in meaning to learning; cognate with Hittite takki- "to take up (a matter), reconcile," Greek δέχομαι (déchomai) "to receive, accept," and Germanic *tēkan, attested in Gothic taikns "sign, token" ("something received and recognised").
│
└──▶ Latin docēre "to teach" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE) — traditionally derived from *dek- with the causative sense "to make accept, to instruct"; the precise morphological derivation is debated (see note).
────▶ Latin doctus "taught, learned" (perfect participle of docēre, 1st c. BCE).
────────▶ Latin doctrīna "teaching, instruction, the body of knowledge taught" (attested from the 1st c. BCE).
────────────▶ Old French doctrine "teaching, doctrine" (10th–12th c.), taken up by Anglo-Norman.
────────────────▶ Middle English doctrine / doctrin "religious teaching" (first attested 14th c.).
────────────────────▶ Modern English doctrine, plural DOCTRINES "set of beliefs, principles, or teachings held and taught by a group" (sense widened to politics, law, and economics from the 17th c.).

Note on debate: the connection of Latin docēre "to teach" to PIE *dek- "to take, receive" is traditional (Pokorny, Walde) but not universally accepted. The semantic bridge — "to make someone receive" → "to teach" — is plausible and parallels the development of Greek and Gothic forms, but some scholars prefer to leave docēre with no secure PIE etymon, noting the lack of a regular formation. The stages from docēre onward are fully attested and uncontroversial.
