FROM PULLING TO DRINKING: THE FAMILY OF DRAW

Draughts is not a word that began as a noun about liquids or games; it began as a verb about pulling. Its deepest known ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰreǵʰ-, "to draw, pull, drag" — a root about the physical act of hauling something toward oneself, the root that underlies the whole Germanic family of draw and drag. In Proto-Germanic this became *draganą, "to draw, pull, carry, lead", and Old English inherited it as the strong verb dragan, "to draw, drag, carry", the direct ancestor of modern English draw. It was one of the great workhorse verbs of Old English: a smith drew metal, a team drew a load, a man drew a sword. The noun draught was then formed from the past stem of this verb, on the regular pattern by which Old English built action-nouns meaning "a drawing, that which is drawn, the result of drawing". Although the plain noun is not directly attested in surviving Old English manuscripts, it appears in early Middle English as draht in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries with exactly this meaning: a pull, a drawing, a load or portion drawn off at one time.

THE FOURTEENTH-CENTURY FAN-OUT

From this single noun of "drawing", Middle English began in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries to spin out a remarkable fan of senses, each one a new application of the same root idea. The oldest and most literal was the sense that opens this card: a draught of drink, the amount of liquid drawn into the mouth and swallowed at one go. Closely related was the draught of air — the current of cool air "drawn" into a room — recorded in English by the fourteenth century and a domestic complaint ever since ("sit by the fire, out of the draught"). At sea, the nautical draught named the depth to which a ship is "drawn down" into the water, the vertical distance from waterline to keel, a sense that mattered increasingly as ocean navigation grew in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. In hunting and fishing, a draught was a single catch, the haul "drawn up" in a net. And in the fourteenth century there appeared the sense that gives the modern plural its most famous life: the board game of draughts, so called because the flat counters are "drawn" (moved) diagonally across the chequered board, recorded in English from around 1400.

LITERARY AND CULTURAL CAREER

Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century English is full of draughts in every sense. Writers used "draught" constantly of drinking, often in contexts of pleasure, excess, or fellowship; a man took deep draughts of wine, of air, of learning, even of love — the word became a favourite metaphor for anything absorbed deeply. Shakespeare, the King James Bible, and the prose of the seventeenth century all use draughts of drink and draughts of air, and the translators drew on the word's nautical depth for passages about ships. The eighteenth century added the draught horse, the beast "drawn on" to haul the plough, and the medical draught, the measured potion to be swallowed at one gulp, prescribed by physicians and drunk in a single draught — the very sense a modern pharmacist would call a dose. Meanwhile the game of draughts, long a pastime of the common room and the fireside, kept its plural name and its simple rules, played with counters drawn forward on a chequered board.

THE SPLIT WITH AMERICA: DRAUGHT OR DRAFT

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought the decisive orthographic split. American English, led by the printing trade and the spelling reforms associated with Noah Webster, simplified draught to draft, bringing the spelling in line with the pronunciation and covering under one form the senses of air, ships, beer, money orders, conscription, and preliminary writing. British English, conservative in orthography, retained the historical draught for liquids, air, ships, animals, and the game. To a modern American ear, "draught" reads as distinctly British; to a modern British speaker, "draft" in its liquid and game senses looks like an Americanism. The drinking sense itself — "large draughts" of water — has slipped from the centre of everyday speech: modern speakers say gulps or swallows, and the draught of water now carries a slightly elevated, literary, or deliberately British flavour, which is exactly the flavour the card's sentence exploits when it speaks of drinking heartily and copiously in large draughts.

THE SOUND OF THE WORD

Phonetically the word has drifted less than its spelling suggests. The -gh- in Middle English draught once stood for a real velar fricative, pronounced something like /x/, in the cluster -ght; over the centuries this fricative was lost, the vowel lengthened before the resulting /ft/ cluster, and the modern pronunciation became /drɑːft/ (British) and /dræft/ (American). The -gh- spelling therefore survives today as a purely historical fossil, telling the story of a sound that disappeared — which is precisely why American spelling reformers, once the fricative was gone, felt free to drop the ugh and write draft. The plural form DRAUGHTS carries all of this history on its shoulders: a verb of pulling, a noun of drawing, seven centuries of sense-development, and two national spellings, all converging on the simple word for a deep swallow of water and a game of checkers.
