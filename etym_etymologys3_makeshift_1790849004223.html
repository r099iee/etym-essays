The compound noun and adjective makeshift possesses a rich morphological history that encapsulates the linguistic vitality of Early Modern English word-formation, developing through the amalgamation of two ancient Germanic verbal roots.

1. Etymology of the First Element: Make

  • The first constituent, make, descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *mag- or *mak-, meaning "to knead, fashion, fit together, or shape."
  • In Proto-Germanic, this gave rise to *makōną ("to fit, make, build"), which branched into Old High German mahhon (modern German machen) and Old Saxon makon.
  • In Anglo-Saxon England, it became Old English macian ("to make, form, construct, cause"). In Middle English, maken was one of the most productive primary verbs in the lexicon, expressing physical construction, causation, and invention.

2. Etymology of the Second Element: Shift

  • The second constituent, shift, traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *skep- or *skei-, meaning "to cut, split, divide, or separate."
  • In Proto-Germanic, this yielded *skiftaną ("to divide, distribute, arrange"). In Old English, it surfaced as sciftan ("to divide, separate, appoint, arrange"). In Old Norse, its cognate was skipta ("to change, shift, divide").
  • During the Middle English and early Tudor periods, shift developed a specialized nominal sense: "an expedient, a device, an ingenious stratagem, or a cunning contrivance" resorted to in a difficult predicament (as in the phrase "to make shift", meaning to manage with difficulty or find a way through). In Elizabethan slang, a shift was frequently a deceitful dodge or trick used by rogues and con artists.

3. The Coining of Make-shift as an Elizabethan Agent Compound:

  • Around the mid-16th century (first recorded in the 1560s), English speakers fused these elements into the rhyming/alliterative compound noun make-shift (originally formed on the pattern of verb-object agent nouns like cutpurse, pickpocket, makesport, or scarecrow).
  • In its earliest documented occurrences, a make-shift did not mean an object or temporary shelter; it was a derogatory label for a **person**—specifically an unscrupulous trickster, rogue, or impoverished parasite who lived by clever dodges, opportunistic schemes, and underhanded contrivances.
  • Writers of the Elizabethan and Jacobean drama utilized make-shift alongside terms like cony-catcher and shifter to populate the London underworld of comedic stage plays.

4. Semantic Shift from Person to Object and Contrivance:

  • During the 17th century, through a process of metonymic transference, the term shifted from the crafty person who makes shifts to the **expedient contrivance itself**.
  • By the mid-to-late 1600s, writers began referring to any temporary resource, rough substitute, or stopgap tool used in an emergency as "a makeshift."
  • By the 18th and early 19th centuries, the noun was routinely converted into an attributive adjective (e.g., "a makeshift bridge," "a makeshift bandage," "a makeshift bed"), shedding its former criminal/deceitful connotations and acquiring an aura of heroic or practical adaptability.

5. 20th-Century Philosophical and Contemporary Applications:

  • In modern intellectual prose, makeshift has been enthusiastically adopted by post-structuralist and existentialist theorists (such as Roland Barthes in *The Pleasure of the Text*, 1973) to describe psychological defense structures, artistic forms, and language itself as improvised human scaffolding built against existential abyss.
  • In everyday English, it remains the quintessential descriptor for human ingenuity under material constraint.
