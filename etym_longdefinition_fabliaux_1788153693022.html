fabliaux (singular fabliau, /ˈfæbliəʊ/, plural /ˈfæbliəʊz/) are short narrative poems in Old French: comic, realistic and frequently obscene or anticlerical, written in octosyllabic rhyming couplets and meant for the town, the tavern and the fair rather than the court. The word is the plural of fabliau, which in the French Middle Ages designated a ‘little fable’ — a brief, told-for-laughs story. About 150 fabliaux survive from the period c. 1160–1340, most of them anonymous, some the work of named poets such as Jean Bodel, Rutebeuf, Henri d’Andeli and Gautier le Leu.

Primary sense — the genre. In literary history the term denotes a body of medieval French verse whose staple is the trick: the cuckolded husband, the clever or voracious wife, the lecherous priest, the naive clerk, the merchant fooled by his own greed. The tales are usually short (often 100–400 lines), plot-driven, and told with an altogether unromantic eye: they record money, food, beds, beatings and lawsuits. Their realism is the realism of the lower and middle urban world — Josse the moneylender, Hercelin the peasant, Boivin the swindler — and their morality, when they have one, is the morality of the winner.

Secondary senses and extensions.

  • Scholarly generic term: since the 19th-century philological revival (Bédier’s Les Fabliaux, 1893), ‘fabliau’ (often plural fabliaux) is used in English and French criticism as the generic label for short comic verse tales, and is extended to Middle English analogues (Chaucer’s Miller’s Tale, Reeve’s Tale, Shipman’s Tale) and to continental imitations.
  • Source-study shorthand: in source criticism, as in Pater’s essay, ‘the old French fabliaux’ name the northern comic tradition on which Boccaccio and Chaucer drew — the bridge between the medieval tale and the Renaissance novella.
  • Modern figurative use: ‘fabliau’ is occasionally used admiringly of any sly, compact comic narrative — ‘an Elizabethan fabliau’, ‘a latter-day fabliau’ — keeping the genre-word alive outside medieval studies.
  • Technical details kept by dictionaries: the singular/plural pair (fabliau/fabliaux, with the French plural -x), the italicised loanword status, and the anglicised pronunciation are all fixed usage points of the word today.

Semantic bridge. The word itself performs its history: fable (Latin fābula, ‘story’) shrunk by a diminutive suffix (-el, -eau) into ‘little story’; the little story in twelfth-century Picardy turned out to be comic by profession; and when the genre died out, 19th-century scholarship revived the old word as a precise label. Every modern sense — genre, source-tradition, extended metaphor — is the old ‘little fable’ grown scholarly, but still telling the same tavern jokes.

Register and tone. In medieval studies the word is technical and neutral; in general writing it carries a whiff of scandal — ‘bawdy fabliaux’, ‘scabrous fabliaux’ — and in source criticism it is precise (‘X borrowed from the fabliaux’). It is a plurale tantum in common critical talk (‘the fabliaux’), with the singular reserved for the individual poem.
