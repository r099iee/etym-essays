- Old English wær "watchful, prudent": the ancient Germanic adjective (kin to Latin verērī "to fear, revere") — perception: sober, wisdom-adjacent, the vigilance of a sentry.
- Middle English war/ware "cautious, aware": the living root of "beware!" and "aware"; the bare form works as adjective and warning alike.
- 16th century: "wary" (ware + -y) is coined and gradually replaces the bare form; Elizabethan usage is practical and strategic — a wary commander, a wary negotiator.
- 18th–19th centuries: fixed as the standard word for prudent, defensive caution; slightly cold — it describes shrewdness that suspects rather than trusts.
- Today: everyday and mildly formal; "wary of" governs ordinary risks (scams, strangers, novelty, commitment); the word still carries a faint sentry's alertness, old as the Germanic watch itself.
