Hottentots is the historical English plural of Hottentot, itself a borrowing from 17th-century Dutch Hottentot used by settlers at the Cape Colony for the Khoikhoi pastoralist peoples of southwestern Africa. Dutch hottentot is universally analyzed as imitative / onomatopoeic: hotten-tot mimics stuttering or the distinctive click consonants of Khoekhoe (with ǀ, ǁ, ǃ, ǂ clicks) unintelligible to Dutch ears. Contemporary Dutch glosses explain it as "one who stammers, stutters," from hotten-totten "to stutter, to stammer" in older Dutch dialects. It was thus from inception an exonym mocking speech, not an autonym; the people call themselves Khoikhoi (more precisely Khoekhoen) meaning "people of people, real people," speaking Khoekhoe, part of the Khoe-Kwadi family.

First Dutch attestations appear in the 1650s-1670s after Jan van Riebeeck founded Cape Town (1652). English travellers quickly borrowed it: Thomas Herbert (1677), William Dampier and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society use Hottentot by the 1670s-1680s. The plural Hottentots follows regular English -s, while Dutch used Hottentotten / Hottentots. French adopted Hottentot, German Hottentotte, Portuguese Hotentote, spreading the term through Enlightenment ethnology.

  • Colonial contact (1652-1800): Dutch and then British writers use Hottentot descriptively but increasingly contemptuously, contrasting "Hottentots" with "Caffres / Kaffirs" (Bantu-speakers) and "Bushmen / Bosjesmans" (San hunter-gatherers). Travelogues dwell on language clicks, pastoral customs, and sharing practices, sometimes admiringly as in the food-sharing passage quoted.
  • Enlightenment racial science (1700s-1800s): Linnaeus (Systema Naturae), Buffon, Blumenbach classify Hottentot as a distinct racial type; the infamous exhibition of Sara Baartman as the "Hottentot Venus" in London and Paris (1810-1815) cements dehumanizing sexualized connotations in European popular culture.
  • Victorian anthropology (1860s-1930s): Hottentots appears ubiquitously in Morgan, Tylor, Frazer as a stock example of "primitive communism," levelling and hospitality. The term is still standard scholarly vocabulary, though already recognized as slang.
  • Apartheid and reclamation (1948-present): Under South African apartheid, Hottentot becomes a legally and socially offensive slur; post-1994 democratic South Africa treats it as hate speech (comparable to Kaffir). Scholarship, museums and the communities themselves insist on Khoikhoi / Khoekhoe / Nama. Modern style guides (OED, Britannica) label Hottentot offensive, archaic, to be avoided except in historical quotation.

Phonologically English retains Dutch /ˈhɔtəntɔt/ adapted as /ˈhɒtənˌtɒt/ with plural /s/. No PIE root is traceable because the word is imitative Dutch slang, not inherited vocabulary; attempts to link tot to a Germanic root for "totter" are folk etymology. Debate: some older dictionaries suggested Hottentot derived from Hautentot (a supposed tribal name), but modern etymologists (OED, van Herk, Nienaber) agree unanimously on the stutter-imitative origin, citing parallel Dutch mock-names like Kaffir (Arabic kafir) and Stotterer. The sharing custom described — surplus shared till all equal — is ethnographically accurate for Khoikhoi pastoralists with strong redistribution norms, though early sources romanticized it.

Today Hottentots survives only in historical texts, museum labels in transition, and offensive colloquial Afrikaans Hotnot. Learners encountering it in Morgan, Engels or 19th-century travel writing must understand it refers to Khoikhoi peoples and must not use it for living people.
