- Old English — leaping bodies: springan means to leap, burst forth, or fly up, of warriors, water, and sunbeams alike.
- Early Middle Ages — fountains and mornings: springs of water and the spring of day extend the leap to everything that bursts forth.
- 13th–14th c. — the season takes the name: the springing-time shortens to spring, the year's great leap back to life.
- 15th–17th c. — coils and traps: metal springs borrow the leap for their stored energy, and springing locks and springing snares enter the language.
- 18th–19th c. — idioms of surprise: springing news, springing leaks, springing questions — the trap's suddenness becomes social.
- Today — pure vigour: springing to attention, to defence, to the feet — the participle of readiness, elastic and optimistic at every age.
