PIE *sem- 'one, together, as one' (reconstructed; c. 4th millennium BCE) → Latin similis 'like, similar, resembling' (derived from *sem-l-i-; attested 2nd-1st c. BCE) → Latin simulāre 'to make like, to imitate, to feign' (1st c. BCE, e.g. in Cicero) → Latin dissimulāre 'to make unlike, to disguise, to conceal' (prefix dis- 'apart, un-' + simulāre; attested from Plautus and Cicero, 2nd-1st c. BCE) → Old French dissembler / dissimuler 'to disguise, dissemble' (12th-13th c.; the -semb- form reshaped under sembler 'to seem, appear') → Middle English dissemblen 'to pretend, to conceal' (late 14th c., e.g. in Chaucer) → Modern English dissemble 'to conceal one's true motives, feelings, or beliefs' (16th c.)

Note on debate: The transmission of the -semb- form is not fully transparent. Old French dissembler may be a direct continuation of Latin dissimulāre whose root was re-formed by analogy with sembler 'to seem', or an independent Gallo-Romance formation on the similis/sembler family. English also borrowed the learned doublet dissimulate directly from Latin dissimulātus in the 15th century, so the two words coexist.
