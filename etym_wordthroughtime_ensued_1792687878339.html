- [Late 14th c. Middle English] — Borrowed from Anglo-Norman *ensuer* / Old French *ensuir* (from Latin *insequī*); originally used in two distinct senses: to actively chase/pursue (*ensue after righteousness*) and to follow in time or result (*as ensuing events demonstrated*) — [perception: an elevated, learned term of law, theology, and chivalric literature]
- [15th–16th c. Early Modern English] — The active sense of "pursuing or striving for a goal" was common in biblical translations (e.g., Tyndale and Coverdale: *"seek peace and ensue it"*); simultaneously used in legal and royal succession contexts — [perception: regarded as a virtuous, earnest verb of moral seeking as well as orderly legal succession]
- [17th–18th c. Classical & Enlightenment Era] — The active transitive sense ("to pursue") gradually became obsolete or archaic, fossilized only in scripture; the intransitive sense ("to happen afterward, to result") became dominant in historical and philosophical writing — [perception: valued as a precise, formal verb for chronicling the cause-and-effect narrative of history]
- [19th c. Romantic & Victorian Era] — Romantic poets like Wordsworth used *ensued* in introspective autobiographical narrative (*"I spare to tell of what ensued"*); Victorian novels favored dramatic scenes where panic, silence, or commotion *ensued* — [perception: established as a polished literary marker of dramatic consequences and narrative transition]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Era] — Widely used in journalism, legal reporting, and narrative prose to describe chaotic, spontaneous, or logical aftermaths (*"pandemonium ensued"*, *"a heated debate ensued"*) — [perception: treated as an elegant, evocative verb of sequential aftermath, though occasionally bordering on a journalistic cliché when paired with "chaos" or "riot"]
