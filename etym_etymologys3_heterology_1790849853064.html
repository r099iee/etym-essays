The etymological composition of heterology represents an erudite neoclassical compounding of two primordial Greek roots, whose intellectual journey reflects the evolution of scientific taxonomy and continental philosophy over two centuries.

The constituent morphological elements trace back to Proto-Indo-European roots:

  • ἕτερος (héteros): Derived from PIE *sm̥-kero- (a compound of *sem- "one" and the contrastive comparative suffix *-teros), originally signifying "the other of two," "different," or "second." In classical Greek, héteros stood in sharp conceptual contrast to allos (another among many) and homos (the same), functioning as the standard marker for duality, difference, and deviation.
  • λόγος (lógos) / -λογία (-logía): Derived from the PIE verbal root *leǵ- ("to gather, collect," and by extension "to speak, reckon, account for"). In Greek antiquity, lógos expanded into an immense philosophical umbrella encompassing speech, reason, principle, ratio, and systematic inquiry, eventually crystalizing into the ubiquitous scientific suffix -logy ("the systematic study or doctrine of").

When combined, the compound hétérologie first surfaced in 19th-century French scientific nomenclature (rapidly anglicized as heterology in Victorian medical compendiums). Early nineteenth-century pathologists, notably influenced by German comparative anatomy and French histology (such as the work of Xavier Bichat and later Rudolf Virchow), required precise morphological terminology to distinguish between homologous tissues (those identical to the host structure) and heterologous tissues (those exhibiting divergent, foreign, or anomalous cell structures). In this initial scientific phase, heterology was strictly empirical, denoting histological deviation and abnormal structural differentiation.

A profound epistemological revolution occurred in the late 1920s and 1930s when the French writer and philosopher Georges Bataille seized upon the term. Dissatisfied with Hegelian idealism and traditional scientific materialism—which he argued attempted to homogenize and assimilate all reality into tidy, productive rational categories—Bataille proposed hétérologie as an anti-idealist "science of what is completely other." Bataille's heterology embraced everything that homogeneous society expels: filth, bodily fluids, erotic delirium, sacrificial destruction, and ecstatic loss. For Bataille, heterology was not merely a passive study, but an active intellectual stance against the totalitarian impulse to make everything "the same."

Following Bataille, the term was enthusiastically embraced by French post-structuralists. Michel de Certeau published Heterologies: Discourse on the Other, examining how historical writing negotiates the voices of the silenced and marginalized. Roland Barthes applied heterology to textual pleasure, arguing that avant-garde writing (such as Severo Sarduy's Cobra) achieves a "heterology by plenitude"—an overwhelming, joyous proliferation of signifiers that shatters the uniform, sterile codes of conventional communication. Thus, heterology completed its extraordinary semantic drift from histological abnormality to the vanguard of radical postmodern philosophy.
