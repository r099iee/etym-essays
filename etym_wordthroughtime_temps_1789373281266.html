- 11th to 13th Century - Emerges in Old French as *tens* / *temps* from Latin *tempus*; simultaneously designates cosmic time, historical ages, seasonal weather changes, and grammatical verb tenses in early Anglo-Norman chronicles and Arthurian romances.
- 14th to 16th Century - Fixed orthographically with the etymological Latin 'p' (*temps*); flourishes in Renaissance music theory and fencing manuals (*temps d'escrime*), where it comes to signify a discrete rhythmic beat and a precise interval of combat opportunity.
- 17th to 18th Century - Codified in the royal academies of Louis XIV as a fundamental technical term in classical ballet choreography (*temps levé*, *temps lié*) and French military drill manuals (*mouvements en plusieurs temps*).
- 19th Century - Standardized across industrial manufacturing, clockwork production, and public transport schedules; French naturalists and meteorologists formalize the distinction between atmospheric *temps* (weather) and chronological *temps* (time).
- 1913 to 1927 - Marcel Proust publishes *À la recherche du temps perdu*, elevating *temps* into the supreme metaphysical hero and villain of modern European fiction, exploring the tension between destructive temporal succession and transcendent artistic memory.
- Mid-20th Century to Present - French urban planners and traffic engineers introduce the civic pedestrian safety instruction *traversez en deux temps* across major metropolitan boulevards; simultaneously in modern Anglophone corporate culture, *temps* develops as a widespread colloquial noun for temporary agency workers.
