- 16th c. - the verb gloom enters English meaning "to look sullen or frown"; it describes a person's dark, lowering countenance, not yet a property of places.
- 17th c. - the noun is coined: first "a sullen look", then extended to environmental darkness, "partial or total obscurity"; the word migrates from the human face to the world around it.
- 18th c. - the physical sense settles as dim, murky darkness; Gothic and graveyard poets adopt gloomy, so that darkness acquires a deliberate mood of dread and claustrophobia.
- 18th–19th c. - the emotional sense hardens into "melancholy, despondency"; one noun now names both a dark place and a sad heart, and Romantic prose harvests the double sense.
- 19th–20th c. - psychological gloom rises to dominance in everyday speech ("gloom and doom", "a mood of gloom", "cast a gloom over"); the physical darkness sense retreats into literary and atmospheric use.
- 20th–21st c. - the word survives chiefly as a mood-word — economic gloom, market gloom — while in fiction it still names a heavy, oppressive darkness almost old enough to be a substance; its emotional weight now outweighs its literal dimness.
