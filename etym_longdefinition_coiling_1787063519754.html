COILING is the present participle (and gerund) of the verb coil: the action of arranging something in a spiral or a series of concentric rings, of winding a rope, cable, snake, or other flexible length round and round itself, or of throwing anything into a circular heap. The word is dynamic and continuous — coiling pictures the movement in mid-progress, the turning winding tighter as you watch. In the source sentence — "...and then coiling his length together, roaring like thunder underground..." — the dragon gathers his immense serpentine body into folded rings, compressing his whole length in order to launch himself out through the gate: the participle renders the action fluid and ongoing, a great winding compaction that precedes the explosive dash.

Primary senses

  • To wind into rings (physical): to twist a flexible thing (rope, hose, hair, snake, cable, wire) so that it lies in a spiral or a set of concentric loops: "she coiled the garden hose by the tap".
  • To twist round something (encircling): to wind one thing round another, or round itself: "the python coiled itself around the branch".
  • To lie in a spiral (state): to rest arranged as a coil: "the cat lay coiled on the rug".
  • Of motion, smoke, scent, water: to move upward or onward in spiralling rings: "smoke coiled from the chimney".

Secondary and figurative senses

  • Nautical/technical: the oldest native idiom is the sailor's coiling of rope into rings on deck — the word's first recorded English uses are maritime.
  • Electrical/mechanical: a "coil" is a spirally wound wire or tube — an electromagnetic coil, ignition coil, coil spring, coil of pipe — and "coiling" describes the manufacturing or motion of such spirals.
  • Figurative tension: "coiling" describes gathering emotional or physical pressure before release — coiled energy, a coiling dread, a spring coiled to strike. This exactly matches the dragon: he coils his length before the thunderous dash.
  • Anatomical/biological: coiling of the intestines, coiling of DNA helices, coiling of snail shells — spirality as a deep structural principle of nature.

Semantic bridge between the senses is the spiral itself: gathering rope into a circle, encircling a branch, smoke spiralling up, and a serpent folding its length are all the same motion — the folding of a line around a centre. The nautical origin is important: the English verb was born aboard ship, and from rope-coiling it spread to springs, wire, serpents, and smoke.

Connotations and register

  • Neutral-technical in seafaring and engineering; sinuous and menacing in descriptions of snakes and dragons; elegant and dreamlike of smoke and scent.
  • The participle form coiling stresses duration — the winding is still happening — and is therefore preferred in narrative prose to set a slow, gathering motion against a sudden, explosive one (as here).

In the source text, coiling works as a present participle in an absolute construction: the dragon, having thrust his head in vain at the little hole, folds his length together — a vivid serpentine compacting — and then moves. The participle makes the compression visible and slow, creating the held breath before the roar and the dash, a rhythm the verb's past or finite forms would flatten.
