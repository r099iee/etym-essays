SCABBARD is a compound of two Germanic elements: the 'sheath' element (Proto-Germanic *skaþō-) and the 'board' element (Proto-Germanic *bordą). Each element has its own chain, and the compound has its own chain. CHAIN 1 — 'sheath' element (*skaþō-): - PIE *sek- 'to cut' (reconstructed; c. 4500-3500 BCE) [presumed source of the sheath-word via the idea of a cut/shaped covering; debated — see note] → Proto-Germanic *skaþō- 'sheath, casing, outer covering of bark or hide' (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-500 CE) → Old English scēað 'sheath' (c. 700-1100) → Middle English schethe 'sheath' (c. 1200-1500) → Modern English sheath 'close-fitting blade-cover' (1500-present) CHAIN 2 — 'board' element (*bordą): → Proto-Germanic *bordą 'board, plank' (reconstructed; c. 500 BCE-500 CE) → Old English bord 'board, plank' (c. 700-1100) → Middle English bord 'board, plank' (c. 1200-1500) → Modern English board (1500-present) CHAIN 3 — the compound 'SCABBARD' itself: - Frankish (West Germanic) *skabard / *skaþ-bard 'board-sheath', i.e. a sword-case made of two wooden slats bound in hide or leather (reconstructed; c. 500-800 CE) → Old French escauborc / escalberc / eschauberc 'sheath for a sword' (attested c. 1000-1100) → Anglo-Norman escawberc / scauberc 'sword-sheath' (brought to England after the Norman Conquest, after 1066) → Middle English scabbard / scabert 'sword-sheath' (attested c. 1275-1400) → Modern English scabbard (1400-present) → plural scabbards → SCABBARDS NOTE ON DEBATE: the precise ancestor of Proto-Germanic *skaþō- is debated; the link to PIE *sek- 'to cut' is plausible (the sheath as something cut or shaped around the blade) but not universally accepted. The Frankish compound is reconstructed, not directly attested in writing, and its exact form (*skabard- vs *skaþ-bard-) is uncertain; the Old French variants escauborc/escalberc/eschauberc show the expected Romance reshaping of Germanic clusters. All reconstructed stages before Old French are therefore best treated as educated reconstructions rather than attested forms.
