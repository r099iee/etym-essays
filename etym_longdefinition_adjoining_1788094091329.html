Adjoining is an adjective (the participial form of the verb adjoin) meaning “next to and sharing a boundary or opening with; contiguous; neighbouring”. Two things are adjoining when they actually touch or connect: an adjoining room shares a wall or a door with the next room; adjoining land shares a boundary line. The word implies direct physical contact, not mere proximity — a house two streets away is near but not adjoining. In the source context, “The rest went into the adjoining cellar with the trapdoors,” the cellar is connected to the main cellar through trapdoors: the elves open them from above, and the barrels (with the dwarves inside) drop through into the river, which flows under both cellars — the word’s precise sense of sharing a boundary and a connection is what makes the escape route intelligible.

Primary senses

  • Of rooms and buildings — “an adjoining room”, “adjoining houses”, “the adjoining wing of the palace”: the most common modern use; in hotels and real estate, “adjoining rooms” specifically means rooms connected by a door (contrast adjacent rooms, which may merely be next door without a connecting door).
  • Of land and estates — “adjoining fields”, “adjoining properties”: sharing a common boundary; in property law the term carries precise consequences for rights of way, easements, party walls, and liability.
  • Of abstract contiguity — “adjoining paragraphs”, “adjoining pages”, “adjoining sections of text”: anything that touches along an edge.

Grammar and morphology

  • The verb adjoin is intransitive in its spatial sense — “the garden adjoins the orchard” — and was transitive in its older sense “to join or attach something to something else” (“to adjoin a clause to a treaty”).
  • Adjoining is the present participle used adjectivally; its near-synonym adjacent, from Latin iacēre “to lie”, stresses position rather than contact.
  • The word is morphologically transparent: ad- “to, towards” + join “to connect”, from Latin adiungere “to fasten to”.

Semantic bridge: the original Latin verb adiungere meant “to attach or fasten one thing to another” — an active, transitive act of joining. In English the word’s centre of gravity shifted from the act of joining to the resulting state: by the 1560s adjoin meant “to lie next to”, and the participle adjoining became a pure spatial adjective describing things that already stand joined or touching. The modern sense is therefore static and descriptive — it reports a relation (shared wall, shared boundary) rather than an action.

Register and nuance

  • Neutral-to-formal: adjoining is standard in legal, real-estate, and technical prose, and slightly bookish in casual speech, where “next door” or “right next to” is more common.
  • Precise: unlike near or close, it requires contact; unlike surrounding, it does not imply enclosure on all sides; unlike attached, it does not require that the two things were built or made as one unit.
  • Technical distinction (surveying and law): adjoining and abutting both imply contact, but abutting often stresses that one property actually touches another along a specific line (as in “abutting owners”); contiguous is the formal Latinate synonym for things sharing a common edge; conterminous / coterminous means sharing exactly the same boundary or extent.

Word family: adjoin (verb), adjacent, adjacency, adjunct (something joined on, as a professor or a note), adjunction (grammar and mathematics), and the whole join / joint / junction / conjunction family from the same Latin iungere.
