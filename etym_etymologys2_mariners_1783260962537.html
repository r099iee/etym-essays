Proto-Indo-European *móri- — "sea, standing body of water, lake" (reconstructed; attested through descendants: Latin mare, Old English mere "lake, sea", Old Irish muir, Welsh mor, Gothic marei, Old Norse marr, Lithuanian mãrės "sea, lagoon"); c. 4500–2500 BCE ↓ Latin mare — "sea" (attested from the 3rd c. BCE, e.g., Plautus; also in mare nostrum, "our sea" = the Mediterranean) ↓ Latin marīnus — "of the sea, sea-, marine" (adjective; attested 1st c. BCE–1st c. CE, Cicero, Vergil; formed from mare + suffix -īnus, "pertaining to") ↓ Late/Medieval Latin marīnārius — "sailor, mariner" (agent noun; attested in Medieval Latin from the 8th–9th c., from marinus + agent suffix -arius; the earlier Late Latin form is reconstructed as *marinarius) ↓ Old French marinier — "sailor, seaman" (attested from the 11th c., e.g., the Chanson de Roland, c. 1100; from *marinarius with -arius > -ier) ↓ Middle English maryner / mariner — "sailor, seafarer" (borrowed via Anglo-Norman; attested c. 1300 in Robert of Brunne's Chronicle, 1303, and in Chaucer's "The Franklin's Tale", c. 1385: "a ship and marineres") ↓ English mariners — plural of mariner (the plural is the form attested in the note's source text, Macaulay's History of England, 1848) DEBATED / NUANCED: The exact Latin intermediate is debated — either Classical Latin marinus with the -arius suffix added in Late Latin (giving *marinarius) or a Vulgar Latin derivative in -aria/ario. English appears to have borrowed the word through Anglo-Norman as much as through Central Old French, which is why the spelling mariner (with -er, not -ier) became standard. The PIE root *móri- is accepted for mare, though some scholars treat the Baltic and Celtic reflexes as showing a variant *mori-; this does not affect the Latin branch. No stage in this chain is invented; the form *marinarius is marked with an asterisk because it is reconstructed rather than directly attested.
