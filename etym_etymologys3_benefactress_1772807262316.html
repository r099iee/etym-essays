Section 1 — the history of “bene / bonus” (the good-half) Every word in the family begins with a small word meaning “well.” Latin bene, the adverb “well,” is the older relative of bonus, the adjective “good,” and the Latin record itself preserves the trace of an older, fuller form: the oldest inscription of the word, the famous Duenos inscription of central Italy (dating roughly to the seventh to fifth centuries BCE), spells the good-word duenos, and Old Latin duonos appears beside it. Scholars reconstruct an adjective something like *du̯eno-, meaning “fitting, worthy, good,” and de Vaan’s treatment of Latin connects bonus and bene to this Proto-Indo-European adjectival base. The reconstruction is not without difficulty—the Indo-European “good” vocabulary is notoriously fragmented—but the Latin internal history is secure: duenos “good” → bonus “good” → bene “well.” The wider Indo-European family is sparse and contested. Whereas the ordinary Germanic words for “good” (English good, German gut, Dutch goed, Gothic gods) descend from a completely different Proto-Germanic *gōdaz, whose deeper PIE root is usually given as *gʰedʰ-, “to unite, to fit together,” the Latin word belongs to a different, smaller group. Greek agathos, the standard classical word for “good,” is unrelated. The practical consequence for English is that the good-half of benefactress is a pure Latin import: no native English speaker would recognize in good the twin of bene, and the “well” of the compound entered English as a learned Latin element in the fifteenth century. It is worth stressing the point because the semantic transparency we feel in benefactress—“well-doing”—is a transparency of Latin learning, not of Germanic inheritance. The career of bene in the classical world is that of a fashionable intensifier: it moved from “well” in the plain sense (bene facere, “to act well”) to an adverb of approval (“indeed,” “excellently”), so that in colloquial Latin bene could answer “fine, good” to the question “how are you?”—a habit inherited by the Romance languages (French bien, Italian bene, Spanish bien), which all carry the Latin word into the modern world as their ordinary word for “well.” Meanwhile the adjective bonus organized an entire moral vocabulary: bonus vir “a good man,” bona fides “good faith,” bonum “the good.” The adverbial branch, however, is the one relevant to benefactress, and its path is short and direct: a PIE adjective of fitness, made over into Latin’s adjective of goodness, flattened into the productive adverb “well,” and then frozen into the first syllable of a learned English agent noun. That first syllable matters for the passage’s tone. Because bene carries the full Roman moral weight of the good, the word benefactress in English is never merely descriptive: it declares that the woman named has done good in an absolute, virtuous sense. When the speaker calls the enslaved Ying-lian Yu-cun’s “great benefactress,” the Latin moral halo of bene is part of the irony—the girl who did nothing herself inherits the virtue of her father’s benevolence, and the word’s Latin learning makes the compliment at once ceremonial and cutting. Section 2 — the history of “facere” (the do-half) The second syllable of benefactress is one of the great doing-roots of the Indo-European world. Latin facere, “to do, make,” descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeh₁-, “to put, place, do,” one of the most securely attested verbal roots in the family. Its sisters show the root at its most ancient: Sanskrit dadhāti, “he puts,” and Greek títhēmi, “I place,” preserve the concrete “put, set” sense (and through Greek give English thesis, theme, and the -thec- of bibliothec), while the Germanic branch, Proto-Germanic *dōną, gives the most familiar doing-verbs of all: English do and deed, German tun, Dutch doen. The root is so widespread because its meaning—placing, setting, making—is the raw material of human action; a culture names its commonest verb of agency from it, and Latin’s facere is simply the Latin face of that universal verb. Latin built an enormous productive machinery on facere. The verb itself is daily in Latin texts of every period, from the Twelve Tables to the Church Fathers. Around it Latin constructed agent nouns in -tor (factor, “maker, doer”), verbal adjectives and nouns (factum “deed, thing done,” the source of English fact and feat), and a long series of prefixed compounds whose English descendants are everywhere: conficere → confect, perficere → perfect, officere → office, sufficere → suffice, reficere → refection, benedicere → benediction (“speaking well,” a blessing), and benefacere → benefactor. The prefix bene- combined with facere to make benefacere, “to do well,” “to do good to,” and from that verb Latin formed the agent noun benefactor, “one who does good,” together with its feminine benefactrix. In the hands of Roman moralists and, above all, of the Church, the “doing well” of benefactor hardened into the specialized meaning “patron, one who confers benefits,” because the most concrete way of doing good was endowment and protection. When English borrowed the word in the fifteenth century (by way of Latin and Old French), it borrowed the whole package: benefactor arrives as a formal, elevated word for a patron or helper, and the family widens with benefice, benefaction, and beneficence, each retaining the root’s stamp. The facere half of the word, in other words, is doing itself—the concept of agency, of making real effects in the world—and that is precisely the sense required by the Yu-cun scene: Ying-lian’s father did good, acted, conferred; his agency, remembered years later, is what the word celebrates. A benefactress is therefore not a woman who merely wishes well but a woman whose acts have changed another person’s life, and the word reserves its full force for exactly that kind of transforming action. The double meaning of facere also explains the word’s dignity. Because the root means both “to do” and “to make,” a benefactor is both an agent and a maker: he makes benefits the way a craftsman makes goods, and the benefits are real objects in the moral world—scholarships, buildings, rescued careers. In the recognition scene this making-power is transferred from Zhen Shi-yin to his daughter by the single word benefactress, so that the girl, who can make nothing in her chains, is credited with the greatest making of all: the making of Yu-cun’s career. Section 3 — the history of “-ess” (the feminine suffix) The tail of benefactress, the suffix -ess, is a small Romance word with a Greek past. Greek formed feminine agent and role nouns with the suffix -ισσα (-issa), as in basílissa “queen” (the feminine of basileus “king”), and the Greek feminine words passed into the church and chancery vocabulary of the Roman world, where Late Latin borrowed the suffix as -issa: abbatissa “abbess,” prophetissa “prophetess.” Old French inherited the pattern as -esse, so that Anglo-French and Middle English acquired it early, and English has used -ess since the thirteenth century to feminize agent nouns: goddess, hostess, huntress, actress, heiress, enchantress, and so on. The suffix is thus a genuine European layer—Greek in origin, Latin and French in transmission, English in use—and it carries that whole history into every word it forms. The history of -ess in English is the history of gendering professions, and its fortunes track the status of women in public life. In the medieval and early modern world, where most public roles were closed to women, -ess forms named the female counterpart of a male role: an abbess was the female head of a convent, a patroness the female patron, an actress the female actor. The forms were not demeaning; they simply recorded that the role existed in a feminine version. Benefactress appears in the seventeenth century precisely in this way, as the natural feminine of benefactor in the patronage discourse of the age—a woman who endows, supports, or saves. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novels and periodicals use benefactress freely, often with a touch of ceremony: the benefactress of a school, of the poor, of a struggling artist. The twentieth century changed the suffix’s status. As women entered the same professions as men, the feminine marking of the noun came to look either old-fashioned or belittling—actor displaced actress in many registers, author displaced authoress, and the marked -ess forms retreated toward the archaic and the literary. Benefactress followed the same curve: today it sounds slightly dated and ceremonial, available to period fiction and pointed rhetoric, while neutral modern prose says female benefactor, patron, or simply benefactor. That stylistic drift matters for the passage: the story, set in an earlier world, uses the ceremonious form, and the ceremony is part of the irony—the great female benefactor of the passage is, in fact, a helpless girl sold into slavery. A final morphological note. The suffix in benefactress attaches to a fully established masculine noun, which is the normal English pattern; the compound thus has a layered history—a Greek suffix riding on a Latin agent noun built on a PIE verbal root, all wrapped around a PIE adjective of goodness. Each layer is itself a complete historical episode, and together they make benefactress a perfect exhibit of how European learned vocabulary accumulates: benefactress is, in the last analysis, “she who does well,” with the doing (facere), the goodness (bene), and the womanhood (-ess) each carrying two millennia of history.
