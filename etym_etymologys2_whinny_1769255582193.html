Chain for whinny: 1. PIE *kʷey- / *key- "to hiss, whistle, make a sharp sound" (reconstructed, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; onomatopoeic in origin) — note: because the base is imitative, a secure PIE reconstruction is debated; many scholars treat the Germanic stem as echoic rather than inherited. 2. Proto-Germanic *hwīnaną "to whistle, whiz, whine" (c. 500 BCE–1st c. CE; attested by Old Norse hvīna "to whistle, whiz," Old High German hwīnan, Gothic hweinan). 3. Old English hwīnan "to whine, whistle, make a whistling sound" (attested c. 9th–10th c. CE, e.g., in describing the flight of an arrow or the wind). 4. Old English hwinnian "to whinny, neigh gently" — a nasalized iterative/frequentative formation on hwīnan (attested c. 10th c. CE); this nasal variant is the direct ancestor of the modern word. 5. Middle English whynien / whinien "to neigh softly, to whinny" (attested c. 14th–15th c. CE; also spelled whinyen, whynyen). 6. Modern English whinny (verb and noun, from the 16th c. onward; the noun "a whinny" is recorded from the early 17th c.). Debated / nuanced note: The proposed PIE etymon *kʷey- is conjectural because the whole word family (whine, whinny, whinge) is sound-symbolic. The Germanic stem *hwīnaną is well attested across the daughter languages, but whether it continues a true PIE root or arose independently as onomatopoeia within Germanic cannot be settled with certainty; no non-Germanic cognate is universally accepted. The stage Old English hwinnian (with its doubled nasal) is the key bridge that gave the word its distinctive form — the -n- infix that produced "whinny" rather than "whine".
