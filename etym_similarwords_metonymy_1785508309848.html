• Metaphor: Similar because both are master tropes that transfer a name from one thing to another; differs because metaphor works by resemblance across distinct domains ("Juliet is the sun"), whereas metonymy works by association within a single domain and never asserts likeness.
  • Synecdoche: Similar because both substitute one expression for another by contiguity; differs because synecdoche strictly exchanges part and whole ("all hands on deck", "Germany won"), whereas metonymy extends to any contiguity — container, cause, place, product, instrument — beyond the part-whole axis.
  • Metalepsis: Similar because it is a figure of substitution closely allied to metonymy; differs because metalepsis is a strained or compressed double metonymy travelling along a chain of associations ("gray beard" for old age), far more literary and recondite than everyday metonymy.
  • Antonomasia: Similar because it replaces one name with another associated expression; differs because antonomasia specifically substitutes an epithet or title for a proper name ("the Iron Duke" for Wellington), while metonymy operates on common nouns, objects, and institutional reference.
  • Irony: Similar because it is one of the four classical master tropes of rhetoric; differs because irony works by reversal and opposition, saying the opposite of what is meant, while metonymy works by association and contiguity and always retains literal relevance.
  • Periphrasis: Similar because both name something indirectly; differs because periphrasis expands a reference into a circumlocution ("the city of the Caesars" for Rome), whereas metonymy compresses one word into another and usually shortens rather than lengthens.
  • Symbol: Similar because a symbol also stands for an associated idea or institution (the flag, the cross); differs because a symbol is a stable, culturally coded sign with a fixed conventional meaning, while metonymy is a productive rhetorical operation performed anew in each utterance.
  • Allegory: Similar because both communicate a second layer of meaning beyond the literal; differs because allegory is an extended sustained narrative in which a whole story stands for another story, whereas metonymy is a single-word or single-phrase substitution within a sentence.
