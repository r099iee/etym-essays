Repertoire denotes the complete list or stock of works a performer or company is prepared to perform, and by extension any stock of available roles, routines, skills or responses. An opera house has a repertoire of Verdi and Mozart, an actor has a repertoire of gestures, a teacher has a repertoire of explanations, a society has a repertoire of roles.

The theatrical sense is primary: repertory theatre keeps several plays in rotation, as opposed to long runs. A soprano repertoire includes lyric and coloratura parts suited to her voice. A jazz trio repertoire includes standards and originals. To enter the repertoire means to become a standard performable work. To extend beyond repertoire is to attempt the unsuitable.

The sociological extension in the prompt treats social life as drama: society offers a recognizable repertoire of roles — parent, worker, rebel, clown — from which individuals cast themselves. Similarly linguists speak of verbal repertoire, psychologists of behavioral repertoire, immunologists of antibody repertoire. In each case the metaphor is stock-in-trade ready for performance.

  • Theatrical stock: plays, operas, ballets a company can stage without new rehearsal from scratch.
  • Musical stock: pieces a musician has mastered and can play on demand, e.g. her Bach repertoire.
  • Acting range: roles and tricks an actor commands, e.g. his repertoire of villains and fools.
  • General skill set: techniques at one disposal, e.g. a repertoire of teaching strategies and jokes.
  • Linguistic repertoire: languages, dialects and styles a speaker controls and switches among.
  • Social role stock: culturally scripted identities available in a society, e.g. repertoire of gender and occupational roles.
  • Biological repertoire: immune or behavioral variants an organism can deploy, e.g. antibody repertoire.

Bridges are straightforward generalization from stage to life. Theatre stock becomes any prepared stock because all skilled action resembles performance: ready, rehearsed, audience-oriented. The sociological use leans on dramaturgy of Goffman and Turner: life is staged, roles are costumes, repertoire is wardrobe. To say society has a recognizable repertoire claims roles are finite, learnable and repeatable, not invented anew each encounter.

Spelling and register notes: repertoire is the French spelling with -oi-; repertory is the anglicized doublet often used for the theatre system itself, repertory company, while repertoire is the stock. Pronunciation in Received Pronunciation keeps French nasal coloring but anglicized stress on first syllable. It sounds cultured and professional, warmer than inventory and more artistic than set or portfolio.

Supplementary detail: theatre managers distinguish active repertoire kept in rehearsal rotation from dormant archive requiring revival work. Opera houses publish season repertoire to signal identity, balancing warhorses that sell with novelties that prestige. Educators map teacher repertoire onto Shulman pedagogical content knowledge, where experts switch routines fluidly while novices cling to one script. Sociologists code role repertoire size as cultural capital: cosmopolitans command many roles, isolates few. Digital platforms now speak of content repertoire and prompt repertoire, extending theatrical readiness to algorithmic performance.
