- An exotic import from the East Indies (17th c.) - entering English through voyage accounts (Hakluyt, c. 1600), the word names the great hanging bronze disc of Java and Bali, an object of wonder from the spice-trade world, heard as the voice of gamelan courts rather than of Europe
- Naturalized as a European instrument (18th-19th c.) - adopted into the orchestra, the theatre, and the dining room, the gong becomes the sound of ceremony and summoning - dinner-gongs in great houses, gongs that open stage performances, the household gong that calls servants
- From temple to ring and stage (late 19th-20th c.) - the gong's sharp strike becomes the boxing-ring signal between rounds, and on the music-hall stage 'to gong' a performer is to cut them off with a flat bronze protest; the instrument gains a blunt, verdict-like authority
- Re-ceremonialized voice (contemporary) - the gong returns to the ritual and meditative uses that surrounded its origins, its deep, long-sustained ring heard as a sound of mindfulness and beginning - an instrument that summons rather than accompanies
