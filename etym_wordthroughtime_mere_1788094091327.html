- (Old English, mere “sea, ocean; lake, pool, pond”) - The everyday word for water in bulk: the sea off the shore and the lake in the hills are both mere. Tone: plain, universal, slightly awesome — the sea that ringed the island world.
- (Middle English, mere “lake, pool, standing water”) - As the ocean-sense passes increasingly to sea (Old English sǣ), mere narrows to inland water: manorial pools, fenland meres, village ponds. Tone: ordinary and local.
- (14th–16th c.) - The homograph collision: the adjective mere, from Old French mer “pure” (Latin merus), floods the language as “only, just, nothing more”; the noun is increasingly islanded in dialect, legal records, and toponyms.
- (17th–19th c.) - Romantic and antiquarian taste revives the noun in poetry and topography: meres appear in Lake District verse and guidebooks; meanwhile the great fenland meres are drained (Whittlesey Mere in 1851) — the word becomes a name for lost water.
- (20th c.–today) - The noun survives as a literary word, a dialect word, and a fossil in hundreds of place names; the adjective, meanwhile, remains one of the most common words in English. Tone today: a mere sounds archaic and elegiac — a word for water that has retreated, like the water itself.
