COUNSELLOR (British spelling; American counselor) is a noun denoting a person who gives advice or guidance — a trusted adviser whose judgment is sought and valued in matters of deliberation. In the passage behind this card, Mill's Autobiography praises his wife as the “almost infallible counsellor”: the context is his dithyrambic, near-worshipful portrait of Harriet Taylor as the guiding intelligence of his life and work. The nuance of the word here is not that of a paid consultant or a professional adviser but of an intimate, trusted source of guidance, one who sits within the deliberation itself and shares in the formation of judgment. To call someone a counsellor is to place that person inside the circle of decision, conferring authority through closeness and trust rather than through office; the tone is elevated, almost courtly, befitting a portrait of intellectual partnership written in the language of biography and homage.

Primary sense — a trusted adviser. In its general sense, a counsellor is anyone whose advice guides another: a monarch's counsellors, a young person's wise counsellor, the counsellors of the state. The word implies a relationship of standing trust and shared deliberation rather than a single, transactional consultation.

  • Legal sense — counsellor-at-law. In law, a counsellor (or counselor) is an advocate, a barrister or attorney admitted to plead before a court — in American usage “counselor-at-law” is a formal title for a practising lawyer, and “counsel for the defence” denotes the legal adviser in a case.
  • Educational and therapeutic sense. In modern life the word has been professionalized: a school counsellor guides students through academic and personal decisions, a marriage counsellor advises couples, a careers counsellor helps plan a working life, a grief counsellor supports the bereaved. In these uses the counsellor is a trained, often licensed professional whose work is listening, guidance, and support rather than executive decision.
  • Historical sense — the king's counsellor. In medieval and early-modern government, the counsellor was a fixed officer of the royal court — the king's counsellors, the privy counsellors — whose advice shaped policy. This courtly sense is the root of the word's dignity and survives in “Privy Counsellor” and “counsellor of state.”
  • Figurative sense. By extension, a book, a principle, or an instinct may be called a counsellor: “let conscience be your counsellor,” “an old book, the best of counsellors.” Here the word retains its full ancient force: a voice that guides decision.

Register, spelling, and collocations. COUNSELLOR is a formal, dignified word. British English keeps the double -ll- (counsellor); American English prefers counselor. It collocates with “wise,” “trusted,” “senior,” “royal,” “marriage,” “school,” “careers,” and “grief,” and appears in the fixed phrase “counsellor-at-law.” The related noun counsel names the advice itself or the body of legal advisers, while the counsellor is the individual person who gives the advice.

Distinctions and semantic boundaries. Against adviser, which is neutral and transactional, counsellor implies personal trust and shared deliberation. Against consultant, an external, typically paid specialist confined to a defined area, counsellor implies a standing relationship of trust. Against mentor, who shapes growth by example and encouragement over time, counsellor is summoned into a specific deliberation and shares responsibility for the decision. In Mill's Autobiography the word is used in its fullest, most intimate sense: the counsellor is the companion who takes part in every deliberation of the philosopher's mind.
