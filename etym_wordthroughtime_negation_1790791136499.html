- Classical Antiquity (1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE) - Latin *negatio* (*negare*, "to say no, deny") served as a formal rhetorical and grammatical term for denying a legal charge or forming negative sentences in grammar.
- Medieval Scholasticism (12th–14th c.) - Became a foundational term in Aristotelian logic and theology (*via negativa* / apophatic theology), establishing that God is understood by negating finite creaturely attributes.
- Early Modern English (15th–17th c.) - Borrowed into English (c. 1420s) in theological and legal writing to denote denial, contradiction, or nullification of claims.
- 19th Century (German Idealism & Nietzsche) - Revolutionized by Hegel's dialectic (the "negation of the negation" as the engine of sublation and historical progress) and Nietzsche's existential philosophy (negation vs. Dionysian affirmation; turning away as sole negation).
- 20th Century (Formal Logic & Existentialism) - In mathematical logic (Frege, Russell), negation became the fundamental unary operator ($ eg$); in Sartre's existentialism (*Being and Nothingness*), negation (*néantisation*) became the core act of human freedom and consciousness creating nothingness.
- 21st Century - Pervades boolean computer logic, linguistic syntax, philosophical ontology, and critical theory as the operation of refusal, distinction, and absence.
