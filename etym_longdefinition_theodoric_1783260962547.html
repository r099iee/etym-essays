Theodoric (proper noun) — the personal name of the greatest of the Ostrogothic kings, Theodoric the Great (reigned 493–526 AD), conqueror and ruler of Italy from Ravenna, and by extension the whole Germanic royal name-element that survives today in German Dietrich, French Thierry, Dutch Diederik, Italian/Spanish Teodorico, and the literary English Theodoric. THE HISTORICAL FIGURE Raised as a young hostage in Constantinople and educated in Roman letters and statecraft, Theodoric invaded Italy at the head of his Ostrogothic people, defeated Odoacer in 493 after a long siege, and established a kingdom that united a Gothic military aristocracy with a Roman civilian administration. From his capital at Ravenna he ruled for three decades through the lay minister Cassiodorus and a network of Roman officials, preserving Roman law, senatorial dignity, road-building, public works, and the mosaics for which Ravenna is still famous. His own mausoleum still stands in Ravenna today. His reign was, by common consent of historians, the last true flowering of Roman civilian culture in the West, and the passage under study is precisely about this: Macaulay lists Theodoric among the chiefs who 'founded Teutonic dynasties in the continental provinces of the Roman empire' and calls them 'zealous Christians' — Theodoric was in fact an Arian Christian, and his tolerant balance between his Arian Goths and the Catholic Romans is one of the great political achievements of late antiquity. POLYSEMY AND SECONDARY SENSES
  • Historical monarch sense — Theodoric the Great (c. 454–526), king of the Ostrogoths, who ruled Italy and whose name appears in the chronicles of Procopius, Jordanes, and Cassiodorus as Theodericus / Theodoricus (Greek Theodērichos). This is the sense in Macaulay's sentence.
  • Epic and legendary sense — the same historical king was transformed by medieval Germanic heroic poetry into Dietrich von Bern ('Dietrich of Verona'), the exiled dragon-slayer and hero of the Nibelungenlied and the Old Norse Þiðreks saga. The historical ruler thus became a towering figure of High Medieval Germanic romance, and the name carried the aura of a legendary hero long after the kingdom itself had fallen.
  • Modern given-name and surname sense — Theodoric is the etymon of living European names: German Dietrich, French Thierry, Dutch Diederik/Dirk, Scandinavian Didrik, and Spanish/Italian Teodorico. In English it survives chiefly as a learned historical name rather than an everyday given name.
  • Symbolic and metonymic sense — in historical prose Theodoric is used as a type of the 'enlightened barbarian' or the tolerant Arian king, the paradox of a Germanic conqueror who preserved Roman civilization better than many Romans; this is the resonance Macaulay exploits.
  • Ecclesiastical and art-historical sense — the name is embedded in the monuments of Ravenna (the Mausoleum of Theodoric, the Arian Baptistery, San Apollinare Nuovo), so that in art and architectural writing 'Theodoric' can stand metonymically for the Ostrogothic renovation of Ravenna.
SEMANTIC REASONING AND HISTORICAL BRIDGES Etymologically the name means 'ruler of the people', from a Germanic compound of *þeudō- 'people, nation' (the root that also gives German deutsch 'German', literally 'of the people') and *-rīks 'ruler, king' (a Celtic loanword into Germanic, ultimately from the Indo-European root *h₃rēǵ- 'to rule' that also gives Latin rex and Sanskrit rājan). The semantic bridge from the two plain elements to the full cultural load of the name runs through history and legend: because the man himself actually was a people's ruler, the name became a dynastic and then a legendary title, and each later sense — epic Dietrich, modern given name, symbolic 'enlightened barbarian' — is a different refraction of that single etymological promise. The passage in Macaulay activates the historical sense with the full weight of its legendary and symbolic associations, using the name as shorthand for Germanic kingship on Roman soil at the moment of its conversion.
