- Old English "strīdan" - the verb already carried the gait sense from its earliest recorded use: taking long, measured steps that cover ground decisively, a walk full of confidence and intent rather than mere locomotion.
- Middle English "striden" to modern "strode" - the strong past form settled as the everyday marker of a firm, unhurried departure, freezing a completed movement so cleanly that it became the verb of authoritative exits in narrative prose.
- Modern English - "stride" keeps the long-step gait for people and animals, while noun phrases ("hit one's stride", "make great strides", "take it in one's stride") transfer the physical image of confident progress to achievement and career: the gait now symbolises control and forward momentum rather than just walking.
