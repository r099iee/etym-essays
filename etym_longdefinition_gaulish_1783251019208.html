Gaulish (adjective; also noun). Primary sense in this context. In the sentence — “Before the Romans arrived, the people of France spoke Gaulish, and the people of Spain spoke Celtiberian — both vibrant, complex Celtic languages” — Gaulish names the extinct Celtic language of ancient Gaul, the tongue actually spoken by the peoples of modern France before the Roman conquest. The context sets it in a telling symmetry with Celtiberian, its sister Continental Celtic language of Spain, and both are deliberately described as “vibrant, complex.” The word is chosen precisely to correct a common assumption — that France was always Latin-speaking: Gaulish stands for a real, living, intricate language, a tongue of a sophisticated but largely unwritten civilisation, recorded in hundreds of inscriptions and lost only after centuries of Romanization. The tone is educational and gently revisionist, presenting Gaulish as the native voice of the land that Latin — and eventually French — displaced. The full polysemy of the word. Gaulish has a small but carefully calibrated set of senses, each with its own bridge:
  • The language. The Continental Celtic language of the Gauls, attested from roughly the 3rd century BC to the 6th century AD in inscriptions written in Greek, Latin, and Old Italic alphabets — the Lepontic, Transalpine, Cisalpine, and Celtiberian traditions among its relatives. This is the primary sense in the context.
  • The adjective of the Gauls. “Gaulish” as a general adjective for the ancient Gauls — “the Gaulish tribes,” “Gaulish culture,” “Gaulish religion” — the pre-Roman people of modern France, Belgium, and neighbouring lands.
  • The substrate sense. In historical linguistics, “Gaulish” also names the Celtic substrate that survived beneath the Latin of Gaul — a layer of vocabulary and sound influence absorbed into Gallo-Roman and, later, French (words like cheval ‘horse’ and mouton ‘sheep’ are traced to Celtic, alongside the countless Gaulish place-names such as Lutetia, Parisii, Condate).
  • The revived scholarly sense. Since the 19th and 20th centuries, “Gaulish” denotes the modern reconstruction of the language from its inscriptions — the study associated with the Coligny calendar, the Larzac and Chamalières lead tablets, and the Pot de Fleurance — a science, not a spoken tongue.
How the secondary senses developed (semantic bridges). The language sense is the root: the Gauls existed and spoke a Celtic tongue, so “Gaulish” naturally came to mean both “of the Gauls” and “their language.” The substrate sense emerged with comparative philology: when scholars realized that French contained words and sounds that could not come from Latin alone, they identified the vanished Gaulish as the source, giving the word a second, hidden linguistic life. The scholarly sense is a 19th–20th century construction from epigraphy: as inscriptions were deciphered, “Gaulish” acquired a technical meaning — a reconstructed corpus — quite distinct from the everyday sense of an ancient people. Distinctions from related terms. Gallic is the Latin-derived adjective for the same region and people, used in collocations like “the Gallic Wars” (Caesar's Commentarii de Bello Gallico) and “Gallic wit,” and today applied by extension to modern France and the French in a mock-heroic or journalistic way; Gaulish more specifically names the language and the ancient people. Celtic is the entire language family of which Gaulish is one member; Celtiberian its sister Continental language of Spain; Galatian the related tongue of the Celts of central Anatolia; Brittonic and Goidelic the Insular branches. Critically, Gaulish is NOT the ancestor of French — French descends from the Latin that replaced Gaulish, though Gaulish left a substrate. Register and collocations. Gaulish is a formal, scholarly, and historical adjective and noun, standard in linguistics, Celtic studies, archaeology, and ancient history. Typical collocations: “the Gaulish language,” “Gaulish inscriptions,” “the Gaulish calendar” (Coligny), “Gaulish tribes,” “the Gaulish tongue.” It belongs to the register of learned description: when used of the people it is sober and technical, and when used of the language it carries the special dignity of a recovered voice — a language lost and then partly brought back to light by scholarship.
