- Old English (c. 900–1100) - 'þȳ lǣs þe' ('by the less that') is a full comparative phrase meaning 'so that [it happens] less'; perception: an ordinary prose instrument of precaution, as natural then as 'so that' is today.
- Middle English (c. 1200–1400) - the phrase contracts to 'leste, lest(e)', often reinforced as 'lest that'; used across sermons, romances and chronicles to build narrative suspense in the manner of 'for fear that'.
- 1500s–1600s - the King James Bible and Shakespeare fix 'lest' plus the subjunctive as the formal standard; it becomes the default sign of the precautionary clause in literary English, with no trace of its comparative origins.
- 1700s–1800s - sermons, parliamentary prose and moral essays keep it alive, but speech begins to desert it; to say 'lest' is to sound bookish and careful.
- 1897 - Kipling's 'Recessional' ('Lest we forget—lest we forget!') freezes the phrase into a memorial formula; the word gains a solemn, commemorative halo it still wears.
- 20th–21st centuries - it recedes from conversation; it thrives in formal writing, legal cautionary language, academic hedging and ritual speech — mildly archaic, fully understood, and deliberately chosen where gravity or caution is wanted.
