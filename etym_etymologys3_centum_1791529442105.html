1. The Proto-Indo-European numeral. The chain begins with reconstructed PIE *ḱm̥tóm "hundred", a form with a syllabic nasal and an initial palatovelar. It is probably a derivative of *déḱm̥ "ten" with the suffix *-tóm, hence something like "a tenfold ten" or "ten tens", though the precise morphological analysis is debated. The numeral is one of the most widely attested items of Indo-European vocabulary, and its varying reflexes provide a compact map of the family: Latin centum, Greek hekaton, Sanskrit śatám, Avestan satəm, Old Church Slavonic sъto, Lithuanian šimtas, Old Irish cét, Welsh cant, Gothic hund and English hundred.

2. Proto-Italic and Latin. In Proto-Italic the inherited form is reconstructed as *kəntom, with the palatovelar already merged into a plain velar and the syllabic nasal resolved into a vowel plus nasal. Latin centum shows the expected outcome: initial c = /k/, e from the reduced vowel, and final -um from the old accusative ending. The word is indeclinable and serves as a cardinal numeral. Its symbol C, originally the sign for /g/ and later for /k/, became the standard abbreviation for centum in Roman inscriptions and survives in the Romance languages and in English abbreviations.

3. Latin attestations and Roman institutions. Centum is ubiquitous in classical Latin. Vergil's Georgics (4.381) has the nymphs who guard "a hundred forests and a hundred streams" (centum quae silvas, centum quae flumina servant). The numeral structures Roman public life: the centuria is a hundred-man unit and a voting group; the centurio commands it; centuriatio is the grid survey of land; centumviri is a hundred-man court. The derivative centesimus gives the ordinal, and centēnus the distributive. These institutions carried the number into medieval and modern European administration, and the English derivatives century, centurion and cent preserve different slices of Roman usage.

4. The Romance outcomes. Latin centum passes regularly into the Romance languages: Italian cento, French cent, Spanish ciento, Portuguese cento, Catalan cent, Romansh tschient, Sardinian chentu. The later palatalization of Latin /k/ before front vowels in French and parts of Spain produces the modern /s/ or /θ/, but that is a separate Romance development and does not affect the classification of Latin as a centum language. Romanian sută is a Slavic loan, a reminder that the Romance lexicon is not uniformly inherited.

5. The nineteenth-century classification. Comparative philology in the nineteenth century needed a shorthand for the two great groups of Indo-European languages. August Schleicher's 1871 Compendium still operated with a single velar series. Karl Brugmann's 1886 Grundriss distinguished palatal and plain velars and grouped languages by labialization. In 1890 Peter von Bradke proposed the division in the form that became canonical, using the Latin and Avestan words for "hundred" as the type specimens. Brugmann adopted the terms in the 1897 edition, and English writers took them up in the 1890s. The labels were thus born as pedagogical tools, not as reconstructible proto-forms.

6. The collapse of the family-tree interpretation. The twentieth century undermined the idea that centum and satem were the two oldest branches. The decipherment of Hittite and the discovery of Tocharian showed centum-type phonology in places where the model did not expect it: Tocharian is the easternmost Indo-European branch yet behaves as centum, and Anatolian preserves a three-way dorsal distinction. Albanian and Armenian complicate the picture further. The modern consensus treats centumization and satemization as overlapping areal innovations, and the terms survive as convenient descriptions of a phonological isogloss. The word centum therefore underwent a semantic shift from "numeral" to "diagnostic" to "descriptive label whose theoretical status is contested".

7. The South Asian sense. A separate development is found in Indian English, where centum (pronounced /ˈsɛntəm/) means a set of one hundred verses in the satakam tradition and, by extension, a perfect score of 100 in an examination. This is a calque of Sanskrit śataka, "a hundred, a century of verses". It is a regional usage, largely confined to South Asia, and it has no connection with the Indo-Europeanist term beyond the shared numeral. The homography is a useful reminder that the same Latin shape can be recruited for different purposes in different Englishes.

8. Sound changes and formal notes.

  • PIE *ḱ → Latin /k/: the palatovelar merges with the plain velar, the defining feature of centum languages.
  • Syllabic nasal: PIE *m̥ becomes en in Latin, giving cent-.
  • Final ending: the old accusative *-om yields Latin -um.
  • Later Romance palatalization: French cent and Spanish ciento show a subsequent change of /k/ before front vowels; this is not part of the centum–satem contrast.
  • English pronunciation: /ˈkɛntəm/ reflects the Latin /k/; /ˈsɛntəm/ is a spelling pronunciation, often proscribed for the linguistic term but standard for the South Asian sense.

9. Literary and cultural afterlife. Beyond linguistics, centum survives in the English derivatives that carry the number through daily life: century in history and sport, percent in commerce, cent in money, centennial in commemoration, centipede in natural history. The Latin numeral also remains a schoolroom example of the stability of numerals across millennia. In the history of linguistics, centum is a case study in how a single ordinary word can become a technical key, and how a technical key can be reinterpreted when new languages are discovered. Its story is thus both lexical and disciplinary: a numeral that became a label, and a label that became a cautionary tale about family trees.
