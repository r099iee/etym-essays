The story of christened begins not in England but in the anointing practices of the ancient Mediterranean. The reconstructed Indo-European root *ghrei-, to rub or smear, underlies Greek khriein, to rub with oil. Anointing with oil marked kings, priests and sacred objects in Israel and Greece. The Septuagint translators used khristos, anointed one, to render Hebrew mashiach, Messiah. Early Christians then called Jesus ho khristos and themselves khristianoi, partisans of the Anointed.

Latin borrowed christianus by the early second century, seen in Tacitus and Pliny, initially as an outsider label with faint contempt. With Constantine and the Christianization of the Empire the word lost stigma and became normative. Missionaries carried christianus to Germanic peoples. In Old English cristen appears by around 900 as both noun and adjective, while the verb cristnian, to make Christian by baptism, appears in late Old English homilies and law codes. Baptism and naming coincided: the priest asked the name, immersed or sprinkled, and entered the Christian name in memory if not yet in writing.

  • Sound change: Greek kh- rendered in Latin as ch-, then in Old English as c- pronounced k; Old English cristnian shows i-vowel and -nian infinitive; Middle English vowel shifts and French scribal ch- restore christ- spelling under Latin influence while pronunciation keeps k-.
  • Morphology: cristen noun plus -ian factitive suffix forming cristnian, to make Christian; later -en infinitive leveled to christen; regular weak past with -ed gives christened, preserving -t- before -ened.
  • Semantic drift: from make Christian to baptize to name at baptism to name generally to launch with ceremony to dub with a term, a classic chain of metonymy plus generalization.

Middle English cristnen already shows the naming sense: to be cristned John means to receive John as baptismal name. By Caxton and the Paston Letters christening feasts are social events. The Book of Common Prayer 1549 formalizes Publick Baptism of Infants, popularly called christening, cementing the doublet baptize for clergy and christen for families. Ship christenings transfer the rite to secular objects: a bottle replaces baptismal water, a sponsor replaces a godmother. Nineteenth-century newspapers christen racehorses, streets and criminals with nicknames, fully secularizing the verb.

Literary attestations abound. Shakespeare has christenings and christendom as community. Dickens describes shabby christenings. Modern science writing loves the verb for stipulative definition, as in Lorenz christening vacuum behavior, because it confesses artifice with charm: we know we invented the label. Contemporary usage keeps both poles: solemn sacrament and playful coinage, united by the idea of a first, witnessed naming that confers identity.

Closing perspective: contemporary christenings split between liturgical revival and secular party, with many parents seeking naming ceremonies without doctrine. Humanist celebrants offer naming days that imitate christenings in structure while omitting baptism. Ship sponsors still pray and smash, preserving pagan-Christian hybrid. In science, christening remains productive: diseases, particles and software releases are christened, often with whimsy that masks priority claims. Lorenz christening vacuum behavior thus participates in long lineage where naming is claiming, blessing and joking at once, conferring handleability upon wild phenomena.
