- Latin — the outsider: extraneus names the foreigner, the one outside the household and tribe; strangeness is a legal and social fact.
- Old French — treating as strange: estrangier becomes a verb, to hold at arm's length, to keep foreigners foreign; courts and lovers both estrange.
- 15th century — English adopts the coldness: estraunge enters for alienating land, hearts, and minds; holding off becomes a general art.
- 16th–17th century — households grow strange: marriage manuals and tragedies write of wives, sons, and friends estranged; the domestic quarrel becomes the word's home.
- 18th–19th century — nations catch the chill: peoples, colonies, and churches are estranged by war, ocean, and doctrine; the private sorrow scales to history.
- Today — the dignified distance: estranged spouses, kin, allies, and voters fill courts and headlines, while Tolkien's unestranged Faithful remind readers what the word mourns — fellowship kept across the ages.
