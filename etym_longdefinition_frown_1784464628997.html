FROWN (verb and noun) — To draw or knit one's brows together, typically as an expression of displeasure, disapproval, worry, or concentration; to scowl. As a noun, the resulting expression: a furrowing of the brows. The verb also carries a fully idiomatic sense in 'frown upon/on,' meaning to disapprove of or discourage something. Core sense and grammatical range. As a verb frown is intransitive in its physical sense ('she frowned at the noise'), and it feeds the fixed construction 'frown on/upon + object' ('lateness is frowned upon here'). As a noun it names the facial expression itself ('a deep frown'). The facial action is the visible contraction of the brow muscles — the corrugator muscles that pull the eyebrows down and together — so that 'to frown' is a precise synonym of 'to knit the brows.' Polysemy and secondary senses. Modern English uses frown across a small but important set of senses:
  • Physical expression of displeasure: to furrow the brows because one is annoyed, angry, or disapproving — the core, everyday sense.
  • Expression of concentration or worry: to knit the brows while thinking hard or worrying, without any anger ('she frowned over the difficult problem'); the brows contract in effort and anxiety as well as in displeasure.
  • Idiomatic disapproval — 'frown upon/on': to regard something with disapproval or discouragement ('tardiness is frowned upon'); the facial gesture is transferred to a social attitude, and the phrase is now the word's most common modern use.
  • Noun of judgment: a frown as a wordless verdict of censure — the 'frown of authority' or 'frown of fate,' a personification used in literary and figurative language.
  • Literary and dated senses: to gaze with a look of displeasure or sternness; formerly also of fortune or fate frowning on a person ('fortune frowned on him'), and of gloomy weather or landscape ('the cliffs frowned over the sea') — a personification of nature's stern aspect.
Semantic reasoning and historical bridge. English frown comes from Middle English frounen (fourteenth century), borrowed from Old French frognier, which meant 'to scowl, to wrinkle the nose, to snort' — the older French sense involves turning up or wrinkling the nose in contempt, the face of disdain, rather than specifically the eyebrows. As the word passed into English, the facial gesture narrowed and moved upward: from the nose-wrinkling snort of contempt to the knitted brow of displeasure, worry, or thought. The modern idiom 'frown upon' completes the drift from facial muscle to social judgment — the physical expression becomes the official gesture of disapproval. Connotation and register. Frown is an everyday, neutral-to-slightly-serious word. It is less fierce than scowl or glower (which imply an aggressive, threat-filled stare) and less distorted than grimace (which twists the whole face, often in pain). It is the standard English word for the brows-only expression of displeasure or deep thought, and the idiom 'frown upon' is the polite way to express moral or institutional disapproval. Related words. The word family in English includes only frown itself and its inflections (frowning, frowned), plus the idiomatic phrase; it is worth distinguishing from native brow (from PIE *bhru-), with which it is often falsely connected: frown is a French loan whose deeper origin is uncertain (possibly Germanic or Gaulish), not a descendant of the English word for the eyebrow.
