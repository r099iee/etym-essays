1. PIE *ten- "to stretch, extend, draw out" (c. 4500-3500 BCE) - a root of physical extension: pulling a thread taut, reaching out, drawing a bow. 2. Latin teneo, tenere "to hold, keep, grasp" (attested from the earliest Latin records, 3rd-1st c. BCE) - developed from *ten- by the bodily bridge "to stretch the hand toward and seize". 3. Latin tenax, stem tenac- "holding fast, grasping, clinging, tenacious" (Classical Latin, e.g. Plautus, 2nd c. BCE) - formed from the stem tene- + the suffix -ax "given to, inclined to", expressing habitual tendency (cf. audax "bold", rapax "grasping"); derivative noun tenacitas "firmness of hold". 4. English tenacious (adjective) "holding fast, clinging, persistent" - borrowed as a learned Latinism, first recorded early 17th c. (c. 1607), with the derivative tenacity (from Latin tenacitas) attested from the 1530s. Note: Some scholars reconstruct teneo as a derivative of *ten- through the notion of stretching toward; the "stretch to grasp to hold" bridge is the standard account of how a root meaning "to stretch" yields a verb meaning "to hold". The -ax formation marks character or habit, so tenax literally means "of a holding nature".
