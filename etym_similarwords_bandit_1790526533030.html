• Brigand: Similar because both denote armed outlaws operating in roving bands in rugged or remote areas; differs because brigand specifically evokes historical Mediterranean or mountain guerrillas associated with plunder, feudal strife, and organized mountain warfare.
  • Outlaw: Similar because both refer to individuals outside the protection and jurisdiction of the law; differs because outlaw is a broader legal status that may apply to fugitive individuals who are not necessarily violent highway robbers.
  • Highwayman: Similar because both ambush and rob travelers on transit routes; differs because highwayman historically specifies a 17th–18th-century horse-mounted robber on English public roads, often romanticized with a code of polite manners.
  • Desperado: Similar because both are violent, lawless criminals operating on frontiers; differs because desperado emphasizes reckless desperation, suicidal audacity, and wild volatility (common in American Old West lore).
  • Marauder: Similar because both engage in raiding and plundering; differs because marauder emphasizes wide-ranging mobile raiding, looting, and pillaging, often in military or post-war contexts.
  • Bandolero: Similar because both refer to armed outlaws; differs because bandolero is culturally specific to Spanish and Latin American traditions of rural bandits, often steeped in romantic peasant folklore.
  • Footpad: Similar because both commit armed street robbery; differs because footpad historically describes an unmounted urban or suburban thief who robbed pedestrians on foot with brute violence.
  • Bushranger: Similar because both are armed bush/frontier robbers; differs because bushranger specifically denotes Australian outlaws who used the wilderness/bush as a refuge.
  • Freebooter: Similar because both seek illicit plunder; differs because freebooter typically connotes maritime piracy, privateering, or mercenary looting for quick personal gain.
