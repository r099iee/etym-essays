Wasser is the German noun for water: the transparent, odourless, tasteless liquid that forms the seas, lakes, rivers and rain, constitutes the greater part of living organisms, and is chemically the compound H₂O. It is a neuter noun, das Wasser, with the genitive des Wassers and the plural die Wasser, the plural being used for distinct bodies, kinds or volumes of water. The word is one of the most ancient and stable items in the Germanic lexicon, and its resemblance to English water — the point of the card's comparison — reflects a common Proto-Germanic ancestor rather than any later borrowing.

Primary sense: the substance. In its core sense Wasser names the liquid itself, whether in nature, in the body or in a container. German distinguishes Trinkwasser (drinking water), Leitungswasser (tap water), Mineralwasser (mineral water), Quellwasser (spring water), Regenwasser (rainwater), Meerwasser (seawater), Süßwasser (fresh water) and Salzwasser (salt water). It also names water as a utility and an economic good: Wasserversorgung (water supply), Wasserpreis (water price), Wasserknappheit (water scarcity) and Wasserrecht (water law) are standard public-policy terms.

  • Bodies of water. Gewässer is the collective and technical term for a body of water — a river, lake or stream — and is the word used in environmental law and geography; Wasser itself can mean a particular stretch or sheet of water, especially in the plural (die Wasser des Rheins).
  • Water in the body. Fruchtwasser (amniotic fluid), Gewebewasser (interstitial fluid), Wassereinlagerung (fluid retention) and the euphemistic Wasser lassen (to urinate) extend the word to physiology and medicine.
  • Weather and landscape. Hochwasser (flood), Niedrigwasser (low water), Hochwasser and Sturmflut, Grundwasser (groundwater), Abwasser (wastewater), Klarwasser and Brauchwasser belong to hydrology, civil engineering and insurance.
  • Chemistry and physics. Wasserstoff (hydrogen, literally “water-stuff”), Sauerstoff (oxygen), Wasserstoffperoxid (hydrogen peroxide), schweres Wasser (heavy water), Wasserhärte (water hardness) and wasserlöslich (water-soluble) show the word as a chemical building block.
  • Ritual and religion. Weihwasser (holy water), Taufwasser (baptismal water) and Osterwasser carry the word into liturgy, where water signifies purification, birth and blessing.

Secondary sense: the compound factory. Few German nouns are as productive in compounds as Wasser. The reason is partly practical — water is everywhere and enters every domain — and partly linguistic, since German favours nominal compounding. The results range from the transparent (Wasserfall, waterfall; Wasserglas, water glass; Wasserkocher, kettle) to the lexicalised (Wassermann, Aquarius and the Water Bearer; Wasserspeier, gargoyle; Wasserwaage, spirit level; Wasserzeichen, watermark). Wasserfloh (water flea), Wasserspinne (diving bell spider), Wasserschlange (water snake) and Wasserpflanze (aquatic plant) show the word used as a classifying prefix in biology.

Tertiary sense: figurative and idiomatic. German idiom exploits water's properties of flow, depth, transparency and concealment. Stille Wasser sind tief (“still waters run deep”) warns that quiet people may be profound or dangerous. Ins Wasser fallen (“to fall into the water”) means that a plan collapses. Wasser auf jemandes Mühlen gießen (“to pour water on someone's mill”) means to support an opponent's argument. Wasser predigen und Wein trinken (“to preach water and drink wine”) describes hypocrisy. Mit allen Wassern gewaschen sein (“to be washed with all waters”) means to be cunning and experienced. Wasser marsch! is the fire brigade's command to open the hoses, and zu Wasser und zu Lande means “by land and sea”. In these expressions the literal liquid is never far away, which is what gives the idioms their concrete force.

Figurative extension: tears and value. Wasser can mean tears (das Wasser steht ihm in den Augen, his eyes are brimming), and it can mean the lustre or “water” of a gemstone, a technical sense borrowed from the old language of jewels. In commerce, Wasser appears in the phrase Wasser auf die Mühle and in the marketing of bottled waters, where the word denotes origin, purity and status rather than chemistry. The astrological Wassermann (Aquarius) and the folklore of Wassermänner, Nixen and Wassergeister give the word a mythological layer that German Romantic painting and opera exploited.

Semantic bridges. The literal sense generates the rest by three routes. Metonymy produces the landscape and utility senses (a river just is its water; a supply is water). Metaphor based on transparency, flow and depth produces the idioms of character and concealment. Technical naming produces the chemical and botanical compounds, where Wasser functions as a classifier rather than a referent. The English cognate water follows a parallel path, so that the two languages can often translate idioms almost word for word — a reminder that the conceptual metaphors are inherited with the word.

Register. Wasser is neutral, elementary and emotionally warm in contexts of life and thirst, but it can turn ominous in compounds of disaster (Hochwasser, Wassernot) and bureaucratic in the language of supply and law. Its simplicity makes it a favourite element in poetry, where it stands for purity, mutability, time and death; its ordinariness makes it equally at home in a plumbing manual.
