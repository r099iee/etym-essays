TEMPERATE is an adjective meaning moderate, measured, and self-restrained — avoiding extremes in behaviour, emotion, appetite, or climate. It has two main spheres. The first is moral and behavioural: a temperate person, temperament, or action shows moderation and self-control (temperate habits, a temperate reply, temperate in drink). The second is geographical and climatological: the temperate regions are the zones of the Earth between the tropics and the polar circles, where heat and cold, rain and drought are comparatively balanced (temperate climate, temperate zone). Both senses descend from Latin temperare, “to mix in due proportion, blend, regulate, restrain”, and both preserve the core image of a justly blended mixture — of humours in the body, of degrees of heat, of the elements themselves. In the sentence “Ser Criston is not temperate,” the word is used in the moral sense: Ser Criston is intemperate, given to excess of temper or violence, a man who does not keep his passions in due proportion.

Primary senses.

  • Moderate, self-restrained (of persons, natures, conduct): avoiding excess, especially in food, drink, anger, or passion; “a temperate man”, “a temperate life”, the opposite of intemperate.
  • Mild (of climate): neither very hot nor very cold; describing the temperate zones of the world and their weather, the sense in which “the region enjoys a temperate climate year-round”.
  • Restrained (of things, speech, language): a temperate argument, a temperate tone, a temperate response — measured and free of vehemence.

Secondary and technical senses.

  • Geography and climatology: the temperate zone, temperate latitudes, temperate climate (in the Köppen system the mild “C” climates); the doctrine of the temperate middle zones is ancient and came from classical geography.
  • Biology and ecology: temperate forests, temperate woodland, temperate flora and fauna — the plants and animals of the mid-latitude zones; “temperate rainforest” names the mild, wet coastal woods of the Pacific Northwest and Chile.
  • Virology: a temperate bacteriophage is one that does not destroy its host but integrates its genome and lies dormant (lysogeny); the term contrasts with virulent phages and is technical jargon.
  • Botany and horticulture: temperate fruit trees and crops — apples, pears, stone fruits — grown in the mid-latitude zones, contrasted with tropical and subtropical species.
  • Historical medical usage: in humoral theory, a temperate temperament was one in which the four humours were blended in due proportion; temperament and temper are cognates from the same root.
  • Materials note: tempered (as of steel or glass) is a distinct but etymologically related word, from the same sense of “treating and regulating a substance”.

Historical semantic bridge. From the physical image of mixing and blending (temperare, “to mix in due proportion”) the word moved to the balanced mixture of the humours in the body, then to the restraint of the passions, then to moderation in general, and — in the ancient geographers’ doctrine of the “tempered” middle zones of the inhabited world — to climate. Every modern sense is a refraction of the single image of due proportion: the temperate man keeps his passions in due measure, the temperate region keeps its heat in due measure.

Register and collocations. In personal application the word is formal and slightly literary; in science it is clinical and exact. It collocates with climate, zone, region, forest, phage, and with habits, speech, reply, man; its antonym is intemperate, and its cognates include temper, temperament, and temperance (the virtue, and the cause of the movement against alcohol).
