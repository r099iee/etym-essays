- A native adjective (Old English through the medieval period) - mirce / mirk means 'dark, gloomy,' but survives chiefly as a dialect word for centuries
- The noun arrives from the North (16th c.) - Old Norse myrkr, 'darkness,' is borrowed to give English the noun murk, which merges with the old adjective; meanwhile murky is established (14th c.) for water and weather
- Romantic and Gothic revival (18th-19th c.) - poets and Gothic writers seize on murk for a darkness thick enough to have substance; Tolkien's Mirkwood later revives the older mirk
- Contemporary - a literary and atmospheric word: a gloom so dense it seems to have body, 'a darkness you could almost reach into'
