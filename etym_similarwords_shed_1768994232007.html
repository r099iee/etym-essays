• slough: Similar in being the natural casting-off of an outer layer; differs because slough is the heavier biological word for dead tissue or a snake's outgrown skin, while shed is the everyday, effortless word for skins, leaves, tears, and burdens.
  • rid: Similar in that "get shed of" means "get rid of"; differs because rid is the standard modern idiom, slightly more analytic and deliberate, whereas shed implies an easy, natural, almost involuntary separation.
  • discard: Similar in throwing something away; differs because to discard is a deliberate act of disposal that judges the thing useless, while shedding implies no judgment — simply letting go.
  • cast off: Similar in releasing or letting go; differs because cast off has a nautical or sartorial flavor (mooring lines, garments), while shed is organic and everyday — skins, leaves, tears.
  • moult: Similar in the loss of feathers, hair, or horns; differs because moult is a technical renewal-word for animals, whereas shed ranges over snakes, trees, tears, and blood with far broader scope.
  • divest: Similar in stripping something away; differs because divest is formal and legal — stripping of rights, goods, or office — while shed is plain and physical.
  • jettison: Similar in throwing something off; differs because jettison is deliberate and sudden, of cargo overboard in an emergency, while shedding is natural and gradual.
  • abandon: Similar in giving up or letting go; differs because abandon carries moral weight and a change of allegiance, while shed is a simple physical separation.
  • cast: Similar in the sense "to cast a skin"; differs because cast is archaic or technical in this use, while shed is the living everyday word.
  • let go: Similar in releasing; differs because let go is general and phrasal, without the specific image of something parting naturally from the surface, which shed preserves.
