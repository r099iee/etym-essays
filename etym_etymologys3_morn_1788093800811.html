1. The PIE base: *merg- 'to blink, flicker.' The ancestor of English MORN is reconstructed as PIE *merg- 'to blink, flicker, twinkle': the Germanic words for 'morning' descend from a stem that appears to describe the delicate flickering of light before sunrise — the sky's first trembling brightness. The closest cited cognate outside Germanic is Lithuanian mirgėti 'to blink, glimmer.' The reconstruction is standard in the handbooks but rests on a small family, so it should be treated as probable rather than certain.

2. Proto-Germanic *murgana- and its cognates. From *merg- the Germanic languages developed *murgana- 'morning, the early day': Gothic maurgins (with r-metathesis), Old Norse morginn (Icelandic morgunn), Old Saxon and Old High German morgan (modern German Morgen), Old English morgen. The alternation between *murgan- and *morgan- reflects normal vowel variation and assimilatory restructuring in the proto-language, not a change of meaning: from the beginning the word meant 'the early day.'

3. Old English morgen — a word with a day-job. In Old English morgen was the plain everyday word for 'morning, dawn,' used in the Chronicle, the homilies and the laws without elevation: events are dated 'on morgen' ('in the morning'), and the word could stand adverbially for 'tomorrow.' It entered the religious round of the day in compounds such as morgen-tīd (morning-tide, morning time) and morgen-sang (the morning service, matins, attested in Old English religious prose), and the calendar names for the great feasts ('midwinter morgen'). In continental Germanic the same word acquired a legal-economic life of its own: German and Dutch Morgen/morgen became an agricultural land-measure — the area a man could plough in a single morning — a textbook example of a time-word hardening into a measure-word.

4. Middle English: the three-way split. From Old English morgen, Middle English developed three variants. Morwen shows the medial -g- weakening to -w- (as in 'sorrow' from sorg); morwe, a further reduction, gave modern morrow, which specialized to 'the following day' (the greeting 'good morrow' preserves the old 'morning' sense). And from the oblique stem morgn- (compare the dative morgne, 'at dawn') came the short form morn(e), attested from the thirteenth century. In Middle English all three were ordinary words and 'morn' was not yet a poeticism: romance and lyric use it freely with morning's plain meaning. The functional split hardened only in later centuries — morrow took 'next day,' morning took everyday prose, and morn was left to verse.

5. Literature and register: from prose word to archaism. In Early Modern English 'morn' still worked in speech-adjacent writing, but its concentration in poetry made it the canonical poeticism of the seventeenth century. Milton's Paradise Lost uses it with full dignity and metrical grace — 'Sweet is the breath of morn' (IV.641) — and the same poem's account of the smith-god Mulciber's fall traces 'from morn / To noon he fell, from noon to dewy eve' (I.742–745), a line that fixed 'from morn to…' as an English idiom for a whole summer's span. Shakespeare had already ennobled it in Hamlet I.i through Horatio's 'the morn, in russet mantle clad.' Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries 'morn' hardened into the poetic word par excellence — the nineteenth century's model 'poetical word,' regularly taught as the archetype of poetic diction and used in hymns ('hail to the morn' patterns), lyrical ballads and set phrases ('morn and eve'). In Scotland, meanwhile, the old adverbial function survived in daily speech: 'the morn' = 'tomorrow,' 'the morn's morn' = 'tomorrow morning,' a living reminder of the word's ancient grammar. The twentieth century kept 'morn' in the poetic and ceremonial registers — headlines ('election morn'), choral texts, fantasy and historical prose.

6. Summary of the drift. MORN never changed what it denotes — morning, dawn — but its social role was completely rewritten. It moved from plain Germanic common noun (with a farmer's land-measure as a continental side-role) to the ordinary prose word 'morning's' twin, to poetry's exclusive property, and finally to a deliberate archaism charged with freshness and nostalgia. Its sound changed with it: Old English /ˈmor.ɡen/ became Middle English /mɔrn/ by loss of the medial syllable, and modern /mɔːn/ arose through the lengthening of vowels before r-plus-consonant clusters in fifteenth-century English (the same change that produced 'morn, born, corn' with their modern long vowels in RP). The word's meaning never wandered; its register travelled the whole width of the language.
