The biography of pony begins in the smallest of words: the Proto-Indo-European base *pau-, meaning "few, little, small", which already carried the smallness that the modern word retains. Its Greek branch produced pauros, "little, few"; its Latin branch produced paucus, the ancestor of modern French peu, of English paucity, and — through the Germanic form *fawaz — of English few itself. But the decisive Latin offshoot for our word was pullus, "the young of any animal": a foal, a chick, an offspring. The semantic step from "small" to "young" is natural in language — the young of a creature is its small form — and Latin used pullus all across the barnyard, for foals, for chickens, and even for human children, in the same way that a "kiddie" is both a small thing and a child. From pullus the trail runs through Late Latin pullanus ("young of an animal") into Old French poulain, "a colt", and then into the diminutive poulenet, "a little foal", recorded in Middle French in the fifteenth century. Here the word takes the decisive turn of its history, for it did not enter mainstream English directly from Parisian French. Instead it travelled north to Scotland: Lowland Scots borrowed it as powney (also powny), first attested in the 1650s, and from Scots it passed into general English in the eighteenth century. This is a loanword's career with a distinctive twist: poulenet crossed the Channel by way of Scotland's French-inflected medieval vocabulary, and the Scots — celebrated breeders of the tough little hill ponies of the Highlands and the Shetlands — had the strongest practical reason to adopt it. The same Latin pullus, meanwhile, led a second life through Old French poulet, giving English pullet, "a young hen", and poultry, the birds of the farmyard: one root, two families, the stable and the henhouse. By the eighteenth century pony was firmly English, defined by height as a small horse of a small breed — the hardy, sure-footed animal of the moor, the mountain, and later the coal-mine, where pit ponies worked by candlelight hauling coal tubs through the galleries. In 1860 the Pony Express gave the word a romantic American lease of life, carrying mail across the plains by relays of small, tough horses, and in the twentieth century the word began its most surprising transfers: the ponytail, the hair gathered and tied to hang like a horse's tail; the "pony" of British slang, twenty-five pounds, from the turf and the East End; the American "pony up", to pay; the "pony glass", a small measure of beer or spirits; the academic "pony", a trot or crib; and, from 1964, the pony car, the sporty small automobile class opened by the Ford Mustang. In every transfer the same core survives — the small, sturdy, serviceable thing, carrying its burden with cheerful endurance. And it is exactly that humble endurance that Tolkien is drawing on. When the company of dwarves set out from the Green Dragon Inn, they are "on ponies, and each pony was slung about with all kinds of baggages, packages, parcels, and paraphernalia." No charger, no white steed, no knight's horse: the ponies are as unheroic as their riders, small beasts of burden plodding under a mountain of luggage. The word, with its history reaching from the smallest Indo-European root to the Scottish hill country, carries the whole tone of the departure — modest, sturdy, and faintly comic — and quietly promises that the great adventure will be undertaken by folk who ride small horses and carry parcels.
