- Old English period (pre-12th c.) — The ancestral roots cynn (race, family, kind) and suffix -scipe (state, condition) were ubiquitous, though the exact compound kinship was not yet documented, with Old English relying on cynren or sibbe for familial ties.
- 13th to 17th c. — Middle English established kin as the dominant everyday word for relatives and bloodlines, but the nominal suffix -ship was reserved for words like friendship, lordship, and worship.
- Early 19th c. (c. 1820s–1830s) — The compound kinship was newly coined and popularized in Romantic and antiquarian English literature (notably in the works of Robert Southey) to express profound blood bonds, Scottish clannish loyalty, and natural affection.
- Mid to late 19th c. — Rapidly elevated into a foundational scientific concept by pioneer anthropologists Lewis Henry Morgan (1871) and Sir Henry Maine, shifting from poetic sentiment to the structural taxonomy of tribal descent and marriage.
- Early to mid-20th c. — Broadened dramatically into aesthetic, philosophical, and literary criticism to denote deep intellectual affinity, structural harmony, and artistic sympathy across disparate disciplines.
- Late 20th to 21st c. — Institutionalized in evolutionary biology via Hamilton's kin selection, while expanding in contemporary posthumanist philosophy and queer studies to include chosen families and multi-species ecological kinship.
