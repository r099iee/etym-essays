Stage 1 — PIE *pewH- “to cleanse, purify” (Pokorny *peu-; the primitive meaning is physical cleaning; the sister reflex Latin purus “clean, pure” gives English pure, purify, purge — reconstructed, 4th–3rd millennium BCE). Stage 2 — Latin putāre “to prune, trim, cleanse; to settle (accounts); to reckon, consider, think” (attested in classical Latin; the shift runs: clearing trees → clearing accounts → clearing the mind). Stage 3 — Latin computāre “to sum up, reckon together, calculate” (com- “together” + putāre; attested in classical Latin). Stage 4 — Old French conter (also cunter, compter) “to count; to tell, relate” — both senses coexist in Old French, numbering and narrating being one word (9th–11th c.). Stage 5 — Old French reconter “to tell over again, relate, recount” (re- “again, back” + conter “to tell”; the ancestor of modern French raconter “to tell”). Stage 6 — Middle English recounten “to relate, narrate in detail” (15th c.). Stage 7 — Modern English RECOUNT “to narrate in detail” (Macaulay's “I shall recount the errors”). HOMOGRAPH NOTE: the English verb re-count “to count again” and the noun recount (an election recount) are a separate, modern formation from re- + count (19th c.), NOT descendants of Old French reconter; the two words merely converge in spelling.
