In its dominant sense in the source sentence, a gig is a light two-wheeled carriage drawn by a single horse: a tall-wheeled, open, one-seat trap built for speed on turnpike roads rather than for work across fields. William Cobbett, riding and walking through the countryside for his Rural Rides, names it as exactly the wrong machine for real country: with a gig you cannot get about amongst bye-lanes and across fields, through bridle-ways and hunting-gates, while to tramp on foot is too slow. The sentence sets up the gig as townish, genteel mobility — fine on the highway, useless the moment England stops being a road and starts being a landscape.

Core meaning in detail: The classic gig had two tall thin wheels, long shafts, a single seat (sometimes with a dickey or a small box behind), minimal springs, and no roof, so that one horse could bowl it along at eight or ten miles an hour. It was the vehicle of country doctors, commercial travellers, small farmers doing market day in style, and dashing young men showing off a fast stepper. Lightness was the whole point: where a coach or wagon muscled through mud by weight and teams, the gig skimmed good surfaces and failed everywhere else, rutting lanes, sticking in field gateways, and jolting its rider over every stone. To own a gig was to announce a certain middling prosperity — above the walker, below the coach-and-pair gentry — and radicals like Cobbett read that announcement with suspicion, as the rolling emblem of tax-eaters, clerks, and townsmen who travelled through the country without ever touching it.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Light two-wheeled one-horse carriage (primary historical sense in this context, from 1791): the tall-wheeled trap described above, the everyday fast transport of doctors, dealers, and dandies until the motor car killed it. This is the sense every reader of Cobbett needs first.
  • A whirling thing; a spinning top (earliest sense, Middle English gigge, c. 1300): originally a child's top that hums as it spins. The bridge is motion and lightness: the top spins fast on a tiny point, and the carriage skims fast on two tall wheels — both are small, quick, slightly unsteady things defined by whirling movement.
  • A flighty, giddy person or thing (16th–17th c., obsolete): Shakespeare's age calls a wanton, skittish girl a gig, and speaks of gigging laughter. The bridge runs through the top: as the toy spins idly and wobbles, so the person spins from fancy to fancy without settling — spin becomes fickleness.
  • A musical engagement; a booked performance (1920s jazz slang, now universal): a band's gig at a club, a one-night stand, then any single paid job of work. The bridge is brevity plus dash: the carriage was a quick turn out and back, and the jazzman's night was the same shape — a short, sharp, stylish outing. Scholars debate whether the jazz sense grew from the carriage (a job you drive out to) or from French gigue, a lively dance, fed through Creole dance halls; both roads lead through lightness and liveliness, and the honest dictionaries record the uncertainty.
  • Any short-term paid job; a unit of freelance piecework (late 20th c., dominant today): a driving gig, a design gig, the gig economy. The bridge is generalization from the musician's booking: once gig meant one bounded engagement, every bounded engagement — courier run, coding task, rented spare room night — could be a gig. The horse has vanished entirely and only the shape of the work remains: brief, priced, and over by morning.
  • A gigabyte or gigahertz, clipped (computing slang, late 20th c.): fifty gigs of storage, a three-gig chip. The bridge is pure clipping for speed: technicians shorten the long unit exactly as owners once shortened the long road, keeping the hard G that sounds like capacity.
  • A pronged spear for fishing or frogging; a fish-gig or frog-gig (noun) and to gig, to spear frogs or fish by lamplight (verb, American): usually treated as a separate shoot from the same old stock of whirling-pronged things, possibly tied to fizgig, the whirling firework and spinning top. The bridge, where one exists at all, is the barbed, twirling implement rather than the carriage, and careful etymologists keep this line fenced off from the trap-and-musician line rather than forcing a union.
  • To work a gig; to perform a booked job (verb, from the musician's noun): bands gigging round the pubs, comedians gigging the circuit. The bridge is the ordinary noun-to-verb slide English performs on every tool of trade: the engagement becomes the act of fulfilling it.

Register and connotation: The carriage gig is brisk, smart, faintly rakish — a young man's vehicle, a busy professional's vehicle, never a labourer's. Cobbett loads it with class meaning: the gig-man rides above the mud he writes about, sees the bye-lanes as obstacles rather than homes, and pays turnpike tolls to avoid the England under his wheels. Modern gig is the opposite end of the same lightness: no longer the employer's smart trap but the worker's fragile booking, celebrated by platforms as freedom and mourned by unions as precarity. One word thus spans Regency dash and app-age anxiety, and both ends share the original physics of the spinning top — quick, small, and apt to wobble.
