damp
- 14th century — English borrows damp from Middle Low German meaning not moisture but gas: a noxious, suffocating exhalation, the feared air of mines and closed chambers (surviving in the miners' choke-damp and fire-damp).
- 16th century — the adjective settles on "slightly wet, moist," and the noun broadens from noxious vapour to mist, fog, and plain humidity — a root of hot smoke cooling into cold wetness.
- 17th–18th centuries — the verb "to damp" means to choke or stifle a fire (damp it down), then figuratively to dull or depress (dampen one's spirits); the word now spans fire and water at once.
- 19th century — ghost and adventure fiction adopt "the damp" as a stock Gothic presence, the chill clinging wetness of vaults and cellars; British building talk adds "rising damp" and "damp-proof course."
- Contemporary — an everyday adjective for cool, sullen, uncomfortable wetness (not soaked, not flooded), still carrying a faint undertow of that old noxious vapour: damp is the cold exhalation of history.
