The physical origin of INFALLIBLE lies in the Indo-European verb base *bhel-, “to strike or strike down,” which in Latin produced the verb fallere, “to trip up, to make someone lose their footing.” The semantic logic is beautifully concrete: the person who stumbles is suddenly brought down from an upright, secure posture to a helpless, sprawled position, and from that physical image the Romans extended the verb to mental life — to be “tripped up” is to be deceived, to be caught off guard, to be duped by appearances. A second branch of the same imagery yields falsus, “that which is deceptive or counterfeit,” the ancestor of English false, and the adjective fallibilis, “liable to err,” built with the suffix -bilis, which marks capacity or susceptibility. The whole family therefore carries a double sense of bodily slipping and moral failing, of losing one's footing in both the literal and the figurative worlds.

The theological turn. The decisive turn toward the modern word occurs in Medieval Latin, when theologians and canonists created the compound infallibilis, joining the negative prefix in- to fallibilis to denote that which cannot err. The concept was forged in the crucible of scholastic debate about the authority of Scripture, of councils, and eventually of the papacy, and it entered English in the fifteenth century through Middle French as a distinctly theological term: what is infallible is what speaks with the voice of God and therefore cannot deceive. For centuries the word remained tethered to religion — most famously in the nineteenth-century dogma of papal infallibility proclaimed by the First Vatican Council in 1870 — so that even today infallibility retains a faint aura of the sacred and the absolute.

Secularization. From the seventeenth century onward, however, the adjective steadily migrated from the pulpit into secular and literary usage, describing any judge, guide, system, or instrument whose verdicts never fail: the infallible physician's diagnosis, the infallible remedy, the infallible method, the infallible instinct. With the growth of science and empiricism, “infallible” was pressed into service for rules and instruments that guarantee correctness, while skeptics attacked every claim to it as the echo of priestly arrogance. The word's categorical grammar — it denies the very possibility of error — made it both precious and suspect: a claim worth hedging, and so it is that the cautious writer says “almost infallible” rather than “infallible.”

Mill and the intimate oracle. It is precisely this hedged, intimate use that Mill employs in the Autobiography. The “almost infallible counsellor” is praised with a word borrowed from the vocabulary of the Church — infallibility is what the Pope claims when defining doctrine — and applied to a wife whose judgment the philosopher took as law. The word thus performs a double gesture: it confers on Harriet Taylor a quasi-divine authority while the “almost” keeps the whole within the bounds of Victorian reverence, stopping just short of the blasphemy of making a human being literally infallible.

  • Sound and form. The English word keeps the Latin negated participle: in- + fall- + -ible (from Latin -ibilis), parallel to fallible and fallibility.
  • Register drift. From a scholastic term of theology the word became a secular word for guaranteed correctness and finally a common, mildly hyperbolic adjective (“an infallible recipe”) — yet it never quite sheds its theological echo of certainty beyond the reach of error.
  • Literary attestations. Beyond the theological writers and the Vatican decrees, the word appears in the empirical language of the scientific revolution, in Victorian biography, and in Mill's Autobiography — the context of this card.

To call anything infallible, then, is to reach for the language of the divine: the word was born in the image of the tripped-up man, passed through the schools where theologians debated the voice of God, and was finally domesticated in the drawing-room, where a philosopher could say — almost — that his wife could never be wrong.
