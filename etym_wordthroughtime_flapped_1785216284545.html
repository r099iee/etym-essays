- [14th century: the blow] - Middle English flappen meant 'to strike or slap' - a verb of blunt physical force, and the earliest noun named the blow itself; the word carried a rough, onomatopoeic, smacking energy.
- [16th century: the wing] - the sense shifts from the effect to the instrument: to flap comes to mean 'to beat the wings up and down,' and soon to flap one's arms; the verb sheds its violence and turns loose and repetitious, the labour of a bird beating air.
- [18th century: loose things in the wind] - the intransitive sense 'to flutter or swing loosely' appears, of sails, flags, and loose clothing; the word now names aimless, repeated motion rather than force, and any trace of purpose drains away.
- [20th century: nervous agitation] - flap slips into informal British idiom for panic - 'don't flap,' 'in a flap' - the same loose, helpless motion turned inward as fluster and anxiety.
- [Contemporary: the human hand] - in the passage the past tense flapped transfers the wing-beat to a hand beating empty air in the dark: all effort and no result, an exact image of blind, comic-pathetic futility. The word has travelled from a hard blow to a loose, helpless sweep.
