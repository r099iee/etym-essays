The biography of afield is, at bottom, the biography of the field itself — of how early European societies divided the world into the settled and the wild, and how that division became frozen into an adverb of departure. The story begins thousands of years before English existed, with the Proto-Indo-European root *pelh₂- ('flat, to spread; broad'), a root built for the great level surfaces of the landscape: open plains, the spread hand, the paved and flattened ground. When the Germanic speakers migrated into the forested expanses of northern Europe, they gave this root a sharply environmental meaning. In Proto-Germanic *felþuz, the flatness became specifically 'open land' — the clearing, the meadow, the ground where a tree-cover had been broken and where grazing, ploughing, and assembly could take place. For these speakers the word carried an almost existential value, because the open field was the opposite of the dark wood: it was visibility, safety, cultivation, and community. That cultural geometry passed almost unchanged into Old English, where feld meant the open, unwooded country beyond the settlement. The contrast the Anglo-Saxons felt between feld and wudu (wood), or between feld and the defended burh, shaped a durable mental map: inside the walls was order; out on the field was exposure. This is why the compound that would become afield began, in the early Middle English period, as the transparent phrase on felde or a feld(e) — literally 'on the field.' The chronicles, romances, and devotional texts of the 13th to 15th centuries use the phrase for battles (taking the field), hunting (riding a-field), and pastoral scenes, all anchored in the literal landscape. Sound change played its part in the fusion. Old English feld was pronounced with a short vowel, /feld/; in Middle English the vowel was lengthened before the consonant cluster ld (the so-called Middle English open-syllable and pre-cluster lengthening), giving roughly /feːld/. When the Great Vowel Shift raised long mid vowels in the 15th and 16th centuries, this /eː/ moved up to /iː/, and so the modern pronunciation of the noun — and of afield — is /fiːld/. Meanwhile the unstressed preposition on eroded in ordinary speech to a mere prefix a-, so that what had been two words (on felde) became one (afelde), exactly as in abed ('on/in bed'), afoot ('on foot'), and akin ('of kin'). The decisive semantic development came in the later Middle English and Early Modern periods. Once afield existed as a single word meaning 'on the field,' it took only a short metaphorical step to mean 'away from home, out and about in the wider world.' By the 16th century the adverb was used of any departure from one's accustomed place, not only onto literal farmland. Shakespeare and his contemporaries deploy it in the language of military service and travel, and the intensifier 'far' attached itself so naturally that 'far afield' became the fixed expression the word is now known by — the expression that supplies the blank on this card. From physical travel the sense moved on to intellectual travel: a discussion that strays 'far afield' has wandered from the point; a researcher who has gone 'far afield' has ranged widely beyond his usual sources. In the modern period afield has become a literary relic in slow motion. The literal 'on the field' sense survives in set military and sporting contexts, and the professional sense — being 'in the field' as opposed to the office or laboratory — keeps the spatial metaphor productive. But as an independent adverb of distance it now carries a distinctly formal, faintly archaic ring, and it thrives chiefly inside 'far afield' and 'go afield.' Its history is thus a model of how a concrete landscape term can be dissolved into pure relation: a word that once named a specific plot of open ground now names only a direction — away, outward, beyond the point — measured silently against the home that was left behind.
