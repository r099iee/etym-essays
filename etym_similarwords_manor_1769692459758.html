• Estate: Similar because both name large landed property; differs because estate is general (any large property, urban or rural, with buildings), while manor specifically evokes the historical feudal unit and its lord.
  • Mansion: Similar because both name a large grand house; differs because mansion refers only to the building, while manor includes the surrounding lands and historical estate rights.
  • Hall: Similar because both can name a country residence; differs because hall emphasizes the great building of an estate and can be smaller, while manor implies the whole estate.
  • Domain: Similar because both mean a territory under control; differs because domain is abstract and general (fields of knowledge, power), while manor is a concrete landed estate.
  • Demesne: Similar because both name a lord's landed holding; differs because demesne is the technical historical term for the lord's own home farmland within the manor, not the whole estate.
  • Hacienda: Similar because both name a large estate with a great house; differs because hacienda is the Spanish colonial estate system of the Americas, while manor is the English feudal system.
  • Fief: Similar because both are feudal land units; differs because fief is the generic legal grant of land held from an overlord, while manor is the specific English estate unit with its residence.
  • Country house: Similar because both name a grand rural residence; differs because country house is the modern generic term, while manor carries the historical estate-and-rights meaning.
  • Seat: Similar because both name a family's country residence; differs because seat emphasizes it as the ancestral home of a family (the seat of the Dukes), while manor emphasizes the estate unit.
  • Property: Similar because both mean land and buildings owned; differs because property is legal and neutral, while manor is specific to grand, estate-like holdings.
  • Territory (slang overlap): Similar because both can mean a defined area under someone's control; differs because territory is general and neutral, while "my manor" is British slang for one's beat or home patch.
  • Villa: Similar because both name a large country residence; differs because villa suggests a grander, often Italianate or Roman-style house, while manor suggests the English landed estate.
  • Baronial estate: Similar because both name a lord's landed property; differs because baronial refers specifically to a baron's holding and title, while manor is the general estate unit.
  • Estate house: Similar because both name the principal residence of an estate; differs because estate house is purely architectural, while manor names the whole institution.
