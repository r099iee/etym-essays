The verb insure (along with its etymological twin ensure) entered Middle English in the late 14th century from Anglo-Norman enseurer or inseurer ('to make secure, pledge, give guarantee, assure'). The prefix in- / en- (from Latin in-, 'in, into, upon, causing to be') was combined with Old French seur (Modern French sûr, 'sure, safe, dependable'). Old French seur developed directly from Classical Latin sēcūrus ('free from care, tranquil, safe, untroubled'), a compound formed from the privative preposition sē- ('without, apart from') and the noun cūra ('care, concern, anxiety, trouble'). Latin cūra traces back to the Proto-Indo-European root *kʷeys-, meaning 'to heed, pay attention to, care.' Thus, to insure fundamentally means to place someone or something in a state of being 'without care' or 'free from anxiety' by establishing ironclad security.

The historical evolution of insure reveals how a general pledge of trust transformed into a foundational pillar of global capitalism and a nuanced grammatical study in modern English:

  • Middle English Pledges and Marital Betrothals (14th–15th Centuries): In early Middle English texts, ensuren and insuren were used interchangeably to denote solemn personal pledges, oaths of fidelity, formal covenants, and even marriage betrothals (to 'insure one's troth' or pledge lifelong fidelity). To insure someone was to grant them a solemn promise of protection and loyalty, banishing doubt and fear.
  • The Rise of Maritime Commerce and Modern Insurance Underwriting (16th–17th Centuries): During the 16th and 17th centuries, as European maritime trade expanded across the Atlantic and Indian Oceans, merchant shipping faced catastrophic financial risks from shipwrecks, piracy, and war. Italian merchants in Lombardy and London introduced formalized contracts of indemnity. By the late 17th century, shipowners and merchants gathered at Edward Lloyd’s coffee house in London (founded c. 1688) to negotiate maritime policies. Financiers wrote their signatures and accepted risk percentages under the policy text—creating the term underwriting and formally establishing the specialized commercial meaning of insure as financial risk indemnification.
  • The Great Fire of London and Property Insurance (Late 17th–18th Centuries): Following the devastating Great Fire of London in 1666, property insurance emerged as an organized domestic industry (pioneered by Nicholas Barbon's Fire Office in 1680), followed by life insurance (the Amicable Society, 1706). Throughout this era, insure and ensure continued to be used interchangeably by major English authors like Jonathan Swift, Benjamin Franklin, and Alexander Pope for both commercial contracts and general guarantees of certainty.
  • 19th- to 21st-Century Stylistic Differentiation: In the late 19th and 20th centuries, English lexicographers and prescriptive grammarians (such as H.W. Fowler in Modern English Usage, 1926) advocated for a functional division of labor among the three related verbs: assure (to give confidence to a person), ensure (to make an outcome certain), and insure (to provide or obtain financial indemnity against risk). While this distinction is strictly maintained in British and international formal publishing, American English and colloquial usage retain the older historical reality where insure frequently serves as a synonym for ensure, meaning to guarantee a successful outcome.
