I. An imitative beginning

PATTED is the past form of the verb pat, and the history of that verb is a model of a purely onomatopoeic word. "Pat" first appears in English in the late 16th century, and its origin is not a classical root but the sound of the action itself - a light, quick blow of the hand. English is rich in such echoic formations: "tap", "rap", "clip", "flap", "whack", "thwack", and "clap" all do in their first syllable what they describe. "Pat" belongs to this family, and it is notable that the same family appears across Germanic and Romance languages in similar forms (compare German patsch and French pat, a light blow). Because the word is imitative, no PIE ancestor can be reconstructed, and the honest historical account begins with the first attestations of the English word rather than with a root. The OED records the verb "pat" from around 1570; the noun "a pat" (a light stroke, or the sound of one) follows in the same period.

II. The 16th-17th centuries: from blow to caress

The earliest senses are physical and neutral: to strike lightly with the hand, to tap. From the very beginning, however, the light tap is distinguished from a real blow - the whole point of patting is that it does not hurt. The 17th century develops the affectionate and soothing uses: to pat a child, to pat an animal, to pat a person on the shoulder in comfort or condolence. These uses are frequent in the domestic and devotional literature of the period. The sound-word has by now become a gesture-word: patting is the touch of reassurance.

III. The 18th-19th centuries: shaping, packing, and the back-pat

The 18th century adds the practical, manual sense: to shape or flatten by gentle pressure. In cookery and craft, one pats butter, dough, snow, or sand into form - the word moves from striking to pressing. By the early 19th century "pat" covers the whole range from the tender touch to the useful press. It is in this same century that the gesture of approval acquires its fixed idiom: to "pat (someone) on the back" is attested as the action of congratulation, and by the 1880s "a pat on the back" is fully figurative - praise or encouragement given as one might give a congratulatory tap. The OED's citations from the 1880s show "a pat on the back" meaning an expression of approval or encouragement, in both journalism and fiction.

IV. The 20th-21st centuries: the idiom completes its career

In modern English the figurative system has matured. "To pat someone on the back" remains the standard image of praise, and "to pat oneself on the back" expresses (usually mildly critical) self-congratulation - one of the few idioms whose reflexive form carries a built-in wink of disapproval. Meanwhile the literal senses flourish across registers: security "pat-downs" at airports (a standard procedure since the 1960s), cooks patting burgers flat, dog-owners patting their pets, and - as a literary extension - rain "patting" against windows. The verb has also produced the adjective "pat" (perfectly apt; "to know something off pat") - a separate but historically entangled word, since something "pat" is something exactly fitted, like a hand and a pat - and the noun "pat" (a light tap; a small flat piece, as of butter).

V. The semantic career in miniature

  • Light blow / tap (1570s) - physical, echoic, neutral.
  • Soothing / affectionate touch (17th c.) - tender register; pats for children and animals.
  • Shaping / flattening pressure (18th-19th c.) - cookery, craft, practical.
  • Back-pat = approval (19th c.) - the gesture becomes an idiom of praise.
  • Figurative pat on the back = praise (1880s onward) - the gesture is entirely abstracted.
  • Pat-downs, patting pockets, patting rain (20th-21st c.) - the literal verb diversifies and never fades.

In Tolkien's sentence the idiom and the gesture meet: the dwarves "praised him and patted him on the back" - the figurative praise is enacted, comically, in a physical pat, the word carrying its whole history from sound to touch to blessing.
