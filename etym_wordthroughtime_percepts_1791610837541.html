- 1830s–1850s — Scholastic revival & philosophical epistemological coinage: introduced into Scottish Common Sense philosophy and British empiricism (notably by Sir William Hamilton and Herbert Spencer) as a deliberate technical Latinate counterpart to concept, distinguishing the immediate object of sensory intuition from abstract conceptualization.
- Late 19th c. — Pragmatism & experimental psychology: adopted by William James in The Principles of Psychology (1890) and Charles Sanders Peirce in semiotics, establishing the foundational philosophical dichotomy between the flow of percepts (sensory reality) and the network of concepts (intellectual schemas).
- 1920s–1950s — Gestalt psychology & visual art theory: popularized within mid-century aesthetic philosophy, particularly through Rudolf Arnheim’s Art and Visual Perception (1954), where percepts are analyzed not as static retinal impressions, but as dynamic visual forces endowed with tension, balance, and spatial vectors.
- Late 20th c. — Cognitive science & computational neuroscience: integrated into the study of the visual cortex (ventral and dorsal processing streams), where percepts denote the unified, multi-feature neural representations synthesized across disparate brain regions.
- 21st c. — Artificial intelligence, robotics & computer vision: utilized in machine perception and cognitive robotics to characterize high-level scene interpretations, spatial segmentations, and object identifications derived from raw sensor arrays.
