Mustering is the present participle and gerund of the verb to muster, and in the source passage it is used as a gerund — the eagles had long suspected “the goblins' mustering”: the massing, the assembling, the ominous gathering of goblin forces. The word means, at bottom, the organised collecting of a group of people (or animals, or abstract resources) into one place, with the specific flavour that they are being brought together to be counted, checked, displayed and made ready.

1. The military core. To muster troops is to assemble them in one place for inspection, roll call, pay, drill or battle. The noun “muster” is the assembly itself — the formal gathering at which every soldier answers to his name. Key terms:

  • Muster roll — the official register of names of those present at a muster (15th century; still used in shipping and the armed forces).
  • Pass muster — to be judged acceptable, good enough: originally to survive the inspection at a muster (by the 1570s).
  • Muster in / muster out — to enrol in, or formally discharge from, military service; “mustering out” became standard American usage during and after the Civil War (a soldier mustered out received his discharge papers and final pay).
  • Muster call, muster parade — the bugle-call or ceremony of assembly.

2. The figurative sense: gathering inner or outer resources. To “muster courage”, “muster strength”, “muster energy”, “muster support”, “muster an argument” or “muster the will” is to bring scattered resources together as one would assemble troops: the willing is treated as a roll call of reserves. This is today the most frequent use in ordinary prose — “she mustered a smile” — and the -ing form is equally idiomatic (“mustering the courage to speak”). The bridge is precise: courage, strength and arguments are imagined as forces that must be collected, counted and set in order before they can be deployed.

3. Non-military concrete assemblies.

  • Australian and New Zealand stock work: to muster cattle or sheep is to round them up out of the bush and paddocks into the yards; a “muster” is the round-up itself, and a “musterer” the stockman who does it (from the mid-19th century). The word is everyday rural speech in the Antipodes.
  • Maritime: a “muster” assembles the ship's crew; the “muster station” (or muster point) is the designated assembly place in an emergency, and “muster drills” rehearse abandonment and fire procedures — the modern safety signage of a ship, oil rig or factory floor.
  • Workplace: a “morning muster” or “team muster” — a brief standing meeting at which staff are counted and briefed (retail, maintenance, film sets: the call-sheet “muster” of cast and crew).

4. Collective-noun and display uses. Historically a “muster” is also any gathering or collection, and traditional collective-noun lore (going back to the 15th-century hunting tradition) has “a muster of peacocks” — the birds shown off together, punning on the original meaning “a display”. The older general sense “a show, a display, an exhibition” survives only in this fossilised corner of the word's history.

5. The semantic bridge. The whole family descends from Latin monstrāre “to show”: a muster is, etymologically, a showing. Troops are mustered to be shown — displayed, counted, inspected — and the assembly is the way of showing them; “pass muster” preserves the showing-tested-by-scrutiny idea; mustering courage is showing your mettle; and the goblins' mustering in the passage means their forces showing themselves — becoming visible, massing, threatening. Everything hangs on the single image of an organised display-collection of strength.

6. Register and connotation. “Mustering” carries a distinctly military-historical colour even in civilian use — a trace of drill-ground formality, order and implied threat. In news and political prose (“mustering opposition”, “mustering votes”) it suggests deliberate, disciplined organisation; in rural Australia it is warmly ordinary; in safety signage it is urgent and procedural. As a word it has never lost the slight edge of inspection: whatever is mustered is gathered to be counted and found ready — or found wanting.
