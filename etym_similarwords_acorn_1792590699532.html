• mast: Similar because both denote the edible nuts and fruits produced by forest trees that sustain wildlife and livestock; differs because mast is a broad collective term encompassing beech nuts, hazelnuts, and chestnuts in addition to oak nuts (further divided into "hard mast" and "soft mast"), whereas acorn refers exclusively to the individual nut of the oak tree.
  • nut: Similar because both designate a hard-shelled, indehiscent fruit containing an edible seed or kernel; differs because nut is a vast botanical and culinary superordinate category (encompassing walnuts, pecans, almonds, and hazelnuts), whereas acorn is a specific botanical nut defined by its distinctive scaly cupule and high tannin content.
  • cupule: Similar because both are intimately connected to the anatomy of the oak fruit; differs because the cupule is strictly the cup-shaped involucral base formed by hardened bracts that clasps the nut, whereas the acorn is the complete fruit comprising both the nut and its cupule.
  • kernel: Similar because both refer to the seed-bearing reproductive heart of a plant; differs because kernel denotes only the inner, softer, edible meat or embryo inside the hard shell, whereas acorn includes the entire intact exterior shell and protective fruit.
  • seed: Similar because both represent the fertilized, ripened ovule containing the plant embryo capable of germinating into a new organism; differs because seed is a universal botanical classification, while acorn is a specialized composite fruit structure consisting of an indehiscent pericarp protecting the seed.
  • beechmast: Similar because both are hard-shelled forest tree seeds produced by members of the family Fagaceae that serve as essential wildlife forage; differs because beechmast consists of the small, sharply triangular nuts of the beech tree (genus Fagus) enclosed in a spiny four-lobed bur, while an acorn is the smooth oval nut of an oak (genus Quercus) set in a scaly cup.
  • chestnut: Similar because both are carbohydrate-rich nuts produced by trees within the Fagaceae family; differs because chestnuts are sweet, low in bitter tannins, and enclosed inside a fiercely prickly bur (cupule) containing two to three nuts, whereas an acorn is typically solitary, high in astringent tannins, and rests in an open cup.
  • pignut: Similar because both historically served as forage for foraging swine in rural woodlands; differs because pignut refers either to the subterranean tuber of the herbaceous plant Conopodium majus or to the bitter nut of certain hickory trees (genus Carya), lacking the oak's cupule structure.
  • drupe: Similar because both are hard-seeded fruits borne by woody plants; differs because a drupe (such as a peach, plum, or cherry) is a fleshy stone fruit whose seed is surrounded by a hardened endocarp (pit) and pulpy mesocarp, whereas an acorn is a dry, non-fleshy indehiscent nut.
  • glans: Similar because both denote the smooth, oval morphology of the oak fruit; differs because glans is the formal Latin and anatomical term for an acorn, used in technical botany and medical anatomy (e.g., glans penis), whereas acorn is the standard vernacular English noun.
