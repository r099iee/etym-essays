The history of ominous begins on a Roman hilltop, where priests called augurs watched the flight of birds and declared whether the gods approved the day's business. Every such sign was an omen, and the adjective ominosus — full of such signs — belonged to the most official vocabulary of the Republic: nothing trivial, nothing metaphorical, just the working machinery of state religion.

From mouth to omen: the Romans themselves debated where omen came from, and scholars still do. The Old Latin form osmen survives in grammarians' notes, and ancient etymologists, fond of moralising, connected it with os (mouth) — an omen being, at bottom, a thing said at a fateful moment, a chance word overheard and taken as prophecy. Modern linguists are less confident: some accept the mouth connection through a lost formation, others suspect a borrowing from the Etruscans, Rome's mysterious neighbours who taught the Romans much of their divination. The honest handbook verdict is that the deeper root is unknown, and that uncertainty is itself part of the word's aura — the sign whose own origin is unreadable.

The English borrowing: ominous enters English in the 1580s, in the great Elizabethan raid on Latin adjectives, and at first it is neutrally learned: ominous dreams, ominous verses, ominous conjunctions of stars — portents of either fortune. Shakespeare's age loved the word precisely because it carried Rome with it; to call a thing ominous was to drape it in a toga. But English speakers, like the Romans before them, mostly consulted the future when they were afraid, so the ill sense crowded out the good: by the Restoration, ominous thunder means trouble, full stop, and dictionaries begin defining the word with threatening and unlucky first.

  • The Gothic weather (18th–19th c.): Ann Radcliffe's novels fill with ominous castles and ominous calms, and the Romantics make the word do landscape work — Coleridge's ominous stars, Shelley's ominous woods — until every respectable storm cloud in English prose is ominous at least once; meteorology and melodrama become inseparable.
  • The Victorian study (19th c.): historians and theologians apply it to politics and providence — ominous symptoms in the body politic, ominous tendencies of the age — and the augur's staff passes quietly to the editorial writer, who reads elections as once priests read eagles.
  • War and medicine (20th c.): two world wars make ominous quiet and ominous lulls stock phrases of reportage, while doctors adopt ominous for symptoms and scans; the priest, the journalist, and the physician turn out to be one professional reader of bad signs wearing three coats.
  • Tolkien's parenthesis (1954): Frodo's fifty, significant or ominous, shows the word fully domesticated — no birds, no entrails, just a hobbit's birthday arithmetic haunted by family precedent; the joke works because every reader knows exactly what it feels like when a number seems to be watching you.

Sound and sense: phoneticians note the word's own gloom — the hollow initial o, the mournful nasal m-n sequence, the sibilant tail — and poets have always leaned on it: say ominous aloud and the mouth performs a small ceremony of dread. That fitness of sound to sense helped the threatening meaning win and kept the neutral one from ever recovering; nobody whispers ominous about good news.

Modern life: today the word divides its time between gravity and play. In clinics, newsrooms, and climate reports it remains deadly serious — ominous diagnoses, ominous milestones, ominous silences before verdicts — while at home it cheerfully inflates trifles: ominously empty fridges, ominously quiet toddlers. Both uses honour the Roman original: the world is still full of signs, and human beings are still the animals who cannot stop reading them.
