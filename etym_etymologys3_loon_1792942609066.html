The loon that will not sing and the loon that wails all night on a black lake are two different words wearing one feathered coat, and their joint history is a textbook case of homographic convergence sweetened by sound symbolism. The human loon comes first in the parish records. Middle Scots loun, attested from the fifteenth century for an idle fellow, rogue, or servant-lad — and in the feminine for a low wench — belongs to the scolding vocabulary of kirk sessions and burgh courts, where loun lounges, loiters, and needs correction. Burns keeps it alive in exactly that colour: lads of loon-like worthlessness, comic and sturdy rather than clinical. South of the border the spelling settles as loon across the nineteenth century with the sense of lout, oaf, and simpleton, the company fool of tavern balladry, the fellow mocked for refusing his verse.

Two nests, one spelling:

  • 15th–18th c. — the rogue-word: Scots loun/loon scolds idle youth; English glossaries explain it as a north-country word for a sorry fellow or wench. No bird is involved and no moon either; the word's company is lout, lubber, and losel, fellow-terms of sturdy Germanic abuse.
  • Medieval — the lame-walker of the north: Old Norse lómr names the great diver, plausibly from a root meaning lame or clumsy on foot, since the bird's legs sit so far aft that ashore it shoves itself along on its belly. Faroese lomur, Swedish lom, and Danish lom keep the same bird-name round the northern seas.
  • 1630s — the bird enters English: traders, fishermen, and settlers in Newfoundland and New England meet the diver where it outnumbers all other waterfowl and borrow its northern name as loom and loon. British ornithology will later prefer diver for the same birds, but American usage fixes loon, and Thoreau's Walden chapters on the loon canonise it in literature.
  • Sound shifts: Scots loun monophthongises toward loon as Scots vowels reshape under English pressure; Norse lómr loses its suffixed -r with its vowel in English mouths, passing through loom spellings before settling on loon. Neither change is debated: both are routine adaptations of borrowed vowels to English patterns.
  • 19th–20th c. — the lunatic leans in: Latin luna, moon, gives lunatic, moonstruck, which clips in American slang to loony and loony bin. Sound alone drags loon the fool into the orbit of loon- the moonstruck, and the loon's own night behaviour — yodelling, tremolo-wailing, laughing across black water — supplies the perfect acoustic alibi: to sound like a loon is to sound unhinged.
  • Modern settlement: dictionaries now list the senses as distinct headwords or numbered branches of one headword according to house style, with careful notes that the fool and the bird are etymologically unrelated and that the moon-word is a third, merely neighbouring party. Usage keeps all three happily entangled wherever affection rules: the loveable loon, the loony loon of the lake, the loon who will not sing.

The ballad insult thus carries an accidental ornithology with it. He that will not sing is called the fool-word, but behind him on night water the bird-word goes on singing its unearthly tremolo whether the company joins in or not — rogue, diver, and moonstruck all answering to one short northern cry.
