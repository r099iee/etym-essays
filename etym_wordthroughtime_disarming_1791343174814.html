- Late 14th c. (verb disarm) "to strip a knight of armor; to deprive of weapons" - borrowed from Old French desarmer; strictly literal and martial.
- 16th c. "to render defenseless; to neutralize military power" - extended to treaties, besieged fortresses, and disbanding armies in Renaissance warfare.
- 17th c. "to subdue passions; to appease anger" - poets and dramatists (Shakespeare, Milton) applied the verb metaphorically to beauty, tears, or eloquence disarming wrath.
- 18th–19th c. (participial adjective disarming) "allaying suspicion, hostility, or reserve" - emerged in novelistic prose to describe candid smiles, modest confessions, and warm manners.
- 20th–21st c. "a hallmark of psychological charm and diplomatic candor" - cemented in modern psychology, political rhetoric, and comedic literature (Wodehouse) to describe the power of open vulnerability.
