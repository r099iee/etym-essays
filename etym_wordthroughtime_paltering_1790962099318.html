- Mid-16th Century (c. 1530s) - Babbling & petty speech: First recorded as *palter*, meaning to babble, mutter, chatter incoherently, or speak worthless nonsense.
- Late 16th Century (c. 1580s–1600) - Elizabethan drama & haggling: Expanded in the works of Shakespeare (e.g. *Macbeth*, *Julius Caesar*) to mean haggling over petty trifles, shuffling, equivocating, and acting deceitfully ('palter in a double sense').
- 17th to 18th Century - The classic idiom *palter with the truth*: Crystallized in philosophical, theological, and judicial prose to denote compromising, trifling, or equivocating with facts or conscience.
- 19th Century - Victorian moral and political rhetoric: Widely employed in parliamentary debates and classic novels to denounce ministers or witnesses who evaded full disclosure.
- Contemporary Usage - Retains an elevated, elegant literary register, most famously preserved in the classic idiom 'paltering with the truth' and recognized in modern behavioral science/negotiation research ('paltering' as using truthful statements to mislead).
