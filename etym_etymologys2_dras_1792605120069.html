Note on historical and philological debate: The etymology of Sanskrit śūdra constitutes one of the most celebrated and contentious puzzles in ancient Indian linguistics. Traditional indigenous Sanskrit grammarians (such as Yāska in the Nirukta and Adi Shankara in the Brahmasūtrabhāṣya) proposed an unhistorical popular folk etymology deriving the word from the verbal root śuc- ("to grieve, sorrow, burn") compounded with dru- ("to run"), interpreting śūdra as "one who runs in sorrow or servitude." Modern historical linguists and historians (most notably Ram Sharan Sharma in Śūdras in Ancient India and Dr. B. R. Ambedkar in Who Were the Shudras?) decisively reject this theological folk etymology. Comparative historical evidence indicates that the Śūdras were originally a distinct historical ethnic tribe or regional confederacy—attested in Hellenistic chronicles of Alexander's invasion as the Sodrai (Σόδραι), who inhabited the confluence of the Indus and Panjnad rivers—who were conquered, absorbed, or incorporated into the Indo-Aryan social hierarchy as the fourth varṇa.
