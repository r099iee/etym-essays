• Apples: Similar because both are classic temperate deciduous pome fruits belonging to the rose family (Rosaceae); differs because apples (genus Malus) are generally spherical with a hollow at the stem, have crisp, non-gritty flesh devoid of stone cells (sclereids), and do not require post-harvest curing to achieve culinary ripeness.
  • Quinces: Similar because both are pome fruits in the Rosaceae family historically used for jellies and stewing; differs because quinces (Cydonia oblonga) are intensely astringent, rock-hard, and unpalatable when raw, requiring prolonged cooking to turn soft, fragrant, and ruby-pink, whereas pears are commonly enjoyed raw and sweet.
  • Medlars: Similar because both are archaic European pome fruits that were traditionally harvested in autumn and eaten in winter; differs because medlars (Mespilus germanica) must undergo "bletting" (incipient fungal breakdown or partial rotting) before becoming edible, yielding an apple-butter consistency, unlike sound dried or fresh pears.
  • Figs: Similar because both are sweet, historic fruits that were routinely dried and stored as essential winter survival rations in European households; differs because a fig is an inverted multiple fruit or syconium produced by the ficus tree (Ficus carica), densely packed with tiny crunchy seeds, entirely distinct from the fleshy pome of the pear tree.
  • Peaches: Similar because both are sweet, juicy tree fruits cultivated in domestic orchards; differs because a peach belongs to the stone fruit or drupe family (genus Prunus), containing a single large, hard lignified pit (endocarp) enclosing the seed, rather than a multi-seeded central pome core.
  • Plums: Similar because both were grown in Little Hans's cottage garden and gathered for seasonal sustenance; differs because plums are small, smooth-skinned drupes of the genus Prunus that yield prunes when dried, whereas pears are large, bell-shaped pomes that dry into dense, leathery slices.
  • Asian pears: Similar because both are true species of the botanical genus Pyrus; differs because Asian pears (Pyrus pyrifolia) are round, russetted, and eaten while rigidly crisp and juicy like an apple, whereas European pears develop a soft, melting, buttery texture only after being picked and mellowed.
