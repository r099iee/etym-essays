The history of desolation is a history of Latin grammar carrying a religious and literary freight into English. The word is built on the Latin adjective sōlus alone, solitary, abandoned, whose own origin is uncertain: some scholars have guessed at a relationship with the Etruscan language or a lost Italic form, but the etymology remains obscure, and honest dictionaries mark it as unknown. From sōlus, classical Latin built the verb dēsōlāre, literally to make quite alone — dē- here is an intensifying prefix meaning thoroughly, completely — hence to abandon, to leave utterly alone, to lay waste. The verb is first found in the classical period (Varro, the first century BCE, uses it of land left deserted), and its perfect participle dēsōlātus abandoned, left waste fed directly into the abstract noun dēsōlātiō a leaving alone, a forsaking, desolation.

The word's decisive channel into the languages of Europe was the Bible. The Latin Vulgate (late fourth century CE) used dēsōlātiō again and again for the Hebrew and Greek terms describing ruined cities, wasted lands, and the judgement of God that empties a place of its people — the desolation of abomination (Daniel), the desolation of Jerusalem, the desolation of the sanctuary. In this biblical register, desolation acquired its permanent double meaning: the physical waste of the ruined place and the theological abandonment of a people or a soul left alone by God. This is why the word, unlike destruction, always carries the note of abandonment: what is desolated has not merely been broken; it has been emptied and left.

Through Old French desolacion the word entered Middle English in the fourteenth century as desolacioun. The earliest English uses are religious: the desolation of cities under divine judgement, the desolation of the land, the desolation of the soul in sin. The word belonged to the vocabulary of the pulpit and of religious translation, and it kept the Vulgate's emotional power. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it expanded beyond the strictly religious, describing the desolation of war — the ruined towns, the emptied countryside, the wastes left by armies and sieges — while remaining a grave and weighty word, far removed from everyday speech.

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries brought the word fully into the literary sublime. Romantic poetry and the Gothic and historical novel found in desolation the perfect name for the ruins and wastes that excited their imagination: the ruined abbey, the abandoned estate, the empty moor, the silent battlefield. Poets from William Cowper and Robert Burns to the Victorian writers used desolation for both landscape and feeling, and the word became a standard of serious prose — the desolation of a place and the desolation of a bereaved heart flowing into one another. It is in this tradition that Tolkien writes, in the mid-twentieth century, of the Desolation of the Dragon: a blasted land emptied by the dragon's fire and plunder, a waste whose very emptiness speaks of the presence that emptied it. The capitalised phrase turns a condition into a named place — the Wasteland of a legendary geography — showing how fully the word can carry a story in its etymology of abandonment.

Modern usage preserves the full inheritance. In news prose, desolation describes the aftermath of war, disaster, and industrial ruin; in environmental writing, the desolation of desert and polar waste; in literature and psychology, the interior desolation of grief and depression. Its register remains formal and literary; the word is rarely spoken casually, because it insists on the scale of the emptiness it names. Etymologically, the word has not drifted far from its Latin root: it still means, exactly, being made quite alone — a place, or a heart, left utterly without company. That simple Latin image, passing through scripture, medieval preaching, and Romantic poetry, is what gives the word its unequalled weight: when English wants to say that something is not just destroyed but abandoned, it reaches for desolation.
