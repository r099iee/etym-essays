Shoeing is a word of pure Germanic stock, and its history is the history of the most ancient foot-covering. The ancestor is Proto-Germanic *skōhaz, the word for 'shoe,' and from it the -ing form shoeing descends through a thousand years of English as the ordinary name of the act of fitting shoes — to a child, to a horse, to a ladder, to a brake. A Germanic word without an Indo-European pedigree: The most remarkable fact about shoeing's ancestor is that *skōhaz is confined to the Germanic languages. It appears in Gothic skohs, Old Norse skór, Old English scōh, Old Frisian skōch, Old Saxon skōh and Old High German scuoh (modern German Schuh, Dutch schoen), but it has no securely attested relatives in Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Celtic or Slavic. This is striking: most Indo-European words for fundamental objects spread right across the family, yet the Germanic word for 'shoe' stands alone. Some scholars have floated a link to the Indo-European root *skeu- / *skēu- 'to cover, conceal, hide' — picturing the shoe as 'the covering' par excellence — but the evidence is thin, and the standard dictionaries (the Oxford English Dictionary, Kluge's Etymologisches Wörterbuch) record the origin as uncertain. Either the word is an old Germanic creation or a very early loan whose source is lost; either way, its roots reach no further back than the Germanic world, and the honest etymology begins at Proto-Germanic. From Old English scōh to modern shoe: In Old English the word was scōh, attested from the earliest English records and already carrying the horseshoe sense: the Old English scōh of a horse is documented, showing that the association of 'shoe' with the equine foot is as old as English itself. The sound history is straightforward: the Old English long vowel /oː/ remained /uː/ in the southern English development, the final -h was lost, and Middle English wrote the word sho, shoo or schone (plural shone), with the verb shon 'to shoe.' In the early modern period the spelling settled as shoe, with the -oe- marking the length of the /uː/, and modern English has used shoe, shod, shoeing and unshod ever since. An ancient verb and its -ing form: The verb is just as old as the noun. Old English already had scōgian and gescōgan 'to fit with shoes, to shoe a horse,' so the noun-verb conversion is native and ancient, and the -ing form shoeing follows the regular formation of the verbal noun. By Middle English, 'shoeing a horse' is a documented, ordinary phrase, and the word named one of the most important crafts of the mounted age — the shoeing-smith (later farrier) who shaped and nailed the iron shoe was indispensable to travel, war, agriculture and trade. The social history of shoeing: The cultural history of shoeing is the history of the horse in human life. In a mounted age the farrier who shoes is one of the most valued craftsmen in any community: his iron shoe protects the hoof from wear on hard roads, prevents splitting and bruising, and keeps horses, ponies and oxen fit for work. The nailed iron horseshoe spread across medieval Europe (with ancient Greek and Roman 'hipposandals' and Celtic practices as partial antecedents), and by the height of horse transport in the 17th-19th centuries, shoeing was a routine, professional operation of every stable, smithy and army camp — its vocabulary fixed: farriery, shoeing-smith, shod, unshod, reshoe. The word was workaday, unsentimental and vital. Widening senses and the motor age: Even as the horse reigned, shoeing was widening beyond the hoof. From early times 'to shoe' meant to fit footwear to a person — especially a child — and 'shoeing-horn' (modern 'shoehorn') named the curved implement for easing a foot into a shoe. In industry, 'shoe' and 'shoeing' came to name any protective fitting or socket: the metal tip of a ladder or pile, the friction block of a brake, the coupling end of a cable or pipe, the runner of a sledge, the sliding electrical contact on a rail. The 20th-century motor car emptied shoeing of its everyday urgency: the horse left the road, and shoeing retreated to specialist farriery and equestrian care, while the human sense (shoeing a child) survived as the humbler, gentler twin. The literary afterlife: In verse and song, shoeing is a token of practical care. In The Hobbit (1937), the Elves of Rivendell sing 'Your ponies need shoeing!' at the very moment the company arrives at the Last Homely House, and the homely iron of the farrier's work is set inside the grace of the elvish welcome — a perfect emblem of the word's double life as craft and care. Proverb and idiom keep the word alive in everyday speech: 'the cobbler's children go unshod,' 'a good smith can shoe a goose,' 'the shoe is on the other foot,' 'to step into someone's shoes,' 'to die with one's shoes on.' Across a thousand years — from Old English scōgian to modern shoeing — the word has carried the same sound, the same sense and the same work: the fitting of a protecting shoe to a foot that must travel.
