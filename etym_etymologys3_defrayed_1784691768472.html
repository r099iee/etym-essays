Defrayed comes to English along the old highway of commerce: the French counting-house. Its ancestor is the Old French verb desfrayer (or deffrayer), "to pay out, to discharge expenses", formed from the prefix des-, from Latin dis- "apart, out of", and the noun frai, "cost, expense". The noun itself descends from Latin fractum, the past participle of frangere, "to break", via Old French frait, "breakage, damage, loss": the underlying image is that expenses are sums broken off from a purse, or losses incurred that must be made good. Frangere belongs in turn to Proto-Indo-European *bhreg-, "to break", the same root that gives English break, German brechen, and Gothic brikan — so that a word of the ledger reaches back to a word of the smithy and the carpenter's yard, and the modern accountant's "defray" hides within it the primitive act of smashing. (An older, once-cited derivation connected frai with the Frankish *friþu, "peace", through a Medieval Latin fredum, a fine paid to restore the peace; modern dictionaries prefer the "breaking" account but note the question.) In the fourteenth century Middle English adopted the verb as defrayen, in the formal commercial and legal language of accounts, contracts, and settlements: to defray was to discharge the costs of an undertaking, frequently on behalf of another. It kept this grave, bookkeeper's register through Early Modern English, where defray appears in wills, charters, and official correspondence — the language of men who thought in items, sums, and settlements — and it has never relaxed into everyday speech. One pays a bill, but one defrays an expense, in documents. The prefix des- contributed the sense of paying out, of drawing money forth from a purse; the whole history of the word is one of written, itemised, formally incurred obligation rather than casual spending, and its register has stayed remarkably stable for seven hundred years. The word crossed the Channel with the Norman accounting of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, lived in the Anglo-French chancery and the exchequer, and passed into Middle English just as the merchant economy was expanding; its formality is not accidental but inherited, the sediment of a written tradition of obligation. That dusty formality is exactly why Tolkien chooses it. Thorin's contract — that model of dwarven business correspondence, with its "cash on delivery", its one-fourteenth share of profits "if any", its guarantees of travelling expenses — reaches its comic peak when it promises that "funeral expenses" are "to be defrayed by us or our representatives, if occasion arises and the matter is not otherwise arranged for." The word carries the full weight of the counting-house into the middle of a fantasy of dragons and treasure; it makes death a contingent line-item, and the sub-clause ("if the matter is not otherwise arranged for") the perfect bureaucratic absurdity. Defrayed thus does double duty: it is historically the most faithful word in the sentence, a genuine relic of Anglo-French commercial language, and stylistically the funniest, because its sobriety is total. The ledger has spoken; the funeral is budgeted; the adventure may now begin.
