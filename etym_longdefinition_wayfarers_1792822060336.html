Wayfarers, the plural of wayfarer, are travellers who journey on foot along the open road — walkers of ways rather than riders or drivers, carrying their lives on their backs and their destinations before them. Pilgrims on the Canterbury road, tramps between towns, hikers with bedrolls, refugees on the march: all are wayfarers when the way itself is their condition.

In the source sentence Tolkien uses the word with a shiver of the uncanny: Frodo is seen walking and talking with the strange wayfarers beginning to appear in the Shire — Dwarves, and worse things in disguise, drifting east along the roads as the Shadow gathers. The Shire has no wayfarers of its own (hobbits stay home), so the strangers' boots on the lane mean the outside world leaking in; respectability watches them pass and whispers, exactly as it whispered about Bilbo.

Core meaning in detail: the compound is transparent — way (road, path, journey) plus farer (one who fares, goes, travels) — literally one who fares along the way. Unlike traveller, which now rides in cars and planes, wayfarer never mechanised: it insists on feet, dust, weather, and slowness. Chapels named for wayfarers, hostels for them, and alms for them all assume the same figure — the stranger afoot, owed shelter because the road is hard. The plural here matters: a single wayfarer may be a pilgrim, but wayfarers are a movement, a sign of the times on the march.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • Travellers on foot, especially strangers on the road (primary sense, from the 15th c.): weary wayfarers at the inn door, a chapel built for wayfarers. The bridge is the compound itself: to fare along the way is to be between homes, and English has always owed such figures a mixture of charity and suspicion.
  • Pilgrims and spiritual seekers (figurative and devotional): wayfarers to Compostela, the wayfarer's chapel, Christians as wayfarers through this life. The bridge runs through medieval piety: every roadside shrine treated the foot-traveller as Christ's stand-in, and the prayer books made all believers wayfarers on the road to heaven.
  • Vagrants and drifters (suspicious register, shared with tramp): strange wayfarers appearing in a settled district. The bridge is the householder's fear: the man without an address may be saint or spy, pilgrim or thief, and Tolkien plays exactly this chord — the Shire cannot tell Dwarves from darker wanderers.
  • Hikers and long-distance walkers (modern recreational): wayfarers on the Pennine Way, hostels kept for wayfarers. The bridge carries pilgrimage into leisure: the same boots, staff, and weather, only the shrine replaced by the summit cairn and the indulgence by the blister plaster.
  • The Wayfarer as proper name (sunglasses, chapels, brands): Ray-Ban Wayfarer sunglasses (1952), Wayfarer chapels and inns. The bridge is romance by association: merchants borrow the striding freedom of the road to sell eyewear, chapels, and motorhomes, trading on dust the buyer will never taste.
  • Wanderers in speculative and gaming worlds (modern genre use): wayfarers between worlds, the Wayfarer class in role-playing games. The bridge is Tolkien himself: his strange wayfarers on the Shire roads taught fantasy that the traveller arriving at the inn door means story is about to happen, and games have been seating cloaked wayfarers in corners ever since.

Register and connotation: wayfarers is poetic, slightly archaic, and warmly romantic — the word of hymns, guidebooks, hostel signs, and fantasy novels, never of timetables or terminals. It dignifies the poor walker: a tramp begs, a migrant flees, but a wayfarer fares, and the old verb lends the road purpose and even holiness. In Tolkien the romance curdles just slightly — strange wayfarers mean strange times — and that double note of welcome and wariness is the word's whole music: every wayfarer is both guest and omen.
