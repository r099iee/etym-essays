- Early 16th c. — Scholarly Latinate arrival: borrowed from Latin contiguus into Early Modern English scholarly prose to describe physical contact and geographical borderlands touching without an interval.
- Late 16th to 17th c. — Philosophical and sensory expansion: adopted by natural philosophers and anatomists to denote physical parts or sensory organs touching in proximity, retaining a literal tactile resonance.
- 18th c. — Enlightenment historiography and imperial geography: popularized by historians like David Hume and Edward Gibbon to describe imperial provinces, adjacent nation-states, and borderlands whose physical contact fostered commerce, alliance, or conflict.
- Late 18th c. — Philosophical epistemology: David Hume formalizes the "Association of Ideas", elevating contiguity in time and space alongside resemblance and causation as fundamental principles of human cognition.
- 19th c. — Legal, cadastral, and real estate codification: hardened into statutory legal jargon defining property boundaries, unbroken municipal annexations, and railway rights-of-way that share a common boundary.
- 20th c. (Mid-century) — American cartographic identity: the phrase "the 48 contiguous states" enters widespread popular and bureaucratic parlance following the admissions of Alaska and Hawaii as non-conterminous states in 1959.
- Late 20th c. to Present — Computational architecture and modern technical usage: firmly established in computer science and digital memory management to designate unbroken sequences of RAM addresses, alongside ongoing formal use in geopolitical boundary disputes.
