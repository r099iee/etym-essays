- Classical Latin (c. 1st c. BCE – 1st c. CE) - *assimilatio* denoted physical resemblance, imitation, or rhetorical comparison ('making like' in form, appearance, or style).
- Late Medieval & Early Modern English (c. 1400–1650) - Entered English via medical and alchemical treatises to describe the bodily digestion and physiological incorporation of nutrients into living flesh.
- 17th to 18th Century - Extended into natural philosophy and chemistry for the absorption of matter, and into phonetics to describe adjacent speech sounds becoming acoustically and articulatory identical.
- 19th to Early 20th Century - Championed by sociologists, imperial administrators, and nation-states as a normative, highly praised civilizing ideal for unifying diverse immigrant and indigenous populations into a homogeneous national body politic.
- Mid-20th Century - Rigorously formalized in developmental psychology by Jean Piaget as the cognitive incorporation of new experiences into existing mental schemas.
- Late 20th to 21st Century - Underwent critical reappraisal in sociology and post-colonial studies, shifting from an uncontested civic virtue to a contested, complex concept frequently contrasted with multiculturalism, pluralism, and cultural preservation.
