The verb abound is an intransitive verb meaning to exist in large numbers, great quantities, or lavish profusion; or, when constructed with the prepositions in or with, to be copiously supplied, densely populated, overflowing, teeming, or abundantly rich in a specified resource, creature, quality, or phenomenon.

Primary, Secondary, and Domain-Specific Senses:

  • Copious Existence (Intransitive: To Exist Plentifully): Used of entities, examples, instances, errors, wildlife, or resources that are present in vast, overflowing quantities within a given environment, field of study, or historical period (e.g., "Examples of structural tension abound throughout Gothic cathedral architecture" or "Rumors of an impending corporate merger abound across the financial district"). In this primary sense, the subject of the verb is the abundant thing itself.
  • Teeming / Replete with Resources (Intransitive with "in" or "with"): Used of a place, ecosystem, text, or institution that is richly endowed, overflowing, or densely stocked with some attribute or physical element (e.g., "The coastal estuaries abound in migratory waterfowl and juvenile marine life" or "Her historical memoirs abound with vivid anecdotes and razor-sharp character portraits"). In this secondary construction, the subject is the container, habitat, or repository holding the abundance.
  • Figurative and Philosophical Discourse: In theoretical physics, cognitive science, and epistemology, abound describes the ubiquitous presence of a phenomenon, paradox, or pattern across diverse scales (as in Rudolf Arnheim's perceptual psychology: "Examples of 'induced structure' abound in everyday optical experience"). It emphasizes that an observed principle is not an isolated anomaly, but a ubiquitous, pervasive reality.
  • Ecclesiastical, Biblical, and Theological Register: In biblical literature and theological discourse—most prominently in the King James Version of the New Testament—abound carries an exalted, spiritual connotation of boundless divine grace, overflowing charity, or moral excess (e.g., Romans 5:20: "where sin abounded, grace did much more abound" or 2 Corinthians 9:8: "that ye, always having all sufficiency in all things, may abound to every good work"). In this register, the word denotes a spiritual spilling over of celestial gifts transcending human merit or measure.
  • Archaic / High-Literary Register: In Early Modern English poetry (such as Edmund Spenser and John Milton), abound was occasionally used transitively or reflexively to signify swelling with pride, luxuriating in wealth, or exulting in excessive emotion.

Polysemy and Historical Semantic Bridges:

The polysemy of abound represents one of the most vivid and poetically coherent hydraulic metaphors in Indo-European linguistics. The word descends via Anglo-Norman and Old French from the Latin verb abundāre ("to overflow, be copious"), which is constructed from the prefix ab- ("from, away from, off") combined with undāre ("to rise in waves, surge, billow", derived from unda, "a wave"). The original concrete image was that of a swollen river, tempestuous sea, or overfilled reservoir surging in high waves until the water broke over its embankments and flooded the adjacent plains. By natural metaphorical extension in Classical Latin, this physical image of surging liquid waves spilling beyond boundaries was mapped onto non-hydraulic realms: fields that "waved" with rich wheat, pantries that overflowed with grain, or minds that surged with eloquent words. When borrowed into Middle English around 1300, the verb effortlessly retained both facets of the metaphor: a container can abound with riches (just as a riverbank brims with water), or the riches themselves can abound (just as water surges everywhere in lavish profusion).
