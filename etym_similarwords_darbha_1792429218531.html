• kuśa: Similar because both terms are used interchangeably throughout Sanskrit ritual literature for the sacred halophytic grass Desmostachya bipinnata; differs because kuśa specifically emphasizes the needle-like sharp tip of the blade (giving rise to the philosophical idiom kuśala, "sharp-witted"), whereas darbha highlights its bundled, fibrous, or woven structural quality.
  • barhis: Similar because both refer to sacred grass employed directly in Vedic sacrificial rites; differs because barhis is a specialized liturgical mass noun denoting the consecrated carpet of strewn grass laid across the altar (*vedi*) for the deities to sit upon, whereas darbha is the physical botanical plant itself.
  • dūrvā: Similar because both are sacred indigenous grasses essential to Hindu ceremonies; differs because dūrvā (Cynodon dactylon or Bermuda grass) is a soft, creeping lawn grass especially dear to Lord Gaṇeśa and symbolic of renewal and fertility, whereas darbha is a stiff, tall, sharp xerophytic grass used in austere fire sacrifices (*homa*) and ancestral rites (*śrāddha*).
  • muñja: Similar because both are resilient riverine grasses prominent in Vedic initiation rituals; differs because muñja (Tripidium bengalense) is renowned for the fibrous sheath used to weave the sacred sacrificial girdle (*mekhalā*) of the young student (*brahmacārin*), whereas darbha is woven into purificatory finger rings (*pavitra*) and meditation mats (*āsana*).
  • śara: Similar because both are tall, reed-like grasses growing across North Indian river basins mentioned in the epics; differs because śara (Saccharum bengalense) was harvested primarily for crafting straight arrow-shafts (hence Sanskrit śara meaning "arrow"), whereas darbha was dedicated entirely to ritual, purificatory, and contemplative functions.
  • kāśa: Similar because both are wild clumping grasses blooming along seasonal river floodplains; differs because kāśa (Saccharum spontaneum) is celebrated in classical Sanskrit lyric poetry (*kāvya*) as the silvery floral herald of autumn (*śarat-ṛtu*) and Durgā Pūjā, whereas darbha is a solemn, year-round sacrificial staple.
  • eragrostis: Similar because in botanical taxonomy both belong to the tough perennial grass family Poaceae; differs because Eragrostis (lovegrass) is an expansive scientific genus containing hundreds of global forage species, whereas darbha refers specifically to the consecrated cultural species Desmostachya bipinnata of South Asia.
  • reed: Similar because both are rigid, fibrous monocots harvested along watersides for mats and structural weaving; differs because reed is a generic English vernacular term for wetland plants of the genera Phragmites or Arundo, completely devoid of the ritual purity and metaphysical sanctity associated with darbha.
  • esparto: Similar because both are tough, coarse perennial grasses harvested for their high tensile strength in weaving cordage and mats; differs because esparto (Macrochloa tenacissima) is native to the Mediterranean basin and utilized for industrial papermaking, whereas darbha carries millennia of Vedic theological veneration.
  • thatch: Similar because dried bunches of both grasses are gathered into sheaves for durable floor covering or roofing; differs because thatch denotes any mundane, utilitarian dead plant matter used for rustic roofing insulation, lacking the consecrated purificatory status of darbha.
