• Start: Similar because it names the beginning of an event or process; differs because it is the neutral everyday word, with no military history and no implication of something arriving against one's will.
  • Beginning: Similar because it is the first part or point of a process; differs because it is broad and static, extendable in space and time ("from beginning to end"), while onset is a moment when something actively arrives.
  • Commencement: Similar because it is a formal beginning; differs because it is ceremonial and institutional (graduations, legal proceedings) and carries no negative or oppressive connotation at all.
  • Inception: Similar because it is the point at which something begins; differs because it is neutral-intellectual, used of ideas, institutions and projects ("since its inception"), and never of attacks or diseases.
  • Outbreak: Similar because it names the sudden beginning of something bad (war, disease); differs because it emphasizes eruption and spread after a quiet period, while onset is the initial moment of arrival, not the subsequent flare.
  • Advent: Similar because it is a formal arrival of a period or figure; differs because it is used of expected, often welcome arrivals (the advent of spring, of printing), while onset leans toward the unwelcome and uncontrollable.
  • Dawn: Similar because it names the first light or beginning of an era (the dawn of civilization); differs because it is purely positive or neutral and poetic, with no trace of force.
  • Assault: Similar because it is the old sense of onset — a violent attack; differs because in modern English assault is the attack itself, active and intentional, while onset keeps only the passive "beginning" sense in ordinary speech.
  • Onslaught: Similar because it is a fierce attack, and once rhymed with onset in sense; differs because it retained only the violent meaning (from Old Norse), never developing the "beginning" sense, and sounds heavier and more destructive.
  • Attack: Similar because it matches the martial sense of onset; differs because it is the fully active noun and verb for aggression of any kind, while onset is the arrival of the aggression, not the act of delivering it.
  • Incipience: Similar because it is a rare technical word for "beginning"; differs because it is clinical-academic and near-obsolete outside specialized writing, with no emotional colour at all.
