- c. 1600 — the phrase enters English as a term for the Venetian republic’s mainland territories, a precise political and geographical label.
- 17th c. — English travel writers extend it to mean the mainland or solid land as opposed to islands and sea; the tone is learned and slightly administrative.
- 1690s — the broad sense “dry land, the land as distinct from the sea” becomes established in general prose.
- 19th c. — it becomes a familiar literary and travel-writing phrase, often with a note of relief after a difficult crossing.
- 20th c. — aviation gives it a new contrast (land versus air), and the jocular “the more firma, the less terra” appears.
- 21st c. — it survives as a slightly humorous, slightly formal idiom for solid ground and, figuratively, for security and reality.
