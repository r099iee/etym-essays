FLIT — a verb (and noun) of quick, light motion and, informally, of secret, hurried departure. 1. The sense in this passage. In David Hawkes's translation of The Story of the Stone (Hong lou meng), the kidnapper who has just double-sold Ying-lian — taking money from one buyer, then reselling her to the Xue household — intends to "do a flit": pocket the payments from both parties and vanish before either household can pin him down. The idiom names a departure that is fast, furtive and almost effortless, an escape designed to outrun discovery rather than to meet it. The narrator's dry aside, "Only he didn't get away with it", underlines how fragile the plan proves to be. The tone is contemptuous: the crook trades in human beings as movable goods, and the word's very lightness makes the moral weight of the crime seem cheaply evadable. It frames the whole double transaction as mobile fraud completed by disappearance. 2. Core literal meaning. To flit is to move swiftly and lightly, darting from one place to another without heavy footfall or any lingering trace. It is used prototypically of small, airborne things — birds, butterflies, bees and moths flitting from branch to branch or flower to flower — and of quick, evanescent phenomena: a glance flits across a face, a shadow flits along a wall, a thought flits through the mind, a rumour flits from ear to ear. The verb stresses the ease and speed of the motion, motion that barely disturbs the medium through which it passes. 3. The informal idiom. "Do a flit", with the fuller variant "do a moonlight flit", means to abscond — to leave somewhere secretly and hurriedly, above all to avoid paying rent, dodging a landlord, a creditor or the law. The "moonlight" of the fuller idiom (19th century) marks departure by night, when the landlord or creditor cannot be confronted. A related noun use, "a flit", denotes the departure itself ("to do a flit"). 4. The older dialect sense. In Scotland and northern England, "to flit" has long meant simply to move house or change one's residence; "the flitting" is the removal itself. This is the oldest English sense of the word and is still fully productive north of the border, where a furniture-removal van can even be called a "flit". It is from this moving-house sense that the criminal idiom of the nineteenth century developed. 5. Full polysemy inventory.
  • Light swift movement (intransitive): to move quickly and lightly, especially of birds, butterflies, moths, and of quick glances, shadows or passing faces.
  • Restless frequent movement: to pass rapidly and repeatedly from one place, topic or mood to another, as in "he flits from one idea to the next".
  • Do a (moonlight) flit (British/informal idiom): to abscond secretly and hurriedly, especially to evade rent, debts or the law.
  • Scottish and Northern English "flit": to move house; to remove one's household goods to a new dwelling.
  • Noun "flit": a quick, light movement; (informal) a secret, hurried departure or vanishing act.
  • Ornithological and zoological jargon: the characteristic irregular, fluttering flight of small birds and insects, as distinct from soaring or gliding.
  • Figurative and literary uses: time flits; an idea flits across the mind; an expression flits across someone's face; a ghost or figure flits through a scene.
  • Technical and creative-industry jargon: in film editing, UI design and stagecraft, a "flit" can denote a swift, seamless transition between scenes or screens, preserving the idea of effortless passage.
6. Register, collocations and the semantic bridge. Collocations include "flit from tree to tree", "flit about / around", "flit to and fro", "do a flit", "moonlight flit", and "flit from one thing to another". The register splits neatly into two streams: a literary-descriptive light-movement sense, and an informal narrative sense of absconding. The bridge between them is motion that is quick, quiet and unburdened: the bird that flits barely disturbs the branch it leaves, just as the debtor who does a flit hopes to leave no trace of himself behind. In this story the criminal idiom is primary, and it fits a kidnapper who treats geography as an exit strategy and human beings as disposable cargo.
