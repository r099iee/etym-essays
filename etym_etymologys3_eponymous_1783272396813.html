A NAME THAT GIVES NAMES: THE ROOT *nō-mn- AND ITS EMPIRE Eponymous is built from one of the most securely reconstructed words in the Indo-European lexicon — the word for "name" itself. Proto-Indo-European *nō-mn- (older notation *h₃néh₃mn̥, "name") is attested in nearly every branch of the family with astonishing consistency: Latin nōmen, Greek ónoma (and dialectal ónyma), English name, German Name, Sanskrit nā́man, Old Church Slavonic imę, Old Irish ainm, Armenian anun. The stability of the word across languages separated for millennia is one of the standard exhibits of comparative philology, and it means that when a Greek said epṓnymos and an English speaker says eponymous, both are drawing on the same ancient word for "name." GREEK ónyma AND THE PREFIX epí In Greek the noun appears as ónoma in Attic and ónyma in other dialects; it is the ónyma form that supplies the famous combining element -onym, familiar from synonym "same name," homonym "same name," antonym "opposite name," and acronym "point name." To this noun Greek added the prepositional prefix epí, "upon, after, in succession to," producing the adjective epṓnymos (ἐπώνυμος): "having a name upon one," i.e. "giving one's name to something" or, from the other side, "named after something." The two-directional logic of the word — active for the giver of the name, passive for the bearer — was present in Greek itself and has come down unchanged. THE EPONYMOUS ARCHON AND THE EPONYMOUS HEROES In classical Athens the adjective did real institutional work. The eponymous archon was the chief magistrate whose name designated the official year: a year was dated "in the archonship of X," so the list of eponymous archons became the backbone of Attic chronology, preserved in the archon lists that anchor Greek history. Beside this civic sense stood the religious-ethnographic one: the ten Athenian tribes created by Cleisthenes (c. 508 BCE) were each named after an eponymous hero — Erechtheus, Aigeus, Pandion, and the rest — legendary figures who gave the tribe a name and a shared ancestry. It is this second sense that the card's context describes: the "eponymous personage from whom the community derive their name" is the ancestor believed to be the founder of the whole community, with the genealogy traced back to a divine source. The logic is that of all ancient ethnic founding: the tribe, city, or people is one because it shares one name and one blood, and the name-giver stands at the head of both. THE ROUTE TO ENGLISH: LATIN AND THE 19TH CENTURY The Greek adjective passed into later Latin as eponymus, and it reached English in the mid-nineteenth century, at the height of the Victorian engagement with Greek history. The noun eponym is recorded from 1844 and the adjective eponymous from 1846, in George Grote's History of Greece — the very work whose account of mythic founders supplies the card's context. Grote used the word precisely in the ancient sense, of the legendary figures after whom Greek tribes and communities were named, and his usage fixed the historical, quasi-sacral colouring of the word in English. THE MODERN EXTENSION: SELF-TITLED ALBUMS AND EPONYMOUS LABELS In the twentieth century eponymous moved from the seminar to the press release. Music journalism made "eponymous album" a standard phrase for a record titled after the artist or band — a self-titled debut — and criticism speaks of "the eponymous hero" of a novel or film, meaning the title character. The business world added the "eponymous label" or "eponymous brand," named after its designer or founder. All of these are extensions of the ancient two-directional logic: the adjective still names the relation of a name to the thing it names, whether the direction runs from the giver (Romulus the eponymous founder) or to the bearer (the eponymous album). What changed is the register and the frequency, not the semantics: a word once confined to archaeology and ethnography now does everyday service wherever a name is given and borne. THE UNCHANGED CORE Eponymous is thus a rare case of a word that has barely drifted. Its meaning today — "giving one's name to, or named after" — is exactly what Greek epṓnymos meant in the fifth century BCE. What has changed is its habitat: from the archon lists of Athens and the genealogies of mythic founders, through the learned prose of Grote, to the album sleeve and the fashion label. The word is a living bridge between the founding myth of a community and the marketing of a self-titled record, and both uses rest on the same ancient, unshakeable Indo-European root for "name."
