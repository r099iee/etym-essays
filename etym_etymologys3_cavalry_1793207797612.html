The cavalry enters history through the stable’s back door. Classical Latin had a perfectly good word for horse — equus, the noble beast of equestrian and the Equites, Rome’s knightly order. But soldiers and grooms used a slangier animal: caballus, the nag, the pack-horse, the worn-out gelding — the sort of word officers’ slang has always preferred to the parade-ground term. Somewhere in the late Empire this humble caballus began elbowing equus out of everyday speech, until by the fall of Rome it was the people’s horse across the West: French cheval, Spanish caballo, Italian cavallo — a stable-boy’s word that conquered an empire of saddles. Scholars suspect caballus itself was borrowed from Gaulish or another conquered tongue, which would make the cavalry’s name a twice-captured prize: a Celtic nag conscripted into Latin, then promoted to command.

The promotion came in Renaissance Italy, the laboratory of modern war. Italian captains, hiring mounted companies for the endless wars of the city-states, called the body of horsemen cavalleria — from cavallo, horse, on the pattern of words like infanteria, the foot. The French borrowed it as cavalerie, the English as cavalry in the sixteenth century, just as gunpowder was remaking the mounted arm with pistols, carbines, and the shock tactics of Gustavus Adolphus. The spelling wobble with calvary — the hill of the Crucifixion, from a wholly different root — has trapped hasty writers ever since, and remains the word’s most famous civilian casualty. Meanwhile the horse-root branched nobly sideways: the same caballus gives chevalier, chivalry, and cavalier — the horseman as gentleman, lover, and code of honour — while cavalry kept the dustier sense of the trooper in the ranks.

From nag to helicopter:

  • Rome — the slang stable: caballus the nag lives in soldiers’ mouths while equus holds the inscriptions; popular speech is already voting with its hooves.
  • The dark ages — the takeover: caballus becomes the everyday horse of France, Spain, and Italy, while knights ride into feudalism on its back.
  • Renaissance Italy — the arm is named: cavalleria organises horsemen into a recognised branch of war, alongside infantry and artillery.
  • 1500s–1600s — the loan travels: French cavalerie and English cavalry naturalise the word just as pistol-armed horse transforms the battlefield.
  • 1790s — the volunteers: Britain mounts its yeomanry cavalry for home service, and Cobbett watches farmers’ hunters do the work of dragoons against reformers.
  • 1914–1945 — the engine replaces the horse: tanks, trucks, and carriers take the shock role; proud cavalry titles transfer to steel, and no one blushes.
  • Vietnam — the sky: air cavalry lifts the trumpet call into helicopters, proving the word never meant the horse at all, but speed, shock, and rescue.
  • Today — the metaphor: send in the cavalry belongs to every office and emergency room, the Western’s bugle-call become universal shorthand for help arriving fast.

Cobbett’s sentence preserves the word at a hinge of English fear: the 1790s, when revolution across the Channel made every gallop sound like invasion and every reform meeting like insurrection. The yeomanry cavalry he hated were cavalry in everything but pay — drilled, sabred, and uniformed — yet their enemy was not the French but the English poor. That is the shadow inseparable from the romance: the same speed and shock that break enemy lines can ring a Peterloo crowd. The cavalry’s long evolution from Celtic nag to helicopter gunship never outran that double meaning, which is why the word still stirs the blood in two opposite ways at once — deliverance galloping over the hill, and power arriving before you are ready.
