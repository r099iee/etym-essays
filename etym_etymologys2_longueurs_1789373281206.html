Proto-Indo-European (c. 3500–2500 BCE)
*del-h₁gʰ- / *dlh₁gʰ-ó-s
"long, extended, protracted"
(source of Sanskrit dīrghá-, Greek dolichós, Old English lang)
↓
Proto-Italic (c. 1000 BCE)
*dlongos
"long in space or time"
↓
Latin (classical period)
longus
"long, protracted, lasting, distant"
↓
Late Latin / Vulgar Latin (c. 4th–6th c.)
*longōre(m)
"length, duration"
(abstract noun formed with suffix -ōrem on longus, replacing classical longitūdō)
↓
Old French (c. 12th c.)
longor / longur
"spatial length, linear distance, duration"
↓
Middle/Early Modern French (c. 15th–17th c.)
longueur
"length; (theat./lit.) tedious or dragging passage"
↓
Modern French Plural (17th–18th c.)
longueurs
"tedious, dragging stretches in an artistic work"
↓
English Borrowing (late 18th c.–present)
LONGUEURS
"dull, excessively protracted passages in literature/art"
