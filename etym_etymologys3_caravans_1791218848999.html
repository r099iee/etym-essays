caravans continues one of the great Silk Road loanwords. Its deepest reconstructible ancestor is Old Persian kāra- meaning army, host or people in arms, from PIE *koro- "army, war" (also seen in Old Lithuanian karas war and Old Irish cuire troop, per the American Heritage Appendix). The semantic link is protection: a desert trading party travelled like a small army, with armed guards against bandits. In Middle Persian (Sasanian) this yielded kārwān, already meaning a company of travellers or pack animals moving together, attested in Pahlavi texts and Manichaean documents.

New Persian kārvān / karwan (کاروان) generalized the sense to any long file of camels, mules, merchants and pilgrims crossing deserts between Persia, Central Asia and the Levant. Arabic borrowed it as qayrawān / kârwân, whence the city name Kairouan in Tunisia — originally a caravan camp. Turkish kervan and Hindi-Urdu karwan preserve the same form. Crusaders and Venetian merchants encountered the institution in the Levant and brought the word west as Medieval Latin caravana, Old French carvane, carevane (13th c.), and Italian caravana (older form of carovana).

  • Sound shifts: Persian /kɑːrvɑːn/ → Italian /karavana/ with vowel epenthesis breaking the /rv/ cluster and voicing /v/; French caravane /kaʁavan/; English /ˈkærəvæn/ with initial stress and reduction of middle vowels. Spelling caravan stabilizes after 1600; earlier carovan occurs.
  • 1588–1700: Desert convoy: First English use (1588, Hariot and Hakluyt) means a company of merchants or pilgrims travelling together through desert or hostile territory, often with camels. Caravanserai (caravan + Persian sarai palace/inn) names the fortified inn that housed them.
  • 1600–1800: Vehicle extension: By metonymy the company name transfers to the vehicles that carry it: covered carts for passengers and goods, then travelling menageries and Gypsy wagons. The shortened form van (19th c.) originally meant a caravan vehicle before narrowing to motor van.
  • 1800–present: Diversification: American wagon-trains are called caravans; British English develops the dominant modern sense of a towed trailer caravan / mobile home (late 19th c.); motorcade and camel-train senses persist. Verb to caravan (1885) means to travel in company.

OED records Middle English caravan before 1500 in travel compilations, with regular use from 1580s; Merriam-Webster dates noun to 1588 via Italian from Persian. French vs Italian mediation is debated — both contributed; the OED calls it of multiple origins (French carouane/caravane and Italian carovana). Klein's connection of karwan to Sanskrit karabha camel is now regarded as folk comparison, not established. The Old Persian kāra- link is given by the American Heritage Dictionary but marked as ultimately via Middle Persian, and some scholars treat the pre-Middle Persian stage as uncertain, so the earliest step should be read as the dictionary's reconstruction rather than a directly attested compound.

In the source sentence, guiding caravans by stars evokes the classic Saharan and Arabian night march, where Bedouin and Tuareg guides used Ursa Major, Orion and Polaris to hold direction across featureless dunes and mountains, exactly the Silk Road and Hajj caravan world that gave the word its romance.
