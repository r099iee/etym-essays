• Unspoken: Similar because both describe communication without speech; differs because unspoken stresses what is felt but never uttered, often emotional, while nonoral is a technical classification of the channel itself.
  • Nonverbal: Similar because both cover gesture, face, and posture beyond words; differs because nonverbal excludes all words including writing, while nonoral excludes only speech and the mouth, so writing can still count as nonoral.
  • Written: Similar because writing is the great nonoral carrier of language; differs because written names one specific medium of marks on surfaces, while nonoral is the whole complementary class — writing plus gesture, image, and touch.
  • Gestural: Similar because gesture is the source sentence’s own example of nonoral richness; differs because gestural names only body movement, while nonoral also embraces writing, diagrams, drugs by vein, and animal anatomy.
  • Silent: Similar because nonoral interaction is often literally silent; differs because silent notes the mere absence of sound, while nonoral specifies which system is absent and implicitly honours what replaces it.
  • Visual: Similar because much nonoral communication travels by sight — signs, pictures, print; differs because visual names the sense appealed to, while nonoral names the channel excluded, whatever sense carries the substitute.
  • Signed: Similar because sign languages are complete nonoral linguistic systems; differs because signed names one family of manual languages, while nonoral is the wider category containing them.
  • Tactile: Similar because touch systems like Braille or taps are nonoral channels; differs because tactile is confined to the sense of touch, while nonoral spans every channel except mouth and voice.
  • Topical: Similar in medicine, where topical creams are a nonoral route; differs because topical names one specific route through the skin, while nonoral is the umbrella for all routes that bypass the mouth.
