The plural snarls carries the whole biography of an imitation. Long before dictionaries, speakers on both sides of the North Sea were shaping the syllable snar with wrinkled noses and bared teeth, performing the growl in the act of naming it. Middle Dutch and Middle Low German snarren meant to growl, grumble, or rattle, and Middle English held the same scrap of sound as snar, to growl or snap at, by the late fourteenth century — kennel vocabulary, beneath the notice of chroniclers but alive in every yard with a dog.

The word's promotion came in the 1590s, when English attached its frequentative -le ending — the workhorse suffix of gabble, prattle, and dazzle — to make snarl the kept-up growl rather than the single snap. The plural snarls was then inevitable and immediately expressive: one snarl may be reflex, but snarls are policy, a sustained texture of hostility. Seventeenth-century writers ran with it, hearing snarls in pulpits, pamphlets, and parliaments, and stretching the noun from the throat to the twisted face that frames the sound.

Stages of the journey:

  • Echoic Germanic roots: short snar-forms across Dutch, Low German, and Middle English, imitating the nasal constriction and throat-rumble of the growl, with no Latin or Greek ancestor behind them.
  • The frequentative lift of the 1590s: snarl fixes the iterative sense — the growl that keeps going — and the plural snarls names the volley, ready for literal packs and figurative press packs alike.
  • Seventeenth-century generalisation: from kennels to controversies, snarls become any hostile chorus, and the noun stretches to cover expressions and utterances as well as sounds.
  • The novelist's kennel chorus: Gothic and Victorian fiction deploys packs of snarls as audible wallpaper for danger, teaching generations of readers to hear character in the sound.
  • Modern plural metaphor: the twentieth century counts hostilities in snarls — snarls of debate, snarls of traffic borrowed from the tangle-twin — while the source sentence restores the literal music: hints dropped among growls, intelligence sieved from menace.

Phonetically the plural changes nothing essential: the sn- onset still wrinkles the speaker's nose, the vowel still rumbles, and the final -lz cluster even prolongs the buzz, as if the growl refused to end. That refusal is the word's meaning. A bark alerts and stops, but snarls continue — and in that continuation the source narrator finds his opportunity, for between one growl and the next a hint drops, a padding footstep is placed at Esgaroth, and patience triumphs over teeth.

The plural also unlocked constructions the singular never managed: snarls of rage, snarls of derision, snarls of traffic borrowed from the knot-twin. Grammarians note how naturally the noun takes of-phrases, turning raw sound into countable collective behaviour, and how headlines exploit that countability — rival snarls, budget snarls — to make hostility sound both numerous and trivial. Yet the oldest use persists wherever dogs guard, crowds bait, or prisoners bargain: singly a snarl may startle, but only snarls, in the plural, wear patience down — or, as the source shows, let slip the very hint the snarler meant to hide.
