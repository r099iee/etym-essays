The word spittoon begins, audibly, with the sound of the act it names: the Proto-Indo-European root *sp(y)eu-, "to spit, to spew, to eject from the mouth," is an imitative or onomatopoeic root, one of those ancient words that mime the explosive spurt of the mouth. Its descendants show the root's restless energy. Greek turned the *sp- cluster into pt- by metathesis, producing ptyein "to spit," and from ptyalon "spittle" and ptysis "a spitting" English medicine borrowed ptialism and hemoptysis, "the spitting of blood." Latin kept the original shape in spuere "to spit," which gave sputum, the clinical name for expectorated phlegm, and spūma "foam," our spume. From the Latin compound conspuere, "to spit upon," Portuguese developed cuspir "to spit," and from cuspir came cuspideira, a vessel for spitting — which English imported, via Portuguese and the nineteenth-century American hotel, as the refined-sounding cuspidor.

The true Sanskrit cognate, ṣṭhīv-, "to spit," and its Hindi descendant thūknā "to spit" show the same root reaching into Indo-Aryan, while the Balto-Slavic languages preserved it in Lithuanian spiáuti and Russian plevat'. The Germanic branch reshaped the root as *spīwan-, "to spew, spit, vomit": Old English had both spīwan, which became our spew, and the iterative spǣtan, "to spit repeatedly," which through Middle English spitten gave the plain verb spit. Around this little verb grew a whole vocabulary of spitting-things — spattle, spittle, spit-box — until, in the nineteenth century, English built the grander vessel by welding spit to the augmentative suffix -oon, taken from French and Italian (-one), the same suffix that produced balloon, cartoon, buffoon, and lagoon.

The result, spittoon, "the great vessel for spitting," is first recorded in English in 1819, precisely as the chewing-tobacco culture of Britain and America was institutionalizing public expectoration: brass spittoons, filled with sand or water, stood in every barroom, railway station, courthouse, and hotel lobby, and their polished gleam became a fixed feature of American public interiors. The word travelled easily — German Spucknapf ("spit-cup") and Dutch spuugbak built their own compounds on the same image — and when chewing tobacco faded in the twentieth century, the spittoon vanished from public life, leaving the word to survive as a historical and comic relic. In The Story of the Stone the spittoon appears in an older and nobler guise, the attendant-held vessel of a Chinese aristocratic banquet, where it testifies to a bodily discipline as ceremonial as any in the West — a reminder that the word for the humblest of vessels can carry the history of manners on its back.
