Single root word: scummy (the adjective formed from the English noun scum plus the adjective suffix -y). Derivation chain: 1. Proto-Indo-European root *skeu- (also *keu-, *sku-), "to cover, conceal, hide, enclose" — reconstructed, c. 4500–2500 BCE. Attested derivatives include Vedic Sanskrit skunāti "he covers," Ancient Greek skytos "hide, leather," Latin cutis "skin" and obscurus "dark, covered over." NOTE: the connection of the Germanic "foam" word to this root is traditional but debated; it rests on the idea that scum is the "skin" or covering that grows over a liquid. 2. Proto-Germanic *skūmaz, "foam, froth; film or dross that forms over a liquid" — reconstructed, Proto-Germanic era, c. 500 BCE–c. 200 CE. Attested descendants: Old Norse skúm "foam, scum," Old High German scūm (modern German Schaum "foam"), Middle Dutch schūm, Middle Low German schūm(e), and rare Old English scūma. 3. Middle English scume / skum, "froth, foam; the film or dross rising to the surface of a boiling, fermenting, or stagnant liquid" — attested c. 1300; borrowed from Middle Dutch schūm or Middle Low German schūm(e) via Anglo-Dutch and Hanseatic trade contacts (some accounts allow a native development of the same Germanic stock). 4. English scum (noun), "surface film of foul froth or waste; figuratively, the most contemptible class of people" — attested from the 14th c. onward; the figurative "refuse of society" sense is recorded from the late 16th–17th c. (Shakespeare uses it in Hamlet: "the scum of the earth"). 5. English scum + the adjective suffix -y —> scummy, "covered with or full of scum; figuratively, despicable, contemptible, sleazy" — the literal adjective is attested c. 1790s; the figurative slang sense develops in the 19th–20th c. — final node: scummy. DEBATED / NUANCED ETYMOLOGY: Two points are worth flagging. First, the Proto-Germanic *skūmaz is secure, but its link to PIE *skeu- "to cover" is a reasonable but not universally accepted hypothesis; some scholars prefer to treat the foam-word as a Germanic-internal expressive formation rather than a regular continuation of the cover root. Second, the English noun may be a Middle Dutch / Middle Low German loan rather than a native continuation of Old English scūma; both routes yield the same modern word. The adjective scummy is an ordinary English formation and is not itself debated.
