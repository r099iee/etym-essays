Note on historical etymology: Semitic philologists note a significant linguistic debate regarding the precise semantic origin of Arabic sūrah. Traditional Arab lexicographers (such as Ibn Manẓūr in Lisān al-‘Arab, al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī, and al-Fīrūzābādī) favored an indigenous Arabic derivation from sūr ("wall, rampart"), arguing that a chapter is termed a sūrah because it encloses its verses like a protective city wall, or from tasawwur ("ascending, climbing"), denoting a step or elevated degree in spiritual status. Conversely, modern comparative Semiticists (notably Theodor Nöldeke, Arthur Jeffery, and Sigmund Fraenkel) argue that the term was an early Judeo-Christian religious loanword into Arabic, borrowed from Syriac šūrtā ("line, series, row of writing, text of scripture") or Hebrew shūrah ("row, line"), which the early Muslim community adapted to signify a discrete textual unit of revelation.
