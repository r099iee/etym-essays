The career of exemplary begins in the marketplace with the Latin verb emere, to take, to buy, to procure — the root behind exempt, redeem, premium and sample itself. With the prefix ex- (out of), Latin formed eximere, to take out, to single out, to remove: the buyer lifting one piece from the heap to inspect it. From its past participle exemptum came the noun exemplum, literally a portion taken out for inspection — a sample, a pattern, a model, and by natural extension a warning instance. A Roman exemplum could be a tile lifted from a kiln-load to show the batch, a paragraph copied from a speech to show the style, a hero's deed recited to show virtue, or a traitor's execution staged to show the price of treason. Display for copying and display for avoidance were one act.

Classical literature institutionalised both displays. Valerius Maximus in the reign of Tiberius compiled Factorum ac dictorum memorabilium libri IX, a handbook of exempla — brave, chaste, grateful, cruel deeds — for orators needing illustrations; Livy prefaced his history by promising readers exempla to imitate and to shun; Cicero argued cases by citing the exempla maiorum, the patterns of the ancestors. Late Latin then adjectivised the noun as exemplaris, meaning that which may be copied or that which serves as a transcript: an exemplar manuscript was the master copy in the scriptorium from which apprentices multiplied texts. Phonologically the path is clean: PIE *em- with e-grade plus thematic -ere gives emere; prefixation with ex- voices nothing but compounds the sense; exemplum adds the instrumental suffix -lum (a thing by which), and exemplaris adds the relational -aris.

  • French mediation and Middle English borrowing (1200s-1400s): Old French took exemplaire for both a model to imitate and a fair copy of a charter, and Middle English borrowed it around 1400 in both senses. Chaucer's Pardoner deals frankly in exempla — moral tales told to loosen purses — and Lydgate praises exemplary lives of saints. Spelling wavered between exemplarie, exemplarye, exemplare before settling on exemplary under Latinising pressure in the sixteenth century, with stress on the second syllable /eɡˈzempləri/ and the x voiced to /ɡz/ between vowels by regular English assimilation.
  • Humanist moral pedagogy (1500s): Erasmus's De copia, Elyot's Governour, Ascham's Scholemaster, and countless courtesy books made exemplary lives the engine of education: boys copied exemplary letters, memorised exemplary speeches, emulated exemplary princes. The schoolroom sense in Ruskin's sentence descends directly from this programme — the pattern girl is the living exemplar set before the class like a fair copy before apprentice scribes.
  • The punitive branch (1600s-1700s): contemporaneously, judges and divines hardened the warning half of exemplum into a doctrine of terror: exemplary justice meant punishment deliberately sharpened to frighten spectators — gibbets at crossroads, fines that ruin, executions timed to market day. Blackstone defends the logic solemnly; Fielding's novels show it failing comically. The phrase exemplary damages enters law in the 1760s (Huckle v. Money) for sums awarded not to compensate but to punish and deter — still a live category in Anglo-American tort.
  • Evangelical schoolroom and Victorian earnestness (1800s): Hannah More, Maria Edgeworth, and the Misses Rice's circle made exemplary the highest conduct mark a girl could earn: exemplary piety, exemplary needlework, exemplary obedience. Ruskin's mother earned it through conscientious effort braced by pride; his aunt earned laughter by refusing it. The word's priggish edge — the suspicion that exemplary people perform goodness — dates from exactly this era of visible piety rewarded with cards and prizes.
  • Modern split register (1900s-present): today exemplary lives in two formal homes that rarely meet: the appraisal (exemplary service, exemplary attendance, exemplary leadership in references, citations, and Ofsted reports) and the courtroom (exemplary damages, exemplary sentence). Both preserve the Roman exhibition: one person lifted from the heap and shown, whether for applause or for fear. The spelling-pronunciation split with exemplar (/eɡˈzemplɑː/) and example (/eɡˈzɑːmpəl/) marks the family's full naturalisation: one root, three English outcomes.

So the mother who grew more and more exemplary was, without knowing Latin, re-enacting Valerius Maximus: a deed taken out, lifted up, and offered to the class. Her sister's laughter is the ancient response of the spirited to exhibited virtue — the reminder that every pattern implies a copy, and not everyone consents to be the paper.
