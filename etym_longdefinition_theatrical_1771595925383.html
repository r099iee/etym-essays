theatrical: of, relating to, or suitable for the theatre; hence, in extension, calculated for display, showy, artificial, exaggerated in manner or style — done for the sake of being seen and applauded rather than for inner truth. In Ruskin's sentence — "The press exerts its power to direct the nation to all that is theatrical, affected, and false in art" — the word is a term of severe artistic reproach. The nuance is that it describes art made for the sake of being seen: art calculated to produce a striking outward effect, to win applause, to stage a show, rather than art that faithfully and humbly records nature. Ruskin groups it with "affected" and "false" — the press steers the public toward the showy, the mannered, and the insincere — and the trio makes the tone unmistakable: this is a critic who believes truth in painting lies in the honest, unperformed study of the natural world, and who treats the merely theatrical as a betrayal of that truth.

Primary senses

  • Of or relating to the theatre: the neutral, technical sense — theatrical costume, theatrical history, theatrical companies, the theatrical profession. Here the word is purely descriptive, carrying no disapproval.
  • Of, suited to, or characteristic of dramatic representation: anything fit to be presented on a stage — theatrical effect, theatrical contrast, theatrical suspense — still neutral or even admiring.
  • Calculated for effect / showy / artificial (pejorative): applied to behaviour, gesture, or art, meaning exaggerated for display, performed for an imagined audience, insincere and stagey. This is the dominant modern sense of "a theatrical gesture," "theatrical posturing," and the sense Ruskin uses against false art.
  • Histrionic (near-synonym sense): in describing people, theatrical can imply over-emotional, play-acting behaviour — though it stops short of the full hysteria that histrionic implies.

The semantic bridge

The word descends from Greek théātron, "a place for viewing," built on the verb theâsthai, "to behold," from the noun théā, "a view, a sight, a spectacle." The theatre is etymologically a seeing-place — the place where spectators look — and that root sense of seeing is what gives the adjective its pejorative edge. To be theatrical is to be arranged to be seen, shaped for an audience's gaze. The word's whole history turns on the antithesis between the stage, where everything is arranged to be looked at, and the natural world, which does not arrange itself for an audience. Ruskin's condemnation of the "theatrical" in art is therefore not a mere dislike of drama but a philosophical position: truthful art does not perform.

Near-synonyms and boundaries

  • Dramatic: similar in being of or like performance; differs because dramatic is essentially neutral, even admiring (a dramatic rescue, a dramatic change), whereas theatrical applied to behaviour is almost always censorious.
  • Histrionic: similar in theatrical over-performance; differs because histrionic is the more savage word — hysterical over-acting and emotional excess — while theatrical may stop at mere staging and display, a calculated effect without inner substance.
  • Melodramatic: similar in inflated showiness; differs because melodramatic means overwrought feeling and exaggerated emotion, whereas theatrical need not be emotional at all — it is about the pose and the sense of performing for spectators.
  • Affected: similar in being insincere or put-on; differs because affected describes the mannered artificiality itself, while theatrical adds the essential motive — performing to be seen, before an imagined audience.
  • Artificial: similar in being false rather than natural; differs because artificial is the broadest term for anything made rather than natural, whereas theatrical names the specific falseness of display and performance.
  • Performative: similar in being done for effect; differs because performative is modern and often analytic — a speech-act or gesture that constitutes what it claims — while theatrical is the older, blunter word for showy display.
  • Stagey (stagy): similar in being exaggeratedly theatrical; differs because stagey is the colloquial, even slangier word for behaviour that overdoes the acting, carrying more comic condescension than Ruskin's severe usage.
  • Showy: similar in being calculated to attract notice; differs because showy points at the ostentatious display itself, whereas theatrical implies the whole frame of performance — an audience, a role, an applause-seeking motive.
  • Overdone / mannered: similar in being excessive and artificial; differs because these describe the excess or the stylistic pretension, without the theatre-frame that makes theatrical a systematic criticism of art's relation to truth.

The word's double life is its essence: in drama it remains a neutral technical adjective, but applied to people or to art it means exaggerating for effect, and the slide between admiration for craft and censure for insincerity has been with the word from its beginning. Ruskin's use fixes it firmly on the side of censure.
