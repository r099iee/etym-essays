Studs (plural) are small, decorative removable fasteners — typically metal or jewelled — used to secure a shirt front, collar, or cuff in place of ordinary sewn buttons. A stud has a shank or back that passes through an eyelet in the fabric and locks; the visible head may be plain, engraved, or set with stones. Bilbo remembers "magic diamond studs that fastened themselves and never came undone till ordered": the ordinary gentleman's fastener is lifted into wizard-gift territory — self-fastening, command-obedient, diamond-faced. The register is slightly grand, gift-lore, faintly comic in Bilbo's breathless recognition of Gandalf; the studs are at once real jewellery-hardware and fairy-tale technology. The plural matches a pair (or set) of such fasteners, as Old Took received them.

Core senses:

  • Shirt / collar / cuff studs — removable decorative fasteners for formal dress; the sense in this card.
  • Ear studs — small earrings with a post and butterfly backing ("diamond studs").
  • Decorative knobs / bosses — small metal or leather bosses on belts, jackets, shields, doors, and saddles ("a leather jacket studded with metal").
  • Wall studs (construction) — the vertical framing timbers in a wall to which plasterboard is fixed.
  • Breeding stud — a stallion kept for breeding, or a place ("stud farm") where such animals are kept.
  • Tire / snow studs — small metal protrusions in winter tyres for grip on ice.
  • Slang stud — an attractive, virile man (a 20th-century metaphorical extension of the breeding-horse sense).
  • Verb to stud — to decorate or scatter with studs or similar points ("a sky studded with stars").

Historical bridge & semantic logic: Old English studu meant a post or pillar — something that stands upright and supports. From "standing support" the word shifted to any short projecting piece: a nail-head, a boss, an ornamental knob on leather or metalwork; the removable shirt-fastener of 18th–19th-century formal wear is the natural clothing extension of the "projecting knob" idea, with jewelled heads turning them into display pieces. The breeding-horse stud traces separately to Old English stod, "a herd of horses, a standing stock" — a different but closely related word of the same "stand" family; dictionaries differ on whether the two are homonyms or one family, and both views are defensible.

Usage notes & idioms: studs in the fastener sense is typically used in the plural for a set (a shirt-front set of studs), while the singular stud names one fastener. The word also generates the verb to stud ("studded with") and the adjective studded ("a diamond-studded collar"). In tailoring the collocations "dress studs", "collar studs", and "cuff studs" distinguish the removable fastener from the sewn button. The jewelled stud is a classic gift object — which is exactly how Old Took's magic pair enters the story as wizard-gift lore, a present grand enough to be remembered across generations.

Differentiate: a button is usually sewn on and functional rather than removable; a cufflink joins two halves through a double cuff while a stud is a single shank fastener for shirt fronts and collars; a brooch pins fabric openly without the eyelet-and-lock logic; a clasp joins two separate parts. Preserve the plural of the remembered gift-set.
