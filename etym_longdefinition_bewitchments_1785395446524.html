BEWITCHMENTS, plural noun: spells, enchantments, or charms — the arts, acts, and contrivances by which a witch or wizard binds, dazzles, or transforms a victim, together with the state of being so charmed. The word is inherently relational and transitive: unlike 'magic' in the abstract, a bewitchment always presupposes an agent who works the charm and a patient who suffers it — a charmer and a charmed, a doer and a sufferer. Every use of the word, literal or figurative, preserves this two-party structure, and it is precisely this that distinguishes bewitchments from the neutral category of 'magic' or the purely decorative 'enchantment'. In its earliest and still central sense, a bewitchment is a magical act performed upon a person or animal, classically to harm, control, or transform them; by extension it denotes the condition into which the victim is thrown — the spellbound, will-drained state of one held fast under a charm. Because the suffix -ment makes it an abstract noun, the plural BEWITCHMENTS can also name a repertoire or body of such arts: a studied collection of spells, illusions, and enchanting contrivances rather than any single trick. In The Hobbit the word appears in a wry, folkloric register that the narration plays for understated comedy. Gandalf had 'made a special study of bewitchments with fire and lights' — the showman's craft of fireworks and illusory lights, benign deception mastered for wonder rather than harm. The nuance here is theatrical rather than sinister: a bewitchment in Gandalf's hands is sleight-of-light, dazzling both eye and mind, the festive enchantment of the Old Took's midsummer parties, remembered by every hobbit who ever saw the magic fireworks. The plural matters: Gandalf's is a repertoire, a studied body of arts, and the word's stately, abstract weight — be-witch-ment-s, four syllables of dry understatement — sets the narrator's amused tone as he deadpans the greatest wizard of the age on the subject of pyrotechnics. Tolkien, a philologist, chose the abstract plural deliberately: it turns the whole of Gandalf's pyrotechnic craft into a magician's discipline, both dignified and faintly absurd. Set beside its neighbours, BEWITCHMENTS separates cleanly. 'Enchantment' implies a songlike, pleasing charm and can be morally neutral — one may speak of the enchantment of a summer evening without any magic; a bewitchment, by contrast, always names an act and its agent, a doer and a sufferer. 'Spells' are narrower, the formulaic utterances of the practitioner, a subset of the wider art. 'Charms' suggest light talismanic or verbal magic, often benign. 'Sorcery' and 'witchcraft' name a practitioner's whole system of arts and its ethos, whereas bewitchment names the act itself and its effect on the victim. 'Glamour' — a word Tolkien also loves — is a spell of the eyes that alters perception, a cousin of bewitchment but narrower. Where 'magic' is a neutral category, BEWITCHMENT is always transitive, always dyadic. Polysemy and secondary senses:
  • Literal magical act — the casting of a spell or charm upon a person, beast, or thing, to bind, dazzle, afflict, or transform; the classic sense of witchcraft and sorcery.
  • Conditional sense — the state or condition of being bewitched: the spellbound, charmed, or transformed condition of the victim while under the charm.
  • Repertoire or art — a body, stock, or studied collection of such enchantments and illusions, as in Gandalf's 'special study of bewitchments with fire and lights'.
  • Figurative attraction — irresistible captivation or fascination of the mind or senses, as of a person 'bewitched' by beauty, music, or a story; the 16th-century metaphorical extension of magic to emotional power.
  • Historical-legal sense — in witchcraft trials, the alleged magical harming of persons, livestock, or crops, the standard charge brought against accused witches across early modern Europe.
  • Showman's craft — theatrical illusion and deceptive spectacle, sleight and dazzling display, the benign trickery of the conjuror and fireworker.
The historical bridge between the literal and the figurative senses was crossed early. Already in the sixteenth century, poets and moralists used 'bewitch' of a person so attractive or delightful that the beholder seemed to lose their will — beauty bewitching the eye, music bewitching the ear — so that the verb carried both a literal and an irresistible figurative sense at once. The abstract noun bewitchment followed a generation later and inherited both strands; hence today a 'bewitching' performance is not magical but riveting, and BEWITCHMENTS can describe either the arts of a witch or the overwhelming spell of a personality. In register the word is stately, literary, and mildly archaic, at home in folklore, fantasy, and period prose; it is rarer and more deliberate than 'magic', carrying an undertone of the sinister or the theatrically mischievous.
