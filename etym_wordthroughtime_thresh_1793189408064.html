- Old English — farm labour pure and simple: þerscan means to beat grain from straw with the flail; the dusty music of the threshing floor is everyday autumn sound.
- Middle Ages — the village year turns on it: threshing fills the winter months in the barn, flails rising and falling in rhythm; the verb also stretches toward any heavy beating.
- Early modern — thresh and thrash divide the spoils: the two spellings coexist, with thrash drifting toward beatings, defeats, and wild movement while thresh guards the farm.
- 18th–19th c. — machines take the flail: threshing machines and then combine harvesters mechanise the verb; the Swing Riots of 1830 see labourers smash the very machines that thresh without hands.
- 19th c. — the committee room borrows it: to thresh out a question becomes the patient metaphor for exhaustive debate — beating a topic till every grain of sense falls out.
- Today — the farm keeps it literal and the office keeps it figurative: combines thresh the wheat while committees thresh out the details.
