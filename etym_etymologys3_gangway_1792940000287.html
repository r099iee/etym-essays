The gangway begins as two of the oldest going-words in Germanic, yoked together on a seventeenth-century deck. Its first half, gang, descends from the Proto-Indo-European root *gʰengʰ-, to step or stride, which gave Proto-Germanic *ganganą, to go or walk — the ordinary verb of motion behind Gothic gaggan, Old Norse ganga, German gehen (by later sound changes), and Old English gangan. In the south gangan eventually lost to go, but in the north and in Scots the noun gang survived richly: a going, a journey, the passage of a mine, the set of teeth or company travelling together, and by extension a company of travellers themselves — the ancestor of the modern gang of workmates and, later, of robbers. Its second half, way, comes from PIE *weǵʰ-, to convey or carry, through Proto-Germanic *wegaz and Old English weg, road or path: the most general travelling-word in the language.

From two goings to one plank:

  • Old and Middle English — the parts wait apart: gang as noun (a going, passage) lives mainly in northern texts and Scots, while way strides through every dialect as road, manner, and direction. No compound is yet recorded; ships are boarded by bridges, ladders, and planks.
  • 17th c. — sailors coin the compound: with warships, merchantmen, and ferries growing larger and their sides higher above the quay, the boarding opening, its platform, and the railed passage serving it fuse under one name: gang-way, the going-way across the water-gap. Early citations treat it interchangeably as opening, walk, and ladder, which is why the word has stayed usefully vague ever since.
  • 18th c. — below decks and into the pits: naval usage extends gangway to the narrow deck passages fore and aft and to the side openings through which boats are hoisted; warship routine fixes to bring officers to the gangway as honour or punishment at one symbolic spot. Ashore, coalfields borrow the word for the main level roadway of a mine, the going-way along which tubs travel.
  • Victorian era — theatres, churches, and parliaments: as public seating grows into disciplined blocks, the slots left for movement inherit the nautical name: theatre gangways, church gangways, and above all the gangways of the House of Commons, where ministers are waylaid in transit. Playbills warn against standing in the gangways; ushers patrol them; the shouted interjection Gangway! — short for clear the gangway — becomes the stagehand's, docker's, and stretcher-Bearer [REDACTED] prerogative.
  • 20th c. — the air age: airfields adopt mobile gangways (stairs on wheels) and then enclosed telescopic gangways (jet bridges), while ocean liners perfect the railed, canopied ship's gangway as a ceremonial threshold photographed at every royal embarkation. The ferry plank of village crossings never disappears beneath this steel: it survives wherever small boats meet unimproved banks.
  • Sound and spelling: phonetically the word is conservative: gang keeps its hard northern -ng with no southern softening, and way follows the Great Vowel Shift from Old English weg to modern way. The hyphen fell away across the nineteenth century (gang-way to gangway), and the plural is regular. No serious etymological debate attaches: both elements are among the best-attested Germanic roots, and the compound's nautical birth is documented in ships' records rather than guessed at.

Tolkien's ferry gangway is thus a small time capsule: the seventeenth-century sailors' compound caught at its simplest, a plank across country water, centuries before airports stretched it into telescopic steel. From PIE striding-roots through Viking gangways to Merry's pony clopping aboard, the word has never meant anything but what it says — the way by which the gang goes.
