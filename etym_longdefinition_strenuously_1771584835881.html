STRENUOUSLY is the adverb of strenuous: “in a manner that requires or shows great effort, energy, or exertion; vigorously, ardently, with sustained determination.” In David Hawkes’s translation of The Story of the Stone (chapter 3), Xi-feng steers Dai-yu towards “the one on the left nearer to her grandmother”—the seat of honour—“an honour which she strenuously resisted until her grandmother explained that her aunt and her elder cousins’ wives would not be eating with them.” The adverb names the peculiar energy of polite refusal: to resist strenuously is to resist with a full expenditure of will—vigorously, actively, and at length—so that the refusal itself becomes a visible performance. In a noble household etiquette is theatre: a well-bred young person must reject an honour loudly enough to prove that she does not presume to it, and then yield with grace. The word therefore carries a double valency: it is at once a description of Dai-yu’s energetic physical gesture of demurral and a coded testimony to her good breeding. Its tone is elevated and ceremonious, the adverb of formal address rather than of gossip, and its buried root sense of firmness or stiffness (Latin strēnuus, “active, brisk”, traditionally linked to the PIE root *ster-, “stiff”) fits a social order in which rank itself is a stiff, unbending structure. The resistance is strenuous precisely because it must push against that structure without ever breaking it. Polysemy and the full range of uses:
  • Physical effort—of the body under load: “he exercised strenuously”, “strenuous labour”, “a strenuous climb”.
  • Sustained mental or moral exertion—of concentrated work: “she worked strenuously to finish the report before the deadline”.
  • Formal denial or resistance—the emphatic, ceremonial use: “strenuously denied the allegations”, “strenuously resisted”, “strenuously objected”; this is the most idiomatically fixed modern use and the one at work in the context sentence.
  • Ardent intensity of purpose—the older adjectival sense (17th century) of “vigorous, prompt, bold, enterprising”, implying ardour and drive rather than mere strain; the adverb inherits this in “strenuously devoted”.
  • Register shift over time—from positive praise of diligence and hardihood (17th–18th c.) to neutral description of anything requiring great effort (18th–19th c.) to a dignified, slightly bookish formality in modern public and literary prose.
The synonym boundaries sharpen the nuance. Vigorously stresses raw physical energy and drive but implies nothing about persistence or a moral posture—one can work vigorously without refusing anything. Earnestly appeals to sincerity and heartfelt conviction; an earnest request is heartfelt rather than effortful, and it lacks the strain and pushback central to strenuously. Laboriously names slow, grinding, even tedious work, with none of the brisk energy of strenuous exertion. Forcefully emphasises the power applied to the outcome rather than the effort poured into the act. Firmly, adamantly, and resolutely describe an immovable refusal, a rock-like finality, but they are static: they say nothing of the labour or active exertion that strenuously insists upon. Energetically is the everyday word for lively activity; intensely describes degree of feeling; determinedly stresses fixed intention; assiduously, industriously, and diligently describe steady, careful application rather than vigour and strain. Related formations: the adjective strenuous, the nouns strenuousness and (rarely) strenuosity. The word is a direct Latin loan into Early Modern English (strenuous, 1590s), with the native suffix -ly added to form the adverb (c. 1650); no Romance intermediary was involved, and the word’s perpetually bookish, Latinate register reflects that straight path from Roman Latin into Tudor English.
