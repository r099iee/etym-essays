bung-oh — also written bung-ho, bungo, bungho and, in jocular extensions, bunghosky — is a dated British slang interjection with two closely related functions: a hearty drinking toast roughly equivalent to “cheers!” or “down the hatch!”, and a casual farewell roughly equivalent to “cheerio!”, “so long!” or “toodle-pip!”. It belongs to the same interwar world of upper-middle-class and public-school slang as “old fruit”, “old bean”, “right-ho”, “pip-pip” and “toodle-oo”, and it is now heard chiefly in period fiction, Wodehouse adaptations and affectionate pastiche. The word is not a noun, not a verb and not a term of abuse; it is a standalone utterance, usually printed with an exclamation mark and often followed by a name or an address term (“bung-ho, old fruit”).

The sense in the Wodehouse passage. In Right Ho, Jeeves (1934), Bertie Wooster observes that some of his friends, if turned down by a girl, “would simply say, ‘Well, bung-ho!’ and toddle off quite happily to find another.” Here the word is unmistakably a valediction: it closes the episode, dismisses the disappointment and restores the speaker’s cheerfulness. The trailing “Well” is characteristic of the construction, because it marks a change of subject and a refusal to dwell on misfortune. The tone is breezy, unembarrassed and slightly throwaway; it is the verbal equivalent of a shrug, a wave and a quick exit. This is the sense that the Anki context illustrates, and it is the sense that most modern readers meet first in Wodehouse.

The drinking-toast sense. The word’s earliest recorded use is as a toast. D. L. Sayers’s The Unpleasantness at the Bellona Club (1921) has Lord Peter Wimsey raise a dry martini with “Bung-ho!”; a Springfield newspaper noted in 1925 that people said “Bungo!” instead of “Here’s how!” over cocktails; Hemingway used “Bung-o!” in The Sun Also Rises (1926); and John le Carré still has a character say “Bung-ho” over a drink in The Honourable Schoolboy (1977). In this use the word accompanies the physical act of drinking, especially spirits or beer, and carries a suggestion of hearty male conviviality. It can be a general good-health formula or a more specific “here’s to you”.

Hearty approval and the rare adjective. From the toast and farewell senses there developed a looser use as a marker of robust agreement or satisfaction. Green’s Dictionary of Slang records a 1955 example from J. P. Donleavy’s The Ginger Man — “Quite. In fact, bung, frightfully ho” — in which the phrase is fractured for comic effect and functions as an adjective meaning “proper, right, good”. This adjectival use is very rare and idiosyncratic; it depends on the reader recognising the interjection and its posh-slang associations. It should not be treated as a standard sense, but it shows how a fixed expression can be played with and re-analysed.

Form, spelling and pronunciation. The word is unstable in spelling, as slang often is. bung-ho is the most etymologically transparent form, preserving the h of the second element; bung-oh treats the second element as the interjection oh; bungo and bungho drop the hyphen altogether. All are pronounced with the stress on the second syllable, /ˌbʌŋˈəʊ/ or /ˌbʌŋˈhəʊ/, and the first syllable rhymes with sung. The variants are not sharply distinguished by meaning, though bung-ho may look more deliberately old-fashioned and bungo more like a casual phonetic spelling. The hyphen in the Anki headword is a lexicographic convenience rather than a fixed rule.

Semantic bridges and disputed origin. The origin of the expression is not securely established, and dictionaries differ in how much they claim. The most persuasive account, given by Green’s Dictionary of Slang, connects it with the British slang verb bung “to throw, fling, sling” (a Scots variant of bang), so that bung-ho! originally evokes the act of throwing a drink down the throat. On this view the toast comes first and the farewell is a later extension: the same hearty gesture that launches a drink also launches a departure. A rival, more intuitive explanation connects the word with the bung of a cask — the stopper that is drawn when a barrel is broached — so that the toast belongs to the moment of opening and sharing drink. That account is attractive but cannot be documented, and it is complicated by the fact that the “stopper” bung (from Middle Dutch bonghe) is a different word from the “throw” bung. The second element, ho, is an old exclamatory particle found in heave-ho, tally-ho, westward ho and similar calls; it adds force and sociability rather than a separable meaning. The compound may therefore be a purely twentieth-century colloquial formation whose components are only partly recoverable. Any statement that the word “comes from” bunghole, or that it is a nautical command, should be treated with scepticism.

  • Toast → farewell: a word said while raising a glass can also be said while raising a hat; both are rituals of good will at a moment of separation.
  • Hearty manner → hearty approval: once the expression is established as a marker of geniality, it can be reused as a general adjective of approval.
  • Upper-class marker → comic archaism: as the social world that produced the word recedes, the word becomes a costume that later writers can put on.

Register, decline and modern life. Bung-oh is marked in modern dictionaries as British, informal and dated. It belongs to the interwar period and to a narrow social milieu: public schools, Oxford and Cambridge, cavalry regiments, country houses, golf clubs and the West End cocktail bar. It is not regional dialect, and it is not working-class slang; its flavour is decidedly “county” and upper-middle-class. After the Second World War it fell out of ordinary use, along with much of the vocabulary of the Bright Young Things. Today it survives in three main environments: Wodehouse and other period fiction; television and stage adaptations of that fiction; and deliberate humorous or nostalgic use by speakers who want to sound like a character from the 1920s. It can also be used ironically, with the irony directed at the poshness of the expression rather than at the person addressed.

Lexicographical note. Because the word is an interjection, it has no plural, no tense and no derivational family of any importance. It should be distinguished from the noun bung “a stopper”, the noun bung “a bribe” (originally a purse), the Australian adjective bung “dead, broken, bankrupt”, and the vulgar bunghole. Those words are either unrelated or only distantly associated by form. The spelling bung-oh in the Anki card is one of several legitimate variants; the hyphen does not imply that the word is a compound of two independently meaningful English words in modern use.
