elms (noun, plural of elm): tall deciduous trees of the genus Ulmus (family Ulmaceae), native to temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, known for their spreading, arching canopies, rough serrated leaves, wind-pollinated flowers, and tough, splitting-resistant timber.

Primary Sense — The Trees

The core sense of elms names the elm trees themselves. Elms are large, often magnificent deciduous trees with broadly spreading or vase-shaped crowns; their leaves are simple, alternate, rough-textured, and sharply serrated (hence the "rough serrated leaves" of the short definition), and their small wind-pollinated flowers are followed by flat, papery, single-seeded fruits called samaras. Elms line village greens, churchyards, avenues, and riverside banks across Europe and North America; in the C.S. Lewis passage on this card, "tall elms" throw long evening shadows across a circle of grass beside a well.

Secondary & Extended Senses

  • Species and cultivars: The genus Ulmus includes the English elm (U. procera), the wych elm (U. glabra), the field elm (U. minor), the American elm (U. americana), and the slippery elm (U. rubra). Botanically the elms form a distinct and ancient genus within the order Rosales.
  • Elm wood — the splitting-resistant timber: Elm wood is exceptionally tough and resistant to splitting, with interlocking, twisted grain that makes it nearly impossible to cleave along the grain. For centuries it was the standard material for wheel hubs and spokes (which endure repeated shock), coffins, boat planking and keels, furniture, chair seats, floorboards, and shipbuilding. Its water-resistance also made it prized for underwater pilings, locks, and water pipes.
  • Dutch elm disease — a twentieth-century catastrophe: "Dutch elm disease" is a fungal wilt (Ophiostoma ulmi and related species) spread by bark beetles, named not for its origin but for the Dutch scientists who identified it in the 1920s. It devastated European and North American elm populations in the 20th century — the American elm, once the signature street tree of North American towns, virtually disappeared from many cities, and much of Britain’s elm landscape was lost in the 1970s. The disease is now a permanent fact of elm ecology, and the word "elms" often carries a note of elegiac nostalgia for that loss.
  • Medicinal — slippery elm: The inner bark of the slippery elm (U. rubra) yields a mucilage used in herbal medicine as a demulcent and soothing agent for sore throats and digestive irritation, and historically as a survival food. The bark’s name (slippery) describes its slimy texture when chewed.
  • Cultural and literary role: Elms are deeply woven into the pastoral imagination — the "elm avenue" of grand estates, the village green shaded by ancient elms, the elm-lined lane arching overhead. In English literature and landscape painting, elms are the emblematic trees of settled, communal, rural life. The plural "elms" is itself a literary staple (as in Lewis’s "bordered with tall elms").
  • Ecological function: Elms support many insects and birds; their shade and windbreak roles in hedgerows and shelter belts make them important trees of the managed landscape, and elm species are now being replanted in disease-resistant cultivars.

Semantic Reasoning & Historical Bridge

Unlike most words, "elm" has barely changed its meaning or form for over three thousand years: it descends directly from the Proto-Indo-European tree-name *el- (or *elem-), which appears to have designated a reddish-brown tree or the elm itself, named for the warm reddish hue of its wood and bark. The same ancient root produced Latin ulmus (the modern genus name Ulmus), the Germanic *elmaz (Old English elm), and the Celtic and Balto-Slavic names. Tree names are among the most conservative vocabulary in a language — people keep calling a familiar tree by the name they inherited — and "elm" is a showcase example: the ancient Indo-European farmers named it, and English speakers still use essentially the same word today. The plural elms is simply the regular pluralization of the singular.

Register & Usage Notes

  • Countable noun; plural "elms"; the singular "elm" also serves attributively ("elm wood," "elm avenue," "elm disease").
  • Pronunciation: /ɛlm/; plural /ɛlmz/.
  • Frequently used in the plural in landscape description (as in the card’s context).
  • Related genus name: Ulmus (Latin), giving botanical terms like "ulmaceous" (of the elm family).
