ponderous
- 'Weighing heavily' (15th c.) - Borrowed from Latin ponderosus, 'weighty'; at first a neutral physical adjective meaning simply heavy, full of weight.
- Weight becomes slowness (16th-17th c.) - The sense of sluggish labour emerges: anything ponderous moves slowly because weight itself slows it; the word extends from mass to motion.
- From the body to the style (18th-19th c.) - Transferred to manner and writing, a ponderous style is heavy, solemn, and laboured, plodding rather than agile; the word acquires a hint of dullness and disapproval.
- The modern double charge - Ponderous now carries both great weight and laborious slowness at once, with literary overtones of sombre dignity or tedious gravity.
circumfusion
- A learned pouring-around (16th-17th c.) - A rare Latinate coinage from circumfundere, 'to pour around'; a dignified, quasi-technical term of learned and alchemical prose.
- The fluid envelope (18th-19th c.) - Settles into use for the act or state of being surrounded by something that spreads or flows about a centre, especially light or liquid spreading through a medium.
- Modern rarity and strangeness - Remains a bookish word, striking precisely because it lets any mass — light, water, even swarming dwellings — behave as a fluid poured around a fixed point.
mean
- 'Common to all' (Old English) - Descends from gemǣne, 'shared by all'; the oldest sense is communal and neutral, of what belongs to everybody.
- The descent to the ordinary - What is shared by all becomes what is ordinary; ordinary slides into vulgar, and vulgar into inferior, so the word degrades socially.
- Shabby, squalid, low (16th-19th c.) - By the early modern period 'mean' describes poor quality and low rank, smallness of scale, the visible signature of low estate; the aristocratic judgment, the opposite of noble.
- Modern coldness - Today 'mean' dwellings are those judged inferior in kind, common in the oldest sense of the word; entirely separate from 'mean' meaning 'to intend', which descends from a different root.
dwellings
- From delay to settlement (Middle English) - The verb 'dwell' shifts from 'to delay, tarry, linger' to 'to remain, abide': lingering long enough in one place becomes staying.
- To make one's home (14th c.) - 'To dwell' now means 'to reside'; the noun 'dwelling' arises as a place where people live and remain.
- The sober legal noun (15th-18th c.) - 'Dwelling' stabilises as the flat, administrative word for a place of residence, the register of statute, survey, and chronicle, deliberately without warmth.
- Modern neutrality - Remains the colourless, functional term for a place of residence, chosen for coldness of tone; a dwelling is not a home, not a cottage, but a unit of residence.
