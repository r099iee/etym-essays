- c. 1400 — the small horse arrives: Middle English nagge names a small riding horse or pony, perhaps from Low German or Scandinavian stable talk.
- 15th–17th c. — contempt creeps in: the word slides from small to sorry, naming worn-out hire-horses and losers' mounts.
- Early 1800s — the verb appears: to nag, to gnaw at someone with constant complaint, surfaces in English and Scots, of debated Scandinavian kin.
- 19th c. — racing irony: stable slang calls even good racehorses nags, tough affection inside contempt.
- 20th c. — the verb conquers: the nagging wife, the nagging doubt, the nagging pain become stock figures of sitcom, novel, and clinic.
- Today — noun fading, verb thriving: real horses are seldom nags now, but bosses, bills, phones, and consciences nag more than ever.
