• Lawn: Similar because both name smooth mown grass around houses and parks; differs because lawn is the plain everyday word with suburban overtones, while greensward is literary and carries pastoral resonance.
  • Turf: Similar because both mean grass with its root-mat, a surface of living green; differs because turf stresses the material itself, even cut sods, while greensward stresses the wide inviting expanse.
  • Sward: Similar because a sward is literally grass-covered ground, the same turf; differs because sward is the bare older root, rarer and starker, while greensward spells out the greenness and sounds softer and fuller.
  • Meadow: Similar because both are open stretches of grass; differs because a meadow is usually rougher, flower-strewn, and mown for hay, while a greensward is close, smooth, and walked upon.
  • Green: Similar because a village green is exactly a communal greensward; differs because green as a noun names the place by function, the common at the settlement's heart, while greensward names it by texture, the grass itself.
  • Grassland: Similar because both describe grass-covered terrain; differs because grassland is the ecologist's large-scale biome term, vast and neutral, while greensward is intimate, human-scaled, and poetic.
  • Sod: Similar because sod is turf, the same living mat of grass and roots; differs because sod is earthy and workmanlike, even coarse in slang, while greensward never leaves its elegant register.
  • Glade: Similar because a glade, like the clearing in the source sentence, is an open grassy space ringed by trees; differs because glade stresses the surrounding woodland and the light falling into it, while greensward stresses the grass underfoot.
