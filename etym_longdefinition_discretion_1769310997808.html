discretion (noun) — the quality of behaving or speaking in such a way as to avoid causing offense or revealing private information; by extension, the freedom or authority to decide what should be done in a particular situation.

Primary sense: tactful prudence

At its core, discretion names the learned social capacity to judge how and when to act or speak. A person who shows discretion knows what to say, what to leave unsaid, and when silence is the wiser course. It is the practiced awareness of the unspoken rules of situation, rank, and audience: when the father in Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice complains that "Kitty has no discretion in her coughs," he means that she lacks the elementary good judgment to time her coughing politely — she coughs "ill," at awkward and embarrassing moments, betraying her ignorance of how her own body's impulses intersect with social expectation. In this sense discretion is not knowledge but applied tact: the fusion of perceptiveness, self-control, and concern for the comfort of others.

Secondary sense: the power to decide

A second, equally central sense is that of freedom of decision — the authority to choose among options according to one's own judgment, without being bound by a fixed rule. This is the sense in which a judge rules "at his discretion," a host leaves the seating "to your discretion," or a manager "uses his discretion" about how to spend a budget. Here discretion is not a quality of character but a delegated right: someone in authority is entrusted with the liberty to weigh circumstances and decide case by case.

Polysemy and secondary modern meanings

  • Judicial and legal jargon: "judicial discretion," "prosecutorial discretion," "sentencing discretion" — the legally recognized latitude of courts and officials to decide according to the merits of each case rather than a mechanical rule.
  • Administrative and corporate usage: "at the sole discretion of," "discretionary spending," "discretionary bonus," "discretionary account" (finance) — the power or judgment exercised by managers, trustees, or fund managers.
  • The idiom "discretion is the better part of valor" (from Shakespeare's Henry IV, Part 1) — a wry saying that prudent self-restraint is wiser than reckless bravery; it jokes that discretion is the greater share of courage.
  • Age of discretion (law and canon law) — the age at which a child is held capable of moral judgment (traditionally seven), when confession and other obligations begin.
  • Older sense of discernment: in earlier English (and in the word's Latin ancestry) discretion meant the power of distinguishing — separating good from bad, true from false, gold from dross — before it narrowed to social tact.
  • Confidentiality and secrecy: "in the strictest discretion," "matters of discretion" — the commitment to keep private things private; a discreet assistant is one who sees but does not tell.

Semantic bridge: from sieving grain to social judgment

The historical bridge runs through the image of the sieve. Latin cernere meant "to sift, separate"; with the prefix dis- ("apart"), discernere meant "to sift things apart from one another," hence to distinguish. The noun discrētiō named that very act of separating. Early Christian moralists, above all Saint Benedict, turned discretio into the supreme monastic virtue — the power to distinguish the appropriate from the excessive. In English this faculty of distinguishing hardened first into "discernment," then into the specific social skill of telling appropriate from inappropriate speech, and finally into the modern meaning of tactful secrecy and confidential reserve.

Distinction from "discreteness"

English has a well-known trap here: discretion and the abstract noun of "discrete" (separate, distinct) are twins, both from Latin discernere. "Discrete" (with the -e- of discrētus) means separate and distinct — discrete entities, discrete mathematics — while "discreet" and "discretion" mean tactful and circumspect. The meanings diverged through the 15th–17th centuries and the spellings split; the pair is one of English's classic near-homographic doublets.

In context

On the card, the father's remark that Kitty "has no discretion in her coughs" uses the word in its social-prudence sense: she lacks the good judgment to avoid causing offense and embarrassment. The word captures the whole comedy of the situation — a girl whose body betrays her at the worst moments, and a father who coldly files the incident under the virtue she does not possess.
