- [Late 13th–14th c.] - Middle English spray / sprai establishes itself in English poetry, meaning a slender twig, shoot, or flowering branch ("upon the spray") - [perception: quintessential lyrical emblem of springtime nature and singing birds]
- [15th–16th c.] - botanical spray expands into decorative arts and jewelry (jeweled sprays shaped like flowering twigs) - [perception: delicate, ornamental craftsmanship]
- [Late 16th–17th c.] - a separate maritime word spray enters English from Middle Dutch sproeien, denoting water blown off wave crests into fine droplets - [perception: salty, vigorous nautical vocabulary of sailors and mariners]
- [18th–19th c.] - Victorian poetry (Tennyson, Wilde) revives the botanical spray as a refined aesthetic image; industrial inventors build mechanical sprayers and atomizers for gardens and perfumes - [perception: high romantic aestheticism paired with practical Victorian engineering]
- [20th–21st c.] - technological explosion of aerosol sprays, medical inhalers, agricultural crop-spraying, and gaming idioms ("spray and pray") - [perception: ubiquitous industrial and technical term, while the botanical sense remains beloved in literature]
