hollows is the plural of the noun hollow: a scooped-out, concave, or sunken place in a surface. In the source context, the dreadful sounds of Smaug's anger echo in "the stony hollows far above" — the caves, recesses, and cavities of the mountain's interior, which magnify and reverberate the dragon's roaring. A hollow is by definition lower, empty, or excavated relative to its surroundings: the opposite of a protrusion or a solid mass.

  • Topographic sense: A low-lying depression in the land — a basin, dell, dip, or small valley, especially one between hills or at the foot of slopes. "The hollows of the Downs", "a leafy hollow in the woods". This is the most common everyday sense of the plural.
  • Physical cavity sense: An empty space inside an object or body — a cavity in rock, a hole in a tree, the hollow of a hand, the hollow of the throat, the eye sockets. Stone, bone, and wood all have their hollows.
  • Adjectival shadow sense: Because the adjective hollow ("empty inside; sunken; insincere") is far more common in English than the noun, the plural noun often carries faint echoes of emptiness and hollowness — a place that sounds, feels, or is empty.
  • Metaphorical and literary sense: Writers use "hollows" for any sunken or recessed space — "hollows of shadow", "hollows of the night", "hollows of the waves" — where the word contributes depth, recess, and sometimes menace.
  • American and place-name sense: In North America and parts of Britain, "hollow" (often pronounced and written "holler") is a common word for a small valley or secluded glen, and it survives in countless place-names, the most famous being Sleepy Hollow in Washington Irving's tale.
  • Register: Neutral, everyday, and durable across registers — from geology and anatomy to poetry and fairy-tale landscape description.
  • Grammar: Regular plural of the noun hollow (a hollow, two hollows). The word family also includes the adjective hollow, the verb to hollow (out), and the derived adverb hollowly ("to ring hollow").

The historical bridge: The noun began in Old English as holh, a cave or den — a sheltered hollow place in the earth. Because caves are both empty and low, the word naturally extended to every kind of concave or excavated place, and from the earth to the body, objects, and the landscape at large. The plural hollows is simply the collective word for all such places — a word that in a single breath names caves, valleys, cavities, and every other hollowed-out space in the world.

Sense distinctions at a glance: A hollow differs from a cave (which implies depth and a roof), from a valley (which implies scale and a longer course), and from a hole (which is smaller, rougher, and often accidental). A hollow is a recessed, basin-like, or excavated space — natural or made — whose defining trait is that it dips below the surrounding level. In rock it is a scoop or cavity; in land it is a sheltered depression; in the body it is the sunken curve where skin meets bone. This breadth is what makes the word so useful in description: it moves effortlessly from geology to anatomy to landscape without losing its core image of a place that has been, or is, hollowed out.
