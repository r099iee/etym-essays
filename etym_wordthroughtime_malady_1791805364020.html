- Late 13th c. — the word enters English as the ordinary term for sickness, often grave, and equally for the sickness of sin in religious prose.
- 14th c. — the modern spelling malady settles, in line with melody and remedy; it remains the ordinary word for illness.
- 16th–17th c. — humoral medicine makes it a condition of the constitution; the word keeps a learned, slightly generalising tone beside the blunter sickness.
- 1733 — George Cheyne's The English Malady fixes the phrase for melancholy and nervous disorder, showing the word's power to diagnose a whole nation.
- 18th c. — the favoured word for grave and royal illness ("the King's malady" at Metz, 1744), valued for its decorous vagueness.
- 19th c. — clinical writing abandons it for disease; it retreats into belles-lettres, historical narrative and translation.
- 20th–21st c. — archaic-elegant; survives in figurative "maladies of society" and in period prose, where it signals gravity, endurance and a certain old-world pity.
