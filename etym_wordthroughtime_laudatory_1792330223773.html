- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Era) — Entered the English language through classical humanism and legal-rhetorical treatises (adapted from Latin laudātōrius), strictly describing formal forensic orations or official epistles composed in commendation of a sovereign, general, or deity.
- 17th–18th Century (Neoclassical Era) — Broadened from civic oratory into literary and aesthetic criticism, applied to prefatory verses, dedicatory epistles to noble patrons, and celebratory odes honoring artistic and philosophical virtue.
- 19th Century (Victorian Era) — Widely utilized in journalistic reviews, biographical memoirs, and academic assessments; simultaneously developed a subtle cautionary nuance among critics when an author’s praise was seen as overly deferential or uncritical.
- 20th–21st Century (Contemporary Era) — Firmly established across academic prose, political reporting, and literary criticism as the standard descriptor for statements, reviews, or speeches expressing genuine, enthusiastic acclaim and commendation.
