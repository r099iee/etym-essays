Par in contemporary English most centrally means an accepted standard, norm, or level of equality, the state in which things are equal, average, or as expected, as in to be on a par with peers, to feel below par, or work of par excellence. It functions both as noun and in fixed phrases, conveying judgment against a benchmark: at par means at face value or equality, above par means better than average, below par means substandard or unwell, and par excellence means pre-eminent example. The underlying image is balance of two sides, neither owing nor exceeding.

Beyond this general sense, par has precise technical senses that all elaborate the same core idea of equality to a norm. In finance, par value is the nominal face value of a share or bond, and to issue at par means at exactly that value, neither at discount nor premium. In golf, par is the number of strokes an expert player is expected to need for a hole or course, e.g. par 4, par 72, with birdie, bogey, and eagle defined relative to it. In education, publishing, and everyday speech, par extends to mean average performance. All share the bridge of equal reckoning.

  • General equality and standard: equal footing, e.g. on a par with, up to par. Polysemy bridge: directly from Latin par equal, via notion of two things made level.
  • Finance par value: nominal value of stock or bond, e.g. shares issued at par. Polysemy bridge: via commercial Latin use where par meant equal exchange value, money at face worth.
  • Golf par: expected strokes for hole or course, e.g. she made par on the 18th. Polysemy bridge: via late 19th-century borrowing of stock-exchange term into sport to mean normal expected score for first-class play.
  • Health and feeling below par: feeling slightly ill or below usual form. Polysemy bridge: via golf and general standard metaphor where body performance is scored like play, below norm equals unwell.
  • Par excellence as intensifier: quintessential, e.g. the Internet as digital artifact par excellence. Polysemy bridge: via French par excellence by excellence, where par shifts from equal to by means of, highlighting the exemplar that defines the standard.

Stylistically par is crisp, Latinate, and versatile, at home in boardrooms, clubhouses, classrooms, and literary essays. It is concise to the point of idiom, rarely inflected except plural pars in golf, and typically appears in prepositional phrases rather than alone. Unlike average, which is statistical and neutral, par implies a normative expectation to be met, with faint moral pressure to measure up. Unlike standard or norm, which can be bureaucratic, par retains a sporting and gentlemanly flavour, evoking fair play and face value. Its brevity makes it ideal for compounds and fixed collocations that compress judgment into three letters.

Grammatical notes include its use as adjective in par value, as adverb in par excellence, and as verb in golf to par a hole meaning to achieve par. Domain nuances matter: bankers hear face value, golfers hear expert score, teachers hear adequate, invalids hear health. Yet all hear equality, the ancient Roman sense living intact after two millennia because human need for a benchmark never fades.
