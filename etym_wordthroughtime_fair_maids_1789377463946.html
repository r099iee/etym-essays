- 14th to 15th Century (Middle English) - The separate words *fair* (beautiful, unblemished) and *maide* (young virginal woman) frequently co-occur in chivalric romances and ballads as a stock poetic epithet (*fair maide*) celebrating physical beauty and chastity.
- Late 16th Century (Elizabethan Era) - Coined by English botanists and herbalists (notably John Gerard, 1597) as *Fair-maids of France* for the newly imported double white mountain buttercup (*Ranunculus aconitifolius* 'Pleniflorus'), inspired by its ruffled, snowy petticoat-like petals.
- 17th to 18th Century (Early Modern Period) - Expands in folk taxonomy: applied to early-blooming Candlemas snowdrops as *Fair Maids of February*; simultaneously corrupted in Devon and Cornish maritime slang to denote smoked pilchards (from Spanish *fumado*).
- 19th Century (Victorian Pastoralism) - Celebrated in Victorian cottage garden literature, folklore compendiums, and children's allegorical tales (such as Oscar Wilde's *The Devoted Friend*) as an emblem of rustic charm and unblemished innocence.
- 20th to 21st Century - Preserved primarily in historical horticulture, heirloom gardening societies, and botanical literature as a nostalgic vernacular name for antique double-flowered white perennials.
