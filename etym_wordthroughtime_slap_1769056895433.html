- Late Middle English (14th–15th c.) - slap (ME slappe) enters English from continental Germanic as a sound-word for a blow with the open hand; the sense is purely physical, with no metaphor yet - a noise that had grown a meaning.
- Late 16th–17th c. - the verb to slap appears (c. 1600) and the noun starts its figurative life in idioms - a slap in the face (an insult), a slap on the wrist (a mild punishment) - so the word comes to name symbolic blows as well as real ones.
- 1670s - the decisive shift: slap becomes an adverb meaning 'suddenly, directly, full upon', because a slap makes immediate, complete, face-to-face contact - the logic of impact transferred to position in space, with nothing between.
- 18th–19th c. - the emphatic compounds set in: slap-bang (1786), slapdash (1670s); the adverbial phrases slap in the middle and slap up against harden into chiefly British colloquial formulas.
- 20th–21st c. - the adverb survives as a distinctly British idiom; a modern speaker would say right or straight, so choosing slap deliberately keeps the flat, sharp image of the palm - exactly the brutal, emphatic force Edmund reaches for.
