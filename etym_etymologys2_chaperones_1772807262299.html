Single root word (CHAPERONES, plural of chaperone). Linear chain from the earliest securely attested stage; the ultimate origin of cappa is obscure, so no secure PIE root is asserted for the garment base. → Late Latin cappa 'cape, hooded cloak' (attested by the 6th–7th c. CE; origin obscure; frequently, but not securely, connected with Latin caput 'head', from PIE *kaput- 'head', as a head-covering — see debate note) → Old French chape 'cape, cope, covering' (9th–12th c., from Late Latin cappa; also the source of English cape and liturgical cope) → Old French chaperon 'hood, soft cap or head-covering' (12th c.; diminutive of chape) → Middle French chaperon 'hood; figurative "one who covers or protects", a person who screens another's reputation' (15th–16th c.) → English chaperon 'a woman who accompanies a young unmarried woman in public to protect her reputation' (1720, borrowed from French) → English chaperone 'chaperon' (19th-c. spelling variant with final -e; also the verb to chaperone) → CHAPERONES 'plural of chaperone; the card's form' (present-day English) Debate note: the origin of Late Latin cappa is disputed. The traditional link to Latin caput 'head' (PIE *kaput-) is plausible but not proven; other proposals suggest a borrowed, possibly Mediterranean or Celtic, source. Standard references (OED, Etymonline) therefore begin the secure record at cappa and treat the earlier history as uncertain rather than inventing a firm PIE root. The chapel < capella connection sometimes added here is popular but debated.
