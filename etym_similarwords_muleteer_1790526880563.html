• drover: Similar because both denote a person who drives livestock along roads or trails; differs because a drover drives herds of cattle, sheep, or pigs to market on the hoof (often across open pastures), whereas a muleteer specifically handles pack mules carrying heavy cargo strapped to saddles over steep terrain.
  • teamster: Similar because both denote drivers of draft animals carrying freight; differs because a teamster historically drove a team of horses or oxen pulling a wheeled wagon or dray (and today refers to commercial truck drivers), whereas a muleteer manages pack animals on narrow trails without wheeled vehicles.
  • packer: Similar because both pack and transport supplies with animals; differs because packer is a broader, modern North American term for anyone who loads pack horses or mules for wilderness expeditions, whereas muleteer is a historic, Mediterranean-tinged European term.
  • wrangler: Similar because both handle working equines; differs because a wrangler specifically rounds up, cares for, and saddles horses on a ranch or western outfit (often for riding), whereas a muleteer drives cargo-bearing pack mules on long commercial trade routes.
  • carter: Similar because both transport goods overland; differs because a carter drives a two-wheeled cart or open wagon, whereas a muleteer operates where wagons and carts cannot travel due to rugged terrain.
  • hostler (or ostler): Similar because both work closely with working beasts of burden; differs because a hostler is a stationary stableman who takes care of horses and mules at an inn or post-station, whereas a muleteer travels constantly on the trail driving the animals.
  • camelier (or cameleer): Similar because both drive pack animals across harsh, unpaved landscapes; differs because a cameleer specializes in desert caravans with camels, whereas a muleteer operates primarily in mountainous and hilly Mediterranean or American terrain with mules.
  • coachman: Similar because both drive equines; differs because a coachman drives high-status passenger carriages or public stagecoaches wearing formal livery, whereas a muleteer is a rugged, working-class freight driver.
  • arriero: Similar because arriero is the exact Spanish cultural equivalent of a muleteer; differs because arriero is a foreign loanword carrying specific Spanish and Latin American regional color, whereas muleteer is the standard English term.
  • wagoner: Similar because both haul goods over distance; differs because a wagoner operates heavy four-wheeled freight wagons on established roads, whereas a muleteer leads pack strings along steep, roadless footpaths.
