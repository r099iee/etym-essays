Northern Dialect Base - The root wuther is a northern English dialect verb for wind blowing with a dull roaring sound, attested in Middle English weather-words and alive in the moorland speech of the north.
19th-Century Literary Fame - Emily Brontë's Wuthering Heights (1847) carries the dialect weather-sense into national literature, so wuthering becomes the famous word for blustery, wind-exposed heights.
Tolkien's 20th-Century Formation - The philologist Tolkien prefixes be- to wuther to make bewuther, meaning "to blow about, fluster, and discompose as if by a roaring wind," coining or reviving it as a partner to bewilder in The Hobbit.
Contemporary Sense - Today bewuthered is a rare literary or dialectal word, essentially localized to Tolkien's prose, meaning physically and nervously buffeted — winded and storm-tossed by a gale of activity.
