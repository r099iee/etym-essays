PIE *merkw- "to become dark" (extended form of *mer- "to flicker, to glimmer; to die, to waste away," with the *-k- suffix; c. 4000-3000 BCE) ↓ Proto-Germanic *merkwaz / *merkwjaz "dark, gloomy" (c. 500 BCE - 200 CE) ↓ Old English mirce "dark, gloomy, murky" (attested c. 9th-10th c., in the Exeter Book and glosses) — the native adjective, which survived into Middle English as mirke, myrke ↓ Old Norse myrkr "darkness, gloom" (c. 9th-13th c.) — the Scandinavian noun brought by Norse settlers and borrowings, reinforcing the native word ↓ Middle English mirke, myrke "dark, gloomy; darkness" (12th-15th c.) — native adjective and Norse noun merged and cross-fed ↓ murk "darkness, gloom" (Modern English noun, standardized from the 16th c.), alongside the adjective murky (14th c., murk + -y) Note: The noun murk is often said to be a sixteenth-century borrowing of Old Norse myrkr, but the native Old English adjective mirce was never fully extinct, and most authorities treat the modern word as a blend of both lines. The PIE root *mer- is also the source of words for death and decay (Latin morior "to die," mors "death," Sanskrit mṛtyu "death," Old English morþor "murder"), and whether the "darkness" and "death" senses are one root or two near-homophones is debated.
