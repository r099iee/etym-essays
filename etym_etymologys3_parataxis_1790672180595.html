Parataxis is a young English word for an old Greek idea. The elements are impeccably classical: para-, beside, alongside, from PIE *per- forward, through, beside, and taxis, arrangement, order, battle-array, from the verb tassein, tattein, to draw up troops, to arrange, itself from PIE *tek- to fashion, to make, seen also in tekton, carpenter, and technique. In Classical and Hellenistic Greek paratassein means to marshal side by side and parataxis the resulting line — soldiers shoulder to shoulder, words set beside words. No ancient grammarian uses it quite in the modern syntactic sense; that precision is German nineteenth-century philology.

The modern career begins with biblical and Homeric scholarship. Analysts of Hebrew narrative, transposed into Koine Greek parataxis — and ... and ... and ... — need a name for style that chains rather than nests. German grammarians supply Parataxe versus Hypotaxe around the 1830s–1850s; English grammars import the pair by the 1840s–1870s. Once available, the antithesis organises whole literary histories:

  • Scripture and epic: the King James Bible's and it came to pass becomes the locus classicus of sacred parataxis; Homeric parataxis is read as primitive-additive mind versus later periodic art.
  • Attic period vs plain record: Thucydidean and Ciceronian hypotaxis — long suspended periods resolved by a main verb — is set against Caesarian, chronicle and folk parataxis as art versus record, hierarchy versus democracy.
  • Modernist revaluation: Hemingway, Stein, Camus and the Authorized Version's admirers invert the hierarchy: parataxis is honest, masculine, contemporary; hypotaxis is mandarin, evasive, Victorian. Strunk and White, Orwell's plain-English crusade and journalism textbooks all preach paratactic virtues while rarely using the Greek name.
  • Orality and typology: Milman Parry and Albert Lord find paratactic-additive composition in oral epic; Walter Ong generalises to aggregative versus analytic consciousness; linguists describe creoles, child language and unplanned speech as naturally paratactic, literate planning as hypotaxis-enabling.

Phonologically the loan is faithful: Greek parátaxis /pa.ra.tak.sis/ becomes RP /ˌpærəˈtæksɪs/, stress on the penult, plural parataxes /-siːz/. Orthography never wavers. Semantically the word widens from clause-linkage to ethos: to call a mind paratactic is now to praise or blame its refusal to rank — egalitarian flatness or evasive refusal to argue. The lament in the prompt belongs to this late stage: once-technical terms have become general critical currency, and their loss leaves talk about prose style impressionistic. To recover parataxis is, paradoxically, to recover the power to see that not ranking is itself a ranking — a choice with a history from phalanx to Hemingway. Later handbooks from Bain to Brooks and Warren institutionalise the pair as the master contrast of English prose rhythm, while structuralist poetics extends parataxis to montage, collage and cinematic cut, proving the Greek drill-field term now orders sentences, images and thoughts alike.

That portability explains the present lament: when students lose parataxis they lose not a curiosity but a diagnostic lens that links syntax to cognition, orality to literacy, and democratic bluntness to aristocratic swell across three millennia of ranked and unranked speech.
