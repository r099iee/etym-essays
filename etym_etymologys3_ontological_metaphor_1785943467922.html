EVOLUTION OF ONTOLOGICAL METAPHOR — PART I: ONTOLOGICAL

The word ONTOLOGICAL descends from the most fundamental verb in any language: the verb "to be." Its Greek and Latin roots reach back to a Proto-Indo-European root *h₁es-/*es- that also underlies English is, Latin est, German ist, and Sanskrit asti. What is remarkable is that a root so ancient and so basic should, in the seventeenth century, be recruited into a new philosophical coinage — ontologia, "the study of being" — and then, three centuries later, be attached to metaphor to name a cognitive mechanism.

1. The Greek verb and its participle

In ancient Greek the root *h₁es- produced the verb eimí, "I am," and its present participle ōn, with genitive óntos, meaning "being, that which is." Greek philosophers took the participle and turned it into a noun: to ontos, "what is real," and the study of it came to be a branch of philosophy concerned with the fundamental categories of existence. The word "ontology" is thus built from two Greek pieces: óntos (of being) and -logia (study, discourse), the latter from lógos, "word, reason, account," itself derived from the PIE root *leg- "to gather, choose, speak." The compound means literally "the account of what exists."

2. Seventeenth-century coinage

Although Greek philosophy had long practiced what we call ontology, the noun ontologia was coined in Neo-Latin in 1606 by the German scholar Jacob Lorhard in his Ogdoas Scholastica, and it was used again in 1613 by Rudolf Goclenius. Both men needed a compact term for the Scholastic-Aristotelian discipline of metaphysics that studies being as being — existence, substance, categories, and reality as such. The coinage was immediately useful and passed into French as ontologie and then into English as ontology in the seventeenth century. The adjective ontological, formed with the English suffix -ical, followed in the eighteenth century and became standard in philosophical writing.

3. Modern extension beyond philosophy

In the twentieth century the word escaped the philosophy seminar. Anthropologists and sociologists borrowed ontological to discuss the "ontological assumptions" built into cultures; scientists spoke of the "ontological status" of theoretical entities; and computer science created its own sense of ontology as a formal specification of concepts in a domain — a structured vocabulary of "what exists" in a system's model of the world. The 1980 coinage "ontological metaphor" belongs to this broader modern dispersal: Lakoff and Johnson used ontological to say that human cognition imposes existence and physical form on abstractions, turning the philosophical study of being into a description of everyday thought.

EVOLUTION OF ONTOLOGICAL METAPHOR — PART II: METAPHOR

The second half of the compound, METAPHOR, has an even longer documented history, stretching from a Greek verb meaning "to carry" through the rhetorical theory of Aristotle and the schools of Rome into modern cognitive science. Its own etymology is itself the model of what it describes: the word "metaphor" is a carrying over of a name from one thing to another.

1. The Greek verb: to carry across

The word is composed of the prefix metá, "across, over, after, with," and the verb phérō, "I carry, bear," from the Proto-Indo-European root *bher- "to carry, bear" — the same root that gives English bear, birth, and burden. The compound verb metapherō means literally "to carry over, to transfer." The earliest recorded use as a term for linguistic transference appears in classical Greek prose, and by the fourth century BCE Aristotle had made metaphorá a precise technical term in his Poetics and Rhetoric: a metaphor, he wrote, is the transference of a name from one thing to another, whether from genus to species, species to genus, species to species, or by analogy. For Aristotle metaphor was a mark of genius, "the one thing that cannot be learnt from others."

2. Rome and the Middle Ages: the trope institutionalized

Latin adopted the Greek noun as metaphora, and Roman rhetoricians from Cicero and Quintilian onward gave it a fixed place in the catalogue of tropes — one of the standard devices by which a word is used in a transferred sense. Quintilian's synonym translatio ("a carrying across," the source of English translation) captured the same image in native Latin. Throughout the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, metaphor remained a category of rhetoric and poetry: a figure of speech, an ornament of language, a deviation from literal meaning that gifted writers deployed for vividness and persuasion. This "deviation" view — metaphor as exceptional, decorative, literary — held sway in European culture for nearly two thousand years.

3. English: metaphor naturalized

The word entered English in the sixteenth century, borrowed from Latin and French, and quickly became part of the standard vocabulary of criticism and education. By the nineteenth century, philologists and literary critics such as I. A. Richards began to push back against the decorative view, arguing that metaphor pervades ordinary language and shapes thought itself. Richards introduced the terms "tenor" and "vehicle" for the two halves of a metaphor — the subject and the image — and helped prepare the ground for a complete reversal of the ancient doctrine.

4. 1980: the cognitive revolution

The decisive change came in 1980 with George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's Metaphors We Live By. They argued that metaphor is not an ornament of language but a fundamental structure of thought: the mind routinely understands one conceptual domain in terms of another, and these mappings — "argument is war," "time is money," "the mind is a container" — govern how we reason, feel, and act. In this framework ontological metaphor is the specific mechanism that grants physical entity, substance, or container status to abstractions, enabling us to quantify, locate, and manipulate what has no physical existence at all. The ancient Greek "carrying over" has become, in modern cognitive science, the very machinery of human understanding.

Summary of the compound's evolution:

  • ONTOLOGICAL: PIE *h₁es- "to be" → Greek ōn/óntos "being" → Neo-Latin ontologia (1606) → English ontology (17th c.) → ontological (18th c.) → modern technical and cognitive use (20th c.).
  • METAPHOR: PIE *bher- "carry" → Greek metá + phérō → metaphorá (Aristotle, 4th c. BCE) → Latin metaphora → English metaphor (16th c.) → cognitive-linguistic concept (1980).
  • The compound "ontological metaphor" fuses the philosophical study of being with the rhetorical concept of transference, naming the mind's habit of giving physical being to abstractions.
