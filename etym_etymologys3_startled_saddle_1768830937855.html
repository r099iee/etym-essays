THE EVOLUTION OF STARTLE The frequentative leap. 'Startle' is built on the old verb 'start,' 'to leap up, move suddenly,' with the frequentative suffix -le: a startle is literally a repeated or reflex starting. The base verb 'start' descends from Old English styrtan, 'to leap up, spring up,' a native Germanic verb of sudden motion, and its own origin lies in a reconstructed Germanic *sturtjaną and, most distantly, an Indo-European root associated with stiff, rigid, springing movement — the idea of a creature going stiff and leaping at once. In Middle English 'start' develops its double life: to leap, and to begin (to start work — from 'to start up'). The physical reflex. In the sixteenth century English forms 'startle' from this base, and its earliest scenes are entirely physical. Startled horses start, startled deer leap; the word belongs to the world of hunting before it belongs to people. The frequentative -le gives it its repeated, involuntary character: a startle is not a considered jump but the body's spasm — the jump that happens before the mind decides. The mind is added. From the seventeenth century 'startle' extends from the body to the mind: to surprise, shock, or alarm. But the word never loses its involuntary core. To be startled is to be caught — the mind jumps as the body does, and the old physical reflex is preserved in the metaphor. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century prose uses it freely for sudden shock of any kind, and the past participle 'startled' hardens into an adjective of the face: 'a startled look,' 'startled eyes.' The betrayed body in modern prose. Contemporary narrative prose — and the adventure and fantasy writing this card comes from — has kept the word at full strength precisely because it is the verb of the betrayed body. On the card, 'she drew back startled' after the drop of blood: the reflex leaps, discipline fails, and the warrior is humanized in an instant. The word's evolution — from the leaping deer of the hunting field to the involuntary recoil of a fighter — is the story of a physical reflex becoming the language of inner shock, and it is why 'startled' still carries the body's signature in every use. THE EVOLUTION OF SADDLE A native word of the sitting people. 'Saddle' is one of the oldest words on this card, and one of the few native Germanic words of horsemanship. It descends from Old English sadol, a direct inheritance from Proto-Germanic *sadulaz — and that word is built on the Indo-European root *sed-, 'to sit,' the root of sit, seat, set, and Latin sedere. The saddle is, etymologically, a 'seat' — a formation from the sitting root with an instrumental suffix, made exactly as Latin made sella, 'seat, saddle,' and Slavic made sedlo. The word is thus a fossil of the moment a riding people named the thing they sat on: the horse's back, the first 'seat.' The rider's seat and the rider's command. From the beginning the saddle is more than furniture: it is the fixed point from which all a horseman's actions are launched — the seat of the archer, the lance, the sword. This is why the saddle becomes, from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, an image of power: 'in the saddle' means in command, in office; to 'unseat' a rival is to strip them of power. The phrase 'to saddle someone with' — to load a burden on a person — transfers the act of loading the saddle onto a horse to the act of imposing obligation. The shape spreads. As technology widened, the saddle shape followed: the nineteenth century gives the bicycle its saddle, then the motorcycle; engineers borrow the word for saddle joints, saddle roofs, and saddles of hills — any structure concave between two high points. The butcher gives 'a saddle of lamb or venison,' the cut of meat taken from the back, named for its shape. The word's later history is one of pure geometric transfer: every saddle is still a seat, and every saddle-shaped thing still curves like a seat between two rises. The saddle on the card. In the mounted combat of this card, the saddle works at its oldest level: 'up from her saddle she snatched a bow' — the seat from which all action launches, the rider's base, balance, and command all at once. Over more than a thousand years of English, 'saddle' has gone from a Germanic word for the thing a rider sat on to a word for every seat, load, and burden in the language — while never quite losing the horse it was made for.
