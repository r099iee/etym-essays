Iberians (plural proper noun) denotes the ancient pre-Roman peoples who inhabited the eastern and southern coasts of the Iberian Peninsula (modern Spain and Portugal) during the Iron Age (roughly 6th to 1st centuries BCE), and by extension in older universal histories any early tribal inhabitants of that peninsula. In Durant's list — Irish, Iberians, Picts, Danes — it functions as one example among supposedly once-cannibalistic barbarian peoples, illustrating his claim that cannibalism was once practically universal.

Historically the Iberians were not a single unified nation but a set of related city-state tribes — Turdetani, Bastetani, Contestani, Edetani, Ilergetes — sharing related (non-Indo-European) languages written in Paleohispanic scripts, distinctive painted pottery, stone sculpture (Lady of Elche, Lady of Baza), and hillfort towns. They farmed, mined silver, and traded with Phoenicians, Greeks, and Carthaginians before falling to Rome after the Second Punic War.

  • Strict archaeological sense (core today): The Iron-Age Mediterranean-coast culture (c. 550–19 BCE) defined by excavated oppida, necropoleis, sculpture, and Iberian scripts. This is how university textbooks use Iberians now — precise, material-culture based, distinguished from Celts, Celtiberians, Lusitanians, and Tartessians.
  • Broad classical sense: In Greek and Roman authors, Iberes/Iberi could mean all peninsula inhabitants indiscriminately, including groups modern scholars separate. Bridge: ancient ethnography used river- and coast-derived blanket names for unknown interiors.
  • Modern geographic-adjectival plural: Iberians as modern inhabitants of Iberia (Spaniards + Portuguese) is rare; one normally says Spaniards and Portuguese, reserving Iberians for antiquity. When used modernly it is geographic shorthand (Iberian lynx habitat, Iberian economy).
  • Durant-era anthropological sense: Primitive tribes cited in early-20th-century comparative histories as savage exemplars. Bridge: 19th-century evolutionist anthropology ranked peoples from savagery to civilisation; Iberians served as a classical-sounding savage. Modern anthropology rejects blanket cannibalism charges as often Roman slander (Strabo on the Irish, etc.) repeated uncritically.
  • Linguistic note: Iberian (singular adjective/noun) also names the undeciphered non-Indo-European language family. Iberians spoke Iberian (and Tartessian/Turdetanian nearby). The language is only partly understood; Basque is sometimes hypothesised as a distant relative, but unproven.
  • Capitalisation and number: Always capitalised as an ethnonym. Singular Iberian = one person or the adjective; plural Iberians = the peoples. Durant's "the Iberians" with definite article marks the historic-tribal reading.

In short, Iberians moves between three time-frames: ancient tribes who left pots and statues, classical barbarians in Roman moral geography, and modern textbook culture-label. Durant invokes the middle frame — barbarians on the edge of civilisation — to argue for a universal savage past. A contemporary reader should keep the archaeological frame alongside it: settled, literate, artistic town-dwellers, not mere cannibal footnotes.
