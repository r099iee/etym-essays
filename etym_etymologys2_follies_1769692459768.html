Proto-Indo-European *bhel- "to blow, swell, inflate, puff up" (reconstructed, c. 4000–3000 BCE)
→ Latin follis "a leather bag, bellows, inflated ball, puffed-out cheek, money-purse" (attested 1st c. BCE; e.g. in Varro and Virgil, of the bellows and the money-bag)
→ Vulgar Latin follis "a windbag, empty-headed person, fool" (semantic extension, Imperial period c. 1st–4th c. CE: a person puffed up with hot air)
→ Old French fol "mad, foolish, senseless; a fool" (11th c.; attested in the Chanson de Roland c. 1100)
→ Old French folie "madness, foolishness, a foolish act, extravagance" (12th c.)
→ Anglo-Norman / Middle English folie "foolishness, folly, a foolish action" (recorded c. 1225 in the Ancrene Wisse)
→ Modern English folly, with plural follies "lack of good sense; foolishness; foolish acts" (present-day).

Note on the debate: The link from Latin follis to PIE *bhel- "to blow/swell" is the standard and well-supported etymology (cf. Germanic bellows, Greek phallos). The semantic shift "inflated bag" > "fool" in Vulgar Latin is inferred from the later Romance evidence (Old French fol, Italian folle) rather than directly attested in texts; it is nonetheless universally accepted. The later senses of English folly (ornamental building, stage revue) are internal extensions, not separate borrowings.
