aqueducts (n., plural of aqueduct) — artificial channels, conduits, or bridge-like structures constructed to convey water over long distances and across valleys, most famously the monumental arched aqueducts of ancient Rome. The word is a Latin compound: aqua, "water," + ductus, "a leading, a conduit" (from ducere, "to lead") — literally "a leading of water."

Primary sense. An engineered water-supply structure that carries fresh water from its source (spring, river, or reservoir) to a city, town, or agricultural area. Roman aqueducts combined underground channels, open conduits, and massive arched bridges (like the Pont du Gard in France or the aqueduct of Segovia in Spain) that carried the water across valleys and uneven terrain by a gentle continuous gradient. The plural aqueducts is used of such structures collectively or as a set, as in the source sentence's "no magnificent remains of Latin porches and aqueducts are to be found in Britain" (Macaulay, History of England, 1848), lamenting that Roman Britain has left none of the monumental waterworks that crown the landscapes of Gaul and Spain.

Polysemy and extended senses. From the engineering core the word has extended into science and metaphor:

  • Modern civil engineering. Any artificial conduit for conveying water — the term covers canals, pipelines, tunnels, culverts and elevated channels built by modern cities and states, e.g., the Croton Aqueduct (New York, 1842), the Catskill and Delaware Aqueducts, and the aqueducts supplying Los Angeles. In this technical use it is a neutral word for water-transport infrastructure.
  • Anatomy and neurobiology. The "cerebral aqueduct" (also aqueduct of Sylvius) is the narrow canal in the midbrain that connects the third and fourth ventricles and conducts cerebrospinal fluid. The term was transferred from Roman waterworks by Renaissance anatomists in the 16th–17th centuries (named for the Dutch anatomist Franciscus Sylvius); the "vestibular aqueduct" and "cochlear aqueduct" are similar fluid channels of the inner ear. Here the word means "fluid-conducting channel" without any bridge or arch.
  • Zoology and botany. Analogous fluid channels in organisms are sometimes called aqueducts by extension, in the same anatomical metaphor.
  • Metaphorical usage. Anything that carries a continuous flow may be called an "aqueduct": a "conduit of ideas," an "aqueduct of information," pipelines of data or finance. The image is Roman — a disciplined, engineered flow — and the word lends grandeur to the thing it describes.
  • Historical and heritage usage. In archaeology and tourism "the aqueduct" means the surviving Roman monument: the Pont du Gard (1st c. CE), the Aqua Appia (312 BCE, Rome's first), the eleven great aqueducts of imperial Rome, the aqueduct of Segovia, of Nîmes, of Constantinople. The plural "aqueducts" in such writing is a byword for Roman civil engineering.

Historical-semantic bridge. Roman engineers called the water-supply channel aquae ductus, "the leading of water," which was tightened into the compound noun aquaeductus — the same construction as English aqua + duct. Latin entered English through French aqueduc in the 16th century (the first English attestations date from the 1530s–1540s), when antiquarians were rediscovering the Roman monuments. The word's subsequent extension — from a specific Roman monument, to any water-conveying channel, to the fluid channels of the human brain, to any engineered flow — follows the familiar path by which a concrete engineering term is generalized into a metaphor of orderly conveyance.
