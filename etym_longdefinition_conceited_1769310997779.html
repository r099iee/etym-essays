conceited (adjective): having or showing an excessively high opinion of oneself — vain, self-important, arrogant, puffed up with one's own imagined worth — and, in its literal historical root, "full of a conceit," that is, full of one's own cherished notion, especially of oneself.

1. Primary sense — excessive self-regard

In modern English, conceited describes a person who thinks too highly of themselves, who is complacently self-satisfied and eager to be admired:
  • of persons: "a conceited young man," "so high and so conceited that there was no enduring him" (the card's Austen context, from Pride and Prejudice);
  • of behaviour, tone, and works: "conceited manners," "a conceited smile," "a conceited book" (a work full of its author's self-admiration);
  • the quality, abstractly: "conceit" and the rarer "conceitedness" name it.
Key features of the primary sense:
  • It is about self-opinion, not appearance: conceit is an inner conviction of one's own excellence, though it may be read in manner, speech, or dress.
  • It implies complacency rather than aggression: the conceited person is pleased with themselves, not necessarily hostile to others — this distinguishes conceited from arrogant (which claims rights over others) and from haughty (which looks down on others with contempt).
  • Register: everyday, idiomatic, mildly indignant censure — the Austen-era and modern default word for annoying self-satisfaction.

2. Historical sense — "full of conceit," ingenious and witty

The word is built on the noun conceit, which once meant simply "a conception, notion, or mental image" (from Latin concipere, "to take in, conceive"). In the 16th–17th centuries, "conceited" literally meant "full of an idea" — and therefore ingenious, imaginative, clever, or witty:
  • "a conceited author" could mean one full of ingenious conceits (striking fancies and metaphors);
  • the literary "conceit" — e.g. the metaphysical conceit of John Donne — preserves this positive sense in the criticism of poetry.

3. Polysemy — the semantic bridge

Semantic reasoning and historical bridge: the shift is a natural narrowing of "full of one's own ideas." A person "full of conceit" was, first, full of clever fancies; but the phrase was increasingly used of someone full of a particularly flattering fancy — the notion of their own importance. To be "full of conceit" came to mean full of the favourite idea of oneself, and the adjective then hardened into its moral sense: thinking too highly of oneself. The old "ingenious" sense survives only in the specialised literary word conceit; the moral sense ("vain") has completely taken over the adjective.

4. Related forms and register

  • conceit (noun) — both "an ingenious notion or figure" (literary) and "excessive self-regard" (now the dominant everyday sense);
  • conceitedly (adverb) and conceitedness (noun) — the rarer derived forms;
  • In modern speech the word remains common but slightly old-fashioned beside arrogant and smug; it carries a knowing, eyebrow-raising tone — exactly the tone of Mrs Bennet's "so high and so conceited" in the card's context, where a man "fancying himself so very great" is the living portrait of the word.
