cuppe is the Old English noun for a drinking vessel, the form that Modern English has worn down to cup. It is a loanword from Late Latin cuppa, a variant of Latin cōpa "cask, tub, barrel", and it belongs to the same early layer of Latin borrowings as win (wine), ciese (cheese) and pund (pound). The word names a small open container for liquid, but over fourteen centuries it has stretched from the dinner table to the sports stadium, the brassiere and the chemist's bench.

Primary sense: the drinking vessel

In Old English a cuppe was a hand-held vessel from which one drank — of wood, horn, pottery, glass or precious metal. It was smaller than a barrel and more personal than a jug, and its typical use was at table, in the refectory or in the hall. The word could be used for secular drinking and, in religious contexts, for the chalice of the Mass, so that the same noun could name a peasant's beaker and the vessel of the Eucharist.

  • Table vessel — the everyday cup of ale, water or wine, passed from hand to hand in a shared drinking culture.
  • Ecclesiastical vessel — the chalice, often of silver or gold, reserved for the consecrated wine.
  • Ceremonial and votive cup — the loving-cup, the welcome-cup and the decorated cup offered to guests or gods.
  • Travel cup — a leather, horn or gourd vessel carried on journeys, the remote ancestor of the modern keep-cup.

Secondary sense: contents and quantity

By metonymy the container gives its name to what it holds: "a cup of wine" is both vessel and draught. A cup then becomes a measure, and the measure becomes a standard in cookery and pharmacy. This is one of the commonest semantic pathways in the language, the same one that produced "glass" (material, vessel, quantity) and "bottle" (container, contents).

  • Cupful — the quantity a cup holds, now usually written as one word and treated as an informal measure.
  • US customary cup — 236.588 millilitres, the standard American recipe measure of eight fluid ounces.
  • Metric cup — 250 millilitres, used in Australia, Canada and parts of Europe.
  • Apothecaries' cup — a small measure of liquid medicine, now obsolete but preserved in old pharmacopoeias.

Trophy, prize and competition

From the ceremonial loving-cup and the silver cup awarded to victors came the sporting sense that now dominates public usage. A cup is first a piece of plate, then the trophy itself, then the competition for which the trophy is played. The semantic chain is material, not abstract: the object is named, then the contest that awards it.

  • A cup — a trophy, and by extension a knockout competition: the FA Cup, the World Cup, the Davis Cup.
  • Cup tie, cup final, cup run — the fixtures and narratives of tournament football and other sports.
  • One's cup — a symbol of one's allotted fortune, as in "my cup runneth over" (Psalm 23).
  • Loving-cup — a large vessel passed round a company, each drinking in turn; the ancestor of many ceremonial trophies.

Anatomical, vestiary and industrial senses

The shape of a cup — a rounded hollow with an open top — has generated a cluster of technical uses that have nothing to do with drinking.

  • Bra cup — the shaped part of a brassiere, graded A to H; from the cup-like shape, established in the 1930s.
  • Protective cup — a hard shell worn over the genitals in sport; named from the shape, not the drink.
  • Cup of a flower — the calyx or corolla, a botanical metaphor for the hollow base of a bloom.
  • Cupping — a medical therapy in which heated cups create suction on the skin; the name returns to the vessel.
  • Cup and cone — the parts of a ball-and-socket joint in engineering, and the bearing that contains them.
  • Cup anemometer — the rotating cups that measure wind speed.

The cup in idiom and religion

The word is heavy with figurative meaning. In the Bible and liturgy the cup is the vessel of blessing or of wrath: "let this cup pass from me" (Matthew 26:39), "the cup of salvation" (Psalm 116:13). To be "in one's cups" is to be drunk (now archaic). "Not my cup of tea" (late nineteenth century) means not to one's taste. "There's many a slip 'twixt the cup and the lip" warns of uncertainty. The Eucharist preserves the older sacramental charge, where the chalice is the cup of the covenant.

Semantic bridge

All the extended senses radiate from the shape and function of a small open vessel: it contains and measures (cupful), it is offered and awarded (trophy), it is shaped like a hemisphere (bra cup, protective cup), and it stands for the portion allotted to a person (the cup of sorrow or joy). cuppe thus shows how a humble household object can become a unit, a prize, a metaphor and a piece of anatomy.
