Shuddered is the simple past tense (and past participle) of the verb shudder — to tremble or shake convulsively, typically in a brief, involuntary wave through the body, from fear, cold, revulsion, or shock. After the dwarves' song of the Mountain in The Hobbit, Bilbo is briefly caught up in the idea of adventure; then "He shuddered; and very quickly he was plain Mr. Baggins of Bag-End, Under-Hill, again." The shudder is the bodily hinge of the scene: a full-body flinch that snaps the romantic spell and restores respectable hobbit identity. Tone: physical, involuntary, slightly comic in its speed of deflation — epic longing discharged as a shiver. The past tense records a completed spasm, not a lingering state, and the narrative moves on the instant the shiver is over. The word is native Germanic, a frequentative formation in the great English family of shaking and shivering verbs, and it has kept its bodily honesty for over seven hundred years. Core senses of shudder / shuddered
  • Verb, physical: to tremble violently and convulsively, the whole body or a large part of it shaking in a brief, involuntary wave — from cold, fear, horror, or revulsion ("she shuddered at the thought," "he shuddered in the icy wind").
  • Verb, expressive: to make such a movement as a reflex of dread or disgust — "I shudder to think" (the fixed idiom of wincing reluctance), "the crowd shuddered as the beast roared."
  • Verb, mechanical / transferred: of machines, engines, vehicles, and structures — to shake, vibrate, or run roughly ("the old engine shuddered to a stop," "the building shuddered in the earthquake").
  • Noun: a convulsive tremor or shiver — "a shudder ran through him," "a shudder of disgust"; the event itself, as a thing that passes over or through a body.
  • Past form (this card): shuddered — the completed spasm, the whole-body flinch of a single past moment.
Polysemy: secondary, figurative, and modern jargon senses
  • Mechanics and engineering: "shudder" describes the violent low-frequency vibration of a mechanism — brake shudder, a shuddering transmission, a shuddering floor — the involuntary shake of a body transferred to machinery.
  • Earth sciences: "the ground shuddered," "earthquake tremors" — the earth-shake sense, where the planet itself performs the convulsive tremor; "shudder" overlaps with "tremor" in seismology's popular register.
  • Figurative "shudder to think": a fixed modern idiom of horrified reluctance to imagine something — the bodily reflex stands in for moral or emotional recoil.
  • Literary and psychological use: "a shudder of revulsion," "the shudder of the sublime" — the word names the physical signature of dread or awe, and writers use it to show feeling before the character can name it.
Semantic bridges The family grows from a Germanic "shake" base: Middle English shodderen / shuderen "to tremble," probably a frequentative — a "keep-on-shaking" intensive — built on the same stock as Dutch schudden "to shake" and the Low German schoderen. From "involuntary repeated micro-shaking of the body" the word stayed close to fear, cold, and disgust, and only later, from the 19th century, was the same involuntary shake transferred to machines, engines, and the trembling earth. Every sense keeps the short, convulsive, whole-body wave. Shuddered in Tolkien's sentence is exactly that completed bodily refusal: one past-tense spasm in which the Tookish spark goes out and the respectable Baggins returns.
