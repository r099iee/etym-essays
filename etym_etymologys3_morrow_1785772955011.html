Morrow is, in effect, a polished fossil of the ordinary Germanic word for "morning." The Proto-Germanic form *murganaz (also reconstructed *murgina-) named the early daylight period of the day; its descendants — the ancestor of English morning, German Morgen, Dutch morgen, and the Scandinavian morning-words — remain basic vocabulary across the whole Germanic family. Beyond Germanic, the deeper history is debated: a traditional attribution links the root to Proto-Indo-European *mer-, "to gleam, glimmer, sparkle," which would make the morning "the shining time," but many authorities stress that no secure Indo-European cognates can be demonstrated and that the word may be an old Germanic or wider north-European formation of uncertain ancestry. The exact pre-Germanic source is genuinely uncertain, and honest accounts of the word's prehistory must say so.

In Old English the form was morgen, a noun meaning "morning, sunrise" that already also served, in narrative context, as "the day following a given day": the morning after a night's rest was the natural "next day." Old English texts use morgen for dawn, for the first meal of the day (hence compounds like morgen-mete, the morning meal), and in time-expressions that pointed forward to the following daylight period. The word was a thoroughly ordinary part of the Old English temporal vocabulary — a functional, everyday noun with no suggestion of archaism.

Middle English reshaped the form. Through the regular loss of intervocalic -g- and subsequent vowel reduction, morgen became morwen, morwe, morowe, and finally morrow (the spelling with -ow reflecting the rounded pronunciation). In Middle English both senses were fully alive: morrow meant "morning" (Chaucer greets with good morwe), and it meant "the next day." Meanwhile a parallel formation built on the same base plus a noun-forming suffix produced morning (morwening), which in time claimed the pure "early part of the day" slot as the unmarked word.

As morning rose, morrow specialized. It thrived in temporal narrative phrases — "on the morrow," "the morrow after" — meaning "the following day," and in the greeting "good morrow." Early Modern English still used it freely in speech and writing of every level; Shakespeare's plays are full of morrows, both in farewell and in greeting, and the phrase "tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" in Macbeth makes the word's own history audible inside the fused form. Biblical English ("take therefore no thought for the morrow," Matthew 6:34, KJV) cemented its solemn, proverbial aura. At the same time, the prepositional phrase to morrow fused into the single adverb-noun tomorrow, which inherited the everyday future-day sense and left bare morrow sounding increasingly old-fashioned.

From the 18th and 19th centuries onward, morrow became a marked rather than default word. Romantic and Victorian poets deployed it for cadence and antique color; historical novelists used it to evoke earlier periods; liturgies and hymns kept "the morrow" for solemn promise. German Morgen and Dutch morgen, which still mean both "morning" and "tomorrow," are a living reminder of the original unity that English broke apart among three words.

  • Sound change. Loss of internal -g- in morgen → morwe/morrow; final vowel reduction; fusion of to morrow into tomorrow (13th century onward).
  • Semantic drift. "Morning" → "the next day after a given night" → "the immediate future"; morrow specialized as morning took the literal dawn sense.
  • Socio-cultural factors. Bible English, Shakespearean drama, Romantic poetry, historical fiction, and liturgy preserved morrow as a solemn/literary token; everyday speech abandoned it to tomorrow.
  • Literary attestations. KJV Matthew 6:34; Shakespeare, Macbeth 5.5 ("tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow"); the "good morrow" greeting tradition continuous from the Middle Ages.
