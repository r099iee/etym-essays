- Early 17th Century (c. 1640s) - Enters the English language directly from Latin *repellēns* via medical and natural philosophy treatises, functioning primarily as a medical adjective for remedies or physical applications that drive back diseased humors, fluxes, or swellings from affected bodily tissues.
- Late 17th to Early 18th Century - Expands into Newtonian physics, mechanics, and natural science to describe repulsive forces operating between material corpuscles or electrical charges, balancing gravitational attraction.
- Mid to Late 18th Century - Undergoes profound metaphorical internalization during the Enlightenment, entering polite literary prose and moral philosophy (Samuel Johnson, Edmund Burke) to describe manners, personalities, vices, or appearances that actively drive people away in disgust, revulsion, or contempt.
- 19th to Mid-20th Century - Flourishes in industrial chemistry, textile manufacturing, and public health, giving rise to consumer nouns and compound adjectives like "water-repellent" fabrics and chemical "insect repellent" (formalized following the military development of DEET in the 1940s).
- Contemporary Era - Firmly entrenched across both technical material science (superhydrophobic and oleophobic coatings) and cultural, literary, and political criticism, where it serves as a devastating, precise condemnation of morally abhorrent conduct or rhetoric.
