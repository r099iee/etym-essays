- 6th c. - Latin Dani names a fierce northern tribe beyond the Roman frontier; the word is exotic, marginal and slightly menacing.
- 9th c. - Old English Denisc is the language of the Viking invaders; to English ears it sounds hostile, pagan and dangerous.
- 9th-11th c. - The Danelaw makes Danes neighbours and settlers; "Danish" becomes a practical label for people, law and speech in half of England.
- 12th-15th c. - The word settles into ordinary use for the kingdom and its people; the fear fades and the adjective becomes geographical and political.
- 16th-18th c. - Protestant Denmark is a trading partner and occasional ally; "Danish" carries a neutral, northern, Lutheran flavour.
- 19th c. - Hans Christian Andersen, Grundtvig and Danish design give the word a charming, literary, progressive tint.
- 20th c. - "Danish modern" and the Danish pastry make the adjective a lifestyle and food label, especially in the United States; Danish bacon feeds Britain.
- 21st c. - Hygge, cycling culture and the "Danish model" of welfare give "Danish" a glow of cosy social democracy, while the language remains a minority interest abroad.
