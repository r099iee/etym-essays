The word cuneiform never passed mouth to mouth from antiquity; it was built on a desk from Latin spare parts to label something no living person could then read. Its two halves are frankly classical: cuneus, wedge, a word Roman carpenters, soldiers, and surveyors knew for any tapering block driven to split or seal; and -formis, shaped, from forma, mould or shape, the most productive adjectival ending in learned Latin. Put together as cuneiformis, wedge-shaped, the compound could have described roof-tiles or leaves. Travellers to Persia gave it a grander job.

From wedge-word to script-name:

  • Latin cuneus in Roman life: wedges split logs, stopped wheels, packed masonry, and gave their name to the wedge-shaped blocks of theatre seating. The word is solid, workmanlike, and common in Vitruvius and military writers, with no bookish mystery about it.
  • 1700 — Hyde prints it: Thomas Hyde at Oxford, describing the Persepolis inscriptions brought home by travellers, needs a label for marks like small nails and wedges and reaches for cuneiformis. Engelbert Kaempfer popularises the word in his travel account, and French scholars naturalise cunéiforme.
  • 18th c. — English adopts it: English antiquaries write of cuneiform characters and cuneiform inscriptions, at first for the elegant Old Persian column at Behistun and Persepolis, only later for the dense Mesopotamian clay tablets whose far greater antiquity was not yet grasped.
  • 1802 — Grotefend first breach: Georg Grotefend, working from copies alone, guesses that recurring Old Persian groups must name a king son of a king, Reads Hystaspes, Darius, Xerxes aright, and proves the wedges encode language rather than ornament.
  • 1835–1847 — Rawlinson on the rock: Henry Rawlinson copies the trilingual Behistun inscription from ropes on a cliff-face, and with Old Persian as crib unlocks Elamite and then Babylonian Akkadian, turning cuneiform from curiosity to archive.
  • 1857 — the Hincks test: the Royal Asiatic Society seals four independent translations of Tiglath-Pileser and opens them together; agreement vindicates the decipherment and fixes cuneiform as readable history.

Sound and spelling journey: Latin cuneus was pronounced with hard /k/; English keeps the hard onset in /kjuː/ by the regular development of Latin cu- before vowels, while the middle vowel settles as short /ɪ/ and the ending -formis becomes -form on the model of uniform and multiform. French keeps the accent in cunéiforme; English drops it. The plural wavers helpfully: scholars write cuneiform tablets for the script and cuneiforms for the foot bones, letting context carry the load.

Semantic drift into flesh and leaf: once wedge-shaped is available as a learned adjective, anatomists claim it for the three tarsal wedges that key the midfoot arch, anatomists of the larynx for small cartilages, botanists for leaves tapering to the stalk. Each transfer is transparent metaphor: the clay nail-head reappears in bone and green leaf. The script-sense meanwhile widens as excavations multiply: from Persepolis show-pieces to Lagash accounts, Mari letters, Hattusa treaties, and Nineveh library, until cuneiform means three thousand years of writing rather than one monument.

Socio-cultural standing and the source sentence: Victorian triumph made decipherment a parable of Western science rescuing the East; later scholars stress Iraqi, Syrian, and Turkish excavators and the living heritage of the region. In orality theory the word does quiet polemical work: set beside Chinese characters and syllabaries, it reminds alphabet-centred scholars that chirographic culture includes clay, reeds, and six hundred signs, not only pens and twenty-six letters. Museum captions, linguistics lectures, and anatomy lists now share one wedge-word, each use keeping faith with the reed pressed edge-on into wet clay.
