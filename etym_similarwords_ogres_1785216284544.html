• giants: Similar because both are huge humanoid beings of legend and fairy tale; differs because a giant need be no more than large and may be perfectly benign, while an ogre is defined by a cannibal appetite for human flesh.
  • trolls: Similar because both are monstrous, man-eating beings that dwell in caves and dark places; differs because trolls are Scandinavian beings of magic or stone-turning power, while ogres belong to the French fairy-tale nursery and the literary conte.
  • goblins: Similar because both are folkloric monsters that menace humans in the same fairy-tale world; differs because goblins are small, malicious, and cunning, while ogres are huge, crude, and defined by appetite rather than wit.
  • monsters: Similar because both are figures of unnatural shape and terror; differs because monster is a catch-all for anything out of nature, while ogre carries a specific fairy-tale pedigree — the man-eating giant of the cautionary tale.
  • cannibals: Similar because both consume human flesh; differs because cannibal names the dietary practice in any being with no folkloric identity, while ogre is a specific folkloric monster of which cannibalism is only one trait.
  • fiends: Similar because both are malevolent, quasi-supernatural beings that prey on humans; differs because a fiend implies devilish evil and cunning, whereas an ogre implies brutish bulk and appetite.
  • tyrants: Similar because both are cruel, oppressive figures; differs because tyrant is an ordinary political word for an absolute ruler, with no supernatural size, while ogre is a folkloric metaphor for cruelty-as-appetite.
  • bogeymen: Similar because both are childish figures of imagined terror; differs because a bogeyman is a generic, formless nursery phantom used to frighten children, while an ogre is a specific, named man-eating giant of literary fairy tale.
