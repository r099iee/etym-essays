Aquaplaning names skimming flat over water instead of cutting through it — in two famous senses: (1) the historical water-sport of riding on a flat board towed fast behind a motorboat, standing or kneeling while holding a rope; (2) in motoring and aviation, the dangerous loss of grip when tyres or wheels ride up onto a film of standing water and slide uncontrollably. Angela nearly getting "inhaled by a shark while __________" at Cannes invokes the first, glamorous Jazz-Age sense: towed over the Mediterranean on an aquaplane board, limbs dangling invitingly near sharks. The comedy lies in treating a near-death by shark as a mere holiday footnote beside Aunt Dahlia's baccarat losses.

Core sporting sense — towed board: The aquaplane is a broad wooden or composite board, gripped by rope from a launch; the rider balances as the board planes on the surface at 15–25 knots. To go aquaplaning is to take that ride. Popular on the Riviera in the 1920s–1930s before water-skis took over, it connotes wealth, daring, sun, and modernity. Example: "They hired a boat for aquaplaning off Cannes."

  • Motoring sense — tyre skid on water (chiefly British; American hydroplaning): when rain floods the road faster than tread can disperse it, the tyre planes on water, steering and braking vanish until speed drops. Example: "The lorry aquaplaned across two lanes." Technical, cautionary, driving-test register. The bridge is physics: hull and tyre both become flat planes supported by water pressure.
  • Aviation extension: aircraft aquaplaning (hydroplaning) on a wet runway on touchdown, increasing landing roll. Example: "The jet aquaplaned in the storm." Professional pilot register.
  • Figurative glide: occasionally of anything skimming slickly — "the talk aquaplaned over awkward facts" — gliding without grip. The bridge is metaphor: mental grip lost like tyre grip. Rare, journalistic.

Grammar, register, and boundaries: Aquaplaning is a gerund-participle: "while aquaplaning" (activity), "aquaplaning is dangerous" (subject). Countable in sport ("an aquaplaning accident") but usually mass. It does not mean ordinary swimming, surfing under own power, or skidding on ice or oil: water must be the planing medium. British spelling keeps aquaplaning with one n; aquaplane is noun and verb base. Modern drivers meet it in warnings; readers of Wodehouse meet it in sunshine.

Wodehouse nuance and period colour: At Cannes in the high summer of the 1920s, aquaplaning belonged with baccarat, cocktails, and speedboats in the Riviera package of rich idleness. The shark detail is pure Wodehouse bathos: a lethal predator reduced to a holiday anecdote, inhaling rather than biting, as if even sharks observed seaside etiquette. The word therefore carries salt, sun-tan oil, rope-burn, and the putter of a motor-launch, quite unlike the grim motorway sense of juddering steering on a rainy highway.
