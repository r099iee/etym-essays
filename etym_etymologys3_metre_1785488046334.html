The story of metre begins with a hand counting. The Proto-Indo-European root *meh₁- meant “to measure, to mete out, to distribute”; its oldest concrete sense was the physical act of portioning grain by the vessel, marking out a field with a rod, or counting out shares among a tribe — a root of the measuring-rod and the counting hand, felt first in the body’s world of portions and boundaries before it ever touched poetry. Greek: from the measure to the measured line Greek built from the root the noun métron, “a measure, a rule, a standard,” together with a whole family of measuring words: geōmetría, literally “earth-measuring,” became the science of geometry; summetría, “measured together,” gave symmetry; and the verb metreîn, “to measure,” governed instruments and standards. The decisive semantic step came when the Greeks applied the word to verse. Greek verse was quantitative: a line was “measured” when its long and short syllables fell into a countable, repeating pattern, and the patterns themselves were given names — íambos (iamb), trochaíos (trochee), dáktylos (dactyl), spondeíos (spondee), anápaistos (anapaest). Métron thus came to mean both the unit of measurement and the system of scansion, and Greek lyric poetry — Sappho, Pindar, the tragedians — was composed and analysed in these terms, the theory codified by the Alexandrian grammarians whose metrical handbooks are the ultimate source of the “lists of Greek terms” the passage mocks. Latin and the Romance transmission Latin borrowed the Greek word as metrum, meaning both a measure and a verse-pattern, and the Roman poets and grammarians (Horace, Quintilian) adapted the quantitative theory to their own verse. The word then passed through Old French as metre into Middle English in the fourteenth century. At first it named instruments and standards of measurement; then, under the revived influence of classical rhetoric and the humanist study of Roman and Greek verse, it became the technical word for the rhythmic scheme of an English line. The crucial point is that English is an accentual language, not a quantitative one: English verse is built on stress, not on syllable length, and every attempt to impose the Greek quantitative feet on English produced exactly the “recherché metres” the passage describes — learned but alien, imported patterns that never naturalised into ordinary English verse. The British spelling and the American reform The spelling metre, consciously modelled on Latin metrum, became the standard of British English, while American spelling reform produced meter, creating the famous divergence in which the verse-word and the measuring instrument are distinguished only by spelling on either side of the Atlantic. In Britain both the prosodic sense and the unit-of-length sense are spelled identically, so that “a metre of metre” can be a line of verse that is a metre long — a joke only possible in British English. The nineteenth-century division of labour After the French metric reforms of the 1790s (which defined the metre as one ten-millionth of the earth’s meridian quadrant, fixed by the mètre des Archives in 1799), the same word came to name the fundamental SI unit of length, and the two senses — the measure of verse and the measure of distance — settled into a single spelling. In the same period, as the passage observes, prosody divided into warring camps: “scientific investigations of syllabic duration or vocalic quality” tried to give English metre an empirical, quantitative basis, while the schoolroom handbooks “paraded lists of Greek terms and recherché metres culled from Swinburne and Bridges” — the late-Victorian poets, especially Swinburne and the metrical experiments of Robert Bridges, whose verse revived exotic classical and invented metres (Sapphic and galliambic lines, logaoedic and choriamibic combinations) that the handbooks treasured but that never governed the living rhythm of English. Sound shifts and semantic bridges summarised
  • PIE *meh₁- → Greek métron: the suffix *-trom (an instrument suffix) gives the reading “that by which one measures” — the metron is literally the instrument of measuring.
  • Greek métron → Latin metrum: a direct cultural loan from Greek to Latin, reflecting the transfer of Greek metric theory to Rome; the Greek epsilon is adapted to the Latin -um ending of neuter nouns.
  • Latin metrum → Old French metre → Middle English metre: the Romance sound changes (loss of final -um, vowel reduction) and the standard Norman transmission of literary vocabulary into English in the fourteenth century.
  • Semantic bridge 1 — “measure” (physical) to “measured verse”: the transfer of the concept of measurement from length and quantity to the counted rhythm of a line, already present in Greek.
  • Semantic bridge 2 — “measured verse” to “unit of length”: the late-eighteenth-century recoinage of the same root for the new French unit, a deliberate scholarly return to the Greek word for “measure.”
The result is a word with two lives: an ancient prosodic life inherited from Greek theory and a modern scientific life coined at the French Revolution. The passage plays on the gap between them — the “recherché metres” of the handbooks are the ancient prosodic life at its most exotic, cut off from the living rhythm of English verse, while the modern unit of length measures everything except the movement of a poem.
