• Moor: Similar because both are wild open uncultivated uplands of heather and grass; differs because a moor is higher, wetter, and peatier, while a heath is typically lowland, sandier, and drier.
  • Wasteland: Similar because both describe land never brought under the plough; differs because wasteland is a legal and economic judgment of uselessness, while heath names a beloved habitat rich in specialist wildlife.
  • Common: Similar because many heaths survive as commons with ancient rights of grazing and gathering; differs because common names the legal status of shared use, while heath names the vegetation and soil.
  • Downs: Similar because both are open rolling country, often protected and walked; differs because downs are chalk hills of short turf and sheep, while heaths are acid flats of heather and gorse.
  • Fell: Similar because both are unenclosed northern wilds of rough grazing; differs because a fell is high rocky hill pasture of the north, while a heath is southern and low-lying.
  • Scrubland: Similar because both are tracts of low woody vegetation too poor to farm; differs because scrubland is a global ecological label, often arid, while heath is specifically the temperate heather community.
  • Heather: Similar because heather is the plant that defines and clothes the heath; differs because heather is the organism, which a gardener can pot, while heath is the whole landscape.
  • Bog: Similar because both are wet acid habitats sometimes found side by side; differs because a bog is waterlogged peatland of sphagnum moss, while a heath is drier ground a walker can cross.
  • Prairie: Similar because both are great open natural grasslands with big skies; differs because the prairie is continental grassland on rich soils, while the heath is a small Atlantic fringe community on poor ones.
