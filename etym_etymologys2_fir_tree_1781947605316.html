FIR-TREE is a transparent English compound of two native Germanic words, each with its own chain. CHAIN 1 — FIR: 1. PIE *perkʷ-u- "oak / tree of the rocky ground" (reconstructed; the same root-family that yields Latin quercus "oak"). The Germanic sense would have shifted from a hard-wood tree to the evergreen conifer — but this deep link is DEBATED and not universally accepted. 2. Proto-Germanic *ferhw- / *furh- "fir" (reconstruction from the attested West Germanic forms; the exact base is disputed). 3. Old English fyrh, furh (genitive fyrhwe) "fir, pine" (attested from the 8th–9th centuries). 4. Middle English firre, fyr, fir "fir" (from the 13th century). 5. Modern English fir "fir" — first element of the compound FIR-TREE. Note on the debate: the connection of fir to the PIE *perkw- family (Latin quercus "oak", and perhaps some Celtic and Baltic tree-words) rests on a small set of Germanic forms and is rejected by some scholars, who treat *ferhw- as a purely Germanic word without a secure Indo-European etymology. The Germanic and English stages above are secure; the PIE stage is plausible but not proven. CHAIN 2 — TREE: 1. PIE *deru- "wood, be firm, tree" (with the variants *doru-, *drēu-), the great "tree-and-wood" root of Indo-European. 2. PIE *drēu-o- / *drew-o- "tree, wood" (the derived thematic form that gives the Germanic line). 3. Proto-Germanic *trewą "tree, wood" (from the *drew-o- grade; the same base underlies the Germanic set). 4. Old English trēow, trēo "tree, wood, timber" (attested from the 8th century; the form also meant "beam, log"). 5. Middle English tre, tree (12th–15th centuries; the modern spelling and vowel settle here). 6. Modern English tree "tree" — second element of the compound FIR-TREE. COMPOUND PATH: - fir + tree — English fir-tree, recorded in Middle English (firre-tre, 13th–14th centuries) as the ordinary folk name for the evergreen conifer; the hyphenated form fir-tree has remained stable since. Related facts: the same PIE *deru- root appears in Sanskrit dāru "wood", Greek drûs "oak" and dôru "wood, spear", Latin durus "hard" (via "firm as wood"), and Baltic and Celtic oak-words; tree is thus a member of one of the oldest word-families in the language.
