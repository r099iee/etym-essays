disquiet is a transparent English formation, dis- + quiet, but its depth comes from the long history of quiet itself, which reaches back to a Proto-Indo-European root for rest. The prefix dis- is the Latin reversative and privative marker meaning apart, away, or not, while quiet descends from Latin quies rest and quietus at rest. To disquiet is therefore literally to un-rest, to undo restfulness — a formation exactly parallel to distrust, disarm, and displease.

The deep ancestor is reconstructed PIE *kʷyeh₁- to rest, be quiet, which also yields Avestan šyāti rest and possibly Greek bed-rest vocabulary. In Latin the root produced a rich family: quies (genitive quietis) rest, repose, sleep, peace; quiescere to rest, keep quiet; quietus quiet, at rest, undisturbed — the source of English quiet, quietus, acquiesce, requiem, and tranquil by distant kinship. Quietus in Roman law and later Christian Latin could mean discharged, released from life, hence Shakespeare's quietus for death. The core Latin sense was never mere silence; it was absence of motion, labour, and war — civic and bodily peace.

  • Latin to French: Late Latin quietare to calm, to set at rest passed into Old French quieter, coier with the same sense. Anglo-Norman carried it into Middle English as quieten, quieten, quite by the 14th century, first as adjective and verb for stillness and peace.
  • First English disquiet (16th century): Disquiet appears as a verb around the 1520s–1560s in Reformation prose: to disquiet the church, to disquiet the conscience, to be disquieted in spirit. Tyndale, Cranmer, and the King James translators use disquieted for the Psalmist's troubled soul: Why art thou cast down, O my soul, and why art thou disquieted within me? The noun follows swiftly, naming both the feeling and the fact of disturbance.
  • Shakespeare and the soul: Shakespeare uses disquiet for mental and political disturbance alike — a disquieted mind, disquieted kingdoms. The word already carries its modern double sense: inward anxiety and outward unrest.

Phonetically the word is conservative: quiet retains Latin qui- as /kwaɪ/, and the prefix dis- remains fully stressed or half-stressed depending on noun-verb stress shift. As a noun, older RP favoured /ˈdɪskwaɪət/ with initial stress; as a verb, /dɪsˈkwaɪət/ with final stress. Modern usage increasingly levels both toward /dɪsˈkwaɪət/, the form given on this card. The spelling never wavered: disquiet has been stable since Caxton, unlike disquyet, dysquyet variants that died by 1700.

Semantically the noun has drifted from strong disturbance toward mild, sustained, respectable worry. In the 16th–17th centuries to disquiet could mean to stir rebellion or torment grievously. By the 18th century, politeness culture softened it: disquiet became the genteel word for concern — what a gentleman felt rather than what a mob did. De Tocqueville's 19th-century use, noting with disquiet the divorce of ideas from words, exemplifies this essayistic register: rational, humane, alarmed but not alarmed into action. Twentieth-century journalism revived the collective sense — industrial disquiet, market disquiet — while psychology largely ceded the field to anxiety, leaving disquiet to literature, editorials, and history.

Today disquiet survives as a formal marker of thoughtful unease. Its adjective disquieting is more frequent than the base noun, applied to anything from statistics to silences. The verb persists in to be disquieted by, a favourite of academic prose. The word's durability lies in its precision: it names neither terror nor trivial worry, but the exact state of calm spoiled by knowledge — quiet, undone.
