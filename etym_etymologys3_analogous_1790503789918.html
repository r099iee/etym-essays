Analogous descends from one of the busiest Greek nouns ever made: logos, reckoning, ratio, word, speech, reason — itself from the verb legein, to gather, collect, pick out, then to count and to say, from PIE *leg- (to collect), the root behind Latin legere (to gather, read, elect), lecture, legend, election, collect. Greek mathematicians gave logos its quantitative edge: a logos is the reckoning of one quantity against another, a ratio. With prefix ana- (up, up along, according to), they formed analogos: up to reckoning, proportionate, as 2 is to 4 so 3 is to 6. Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE) uses analogon for continued proportion; Aristotle extends it to grammar (regular inflections are analogous) and to poetry (metaphor works by analogia); the Stoics make analogia a road to knowledge of the unknown from the known.

Latin borrowed the adjective with minimal change as analogus (proportionate) and the noun as analogia. Varro's De lingua Latina stages the great grammatical war between analogists (language is regular, follow proportion) and anomalists (language is irregular, follow usage) — a debate English schoolrooms still rehearse whenever helped ousts holp. Quintilian teaches orators to argue ex analogia, from parallel cases. Late Latin hands the pair to Boethius and the schoolmen, who make analogia the logic of theology: words said of God and creatures are neither univocal (same sense) nor equivocal (different sense) but analogous — proportionate as copy to original. Phonologically the Greek diphthong is flattened, the g hardened before o, and the ending Latinised; English later restores a Greek flavour in spelling with -ous.

  • Middle English analogy and early modern analogous (1300s-1600s): Chaucer and his contemporaries borrow analogie via French for proportion and correspondence; the adjective analogous follows around 1600–1640 (first in theological and grammatical prose), formed with productive -ous on the Latin stem analog-. Stress settles on the second syllable /əˈnæləɡəs/, the a- reducing to schwa, the g staying hard. Early uses are mathematical and logical: analogous terms, analogous proportions, analogous reasoning.
  • Enlightenment instrument (1700s): Hume, Reid, Paley, and the natural theologians make analogy the bridge between human contrivance and divine design — the watch and the world are analogous — while critics warn that analogy is not identity. Law absorbs the method as reasoning from analogous precedents; medicine as diagnosis from analogous symptoms. The word's cautionary formality is fixed in this era of method: to say analogous is to claim warrant without claiming sameness.
  • Biology's sharp split (1840s-1860s): Richard Owen distinguishes analogue (same function) from homologue (same structure under every variety of form and function); Darwin in the Origin adopts the distinction to argue convergence: sharks, ichthyosaurs, and dolphins acquire analogous streamlining from different ancestors. Every biology textbook since drills the pair with wings and eyes: analogous is doing alike, homologous is being kin. Linguistics mirrors the move with analogical change — irregulars regularised after dominant patterns — analysed by the Neogrammarians as psychology, not sound law.
  • Analog versus analogous (1900s-present): electronics revives the clipped noun-adjective analog (continuous, non-digital: analog signal, analog watch) from the same Greek root via analogy as mechanism, while analogous keeps the older logical sense. The siblings rarely meet: engineers contrast analog with digital, humanists contrast analogous with identical or homologous. The source sentence's analogous way belongs firmly to the older, inferential family — knowledge transferred by lived proportion from signing hands to speaking hands.

So the signer who knows gesture in an analogous way stands at the end of a road paved by Euclid, Aristotle, Varro, Aquinas, Hume, Owen, and Darwin: gathering (legein), reckoning (logos), and reading upward (ana-) from the known proportion to the unknown one. Hands differ, ratios match, understanding crosses.
