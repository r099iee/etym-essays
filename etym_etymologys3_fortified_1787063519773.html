Part I — FORTIS: height becomes strength

The backbone of fortified is Latin fortis "strong, robust, brave." The word's oldest layer is an Indo-European root *bhergh-, whose primary sense was spatial — "high, elevated" — the root that surfaces all over Europe in words for hill-forts and raised settlements: English borough and burgh, German Burg, and, farther east, Sanskrit bṛhánt- "high, great." The semantic bridge from "high" to "strong" is ancient and universal: a hilltop is the strong place, the defensible place; height and might were the same idea to speakers of early Italic, just as "high-ranking" and "the high and mighty" still blur them today. Latin's fortis — from an Italic form related to forctis recorded by Roman grammarians — carried "strong" in every dimension: physical (a strong man, a strong wall), moral (a brave soldier), and institutional (a strong state).

Part II — FICARE: making

Latin also supplied the second half of the verb. -ficāre is the combining form of facere "to do, make" — itself from the Indo-European root *dʰeh₁- "to put, place, do," the same root as English do, Greek tithēmi "I place," and Sanskrit dadhāti "he puts." In compounds, facere became a verbalising machine: magnificare "to make great," sacrificare "to make holy," amplificare "to enlarge" — and, with fortis, fortificāre, "to make strong." The compound is late: it belongs to the later Latin of the Empire and of the Church (attested in Christian Latin writers of about the 4th century, notably in psalm- and prayer-language such as "fortify my heart"), and it carried the plain meaning of strengthening body, mind, or walls.

Part III — Through French into English

From Late Latin, fortificāre passed into Old French as fortefier / fortifier (attested from about the 11th century), and after the Norman Conquest it entered Middle English as fortifien in the 14th century. In English the word at once split into two lives:

  • Military: to surround or strengthen a place with defensive works — castles, walls, ramparts — the sense that at once became the word's flagship. Fourteenth- and fifteenth-century chronicles and romances describe towns being "fortified" against siege, and the noun fortification named both the act and the works themselves.
  • Moral / figurative: from the very beginning the verb also meant "to strengthen (the heart, the spirit, the argument)," on the obvious bridge from strengthening stone to strengthening resolve. "Fortified by grace," "fortified in his purpose" kept this sense alive through the centuries.

Part IV — The modern widening

From these two seeds the modern range of senses grew by steady metaphorical and technical extension:

  • Structural: reinforced concrete, fortified bridges and buildings — physical strength beyond the purely military.
  • Moral and mental: "fortified by hope," "a fortifying breakfast" — the figurative sense is now the everyday one in general prose.
  • Argumentative: a case "fortified" by evidence (19th–20th centuries).
  • Nutritional (20th century): the great modern innovation — with public-health campaigns against deficiency diseases (beriberi, rickets, scurvy, anaemia), governments and manufacturers began adding vitamins and minerals to staples in the 1920s–1940s: fortified flour, fortified milk, fortified cereals. In food science "fortification" acquired a technical definition (adding nutrients beyond original levels, as against "enrichment," which restores them).
  • Fortified wines: wines strengthened by added spirit — port, sherry, Madeira, Marsala — a trade category so old and fixed that "fortified wine" is now simply a kind of drink.
  • Financial and digital (late 20th–21st c.): "fortified balance sheets," "fortified reserves," "fortified networks" — the word keeps absorbing every new field that values resistance to shock.

The past-participle form fortified has ridden all these waves at once. In the single sentence of this card's source — a dragon who boasts that his armour is "like tenfold shields" while the hobbit needles him about being "so fortified" — the word spans fourteen hundred years: *bhergh- height, Latin fortis strength, Late Latin fortificāre, Old French fortifier, English walls and breakfast cereal, all folded into one flexible, fully modern word.
