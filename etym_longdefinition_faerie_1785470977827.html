FAERIE (also spelled FAERY, FAYRY; now the deliberately archaic literary form) denotes the enchanted realm, otherworld, or collective people of supernatural beings — and, secondarily, the state or condition of enchantment itself. In the passage, "the secret realm of faerie, where courtly sprites held midnight revels under silver boughs," the word names not a single creature but the enchanted, perilous otherworld itself. The archaic spelling deliberately removes the word from the modern nursery and places it in the tradition of high fantasy and medieval romance — the tradition of Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene and of J.R.R. Tolkien — in which the fairies' realm is a place of beauty, danger, illusion, and ancient nature magic rather than of tinsel-winged pets.

Polysemy and the full range of senses.
  • The realm / otherworld (primary): the land where the fairy folk dwell, conceived as a parallel, often perilous world — elfland, fairyland in its older sense. "The Queen of Faerie," "into Faerie," "the realm of faerie."
  • Collective people: the fairy folk as a body or race — "all the faerie" meaning the elves and sprites collectively. In Middle English the word functioned as a collective noun before it ever named a singular creature.
  • Enchantment / sorcery (the oldest sense): the condition of being enchanted, the supernatural power or illusion exercised by the fairy folk. Old French faerie originally meant "enchantment, sorcery" — "to be under faerie."
  • An individual fairy (obsolete-rare): the word could occasionally name a single supernatural being; it is the ancestor of the modern "fairy."
  • Archaic literary spelling of "fairy" (modern sense): as an adjective or noun in revived usage, "faerie" simply means "of or belonging to fairies" — "faerie queen," "faerie folk," "faerie gold."
  • Modern fantasy-tradition sense: in post-Tolkien fantasy, "faerie" names the dangerous, alien, amoral otherworld of Celtic-derived folklore — the Seelie/Unseelie world — as opposed to the sanitized Victorian fairy. It often carries connotations of stolen time (a night in faerie = a century on earth) and of glamour, the illusion that captivates mortals.

Semantic bridges. The historical bridge runs from "fate" to "magic" to "place." The word descends from Latin fatum "that which is spoken (by the gods), destiny"; in Vulgar Latin the plural fata became a singular goddess of destiny, and in Old French the fee was the woman of fate turned enchantress. Her power was faerie — enchantment; the domain where she worked that power became faerie — the realm. English preserved the environmental sense long after the modern word "fairy" narrowed to a single winged creature, and the -ie spelling, fixed by Spenser in 1590, became the badge of the older, grander meaning. Register: archaic, poetic, and high-fantasy; the word signals adult, literary, and mythic seriousness, in deliberate contrast to the nursery "fairy." Collocations: "the Faerie Queene," "faerie folk," "faerie realm," "Queen of Faerie," "faerie tale," "the hills of faerie," "faerie gold." The plural-singular structure of the word is delicate: it is a place, a people, and a power all at once, and the best usage lets all three coexist.
