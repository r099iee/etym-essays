Three morphemes compose the word: un-, rival, and -ed. Each is traced. Chain 1 — the core “rival”: - PIE *h₃rei- “to flow, to stream” (also reconstructed *h₃rey-; the root of words for watercourses) → Latin rīvus “a stream, brook, watercourse” (Classical Latin) → Latin rīvālis “one who uses or shares the same stream; a neighbour who draws from the same brook; hence a competitor, especially a suitor in love” (Classical Latin; from rīvus + the suffix -ālis) → Old French rivale / Middle French rival “competitor” → English rival (noun, 16th century, first recorded c. 1577) “a competitor, opponent, one striving for the same object” → English rival (verb, 17th century) “to be in competition with, to equal or surpass” → rivalled (past participle / adjective, 17th century) “met by an equal in competition; matched” → un- + rivalled = unrivalled (adjective, 17th century, first recorded 1625) “having no rival or equal” → Modern English UNRIVALLED. Chain 2 — the negative prefix un-: - PIE *ne “not” (the basic Indo-European negative particle) → zero-grade form *n̥- → Proto-Germanic *un- → Old English un- (prefix, attested from the earliest records) → Middle English un- → English un-, used to negate adjectives and participles, as in unrivalled. Chain 3 — the adjectival/participial suffix -ed: - PIE *-tó- (verbal adjective suffix) → Proto-Germanic *-daz / *-þaz → Old English -ede / -od → Middle English -ed → English -ed, converting the verb rival into the participial adjective rivalled and hence unrivalled. Note on debate: the transfer of rīvālis from “sharing a stream” to “competitor in love” is classical and well attested (the Roman jurists describe rivals as neighbours sharing a watercourse, and the erotic sense develops in Roman poetry); there is no dispute about the main chain. The spelling unrivalled (with double l, the British convention before vowel-initial suffixes) is standard in British English, while American English prefers unrivaled; both derive from the same form. The exact laryngeal grade of PIE *h₃rei- “to flow” is a matter of scholarly convention rather than genuine controversy.
