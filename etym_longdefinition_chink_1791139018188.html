chink in this context is a noun meaning a narrow opening, slit, crack or fissure in a wall, door, shutter or other barrier through which light, air, sound or sight can pass — crevice, cranny, cleft, slit. Dickens — “pouring in at every chink and keyhole” — uses it for the tiny gaps around windows and doors that cannot keep London fog out. The word also exists as a separate homonym meaning a sharp metallic ringing sound (clink), but that is not the sense here.

Detailed senses of chink n.1 = slit (the relevant homonym):

  • Narrow crack in a barrier: “chink in the wall, chink in the floorboards, chink between curtains.” Implies length much greater than width, barely open yet permeable. Smaller than a gap, longer than a hole.
  • Slit admitting light / air / view: “a chink of light under the door, a chink of daylight.” Focuses on what comes through, not the crack itself. Dickens inverts it: what pours in is fog.
  • Figurative vulnerable spot: “chink in the armour, chink in his argument, chink in their defences.” A tiny flaw that undoes strength. This is the most frequent modern use in news and debate.
  • Log-building term (American): chinking (noun/verb) = mortar or clay daubed between logs to fill chinks. “Re-chink the cabin.” Preserves the building-craft origin.
  • Verb to chink (from same root): to fill cracks (“chink a wall”), or to peer through (“chink through blinds” – rare). Do not confuse with to chink = to clink (to make coins ring).

The other homonym for completeness — chink n.2 / v.2 = sound:

  • Sharp ringing: “chink of coins, chink of glasses, chink of keys.” Near-synonym of clink, jingle, tinkle, slightly thinner and higher. As verb: “glasses chinked.” Entirely imitative, unrelated in origin to the slit.

Usage profile for the slit sense:

  • Collocates: every chink, narrow chink, tiny chink, chink in the armour, chink of light, through the chink, fill/seal/stop the chinks.
  • Grammar: countable (“a chink, many chinks”). Often paired: “chinks and crannies, chinks and keyholes.”
  • Register: neutral-literary, slightly bookish. Everyday speech often prefers crack, gap, slit, reserving chink for light-effects and the armour idiom.
  • Connotation: secrecy, leakiness, vulnerability, hope. A chink lets the outside in (cold, spies) or the inside out (light, truth).
  • Distinctions: a crack implies breakage; a chink can be intentional (shutters). A cranny is a cosy nook; a chink is a passage. A crevice is in rock; a chink is in built work.

Why one form holds two meanings is accident: Germanic cinu (split) converged in sound with imitative chink! (ring). Context disambiguates — fog pours through slits, coins produce rings. Mastering chink means keeping both apart while hearing why poets love their collision: a wall’s slit and a coin’s ring are both thin, bright, narrow things.
