- 1640s - [Martial & Fencing Term] - Borrowed from French dueling manuals into English, strictly denoting the weak, flexible upper third of a sword blade opposite the forte.
- 1670s–1700s - [Restoration Satire & Social Comedy] - Metaphorically applied in English drama (Dryden, Congreve) and moral essays to denote minor, laughable defects in character and vanity.
- 1800s - [Victorian Domestic Novel & Gentle Irony] - Celebrated in 19th-century literature (Austen, Thackeray, Eliot) as the defining feature of realistic human characterization, distinguishing benign quirks from true vices.
- 1900s–Present - [General Literary & Conversational Register] - Preserved in educated modern English for affectionate descriptions of personal eccentricities, endearing habits, and subtle vulnerabilities.
