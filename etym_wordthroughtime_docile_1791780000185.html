- Late 15th to 16th c. – Teachable, apt to learn, intellectually receptive - Used in early English pedagogical and humanist texts in direct imitation of Latin *docilis*; described bright, eager pupils capable of absorbing instruction.
- 17th c. – Easily trained, manageable, amenable to discipline - Expanded from human schooling to animal training (hunting dogs, draft horses); began emphasizing behavioural tractability alongside intellectual aptitude.
- 18th to 19th c. – Gentle, quiet, submissive, compliant - Increasingly applied to domestic servants, factory workforces, and subject populations; praised by authorities for lack of resistance or rebellion.
- 20th c. to Present – Passively obedient, sheep-like, subdued - Acquired an ambivalent or distinctly critical edge in political philosophy and sociology (e.g., Foucault's "docile bodies"), often connoting a lamentable absence of critical thought, independence, or spirited rebellion.
