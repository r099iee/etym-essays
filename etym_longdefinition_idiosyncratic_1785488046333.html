In the opening sentence of this chapter, “idiosyncratic” stands at one pole of a satirical spectrum — “the comically idiosyncratic” opposed to “the laboriously obvious” — and it is the rare word that carries a whole judgement inside its own syllables. The object being described is scholarly and critical ardour, the flood of books about English metre; the author’s tone is wry and amused, for the word is applied to prose that is not merely distinctive but comically distinctive, so saturated with one critic’s private tastes, verbal tics, and personal methods that it becomes a parody of itself. Idiosyncratic here does not mean merely “unusual” or “odd”; it names a systematic individuality — a way of writing so completely the fingerprint of one person that a reader could identify the author from a single page. The comedy lies in the collision between the scholar’s solemn pretensions to universality and the utterly personal stamp the writing actually bears. The word is a modern English adjective built on the older noun idiosyncrasy, and its senses have widened through two centuries of medical and literary use:
  • Core sense — peculiar to one person: a habit, trait, mannerism, or style characteristic of a particular individual (“an idiosyncratic way of marking the scansion”, “idiosyncratic spelling”). Descriptive, comparative, and mildly clinical.
  • Medical sense — an unusual bodily reaction: in medicine, idiosyncrasy (and idiosyncratic) names a peculiar, individual response to a drug, food, or stimulus — an allergy-like reaction that others do not share (“an idiosyncratic drug reaction”). This is the oldest modern sense, from the 17th-century doctrine of temperaments.
  • Stilistic sense — an author’s or artist’s signature: a distinctive, recognizable stamp of style, method, or technique (“every major poet develops an idiosyncratic technique”); often admiring, sometimes wry.
  • Humorous or satirical sense — comic individuality: the passage’s “comically idiosyncratic” — individuality so extreme it becomes a parody of itself; the wry, amused edge of the word in criticism.
  • Psychologising sense — a personal quirk: an odd but harmless habit or preference that marks one person out; lighter and more affectionate than the clinical sense.
The historical bridge is the Greek compound: idio- (from ídios, “one’s own, private”) + syncrasia (a blending, from sýn, “with, together”, and krâsis, “a mixing”), literally “a private blending”. In Greek medicine the word idiosynkrasía named a person’s own mixture of the four humours — the bodily blend that made each person unique and susceptible to particular diseases; the healthy balance was eukrasía, the poisoned imbalance dyskrasía. From this medical doctrine English borrowed idiosyncrasy in the early 17th century as a clinical term, widened it in the 18th century to personal character and temperament, and in the 19th century settled it as a neutral description of the individual quirk. The adjective idiosyncratic followed, and today it labels any personal quirk or distinctive stylistic stamp — comparative and descriptive rather than judgemental, and capable of a wry, amused tone when the quirk is comically overdone, exactly as in this chapter’s spectrum of scholarly and critical ardour.
