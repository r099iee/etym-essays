INDISPOSED is an adjective meaning, first, "slightly unwell or sick," and, second, "unwilling, averse, or not inclined to do something." The word is the past participle of the rare verb to indispose, "to make unfit or unwilling," which never prospered; it is the adjective that has carried the whole history of the word. Indisposed is a formal, polite word, and its two meanings work together in a single social manoeuvre: the person who is indisposed is usually declining something, and "being slightly unwell" is the polite face of "not being inclined." The word is a masterpiece of euphemism, and that is why it has survived for five centuries.

1. The medical sense — slightly unwell. To be indisposed is to be not entirely well: mildly ill, unwell, ailing, out of sorts, or too unwell to do one's usual business, though not dangerously or gravely ill. The word deliberately denotes minor, temporary, vague indisposition rather than serious illness. In this sense it is almost always used of a person's inability to act or attend: the ambassador is indisposed and cannot receive visitors; the performer is indisposed and cannot go on; the judge is indisposed and the court rises; the lady is indisposed and keeps to her room. The card's example ("The ambassador was INDISPOSED and could not attend") uses exactly this sense. The mildness of the word is its whole point — it says "not well" without specifying symptoms, severity, or cause.

2. The volitional sense — unwilling, averse, disinclined. Indisposed also means "not disposed, not inclined, unwilling, or averse," and it can take an infinitive: "he felt indisposed to join the argument," "she was indisposed to comply," "the minister was indisposed to listen." Here the word is the negative of disposed ("inclined, willing, ready"), and it shades from genuine reluctance to simple lack of inclination. The card's example ("He felt INDISPOSED to join the argument") uses this sense. This sense is structurally the plainest — it is the simple negation of the disposition word — and it survives chiefly in formal or literary use.

3. The social-euphemistic sense — the polite refusal. The most characteristically English use of indisposed is as a social and diplomatic euphemism. To say that "Lord X is indisposed" is to decline an invitation, an audience, an appearance, or a meeting without giving offence, because the reason offered is both respectable and conveniently vague. The word lets a host, a secretary, or a servant announce an absence while leaving it unclear whether the person is genuinely ill, unwilling, or merely unwilling to give the true reason. In this role indisposed is a shield: it converts "I do not want to" into "I am not able to," and it converts private illness into a formula that does not require embarrassing detail. The reading context of the card ("But your handmaiden said that you were INDISPOSED") is a perfect example: the handmaiden uses the polite formula to protect her lady's privacy.

4. Etymology and the body-disposition metaphor. The deep history of the word is a bodily one. Indisposed is built on Latin indispositus "disordered, not properly arranged," from in- "not" and dispositus "set in order," the past participle of disponere "to set in order, arrange." In medieval medicine the body was a system of humours that had to be properly disposed — set in order; an indisposed body was one whose humours had fallen into disorder, producing mild illness or indisposition. The same root-idea of "being arranged, set, or inclined" then produced the mental sense: to be "disposed" is to be set or inclined toward something, and to be "indisposed" is to be not so set, hence unwilling. The physical and the mental senses are thus two faces of the same Latin metaphor of ordering and inclination.

5. Register and distribution. Indisposed is formal and slightly dated in the medical sense; in casual speech people say unwell, ill, sick, or under the weather. It survives strongly in official notices, medical and diplomatic language, polite correspondence, and euphemistic social formulas. It is a predicate adjective — one says "she is indisposed," not "an indisposed woman," though attributive use is possible in formal prose ("the indisposed guest"). The word's polite vagueness has kept it alive for five hundred years, because English-speaking society has always needed a respectable way to say no.

Summary of senses.

  • Slightly unwell; mildly ill; too unwell to attend or act (the medical sense).
  • Unwilling, averse, or not inclined, with or without to + infinitive (the volitional sense).
  • A polite, euphemistic formula for declining invitations or attendance without giving the true reason (the social sense).
  • A formal predicate adjective, built on the Latin metaphor of bodily humours being "set in order" (indispositus).
