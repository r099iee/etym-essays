Imitative root *ker- / *ger- "to cry hoarsely" (sound-symbolic harsh-call family; reconstructed impressionistically across Indo-European — the same expressive type that names the crane and the curlew; not a regular inherited root, but the acoustic template for many hoarse-call words) → (Germanic imitative group) Old English cracian (v.) "to make a sharp noise" → Middle English craken → Modern English crack (a parallel branch of the same harsh-sound family) → Middle English crouken (v.) "to make a low, hoarse sound", used of the crow, raven, and crane (early 14th century) → Modern English croak (v.) "to make a low, hoarse, guttural sound", of frogs from c. 1400; (figurative) "to forebode evil, complain, grumble" from the mid-15th century (from the raven as bird of ill omen); (n.) "a low, hoarse, guttural sound" (1560, back-formation); (slang) "to die" (1812, from the death-rattle) → present participle croaking "making a harsh, low, hoarse sound in the throat" → Modern English CROAKING Note on debate: the OED treats croak as an imitative formation with no secure inherited Indo-European pedigree; the *ker-/*ger- "harsh-cry" grouping is an impressionistic family of parallel, independently coined sound-symbolic words rather than a single attested descent. Latin crocīre "to croak" (of a raven) and Sanskrit krauñcaḥ "the curlew, the harsh caller" (whence Hindi kraunch, a tatsama) are kindred parallel formations of the same acoustic type, not proof of a shared inherited root. No attested form directly links Old English cracian to Middle English crouken; the family is connected by imitative type, not by recorded sound-change.
