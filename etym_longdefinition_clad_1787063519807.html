Core meaning. Clad is the past tense and past participle of the verb clothe — an alternative form alongside the regular clothed — and, more importantly, a participial adjective meaning "dressed", "wearing", or "covered with a specified garment or material". When someone is clad, they are fully covered by what is named or implied: clad in a coat of rings, clad in furs, clad in black. By extension the word covers anything fitted with an outer covering layer: a building clad in stone, a ship clad in iron, a mountain clad in snow.

Primary sense — dressed, wearing. In its oldest and still most common use, clad describes a person (or personified thing) wearing clothing or armour. Key properties:

  • Construction: it typically appears in the pattern clad in + garment/material, or as a compound adjective (armour-clad, fur-clad, leather-clad), or with a colour (clad in white).
  • Completeness: unlike dressed, which may suggest ordinary everyday clothing, clad stresses full, often striking or emblematic covering — robes, mail, armour, skins.
  • Register: literary, formal, or archaic-flavoured; it is rare in casual conversation, where dressed, wearing, or in are preferred.

Secondary sense — covered or coated with a material. A modern, technical extension of the clothing metaphor: anything given a protective or decorative outer layer is clad.

  • Architecture and engineering: a building or wall clad in stone, timber, glass, or metal panels — cladding is the industry term for the outer skin of a structure.
  • Metallurgy: a metal clad with another (e.g. copper-clad steel) has one metal bonded over another, as armour plated a warship in the era of the ironclad.
  • Fibre optics: the cladding of an optical fibre is the lower-refractive-index layer that confines light in the core.
  • Nature and landscape (poetic): hills clad in mist, forests clad in autumn colour — natural covering described with the clothing image.

Tertiary senses and extensions.

  • Figurative protection: a place or idea "clothed" in secrecy or majesty can be clad in those abstractions (archaic-literate: a promise clad in mystery).
  • Compound formation: clad is extremely productive in compounds — steel-clad, iron-clad (whence the figurative adjective ironclad "unbreakable", of a promise or guarantee), snow-clad, armour-clad, skin-clad, white-clad.
  • Feminine/literary uses: in ballads and fantasy prose, clad names heroic or ceremonial dress (a king clad in royal robes), which is exactly its force in the source sentence about Thorin.

Grammar and behaviour. Clad is used as (1) the past tense/past participle of clothe in the sense "dressed" (she clad herself in white — now archaic as a finite verb); (2) a predicative adjective (he was clad in armour); (3) an attributive adjective (a clad figure — rare; normally in compounds); and (4) the head of reduced clauses (clad in a silver coat, Thorin looked royal). It is morphologically an old strong participle and does not take the regular -ed ending.

Register and connotation. Clad is distinctly formal, poetic, or archaic in tone when applied to people. It is neutral and technical in construction and materials (cladding, ironclad). The word always implies completeness of covering and often carries a visual, almost ceremonial vividness — a writer chooses clad over dressed to make the covering itself conspicuous.

Semantic history in brief. The noun cloth and verb clothe are native English words (Old English clāþ, clāþian), and clad is an early past participle whose strong form was reinforced by the Old Norse participle klæddr "clothed". Over time the two forms split in register: clothed remained the ordinary participle, while clad became the poetic/literary form and, from the 19th century, the technical word for outer coverings.
