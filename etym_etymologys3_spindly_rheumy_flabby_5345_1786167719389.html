Part I: The Etymological Evolution of SPINDLY

The history of spindly is rooted in the ancient world of hand-spinning wool. Derived from the name of the central wooden rod around which thread was wound, it shows how pre-industrial technology provided Western culture with its primary metaphor for physical slenderness and structural fragility — a word that moved from the spinning-wheel to the skeleton, and from the skeleton to the seed-stem.

1. Prehistoric Roots: PIE *(s)pen-

Linguists trace spindle and spindly to the Proto-Indo-European root *(s)pen-, which signified "to draw, stretch, or spin." This ancient root spread across the major Indo-European language families. In Germanic it produced Proto-Germanic *spinnaną ("to draw out fibre and twist into thread"), giving Old English spinnan and modern English spin; in Latin it produced pendēre ("to hang, to be drawn out, to be suspended"), which entered English as pendant, pendulum, depend, append, and suspend. The deep semantic core is extension: the drawing-out of thread from a mass of fibre, and by extension anything stretched thin along its length. The sound correspondence between Germanic sp- and Latin p- is a regular reflex of the same PIE cluster, one of the well-understood laws of comparative grammar.

2. The Hand-Spindle in History and Language

Before the spinning wheel, spinning was done with a drop spindle — a thin, tapering wooden rod, weighted at one end, on which the newly spun thread was wound. The spindle was drawn out long and thin so that thread could wrap tightly around its shaft, and for millennia it defined the standard of slenderness in Western technology. Its name passed through Old English spinel and Middle English spindel into modern spindle. Because the object was proverbially thin, English built the metaphor of "spindle-shanks" for absurdly skinny legs, and eventually, in the 17th century, attached the adjectival suffix -y to create spindly: "long and thin like a spindle."

3. The Seventeenth-Century Coining and Its First Applications

When spindly was coined in the 1600s, it was applied first to human limbs — legs and arms stretched out beyond strength, too weak for the body they carried. The word carried a double judgment: it named thinness, but also mechanical fragility, the sense that the limb might give way under its own length. This is the crucial distinction from a merely descriptive word like slim: spindly was never a compliment.

4. The Eighteenth and Nineteenth Centuries: The Word Spreads

In the 18th and 19th centuries spindly spread from bodies to the vegetable and mechanical worlds. Horticulturists applied it to etiolated seedlings — plants that, starved of light, grow unnaturally pale, tall, and weak, their stems stretched too thin to stand. Carpenters and householders applied it to furniture legs and scaffolding that looked as though they might buckle. By the end of the 19th century the word's range was fixed: anything long, thin, and structurally unsound, whether flesh, fibre, or furniture.

5. Modern Usage and Figurative Extension

Today spindly remains the standard word for disproportionate, fragile thinness. It is used of the limbs of the underfed and the overgrown, of seedlings and saplings, of chair legs and crane booms. In figurative use it reaches into argument and institution: a "spindly alibi" or a "spindly business model" is one stretched too thin to support its own weight. In Vance's portrait — "a great head on a small spindly body" — the word carries its full force: the body is not merely thin but structurally incapable of supporting its burden, a mechanical prophecy of collapse folded into a single adjective.

Part II: The Etymological Evolution of RHEUMY

The history of rheumy is the history of a medical theory fossilized inside an adjective. Its Greek ancestor means simply "a flow," and the word preserves — for readers who know the etymology — the ancient doctrine that the body's fluids stream like rivers and that illness is a fluxion, a leak, a discharge.

1. Prehistoric Roots: PIE *sreu-

The deepest root is *sreu-, "to flow, to stream," one of the most widespread roots of Indo-European. Its reflexes include Sanskrit srávati ("it flows"), Latin fluere ("to flow," whence English fluid, fluent, influence), Greek rhein ("to flow"), and Proto-Germanic *straumaz (English stream). The root is water itself — rivers, currents, and the streaming of liquids — and every word built on it carries an image of continuous, directional movement.

2. Greek rheuma: The Fluxion Doctrine

In Ancient Greek, rheuma (from rhein, "to flow") meant "a flow, a stream, a current," and in the medical system of the Hippocratic and Galenic schools it took on a technical sense: a fluxion, the flow of a humour from one part of the body to another. Disease, in this system, was a disturbance of flow — a catarrh was a rheuma streaming from the head, and joint pain (rheumatismos) was a fluxion settling in the limbs. The theory treated the body as a hydraulic system of humours, and its vocabulary — rheuma, fluxion, catarrh — shaped medical language in Europe for two millennia.

3. Latin and Old French: rheuma and reume

Greek medicine passed rheuma to Latin, which used it in the same technical senses and transmitted it to the vernaculars. Old French turned it into reume or rheume, meaning a cold, a catarrh, a watery discharge, and Middle English borrowed the word as reume / rheum in the 14th century. In medieval and early modern English medicine, "rheum" named any thin watery discharge — of the eyes, nose, or lungs — and the eyes' moisture was read as a visible symptom of the body's humoral economy.

4. The Coining of rheumy and Its Literary Career

The adjective rheumy was formed from rheum + -y in the 17th century, meaning "full of rheum, discharging moisture, watery." In the 1600s and 1700s it was still a quasi-medical word. But as humoral theory died out in the 19th century, rheumy survived — strikingly — as a purely descriptive, pitying adjective. It became the standard literary word for the weeping, filmy, moisture-laden eyes of age and sickness: the eyes of the very old, the very ill, or the very feeble, which seem to leak their own substance.

5. Modern Usage and Vance's Portrait

Today rheumy is essentially a literary and poetic word. It describes the eye that waters without tears — the chronic, painless moisture of old age — and by extension anything raw, damp, and weeping with feebleness. In Vance's portrait — "weak rheumy eyes" — the word does exactly the work of two thousand years of medicine compressed into one syllable of pity: the eyes leak, the body is failing, and the reader registers both the physical discharge and the ancient theory of the body's flows that once explained it. The word is thus a living fossil of Greek humoral medicine.

Part III: The Etymological Evolution of FLABBY

Flabby is a word of pure sound-symbolism: it has no PIE ancestor, no classical pedigree, and no learned derivation. It is built on the same imitative base as flap and flop, and its history is the history of English's native genius for making meaning from the mere shape of a sound — the loose, wet, swinging motion the word imitates.

1. The Imitative Base *fl-

English possesses a rich family of expressive words built on the initial cluster fl-, which imitates a particular kind of loose, swinging, spreading motion: flap, flop, flip, flounce, flounder, flutter, fly, flail, flab. The semantic core of the cluster is movement that is loose, uncontrolled, and slightly comic — the slapping of a loose sail, the flopping of a fish, the flapping of an unfastened strap. This is an expressive or sound-symbolic stratum of the vocabulary, deliberately different from the learned Latin layer, and its words resist ordinary historical derivation: they are formed by the ear and the mouth, not by inheritance.

2. Middle English flappe and the Family of Flapping

The immediate textual ancestor of the family is Middle English flappe, "a blow, a slap," with the verb flappen, "to strike, to beat, to flap." The sense of a loose striking or slapping motion is present from the beginning — the blow of something broad and soft, not the sharp cut of an edged weapon. From this base English developed flap (both the action and the loose hanging piece that flaps) and, by the late 17th century, the noun flab: "soft, loose flesh," the substance that flops and swings when the body moves.

3. The Coining of flabby in the 1690s

From flab English formed the adjective flabby (first attested around the 1690s) by adding the suffix -y: literally "full of flab," "of the nature of loose flesh." The word's earliest recorded uses are exactly physical — soft, pendulous, toneless flesh that yields under pressure and does not spring back. The imitative base is perfectly legible in the meaning: flabby flesh is flesh that flops.

4. The Figurative Expansion of the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Centuries

Almost immediately the word began its figurative career. By the 18th-19th centuries flabby was applied to anything soft, lax, and lacking vigour or structure: a flabby argument, flabby prose, a flabby will, a flabby conscience, flabby markets. The physical image — tissue without tone, substance without resilience — transferred cleanly to the moral and intellectual realms, so that the word became a standard term of criticism for weakness of every kind. This is the classic pattern of English sensory words: the body provides the image, and the mind supplies the meanings.

5. Modern Usage and the Confusion with Flaccid

In modern English flabby remains primarily physical — the tactile opposite of firm, taut, muscular — with the figurative senses of slackness persisting alongside. It is frequently confused in learned writing with flaccid, but the two are etymologically unrelated: flaccid is a 17th-century borrowing from Latin flaccidus (from flaccus, "flabby, hanging down"), while flabby is the native imitative formation. In Vance's portrait — "a flabby button of a nose" — the word supplies the softest, most formless note in the catalogue: a nose with no structure at all, yielding like dough, the sound-symbolic fl- itself enacting the loose, unmuscular collapse it names.
