PEJORATIVE: Expressing or conveying disapproval, contempt, or belittlement; disparaging in force, depreciatory, or uncomplimentary — said especially of words, terms, senses, affixes, or remarks that lower the status or value of what they describe. The adjective is also used as a noun: a pejorative is a word, expression, or sense whose function is to put something or someone down. The core idea is downward evaluation: the pejorative term does not merely describe its object, it debases it, shrinks it, or marks it as unworthy of respect. Nuance in this context. In the card's sentence — "The critic's review was filled with __________ remarks aimed at trivializing the novel's themes" — the adjective does double work. It describes the critic's language as disparaging, but the fuller target is revealed by the phrase "aimed at trivializing the novel's themes": the pejorative remarks do not merely insult — they work to shrink the value of the book, to make its themes look small, cheap, or unworthy of serious attention. The tone is that of hostile criticism dressed in the language of evaluation, and the word "pejorative" names precisely that weaponized disparagement: it is language whose function is to lower the status of its object. This is the word's classic critical-discourse register, and the collocation "filled with pejorative remarks" shows the adjective at its most familiar, applied to a person's words rather than to a single lexical item. Grammatical roles.
  • As an adjective: "a pejorative term," "pejorative remarks," "the word has acquired a pejorative sense" — describing words, senses, affixes, epithets, and remarks that carry a negative evaluative charge.
  • As a noun: "that's a pejorative," "the term is a pejorative" — a word or expression whose function is to disparage; the noun use follows the adjective's arrival in English in the 1880s.
  • In linguistics: "pejorative suffix," "pejorative prefix," "pejoration" (the noun naming the historical process by which a word's meaning drifts toward the worse — e.g., vulgar "common" → "crude", silly "blessed, innocent" → "foolish") — the term is a precise technical instrument of lexicography and historical semantics.
Polysemy and degrees of force.
  • Sense 1 — inherently disparaging (of words and senses): the primary technical sense; a word or one of its senses is pejorative when it carries a built-in negative evaluation — "bureaucrat" in hostile use, "old hag," "tattletale," the suffix -ish in "childish," the prefix mis- in "misadventure." The evaluation is part of the meaning, not supplied by context.
  • Sense 2 — disparaging in use (of remarks and speech): the everyday sense seen in the card's sentence; a remark is pejorative when its function is to belittle, trivialize, or express contempt, regardless of whether the words themselves are insults — the critic's "remarks" are pejorative because they are aimed at lowering the novel's worth.
  • Sense 3 — the neutral label versus the loaded practice: a crucial nuance — the word pejorative is itself neutral. It does not condemn the language it describes; it classifies it. Saying that a term is pejorative is a statement of fact about how the term works, not an insult directed at the person using it. This is what allows lexicographers to write "the word has acquired a pejorative sense" in a dictionary definition without taking sides.
The historical bridge. The word entered English in 1882 as a borrowing from French péjoratif, from Late Latin peiōrātus, "made worse," the past participle of peiōrāre, "to make worse," from Latin peior, "worse," the comparative of malus, "bad." Peior itself is an ancient comparative descended from a PIE root meaning "to go, walk, step": the word for "worse" is literally "stumbling, going badly" — the one who fares worse is the one who trips. The whole family thus encodes a spatial metaphor: to make a thing pejorative is to push it downhill, to make it "worse," to set it beneath its former self. When the adjective was formed in French on the Latin participle, it named not a feeling but a function — the tendency of a form to make worse — and English borrowed the word to describe exactly that tendency in language. Collocations and register. The adjective collocates with "term," "word," "sense," "connotation," "remark," "use," "meaning," and "label": "a pejorative term," "pejorative connotations," "used in a pejorative sense," "a pejorative label." It is often framed by "strongly," "increasingly," "purely," and "merely": "a strongly pejorative word." The register is formal and intellectual — the word belongs to lexicography, linguistics, literary criticism, and journalism about language — and it carries a slight learned flavor that makes it at home in the critical prose of the card's sentence. Derived forms: pejoration (the process), pejoratively (adverb), and the near-antonym melioration / amelioration (the process by which a word's meaning drifts toward the better, as "knight" from "servant, boy" toward "noble warrior").
