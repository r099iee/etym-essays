- 16th century playful jest - enters English from Latin with the sense of playing upon something, a jesting or mocking touch at a matter; an allusion could be a playful hit at a person.
- 16th-17th century literary turn - the word shifts from jesting to the rhetorical figure of oblique reference: an allusion touches a person, text, or event without naming it, demanding that the reader supply the missing link.
- 18th-19th century social danger - as in the Mill circle, an allusion becomes a socially charged gesture, a verbal nudge at a taboo subject that polite society may forbid even to name; the cost of the indirect mention is measured in friendship and reproof.
- Contemporary use - a neutral, everyday word for any passing indirect reference, and still a standard term of literary criticism for echo and intertextuality.
