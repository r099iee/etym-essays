- Roman Antiquity (c. 1st c. BCE–3rd c. CE) — In Classical Latin, papiliō meant "butterfly" or "moth"; Roman legionaries humorously applied the diminutive term to their leather and canvas campaign tents because the twin entrance flaps spread wide and flapped in the wind like butterfly wings — [perception: informal military slang of soldiers on campaign].
- High Middle Ages & Chivalric Era (12th–14th c.) — Inherited into Old French as paveillon and borrowed into Middle English during the Crusades; became the supreme symbol of feudal chivalry, describing the richly embroidered, heraldic silk tents of kings and knights at tournaments (epitomized in Arthurian romance) — [perception: exalted emblem of royal magnificence, knighthood, and martial pageantry].
- Renaissance & 18th Century Classical Architecture — Shifted from temporary canvas tents to permanent ornamental garden architecture: symmetrical pleasure houses, park follies, banqueting halls, and protruding corner wings of baroque palaces (such as the Tuileries) — [perception: aristocratic luxury, pastoral retreat, and architectural refinement].
- Victorian Era & Sporting Culture (19th c.) — Codified in English village and collegiate athletics as the cricket pavilion (where players dressed, stored equipment, and socialized on broad verandas), alongside Florence Nightingale's "pavilion-plan" hospital wards designed for maximum ventilation — [perception: community hub of genteel sportsmanship, civic leisure, and hygienic public architecture].
- Modern & Contemporary Era (20th–21st c.) — Signifies expansive international exhibition halls at World Expos ("the French Pavilion"), grand outdoor celebration marquees for weddings and festivals (echoed in Bilbo's birthday pavilion), and municipal park shelters — [perception: celebratory, expansive, and festive public venue].
