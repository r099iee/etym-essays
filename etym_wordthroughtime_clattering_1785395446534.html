- Old English clatrian - earliest recorded sense: to make rapid, sharp, repeated sounds; a purely imitative word whose very shape mimics struck metal or stone.
- Middle English clateren (13th c.) - 'to strike or knock with repeated sharp sounds', used of kitchens, stables, workshops, and hooves on cobbles.
- 16th c. onwards - settles into the modern form clatter, with the noun in use from the 16th c.; the sound is light, hollow, busy, never musical, never deep, and the register is plain and domestic.
- Modern - because the word was coined to imitate a sound, its meaning cannot drift: it still names rapid, light, hard percussion, and its casual, domestic flavour makes its transfer to falling mountain rock all the more unnerving.
