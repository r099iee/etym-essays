The adverb steadily represents the culmination of an ancient Germanic evolutionary lineage descending directly from one of the most prolific and foundational verbal roots in the Proto-Indo-European language: *steh₂- (or *stā-), meaning "to stand, to stay firm, to remain upright."

This primary root produced a vast network of cognates across the entire Indo-European linguistic family, underpinning concepts of physical uprightness, stability, place, and establishment. Key cognates include:

  • Sanskrit: tiṣṭhati (तिष्ठति, "he stands") and sthāna ("place, standing").
  • Ancient Greek: hístēmi (ἵστημι, "to stand, cause to stand") and stásis ("standing, position").
  • Classical Latin: stāre ("to stand"), stabilitās ("firmness"), and statio ("standing position").
  • Slavic: Old Church Slavonic stojati (стояти, "to stand") and Russian stojat'.
  • Germanic: Proto-Germanic *standaną ("to stand") and *stadiz ("standing, place, town").

In Proto-Germanic, the nominal derivative *stadiz designated a fixed physical location or standing ground. This word entered Old English as stede (meaning "place, position, locality"), surviving in modern English compounds such as homestead, bedstead, roadstead, and the fossilized prepositional phrase instead ("in the place of") and idiom in good stead.

Concurrently, Proto-Germanic formed an adjectival derivative with the suffix *-gaz: *staþigaz, signifying "fixed in place, standing fast, unshakeable." In Old English, this appeared as stæððiġ ("firm, stable, steady"), though the primary Old English adjective for steadfast loyalty was stedefæst (modern steadfast). During the Middle English period (c. 1150–1500), the adjective stedy (or stidi) solidified its meaning as "firmly positioned, not shaking, unwavering."

The adverbial form developed through the regular addition of the Old English adverbial suffix -līċe (originally derived from līc, meaning "body, form, likeness"). In Middle English, this produced stediliche (c. 1250–1300), which by the 15th and 16th centuries was phonetically reduced and orthographically regularized into Early Modern English steadily.

A crucial semantic transition occurred between the late Middle Ages and the early Modern period: while the root *steh₂- originally denoted absolute, static immobility ("standing still in one place"), steady and steadily underwent dynamic metaphorical drift. Speakers began applying the concept of "unshakeable firmness" not merely to motionless objects, but to motion itself. A sailor who kept a ship on a firm heading without rolling or pitching was said to steer steadily. By the 17th century, maritime commands codified this in the directive "steady as she goes."

During the 18th-century Enlightenment and the 19th-century Industrial Revolution, steadily became indispensable in scientific, mechanical, and economic literature. It was used by natural philosophers and economists (such as Adam Smith and Charles Darwin) to describe gradual, continuous natural processes—such as the steady accumulation of capital, the steady erosion of geological strata, or the steady ticking of precision chronometers. In modern English, steadily bridges the concrete domain of physical balance with the abstract domain of gradual, relentless progression.
