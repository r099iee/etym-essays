• Ditch: Similar because a ditch is the excavated twin of the dike, and the two words were once one; differs because a ditch stresses the hollow channel that drains or bounds, while a dike stresses the raised bank or wall that holds water back.
  • Embankment: Similar because both are raised banks built to carry or restrain; differs because embankment is the engineer's general term (railway embankments, river embankments), while dike is the older landscape word, redolent of fens and Dutch polders.
  • Levee: Similar because both are flood banks along rivers; differs because levee is the American and French term of the Mississippi and the Rhone, while dike belongs to Britain, the Fens, and the Netherlands.
  • Dam: Similar because both are barriers that hold water back; differs because a dam blocks a stream outright across its course to impound a reservoir, while a dike runs alongside water to keep it in its channel or off the land.
  • Seawall: Similar because both defend land against the sea; differs because a seawall is a vertical engineered face of stone or concrete, while a dike is typically a long earthen bank, grassed and grazed.
  • Bund: Similar because both are low embankments holding water in or out, especially in flat farmland; differs because bund is the South and East Asian term of paddy fields and tanks, while dike is North Sea vocabulary.
  • Causeway: Similar because the source sentence pairs them and both are raised ways over wet ground; differs because a causeway is built to be travelled on, while a dike is built to hold water back, even when a path runs along its top.
  • Trench: Similar because both are long cuts in the earth, and northern dialect still calls field drains dikes; differs because a trench is dug for war, pipes, or archaeology, while a dike belongs to water management and the open landscape.
