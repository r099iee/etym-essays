The biography of HAVE TO DO (in three movements, one for each root)

Movement I — HAVE: from "seize" to "hold" to "possess"

The verb have reaches back to the Proto-Indo-European root *keh₂p-, "to seize, grasp, take hold of." This is the root of Latin capere, "to seize" — the ancestor of capture, capable, accept, conceive — and its meaning is fundamentally about the hand: taking hold of something. The Germanic branch reshaped this root into *habjaną, "to have, to hold," a verb whose shift from "seize" to "hold, possess" is the classic move from an act to its result: what you have seized you hold, and what you hold you have. The cognates are everywhere in early Germanic: Gothic haban, Old Norse hafa (Danish have, Swedish ha), Old Saxon hebbian, Old High German habēn (modern German haben). Old English inherited habban, and the word has done enormous grammatical work ever since — not only "to possess" but the marker of perfect aspect ("I have seen") and the base of the obligative "have to" and the possessive idioms that concern us here. The etymology is secure and uncontested, and it matters for the idiom: "have to do with" begins as possession — having business, having a concern.

Movement II — TO: a tiny word of direction

The preposition to is one of the shortest words in the language and one of the oldest. Its ancestor is the Proto-Indo-European directional stem *do, "to, toward," which produced Germanic *tō and Old English tō. In Old English tō was both a preposition ("to, toward, at") and the marker of the inflected infinitive — a form of the verb made with the ending -ne used after tō, meaning "for the doing of." It is this grammatical function that builds the idiom: tō dōnne, "for doing," appears after habban to express a business held for performance. Sound change has been trivial — tō to to is a lengthening and shortening of a vowel — and the word's function has if anything grown: modern to marks the infinitive of every English verb, a task it took over in Middle English as the old inflectional endings of the infinitive decayed.

Movement III — DO: from "put" to "do"

Do comes from the Proto-Indo-European root *dʰeh₁-, "to put, place, set, make" — one of the most productive roots in the entire family. Its reflexes span the Indo-European world: Sanskrit dadhāti, "he places"; Greek títhēmi, "I put," with its vast family (thesis, theme, hypothesis, synthesis); Latin faciō, "I make, do," the source of fact, factory, fashion, and feat. In Germanic the root produced *dōną, "to do, act, put," and Old English dōn. The semantic path from "put" to "do" runs through "to set something in motion, to perform an action"; the old concrete sense "put, place" is still visible in doff (do + off, "put off") and don (do + on, "put on"). Modern do has become the great stand-in verb of English — the auxiliary of questions, negatives, emphasis, and pro-forms ("I do," "so do I") — but in the idiom "have to do with" it keeps its oldest business-sense of performing or transacting an affair.

The coalescence: how the three words became a relational idiom

  • Old English (c. 9th–11th c.): "habban tō dōnne" — literally "to have [something] for the doing." The inflected infinitive tō dōnne expresses a purpose: the thing one "has to do" is business held to be performed. The construction is ordinary syntax, but it contains the whole future idiom.
  • Middle English (12th–15th c.): the inflectional infinitive decays, and "haven to don" is levelled; at the same time the phrase acquires its second element — with — and "haven to don with [a person]" comes to mean "to have business or dealings with." The step from "business to perform" to "dealings with someone" is the semantic pivot.
  • Early Modern English (16th–17th c.): "have to do with" is fully idiomatic and broadens from persons to things: one can "have to do with" a matter, a subject, a question, meaning "to be concerned with, to relate to." The possessive logic fades; the phrase is now a relational idiom pure and simple.
  • 18th–19th c. consolidation: the idiom is universal in prose and speech; it also acquires its celebrated negative and interrogative forms — "I will have nothing to do with it," "what has that to do with this?" — which are among the most frequent sentences in the language.
  • 20th–21st c.: the idiom remains stable, while the homophonous obligative "have to" (= "must") completes its own separate grammaticalization into a modal marker. The two are now entirely distinct constructions sharing only their surface form: "have to do with X" = relate to X; "have to do X" = be obliged to do X.

Debated points

The individual etymologies are secure (PIE *keh₂p- for have, *do for to, *dʰeh₁- for do). The dating of the coalescence of "have to do with" into a relational idiom is approximate — the written record shows the dealings-sense emerging in the 14th century and the general relational sense by the 16th, but the spoken idiom certainly preceded both. The only genuine nuance is that the obligative "have to" (must) and the relational "have to do with" developed in parallel from the same Old English construction and are best described as separate grammaticalizations of one source.
