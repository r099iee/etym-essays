CLATTERING is a purely echoic, imitative word. No Proto-Indo-European etymon can be reconstructed for the clatter family: it was coined in Germanic as a phonetic imitation of a sharp, repeated, hollow percussion, and its Indo-European parallels are independent creations, not cognates. Stage 1 — Echoic base *klat- (sound-symbolic, no date): the acoustic formula of a sharp blow on a hard, hollow body; the same skeleton recurs independently across Germanic and Slavic as convergent creations rather than as descendants of a single inherited ancestor. No Sanskrit, Hindi, or Odia cognate exists. Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *klatrōną "to clatter, rattle repeatedly" (iterative verb in -r-): the -rōn- suffix marks repeated or intense action; reconstructed from the West Germanic attestations below, and probably coined over and over from the same sound rather than inherited as one word. Stage 3 — Old English clatrian "to clatter, rattle" (attested in Old English texts; the noun clatrung "noise, clatter" is also recorded). Stage 4 — Middle English clateren (13th century) "to strike or knock with repeated sharp sounds", used of kitchens, stables, workshops, and hooves on cobbles. Stage 5 — English clatter (verb, 15th century) "to make rapid short sharp noises". Stage 6 — English clatter (noun, 16th century) "a rattling noise, a din of repeated impacts". Stage 7 — clattering (present participle in -ing): the exact form of this card, in continuous use from the early modern period onward. DEBATED AND NUANCED POINTS: The Germanic base *klatrōną is a reconstruction, not an attested word, but the Old English verb clatrian is itself recorded, so the chain from Old English onward is secure. The parallels — Old Norse klaka "to chatter, twitter" (source of English clack), Middle Dutch clateren, Middle Low German and Middle High German klatern, German klappern, Latin clangere "to clang", Greek κλάζω (klázō) "to clang, shriek" — are independent sound-symbolic creations in the same acoustic field, NOT ancestors of the English word, and must not be mistaken for cognates.
