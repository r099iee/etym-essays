Marvelling (British spelling; American marveling) is the present participle and gerund of the verb marvel: to be filled with wonder, astonishment or amazement; to regard something with delighted or awed surprise. It names both the feeling (wonder) and its expression (gazing, exclaiming, reflecting), and it carries a distinctly warm, contemplative tone: one marvels at what is impressive or beautiful, not at what is merely surprising.

1. Core senses.

  • To wonder at: "marvelling at the skill of the craftsman" — the standard construction with at, naming the object of wonder.
  • To be amazed that...: "she marvelled that the bridge still stood" — clause complement expressing astonishment at a fact.
  • Absolute participle: "Marvelling, he found he could understand its tongue" (the context sentence) — the participle opens the sentence as a floating clause describing the speaker's state of wonder.

2. The noun and adjective family. Marvel (noun) = a wonderful or astonishing thing: "a marvel of engineering", "the marvels of the modern world". Marvellous/marvelous (adjective) = wonderful, astonishing, excellent — one of the most common adjectives of approval in British English ("a marvellous idea"). The semantic bridge runs both ways: something that makes you marvel is a marvel, and the marvellous is whatever deserves marvelling at.

3. Semantic depth: wonder, miracle and curiosity. The word's ancestor, Latin mīrābilia, meant "wonderful things" and fed Old French merveille, which meant both "wonder, astonishment" and "miracle". The religious sense (marvel = miracle, God's astonishing works) and the secular sense (marvel = anything astonishing in nature or art) have coexisted since the Middle Ages and still shade into each other: a "marvel of nature" retains a faint flavour of the miraculous.

4. Spelling: marvelling vs marveling. British English doubles the final -l before vowel suffixes after a single vowel: marvel – marvelling – marvelled. American English follows the general rule and keeps a single -l: marveling – marveled. Both are correct in their own dialects; the context sentence uses the British form.

5. Register and nuance. Literary and slightly formal; in speech, "I was marvelling at..." has an affectionate, reflective quality. It is gentler than astonished (which implies shock), warmer than amazed (which can be neutral), and more active than awestruck (which implies being overwhelmed): to marvel is to wonder with pleasure and attention, to dwell on the remarkable.

6. Summary. To be marvelling is to be filled with delighted, awed wonder at something remarkable — a feeling rooted in the ancient word for "wonderful things", and still carrying the double heritage of miracle and marvel: astonishment at what is wonderful, whether divine or merely human.
