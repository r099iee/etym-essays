Proto-Indo-European *wel- / *welH- "to turn, roll, wind, wrap" (the root of Latin volvere "to roll", Greek eilyein "to wrap, wind round", Sanskrit valati "he turns, rolls") → Proto-Germanic *walg-ijōn- / *wilig-ōn "the pliant, bending tree" (a derivative of the same root with a -g- extension and a noun-forming suffix; cf. Dutch wilg, Low German wilge, Old Saxon wilgia) → Old English welig / wilig "willow, osier" (attested from the 8th c.) → Middle English wilghe → wilowe "willow" (14th c.) → Early Modern English willow (16th c.) → Modern English willow + plural -s → willows. Note on the debated stage: the precise shape of the reconstructed Proto-Germanic intermediate (*wilig-, *welig-, with the -g- extension of the *wel- root) is uncertain, though the family of forms (Dutch wilg, Low German wilge, Old Saxon wilgia, Old English welig) is secure; the root is sometimes written *welH- with a laryngeal. The Latin and scientific genus name salix "willow" is NOT from *wel-: it descends from a separate Proto-Indo-European root *salik- (cf. Welsh helyg, Old English sealg "sallow"), and belongs to a different branch of the family.
