CHAIN: FROM PIE TO TEMPERAMENT (6 stages)

  1. Proto-Indo-European *temp- "to stretch, span" (traditional reconstruction, c. 4th–3rd millennium BCE; DEBATED — see note below; the link to Latin tempus pictures time as a measured, stretched span).
  2. Latin tempus, temporis "time, season, due measure, right moment" (attested classical, from Plautus onward).
  3. Latin temperāre "to mix in due proportion, moderate, restrain, temper" (attested classical; derived from the stem temper- of tempus, "due measure", with the sense of keeping within due measure).
  4. Latin temperamentum "due mixture, proper proportion, the constitution (of the body)" (attested classical/imperial; temperāre + result-suffix -mentum; medical writers use it of the humoral balance).
  5. English temperament "the constitution of the body resulting from the mixture of the four humors; a person's nature" (15th century, borrowed from Latin temperamentum and reinforced by Middle French tempérament).
  6. English TEMPERAMENT — modern form, with the specialized musical and psychological senses developed from the 16th century onward.

DEBATED / NUANCED POINTS

  • The deeper PIE ancestry of Latin tempus is not securely established: de Vaan records the etymology of tempus as uncertain, and the traditional link to a root *temp- "to stretch, span" (time conceived as a stretched span) is an inference, not a certainty; treat the PIE stage as traditional and probabilistic.
  • The derivation of temperāre from tempus ("due measure") is the standard account and is accepted; the mnemonic on this card reads the word as TEMPER + MENT, which is etymologically apt in spirit — temper- is indeed the stem of temperāre — though the true suffix is Latin -mentum, not the English word "ment".
  • The related English word temper (noun and verb) comes from the same temperāre; temperature, temperance, and tempera (the paint, "tempered" with egg) are all members of the same Latin family.
