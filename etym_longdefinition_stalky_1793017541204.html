Stalky is a plain descriptive adjective meaning full of stalks, having prominent stiff stems, or growing tall on stiff upright stems like the coarse weeds of a neglected place.

In the source sentence it opens a catalogue of rank vegetation on ground where no tree grows, all rough grass and tall plants seeding into fluff and ash. Here stalky does honest humble work: it tells the reader that the plants are all stem and little leaf, coarse, fibrous, gone to seed, the kind of vegetation that whips at the legs and rattles dry in the wind rather than anything lush or flowered.

Core meaning in detail: A stalk is the stiff main stem of a plant, especially an herbaceous one, and stalky applies that noun as a quality: stems that are conspicuous, stiff, thick, or disproportionately large beside the leaves, flowers, or edible parts. Celery left too long in the ground goes stalky; cabbage with more rib than leaf is stalky; a neglected border at summer's end is all stalky growth, seed-heads on poles. The word always carries a faint judgement of coarseness: stalky things are useful substance outweighed by structure, food outweighed by fibre, flower outweighed by stem.

All senses, shades, and neighbours:

  • Botanical, having many or stout stalks (primary sense, from the 16th c.): the everyday gardener's and botanist's use, exactly as in the source sentence, for plants whose stems dominate the eye. The bridge is direct composition: stalk plus the adjectival -y meaning characterised by or full of, precisely as in leafy, weedy, or seedy.
  • Cookery, too much stalk in proportion to the edible part: stalky spinach, stalky broccoli, stalky coriander past its prime. The bridge runs from the garden to the kitchen: the same stiffness that looks coarse in the border eats coarse on the plate, so cooks use stalky as a quiet complaint and trim accordingly, rescuing leaf from rib.
  • Horticultural, drawn up into long thin stems, near-leggy: seedlings raised in poor light go stalky, pale and stretched, all stem and no substance. The bridge is cause and effect: the plant spends its strength reaching for light, so the stem outruns everything else, and stalky shades toward leggy and drawn, the nursery word for a sowing gone wrong.
  • Figurative and occasional, stiffly upright like a stem: writers occasionally lend the word beyond the garden, as in stalky columns of weeds along a fence or, rarely, a stalky young figure, tall and still filling out. The bridge is visual metaphor: anything thin, straight, and rigidly upright recalls the plant stem, though this stays a nonce colour rather than an established dictionary sense.
  • A false friend distinguished, the pursuing stalk: the verb stalk, to pursue stealthily, and its agent stalker belong to an entirely different word with a different history, and stalky never means stealthy or menacing. The two stalks are homographs with separate origins, and only puns ever join them.

Register, grammar, and connotation: stalky is a workaday word of sheds and kitchens rather than of poetry, plain where verdant or luxuriant would preen. It is gradable, taking stalkier and stalkiest, and works both attributively, as in stalky growth, and predicatively, as in the border has gone stalky. Its feeling is mildly dismissive but never disgusted: stalky vegetation is rank rather than rotten, honest coarseness at the wrong season, the plant world's equivalent of stubble. Florists turn the judgement inside out, prizing long straight stems for cutting, yet even they would call an arrangement stalky only when stem shows where bloom should be.
