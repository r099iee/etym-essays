Single root word: STUFFY. The chain traces through French and Latin to a Germanic verbal base; the deepest origin is debated between Germanic and Greek candidates, and that debate is flagged at the bottom. Proto-Germanic *stoppōną "to plug, stop up, stuff" (reconstructed verb; attested in Old High German stopfon "to plug, stuff" and Old English -stoppian "to stop up"; the Latin cousin stuppāre "to stop with tow" from stuppa "tow, oakum" < Greek styppē is an areal reinforcement of the same idea) — (~1st millennium BCE) → Old French estoffer "to equip, stock, pad, quilt; to cram" (verb; ~11th–12th c.; with the noun estoffe "quilted material, padding, equipment") → Anglo-Norman / Middle English stuffe (noun) "quilted padding, material" (early 14th c.) and stuffen (verb) "to furnish, pad, cram full, plug up" (mid-14th c.) → English stuff "to cram, fill, plug; the general word for material or substance" (noun and verb; 14th–16th c.) → English stuffy (adjective, formed with the suffix -y "full of, characterized by") — first recorded c. 1560s as "full of stuff, substantial"; modern senses: "ill-ventilated, close" (recorded 1831), "prim, formal, strait-laced" (recorded from mid-19th c.) → Modern English STUFFY (adjective; close and airless; congested; prim and formal). Note on debate: two origins compete for the underlying "stuff/stop" verb. (1) The Germanic route — Proto-Germanic *stoppōną "to plug" (cf. German stopfen, English stop) — is the mainstream modern view (Etymonline, OED). (2) An older scholarship derived it from Greek styfein "to contract, make astringent" (cf. styptic), but this is now generally rejected for the "stuff" verb; Greek styppē "tow, oakum" feeds only the Latin stuppa/stopper branch. The adjective stuffy itself is a straightforward English formation from stuff + -y and is not in dispute.
