Talons are the long, sharp, deeply hooked claws of a bird of prey — eagles, hawks, falcons, owls — built around a massive hind claw, the hallux, that delivers the killing grip. In the moment of the eagles' arrival — “Just at that moment the Lord of the Eagles swept down from above, seized him in his talons, and was gone” — the word compresses sudden, impersonal, unstoppable avian power into a single unit. The organ of the predator is here turned to rescue, so that the talons read as instruments of fate and deliverance, seizing Bilbo with the same lethal certainty they would use to seize prey, and lifting him out of the flames in a single sweep. The nuance is control without effort: the Lord of the Eagles does not reach, does not strain — he seizes, and is gone, and the word's hard, hooked consonants (t…l…n) enact the grip the sentence describes. Because talon carries an ominous, predatory tone in English, the rescue keeps its edge of danger: being caught in talons is terrifying even when it saves your life, and the reader feels both the threat and the grace.

1. The core sense and its anatomy.

Anatomically, the talons of a raptor are the four claws of each foot, with the hindmost — the hallux, corresponding to our big toe — the longest and most powerful, the hook that strikes and holds. The other three toes grip around the prey, and the whole arrangement forms a circle of pointed, scimitar-curved blades that can pierce fur, hide, and scales and lock shut with irresistible force. In falconry, the talons (sometimes called the bird's 'foot' or, historically, its 'heel' — see the etymology notes) are the raptor's chief weapon, and a trained hawk is valued for the strength and grasp of its talons. The singular talon names one claw; the plural talons names the whole grasping set, and it is the plural that English uses in nearly all real and figurative contexts — one speaks of an eagle's talons, not of an eagle's talon.

2. The near-synonym field and register.

The synonym field turns on register and anatomy. Claw is the everyday word for the sharp curved nails of cats, bears, crabs, and machinery — emotionally neutral, broad in application; every talon is a claw, but not every claw is a talon. Talon is almost exclusively raptorial and literary: it belongs to birds of prey and mythic beasts (a dragon's talons, an eagle's talons), and by extension to cruel human hands — a tyrant's talons, to be in someone's talons, to fall into the talons of the law — where it always implies grasping and seizing rather than mere scratching. Nail belongs to a different appendage class altogether, the flat plates of the fingers and toes; hoof to the ungulates; spur is a defensive spike, not a grasping weapon, and it is the word for the eagle's rear fighting claw rather than its gripping front ones. Grip and pounce name the action, not the organ: a pounce is the strike, a grip is the hold. The true distinction of talon is double: it is both anatomically precise (the raptor's hooked grasping claw) and register-marked (literary, ominous, predatory).

3. Secondary and technical senses.

  • Falconry and ornithology: the grasping claws of a bird of prey, especially the hallux; in old falconry manuals the talon was the bird's 'heel', the hind claw that strikes.
  • Card games (technical): the talon is the undealt stock of cards left in the pack, from the Old French and French sense of talon as the heel of a stack or the reserve pile — the remnant of the deck, so called because it 'sits at the heel' of the layout.
  • Botany (rare, technical): a talon is the long, recurved spur-like base of certain flower petals or the claw of a petal, named for its hooked shape.
  • Architecture and mechanics (rare): a hook, catch, or claw-shaped fitting; in heraldry, talons appear as the claws of displayed beasts.
  • Figurative and literary use: 'in the talons of' — captivity, tyranny, ruthless control — the word of being seized and held by something stronger than oneself, whether a dragon, a tyrant, the law, or fate; always grasping, never merely touching.

4. Why the word fits the passage.

Tolkien chooses talons because the word keeps both halves of the rescue together: the lethal, grasping, predatory claw and the sudden grace of deliverance. The Lord of the Eagles seizes Bilbo in his talons — the reader feels the grip of a killer's instrument and, in the same instant, the safety it provides. No synonym would do: claw is too neutral, hand too human, grip an abstraction. Only talons carries at once the raptor's menace, the anatomical precision of the hallux and its hooked blades, and the literary register of mythic power — the talons of eagles, of dragons, of fate itself.
