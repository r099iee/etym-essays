- Early 14th c. - 'scorchen' appears meaning to burn or singe a surface - scorched land means fire-ravaged, dried-out ground
- 16th c. - the participle 'scorched' is standard for surface damage by fire and sun - scorched linen, scorched skin, scorched grass
- 17th-18th c. - the word spreads into weather and cooking - scorching heat, scorched pans; 'scorch' becomes a noun for a burn-mark
- 19th c. - military 'scorched earth' crystallises - burning everything an advancing army could use - the phrase enters the vocabulary of war
- 20th c. - 'scorched-earth' is headline vocabulary in two world wars, then moves into business (scorched-earth takeover defence) and politics - 'scorching' becomes an intensifier for heat and speed
- 21st c. - climate reporting makes 'scorched' the signature word for drought and fire-ravaged landscapes - colloquially, to scorch someone is to defeat or tongue-lash them utterly
