Farthing names the smallest coin of old England: a little bronze disc worth one quarter of an old penny, hence one nine-hundred-and-sixtieth of a pre-decimal pound, circulating from medieval silver cuttings to the bronze pieces withdrawn in 1961. To say a thing is not worth a farthing is to price it at nothing; to say rents leave not a single farthing, as Cobbett does in the source sentence, is to say the farmer's whole surplus has been devoured by rates and manure before the landlord sees a coin.

In the source sentence the word carries its literal accounting sense sharpened into protest: manure, poor-rates, road-rates, and county rates pile cost upon cost, and if one farthing of rent survives the heap Cobbett calls it strange. The coin is tiny, but the arithmetic is enormous, because the farthing is the unit in which the labourer's margin is measured: lose farthings and you lose pounds, lose pounds and you lose the farm. Every reader of 1820s England felt the coin in the palm, brown and light, and understood that a budget balanced in farthings was a life balanced on a knife-edge.

Core meaning in detail: Physically the farthing ran through three great incarnations: the medieval silver farthing, often literally a penny cut into four quarters along the cross; the hammered and milled silver and copper farthings of the Tudors and Stuarts; and the familiar bronze farthing of 1860 to 1956 with Britannia or a wren on its reverse, withdrawn after decimalisation approached. At every stage it was the people's small change: the price of a bun, a toll, a candle-end. Its name simply means fourthling, the little fourth, from Old English feorðing, a fourth part.

All senses, primary and secondary:

  • The quarter-penny coin itself, in silver, copper, or bronze (primary historical sense, from 13th c.): a pocketful of farthings. The bridge is the name's own arithmetic: fourth plus the diminutive -ing makes the little fourth of a penny, cut, struck, or cast across seven centuries.
  • Anything of the smallest value, or the smallest possible amount of money (figurative, from 14th c.; the source sense): not worth a farthing, not a farthing of rent. The bridge runs from purse to proverb: because the coin was the least that law recognised, it became the standard image of near-nothingness in sermons, novels, and farm accounts alike.
  • A trifle or jot in general non-monetary use (extended idiom): it does not matter a farthing, he does not care a farthing. The bridge widens the money image into pure degree: concern, difference, and worth are all weighed on the same tiny scales, and the farthing marks the zero point.
  • Rhetorical emphasis of thoroughness or ruin (Cobbett's use): not one single farthing left. The bridge adds the intensive single: a farthing is already minimal, and denying even one of them turns shortage into completeness, the ledger swept clean by rates.
  • The farthing as a unit of land, rent, and tax in medieval records (technical, historical): farthingales of land and farthing rents in Domesday-descended surveys. The bridge runs through division: just as the penny divides into four coins, the hide and the virgate divide into farthing-shares for assessment, and the coin's name becomes a surveyor's fraction.
  • A small thing or creature viewed with affection or pity (rare, dialectal and literary): a farthing babe, a farthing candle. The bridge is tenderness toward littleness: what is worth almost nothing in money may be worth everything in feeling, and writers reach for the smallest coin to name it.

Register and connotation: farthing is nostalgic and moralising today, the coin of Dickens, church collections, and grandparents' tales, but it was once hard economics, the difference between eating and begging. Its idioms always moralise value: not worth a farthing judges quality, to the last farthing judges honesty in paying debts, and a farthing for your thoughts's ancestor, a penny for them, judges curiosity. Cobbett wields it as a weapon: count the farthings and you expose the system that steals them.

Collocations and grammar: fixed frames include not worth a farthing, not a farthing, to the last farthing, every farthing of it, and the intensified not one single farthing. As a coin noun it counts plainly (three farthings, a handful of farthings), while as an idiom noun it almost always appears in the negative, where absence is the point.
