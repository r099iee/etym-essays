The mystery of the bar. Unlike most words of the English domestic and legal vocabulary, bar has no secure classical Latin ancestry. Classical Latin expressed “bar, bolt” with vectis, sera and obex, and “rod” with virga or baculum; there is no barra in the literary language until late antiquity. When barra surfaces in Late Latin and the Romance languages — French barre, Italian sbarra, Spanish and Portuguese barra, Occitan barra — it appears as a fully formed word for “beam, bar, barrier” with no obvious Latin parent. The principal scholarly proposals: (1) a Celtic source — Gaulish *barros “the bushy top or tip of a tree”, hence a lopped branch usable as a cross-beam, cognate with Old Irish barr and Welsh bar “top, crest, point”; the semantics are attractive (a piece of branch as a beam) and the distribution fits a Gallic word absorbed into the Vulgar Latin of Roman Gaul; (2) alternatively, an Iberian or Mediterranean substrate word, with the Romance distribution explained as a trade and fortification term — the Romans’ wooden stockades and palisades being the likeliest place for a foreign “bar” to enter the camp vocabulary. The honest verdict, repeated by the OED and Etymonline, is that the origin is uncertain, with the Celtic hypothesis the most favoured. This uncertainty is background only: the word’s recorded history in English begins with the Middle English loan, not with the root.

The French word and the English noun. Old French barre “beam, rod, bar, gate-bar, barrier” (attested from the 10th century) passed into Anglo-Norman and Middle English around 1200. English took the whole French bundle at once: the physical bar — the cross-beam of a door or gate (c. 1200), the bar of metal, the bar of a prison — the sandbank sense (“bar” of a river or harbour mouth, the submerged ridge that bars the way; in English from the 16th century), and the legal bar — the railing of a court, attested early, the place where prisoners and counsel stand “at the bar”. The polysemy was already present in French, and English adopted it wholesale; the family of meanings has never since separated cleanly, because all of them draw on the single image of a straight barrier laid across a passage.

The verb and the participle. English derived the verb bar (“to fasten with bars”) about 1300. The figurative senses followed fast: “to obstruct” (14th century: ways and plans barred), “to block a claim or a person’s rights” (14th–15th centuries), and by the 16th century “to exclude, prohibit” — barred from office, barred from the succession, barred from a guild. The participle barred is attested in every one of these senses within a century of the verb. In law the term settled into fixed collocations: a claim barred by a statute — the doctrine of limitation, that after a fixed period a cause of action dies — is formulated from the 16th–17th centuries, with the compact forms statute-barred and time-barred crystallising in the 19th–20th centuries. In the same legal ecosystem “the Bar” (the profession) emerged in the 16th century, “barrister” shortly after, and disbar (to expel a lawyer from the profession) in the 17th century. Music borrowed French barre again in the 17th century for the vertical line dividing measures — the “bar” of a score — extending the straight-line image into notation itself.

Stripes, birds, stars. Independently of the fastening senses, bar and barred developed the visual sense “a transverse stripe, a band”. Heraldry regulated the bar — a horizontal band across the shield, with the thin barrulet and bar-wise placement — in the treatises of the 15th–16th centuries. Natural history used “barred” of striped plumage, wings, shells and fish from the 17th century onward, culminating in the Barred Owl (Strix varia), so named in 19th-century American ornithology for the transverse barring of its breast and wings, and the barred-marked game fish of angling literature. Twentieth-century astronomy added the barred spiral galaxy — a spiral galaxy whose arms emerge from a central bar of stars, a structural type identified by Hubble’s classification of 1926 and now the type to which our own Milky Way belongs. The bridge in every case is simple shape-matching: a barred surface looks like a grate of bars.

Modern extensions. The 20th century added the informal British “he’s barred” — banned from a pub or club, i.e. turned away at the door, from the physical sense of the barred entrance. Sport and immigration law use “barred from competing”, “barred from entering” daily; 21st-century platform culture adopted “barred” for suspended or banned accounts. The adjective thus remains quadruple-faced — fastened, blocked, excluded, striped — but every face is traceable to one image: the beam laid across the doorway, which bars the passage and draws a straight line across the dark.
