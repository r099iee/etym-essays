- 1300s: the verb “gloumen” = to become dark, to look sullen - first the face darkens
- 1500s: “glum” is an adjective - sullen, morose, of sour and gloomy temper - describing a person’s dark expression and mood
- 1600s–1700s: glum stays a word for personal moodiness - a glum fellow, a glum silence; still chiefly of faces and tempers
- 1800s: extended to weather and scenes - a glum sky, a glum little town - gloom spreads from the person to the atmosphere
- 1900s–present: glum is everyday, mildly comic in its bluntness - “he looked glummer than ever” - the comparative glummer is used freely, and “glummer and glummer” pictures a mood sinking steadily
- Today the word is short, plain and slightly humorous - it deflates grand sadness into a simple, visible, silent case of the blues
