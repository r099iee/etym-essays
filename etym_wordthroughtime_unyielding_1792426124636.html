- [Early Modern English (late 16th–17th c.)] - the negative compound unyielding emerged in Elizabethan drama (Shakespeare) to describe adamantine physical stones and unbroken human wills - [perception: admired in epic poetry as a mark of heroic constancy and tragic grandeur]
- [18th c. Enlightenment] - used extensively in civil engineering treatises for solid granite bedrock foundations (unyielding strata) and in political philosophy for unbending constitutional principles - [perception: associated with structural reliability and uncompromising civic integrity]
- [19th c. Victorian & Romantic Era] - celebrated by Romantic poets (Shelley, Byron) as the supreme epithet for Prometheus and revolutionary defiance against tyranny - [perception: honored as the definitive mark of moral sublime and heroic resistance]
- [20th–21st c. Contemporary Era] - widely utilized across diplomacy, material physics, whistleblowing ethics, and sports psychology to denote unbreakable grit, hardened alloys, and principled refusal to capitulate - [perception: universally recognized as the gold standard of resilience, though occasionally critiqued when diplomacy demands compromise]
