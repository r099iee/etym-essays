• magician: Similar because both refer to an individual who performs feats that appear supernatural or impossible, whether through stage illusion or arcane arts; differs because magician is the broadest and most prestigious term, encompassing exalted mythical sorcery, deep spiritual theurgy, and grand theatrical performances, whereas conjuror carries historical connotations of spirit invocation, street performance, rustic wonder-working, or manual sleight of hand.
  • illusionist: Similar because both describe modern entertainers who manipulate perception and equipment to stage impossible phenomena; differs because illusionist is a sophisticated, formal title preferred by large-scale theatrical performers (such as David Copperfield) who disclaim all supernatural claims, whereas conjuror retains earthy, historical, and occult resonance.
  • sorcerer: Similar because both historically claim or appear to manipulate hidden supernatural forces; differs because a sorcerer (derived from Latin sors, "fate/lot") is traditionally viewed as dark, sinister, and dealing with malevolent witchcraft or genuine occult pacts, unlike the entertaining or wandering conjuror.
  • wizard: Similar because both denote practitioners of miraculous, esoteric wonders (as hobbits describe Gandalf as both wizard and conjuror); differs because wizard (originally "wise one", from Middle English wys + -ard) denotes immense scholarly wisdom, profound cosmic age, and intrinsic ontological power, whereas conjuror emphasizes the active performance of tricks, invocations, or marvels.
  • prestidigitator: Similar because both specialize in baffling spectators through rapid digital dexterity and hidden props; differs because prestidigitator is an ornate, humorous Latinate term (literally "quick-fingered") used specifically for tabletop sleight-of-hand artists, lacking conjuror's historical association with spirit summoning.
  • necromancer: Similar because both possess historical roots in summoning the supernatural; differs because a necromancer specifically practices divination by communicating with the physical corpses or spirits of the dead (from Greek nekros + manteia), representing a dark, forbidden branch of magical art, whereas conjuror extends to white magic, illusion, and harmless entertainment.
  • juggler: Similar because historically in Middle English and Old French, jougleur / juggler and conjuror were virtually synonymous terms for itinerant entertainers who performed tricks, illusions, and acrobatics; differs because in modern English a juggler exclusively manipulates tossed physical objects in the air, having lost all magical connotations.
  • thaumaturge: Similar because both denote workers of wonders and apparent miracles; differs because thaumaturge is a scholarly, philosophical, or theological designation applied to religious saints or high hermetic philosophers, operating at the highest academic register.
  • enchanter: Similar because both project magical power through spoken or chanted words; differs because an enchanter (from Latin incantare) relies primarily upon sung spells, hypnotic charms, and mind-altering bewitchment, whereas a conjuror calls forth tangible entities or executes visual wonders.
  • charlatan: Similar because both are often suspected of deceiving the public through hidden trickery and false pretenses; differs because a charlatan is an outright fraud who swindles people through false medical or scientific claims, whereas a conjuror's illusions are typically understood and enjoyed as theatrical or folkloric wonderment.
