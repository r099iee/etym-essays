Two root chains, given in isolation, then the compound: CHAIN 1 — "foot" (first element): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *ped- / *pod- "foot; to step, tread" (reconstructed root, 4th-3rd millennium BCE). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *fots "foot" (reconstructed; by Grimm's Law PIE *p- becomes PGmc *f-). Stage 3 — Old English fot "foot; also base, bottom, lower part of a mountain" (attested c. 9th century). Stage 4 — Modern English foot, used with its long-established transferred sense "the base of a mountain" as the first element of the compound. CHAIN 2 — "hill" (second element): Stage 1 — Proto-Indo-European *kel- "to rise, tower, project, be prominent" (reconstructed; also the source of Latin collis "hill," columna "column," culmen "summit"). Stage 2 — Proto-Germanic *hulliz "hill" (reconstructed; by Grimm's Law PIE *k- becomes PGmc *h-). Stage 3 — Old English hyll "hill, natural elevation" (attested c. 9th century). Stage 4 — Modern English hill "a natural elevation of land." COMPOUND (both chains converge): Stage 5 — English foothill "a low hill at the base of a mountain range," formed by compounding foot + hill (first recorded in American English c. 1856-1857). —> foothill. Debate / nuance note: Both PIE roots are securely reconstructed. The only nuance is that "foot" for a mountain base is an ancient, independent anthropomorphic metaphor found also in Latin (pes montis), Greek (podos orous), and Sanskrit (pada); the English compound itself is young (19th century) and fully transparent.
