Wards, in the locksmithing sense, are the internal ridges, plates, or projections inside a lock that block any key not cut with matching grooves — and, correspondingly, the slots or notches on a key that clear those projections. They are the lock's mechanical password: shape must answer shape before the bolt will turn. In The Hobbit, Gandalf hands Thorin "a key with a long barrel and intricate wards, made of silver": the plural paints complexity and craft, a small precious engine of access to the Mountain's secret door. Tone: technical, tactile, slightly antique-trade; wonder arrives through hardware detail, not through a vague "magic key" label alone. The word is the plural of the noun ward, and it comes from the verb ward "to guard": the ridges are the guards of the mechanism — internal watchmen that keep out any key without the right profile. This is why the same word family covers so much of the vocabulary of protection: a hospital ward is a guarded room, a ward of court is a person under legal protection, a warden is a keeper, and to ward off is to keep danger away. Primary senses of ward / wards
  • Lock hardware (plural): the internal projections or ridges of a lock that prevent the turning of a key unless it has corresponding grooves; also the grooves or webs of a key that pass those projections.
  • Guardianship and custody: the state of being under protection or control — "a minor in ward"; a ward is also the person so protected (especially a child under a guardian).
  • A guarded place: a division of a hospital (a hospital ward), a division of a city for administration and elections (an electoral ward), or, archaically, a guarded room or prison.
  • One who guards: a ward (archaic) as a watchman or keeper; the verb to ward (off) as to defend against or fend away.
Polysemy and secondary modern senses
  • Ward (verb): to guard, protect, or watch; chiefly surviving in to ward off — to keep away or repel (blows, danger, illness) — and in set phrases.
  • Wards of a key: in locksmithing the term also names the corresponding grooves, slits, or webs cut into the key's bit that slide past the lock's wards.
  • Legal ward: a minor or other person placed by law under the care of a guardian or court — "ward of court", "ward of the state".
  • Hospital ward: a large room or division of a hospital where patients are cared for — the guarded room of the sick.
  • Electoral ward: a subdivision of a city or borough for local government and elections (a political division, etymologically a "guard"-district).
  • Warden / warder: derived nouns for keepers and guards — a prison warder, a park warden — built on the same root.
  • Wardrobe: a guarded place for clothes (from ward-robe, a room where robes were kept); steward: a house-guard (from Old English stig-weard, keeper of the hall).
  • -ward / -wards (directional suffix): toward, homeward, onward — a separate spelling-convergent development from a different root meaning "turn", not from the guard-sense.
The semantic history of wards is one of the cleanest in English: one ancient root of guarding and watching branched outward into custody, protected rooms, protected people, repelling blows — and, in the hands of locksmiths, the small metal ridges that guard a lock against the wrong key. Every sense is the same image of protection applied to a different thing, and the plural wards on Gandalf's silver key carries the whole family's weight of watchfulness.
