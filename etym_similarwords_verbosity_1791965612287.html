• wordiness: Similar because it is the plain English equivalent and names the same excess; differs in being informal and neutral in tone, without verbosity’s suggestion of stylistic vice.
  • prolixity: Similar in denoting tedious length; differs in emphasising duration and sprawl rather than mere quantity of words, and is more formal and literary.
  • verbiage: Similar as a mass of words; differs in stressing emptiness or jargon — words without substance — whereas verbosity stresses the excess relative to the thought.
  • loquacity: Similar as excessive speech; differs in being about talkativeness as a habit of personality, not about prose style or writing.
  • garrulousness: Similar in excessive talking; differs in being informal, chatty, and often applied to a person’s manner rather than to a text.
  • circumlocution: Similar as a cause of wordiness; differs in naming a specific device — talking around a point — rather than the overall quality.
  • redundancy: Similar as repetition that pads a text; differs in being countable and technical, a property of information rather than of style.
  • pleonasm: Similar as superfluous words; differs as a grammatical term for a specific redundancy (as in “free gift”), not a general judgement of a style.
  • periphrasis: Similar in roundabout expression; differs as a rhetorical term that can be elegant and deliberate, where verbosity is always a fault.
  • long-windedness: Similar in effect on the reader; differs in being colloquial and chiefly about speech, and in stressing tedium rather than self-indulgence.
  • grandiloquence: Similar in inflated style; differs in being about pompous, high-sounding diction rather than sheer quantity of words.
