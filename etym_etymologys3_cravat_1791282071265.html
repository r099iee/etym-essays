Unlike most English dress words with Latin or French tailoring roots, cravat begins as an ethnonym. Its ancestor is the Slavic self-designation *xъrvatъ, reconstructed for the early medieval Croat tribe, attested in Old Church Slavonic-adjacent sources, Byzantine Greek as Xrobatoi (in Constantine Porphyrogenitus, 10th c.), and Latin as Chroati / Croatae. The deeper origin of Hrvat itself is famously disputed: one school derives it from Proto-Slavic internal formation, another — currently influential — from an Iranian (Sarmatian/Alanic) name *xar-va(n)t- meaning "guardian" or "one who herds," borrowed into Slavic during steppe contacts. The debate does not affect the French-English story, but lexicographers note it explicitly rather than choosing a false certainty.

By the early modern period Hrvat unambiguously meant a Croat. During the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648) Croatian light cavalry serving the Habsburgs and then France wore distinctive knotted linen neckerchiefs — practical dust and sweat cloths that also marked unit identity. French observers in Paris in the 1630s, struck by the look during the reign of Louis XIII and early Louis XIV, borrowed both the object and a garbled form of the wearers' name. French Croate ("Croat") metathesized and voiced to cravate by the 1650s, first meaning "Croatian soldier" and almost immediately "the neckcloth in the Croatian manner." This is a classic eponym-by-association, like cardigan or raglan from later commanders.

Phonologically the shift Croate /kʁoat/ → cravate /kʁavat/ inserts a medial -v- and an extra syllable, a folk-etymological reshaping common in French borrowings of foreign names to fit French syllable patterns. English borrowed cravate wholesale in 1656 as cravat, anglicizing spelling (dropping the final -e) and pronunciation to /krəˈvæt/. Early attestations cluster around Royalist newsbooks and tailors' bills during the Interregnum and Restoration — Charles II's court, exiled in France, brought the fashion home.

  • Baroque display (1660-1720): lace steinkirks and flowing muslin cravats dominate portraiture (Lely, Kneller). Size and costly lace signal wealth; pamphlets mock fops who starve to buy lace.
  • Georgian starch and stocks (1720-1800): military discipline stiffens civilian dress into stocks; civilian cravats grow taller, requiring buckram and starch. Caricaturists link high cravats to pride.
  • Regency art (1800-1825): Beau Brummell reduces color to white linen and elevates tying to moral philosophy — cleanliness, restraint, perfect fold. His Oriental and imitators' manuals (Neckclothitania, 1818) treat knots as characterology.
  • Victorian narrowing (1850-1900): industrial weaving, the lounge suit, and the sewn long tie shrink the cravat; it survives in formal, legal, and sporting dress, then as nostalgia.

Socioculturally the cravat tracks masculinity itself: from mercenary swagger to courtly grace to dandy self-control to bourgeois respectability to wedding archaism. Its 20th-century afterlife splits in two. Fashion keeps it as wedding ascot and vintage revival, deliberately invoking heritage. Medicine keeps it as cravat bandage — field manuals from the American Civil War onward instruct soldiers to fold a triangular bandage "into a cravat," preserving the word wherever first aid is taught even as daily neckwear vanished. Adjusting the cravat in fiction, as in the source sentence, thus condenses centuries of male self-fashioning into one small verb phrase.
