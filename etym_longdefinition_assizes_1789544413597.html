Assizes (always used in the plural, historically also appearing as assize) denotes the periodic court sessions held in each county of England and Wales by itinerant judges of the High Court to administer civil and criminal common law justice, trying serious indictable felonies before a jury.

The term encompasses a multi-layered history of constitutional, legislative, judicial, and economic meanings spanning eight centuries of English legal history:

  • Primary Judicial Sense — The English County Circuit Courts: The royal court sessions held typically twice or three times a year (the Lent, Summer, and Winter circuits) across the various judicial circuits of England and Wales (such as the Northern, Midland, Western, and South-Eastern circuits). Visiting High Court judges, traveling under royal commissions of oyer and terminer (to hear and determine) and general gaol delivery (to empty the county prisons), tried serious offenses including murder, treason, burglary, arson, highway robbery, and high-value grand larceny.
  • Historical Committal Distinction in Victorian Crime: In 19th-century English criminal procedure (as depicted in Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of the Blue Carbuncle"), when a police magistrate determined that an accused felon (such as John Horner) could not be dealt with summarily due to the gravity of the theft and previous convictions, the magistrate committed the prisoner to stand trial before the traveling royal judges at the next county Assizes.
  • Medieval Legislative Decrees and Royal Edicts: In Anglo-Norman and Plantagenet England, an assize was a royal legislative enactment or constitutional statute promulgated by the King in Council. Famous examples include the Assize of Clarendon (1166), which established the foundation of the grand jury, the Assize of Northampton (1176), and the Assize of Arms (1181).
  • Economic and Commercial Price Regulations (Assize of Bread and Ale): Medieval statutory ordinances establishing the legal standard, weight, quality, and maximum retail price of staple consumer goods in proportion to the market price of grain, enacted to prevent price gouging by bakers, millers, and brewers.
  • Real Property Writs and Actions (Petty Assizes): In medieval English property law, summary real actions—such as the Assize of Novel Disseisin and the Assize of Mort d'Ancestor—designed to restore possession of lands to individuals who had been wrongfully dispossessed.
  • The Judicial Body or Jury Itself: In early feudal law, the sworn body of twelve knights or freeholders summoned to sit together and deliver a verdict on land ownership or criminal presentments.

Semantic Bridges & Polysemic Development: The foundational concept binding royal courts, statutory price regulations, land actions, and legislative edicts together is the Latin verb assidere ("to sit beside"). When the king's justices or municipal bailiffs "sat down" together in formal session, their "sitting" (Old French assise) produced both the assembly itself, the decrees they enacted while seated, the prices they fixed, and the court trials they conducted. Over centuries, the plural form Assizes became specialized almost exclusively for the majestic county criminal and civil trials presided over by traveling red-robed royal judges.

Modern Abolition and Historical Legacy: The Assizes operated continuously in England from the reign of Henry II until they were formally abolished by the Courts Act 1971, which established the unified, permanent Crown Court system. Despite their statutory replacement, the Assizes remain iconic in British social, legal, and literary history, immortalized in the trial scenes of Charles Dickens, George Eliot, Thomas Hardy, and Sir Arthur Conan Doyle.
