The biography of shudder begins with violence and speed. Its ultimate root, Proto-Indo-European *(s)keud-, meant "to shake, throw, hasten, drive" — a root of sudden, hurled motion, visible across the family in Vedic Sanskrit skundáti "to hasten, spring forward" and in the Germanic shoot-line that gives English shoot. The very first semantic thread, then, is not trembling but projecting: the same root throws an arrow, darts an animal forward, and sets a ship scudding. Trembling is a later, grammaticalised offshoot — the root's own shaking, slowed down and made repetitive. The Germanic split. The Germanic branch split the root into two faces. On one side, Proto-Germanic *skeutaną hardened into "to shoot" (Old English scēotan, Old Norse skjóta, Old High German skiozan → German schießen), with its frequentative scuttle still meaning "to move in quick darting bursts." On the other, a *skud- variant settled into plain shaking: Middle Dutch schudden, Old High German scutten → German schütteln "to shake." It was from this shaking strain that English's word was born — through a frequentative formation *skudrōn, which added a reduplicating -r- to mark repeated, iterated action, giving "to shake again and again," i.e. to tremble. That formation reached English in the early fourteenth century as Middle English schoderen (c. 1300), side by side with Middle Low German schōderen and Middle High German schüderen (modern German schaudern "to shudder, be appalled, shiver with horror") — the languages of the medieval North Sea, all trembling in unison. Sound changes. The word's pronunciation records the great Germanic-to-English shifts. PIE *(s)keud- yields Germanic *skud- with an initial /sk/ cluster, and in late Old English and early Middle English that initial /sk/ before the palatalising environment was reshaped to /ʃ/, spelled sch- — exactly the shift that turned Old English scēotan into "shoot" and scip into "ship." Middle English schoderen thus shows the sch- spelling, later regularised as sh-; the medial consonant and vowels settled into modern /ˈʃʌdə/, with the unstressed final syllable eroding to /ə/ in standard British English. The exact conditioning of the palatalisation in this particular word is debated, because the medial vowel was not a typical front vowel and the OED flags the pre-Middle English route as uncertain — a possible borrowing of Middle Low German schōderen stands as an alternative to a native frequentative formation — but the attested Middle English form leaves no doubt about the outcome: the word's modern initial sound is /ʃ/. From shaking to shuddering. Once "shudder" entered English it travelled the classic path from physical to emotional sense. At first it meant bodily shaking of almost any sort; by the sixteenth century it had specialised toward the involuntary tremor of horror, dread, and revulsion, acquiring its modern moral weight — the shudder as the body's judgement delivered before the mind speaks. The noun form (in "a slight shudder") and the abstract fear-collocations ("to shudder at the thought," "I shudder to think") belong to the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, by which time the word had become a staple of Gothic and Victorian fiction — the vocabulary of the haunted house, the ghost story, and the spine-tingling thriller. It is precisely this register that C.S. Lewis draws on when Susan, staring into the cold, dark opening of the buried treasure chamber, feels the old inherited word run along her nerves. Semantic drift in summary.
  • PIE *(s)keud- "to shake, throw, dart, drive" — sudden projected motion.
  • Proto-Germanic *skudaną "to shake" → frequentative *skudrōn "to shake repeatedly, tremble."
  • Middle English schoderen (c. 1300) "to tremble" — plain bodily shaking with no moral colouring.
  • 16th c. — specialisation toward the involuntary tremor of horror, dread, and revulsion.
  • 18th–19th c. — the noun and the fear-collocations; Gothic and Victorian fiction adopt it as a stock atmospheric word.
  • Contemporary — literary and expressive; still the sharp single convulsion of revulsion or uncanny dread (and of cold), heavier than a shiver and finer than a quake; also applied metaphorically to vibrating machines ("the train shuddered to a halt").
What began as a hurled dart in the Indo-European dawn has become the smallest, quietest possible admission of dread — and the Narnian sentence lets us hear the whole journey in one word.
