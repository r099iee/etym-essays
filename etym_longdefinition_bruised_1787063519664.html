Bruised describes flesh that has been injured by a blow, pressure, or impact in such a way that the skin is not broken but the underlying tissue is damaged, discoloured, and tender. The bruised area swells, turns black and blue and then through green and yellow as it heals, and aches when touched. The word is the past participle of the verb to bruise, and its domain extends far beyond skin: fruit, feelings, pride, and even economies can all be bruised.

Core sense — physical contusion: In medicine a bruise is a contusion: damage to blood vessels beneath the skin caused by blunt force, allowing blood to leak into the tissues and producing the familiar discolouration. Everyday English uses bruised for this state — 'a bruised knee', 'a bruised arm', 'bruised and sore'. This is the sense of the literary context in which a castaway is 'so sore and stiff, so bruised and buffeted' that he can hardly stand: the blows of the waves and the rocks have left him covered in tender, discoloured contusions.

  • Sense 1 — Physically contused: injured by a blow without breaking the skin; marked by bruising ('his face was bruised and swollen').
  • Sense 2 — Of fruit and soft produce: damaged by rough handling or pressure, showing soft brownish marks beneath the skin ('a bruised peach', 'bruised apples in the crate').
  • Sense 3 — Emotionally wounded: hurt in feelings, pride, or dignity ('a bruised ego', 'her bruised feelings', 'a bruised spirit'); the emotional bruise is invisible but still aches.
  • Sense 4 — Metonymic and transferred uses: anything battered or damaged by mistreatment, competition, or fortune — 'a bruised reputation', 'a bruised economy', 'bruised by the loss'.
  • Sense 5 — Of food preparation: to crush or soften by pounding, as in bruising herbs or a bruised (lightly crushed) berry to release flavour — a deliberate, culinary sense of the verb.

Register and tone: Bruised is plain, everyday English, far commoner in speech than the medical contused. It is concrete when applied to bodies and fruit, and figurative when applied to feelings and pride; in both uses it suggests damage that does not destroy — a bruise is temporary, it heals — so bruised often carries a note of pain that is survivable, of strength dented rather than broken.

Grammar and collocation: As a past-participial adjective bruised is freely attributive ('a bruised knee') and predicative ('she was bruised'); it participates in the fixed pair 'bruised and battered', describing someone knocked about by repeated blows, and in the semi-fixed 'sore and bruised' of the source text. The verb to bruise is used transitively ('the fall bruised his arm'), and the derived noun bruise names the mark itself, from a fingertip-size bruise to an extensive battering.

Polysemy summary: The senses of bruised form a coherent chain by metaphor: a physical blow leaves a dark, tender mark on the body; rough handling leaves the same kind of dark, soft mark on fruit; and the blows of life — insult, rejection, failure — leave invisible marks on feelings, pride, and reputation that ache in exactly the same way. All modern senses are the literal one extended; none has broken free of the underlying picture of impact, tenderness, and slow healing.
