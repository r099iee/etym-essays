Etymology of PROFFERED — Latin proferre via Old French (a compound of two Indo-European roots): CHAIN FOR THE PREFIX pro-: Stage 1 (PIE): *per- (PIE) — "forward, in front of, through" — the root of English "for", "fore", and "from". Stage 2 (Proto-Italic): *por / *pro — "forward, forth". Stage 3 (Latin, attested): pro- (prefix, classical period) — "forward, forth, on behalf of". CHAIN FOR THE VERBAL ROOT ferre: Stage 1 (PIE): *bher- (PIE) — "to carry, bear, bring" — the root of English "bear" and "birth", Sanskrit bharati ("carries"), Greek pherein ("to carry"). Stage 2 (Proto-Italic): *ferō — "I carry". Stage 3 (Latin, attested 1st c. BCE): ferre — "to carry, bring, bear". COMPOUND: Stage 4 (Classical Latin, 1st c. BCE): proferre (pro- "forward" + ferre "to carry") — "to carry forward, bring forth, produce, extend, utter". Stage 5 (Old French, c. 11th–12th c.): proffrir (inflected proffre, proferons) — "to offer, present, hold out"; the related profrer appears alongside, reshaped by analogy with offrir "to offer". Stage 6 (Anglo-Norman): profrer / proffrer — "to offer for acceptance". Stage 7 (Middle English, c. 1250): profren / proffren / proferen — "to offer for acceptance, hold out". Stage 8 (Early Modern English, 16th c.–present): proffer (verb; re-spelled with double "ff" by influence of "offer"); past participle proffered. Stage 9 (modern, present day): PROFFERED — "held out (something) to someone for acceptance; offered."
