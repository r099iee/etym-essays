• Lake: Similar because both name large inland bodies of standing water. Differs because lake is the standard modern word, from Latin via French, with no archaic or poetic weight; mere is literary, dialectal, or toponymic.
  • Pool: Similar because both name still water — the source line pairs “mere and pool.” Differs because pool is smaller, often enclosed or man-made (swimming pool, rock pool); a mere is a broad natural sheet, though dialect can blur them.
  • Pond: Similar because both are small standing waters. Differs because pond is the everyday small-water word, often artificial; mere is grander, older, natural — a pond you can throw a stone across, a mere whose far shore you often cannot see.
  • Tarn: Similar because both are lakes. Differs because tarn is specifically a small mountain lake — dark, rock-bound, the Lake District word — while mere is the lowland wetland water.
  • Loch: Similar because both are lakes. Differs because loch is the Scottish Gaelic word (Irish lough) for a lake or sea-arm; mere is the English lowland word — regional brands of the same reality.
  • Lough: Similar because both are lake-words of the British Isles. Differs because lough is the Irish-Gaelic form, used especially of sea-inlets and great lakes; mere is English, usually inland and freshwater.
  • Lagoon: Similar because both are broad shallow waters. Differs because lagoon is specifically coastal or atoll water, separated from the sea; mere is inland.
  • Water (poetic/archaic): Similar because both can mean a body of standing water (“the waters of the mere”). Differs because water is the general substance-word, countable only in poetry; mere is a named kind of place.
  • Fen / Marsh / Bog: Similar because all belong to the same wetland landscape as the mere. Differs because they name wet land — tracts of sedge and peat — while the mere is the open water among them; they are the mere’s companions, not its synonyms.
  • Inland sea: Similar because both are large enclosed waters. Differs because inland sea (Caspian, Aral) is a geographical-technical term for a sea-sized lake; mere is small enough to hold a single horizon.
  • Sea: Similar because mere once meant the sea and its cognates still do (German Meer). Differs because in modern English sea is the salt ocean and mere the inland fresh water; only archaism re-unites them.
  • Broad (of the Norfolk Broads): Similar because both are broad shallow wetland waters of East Anglia. Differs because broad is the local Norfolk word for the navigable shallow lakes; mere is the general literary-English word.
