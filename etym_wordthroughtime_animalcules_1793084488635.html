- 1590s — the word coined: English forms animalcule from Latin animalculum, a little animal — at first a bookish diminutive for any small creature, insect or mouse.
- 1670s — the lens gives it work: Leeuwenhoek's letters to the Royal Society fill animalcules with new wonders — pepper-water eels, dental plaque swarms, swimming sperm — and Europe gasps.
- 18th c. — drawing-room marvels: popular microscopists display animalcules to fashionable audiences; every gentleman's library wants a solar microscope and a drop of ditch-water.
- Early 19th c. — geology ennobles them: Ehrenberg and others show chalk and limestone built of animalcule shells, so the tiniest life joins the making of continents — Emerson's image is born of this triumph.
- Late 19th c. — science outgrows the word: bacteriology sorts the swarm into bacteria, protozoa, and the rest, and animalcule retires from the laboratory to the history book.
- Today — poetry and quotation: kept alive by Emerson's sentence, by historians of science, and by writers who prefer wonder to nomenclature.
