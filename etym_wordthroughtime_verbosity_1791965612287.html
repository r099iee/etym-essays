- 16th c. — Borrowed by humanist writers as the Latin name for wordiness, a recognised fault of style opposed to brevity.
- 17th c. — Paired with the new adjective verbose and applied chiefly to sermons, treatises, and legal prose.
- 18th–19th c. — Extends to parliamentary and journalistic writing; implies obscurity, padding, and self-importance.
- Mid-20th c. — Acquires a neutral technical sense in computing: the level of detail a program reports in its output.
- 21st c. — Still pejorative for prose, neutral for software settings; increasingly used of verbose machine-generated text.
