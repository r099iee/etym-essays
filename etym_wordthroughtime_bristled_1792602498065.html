- [Late Old English / Early Middle English, c. 11th–13th c.] — Nominal stem byrst ("stiff animal hair"), referring strictly to the coarse bristles of swine and wild boars used by shoemakers and leather crafters — [perception: purely concrete, rustic craft term describing swine hair]
- [Late 14th–15th c. Middle English] — Verbalized with frequentative suffix as brustlen / bristlen, describing the dynamic physical act of an enraged boar or hound raising its dorsal hair in battle — [perception: vivid, frightening physical marker of animal fury and predatory confrontation]
- [16th–17th c. Early Modern English] — Extended poetically and martially to rows of pikes, spears, and fortress palisades standing erect (e.g. Shakespeare: "like quills upon the fretful porpentine"; Milton: armies bristling with spearheads) — [perception: grand martial aesthetic evoking impregnable, terrifying physical defense]
- [18th–19th c. Enlightenment & Victorian Eras] — Crystallized into figurative interpersonal use where an individual bristles at a slight or insult, stiffening in indignant social resistance — [perception: expressive psychological marker of wounded pride and prickly aristocratic decorum]
- [20th–21st c. Modern & Contemporary Global English] — Ubiquitous in high-register geopolitical and literary prose, describing fortified borders, missile silos, warships, and defensive diplomatic attitudes — [perception: versatile, evocative verb for dense weaponization or sharp defensive irritability]
