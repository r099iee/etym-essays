The story of accrued is the story of the verb accrue, which enters English as a learned borrowing from French and Latin and then becomes a workhorse of modern finance and law. Its depth lies not in great sound changes but in a steady narrowing from physical growth to institutional entitlement.

Its ultimate root is the Proto-Indo-European root conventionally written *ker- (sometimes *kreH-) meaning to grow or increase. This root underlies Latin crescere — to grow, arise, spring up — familiar from crescent (growing moon), increase, decrease, concrete (grown together), and crew in its older sense of a band that has grown. Latin prefixed ad- (toward) to make accrescere: to grow toward, to be added to by growth, to augment. Cicero and Livy use accrescere for states, revenues, and reputations increasing.

  • Latin to French: Vulgar Latin *accrescere yields Old French accreistre / acreistre, past participle feminine acreue (profit or increase). In medieval French it already had a commercial colour: increase of property, growth of revenue.
  • Borrowing into English (14th–15th c.): Middle English takes acrewe meaning to grow, to be added as increase, often of natural things. Early attestations are literary and botanical rather than financial.
  • Early Modern moralization (16th–18th c.): Writers on trade, husbandry, and natural law use accrue for advantages growing to a person from labour, thrift, or time. The characteristic construction "accrue to" hardens here — benefits accrue to the worker, the heir, the nation. This is exactly the Durant-style usage in the source sentence.
  • Victorian accounting and law (19th c.): With double-entry bookkeeping, accrual accounting, and modern limitation statutes, accrued becomes a term of art: accrued interest, accrued wages, accrued rights, cause of action accrued. Dictionaries distinguish transitive (to accumulate by effort) from intransitive (to come as natural increase).
  • Twentieth-century HR and finance: Accrued leave, accrued pension, accrued liabilities dominate payslips and balance sheets. Accrual vs. cash accounting becomes a foundational distinction taught worldwide.

Phonetically the change is modest: Latin /akkreskere/ loses the second s syllable in French, gains the Old French diphthong, then is re-Latinized in English spelling as accrue with mute -ue and stress on the second syllable. The modern past form simply adds regular -d. Semantically the drift is from grow, to grow toward someone, to belong by growth, to be recognized as owed even before payment. That is why economists and historians love the word: it fuses nature and right — increase that ought to belong to the increaser.

Uniquely, accrued preserves both active and passive perspectives in one form: interest accrues (grows by itself) and is accrued (is recorded as grown), allowing accountants to speak of growth as automatic yet owned. This dual voice explains its survival against simpler rivals like gained or added.
