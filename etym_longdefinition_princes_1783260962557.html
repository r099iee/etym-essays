princes (plural noun) — the standard English plural of prince, denoting sovereign rulers, monarchs, or male members of a royal family; in its oldest and widest sense, any ruler or potentate regardless of title. The word descends from Latin princeps, "first in rank" or "first-taker", a compound of primus ("first") and capere ("to take, seize"). On this card it appears in Edward Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, where the "German princes who reigned at Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna" are the barbarian kings of the successor kingdoms of the Western Roman Empire; the plural quietly performs its broadest historical job, naming the whole class of early medieval sovereign rulers of the Germanic kingdoms.

Sense 1 — Sovereign rulers and monarchs. In its primary historical sense, prince (and so princes) names anyone who rules, especially a ruler of a state, principality, or territory. English inherited from Latin princeps a width that modern royal titles have largely narrowed: in medieval and early modern English a king could be — and routinely was — called a prince, so that the title attached to emperors, kings, and dukes alike. Gibbon's "German princes who reigned at Paris, Toledo, Arles, and Ravenna" are precisely such men: the rulers of the Visigothic, Burgundian, and Frankish kingdoms who inherited the political skeleton of the Roman provinces. In this sense the plural is near-synonymous with "monarchs", "sovereigns", and "potentates".

Sense 2 — Male members of a royal family; the heir. Since roughly the early modern period, prince has narrowed in ordinary usage to denote the male offspring of a reigning monarch, or the ruler of a small principality (such as the medieval German principalities, the Principality of Monaco, or Liechtenstein). The heir to a throne is the crown prince or Prince of Wales; a prince consort is the husband of a reigning queen. The plural princes in this sense means "royal princes" — sons and brothers of kings — whose plurality matters in dynastic and succession contexts, first in line, second in line, and so on.

Sense 3 — The Roman princeps and the Machiavellian "Prince". The Latin word princeps was the title of the Roman emperor as "first citizen" (princeps senatus, "first of the senate"), a republican fiction that Augustus used to veil autocracy. In the Renaissance, Niccolo Machiavelli's treatise Il Principe ("The Prince", 1513) gave the word a permanent political-philosophical sense: the sovereign ruler conceived as a cold calculator of power. Both registers echo when "prince" is used abstractly for "the ruler" in political theory rather than for a particular titled man.

Sense 4 — Metaphoric and figurative extensions. Because princeps originally meant "first in rank" rather than "royal by birth", English freely builds figurative prince-phrases:

  • Prince of the Church — a cardinal, from the medieval view of cardinals as the princes of the ecclesiastical hierarchy.
  • Merchant prince — a wealthy, powerful commercial magnate of the industrial age, a "prince" of trade rather than of blood.
  • A prince among men — an exceptionally noble, generous, or admirable person, extending "first in rank" to moral pre-eminence.
  • Prince of darkness / Prince of this world — Satan, a biblical and literary title (Milton's Paradise Lost).
  • Prince of Peace — a title of Christ (Isaiah 9:6), "first" in the heavenly hierarchy.
  • Prince Charming — the fairy-tale ideal of the royal suitor, from Perrault's French.

Each of these rests on the same semantic bridge: the Latin compound meant "the one who takes first place" (primus "first" + capere "to take"), so that princeliness was originally a matter of precedence and pre-eminence, and only later of inherited royal blood.

Sense 5 — Derived and associated vocabulary. The word family is extensive: principality (a territory ruled by a prince; also "the principalities" as a rank of angels), princely (befitting a prince; munificent), principal ("first, chief", the later Latin doublet via principalis), principle (a "first" rule or cause, via Latin principium "a beginning"), and principate (the early Roman Empire under the princeps). This family makes the plural princes the most ordinary member of a large Latin-built set in English.

Register and usage. In modern English princes is neutral-to-formal, at home in history, monarchy reporting, fairy tale, and political theory. It has a markedly literary register on this card, where it deliberately contrasts Christian, Romanized barbarian kings who "listened with reverence to the instructions of bishops... and took part eagerly in disputes touching the Nicene theology" with the pagan rulers of Wessex and Mercia who "were still performing savage rites in the temples of Thor and Woden". The word here is a term of civilization-history, marking how far the Germanic kingdoms of the Continent had been drawn into the Roman-Christian world order that the Anglo-Saxon kings had not yet entered.
