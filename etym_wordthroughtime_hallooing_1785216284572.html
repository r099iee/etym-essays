- [late 14th century: the cry of the chase] - Middle English halow means “to pursue with shouts, to shout in the chase”; from its first records the word is the sound of the hunting field, the cry that raises and spurs the hounds.
- [mid-15th century: the cry at sea] - the same shout, halow, becomes a shipman's call to incite effort aboard ship; the huntsman's cry is put to work on the rigging, one more workplace of the raised voice.
- [17th century: the cry takes form] - the form stabilizes as halloo — the verb (1602, Marston) “to call or shout to, to hail, to urge on hounds,” the interjection (1671) — the word settles into its full hunting vocabulary.
- [19th–20th century: the gentleman's word] - the cry's family colonizes the telephone age — hello, hallo, holler — while hallooing itself recedes into the pageantry of the sport, becoming a consciously old-world, ceremonial, gentlemanly cry.
- [Contemporary: the ceremonial relic] - hallooing survives as the archaic, theatrical call of the chase, the sound of pursuers who announce their sport; that is why Tolkien puts it in goblin mouths — beasts of the dark borrowing the gentleman's cry of the hunt.
