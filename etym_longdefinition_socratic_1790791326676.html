Socratic is an eponymous adjective designating anything pertaining to, characteristic of, or derived from the life, thought, philosophical methodology, or pedagogical style of the Athenian philosopher Socrates (c. 470–399 BCE). It specifically evokes a distinct philosophical posture characterized by intellectual humility, relentless dialectical questioning, the unmasking of unexamined dogmas, and the pursuit of ethical clarity through rigorous cross-examination.

In philosophy, pedagogy, law, literary criticism, and cognitive psychology, Socratic encompasses several highly influential concepts and applications:

  • The Socratic Method (Elenchus / Dialectic): A cooperative, question-driven form of argumentative dialogue between individuals, based on asking and answering questions to stimulate critical thinking and illuminate underlying ideas. Rather than lecturing or presenting dogma, the practitioner poses systematic, probing questions that lead the respondent to examine the validity of their beliefs, uncover internal contradictions, and arrive at definitions of fundamental virtues (such as justice, piety, or courage).
  • Socratic Irony (Eirōneia): A rhetorical and conversational stance in which the speaker feigns ignorance, naivety, or lower intelligence than they actually possess. By adopting the posture of an eager, humble student seeking enlightenment from a self-proclaimed expert, the speaker gently coaxes the interlocutor into elaborating their flawed reasoning until they inevitably stumble into glaring self-contradiction, exposing their false confidence.
  • Socratic Intellectualism and Ethics: The foundational philosophical doctrine attributed to Socrates that virtue is knowledge (aretē is epistēmē) and that all moral wrongdoing stems from ignorance rather than deliberate malice ('no one does wrong willingly'). According to this view, if a person truly understands what is good and just, they will inevitably act accordingly; thus, moral education and intellectual clarity are inseparable.
  • The Socratic Problem: In classical scholarship and historiography, the methodological dilemma arising from the fact that Socrates wrote nothing himself. Modern knowledge of his life and philosophy relies entirely on the surviving, often contradictory accounts of his contemporaries and disciples—principally Plato's dialogues, Xenophon's memoirs, Aristophanes's comedic satire (The Clouds), and Aristotle's later commentary—making it difficult to disentangle the historical Socrates from literary embellishments.
  • Modern Pedagogical and Legal Socratic Teaching: Widely used in modern higher education, particularly in American law schools (popularized by Christopher Columbus Langdell at Harvard Law School). The law professor calls on students without warning, posing rapid-fire hypothetical scenarios and interrogating their legal arguments to train future attorneys in analytical precision, rapid logical deduction, and the defense of legal principles under intense pressure.
  • Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (Socratic Questioning): In clinical psychology (CBT), therapists use Socratic questioning to help patients gently identify, examine, and dismantle their own automatic irrational beliefs, cognitive distortions, and maladaptive thought patterns without direct confrontation.

Historical Semantic Bridges and Polysemy: Derived from the proper name of Socrates, the adjective moved from classical Greek descriptions of the philosopher's inner circle to Latin scholastic philosophy, Renaissance humanism, and modern pedagogy. Over time, it developed a permanent semantic polarity: denoting on one hand a supreme pedagogical ideal of collaborative, enlightened truth-seeking, and on the other hand an intimidating, razor-sharp instrument of intellectual cross-examination that drives an opponent into humiliating public contradiction.
