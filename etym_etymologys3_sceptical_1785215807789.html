The biography of sceptical opens at the Indo-European level with the root *spek-, 'to observe, to watch, to look at closely.' This is one of the most productive verbal roots of the family, and nearly every branch of Indo-European preserves some descendant of it: Latin specere 'to look at,' with its huge English family (species, special, speculum, speculate, inspect, respect, perspicacious, circumspect), Sanskrit paśyati 'he sees,' and Greek skeptesthai. The Greeks turned the root into skeptesthai, 'to look about for, to examine, to consider,' a verb used already by Homer for the action of spying out terrain or scanning a situation with careful attention.

From this verb the philosopher Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE) and his followers took the name by which the school is still known: the Skeptikoi, the 'inquirers' or 'considerers.' For Pyrrho and his successors — notably the physician-philosopher Sextus Empiricus of the second century CE, whose Outlines of Pyrrhonism and Against the Mathematicians are the great surviving handbooks of the school — the appropriate response to any claim was to examine it, find reasons on both sides, and then suspend judgment (epochē); since certainty was unattainable, the wise mind withheld assent and thereby found tranquillity (ataraxia). The word was thus from its birth not merely a description of doubt but the name of a way of life: systematic, disciplined, and deliberate. This is the meaning that lurks, half-forgotten, behind every modern use: to be sceptical is etymologically to be a careful observer who refuses to be rushed.

The route of the word into English was roundabout and learned:

  • Greek skeptikos passed into Latin as scepticus, chiefly as a scholarly label for the followers of the school, preserved in Diogenes Laertius' Lives of the Philosophers and in Renaissance editions and translations.
  • From Renaissance Latin and Middle French (sceptique, 16th century) the adjective reached English in the 1580s. Early English usage was strictly philosophical: a 'sceptick' was a member or follower of Pyrrhonism, someone who questioned the possibility of certain knowledge.
  • Across the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries the word broadened beyond the technical school to describe any person disposed to doubt unproven claims, and the adjective sceptical followed the same path; the noun scepticism and the verb scepticise were formed in parallel.
  • In the seventeenth century the word was also respelled with a k (skeptical) on the model of Greek, which is why British and American spelling diverge to this day: the sceptical of this card is the British form, retaining the Latinate c, while skeptical is the American form using Greek kappa.

The seventeenth-century intellectual revolutions fixed the modern meaning. For the scientists of the Scientific Revolution — Bacon, Galileo, Boyle, Newton — 'scepticism' became a badge of method: the refusal to accept authority, tradition, or mere appearances without experiment and demonstration. The French philosopher Descartes turned systematic doubt into the founding act of modern philosophy; the Scottish philosopher David Hume pushed scepticism to the limits of empiricism; and the eighteenth-century Enlightenment made 'the sceptical inquiry' central to the critique of superstition, priestcraft, and dogmatism. In all this the word shed its ancient connotation of a school with doctrines of its own and became the generic name for the critical attitude itself.

By the nineteenth century the word had broadened still further, from the philosophical seminar into everyday speech and into biography, where it could describe a merely human and even physical register of disbelief — exactly the 'sceptical lip' that the context of this card invokes. John Stuart Mill, writing of the readers who greet emotional declaration with a curled lip, draws on the fully modern, colloquial sense: the sceptical reader is the world-weary one who no longer believes fine phrases. The word had travelled from 'careful observer' (Greek skeptesthai) through 'member of the doubting school' (Pyrrho) and 'principled doubter of unproven claims' (the Enlightenment) to 'instinctively disbelieving' (the modern colloquial), and all four senses remain alive, distributed across registers: the scientific sceptic, the philosophical sceptic, the healthy sceptic, and the world-weary reader who curls the lip. A false cognate should be noted and excluded: Greek skēptron, 'staff, sceptre,' comes from a separate root *skep- 'to support,' and has nothing to do with seeing or doubting.
