arras — A rich ornamental wall hanging or tapestry, historically the celebrated woven textile produced in the workshops of the city of Arras in Artois, northern France, from the thirteenth to the sixteenth century. In its fully literal, historical sense, an arras was a large, heavy, woven textile panel — typically of wool, often enlivened with silk or gold thread — hung upon the walls of a great hall, a chapel, or a chamber, serving simultaneously as insulation, decoration, and the portable "architecture" of medieval interiors. Because these hangings were made in Arras, the city's name passed onto the product itself, exactly as champagne names wine from Champagne and cashmere names wool from Kashmir; the word is thus an eponym, a place-name turned product-name, and one of the clearest lexical fossils of medieval European trade.

From the very beginning the word carried two complementary associations that continue to govern its use. First, the association with wealth and ceremony: an arras was among the most prized and expensive furnishings a household could own, routinely listed in medieval inventories and wills alongside plate and jewels, and used to proclaim the rank of its owner on feast days and occasions of state. Second, the association with concealment: because arras hangings were heavy, opaque, and often hung proud of the wall on rods or tenter-frames, they left a gap behind them — a space large enough to hide a person, a doorway, or an eavesdropper. This second association is what gives the word its immense literary afterlife. In Shakespeare's Hamlet, Polonius conceals himself "behind the arras" in Gertrude's closet to spy on her interview with Hamlet, and Hamlet, suspecting treachery, stabs through the hanging and kills him. The scene made "behind the arras" a proverbial English phrase for hidden observation, and it fixed the arras in the popular imagination as a veil that hides while appearing merely ornamental — exactly the image the present passage exploits in "the arras of cryptic silence."

The word's full spectrum of senses may be itemized as follows:

  • Primary (historical-literal) sense: a rich tapestry or ornamental wall hanging of woven fabric, especially one produced in the manner of the celebrated Arras workshops.
  • Metonymic/archaic sense: the woven fabric itself as a material — "arras work," "a hanging of arras" — where the word names the textile rather than the hung panel.
  • Shakespearean/connotative sense: a hiding-place, a screen that conceals while seeming decorative; the sense activated in "behind the arras" and in any modern metaphor of hidden eavesdropping or veiled secrecy.
  • Modern figurative sense: any thick, opaque, concealing covering — here the "arras of cryptic silence," i.e. silence imagined as a heavy woven curtain behind which secrets, whispers, or "sunken seas" lie hidden.
  • Archaic legal/clothing uses: occasionally the word denotes a tapestry-woven coverlet or a bed-hanging; rare and obsolete today.

Set beside its near-synonyms, arras is by far the most specific and archaic. Tapestry emphasizes the woven pictorial narrative and remains the general modern term; hanging is plain and colorless; curtain suggests a functional partition that can be drawn aside and implies windows or openings; drapery stresses decorative folds; veil is thin, delicate, and usually partial, associated with modesty or mourning. Arras alone names both the object and its heritage — a wall hanging of rich woven fabric, typically patterned with scenes, produced in the manner of the celebrated Arras workshops — and it alone carries the sense of full, furniture-like concealment of an entire space. Where a curtain can be twitched, an arras is a wall; where a veil is translucent, an arras is opaque. To hide behind an arras is to vanish completely, and it is this total occlusion that is transferred to "cryptic silence": a silence behind which a whole whispering world, "sunken seas" and all, is hidden. In collocation, the word is almost always framed by the preposition behind — "behind the arras," "hid behind the arras," "behind the arras of …" — which is precisely the frame this sentence deploys; the phrase has become a set piece of English for concealed presence, and the passage draws the full force of that idiom into its metaphor of a listening earth.

Register and usage: the word is now consciously archaic and literary. Ordinary speech has long retreated to tapestry or wall hanging, and modern prose uses arras for period flavour, stage directions, or deliberate Shakespearean allusion; when a modern writer reaches for it, the word imports with it the whole medieval-courtly world of great halls, hidden doorways, and eavesdroppers. In this passage the metaphor works because the arras is the exact image of a silence that is not empty but crafted, layered, and concealing — a silence with thickness and depth, behind which the earth's secrets, "the sigh of sunken seas far down," are kept like treasure behind a woven wall.
